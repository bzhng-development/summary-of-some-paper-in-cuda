Here are the takeaways from this Ilya Grigorik interview (grew to Distinguished Engineer at Shopify, originally founded and sold PostRank to Google):

**Waterloo’s co-op program and the power of iterative shots on goal**

Ilya credits the University of Waterloo’s co-op program as the single most important factor in developing strong engineers. The program alternates study and work terms continuously—no summers off—giving students “at least six shots on goal for trying things.” This structure removes the stigma of experimentation because every three-month stint is expected; no one looks at a resume and asks “are you really committed?” when you did a three-month role and moved on. Students try different industries, company sizes, and types of work, discovering early what doesn’t click (“I really want to go to Wall Street… no, I actually don’t like the city / the industry / the people”). Beyond self-discovery, the applied experience builds pragmatic engineering instincts: “I actually had to build a thing,” and suddenly big-O notation becomes an “unissue because I was sorting 10 items in an array. Who cares?” He calls the co-op model “an open secret” and wishes more universities would copy it.

**The itch that became PostRank**

After undergrad, Ilya applied to grad school at University of Toronto—a well-worn escape route for students who weren’t ready to commit. But during the intervening summer he had “a particular itch.” His insight, circa 2010-2011: Google’s original PageRank treated links as social currency on a static web, but the Web 2.0 revolution meant dynamic content—comments, blogs, Reddit threads, likes—was now a much richer signal. “What if you built a system that went out and aggregated all of these interactions, then built a better version of PageRank?” That became PostRank, an algorithm for ranking posts based on social engagement. He still remembers the day—July 8th—when he released it and “didn’t sleep for three days because the servers were melting down,” partly from demand, partly because “my code was terrible.” During the chaos of investor conversations, he looked at his grad-school syllabus and realized the courses that excited him most were on entrepreneurship and building a company. “I can read those books on my own time. I could just go and try it.” He asked his advisor for a one-year deferral, closed the round, and never did finish that graduate degree.

**The Google acquisition: friendly, fast-tracked, and team-level**

The acquisition started unremarkably: they presented at a Bay Area conference, and Phil Mui—lead PM on Google Analytics—walked up afterward and said “Hey, this is cool. Do you want to have a chat?” A train ride to Mountain View and an interrogation later, the conversation ended with “maybe we should do something together.” This was right when Google and Facebook were drawing battle lines over social (the Google+ era), and Google realized they could use PostRank’s experience and infrastructure to fast-track social analytics inside Google Analytics. “Frankly, they did not care for the PR product we were building. What they cared about was the experience and some of the infrastructure we’d accumulated.” The team went through a standard full-panel interview process—Ilya included—to vet quality and level everyone. The deal was closer to an acquihire than a product acquisition; they rebuilt their stack from scratch inside Google Analytics. That trial-by-fire was humbling: “We went from managing tens of thousands of analytics accounts to operating in a service that was literally executing on more than half the internet.” He remembers an SRE conversation where adding a bit field meant “this number of exabytes of data”—“oh my gosh, okay, let me think about this more carefully.”

**Joining Google as EM and the deliberate step off the director track**

Ilya joined as founder/CTO of PostRank and was placed as an engineering manager inside Google Analytics. He spent about a year effectively rebuilding the social-analytics stack and getting the team on a good trajectory. In a career sit-down, a VP told him: “Look, you have all the things you need, you’re on track for director.” But his curiosity wouldn’t let him stay. Google was an open-culture technical playground, and he’d spent every free moment diving into internal design docs and wikis. “I remember building PostRank and reading papers about BigTable and Hadoop, trying to replicate the magic that Google had. Then I’m reading the internal design documents and I’m like, oh my God, this is three generations ahead of that paper.” He discovered a group called **Make the Web Fast**—a Sergey-originated project on improving internet speed—and became fascinated. He had “an opportunity to cash in some chips”: the director path was clear, but he could instead “take a lateral step and go work as an IC in this field, not quite even sure how I’m gonna contribute.” His manager warned him Google’s bar was high and he’d likely be down-leveled, resetting his career track. Ilya’s response: “I think that’s a trade worth having because I get to control my time. I get to work with people I look up to.” The math was simple: as a manager, “your time is managed by others. You are in the service of others.” As an IC, he’d have more self-direction to pursue interesting research. He calls the pivot “an amazing right decision for me.”

**The seesaw: IC to manager as a “tour of duty” in service of a problem**

Ilya seesawed between IC and manager multiple times—a pattern he says is “vastly underappreciated and surprisingly common” among high-performing principal-plus engineers. His framework: management is a **tour of duty** you take on when a problem demands it. The sequence goes: as a principal IC, you do trailblazing path-finding work, figure out the shape of an ambiguous problem, then realize “I need to recruit a team of people to help me actually build the thing. I know how to get to the top of that hill with a machete, but now I need to build a highway.” That naturally turns you into a TL, and “it’s not a far stretch to switch to a manager.” His own example: after doing IC web-standards work inside Make the Web Fast—defining browser performance metrics with W3C and IETF—the next problem became ecosystem adoption. Convincing analytics vendors and open-source projects to adopt those metrics “does not scale with one human.” He realized that without intervention, adoption would take three to five years, and his question was “how can I compress it into one to two years?” The answer: “inject energy into the system” by switching to manager, recruiting a team, and executing the go-to-market push as Director of Developer Relations.

The tour ends when the mission succeeds or becomes self-sustaining. His success metric as a principal engineer is the Homer Simpson disappearing-into-the-bushes meme: “If I can pull that off on a project and the team continues executing in the right direction with good velocity, that’s success for me.” Some problems he exits; others are “in my bones” and he commits to a two-year tour, with an explicit succession plan. At the end, he becomes an IC again to find the next thing to solve. He’s emphatic that organizations should remove the stigma from this transit: too many directors, after a couple of drinks, admit they were happiest as senior ICs but felt they had to “grow” into management. “Let’s set up our organizations where it’s okay for people to transit between those roles. You’re not penalized, and let’s not as an industry position it as some sort of demotion.”

**Principal+ engineering at Shopify: no job description, just agency**

When people ask what a principal engineer *does*—what boxes to tick—the question itself signals they aren’t one yet. “The problems become ambiguous enough at that level where you kind of have to figure it out yourself.” His framing for new principal engineers at Shopify: “You’re gonna be dropped, parachuted into foreign terrain. You’re in a reconnaissance mission, and within the first seven days you should figure out the lay of the land and figure out where the problems are. Build alliances with your directors, VPs, whoever it needs to be, figure out what their problems are, and then understand the situation and figure out where you can apply your particular skillset to help. I don’t have a prescription for you—it is your agency.”

At the Distinguished Engineer level (VP-equivalent), it’s even more amorphous. The proof is **dynamic range** across two axes. Technically: can you operate at all layers of the stack, going “all the way down to the bare metal” while also engaging with business requirements and translating VP/product thinking into actionable code? Executionally: can you flex between fast-mode startup-style exploration (“fire, see where it lands, aim, fire again”) and slow-mode platform thinking where API design decisions have “second and third order repercussions years down the road for our partner ecosystem”? By definition, senior roles only get hard problems with incomplete information or imperfect decisions; dynamic range gives confidence you can navigate yourself out of whatever weird-shaped problem you’re handed. He’s often the most senior person in the room but “rarely the most knowledgeable”—his contribution is understanding the fundamentals of the platform, understanding business requirements, and helping the people who know the messy middle navigate the decision space. “I have a toolkit to help them facilitate that.”

**“Don’t be the best, be the only”**

This is Ilya’s career philosophy and the advice he’d give his younger self. Optimizing to be the *best* in a single domain is one valid path, but it’s fragile: “What if you pick the wrong field? What if the world changes under you? What if your particular company does not need that particular skill set?” The alternative is building a **talent stack**—a portfolio of skills you can juggle—so that you’re the *only* person with that particular intersection. It’s literally probability math: multiply out the odds that someone knows enough about databases *and* graphics *and* marketing *and* security, and you become uniquely versatile. He’s explicit about his own stack: “I was a mediocre designer, a mediocre salesperson, I was okay at support.” But combined, those mean “I could look at a problem and say, yeah, I could link together a website. I know Photoshop well enough. I know how to host it and I know how to secure it, and I can stand all that up for you in the next two days. You don’t have to hire a team for that.” Once a specialist arrives, he drops that skill from his quiver and focuses elsewhere.

A key insight: you can acquire 80–90% competence in a domain in days to weeks (“especially now with LLMs”). Getting to 90% might take a year, but the 80% level is enough to converse fluently. He once bought 15 marketing books on Amazon and spent a week doing nothing but reading them so he could speak the right lexicon with the marketing team. “Oftentimes when I find myself in conversations at Shopify, I am the translator between all of these different parts of the company. That gives me a lot of versatility.”

**Career regrets: the filter that success creates**

His primary regret is letting the pressure of past success stop him from trying. Early in his career he’d publish anything—blog posts he now finds “flat-out embarrassing,” GitHub code that’s “embarrassing”—but those milestones were essential. “It was the limit of my ability at the time when I did it, and I’m so glad that I did it, because it was putting that work out there that gave me the right feedback and encouragement that led me to improve.” The trap: as your audience and reputation grow, you start filtering. “Ryan’s interviews are really good, so in the next one I expect it to be out of the park—better than ever.” The courage to be wrong, to be disliked, to make a mistake, gets squeezed out. Ryan relates: as his newsletter subscriber count grew, he found himself rejecting more topic ideas during ideation. Ilya’s antidote is “don’t let that expectation become a gate and filter for the work you put out, because that feedback and exposure is the quintessential ingredient you need for growth.”

The same dynamic plays out when stepping into a new technical domain as a distinguished engineer. The key is how you show up. He never arrives as the person with all the answers; he arrives curious, asking “why? Help me understand this.” The way you ask questions matters enormously—“there’s a subtle difference between trying to attack someone’s work and being curious.” He sets expectations upfront: “Guys, I don’t know what you know. I need to very rapidly build my mental model of this space, so I’m gonna ask a lot of dumb questions—excuse me upfront. I also have my expertise and I know some other things you don’t, so help me navigate through this.” That framing turns the interaction into a collaborative, symbiotic exercise: he clears roadblocks they can’t, they bring knowledge he doesn’t have.

**Ken Robinson and rethinking education as choosing your own adventure**

When asked for a top career-impacting book, Ilya names Ken Robinson—not one book but his entire body of work, especially the TED Talks (“20 minutes of your time that is gonna be the highest ROI”). Robinson’s core message: stop treating education like a factory-line model where we bin people by age and teach them fixed skills in sequence. “We all spike in different ways at different points in our life. Let’s have an environment where people can choose their own adventure.” This philosophy of mixing skills, staying creative, and rejecting pigeonholing is “probably what informed a lot of my thinking” about combining skill sets and being the only.

**Advice to his younger self: be braver, chase passion, don’t filter**

Two pieces. First, “don’t be the best, be the only”—trust that the ability to negotiate across many domains will pan out, even when it feels uncomfortable watching specialist friends excel. Second, be braver about putting work out there. Ilya was “deathly afraid of public speaking” through high school, running away from class to avoid it, yet public speaking eventually became part of his nine-to-five. The difference: when he was forced to present something he had no investment in, it was a test of knowledge and produced cold sweats. But when pitching PostRank—something he was passionate about and wanted to change in the ecosystem—“that turned out to make all the difference.” “If you told me early in my college career that my future holds public speaking, I would say that you’re crazy.” Chase your passions, and a lot of things flip in how you engage.

---

His **tour of duty** framing redefines the manager/IC seesaw not as a ladder but as tool selection—you pick the role that solves the problem fastest, then return to IC to find the next hill. That resilience echoes his **“don’t be the best, be the only”** talent-stack philosophy: both reject single-track optimization in favor of versatility and anti-fragility. The **dynamic range** he demands of a Distinguished Engineer is the organizational expression of that same idea—being the person who can translate between bare metal, business requirements, and marketing lexicon, then disappear into the bushes once the highway is built.
