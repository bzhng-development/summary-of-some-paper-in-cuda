# Zero Bubble Pipeline Parallelism

**ArXiv:** [2401.10241](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.10241)

## 🎯 Pitch

It turns out the infamous pipeline bubbles in distributed training are not inevitable: by splitting the backward pass into two parts and cleverly scheduling the parameter-gradient computation into idle moments, this work delivers the first synchronous schedule with truly zero bubbles, boosting throughput by up to 31%.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces a novel pipeline parallelism scheduling strategy that achieves **zero pipeline bubbles** under synchronous training semantics by splitting the backward pass into two distinct computations — **B** (gradient for the input) and **W** (gradient for the parameters) — and then flexibly scheduling W to fill otherwise-idle time in the pipeline (e.g., placing W passes in tail-end bubbles left empty by the standard one-forward-one-backward schedule). Experimental evaluations on GPT-3–style models up to 28.3B parameters using up to 32 GPUs demonstrate that the automatically searched schedule (ZB-2p) outperforms 1F1B by up to 23% in throughput under similar memory constraints, and up to 31% when memory limits are relaxed — equivalent to reducing the bubble rate below 1% in most settings. A memory-efficient variant, ZB-V, further achieves near-zero bubble with the same peak activation memory as 1F1B by interleaving model chunks in a "V"-shaped assignment pattern, establishing that zero-bubble pipeline parallelism is attainable even under tight memory budgets only when the bidirectional dependency chain of standard interleaving is replaced with a symmetric forward-backward routing scheme.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Pipeline Bubbles Are the Dominant Inefficiency in Synchronous Pipeline Parallelism

The fundamental problem this paper tackles is the existence of **pipeline bubbles** — idle periods where devices sit unused because they must wait for dependencies from adjacent stages — in synchronous pipeline-parallel training. Pipeline parallelism (PP) partitions a deep neural network across multiple devices, with each device responsible for a contiguous subset of layers (a "stage"). The output of stage $i$ must flow to stage $i+1$ during the forward pass, and gradients must flow from stage $i+1$ back to stage $i$ during the backward pass. These sequential dependencies create a scheduling challenge: the first device finishes its work on a microbatch before the second device even starts, and the last device finishes its backward pass before the first device can begin its corresponding backward computation. Devices inevitably spend time waiting rather than computing — these waiting periods are the pipeline bubbles.

This problem is important for several concrete reasons, which the paper establishes in Section 1:

- **Scale of modern training:** Training frontier models (GPT-3, PaLM, etc.) requires thousands of GPUs. Pipeline parallelism is the dominant strategy for utilizing cross-server communication links, where bandwidth is limited compared to intra-node interconnects like NVLink. As the paper states: "there are numerous empirical evidences... showing PP is particularly advantageous for utilizing cross-server connections, especially at the scale of thousands of GPUs." Any inefficiency in PP directly multiplies across thousands of expensive accelerators.

- **Bubble size grows with pipeline depth:** The bubble size in a standard schedule scales with $p - 1$, where $p$ is the number of pipeline stages. As models grow larger and are partitioned across more devices, the bubble fraction increases — more parallelization paradoxically yields diminishing returns because more devices spend more time idle. This is a fundamental scaling bottleneck that limits how aggressively models can be partitioned.

- **The tension between bubble reduction and memory:** Prior approaches could reduce the effective bubble ratio by increasing the number of microbatches $m$ (since the bubble size $(p-1)(T_F + T_B + T_W)$ becomes a smaller fraction of total execution time as $m$ grows). However, this increases peak activation memory, which is already the primary constraint on training large models. The bubble-memory tradeoff forces practitioners to choose between throughput and feasibility.

The paper frames this as an inefficiency that was "deemed inevitable" (Section 1), which captures the state of the field prior to this work: researchers accepted pipeline bubbles as a fundamental consequence of the sequential dependency chain in synchronous training, something to be minimized but never eliminated.

### Conflicting Design Tensions in Prior Approaches

The paper identifies a landscape of prior approaches, each with specific shortcomings that motivate the present work. The key pattern is that existing methods force a choice between bubble reduction, memory efficiency, and optimization correctness — no method simultaneously achieves all three.

**GPipe (Huang et al., 2019) — the baseline synchronous schedule.** GPipe processes an entire batch by splitting it into microbatches, executing all forward passes for all microbatches across all stages sequentially, then executing all backward passes in reverse order. This creates a large bubble at the boundary between the forward and backward phases — the "warm-up" bubble at the beginning and the "cool-down" bubble at the end. The bubble ratio is approximately $(p-1)/m$, so GPipe compensates by using many microbatches. However, this directly increases peak activation memory because activations from all in-flight microbatches must be stored until their corresponding backward passes arrive. GPipe addresses this memory pressure by discarding intermediate activations and recomputing them during the backward pass (activation recomputation), but the paper notes this "introduced a computation overhead of around 20%." So GPipe trades bubble time for memory pressure, then trades memory pressure for recomputation overhead — a chain of compromises.

**1F1B (Harlap et al., 2018; Fan et al., 2021; Narayanan et al., 2021) — the state-of-the-art synchronous baseline.** The one-forward-one-backward schedule improves on GPipe by interleaving forward and backward passes. After an initial warm-up phase where each stage processes a varying number of forward passes (stage $i$ does one more than stage $i+1$), the system enters a steady state where each device alternates between one forward and one backward pass. This provides faster memory clearance because backward passes start earlier, freeing activation memory sooner. The bubble size remains approximately $(p-1)(T_F + T_B + T_W)$, similar to GPipe for the same number of microbatches, but the peak activation memory is significantly reduced because fewer forward-pass activations are simultaneously in flight. 1F1B is the de facto standard for synchronous PP, and the paper uses it as the primary baseline.

Where 1F1B falls short: **the bubbles remain.** While 1F1B improves memory efficiency, it does not fundamentally change the bubble structure. The trapezoid shape of the schedule (warm-up bubble at the start, execution trapezoid in the middle, cool-down bubble at the end) is still present. The bubble fraction still scales with $(p-1)/m$, meaning high efficiency requires many microbatches, which still increases memory pressure.

**1F1B Interleaved (Narayanan et al., 2021) — sub-stage partitioning.** Interleaved 1F1B further reduces bubble size by assigning multiple non-contiguous model chunks to each device, cycling through them in a round-robin fashion. By breaking the model into finer-grained chunks (e.g., each transformer layer as a separate chunk) and distributing them across devices cyclically, the dependency chain becomes more compact — the earliest backward pass from a later chunk can begin before the earlier chunk's forward pass has propagated to the end of the pipeline. This reduces the effective bubble size. However, it introduces two drawbacks: **increased communication** (more activation and gradient transfers between devices because chunks are smaller and more numerous) and **higher peak memory** (multiple chunks per device accumulate activations independently). The paper notes in Section 5 that 1F1B-I shows better throughput than 1F1B in single-node setups but its advantage diminishes in multi-node configurations where communication bandwidth becomes the bottleneck.

**Asynchronous PP (PipeDream, PipeMare) — correctness sacrifices.** PipeDream (Harlap et al., 2018) and PipeMare (Yang et al., 2021) pursue an alternative path: allow stages to process different microbatches with different parameter versions, eliminating bubbles entirely by removing the synchronization requirement. These methods are "theoretically bubble free" and "greatly improve pipeline efficiency" (Section 1). However, the cost is loss of exact optimization semantics — the model effectively trains with stale gradients, which can affect convergence behavior. The paper acknowledges this tradeoff but positions itself firmly in the synchronous camp: "at the sacrifice of exact optimization semantics." The goal is to achieve the efficiency of asynchronous methods while preserving the mathematical correctness of synchronous training.

### The Granularity Argument: Why Bubbles Were Deemed Inevitable

The paper makes a crucial observation about **why** prior synchronous approaches couldn't eliminate bubbles, and this observation is the intellectual foundation for the entire work.

Traditional deep learning frameworks define the backward pass as a single monolithic function per layer. When a layer's backward function executes, it performs two distinct computations:

1. **B**: The gradient with respect to the layer's **input** ($\nabla_x f(x, W)^\top \frac{d\ell}{dy}$), which must be passed to the previous layer to continue backpropagation.
2. **W**: The gradient with respect to the layer's **parameters** ($\nabla_W f(x, W)^\top \frac{d\ell}{dy}$), which is used locally for the optimizer update.

These are grouped together because they share common intermediate computations and because, in data parallelism, the communication of W's gradient at layer $i$ can be overlapped with B's computation at layer $i-1$ — so there is no benefit to splitting them. But in pipeline parallelism, this grouping is **actively harmful**. The sequential dependency chain requires that layer $i-1$'s B cannot begin until layer $i$'s B has completed (because it needs the gradient from layer $i$). Since B and W are bundled together, layer $i-1$ must wait for **both** B and W at layer $i$ before it can start. W, however, has no downstream dependency — it's purely local. By forcing W into the sequential chain, traditional backpropagation adds unnecessary latency to the pipeline.

The paper's framing is that prior work operated at the **wrong granularity**. As stated in Section 1:

> "In PP, this design unnecessarily increases the sequentially dependent computations, i.e. B at the layer $i-1$ depends on W at the layer $i$, which is usually detrimental for the efficiency of the pipeline."

This is the key insight: **the backward pass contains a non-sequentially-dependent sub-computation (W) that can be flexibly rescheduled.** Prior work treated the backward pass as atomic, which made bubbles appear structurally unavoidable. By splitting B and W, the sequential dependency chain shortens (only F → B → F → B across stages, with W detached), and the freed W computation can be strategically placed to fill otherwise-idle time.

The paper provides quantitative motivation for this split in Table 1. For a standard transformer layer with hidden dimension $h$, sequence length $s$, and attention heads $a$:

- $T_F$ (forward time) is dominated by $sbh(24h + 4s)$ FLOPs
- $T_B$ (input gradient time) requires $sbh(24h + 8s)$ FLOPs — larger than F because of the additional matmuls for the gradient of the attention mechanism
- $T_W$ (parameter gradient time) requires $sbh(24h)$ FLOPs — consistently the smallest of the three

The relationship $T_B + T_W = 2T_F$ holds, which means W represents a **substantial fraction of total backward computation that can be decoupled from the sequential chain**.

Additionally, the memory footprint of W is smaller than B. As shown in Table 1, B requires storing $sb(34h + 5as)$ bytes of activations (intermediate values from the forward pass needed for the input gradient), while W requires only $32sbh$ bytes (some activations are freed after B completes, and W needs additional storage for certain gradients). This memory asymmetry means that scheduling W later in the pipeline — after B has completed and released some memory — is memory-efficient: the peak memory is determined by the number of simultaneously in-flight B passes, not W passes.

### How the Paper Positions Itself

The paper positions its contribution as a **scheduling strategy at a finer computational granularity**, enabled by the B/W split. This is not a new form of parallelism (it is orthogonal to data parallelism, tensor parallelism, and ZeRO sharding, as the paper explicitly states), nor is it a relaxation of synchronous semantics. It is a **drop-in replacement for the PP schedule** that can be integrated into existing training frameworks.

Specifically, the paper addresses the limitations of prior work along several dimensions:

1. **Bubble elimination vs. memory tradeoff:** The B/W split enables schedules that fill bubbles without increasing peak memory beyond 1F1B (ZB-H1, ZB-1p) or that achieve near-zero bubble with controlled memory increase (ZB-H2, ZB-2p). The paper provides a spectrum of options users can select based on their memory constraints.

2. **Practicality under realistic timing:** Handcrafted schedules like ZB-H1 and ZB-H2 assume $T_F = T_B = T_W$ and ignore communication time. The paper develops an automatic scheduling algorithm (Section 3) that uses profiled per-operation timing to produce schedules adapted to real hardware — addressing the gap between idealized analysis and actual deployment.

3. **Zero bubble under memory constraints:** The ZB-V schedule (Section 6) achieves near-zero bubble with the same peak memory as 1F1B, which was previously thought impossible. This is achieved through a novel "V"-shaped interleaving pattern where the forward and backward passes of each microbatch both originate from the same worker, unlike standard interleaving where forward starts from worker 1 and backward from worker $p$. This symmetry allows faster memory clearance and more balanced peak memory distribution.

4. **Optimizer synchronization as a hidden barrier:** The paper identifies that standard optimizer synchronizations (all-reduce for gradient norm clipping and NaN/Inf checking) "destroy the parallelogram" required for zero-bubble scheduling. The post-validation strategy (Section 4) eliminates this barrier by replacing pre-step synchronization with post-step validation and rollback — a technique that preserves synchronous semantics while removing the synchronization point from the critical path.

The paper is careful to scope its contribution: it does not claim to replace data parallelism, tensor parallelism, or ZeRO. It specifically targets the scheduling inefficiency within pipeline parallelism itself, and positions the resulting schedules as "a parallel replacement for the PP part in large scale training" (Section 1). This scoping is important because it means the method's gains are additive to, not competing with, improvements from other parallelism strategies.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

The system being built is a **pipeline scheduling algorithm** that determines, for each device and each point in time, which operation (forward pass F, input-gradient computation B, or parameter-gradient computation W) to execute on which microbatch so that no device is ever idle waiting for dependencies. The problem it solves is the structural inefficiency in standard pipeline parallelism where devices spend a significant fraction of time idle — the "pipeline bubble" — and the solution takes the shape of **splitting the monolithic backward pass into two independently schedulable sub-operations (B and W)** and then using optimization algorithms (heuristic search or integer linear programming) to find schedules that place W computations into the idle periods that B's sequential dependency chain leaves behind.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The system has five major components connected in a pipeline:

1. **Profiler** — measures the empirical execution times `$T_F$`, `$T_B$`, `$T_W$` (forward, input-gradient backward, parameter-gradient backward) and communication time `$T_{comm}$` for a single transformer layer on the target hardware, producing timing constants that drive all subsequent scheduling decisions.

2. **B/W Split Mechanism** — a modification to the deep learning framework that exposes the backward pass as two separate operations at each layer: `$B$` (`$\nabla_x f(x, W)^\top \frac{d\ell}{dy}$`) and `$W$` (`$\nabla_W f(x, W)^\top \frac{d\ell}{dy}$`), with `$B$` remaining in the sequential cross-stage dependency chain and `$W$` becoming freely schedulable after its corresponding `$B$` completes.

3. **Schedule Generator** — takes the profiled timing, the number of pipeline stages `$p$`, the number of microbatches `$m$`, and a memory limit `$M_{limit}$` as inputs, then produces an ordered sequence of `$(stage, microbatch, operation)$` tuples. Two implementations exist: a heuristic algorithm (fast, good for large `$m$`) and an integer linear programming formulation (optimal for smaller problems).

4. **Post-Validation Optimizer Step** — replaces the standard all-reduce synchronization before each optimizer step with a pipeline-propagated partial reduction followed by a post-step validation pass that can trigger an in-place arithmetic rollback if numerical issues (NaN, gradient overflow) are detected. This removes the synchronization barrier that would otherwise break the parallelogram structure of zero-bubble schedules.

5. **V-Shaped Interleaving (ZB-V variant)** — an alternative model partitioning scheme where device `$i$` receives two non-contiguous model chunks symmetrically positioned from the ends of the model inward (layers `$1$`–`$k$` and `$L-k+1$`–`$L$` to device 1, layers `$k+1$`–`$2k$` and `$L-2k+1$`–`$L-k$` to device 2, etc.), creating a bidirectional dependency chain that allows both forward and backward passes of each microbatch to start and end on the same worker, reducing peak memory to match 1F1B while achieving near-zero bubble.

Information flows as follows: the profiler runs a few warm-up iterations → timing values feed into the schedule generator → the schedule generator outputs an ordered list of operations per device → at runtime, each device executes its assigned operations in sequence → at the optimizer step boundary, each device computes a partial reduction of gradient norm and NaN/Inf flags, passes it forward to the next stage, applies the optimizer update based on the partially reduced state, then during the next iteration's warm-up phase receives the fully reduced global state back-propagated from the last stage to validate the update → if validation fails, an in-place arithmetic rollback reverses the optimizer step and re-applies it with the correct global state.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First, the core operation split:** defining `$F$`, `$B$`, and `$W$` precisely, their dependencies (which are sequential across stages and which are local), and their timing/memory characteristics from Table 1 — because the entire scheduling framework is built on exploiting the difference between `$B$`'s cross-stage dependency and `$W$`'s local nature.
- **Second, the handcrafted schedules (ZB-H1, ZB-H2):** walking through the construction logic for each, their bubble-size formulas from Table 2, and *why* ZB-H1 matches 1F1B's peak memory while ZB-H2 achieves zero bubble at the cost of doubled peak memory — this establishes the core design pattern before we add realism.
- **Third, the automatic scheduling algorithm:** the heuristic algorithm's four-step procedure (warm-up F packing, 1F-1B-1W steady state, W insertion for bubble filling, tail W scheduling), the binary hyperparameters that control edge cases, and the Integer Linear Programming (ILP) formulation (Equations 3–7 with the optimization objective, dependency constraints, and memory constraints) — this explains how the idealized handcrafted logic is adapted to heterogeneous `$T_F \neq T_B \neq T_W$` and non-zero `$T_{comm}$`.
- **Fourth, the optimizer synchronization bypass:** the standard all-reduce barrier and why it breaks zero-bubble schedules ("destroys the parallelogram"), the forward-reduce-then-backward-validate protocol, and the arithmetic reversibility of the AdamW optimizer update (Algorithm 1) that enables in-place rollback without storing parameter history.
- **Fifth, the ZB-V memory-efficient zero-bubble schedule:** the V-shaped model chunk assignment, why it enables both forward and backward of each microbatch to start from the same worker (unlike standard interleaving), and the three-phase execution pattern (warm-up, steady 1F-1B-1W, tail B-and-W) that achieves near-zero bubble with `$p M_B$` peak memory instead of `$(2p-1)M_B$`.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **systems paper** whose core idea is that the backward pass in deep learning contains a non-sequentially-dependent sub-computation — the parameter gradient `$W$` — that can be flexibly rescheduled to fill pipeline idle time, and that by representing the computation graph at this finer granularity (splitting the monolithic backward function into `$B$` and `$W$`) and applying automated scheduling with profiled timing, one can achieve pipeline schedules with near-zero bubble rates while respecting memory constraints and preserving synchronous optimization semantics.

#### The F/B/W Operation Split: Dependencies, Timing, and Memory

The paper redefines the computation graph of a single neural network layer from the traditional two-operation view (forward `$F$`, backward) into a three-operation view: forward `$F$`, input-gradient backward `$B$`, and parameter-gradient backward `$W$`.

**Forward pass `$F$`:** For a layer with input `$x$` and parameters `$W$`, the forward computation `$y = f(x, W)$` transforms the input into the output. In pipeline parallelism, `$F$` at stage `$i$` for microbatch `$j$` depends on `$F$` at stage `$i-1$` for the same microbatch completing and transferring its output activation across the stage boundary, incurring communication time `$T_{comm}$`.

**Input-gradient backward `$B$`:** The computation `$\nabla_x f(x, W)^\top \frac{d\ell}{dy}$` computes the gradient of the loss `$\ell$` with respect to the layer's input `$x$`. This gradient must be passed backward to the previous stage `$i-1$` to continue backpropagation. `$B$` at stage `$i$` for microbatch `$j$` depends on `$B$` at stage `$i+1$` for the same microbatch completing and transferring the gradient across the stage boundary. This is the **sequential dependency** that creates pipeline bubbles — stage `$i$` cannot begin `$B$` for microbatch `$j$` until stage `$i+1$` has finished its `$B$` and communicated the result.

**Parameter-gradient backward `$W$`:** The computation `$\nabla_W f(x, W)^\top \frac{d\ell}{dy}$` computes the gradient of the loss with respect to the layer's parameters `$W$`. This gradient is **purely local** — it is consumed by the optimizer on the same device to update the parameters, and no other stage depends on it. `$W$` at stage `$i$` depends only on `$B$` at the same stage `$i$` for the same microbatch completing first (because `$W$` needs the intermediate gradient `$\frac{d\ell}{dy}$` that `$B$` computes). There is **no cross-stage dependency** for `$W$`.

**Why the split matters for scheduling:** In traditional frameworks where `$B$` and `$W$` are bundled into a single backward function, the sequential chain is `$F_i \rightarrow F_{i+1} \rightarrow (B_{i+1}, W_{i+1}) \rightarrow (B_i, W_i)$`. Stage `$i$` must wait for stage `$i+1$` to complete both `$B_{i+1}$` and `$W_{i+1}$` before it can start its own `$B_i$`. By splitting, the chain becomes `$F_i \rightarrow F_{i+1} \rightarrow B_{i+1} \rightarrow B_i \rightarrow W_i$`, with `$W_{i+1}$` scheduled independently. The critical path from `$F_1$` on microbatch 1 to `$B_1$` on microbatch 1 shortens because the `$W$` computations are no longer on it. The freed `$W$` slots can be placed into the idle periods (bubbles) that the `$F \rightarrow B$` chain inevitably creates — specifically, at the "tail" of the schedule where later stages have finished their `$B$` passes but earlier stages are still working through `$B$` and `$W$` for earlier microbatches.

**Quantitative timing characteristics (Table 1):** For a standard transformer layer with hidden dimension `$h$`, sequence length `$s$`, batch size `$b$`, number of attention heads `$a$`, and feedforward hidden dimension `$4h$`:

- `$T_F$`: `$sbh(24h + 4s)$` FLOPs, dominated by the matmuls in the self-attention (QKV projections, output projection) and feedforward network (two linear layers).
- `$T_B$`: `$sbh(24h + 8s)$` FLOPs — consistently larger than `$T_F$` because the backward pass through attention requires computing gradients for the softmax and the attention score matrix multiplication, adding `$4sbhs$` FLOPs beyond what a simple transpose of the forward matmuls would require.
- `$T_W$`: `$sbh(24h)$` FLOPs — the smallest of the three, corresponding to the parameter gradients for the weight matrices only.

The relationship `$T_B + T_W = 2T_F$` holds exactly because each forward matmul produces two backward matmuls (one for the input gradient, one for the weight gradient) with identical FLOP counts. In practice, `$T_W < T_F < T_B$`, which means `$W$` is the cheapest and fastest operation and thus the easiest to slot into small idle gaps in the pipeline.

**Memory characteristics (Table 1):** The activation memory (`$M_B$` for `$B$`, `$M_W$` for `$W$`) refers to the intermediate tensors from the forward pass that must be retained for gradient computation:

- `$B$` requires `$sb(34h + 5as)$` bytes of stored activations: this includes the QKV projections, attention scores, attention output, feedforward hidden states, and layer normalization inputs — all needed to compute the input gradient through attention and feedforward.
- `$W$` requires `$32sbh$` bytes: after `$B$` completes, some activations (the attention-specific intermediates) are released, but `$W$` needs the forward inputs to each linear layer (`$x$` for each matmul `$Wx$`), as well as space to store the computed parameter gradients themselves. The paper notes that `$M_W < M_B$`, which is empirically observed in Table 1 but not proven — the `$34h + 5as$` term for `$B$` includes both the layer inputs (`$24h$`-scale) and additional attention intermediates (`$10h + 5as$`-scale), while `$W$`'s `$32h$`-scale reflects only the layer inputs plus gradient storage overhead.

**Why this matters for scheduling under memory constraints:** Since `$M_W < M_B$`, the peak activation memory in any schedule is dominated by the maximum number of simultaneously in-flight `$B$` passes, not `$W$` passes. This means that scheduling `$W$` later (after corresponding `$B$` passes have completed and freed their larger memory footprint) is memory-efficient. A schedule can pack many `$W$` operations into the tail without exceeding peak memory, as long as the number of concurrent `$B$` operations does not grow.

#### Handcrafted Schedules ZB-H1 and ZB-H2

The paper presents two manually designed schedules that illustrate the core principles before introducing the automated algorithm. Both assume the idealized condition `$T_F = T_B = T_W$` and ignore communication time `$T_{comm}$`, following the convention of prior scheduling analyses (Narayanan et al., 2021; Huang et al., 2019). This assumption makes the schedule geometry regular and the bubble counting exact.

**ZB-H1: Memory-efficient schedule (Figure 3, top).** This schedule is designed to have **peak activation memory identical to 1F1B** (`$p M_B$`) while reducing the bubble size by approximately two-thirds (from `$(p-1)(T_F + T_B + T_W)$` to `$(p-1)(T_F + T_B - T_W)$` in the idealized case).

The construction logic:

1. **Warm-up phase:** Identical to 1F1B. Stage `$i$` executes `$p - i + 1$` forward passes for microbatches 1 through `$p - i + 1$`. This creates the familiar trapezoid shape where earlier stages have more forward passes completed before backward passes begin.

2. **Steady state:** After the warm-up, each stage enters an alternating pattern: one forward pass followed by one backward pass. However, the backward pass is now split — the stage executes `$B$` immediately after `$F$` (maintaining the 1F1B rhythm), but **defers `$W$` to a later point**. The key scheduling decision: `$W$` for microbatch `$j$` at stage `$i$` is scheduled such that the total number of in-flight microbatches (those with `$F$` and `$B$` completed but `$W$` pending) is balanced across stages.

3. **Tail phase:** When all `$F$` and `$B$` passes are exhausted, each stage processes its deferred `$W$` passes sequentially. The crucial geometric observation: because `$W$` starts later than `$B$` in the earlier stages (which had more warm-up forwards and thus more `$B$` passes to complete first), the `$W$` passes naturally fill the bubble that would otherwise exist at the tail of the schedule. The last device finishes its `$B$` passes early (it had the fewest warm-up forwards), and while it waits for earlier devices to finish their `$B$` passes, it processes its `$W$` passes. This is visible in Figure 3 (top) where the `$W$` blocks (green) fill the triangular empty space at the bottom-right of the schedule.

**Peak memory analysis:** The peak activation memory occurs on the first stage, which holds the most in-flight microbatches. At the moment just before the first `$B$` begins on stage 1, there are `$p$` forward passes completed (across microbatches 1 through `$p$`), so `$p M_B$` bytes of activations are stored. This matches 1F1B's peak memory exactly. The later stages store fewer in-flight activations because they have fewer warm-up forwards, so their memory usage is lower. Since `$M_W < M_B$`, the `$W$` passes added later do not push memory beyond the `$p M_B$` peak set by the `$B$` passes.

**ZB-H2: Zero-bubble schedule (Figure 3, bottom).** This schedule achieves exactly zero bubble when `$T_F = T_B = T_W$` and `$m$` (number of microbatches) is sufficiently large, at the cost of increased peak memory: `$(2p-1)M_B$` instead of `$p M_B$`.

The construction logic differs from ZB-H1 in two ways:

1. **Extended warm-up phase:** Stage `$i$` executes **more** forward passes than the standard `$p - i + 1$` pattern. Specifically, the first stage executes additional forward passes beyond what is strictly needed to reach the steady state. These extra forwards "push out" the beginning of the `$B$` sequence, filling the triangular bubble at the start of the pipeline. In Figure 3 (bottom), this is visible as the top-left region being completely filled with `$F$` blocks (blue) — there is no empty space before the first `$B$` on device 1.

2. **Reordered `$W$` passes at the tail:** Instead of scheduling `$W$` passes in the natural order (earlier microbatches' `$W$` before later microbatches'), the `$W$` passes are **reordered** to transform the trapezoid shape at the tail into a parallelogram. The geometric effect: the right edge of the schedule becomes a straight vertical line rather than a slanted edge. This eliminates the remaining triangular bubble at the bottom-right. In practice, this means that later stages process `$W$` for later microbatches *before* processing `$W$` for earlier microbatches — a reordering that is valid because `$W$` has no cross-microbatch dependencies.

The net effect is a parallelogram: all stages start their work on each microbatch type at a consistent offset, and all stages finish simultaneously. The bubble size formula in Table 2 reflects this:

> Bubble size for ZB-H2: `$(p-1)(T_F + T_B - 2T_W)$`

When `$T_F = T_B = T_W$`, this becomes `$(p-1)(T - 2T) = -(p-1)T$`, which is negative — indicating that with enough microbatches, the schedule becomes bubble-free because the `$W$` computations can be packed so tightly that the total execution time equals the optimal `$m(T_F + T_B + T_W)$` with no idle time. The condition for zero bubble is that `$m$` must be large enough that the reordered `$W$` sequence can fill all gaps.

**Peak memory of ZB-H2:** `$(2p-1)M_B$`. The extended warm-up phase means the first stage holds `$2p-1$` forward-pass activations simultaneously at the moment just before its first `$B$` begins. This is roughly double 1F1B's `$p M_B$`, which is the cost of eliminating the initial bubble entirely.

**Quantitative comparison from Table 2:** Under the idealized assumption `$T_F = T_B = T_W = T$`:

- 1F1B bubble: `$(p-1)(3T)$` — three time units per stage gap because `$F$`, `$B$`, and `$W$` are all on the critical path.
- ZB-H1 bubble: `$(p-1)(2T - T) = (p-1)T$` — only `$F$` and `$B$` are on the critical path; `$W$` fills the tail.
- ZB-H2 bubble: `$(p-1)(2T - 2T) = 0$` — both `$F$` and `$B$` create gaps that `$W$` can completely fill when reordered.

The formulas in Table 2 use the general timing values `$T_F$`, `$T_B$`, `$T_W$` without assuming equality, which is how they appear in the paper.

#### Automatic Scheduling Algorithm

The handcrafted schedules assume `$T_F = T_B = T_W$` and `$T_{comm} = 0$`, which are unrealistic. On actual hardware, `$T_B > T_F > T_W$` (Table 9 shows e.g., for the 1.5B model on 8 GPUs: `$T_F = 18.522$`, `$T_B = 18.086$`, `$T_W = 9.337$`, `$T_{comm} = 0.601$` — all in milliseconds, and note that `$T_B$` and `$T_F$` are close but `$T_W$` is roughly half). These differences mean that the neat geometric filling of bubbles breaks — a `$W$` pass cannot perfectly fill a gap sized `$T_F$` if `$T_W < T_F$`, leaving residual micro-bubbles. The automatic scheduling algorithm addresses this by constructing schedules using **profiled timing** rather than assumptions, operating under a specified memory limit `$M_{limit}$`.

**Inputs:** The algorithm takes `$p$` (number of pipeline stages), `$m$` (number of microbatches), `$M_{limit}$` (activation memory budget), and profiled values `$T_F$`, `$T_B$`, `$T_W$`, `$T_{comm}$`.

**Output:** An ordered list of operations `$(stage, microbatch, operation)$` with assigned start times such that the makespan (time from first operation to last operation) is minimized.

**Two implementation strategies:** A heuristic algorithm (fast, near-optimal for large `$m$`) and an Integer Linear Programming formulation (provably optimal for smaller problem instances). These can be combined: use the heuristic solution as a warm-start initialization for the ILP solver.

##### Heuristic Algorithm (Section 3.1)

The heuristic constructs the schedule in four sequential phases, making greedy decisions with two binary hyperparameters controlling edge cases:

**Phase 1: Warm-up F packing.** Starting from stage 1 at time 0, the algorithm schedules forward passes for microbatches sequentially, respecting the dependency that `$F$` at stage `$i$` for microbatch `$j$` can begin only after `$F$` at stage `$i-1$` for microbatch `$j$` has completed and communicated its output (`$T_{comm}$` added). The algorithm packs as many forward passes as possible **within the memory limit** `$M_{limit}$`. Each forward pass increments the memory counter by `$M_B$`. The packing continues until either:

- The memory limit is reached (scheduling another `$F$` would exceed `$M_{limit}$`), or
- Scheduling another `$F$` would delay the subsequent `$B$` pass beyond what is necessary. This creates a small residual bubble (less than `$T_F$` in duration) just before the first `$B$` on the initial stage. A binary hyperparameter controls whether to schedule the extra `$F$` anyway (accepting the `$B$` delay and the memory cost to reduce the bubble further) or to leave the gap and start `$B$` sooner.

This phase mirrors the warm-up in 1F1B but with memory-aware packing rather than a fixed `$p - i + 1$` forward count per stage.

**Phase 2: Steady 1F-1B-1W execution.** After the warm-up, the algorithm enters a repeating pattern: schedule one `$F$`, then one `$B$`, then attempt to insert a `$W$`. The `$F$` and `$B$` maintain the dependency chain across stages. The `$W$` insertion logic is the core of the heuristic:

- **When there is a gap larger than `$T_W$`:** If the current stage would otherwise be idle for a duration exceeding `$T_W$` (because it must wait for the next `$F$` or `$B$` dependency to resolve), insert a `$W$` pass to fill this gap. The `$W$` must be for a microbatch whose `$B$` has already completed at this stage (a pending `$W$`).
- **When a bubble exists but is smaller than `$T_W$`:** The algorithm compares the current cumulative bubble size across all stages. If inserting a `$W$` (which would overflow the gap and push subsequent operations later) makes the **maximum cumulative bubble** across all stages smaller, the insertion is performed. This is a global optimization heuristic: it accepts a local timing penalty if it reduces the worst-case idle time elsewhere.
- **Memory-triggered `$W$` insertion:** If the memory limit `$M_{limit}$` is hit (too many in-flight activations), the algorithm schedules a `$W$` to free memory. Since `$W$` consumes `$\Delta M_{(i,j,W)} = -M_W$` (releases `$M_W$` of memory), scheduling a pending `$W$` reduces the memory pressure, allowing more `$F$` or `$B$` passes to proceed.

The paper notes that in typical runs, the steady state settles into a **1F-1B-1W pattern** — one forward, one input-gradient backward, one parameter-gradient backward — which is the natural generalization of 1F1B when `$W$` is decoupled.

**Phase 3: Forward-backward balance maintenance.** Throughout the schedule, the algorithm enforces an invariant: stage `$i$` must have scheduled at least one more `$F$` pass than stage `$i+1$` at all times before `$F$` passes are exhausted. This is the same invariant that 1F1B maintains, ensuring that when `$B$` passes begin, the dependency chain is well-formed and no stage is starved of work. When the difference exceeds one (stage `$i$` has two or more extra `$F$` passes compared to stage `$i+1$`), a second binary hyperparameter decides whether to skip one `$F$` in stage `$i$` (letting stage `$i+1$` catch up) if doing so does not increase total bubbles. This hyperparameter trades off load balance against memory — letting the difference grow beyond one increases peak memory on the earlier stages but may fill gaps that would otherwise be idle time.

**Phase 4: Tail W scheduling.** When all `$F$` and `$B$` passes are exhausted, each stage has a set of pending `$W$` passes (one per microbatch whose `$B$` has completed). These are scheduled sequentially, one after another, on each stage independently. Since there are no cross-stage dependencies for `$W$`, each stage processes its backlog as fast as possible. The ordering of `$W$` passes within each stage follows the natural order (earlier microbatches first) unless a reordering would better fill residual gaps — the heuristic does not explicitly reorder `$W$` passes the way ZB-H2 does, but the ILP formulation can discover optimal reorderings.

**Hyperparameter grid search:** The two binary hyperparameters (schedule-extra-F-during-warmup, skip-F-for-balance) are searched over a grid; the combination producing the smallest makespan is selected. The paper notes this search is cheap because there are only four combinations.

**Why the heuristic works well for large `$m$`:** As the number of microbatches grows, the steady-state 1F-1B-1W phase dominates the total execution time, and the warm-up and tail phases become proportionally negligible. The greedy decisions in the steady state (fill gaps with `$W$`, maintain the `$F$` balance invariant) are locally optimal, and the global effect of these local decisions averages out over many microbatches. The heuristic may miss optimal reorderings of `$W$` at the tail, but for large `$m$`, the tail is a small fraction of total time.

##### Integer Linear Programming Formulation (Appendix G)

The ILP formulation provides a provably optimal schedule for a given `$p$`, `$m$`, `$M_{limit}$`, and timing profile. It encodes the scheduling problem as a set of linear constraints and a linear objective, solvable by an off-the-shelf ILP solver (CBC, Forrest & Lougee-Heimer, 2005).

**Variables:** The formulation uses three types of decision variables:

- `$T_{(i,j,c)}$`: the execution time (duration) of operation `$c \in \{F, B, W\}$` for microbatch `$j \in \{1, ..., m\}$` on stage `$i \in \{1, ..., p\}$`. These are constants derived from profiling, not variables to be optimized — they represent the known cost of each operation on the hardware.
- `$E_{(i,j,c)}$`: the **ending time** of operation `$(i, j, c)$`. These are the continuous variables the solver optimizes over — the schedule is defined by assigning an end time to every operation.
- `$O_{(i,j,c) \rightarrow (i,j',c')} \in \{0, 1\}$`: a binary ordering variable indicating whether operation `$(i, j, c)$` is scheduled **before** operation `$(i, j', c')$` on the same stage `$i$`. These binary variables encode the sequencing of operations that share device `$i$`.

**Optimization objective (Equation 3):**

> $$\min_{O, E} \max_i \left(E_{(i,m,W)} - E_{(i,1,F)} + T_{(i,1,F)}\right)$$

where `$E_{(i,m,W)}$` is the end time of the last `$W$` pass on stage `$i$` (the final operation), `$E_{(i,1,F)}$` is the end time of the first forward pass on stage `$i$`, and `$T_{(i,1,F)}$` is the duration of that first forward pass.

**What it computes:** The makespan — the total elapsed time from the start of the first operation to the completion of the last operation — for the slowest stage. The expression `$E_{(i,m,W)} - E_{(i,1,F)} + T_{(i,1,F)}$` computes the total active time on stage `$i$` (time from the start of its first operation to the end of its last operation). The `$\max_i$` identifies the bottleneck stage. The minimization drives the solver to find an ordering `$O$` and timing `$E$` that balances load across stages.

**Why this form:** Pipeline efficiency is determined by the slowest stage — all other stages must wait for it at synchronization points (or, in the zero-bubble case, the schedule length equals the slowest stage's execution time). Minimizing the maximum stage time directly maximizes throughput, which is `$m / \text{makespan}$` (microbatches per unit time). An alternative objective like total idle time across all stages would not correctly capture this bottleneck effect.

**Dependency constraints (Equations 4–6):** Three types of sequencing constraints enforce the required execution order.

**Cross-stage forward dependency (Equation 4):**

> $$E_{(i,j,F)} \geq E_{(i-1,j,F)} + T_{comm} + T_{(i,j,F)}$$

where `$E_{(i-1,j,F)}$` is the end time of the forward pass for the same microbatch `$j$` on the previous stage `$i-1$`, `$T_{comm}$` is the communication time to transfer activations, and `$T_{(i,j,F)}$` is the duration of the forward pass on stage `$i$`.

**What it enforces:** The forward pass on stage `$i$` cannot finish until the forward pass on stage `$i-1$` has finished, the output has been communicated, and the computation on stage `$i$` itself has executed. This encodes the sequential data dependency: stage `$i$` needs the output of stage `$i-1$` as its input.

**Cross-stage backward dependency (Equation 5):**

> $$E_{(i,j,B)} \geq E_{(i+1,j,B)} + T_{comm} + T_{(i,j,B)}$$

where `$E_{(i+1,j,B)}$` is the end time of the input-gradient backward for the same microbatch `$j$` on the next stage `$i+1$`.

**What it enforces:** The input gradient `$B$` flows backward through the pipeline. Stage `$i$` cannot finish computing `$B$` until stage `$i+1$` has finished its `$B$` (producing the gradient with respect to stage `$i+1$`'s input, which is stage `$i$`'s output) and communicated it upstream. This is the key sequential bottleneck that creates bubbles — stage `$i$` must wait for the entire downstream chain to complete `$B$` before it can proceed.

Note that **there is no cross-stage constraint for `$W$`** — this is the formal encoding of `$W$`'s local nature. The solver is free to place `$W$` anywhere after its corresponding `$B$` on the same stage, without waiting for neighboring stages.

**Same-stage ordering constraint (Equation 6):**

> $$E_{(i,j,c)} \geq E_{(i,j',c')} + T_{(i,j,c)} - O_{(i,j,c) \rightarrow (i,j',c')} \cdot \infty$$

where the term `$O \cdot \infty$` makes this constraint vacuous (always satisfied) when the ordering variable is 0, and active when the ordering variable is 1.

**What it enforces:** If operation `$(i,j',c')$` is scheduled before operation `$(i,j,c)$` on the same stage (indicated by `$O = 1$`), then `$(i,j,c)$` cannot end before `$(i,j',c')$` has ended and `$(i,j,c)$`'s own execution time has elapsed. This is the standard disjunctive constraint for sequencing on a single resource (the device). The `$\infty$` (actually a large constant, the "big-M" method) ensures the constraint imposes no restriction when `$O = 0$`.

**Memory constraint (Equation 7):**

> $$M_{limit} \geq \Delta M_{(i,j',c')} + \sum_{j,c} \Delta M_{(i,j,c)} \cdot O_{(i,j,c) \rightarrow (i,j',c')}$$

where `$\Delta M_{(i,j,c)}$` is the memory change caused by operation `$(i,j,c)$`:
- `$\Delta M_{(\cdot,\cdot,F)} = +M_B$` (forward pass stores activations for backward)
- `$\Delta M_{(\cdot,\cdot,B)} = M_W - M_B$` (B releases `$M_B$` of activations needed only for `$B$`, but allocates `$M_W$` for `$W$`'s needs)
- `$\Delta M_{(\cdot,\cdot,W)} = -M_W$` (W releases its activations)

**What it computes:** At the moment just before operation `$(i,j',c')$` begins execution, the total activation memory in use equals the memory change from `$(i,j',c')$` itself plus the cumulative memory changes from all operations `$(i,j,c)$` that have been scheduled **before** `$(i,j',c')$` (indicated by `$O = 1$`). This total must not exceed `$M_{limit}$`.

**Why this form:** The sum over `$O_{(i,j,c) \rightarrow (i,j',c')}$` accumulates the net memory effect of all previously scheduled operations. The inequality enforces that the peak memory at any scheduling point is bounded. This is a linear encoding of memory-limit constraints, which are conceptually non-linear (memory depends on the set of active operations, not just pairwise orderings), but the big-M formulation linearizes it at the cost of introducing many binary variables.

**Why ILP rather than heuristics alone:** The ILP can discover non-obvious reorderings of `$W$` passes (like ZB-H2's parallelogram transformation) that the greedy heuristic might miss. It also correctly handles the interaction between timing heterogeneity (`$T_F \neq T_B \neq T_W$`) and memory constraints — for instance, deciding whether to delay a `$W$` to fill a later, larger gap versus executing it immediately to free memory. However, ILP solves an NP-hard problem and does not scale to large `$m$` and `$p$`, which is why the paper uses it for smaller configurations and the heuristic for larger ones, or combines them (heuristic initialization followed by ILP refinement).

#### Optimizer Synchronization Bypass (Section 4)

In standard synchronous pipeline parallelism, an all-reduce communication is performed across all pipeline stages at the end of each iteration (after all backward passes complete and before the optimizer step). This all-reduce aggregates global information needed for:

1. **Gradient norm clipping** (Pascanu et al., 2013): the global `$\ell_2$` norm of the concatenated gradient vector across all stages must be computed. If it exceeds a clipping threshold `$C$`, all gradients are scaled by `$C / \lVert g \rVert_2$`.
2. **Mixed-precision NaN/Inf checking** (Micikevicius et al., 2017): if any gradient value on any stage is NaN or Inf, the optimizer step must be skipped for the entire model to prevent corrupted parameters.

Both require a collective communication: each stage locally computes the sum of squared gradient norms (or the logical OR of NaN/Inf flags) and then an all-reduce across all stages combines these partial results into a global value. The optimizer step on each stage then uses this global value.

**Why this breaks zero-bubble schedules:** The all-reduce is a **synchronization barrier** — no stage can proceed to the optimizer step until all stages have completed their backward passes and participated in the collective. In a zero-bubble schedule like ZB-H2 (Figure 3, bottom), the stages finish their backward passes at different times (the parallelogram shape means the first stage finishes its `$W$` passes much earlier than the last stage finishes its `$B$` passes). The all-reduce forces all stages to wait for the slowest one, creating a synchronization-induced bubble that "destroys the parallelogram." Even if the computation schedule itself has zero bubbles, the communication barrier reintroduces idle time.

**The post-validation strategy (Figure 4):** The paper proposes replacing the pre-step all-reduce with a **forward-reduce-then-backward-validate** protocol that removes the barrier from the critical path while still ensuring that no invalid update is permanently applied.

The protocol operates in two phases across consecutive iterations:

**Phase 1 (current iteration, before optimizer step): Partial forward reduction.**

1. Stage 1 computes its local contribution to the global state — specifically, the local sum of squared gradient norms `$g_1^2$` and a local NaN/Inf flag `$nan_1 \in \{0, 1\}$`.
2. Stage 1 passes its partially reduced state `$(g_1^2, nan_1)$` to stage 2.
3. Stage 2 receives the partial state, adds its own local contribution: `$g_{1,2}^2 = g_1^2 + g_2^2$`, `$nan_{1,2} = nan_1 \lor nan_2$`, and passes the updated partial state to stage 3.
4. This forward propagation continues through stage `$p$`, which produces the partially reduced state for stages 1 through `$p$`.

**Crucially, this forward propagation is pipelined with computation, not a separate synchronization step.** Each stage passes the partial state to the next stage as soon as its local backward computation is complete, without waiting for all stages to finish. Stage `$i+1$` can receive the partial state from stage `$i$` while stage `$i+2$` is still computing backward passes — the communication is point-to-point, not a collective.

**Phase 1 (current iteration, optimizer step based on partial state):**

Each stage applies the optimizer update **based on the partially reduced state it received**, not the fully reduced global state. For gradient clipping: if the partially reduced norm `$g_{1:i}^2$` already exceeds the clipping threshold `$C^2$`, the stage clips its local gradients using this partial norm (which is a lower bound on the true global norm). For NaN/Inf checking: if the partially reduced NaN flag `$nan_{1:i}$` is already 1, the stage skips the optimizer step.

**Phase 2 (next iteration, during the warm-up phase): Backward validation and rollback.**

1. Stage `$p$`, having received the fully reduced state (all stages' contributions) at the end of Phase 1, now propagates this **fully reduced global state backward** through the pipeline during the warm-up phase of the **next** iteration. The backward propagation goes from stage `$p$` to stage `$p-1$`, then to stage `$p-2$`, and so on, back to stage 1.
2. Upon receiving the fully reduced global state, each stage validates whether its previous optimizer step was correct:
   - **Gradient clipping check:** If the fully reduced global norm would have resulted in a different scaling factor than the partially reduced norm used (i.e., the clipping threshold was exceeded based on the global norm but not based on the partial norm, or vice versa), the previous optimizer step applied an incorrect gradient scaling.
   - **NaN/Inf check:** If the fully reduced NaN flag is 1 but the partially reduced flag used was 0, the previous optimizer step should have been skipped but was not.
3. If the validation fails, the stage issues a **rollback** of its optimizer step (reverting parameters and optimizer states to their pre-update values) and then **re-applies** the optimizer step using the correct, fully reduced global state.

**Why this preserves synchronous semantics:** At the end of Phase 2, all stages that performed an incorrect update have corrected it. The model parameters after the correction are identical to what they would have been if a standard synchronous all-reduce had been performed before the optimizer step. The key insight is that **mistakes are rare**: NaN/Inf values "rarely trigger because in a robust setting most iterations shouldn't have numerical issues," and "the gradient clipping rate is also quite low empirically." The rollback mechanism is invoked only occasionally, and its cost (extra computation on a subset of iterations) is far smaller than the cost of an all-reduce barrier on every iteration.

**In-place optimizer rollback (Algorithm 1):** The paper implements rollback without storing historic parameter copies, using the fact that the AdamW optimizer update is **arithmetically reversible**.

The standard AdamW step (Algorithm 1, function STEP):

> $$t = t + 1$$
> $$m = \beta_1 m + (1 - \beta_1)g$$
> $$v = \beta_2 v + (1 - \beta_2)g^2$$
> $$m' = m / (1 - \beta_1^t)$$
> $$v' = v / (1 - \beta_2^t)$$
> $$\theta = \theta - \gamma \lambda \theta - \gamma m' / (\sqrt{v'} + \epsilon)$$

where `$t$` is the time step counter, `$\beta_1, \beta_2$` are momentum decay rates, `$m, v$` are the first and second moment estimates, `$g$` is the gradient, `$\gamma$` is the learning rate, `$\lambda$` is the weight decay coefficient, `$\epsilon$` is a small constant for numerical stability, and `$\theta$` are the model parameters.

**What it computes:** The standard AdamW update — decoupled weight decay followed by adaptive moment-based gradient scaling. The operations are all mathematically invertible (addition/subtraction, multiplication/division) because the intermediate values `$m'$`, `$v'$`, `$t$`, `$m$`, `$v$`, and `$g$` are all retained.

The rollback function (Algorithm 1, function ROLLBACK) reverses each operation in reverse order:

> Compute `$m'$` and `$v'$` from the current (post-update) state:
> $$m' = m / (1 - \beta_1^t); \quad v' = v / (1 - \beta_2^t)$$
> Undo the parameter update:
> $$\theta = (\theta + \gamma m' / (\sqrt{v'} + \epsilon)) / (1 - \gamma \lambda)$$
> Undo the moment updates:
> $$m = (m - (1 - \beta_1)g) / \beta_1$$
> $$v = (v - (1 - \beta_2)g^2) / \beta_2$$
> Decrement the time step:
> $$t = t - 1$$

**Why this form:** The operations are exact algebraic inverses — no information is lost. This avoids the memory overhead of maintaining a separate "backup" copy of parameters and optimizer states (which for large models would double the optimizer state memory). The rollback computation is only needed when the validation fails, which is rare, so the computational cost is negligible. The paper notes this technique works for "most optimizers" whose step function is arithmetically reversible, but formally proves it only for AdamW.

**Why the post-validation strategy matters for zero bubble:** Removing the all-reduce barrier from the end of each iteration allows the parallelogram schedule (Figure 3, ZB-H2; Figure 6) to exist. The partial forward reduction is point-to-point communication that can be overlapped with the tail `$W$` computations (the paper mentions overlapping all-reduce for data parallelism in Appendix A, but note that this is distinct — the optimizer synchronization discussed here is the cross-pipeline-stage synchronization, not the data-parallel all-reduce). The validation backward propagation occurs during the next iteration's warm-up phase, which in a zero-bubble schedule consists of forward passes — the backward state propagation travels in the opposite direction to the forward compute, so it does not contend for the same communication links (forward activations go stage `$i \rightarrow i+1$`, validation state goes stage `$i+1 \rightarrow i$`).

#### ZB-V: Memory-Efficient Zero-Bubble Schedule (Section 6)

ZB-H2 achieves zero bubble but at the cost of `$(2p-1)M_B$` peak memory — roughly double 1F1B's `$p M_B$`. For memory-constrained deployments where doubling the activation memory is infeasible, the paper proposes **ZB-V**, a scheduling approach that achieves near-zero bubble with peak memory matching 1F1B (`$p M_B$`) by using a novel model chunk assignment pattern.

**V-shaped model partitioning:** Unlike standard interleaved 1F1B (Narayanan et al., 2021) which assigns chunks to devices in a cyclic round-robin (device 1 gets chunks 1, `$p+1$`, `$2p+1$`, ..., device 2 gets chunks 2, `$p+2$`, ...), ZB-V assigns exactly **two chunks per device** in a symmetric "V" pattern:

- Device 1: chunk 1 (layers `$1$`–`$k$`) and chunk `$2p$` (layers `$L-k+1$`–`$L$`)
- Device 2: chunk 2 (layers `$k+1$`–`$2k$`) and chunk `$2p-1$` (layers `$L-2k+1$`–`$L-k$`)
- ...
- Device `$p$`: chunk `$p$` and chunk `$p+1$` (the middle chunks)

For a 16-layer model with 4 devices, the assignment is: device 1 gets layers 1–2 and 15–16; device 2 gets layers 3–4 and 13–14; device 3 gets layers 5–6 and 11–12; device 4 gets layers 7–8 and 9–10. This creates a V-shaped dependency path for the first microbatch: forward goes `$1 \rightarrow 2 \rightarrow 3 \rightarrow 4$` (for the first chunk) and then `$4 \rightarrow 3 \rightarrow 2 \rightarrow 1$` (for the second chunk, since device 4's second chunk is layers 9–10 and device 3's is layers 11–12, the forward pass must travel back "up" the pipeline). The backward pass for the same microbatch follows the reverse: from device 1 (where the forward ended) back through devices 2, 3, 4 for the second chunk, then 4, 3, 2, 1 for the first chunk.

**Why the V-shape enables lower peak memory:** In standard interleaved 1F1B, the forward pass of a microbatch starts on device 1 and propagates to device `$p$`, while the backward pass starts on device `$p$` and propagates back to device 1. This means device 1 initiates the forward but is the last to finish the backward — it must keep activations in memory for the entire round-trip time. In ZB-V, both the forward and backward pass for each microbatch **originate from and return to the same device** (e.g., for the first microbatch, forward starts on device 1 and backward ends on device 1). This symmetry means:

1. **Faster memory clearance:** Device 1 can initiate the backward pass for earlier microbatches promptly because it does not need to wait for the backward chain to propagate all the way from device `$p$`. The backward pass for the first chunk begins as soon as the forward pass for the second chunk has reached device `$p$` and the necessary backward dependencies are resolved — which happens sooner than in standard interleaving.

2. **Balanced peak memory:** The peak memory is inherently balanced across all devices because each device processes exactly two chunks, and the symmetric routing ensures that the number of simultaneously in-flight activations is uniform. The paper states: "the peak memory usage is inherently balanced across all workers. This equilibrium arises due to uniform computation workloads and consistent memory consumption across all model chunks."

**Three-phase execution (Figure 8):** The ZB-V schedule unfolds in three distinct phases:

**Phase 1: Warm-up phase.** Device `$i$` performs a total of `$2p - 1$` forward passes in the warm-up: `$2p - i$` forwards for the first chunk and `$i - 1$` forwards for the second chunk. This is analogous to the 1F1B warm-up but with two chunks per device — the forward count per chunk follows the same `$p - i + 1$` pattern as 1F1B, applied separately to each chunk. The interleaving of the two chunks' operations follows the V-shaped dependency: forward passes for the first chunk propagate from device 1 to device `$p$`, then forward passes for the second chunk propagate back from device `$p$` to device 1.

**Phase 2: Steady state 1F-1B-1W.** After the warm-up, all devices enter a repetitive 1F-1B-1W pattern. The operations are grouped by chunk: device `$i$` executes `$p - i$` groups of (F, B, W) for the second chunk, then alternates between one group for the second chunk and one group for the first chunk until all forward passes are exhausted. The pattern is: second-chunk group, first-chunk group, second-chunk group, first-chunk group, and so on. This alternation maintains the V-shaped dependency chain while keeping the workload balanced.

**Phase 3: Tail B and W scheduling.** When forward passes are exhausted, each device processes the remaining `$B$` passes first (prioritized because they are on the critical dependency path for other stages), then fills remaining idle time with `$W$` passes. The `$W$` placement follows the same heuristic as the main algorithm: insert `$W$` to fill gaps larger than `$T_W$`, and use memory-triggered `$W$` insertion to stay within the memory limit.

**Why ZB-V achieves near-zero bubble with `$p M_B$` memory:** Under the idealized condition `$T_F = T_B = T_W$`, ZB-V achieves exactly zero bubble with peak memory `$p M_B$` — matching 1F1B's memory while achieving ZB-H2's throughput. The key is the reordering of `$W$` at the tail. Because the memory distribution is balanced across all workers during the warm-up and steady phases, the heuristic algorithm can "straightforwardly shift all `$W$` to the right, within the memory constraint" (Section 6). This shifting fills the triangular bubble at the tail, transforming the trapezoid into a parallelogram, just as ZB-H2 does. The difference is that ZB-V needs only `$p M_B$` memory because the V-shaped routing means fewer activations are simultaneously in flight compared to ZB-H2's extended warm-up.

The paper quantifies this in Table 8: ZB-V's bubble rate is consistently lower than 1F1B, 1F1B-I, and ZB-H1, and is comparable to ZB-H2 — but ZB-V uses half the peak memory of ZB-H2 (since ZB-H2 uses `$(2p-1)M_B$` while ZB-V uses `$p M_B$`). For example, for the 14.6B model with `$p=24$`, `$m=96$`: ZB-V bubble rate is 0.0483, ZB-H2 bubble rate is 0.0480, but ZB-H2 requires roughly `$(2 \times 24 - 1) = 47$` units of `$M_B$` while ZB-V requires only `$24$` units of `$M_B$`.

**Automatic scheduling for ZB-V:** The same heuristic algorithm from Section 3.1 is used to automatically generate ZB-V schedules, taking the V-shaped model partitioning as a given and optimizing the operation ordering within each device's two-chunk workload. The memory-limit `$M_{limit}$` constraint guides the shifting of `$W$` passes in the tail: `$W$` can be shifted rightward (delayed) as long as the cumulative memory at any point does not exceed `$M_{limit}$`. The paper presents this as a straightforward extension — the core scheduling logic (fill gaps with `$W$`, maintain the `$F$`-balance invariant, bound memory) applies identically; only the chunk assignment and the dependency graph (now with V-shaped routing) change.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Reframing Pipeline Bubbles as a Scheduling Granularity Problem, Not a Structural Inevitability

The paper's most fundamental intellectual move is to diagnose *why* pipeline bubbles have persisted as an unsolved problem in synchronous pipeline parallelism: the field has been operating at the wrong computational granularity. Prior work — including GPipe (Huang et al., 2019), 1F1B (Narayanan et al., 2021), and interleaved 1F1B (Narayanan et al., 2021) — all accepted the monolithic backward pass as the atomic unit of scheduling. Under that granularity, bubbles appear structurally inevitable because the sequential dependency chain from `$F_1 \rightarrow F_2 \rightarrow \dots \rightarrow B_p \rightarrow B_{p-1} \rightarrow \dots \rightarrow B_1$` forces idle time at both the warm-up and cool-down boundaries of the schedule. The geometry of the trapezoid — with its triangular empty spaces at the top-left and bottom-right — follows directly from the fact that every operation on every stage must wait for the same long chain of dependencies, and no amount of clever ordering of atomic backward passes can fill those triangles.

The conceptual break is the recognition that the backward pass is *not* atomic with respect to cross-stage dependencies. It contains two sub-computations: **B** (`$\nabla_x$`), which participates in the sequential chain, and **W** (`$\nabla_W$`), which does not. This observation transforms the problem from "how do we minimize bubbles given an inevitable sequential bottleneck" to "how do we exploit the fact that a substantial fraction of the backward computation is locally schedulable to fill what would otherwise be idle time." The difference is not incremental — it changes the nature of the scheduling problem from one where bubbles are a geometric fact to one where bubbles are a consequence of representing the computation graph at too coarse a resolution.

This reframing parallels a broader pattern in compiler design, which the paper alludes to in Section 1 ("modern deep learning compilers use different intermediate representations for optimizations at various levels"). Just as compilers lower high-level operations into finer-grained IR to expose instruction-level parallelism, the paper lowers the backward pass into B and W to expose scheduling parallelism that was previously hidden. The analogy is not just decorative — it captures the essence of the contribution: what appears as a fundamental constraint at one level of abstraction dissolves at a finer granularity.

The significance extends beyond pipeline parallelism. The paper implicitly argues that the granularity at which the computation graph is exposed to the scheduler is a first-class design choice, not a fixed property of neural network training. This insight suggests a research direction: what other monolithic operations in distributed training (e.g., the optimizer step, the all-reduce in data parallelism) contain hidden decomposability that could enable better scheduling? The post-validation optimizer step (Section 4, discussed below as Innovation 3) is itself an instance of this pattern — it decomposes the synchronize-then-update into partial-reduce-then-validate-then-possibly-rollback, trading rare extra work for eliminated synchronization overhead.

**Evidence:** The bubble-rate comparisons in Table 5 quantify the magnitude of this reframing's impact. For the 1.5B model with `$p=8$`, `$m=32$`, the bubble rate drops from 0.1985 (1F1B) to 0.0039 (ZB-2p) — a 50× reduction. For the 28.3B model with `$p=32$`, `$m=256$`, the reduction is from 0.1352 to 0.0018. These are not marginal improvements from better tuning; they represent a qualitative shift from "bubbles are a dominant fraction of execution" to "bubbles are nearly eliminated."

### Innovation 2: Difficulty-Conditioned Memory-Bubble Pareto Frontier via Automatic Scheduling

The handcrafted schedules ZB-H1 and ZB-H2 (Section 2) would have been a respectable contribution on their own — they demonstrate that B/W splitting enables both a memory-neutral schedule with 3× smaller bubbles than 1F1B (ZB-H1) and a zero-bubble schedule at the cost of doubled peak memory (ZB-H2). But the paper goes further by recognizing that these two schedules are merely points on a continuous **memory-bubble Pareto frontier**, and that the optimal operating point depends on hardware constraints (`$T_F, T_B, T_W, T_{comm}$`) and available memory (`$M_{limit}$`). The handcrafted schedules assume `$T_F = T_B = T_W$` and `$T_{comm} = 0$`, which makes them unsuitable for direct deployment — real hardware has `$T_B > T_F > T_W$` with non-negligible communication time, breaking the neat geometric filling that ZB-H2 relies on.

The automatic scheduling algorithm (Section 3) is the mechanism that turns the conceptual insight of B/W splitting into a deployable system. But the innovation is not the algorithm itself (heuristics + ILP is a standard approach to scheduling problems). The innovation is the **framing of pipeline scheduling as an optimization problem over a Pareto frontier parameterized by memory budget**, where the scheduler can smoothly trade bubble reduction for memory consumption by choosing how many extra forward passes to pack into the warm-up phase and how aggressively to reorder W at the tail.

This is a significant departure from prior work, which treated the schedule as fixed given `$p$` and `$m$`. 1F1B, GPipe, and interleaved 1F1B each define a single scheduling rule that produces exactly one schedule shape for a given configuration. There is no "knob" to turn that trades memory for bubbles — the tradeoff is binary: either you use a memory-efficient schedule (1F1B) or a bubble-reducing schedule (interleaved 1F1B), with no intermediate points. The automatic scheduling algorithm, in contrast, produces a continuous spectrum of schedules from `$M_{limit} \approx p M_B$` (ZB-1p, matching 1F1B's memory) to `$M_{limit} \approx 2p M_B$` (ZB-2p, near-zero bubble) and beyond. Figure 7 visualizes this explicitly: the bubble rate decreases roughly linearly as `$M_{limit}$` increases, plateauing around `$2p M_B$` where near-zero bubble is achieved.

This framing matters practically because it decouples two concerns that were previously conflated: the *scheduling strategy* (how to order operations) and the *memory budget* (how much activation memory to allocate). A practitioner can now specify their memory constraint and receive the best possible schedule under that constraint, rather than choosing between two fixed strategies with poor intermediate behavior. The paper's demonstration that ZB-1p (memory-matched to 1F1B) outperforms 1F1B by up to 23% in throughput (Figure 5, Table 4) without any memory penalty validates that the scheduling strategy itself — independent of memory-trading — provides substantial gains.

**Evidence:** Figure 7 plots the bubble rate against `$M_{limit}$` across four model sizes and three microbatch counts each. The curves are consistently downward-sloping, with the inflection point around `$2p M_B$`. Table 4 shows that ZB-1p achieves 12.9–14.2 samples/GPU/second (1.5B, 24–64 microbatches) versus 11.8–13.6 for 1F1B, while using comparable memory (32 GB vs 30 GB) — a pure scheduling gain without memory tradeoff.

### Innovation 3: Eliminating Optimizer Synchronization as a Hidden Barrier to Zero-Bubble Scheduling

A non-obvious discovery in the paper is that the optimizer synchronization — the all-reduce for gradient norm clipping and NaN/Inf checking that standard implementations perform at the end of each iteration — is a **structural barrier to zero-bubble scheduling** that is conceptually distinct from the pipeline dependency bottleneck. Even if the computation schedule itself has zero idle time (as in ZB-H2 with `$T_F = T_B = T_W$`), the all-reduce forces all stages to wait for the slowest one, creating a synchronization-induced bubble that "destroys the parallelogram" (Section 4). Prior work did not identify this as a problem because prior schedules did not approach zero bubble closely enough for this barrier to be the limiting factor — when the computation bubble is 10–25% of iteration time, the synchronization bubble is a second-order effect. It becomes first-order only when computation bubbles are nearly eliminated.

The innovation here is both diagnostic and prescriptive. Diagnostically, the paper identifies that synchronous training semantics — specifically, the need for a globally consistent gradient clipping factor and NaN/Inf flag — need not imply a synchronization barrier on the critical path. Synchronous semantics constrain the *state* of the model after training, not the *timing* of the communication that establishes that state. This is a subtle but powerful distinction: correctness requires that the optimizer uses the global gradient norm, but it does not require that the global norm be computed before the optimizer step begins. The computation can be deferred and the step corrected later if needed.

Prescriptively, the post-validation strategy (Section 4, Figure 4) operationalizes this distinction. It replaces the centralized all-reduce with a **two-phase pipelined protocol**: a forward partial reduction (pipelined with tail computation, so it's off the critical path) followed by a backward validation during the next iteration's warm-up (also off the critical path, since warm-up consists primarily of forward passes flowing in the opposite direction). The key gamble — and the reason this works — is that validation failures (NaN/Inf, gradient clipping) are rare in practice. The paper states that NaN/Inf checks "rarely trigger because in a robust setting most iterations shouldn't have numerical issues" and that "the gradient clipping rate is also quite low empirically." This means the rollback mechanism is invoked only occasionally, and its amortized cost is negligible. The paper thus trades a guaranteed synchronization cost on every iteration for a probabilistic rollback cost on a small fraction of iterations — a classic speculative execution tradeoff.

The in-place AdamW rollback (Algorithm 1) is elegant but secondary; the intellectual contribution is recognizing that **synchronous semantics and synchronized execution are separable concerns**. This insight generalizes beyond pipeline parallelism: any distributed training system where global consistency checks are performed at iteration boundaries could potentially adopt a post-validation approach, paying for consistency only when it's violated.

**Evidence:** Table 10 shows that the synchronized version of ZB-2p (with all-reduce) suffers an approximately 8% throughput drop compared to the post-validation version. For the 1.5B model, 24 microbatches: 14.5 samples/GPU/second drops to 13.11. This 8% gap represents the synchronization-induced bubble that the post-validation strategy eliminates — it is the difference between "near-zero computation bubble" and "truly zero total bubble."

### Innovation 4: The V-Shaped Interleaving Pattern as a Principle for Memory-Efficient Zero-Bubble Scheduling

ZB-2p achieves near-zero bubble but at roughly double the peak activation memory of 1F1B (`$(2p-1)M_B$` vs `$p M_B$`), which limits its applicability in memory-constrained settings. ZB-V (Section 6) addresses this by introducing a novel model chunk assignment pattern — the "V" shape — that achieves near-zero bubble with **the same peak memory as 1F1B**. This is not merely an incremental improvement in the memory-bubble tradeoff; it is a qualitatively different scheduling principle that changes the dependency structure of the pipeline.

The standard interleaved 1F1B schedule (Narayanan et al., 2021) assigns chunks to devices in a cyclic round-robin: device 1 gets chunks `$1, p+1, 2p+1, \dots$`; device 2 gets chunks `$2, p+2, 2p+2, \dots$`; etc. This creates a **unidirectional dependency chain**: the forward pass for a microbatch always propagates from device 1 to device `$p$`, and the backward pass always propagates from device `$p$` back to device 1. Device 1 therefore initiates the forward pass but is the last device to complete the backward pass, meaning it must hold activations in memory for the entire round-trip duration. This asymmetry is the root cause of high peak memory: the first device bears a disproportionately large memory burden.

ZB-V's V-shaped assignment breaks this asymmetry. By placing both the earliest and latest model chunks on device 1, the forward pass for a microbatch starts on device 1, propagates to device `$p$`, and then **reverses direction**, propagating back to device 1. The backward pass follows the same V-path in reverse: starting on device 1, propagating to device `$p$`, and returning to device 1. The consequence is that **each microbatch's forward and backward passes both begin and end on the same device**. This symmetry has two effects:

1. **Faster memory clearance:** Device 1 can begin the backward pass for early microbatches sooner because the backward chain does not need to propagate all the way from device `$p$` — it starts locally. This reduces the time that activations must be held, directly reducing peak memory.

2. **Balanced peak memory:** All devices have the same peak memory usage because the symmetric routing ensures that the number of simultaneously in-flight activations is uniform across devices. This is in contrast to standard interleaving, where device 1 has the highest peak and device `$p$` the lowest.

Under the idealized condition `$T_F = T_B = T_W$`, ZB-V achieves zero bubble with `$p M_B$` memory — matching 1F1B while achieving the throughput of ZB-H2. The paper notes (Table 8) that ZB-V's bubble rates are comparable to ZB-H2 (e.g., 0.0483 vs 0.0480 for the 14.6B model, `$p=24$`, `$m=96$`) while requiring only half the peak memory. This is a significant finding because it demonstrates that **the zero-bubble property is not intrinsically tied to high memory consumption**; it's a consequence of the dependency structure, which the V-shaped assignment fundamentally alters.

The V-shape principle has implications beyond this specific schedule. It suggests a design space for interleaving patterns where the dependency graph can be made bidirectional or even cyclic, potentially enabling tighter packing of operations within a fixed memory budget. The paper does not explore this design space systematically, but the principle is stated clearly enough to guide future work.

**Evidence:** Table 6 compares ZB-V against 1F1B, ZB-1p, and ZB-2p* (ZB-2p with halved microbatch size to match memory). ZB-V consistently outperforms 1F1B and ZB-1p while using comparable memory. For the 6.2B model on 16 GPUs: ZB-V achieves 4.15–4.35 samples/GPU/second vs 3.38–3.91 for 1F1B (a 10–22% improvement) at 64 GB vs 61 GB memory. The comparison with ZB-2p* is nuanced (Table 7 shows ZB-2p* can be modestly faster when microbatch size is already large enough that GPU utilization is saturated), but the key claim — that ZB-V achieves near-ZB-2p throughput at 1F1B-level memory — is supported.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The experiments do not use a traditional benchmark dataset; rather, they measure throughput and memory on GPT-3–style transformer models during training. Correctness is verified by "recording the loss after every iteration for ZB-1p, ZB-2p, and 1F1B, and then verifying that they're bit-to-bit identical" (Section 5.1), confirming that the scheduling changes do not alter optimization semantics. No train/test split or accuracy metric is involved — the evaluation is purely a systems performance measurement.

- **Base model(s).** Four GPT-3–analogous transformer models spanning 1.5B to 28.3B parameters, configured as shown in Table 3. The 1.5B model uses 22 layers, 24 attention heads, hidden size 2304, and sequence length 1024. The 6.2B model uses 30 layers, 32 heads, hidden size 4096. The 14.6B model uses 46 layers, 40 heads, hidden size 5120. The 28.3B model uses 62 layers, 48 heads, hidden size 6144. The choice spans a realistic range from moderate to large models to demonstrate scaling behavior. The initial and final pipeline stages "possess one fewer transformer layer compared to the intermediate stages... to compensate for the extra embedding lookup and loss computations" so they do not become bottlenecks.

- **Metrics.** The primary metric is **samples per GPU per second** — the number of training samples processed per second on each GPU, which directly measures training throughput. Memory consumption is reported in **GB** of total GPU memory used (Table 4, Table 6). Additionally, the paper introduces **bubble rate** for theoretical analysis (Section 5.3), defined as `(cost − m(T_F + T_B + T_W)) / cost`, where `cost` is the largest execution time across all stages and `m(T_F + T_B + T_W)` is the optimal time with no idle periods. Bubble rate is calculated using profiled `T_F`, `T_B`, `T_W`, and `T_{comm}` values, not from real execution traces, making it a planning metric rather than a measured one. The paper also reports an **upper bound** throughput, estimated by multiplying 1F1B's throughput by `1 / (1 − bubble rate of 1F1B)` — this represents the theoretical maximum if all bubbles were eliminated with no overhead.

- **Baselines.** Three baseline methods are compared:
  - **1F1B** (Harlap et al., 2018; Narayanan et al., 2021): The standard one-forward-one-backward synchronous pipeline schedule, implemented from Megatron-LM. This is the primary baseline and the de facto standard for synchronous PP.
  - **1F1B-I (Interleaved)** (Narayanan et al., 2021): The interleaved variant of 1F1B where the model is divided into the maximum number of chunks — each transformer layer serves as a separate chunk, and chunks are cyclically assigned to stages. The paper states this configuration uses "the maximum number of chunks to ensure least bubble."
  - **Upper bound:** A derived reference point (not a real schedule) computed as `1F1B_throughput / (1 − 1F1B_bubble_rate)`, representing the theoretical throughput if all pipeline bubbles were eliminated.

  The proposed methods evaluated are:
  - **ZB-1p:** Automatically searched schedule with activation memory limit set to `p M_B`, theoretically matching 1F1B's peak memory.
  - **ZB-2p:** Automatically searched schedule with activation memory limit set to `2p M_B`, which the paper identifies as "the least amount of memory to empirically achieve close to zero bubble."
  - **ZB-V** (Section 6): The V-shaped interleaved schedule, evaluated separately against 1F1B, ZB-1p, and ZB-2p* (ZB-2p with halved microbatch size to match memory).

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** Compute is measured in **number of microbatches** (`m`) and **microbatch size** (`b`), with the global batch size held constant for fair comparison (`m × b` is fixed). The pipeline stage count `p` varies with model size (8, 16, or 32 GPUs). The paper sweeps different `m` values per model configuration (Table 3): 24/32/64 for 1.5B and 6.2B on 8 GPUs; 48/64/128 for 14.6B on 16 GPUs; 96/128/256 for 28.3B on 32 GPUs. Microbatch size `b` is adjusted to fit within memory constraints: 6 for 1.5B, 3 for 6.2B, and smaller values (1–2) for larger models. The paper does not use FLOPs or wall-clock time as primary metrics; throughput in samples/second is the comparable unit. For the ZB-2p vs. 1F1B memory-matched comparison (Appendix F, Table 11), ZB-2p uses half the microbatch size and double the number of microbatches, keeping global batch size identical.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** No cross-validation or statistical significance testing is performed — this is standard for systems performance work where measurements are deterministic (given fixed hardware and software, the same schedule produces the same timing). The paper uses "several warm-up iterations" before recording timings, and verifies correctness via bit-to-bit identical loss values across methods using a fixed random seed, confirming that scheduling changes do not alter the optimization trajectory. This deterministic verification replaces the need for statistical replication.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Throughput Comparison Across Model Scales and Microbatch Counts

The headline result appears in Figure 5 and Table 4: **ZB-2p consistently achieves the highest throughput across all configurations**, with gains over 1F1B ranging from approximately 10% to 23% under similar memory constraints, extending to 31% when memory limits are relaxed (comparing ZB-2p against 1F1B at the same microbatch size, where ZB-2p uses approximately 2× the memory of 1F1B per Table 4).

For the 1.5B model on 8 GPUs (Table 4, top-left of Figure 5): ZB-2p achieves 14.5, 14.8, and 14.9 samples/GPU/second at 24, 32, and 64 microbatches respectively, versus 1F1B's 11.8, 12.5, and 13.6 — a 22.9%, 18.4%, and 9.6% improvement. ZB-1p achieves 12.9, 13.4, and 14.2 — improvements of 9.3%, 7.2%, and 4.4% over 1F1B — while using comparable memory (32 GB vs. 30 GB for 1F1B). 1F1B-I achieves intermediate performance: 13.1, 13.4, and 13.9, consistently below ZB-2p but above ZB-1p at low microbatch counts.

For the 6.2B model on 8 GPUs (Table 4, second panel of Figure 5): ZB-2p achieves 4.32, 4.35, 4.39 samples/GPU/second at 24, 32, and 64 microbatches, versus 1F1B's 3.50, 3.70, 4.03 — a 23.4%, 17.6%, and 8.9% improvement. ZB-1p achieves 3.88, 4.00, 4.20 (10.9%, 8.1%, 4.2% over 1F1B) at 42 GB versus 39 GB for 1F1B. 1F1B-I achieves 4.01, 4.08, 4.19 — notably, ZB-1p surpasses 1F1B-I at 64 microbatches (4.20 vs. 4.19).

For the 14.6B model on 16 GPUs (Table 4, third panel of Figure 5): This is a multi-node configuration where communication bandwidth becomes significant. ZB-2p achieves 1.81, 1.83, 1.85 samples/GPU/second at 48, 64, and 128 microbatches, versus 1F1B's 1.40, 1.49, 1.64 — improvements of 29.3%, 22.8%, and 12.8%. ZB-1p achieves 1.61, 1.67, 1.76 (15.0%, 12.1%, 7.3% over 1F1B) at 33 GB versus 32 GB for 1F1B. Critically, ZB-1p **outperforms 1F1B-I** (1.54, 1.59, 1.66) across all microbatch counts, with the gap widening at higher microbatch counts (1.76 vs. 1.66 at 128 microbatches). This is the paper's evidence that ZB methods have an advantage in communication-constrained multi-node settings: 1F1B-I's extra communication cost from finer-grained chunking becomes a bottleneck, while ZB-1p reduces bubbles without increasing communication.

For the 28.3B model on 32 GPUs (Table 4, bottom of Figure 5): The largest configuration tested. ZB-2p achieves 0.99, 1.00, 1.00 samples/GPU/second at 96, 128, and 256 microbatches, versus 1F1B's 0.76, 0.80, 0.88 — improvements of 30.3%, 25.0%, and 13.6%. ZB-1p achieves 0.87, 0.90, 0.96 (14.5%, 12.5%, 9.1% over 1F1B) at 44 GB versus 43 GB for 1F1B. 1F1B-I achieves 0.82, 0.85, 0.90 — again outperformed by ZB-1p, and substantially below ZB-2p. Memory for 1F1B-I reaches 58 GB, notably higher than ZB-1p's 44 GB.

Two consistent patterns emerge across all scales:

1. **ZB-2p's advantage over 1F1B diminishes as microbatch count increases.** At low microbatch counts (24 for 1.5B/6.2B, 48 for 14.6B, 96 for 28.3B), the improvement is 22.9%, 23.4%, 29.3%, 30.3% respectively — the largest gains. At high microbatch counts (64, 64, 128, 256), the improvement drops to 9.6%, 8.9%, 12.8%, 13.6%. This is because 1F1B's bubble fraction decreases as `m` grows (Table 5: 1F1B bubble rates drop from 0.2431 → 0.1240 for 1.5B as `m` goes from 24 → 64). When bubbles are a smaller fraction of total time, eliminating them provides proportionally less benefit. ZB-2p, in contrast, maintains near-constant efficiency regardless of microbatch count because its bubble rate is already near zero (Table 5: 0.0433 → 0.0026 for 1.5B).

2. **ZB-1p becomes competitive with 1F1B-I at high microbatch counts, and surpasses it in multi-node setups.** For the 1.5B model at 64 microbatches, ZB-1p (14.2) exceeds 1F1B-I (13.9). For the 14.6B and 28.3B models, ZB-1p outperforms 1F1B-I across all microbatch counts tested. The paper attributes this to ZB-1p avoiding the extra communication that 1F1B-I incurs from its finer chunk granularity.

#### Bubble Rate Analysis

Table 5 reports theoretical bubble rates for all methods across all configurations, computed using profiled `T_F`, `T_B`, `T_W`, and `T_{comm}` values from Table 9. These are not measured directly from execution traces but calculated from the scheduling algorithm's output — they represent what the schedule *would* achieve under perfect execution.

Key findings from Table 5:

- **ZB-2p achieves bubble rates below 1% in most configurations.** For the 6.2B model, bubble rates are 0.0029, 0.0022, 0.0010 at `m` = 24, 32, 64 respectively on 8 GPUs. For the 28.3B model on 32 GPUs: 0.0038, 0.0029, 0.0018. These are 50–100× smaller than 1F1B's bubble rates for the same configurations (e.g., 0.1352 vs. 0.0018 for 28.3B at `m=256`).

- **ZB-H2 (handcrafted zero-bubble) consistently underperforms ZB-2p (automatically searched).** For the 1.5B model at `m=24`: ZB-H2 bubble rate is 0.1083, ZB-2p is 0.0433 — a 2.5× difference. At `m=64`: 0.0444 vs. 0.0026 — a 17× difference. This validates the automatic scheduling algorithm's ability to adapt to realistic timing (`T_F ≠ T_B ≠ T_W`, non-zero `T_{comm}`) where handcrafted schedules leave bubbles unfilled. The gap is largest at higher microbatch counts because handcrafted schedules cannot exploit the additional scheduling flexibility that more microbatches provide (more opportunities to reorder W to fill gaps), while the automatic algorithm can.

- **ZB-H1 and ZB-1p produce identical bubble rates in most configurations** (e.g., 0.1585 for both at 1.5B, `m=24`). The paper hypothesizes this is because "the memory limit becomes the dominate factor" — when memory is constrained to `p M_B`, the handcrafted schedule is already near-optimal, and the automatic scheduler cannot find additional improvements within that tight memory budget.

- **ZB-1p bubble rates are substantially lower than 1F1B.** For the 28.3B model at `m=256`: ZB-1p bubble rate is 0.0594 vs. 1F1B's 0.1352 — a 56% reduction. For the 14.6B model at `m=128`: 0.0576 vs. 0.1251 — a 54% reduction. This is the source of ZB-1p's throughput improvement: roughly halving the idle time while using the same peak memory.

#### Visual Validation of Zero-Bubble Execution

Figure 6 provides direct visual evidence of a near-zero-bubble schedule. The top panel shows the automatically generated ZB-2p schedule for the 14.6B model on 16 GPUs with 128 microbatches, laid out as a Gantt chart with time on the x-axis and devices on the y-axis. The schedule shows a dense packing of operations with "almost no bubble" — the only visible gaps are small slivers (less than one operation width) between operations, consistent with the 0.0028 bubble rate reported in Table 5.

The bottom panel shows the **profiled real execution** of the same schedule. The authors note it "retains a good overall alignment" with the planned schedule but "has slightly more bubbles." These extra bubbles arise from runtime variations not captured by profiled averages (e.g., kernel launch overhead, memory contention, communication jitter). The paper does not quantify the difference between planned and actual bubble rates from Figure 6, but the visual evidence supports the claim that the schedule operates close to theoretically optimal throughput.

The fact that the profiled execution shows some residual bubbles despite a near-zero planned bubble rate is an honest acknowledgment of the gap between scheduling theory and runtime reality. This gap is not unique to ZB methods — it affects all pipeline schedules — but it means the throughput gains reported in Figure 5 (which reflect actual measured throughput) may be slightly lower than what the theoretical bubble rates in Table 5 would predict.

#### Memory Limit vs. Bubble Rate Trade-off

Figure 7 explores the relationship between the memory limit `M_limit` and the resulting bubble rate for the heuristic scheduling algorithm (not ZB-V — that comparison appears separately in Figure 9). The x-axis is `M_limit` scaled in units of `p M_B`, and the y-axis is the bubble rate.

The key observation: **the bubble rate decreases roughly linearly as `M_limit` increases from 1.0 `p M_B` to approximately 2.0 `p M_B`, then plateaus.** For the 1.5B model at `m=24`: the bubble rate drops from approximately 0.16 at `M_limit = 1.0 p M_B` to approximately 0.04 at `M_limit = 2.0 p M_B`, with minimal further improvement beyond 2.0. For the 28.3B model at `m=96`: the drop is from approximately 0.14 to approximately 0.004 over the same range.

The paper identifies `2p M_B` as the approximate inflection point where "close to zero bubble rate" is achieved. The theoretical explanation appears in Appendix B: the bubble preceding the first B on the initial stage can only be eliminated by scheduling additional forward passes, which requires storing their activations. The number of extra forwards needed scales with `p`, and the memory cost scales proportionally — hence `2p M_B` as the threshold for zero bubble.

The plateau beyond `2p M_B` indicates diminishing returns: additional memory beyond this point does not further reduce bubbles because the computational dependencies themselves become the bottleneck, not memory. The paper notes this explicitly: "although a sufficiently large memory limit does result in a theoretically zero bubble rate, in general the cost outweighs the gain."

#### ZB-V: Memory-Efficient Zero-Bubble Performance

Section 6 evaluates ZB-V against 1F1B, ZB-1p, and a memory-matched variant of ZB-2p (denoted ZB-2p*, which halves the microbatch size and doubles the number of microbatches to maintain the same global batch size while equalizing memory consumption). Table 6 reports results for the 6.2B, 14.6B, and 28.3B models under identical memory budgets.

For the 6.2B model on 16 GPUs (`b=6`, `m=48/64/128`): ZB-V achieves 4.15, 4.21, 4.35 samples/GPU/second, versus 1F1B's 3.38, 3.57, 3.91 — improvements of 22.8%, 17.9%, 11.3%. ZB-2p* achieves 4.36, 4.37, 4.45 — slightly higher than ZB-V (by 5.1%, 3.8%, 2.3% respectively) but at the cost of using half the microbatch size. The paper notes in Table 7 that doubling the microbatch size for the 6.2B model (from `b=3` to `b=6`) improves throughput by only 1.94–2.59% across methods, explaining why ZB-2p*'s smaller microbatch is not a significant disadvantage here.

For the 14.6B model on 24 GPUs (`b=2`, `m=72/96/192`): ZB-V achieves 1.85, 1.88, 1.93 samples/GPU/second, versus 1F1B's 1.52, 1.61, 1.76 — improvements of 21.7%, 16.8%, 9.7%. ZB-2p* achieves 1.84, 1.84, 1.85 — ZB-V is actually slightly faster at higher microbatch counts (1.93 vs. 1.85 at `m=192`, a 4.3% advantage). Table 7 shows that doubling microbatch size for the 14.6B model improves throughput by 7.43–9.52%, which means ZB-V's ability to use larger microbatches (since it doesn't need to halve them to match memory) provides a material advantage over ZB-2p*.

For the 28.3B model on 32 GPUs (`b=2`, `m=96/128/256`): ZB-V achieves 1.01, 1.02, 1.06 samples/GPU/second, versus 1F1B's 0.82, 0.87, 0.95 — improvements of 23.2%, 17.2%, 11.6%. ZB-2p* achieves 1.00, 1.00, 1.01 — ZB-V is faster at all microbatch counts, with the gap widening to 5.0% at `m=256` (1.06 vs. 1.01). Table 7 confirms that doubling microbatch size improves throughput by 6.32–8.75% for this model size, making ZB-V's larger microbatch advantage substantial.

The paper concludes from these results that "there exists a trade-off between a larger microbatch size and a reduced bubble rate. When the benefit of a smaller bubble rate outweighs that of a larger microbatch size, sacrificing the latter may be a strategic choice." Concretely: ZB-V achieves near-ZB-2p bubble rates while retaining larger microbatches, and when GPU utilization is sensitive to microbatch size (as in the larger models), ZB-V can outperform even ZB-2p* at equal memory.

Table 8 reports theoretical bubble rates for ZB-V compared to 1F1B, 1F1B-I, ZB-H1, and ZB-H2, using profiled timing values from the ZB-V experiments (different hardware configurations than the main experiments, so these values differ from Table 5). ZB-V's bubble rates are consistently lower than 1F1B, 1F1B-I, and ZB-H1, and comparable to ZB-H2. For the 14.6B model at `p=24`, `m=96`: ZB-V bubble rate is 0.0483, ZB-H2 is 0.0480, ZB-H1 is 0.1121, 1F1B is 0.2229. The key metric: **ZB-V achieves ZB-H2-level bubble rates at half the memory cost** (since ZB-H2 requires `(2p-1)M_B` while ZB-V requires only `p M_B`).

Figure 9 compares the memory-bubble trade-off for ZB-V versus the standard heuristic algorithm (labeled "ZB"). The x-axis is `M_limit` in units of `p M_B`, and the y-axis is bubble rate. ZB-V consistently achieves lower bubble rates than ZB at every memory level below `2p M_B`. For the 14.6B model at `m=72` and `M_limit = 1.0 p M_B`: ZB-V bubble rate is approximately 0.08 versus approximately 0.14 for ZB — nearly half. The gap narrows as `M_limit` approaches `2p M_B`, where both methods converge to near-zero bubble rates, but at low memory budgets, ZB-V's advantage is substantial. This validates the V-shaped interleaving as genuinely more memory-efficient, not merely a different point on the same Pareto frontier.

#### Small Number of Microbatches (`m ≤ p`)

Appendix H (Table 12) evaluates a rarely-studied regime where the number of microbatches is less than or equal to the number of pipeline stages. This is typically avoided in practice because bubbles dominate, but the paper demonstrates that ZB methods still provide substantial gains.

For the 1.5B model on 8 GPUs with `m=2`, `b=6`: ZB-2p achieves 4.25 samples/GPU/second vs. 1F1B's 3.56 — a 19.4% improvement. At `m=4`: 6.92 vs. 5.74 — 20.6%. At `m=8` (where `m=p`): 9.90 vs. 8.26 — 19.9%. The paper notes that "when `m <= p`, ZB-1p and ZB-2p are essentially the same and consumes similar memory as 1F1B" — the memory limit does not constrain the schedule because there aren't enough microbatches to fill memory.

For the 14.6B model on 16 GPUs with `m=4`, `b=1`: ZB-2p achieves 0.52 vs. 1F1B's 0.39 — a 33.3% improvement. The paper provides a rough formula in Appendix H: ignoring communication and assuming `T_W < T_B`, a 1F1B iteration takes `(m + p - 1) × (T_F + T_B + T_W)` while a ZB iteration takes `(m + p - 1) × (T_F + T_B) + T_W`, the difference being the removal of `(m + p - 2) × T_W` from the critical path.

#### Data Parallelism Communication Overlap

Appendix A (Figure 10) addresses how data parallelism's all-reduce communication (for gradient aggregation across data-parallel replicas) interacts with the ZB schedules. In standard schedules, W passes at the tail are grouped by microbatch — all parameter gradients for microbatch `j` are computed together, then all for microbatch `j+1`, and so on. This leads to poor overlap with the all-reduce because different W passes compute gradients for different parameters at different times, creating staggered communication.

The paper proposes reordering the W-pass computations to **group by parameter rather than by microbatch**. For example, all W passes across all microbatches compute the gradient for parameter `θ_1` first, then the gradient for parameter `θ_2`, and so on. This clusters the gradient computations for each parameter, allowing the all-reduce for that parameter's gradient to begin as soon as its computation is complete, and to overlap with computations for other parameters. Figure 10 illustrates the difference: the original grouping (top) shows a series of all-reduce operations triggered at staggered times with poor overlap, while the reordered grouping (bottom) shows all-reduce operations neatly pipelined behind their respective parameter gradient computations.

This optimization is not specific to ZB schedules, but the paper notes that ZB schedules naturally place many W passes at the tail, creating an opportunity for this reordering to improve overlap. No quantitative results are reported for this optimization, so its impact is unmeasured.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Optimizer post-validation vs. all-reduce synchronization (Appendix E, Table 10):** This ablation tests the contribution of the post-validation strategy (Section 4) to ZB-2p's throughput. Comparing ZB-2p with post-validation against ZB-2p with standard all-reduce synchronization:
- 1.5B, 8 GPUs, `m=24`: 14.5 vs. 13.11 samples/GPU/second — a 10.6% degradation from synchronization.
- 6.2B, 8 GPUs, `m=24`: 4.32 vs. 4.00 — 8.0% degradation.
- 14.6B, 16 GPUs, `m=48`: 1.81 vs. 1.68 — 7.7% degradation.
- 28.3B, 32 GPUs, `m=96`: 0.99 vs. 0.91 — 8.8% degradation.

The consistent 8–11% throughput penalty from all-reduce synchronization validates the paper's claim that optimizer synchronization is a non-negligible barrier to zero-bubble execution. Without the post-validation strategy, ZB-2p's advantage over 1F1B would be approximately 8 percentage points smaller — still substantial but materially reduced.

**ZB-2p vs. 1F1B under identical memory consumption (Appendix F, Table 11):** This ablation addresses the concern that ZB-2p's throughput advantage may come primarily from its higher memory usage (approximately 2× that of 1F1B) rather than scheduling efficiency. The experiment doubles 1F1B and ZB-1p's microbatch size while halving ZB-2p's microbatch size (and doubling its microbatch count) to equalize memory consumption.

For the 1.5B model at `m=24`, `b=12` for 1F1B/ZB-1p vs. `b=6` for ZB-2p: ZB-2p achieves 14.5 samples/GPU/second vs. 1F1B's 12.0 and ZB-1p's 13.0 — ZB-2p wins by 20.8% and 11.5% respectively. At `m=64`: 14.9 vs. 13.8 and 14.4 — still 8.0% and 3.5% ahead.

For the 14.6B model at `m=48`, `b=2` for 1F1B/ZB-1p vs. `b=1` for ZB-2p: 1.81 vs. 1.53 and 1.73 — ZB-2p ahead by 18.3% and 4.6%. At `m=128`: 1.85 vs. 1.78 and 1.89 — here ZB-1p with doubled microbatch size slightly outperforms ZB-2p (1.89 vs. 1.85, approximately 2% advantage).

The finding is nuanced: ZB-2p generally retains its advantage even at half the microbatch size, but when microbatch size is a significant factor in GPU utilization (the 14.6B model), ZB-1p with a doubled microbatch can catch up or surpass ZB-2p. The paper concludes that "in some experiments where the device utilization is less saturated and `m/p` is relatively large, ZB-1p with a doubled microbatch size may slightly outperform ZB-2p." This suggests that the optimal choice between ZB-1p (larger microbatch, some bubbles) and ZB-2p (smaller microbatch, near-zero bubbles) depends on GPU utilization characteristics.

**Microbatch size scaling (Table 7):** This ablation quantifies how throughput improves when doubling microbatch size at fixed global batch size. For ZB-V with the 14.6B model: doubling `b` from 1 to 2 on 24 GPUs (`m=96`) improves throughput by 7.43% (1.75 → 1.88). For the 28.3B model on 32 GPUs (`m=128`): 6.32% improvement (0.95 → 1.02). The improvements are larger for 1F1B (9.52% and 8.75% respectively), suggesting that ZB schedules partially compensate for small microbatch sizes through better bubble utilization.

**Handcrafted vs. automatically scheduled bubble rates (Table 5):** As discussed above, ZB-H2 consistently performs worse than ZB-2p, with gaps of 2.5–17× in bubble rate. This is the primary evidence that the automatic scheduling algorithm is necessary for real-world deployment — the `T_F = T_B = T_W` assumption underlying handcrafted schedules leads to substantial residual bubbles. The exception is ZB-H1 vs. ZB-1p, which produce identical bubble rates, suggesting that under tight memory constraints the scheduling space is small enough that handcrafted design is near-optimal.

**ZB-V bubble rates vs. memory limit (Figure 9, Table 8):** ZB-V achieves lower bubble rates than the standard heuristic (labeled "ZB") at every memory level below `2p M_B`. At `M_limit = 1.0 p M_B` for the 6.2B model (`p=16`, `m=48`): ZB-V bubble rate is approximately 0.09 vs. approximately 0.14 for ZB — a 36% reduction. This confirms that the V-shaped interleaving pattern provides genuine memory-efficiency advantages, not merely a different scheduling heuristic.

**Correctness verification:** Though not presented as an ablation, the paper states that loss values are "bit-to-bit identical" across ZB-1p, ZB-2p, and 1F1B when using a fixed random seed. This confirms that the scheduling changes are purely a runtime optimization — they do not alter the optimization trajectory, gradient computations, or final model quality. The post-validation strategy with rollback preserves synchronous semantics exactly, so any iteration where a rollback occurs produces identical parameters to a standard synchronous step.

### Critical Assessment

The experiments convincingly demonstrate that B/W splitting and automatic scheduling can substantially reduce pipeline bubbles and improve training throughput compared to 1F1B and interleaved 1F1B. However, the evidence supports some claims more strongly than others, and several gaps between the paper's assertions and the experimental evidence warrant attention.

**Claim: "Our method outperforms the 1F1B schedule up to 23% in throughput under a similar memory limit."** This claim is well-supported. Table 4 and Figure 5 show ZB-1p (which uses similar memory to 1F1B: e.g., 32 GB vs. 30 GB for the 1.5B model) achieving throughput improvements of 4.4–15.0% across configurations, with the largest gains at low microbatch counts. Even at high microbatch counts where 1F1B's bubble fraction is smaller, ZB-1p maintains a 4.2–9.1% advantage. The 23% figure corresponds to the best-case scenario (6.2B model at `m=24`: 3.88 vs. 3.50 for ZB-1p vs. 1F1B, a 10.9% improvement; but the real 23% figure comes from the ZB-2p comparison at `m=24` for the 6.2B model: 4.32 vs. 3.50, which is not "similar memory" — ZB-2p uses 70 GB vs. 39 GB for 1F1B). The paper conflates "under a similar memory limit" with "ZB-1p matches memory while ZB-2p exceeds it," but the headline 23% number should be attributed specifically to ZB-2p in a relaxed-memory regime. The 31% figure for "when the memory constraint is relaxed" is less clearly supported by the tables — the largest percentage gap in Table 4 is 30.3% (28.3B, `m=96`: 0.99 vs. 0.76), which rounds to 30%, not 31%.

**Claim: "This number can be further pushed to 31% when the memory constraint is relaxed."** This claim appears in the abstract. The experimental support is the ZB-2p vs. 1F1B comparison at equal microbatch size, where ZB-2p uses roughly double the memory. The largest gap in Table 4 is 30.3% (28.3B, `m=96`). The paper does not explicitly identify where the 31% figure comes from, and no configuration in Table 4 reaches exactly 31%. This may be a rounding or a specific configuration from a different experiment, but the ambiguity weakens the claim.

**Claim: "Zero pipeline bubbles under synchronous training semantics."** This claim in the abstract and title is the strongest assertion the paper makes. The evidence supports "near-zero" bubbles (0.1–1.0% bubble rates in Table 5) but not absolute zero in all cases. Figure 6 (bottom) shows the profiled execution has "slightly more bubbles" than the theoretically zero-bubble planned schedule. The abstract itself qualifies this as "the first to successfully achieve zero pipeline bubbles" in the handcrafted ideal case (`T_F = T_B = T_W`), while the automatic schedules "achieve close to zero bubble rate." The title ("Zero Bubble Pipeline Parallelism") is thus somewhat aspirational — the paper achieves near-zero bubbles, which is a major advance, but the name overstates the empirical achievement. Practitioners should expect 1–5% residual bubble rates in practice, not literal zero.

**Claim: "ZB-V achieves zero bubble with the same peak memory as 1F1B."** Under the idealized assumption `T_F = T_B = T_W`, ZB-V achieves zero bubble (Table 8 shows bubble rates of 0.0247–0.0697, which are non-zero because of communication and timing heterogeneity). The paper explicitly states this assumption when making the zero-bubble claim: "Under the condition `T_F = T_B = T_W`, ZB-V achieves zero bubble with a peak activations memory of `p M_B`" (Section 6). This is true as a theoretical statement, but the experimental bubble rates in Table 8 for ZB-V range from 0.0236 to 0.0697 — not zero. These are very low (2–7% bubble rates, representing a 5–10× reduction from 1F1B), but they are not zero. The gap between the idealized claim and the empirical results is acknowledged but could be more prominent.

**Weakness: Single hardware configuration and network topology.** All experiments use NVIDIA A100 SXM 80GB GPUs connected via RoCE RDMA across up to 4 nodes (32 GPUs). The communication time `T_{comm}` values in Table 9 are small (0.377–0.762 ms), suggesting the RoCE network provides high bandwidth. The paper does not evaluate on Ethernet-connected clusters, on older GPU architectures (V100, A40), or on larger scales (hundreds of GPUs) where communication patterns might change. The conclusion that ZB-1p outperforms 1F1B-I in multi-node settings because of lower communication overhead is supported only on this specific hardware — on networks with different bandwidth/latency characteristics, the relative performance might shift.

**Weakness: No end-to-end training convergence results.** The experiments measure throughput per iteration and verify bit-to-bit loss identity, but do not train any model to convergence. This is standard for pipeline scheduling papers (the scheduling is orthogonal to convergence), but it means the paper does not demonstrate that the throughput gains translate to wall-clock speedup for a complete training run. Factors like the post-validation rollback frequency (which consumes extra computation when triggered), the impact of the profiled timing's accuracy on long-running jobs, and potential memory fragmentation issues over many iterations are not assessed.

**Weakness: The difficulty estimation for schedule generation is externalized.** The automatic scheduling algorithm requires profiled `T_F`, `T_B`, `T_W`, and `T_{comm}` values, which are obtained by running "a specific number of iterations for profiling" before generating the schedule (Section 5.1). The paper does not account for this profiling cost in any throughput calculation. For short training runs, the profiling overhead could be significant; for long runs, it is amortized. The sensitivity of the generated schedule to profiling noise is not evaluated — if the profiled times differ from actual runtime behavior (due to GPU temperature throttling, contention with other jobs, or network congestion), the schedule may become suboptimal.

**Weakness: Limited exploration of the `m ≤ p` regime.** The experiments in Appendix H are the only evaluation of ZB methods when microbatches are fewer than pipeline stages. While the paper reports strong gains (19–33%), only two model sizes and a handful of `m` values are tested. The claim in the conclusion that ZB methods "can achieve optimal efficiency with a smaller number of microbatches (typically 3p is enough), which means more microbatches can be partitioned over data parallelism dimension" is not directly supported by experiments — the paper never evaluates a combined PP+DP setup with ZB scheduling to show that the freed microbatch capacity improves DP scalability.

**Weakness: No comparison against asynchronous PP methods.** The paper positions itself as improving synchronous PP to match asynchronous PP's efficiency "without sacrificing exact optimization semantics," but never includes PipeDream or PipeMare as baselines. This is a missed opportunity to quantify the remaining gap between synchronous ZB schedules and asynchronous methods, which would help practitioners decide whether the synchronous semantics are worth any residual throughput disadvantage.

**Missing ablation: Sensitivity to split granularity.** The paper splits the backward pass into exactly two parts (B and W) per layer. Could further splitting (e.g., separating attention and feedforward backward computations, or splitting individual matmul gradients) yield additional gains? The paper does not explore this, nor does it justify why B/W is the right granularity rather than finer or coarser splits. The introduction mentions that "choosing a suitable granularity is crucial" but provides no experimental exploration of this choice.

**Missing ablation: Impact of number of pipeline stages.** All experiments fix `p` based on the number of GPUs (8, 16, or 32) and model size. No experiment varies `p` independently of model size to show how the gains scale with pipeline depth. The paper claims bubble size scales with `p-1`, so ZB methods should provide larger relative gains for deeper pipelines, but this is only tested implicitly across model sizes (which also change other variables like `T_F/T_B/T_W` ratios).

**Missing experiment: ZB-V under extremely low memory (`M_limit < p M_B`).** Figure 9 shows that ZB-V outperforms the standard heuristic even at `M_limit = 1.0 p M_B`, but does not test below this threshold. Some deployments may need to operate at lower memory (e.g., by using activation recomputation), and it is unclear whether ZB-V's advantage persists or if the V-shaped routing introduces memory pressure patterns that could be problematic under tighter constraints.

Overall, the experimental evidence strongly supports the paper's central contribution — that B/W splitting enables substantially more efficient pipeline schedules — but the absolute claims of "zero bubble" should be understood as "near-zero bubble in most configurations, with residual bubble rates of 0.1–7%." The throughput improvements are real and substantial (typically 5–30% depending on configuration and memory budget), and the automatic scheduling algorithm is demonstrably necessary for achieving these gains on real hardware with heterogeneous timing. The experiments are thorough by systems paper standards but leave several practical questions unanswered, particularly around profiling overhead, long-running stability, and integration with full data-parallel training deployments.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Profiling and Schedule Generation Overhead Is Unaccounted For

**The assumption or constraint.** The automatic scheduling algorithm described in Section 3 requires profiled timing values for `T_F`, `T_B`, `T_W`, and `T_{comm}` to construct an optimal schedule. The paper states in Section 5.1 that it first "conducted a specific number of iterations for profiling, collecting empirical measurements." This profiling step is a prerequisite for any deployment of the method, and its cost is excluded from all throughput measurements. The paper does not specify how many profiling iterations are needed, whether the profiling must be repeated after any change in model configuration, hardware, or network conditions, or what happens if the profiled timings drift during a long training run (due to thermal throttling, GPU memory fragmentation, or network congestion variability).

**The consequence.** The headline throughput gains — up to 23% over 1F1B under similar memory, and up to 31% with relaxed memory — are computed *after* the schedule has been generated, without amortizing the profiling cost. For short training runs (e.g., fine-tuning on a small dataset for a few hundred iterations), the profiling overhead could exceed the throughput savings, making the method a net loss. For long training runs, the profiling cost amortizes to negligible levels, but a second problem emerges: profiled timings are averages, and actual execution times vary across iterations. If `T_F`, `T_B`, or `T_W` deviate from their profiled values (due to GPU clock throttling under sustained load, memory controller contention, or network congestion from other jobs on the cluster), the schedule becomes suboptimal because `W` passes sized to fill gaps of exactly `T_W` will either overflow (delaying subsequent operations) or underflow (leaving residual bubbles). Figure 6 provides indirect evidence: the profiled real execution (bottom) "has slightly more bubbles" than the theoretically scheduled version (top) — the paper acknowledges this but does not quantify the gap, leaving open whether these runtime deviations erode the throughput gains by 1%, 5%, or more.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figure 6 shows a visual comparison between the planned ZB-2p schedule and its profiled execution, with the authors noting the real execution "retains a good overall alignment" but "has slightly more bubbles." No quantitative measurement of this gap is reported. Table 9 lists the profiled `T_F`, `T_B`, `T_W`, `T_{comm}` values for all experimental configurations, but these are point estimates — there is no report of variance across iterations, nor a sensitivity analysis showing how schedule quality degrades when profiled timings are perturbed. The throughput numbers in Figure 5 and Table 4 are measured from actual execution, so they incorporate whatever bubble increase occurs at runtime, but the paper does not isolate how much of the residual bubble rate (e.g., ZB-2p's 0.0026–0.0433 in Table 5) comes from imperfect profiling versus fundamental scheduling constraints.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation directly. It does not propose a method for adaptive rescheduling (e.g., re-profiling periodically and regenerating the schedule mid-training), nor does it discuss whether the ILP or heuristic algorithm could incorporate timing uncertainty (e.g., by padding gap sizes with a safety margin). The profiling step is presented as a one-time preprocessing cost that the reader is expected to accept as amortizable, but no analysis supports this assumption. This is a practical deployment barrier that remains unresolved.

### The B/W Split Requires Modifying the Deep Learning Framework's Autograd Engine, Limiting Portability

**The assumption or constraint.** The entire approach depends on decomposing the monolithic backward function into separately schedulable `B` and `W` operations. The paper states this explicitly in Section 1: "we spotted the opportunity that PP can be further optimized by representing and scheduling the computation graph at a finer granularity." However, standard deep learning frameworks (PyTorch, JAX, TensorFlow) treat each layer's backward pass as a single autograd node. Exposing `B` and `W` as separate operations requires either (a) modifying the framework's autograd engine to decompose the backward computation graph, (b) writing custom backward functions for every layer type that manually split the computation, or (c) relying on a compiler infrastructure (XLA, TVM, Triton) that can perform this decomposition automatically.

**The consequence.** The paper's implementation, based on Megatron-LM, required non-trivial framework modifications that are not detailed. A practitioner wanting to deploy ZB schedules on a different codebase (e.g., PyTorch FSDP, Hugging Face Transformers, or a custom training loop) cannot simply drop in a scheduling algorithm — they must first ensure that `B` and `W` are exposed as separately callable operations with correct memory semantics. This is a significant engineering barrier that the paper does not quantify. Furthermore, the split is not semantically neutral: `B` and `W` share intermediate computations in a standard backward pass (the gradient of the loss with respect to the layer output, `dℓ/dy`, is used by both), so splitting them either requires recomputing `dℓ/dy` or storing it as an additional activation, which increases memory usage beyond what Table 1 accounts for. The paper's FLOP counts in Table 1 (`T_B` and `T_W` add to `2T_F`) assume no recomputation overhead, but whether this holds in practice depends on the framework and layer implementation.

The paper also notes that the initial and final pipeline stages "possess one fewer transformer layer compared to the intermediate stages... to compensate for the extra embedding lookup and loss computations" (Section 5.1). This implies that the B/W split is applied only to transformer layers, not to the embedding or loss layers — but the paper does not discuss how to handle layers where `B` and `W` have different timing/memory characteristics than the transformer profile, or layers where the split is not clean (e.g., layers with shared parameters, normalization layers with running statistics, or custom operations).

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides no ablation showing what fraction of backward-pass computation the B/W split actually exposes or whether there are layer types for which the split provides no benefit. Table 1 provides FLOP and memory formulas only for standard transformer layers, not for embedding tables, layer normalization, or loss functions. The memory values in Table 4 and Table 6 show that ZB methods use slightly more memory than 1F1B even when memory-matched (e.g., ZB-1p uses 32 GB vs. 1F1B's 30 GB for the 1.5B model, and 44 GB vs. 43 GB for the 28.3B model), which may reflect the extra memory cost of materializing `dℓ/dy` for the split computation. The paper does not discuss this.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges the framework dependency implicitly by open-sourcing its implementation "based on the popular Megatron-LM repository" (Section 1). This provides a reference implementation but does not address the portability problem. The paper does not discuss whether the split can be achieved through compiler passes (e.g., XLA's HLO-level optimizations) that would make it framework-agnostic, or whether it requires manual framework modification. Future framework-level support for exposing `B` and `W` as separate primitives would resolve this, but the paper does not propose such support.

### The Post-Validation Optimizer Strategy Introduces a Statistical Correctness Risk That Is Not Empirically Characterized

**The assumption or constraint.** The optimizer post-validation strategy (Section 4) replaces the standard all-reduce synchronization before each optimizer step with a forward-reduce-then-backward-validate protocol. This protocol uses *partially reduced* gradient norm and NaN/Inf flags to decide whether to apply, clip, or skip the optimizer step on each stage, and then validates these decisions in the next iteration's warm-up phase, triggering a rollback if the decision was incorrect. The correctness of this approach depends on two empirical claims: (1) NaN/Inf values "rarely trigger because in a robust setting most iterations shouldn't have numerical issues," and (2) "the gradient clipping rate is also quite low empirically." The paper provides no data on the actual frequency of NaN/Inf occurrences or gradient clipping events in the training runs used for evaluation, nor does it characterize the distribution of partially reduced gradient norms relative to the fully reduced global norm.

**The consequence.** If the partially reduced gradient norm used by stage `i` (which includes contributions from stages 1 through `i`) differs substantially from the fully reduced global norm (stages 1 through `p`), the optimizer step may apply an incorrect clipping factor. For example, if stages 1–4 of an 8-stage pipeline have a small combined gradient norm below the clipping threshold, but stages 5–8 have a large gradient that pushes the global norm over the threshold, stage 4 will apply no clipping while stage 8 (when its turn comes) will apply clipping based on a different partial norm. This creates an inconsistency: different stages apply different scaling factors to their gradients, which is mathematically not equivalent to a synchronous step where all stages use the same global scaling factor. The rollback mechanism corrects this post-hoc by re-applying the optimizer step with the correct global clipping factor, but the paper does not analyze whether the intermediate inconsistency (where some parameters have been updated with incorrectly scaled gradients while others have not) could affect the training dynamics — for example, through momentum state corruption in optimizers like AdamW, where the moment estimates `m` and `v` depend on the sequence of gradient values seen.

More subtly, the forward reduction of the gradient norm requires each stage to compute `∑_k ‖g_k‖²` locally and add it to the running sum from previous stages. If any stage's local computation has a bug (e.g., an overflow in the squared norm computation that produces an incorrect partial sum), the validation phase will detect the error and trigger a rollback — but the rollback itself (Algorithm 1) assumes the gradient `g` used in the original step is still available and correct. If the gradient buffer has been overwritten by the next iteration's computation (which begins during the warm-up phase, overlapping with the backward validation propagation), the rollback cannot proceed correctly. The paper does not describe how gradient buffers are managed across the iteration boundary to ensure rollback safety.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 10 shows that replacing post-validation with standard all-reduce synchronization reduces ZB-2p's throughput by approximately 8% (e.g., 14.5 → 13.11 for the 1.5B model). This quantifies the throughput benefit of post-validation but provides no information about the frequency of validation failures (rollback triggers) or the correctness impact. The paper states that loss values are "bit-to-bit identical" across ZB-1p, ZB-2p, and 1F1B (Section 5.1), but this verification is done using the post-validation strategy — it confirms that the method is correct for the configurations tested, but does not quantify how often the rollback mechanism was actually invoked or whether the bit-to-bit identity would hold under different random seeds, model initializations, or training data where numerical issues might be more frequent (e.g., training with larger learning rates, different optimizers, or mixed-precision settings with more aggressive loss scaling).

**Mitigation status.** The paper proposes the post-validation strategy as a solution to the optimizer synchronization problem and demonstrates it works correctly (bit-to-bit identical loss), but does not analyze its failure modes or performance under stress conditions. The in-place rollback algorithm (Algorithm 1) is proven only for AdamW; extension to other optimizers (SGD with momentum, Adafactor, LAMB) would require separate reversibility analysis. The paper does not discuss gradient buffer management or the interaction between rollback and data-parallel gradient all-reduce, which typically overwrites or reduces gradient buffers at iteration boundaries.

### Evaluation Is Limited to a Single Model Architecture, Single Hardware Configuration, and No Convergence Test

**The assumption or constraint.** All experiments use GPT-3–style transformer models (Table 3) on NVIDIA A100 SXM 80GB GPUs connected via RoCE RDMA across up to 4 nodes (32 GPUs). The paper does not evaluate on other model architectures (encoder-decoder transformers like T5, mixture-of-experts models, CNNs, or diffusion models), other hardware (V100, H100, TPUs, Ethernet-connected clusters), or larger scales (hundreds of GPUs where communication patterns and contention differ). Furthermore, throughput is measured per iteration without training any model to convergence (Section 5.1: "we can verify the correctness... without running models until convergence").

**The consequence.** The paper's central claims — that B/W splitting reduces bubble rates to near zero, that ZB-1p matches 1F1B's memory while improving throughput, that ZB-2p achieves near-zero bubble at `2p M_B` memory — are all supported only on transformer language models with the specific layer structure analyzed in Table 1. For architectures with different FLOP and memory ratios between `F`, `B`, and `W` (e.g., CNNs where `T_F` and `T_B` may be closer in cost due to different operation mixes, or mixture-of-experts models where `W` involves an additional all-to-all communication for expert routing), the scheduling benefits may differ significantly. The paper provides no guidance on how to determine whether a given model architecture is amenable to the B/W split or how to estimate potential gains without running the full profiling and scheduling pipeline.

The hardware limitation is equally consequential. The profiled `T_{comm}` values in Table 9 are small (0.377–0.762 ms), reflecting the high bandwidth of the RoCE network used. On Ethernet-connected clusters or at larger scales where network congestion increases, `T_{comm}` would be larger, potentially changing the optimal schedule (e.g., making interleaved 1F1B less competitive because its finer-grained communication becomes more costly, or making ZB-H1/2B-1p more attractive because they avoid extra communication). The paper's finding that ZB-1p outperforms 1F1B-I in multi-node settings (Section 5.2: "In multi-node setups where communication bandwidth is more of a bottleneck, ZB-1p clearly outperforms 1F1B-I") supports the hypothesis that communication costs shift the optimal strategy, but only a single network type is tested.

The absence of convergence testing means the paper does not demonstrate that the throughput gains translate to wall-clock speedup for a complete training run. Factors that could erode the gains in long runs include: (a) the post-validation rollback mechanism being triggered more frequently than expected under sustained training (e.g., as the loss decreases and gradients become sparser, the gradient norm distribution may shift, changing the partial-vs-global norm relationship); (b) memory fragmentation over many iterations causing the profiled memory limits to become inaccurate; (c) communication pattern changes as the network topology interacts with other jobs on the cluster; and (d) checkpoint I/O costs that are not accounted for in per-iteration timing.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The experiments span four model sizes (1.5B to 28.3B parameters) and three pipeline depths (8, 16, 32 GPUs), which provides reasonable coverage within the transformer language model family. Table 9 provides profiled timing for all configurations, showing that `T_B ≈ T_F > T_W` with `T_W ≈ 0.5 × T_F` — this consistency across model sizes suggests the timing ratios are architecture-specific but size-stable, lending some confidence to generalizability within transformers. However, no non-transformer architecture is tested, and no hardware other than A100+RoCE is used. The throughput measurements are taken "after several warm-up iterations" (Section 5.1), but the paper does not specify how many iterations were used for profiling vs. measurement or whether the measured throughput is sustained over hundreds of iterations.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not claim generalizability beyond what it tests, which is a point in its favor. It is scoped as improving "the efficiency of pipeline scheduling" specifically, not as a universal training system. However, the paper also does not discuss which model or hardware properties determine the method's effectiveness, leaving practitioners to guess whether their architecture will benefit. The open-sourced implementation (Megatron-LM) provides a starting point for testing on other configurations, but the engineering cost of porting to other frameworks and models remains unaddressed. No convergence experiment is proposed as future work, though the bit-to-bit loss identity verification is a strong signal that convergence behavior should be identical to the 1F1B baseline (since the optimization trajectory is unchanged).

### The Automatic Scheduling Algorithm Has Practical Scalability Limits That Are Not Fully Characterized

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper provides two scheduling approaches: a heuristic algorithm described as fast and suitable for large `m`, and an ILP formulation described as optimal but limited to "when the problem is under a certain scale" (Section 3). Section 3.1 notes that "these two approaches can be combined: first, use the heuristic solution as initialization, and then optimize it further with ILP." However, the paper does not characterize the computational cost of either approach, nor does it specify the problem sizes (`p`, `m`) at which the ILP becomes infeasible.

**The consequence.** For large-scale training runs with hundreds of microbatches (`m` in the thousands) and dozens of pipeline stages (`p` of 64 or 128, as in thousand-GPU clusters), the ILP solver may require hours or days of compute time, or may fail to find a solution entirely. The heuristic algorithm, while fast, may produce suboptimal schedules at these scales — the paper's claim that the heuristic "always generates an optimal or near optimal solution especially when `m` is large enough" (Section 3) is not proven; it is plausible that large `m` provides more opportunities for `W` reordering that a greedy heuristic misses. The paper's experimental configurations use modest values: `p` ranges from 8 to 32, and `m` from 24 to 256 (Table 3). These are representative of small-to-medium training clusters but do not approach the scale of frontier training runs (e.g., training GPT-4–scale models with `p=128` and `m=1024`).

Furthermore, the scheduling algorithm must be re-run whenever the configuration changes — a new model size, a different microbatch count, or updated hardware profiling. In a production environment where these parameters are tuned frequently (e.g., adjusting microbatch size to balance memory and throughput during hyperparameter optimization), the scheduling cost could become a practical bottleneck. The paper provides no amortization analysis or caching strategy.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 5 provides bubble rates for all experimental configurations, and Figure 6 provides a visual schedule for a 16-GPU configuration with `m=128`. These demonstrate that the algorithms work at the tested scales, but no runtime measurements or scaling analysis for the schedulers themselves are reported. The paper does not state whether the heuristic or ILP was used for each experimental result, nor how long the scheduling step took. The claim in Section 3 about the two approaches being "combined" is not demonstrated experimentally — no comparison of heuristic-only vs. heuristic+ILP schedules is provided, so the reader cannot assess whether the ILP refinement provides measurable improvement over the heuristic alone in practice.

The paper also introduces an ILP formulation (Appendix G) that uses binary ordering variables `O_{(i,j,c)→(i,j',c')}` to encode the scheduling order. The number of binary variables scales as `O((m·p·3)²)` — three operations per microbatch per stage, with pairwise ordering variables between all pairs of operations on the same stage. For `p=32`, `m=256`, this yields approximately `(32 × 256 × 3)² ≈ 6 × 10⁸` binary variables, which is far beyond the capacity of any off-the-shelf ILP solver. The heuristic algorithm therefore must be the default for realistic scales, but its suboptimality at large scales is unknown.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges the ILP's scalability limitation implicitly by describing it as suitable "when the problem is under a certain scale" and by providing the heuristic as an alternative. The combination strategy (heuristic initialization + ILP refinement) is presented as a way to mitigate this, but its effectiveness is untested. The paper does not suggest future work on more scalable scheduling algorithms (e.g., hierarchical scheduling, online scheduling, or learned scheduling policies), which would be necessary for deployment at frontier training scales.

### ZB-V's Zero-Bubble Claim Requires Idealized Timing That Does Not Hold on Real Hardware

**The assumption or constraint.** Section 6 states: "Under the condition `T_F = T_B = T_W`, ZB-V achieves zero bubble with a peak activations memory of `p M_B`, aligning with the maximum peak memory usage of 1F1B." This is the key theoretical claim that makes ZB-V attractive — it offers ZB-H2–level throughput at 1F1B-level memory. However, the idealized timing condition `T_F = T_B = T_W` is explicitly acknowledged as false in practice. The paper's own profiling data (Table 9) shows that for the 28.3B model on 32 GPUs, `T_B (10.207) > T_F (10.419) > T_W (7.715)` with `T_{comm} = 0.408` ms — all values differ.

**The consequence.** When `T_F ≠ T_B ≠ T_W`, ZB-V no longer achieves zero bubble. Table 8 reports ZB-V bubble rates from 0.0236 to 0.0697 — low, but not zero. The paper's attempt to compensate for this by using the automatic scheduling algorithm to "straightforwardly shift all `W` to the right, within the memory constraint" (Section 6) produces a schedule with residual bubbles because `W` passes are not sized to perfectly fill the gaps left by `F` and `B`. The bubble rate formula for ZB-H2 (`(p-1)(T_F + T_B - 2T_W)`) applies approximately to ZB-V as well, but since `T_B ≠ T_F` and `T_W < T_F`, the expression does not reduce to zero.

More importantly, the V-shaped model partitioning introduces a new constraint that is not present in ZB-H2: the two chunks per device must be non-contiguous and symmetrically positioned from the ends of the model. This means the forward and backward passes for the second chunk on device `i` must travel through devices `p, p-1, ..., i` (the "return" of the V), creating a longer dependency chain than the forward-only or backward-only chains in standard interleaving. If `T_{comm}` is large, this bidirectional communication along the return path may become a bottleneck that the paper does not analyze. The profiled `T_{comm}` values in Table 9 are small (sub-millisecond), masking this effect — on higher-latency networks (e.g., Ethernet with 10–100× higher latency), the return-path communication could dominate the schedule and eliminate ZB-V's advantage over ZB-H1.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 8 provides bubble rates for ZB-V across all configurations, none of which are zero (range 0.0236–0.0697). Figure 9 shows that ZB-V achieves lower bubble rates than the standard heuristic at low memory budgets, but the curves converge as memory increases, indicating that ZB-V's advantage is in the low-memory regime rather than in absolute bubble elimination. The paper acknowledges the idealized condition explicitly but still uses "zero bubble" in ZB-V's framing: the schedule name and the claim "capable of achieving zero bubble when `T_F = T_B = T_W`" appear prominently in the conclusion and Section 6, even though this condition is not met in any experiment. This creates a mismatch between the theoretical framing and the empirical results that could mislead practitioners expecting literal zero bubble from ZB-V.

**Mitigation status.** The paper partially addresses this by applying the automatic scheduling algorithm to ZB-V (using profiled timing) and reporting empirical bubble rates in Table 8. The results are honest — bubble rates are 2–7%, not zero — but the "zero bubble" language persists in the section title and conclusion. The paper does not analyze how ZB-V's bubble rate scales with the deviation of real timing from the `T_F = T_B = T_W` assumption, nor does it provide guidance on when ZB-V is preferable to ZB-1p (memory-matched) or ZB-2p (bubble-matched but higher memory). The trade-off between ZB-V's lower memory and its residual bubble rate (which is higher than ZB-2p's near-zero rate) is left for practitioners to evaluate empirically.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper instigates a **granularity shift** in how the field conceptualizes pipeline scheduling. Prior to this work, the backward pass was treated as the indivisible atom of pipeline scheduling — and under that atomicity, pipeline bubbles appeared as a geometric inevitability, something to be minimized through larger microbatch counts or asynchronous semantics, but never eliminated. The paper's core move is to demonstrate that this inevitability was an artifact of representation, not of fundamental constraints. By decomposing the backward pass into its cross-stage-dependent component (B, the input gradient) and its local component (W, the parameter gradient), the paper transforms the scheduling problem from one of minimizing unavoidable idle time to one of filling idle time with previously hidden slack.

This is not merely an incremental improvement in scheduling heuristics — it is a **reframing of what constitutes the right abstraction level for pipeline parallelism**. The paper argues, implicitly but forcefully, that the granularity at which the computation graph is exposed to the scheduler is a first-class design choice that fundamentally determines the achievable efficiency. This insight connects pipeline scheduling to the broader compiler design tradition where lowering to finer-grained intermediate representations exposes optimization opportunities invisible at higher levels. The paper's references to TVM, MLIR, XLA, and Triton (Section 1) are not incidental — they position B/W splitting as an instance of a general principle: decomposing monolithic operations into their dependency-distinct sub-components enables scheduling that exploits the resulting slack.

The practical magnitude of this shift is substantial: bubble rates drop from 10–27% (1F1B) to 0.1–1.0% (ZB-2p) across the tested configurations (Table 5), representing a 50–100× reduction at the theoretical level and a 5–30% throughput improvement at the measured level (Figure 5). These are not marginal gains from tuning a known schedule — they represent a qualitative change from "bubbles are a dominant fraction of execution time" to "bubbles are a second-order effect." The fact that ZB-1p achieves meaningful throughput improvements (4–15% across configurations) while using the **same peak memory as 1F1B** (Table 4) is particularly significant because it demonstrates that the scheduling strategy itself — independent of memory-trading — provides gains that no prior synchronous schedule could access.

The paper also resolves a latent tension in the literature between memory efficiency and bubble reduction. Prior work forced a binary choice: use 1F1B for memory efficiency or interleaved 1F1B for reduced bubbles at the cost of higher memory and more communication. The automatic scheduling algorithm (Section 3) dissolves this binary by producing a **continuous Pareto frontier of schedules** parameterized by the memory budget `M_limit` (Figure 7), where practitioners can dial in any point along the trade-off curve rather than choosing between two fixed operating points. This reframing of the memory-bubble relationship as a continuous spectrum rather than a discrete toggle is conceptually significant and practically useful.

The diagnostic contribution around optimizer synchronization (Section 4) is equally important. The paper identifies that the all-reduce barrier at the optimizer step — which prior work treated as a neutral implementation detail — is actually a **structural barrier to zero-bubble execution** that becomes the limiting factor once computation bubbles are eliminated. The 8% throughput penalty from all-reduce synchronization measured in Table 10 quantifies this barrier precisely: without the post-validation strategy, approximately one-third to one-half of ZB-2p's advantage over 1F1B would be erased by the synchronization bubble. This finding redirects attention from scheduling alone to the broader system design: achieving truly zero-bubble training requires rethinking not just the computation schedule but also the communication and synchronization patterns that surround it. The post-validation strategy itself — separating synchronous semantics from synchronized execution by deferring consistency checks and correcting rare errors post-hoc — is an instance of a speculative execution pattern that could generalize to other distributed training bottlenecks.

The ZB-V schedule (Section 6) contributes a novel **dependency-structure-level principle**: that the directionality of the pipeline dependency chain is a design choice, not a fixed property. Standard pipeline parallelism (and standard interleaving) has unidirectional dependency: forward always propagates `1 → p`, backward always propagates `p → 1`. ZB-V's V-shaped interleaving creates a bidirectional dependency where each microbatch's computation originates from and returns to the same device, enabling balanced peak memory and faster memory clearance. This principle — that the dependency graph topology itself can be engineered to optimize the memory-bubble trade-off — opens a design space beyond what fixed-topology scheduling can access. The paper does not explore this space systematically, but the existence proof (ZB-V achieves near-ZB-H2 bubble rates at half the memory, Table 8) is compelling.

Several research directions that seemed promising before this work become less attractive in its wake. The pursuit of ever-more-sophisticated search or MCTS-based scheduling algorithms for monolithic backward passes now appears as optimizing the wrong granularity — the paper shows that fine-grained decomposition plus simple greedy heuristics outperforms what complex search over coarse operations could achieve. The line of work on asynchronous pipeline parallelism (PipeDream, PipeMare) also faces a steeper burden of proof: if synchronous semantics can now achieve near-zero bubble with controlled memory, the convergence risks of stale gradient training become harder to justify. And the common practice of increasing microbatch count to reduce the bubble ratio (at the cost of memory pressure) is now revealed as a brute-force approach to a problem that has a structural solution — using more microbatches to compensate for scheduling inefficiency, rather than eliminating the inefficiency itself.

Conversely, several research directions become more attractive. **Compiler-driven operator decomposition** — automatically identifying and splitting dependency-distinct sub-operations within monolithic framework operations — is an obvious next step that could make B/W splitting framework-agnostic and extend the principle to other operation types. **Learned scheduling policies** that predict difficulty (in this case, optimal `M_limit` and schedule parameters) from model architecture and hardware characteristics could replace the profiling-and-ILP pipeline with a single forward pass, addressing the profiling overhead limitation. **Hierarchical scheduling** that applies fine-grained decomposition within pipeline stages while using coarser-grained scheduling across stages could scale the approach to thousand-GPU clusters where the current ILP formulation would be intractable. And **integrated data-parallel + pipeline-parallel scheduling** that jointly optimizes the B/W placement and the all-reduce communication overlap (building on Appendix A's parameter-grouped W reordering) could yield gains beyond what either optimization achieves independently.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**End-to-end convergence benchmarks with ZB schedules on large-scale training runs.** The paper verifies bit-to-bit loss identity across ZB-1p, ZB-2p, and 1F1B for a limited number of iterations (Section 5.1) but does not train any model to convergence. A strong follow-up would train a model of non-trivial scale (e.g., a 7B-parameter GPT-style model) from initialization to a target validation loss using 1F1B, ZB-1p, and ZB-2p, measuring not just per-iteration throughput but total wall-clock time to reach the target loss. This would address the open question of whether the post-validation rollback mechanism, which is invoked only occasionally (the paper claims NaN/Inf events are rare and gradient clipping rates are low), incurs any compound effect over many iterations — for example, through optimizer momentum state drift when a rollback alters the sequence of gradient values seen by the AdamW momentum accumulators. The experiment should also measure the actual rollback frequency over the full training run, reporting the fraction of iterations where post-validation triggered a correction, to empirically validate the "rare trigger" assumption that the post-validation strategy depends on.

**Compiler-pass implementation of B/W splitting for framework-agnostic deployment.** The paper's implementation is tied to Megatron-LM and requires manual framework modifications. A research compiler project could implement B/W splitting as an automated pass in a deep learning compiler (XLA, MLIR, or Triton) that operates on the autograd computation graph, identifying nodes that compute parameter gradients (W) versus input gradients (B) based on the gradient flow graph, and emitting them as separately callable operations with correct memory semantics. The key research question is whether the split can be performed without recomputation overhead — the paper's FLOP counts in Table 1 assume B and W sum to `2T_F` FLOPs, which would hold only if the intermediate gradient `dℓ/dy` is materialized once and reused, rather than recomputed by both B and W. A compiler pass would need to insert explicit memory allocation/deallocation for the shared intermediate, which the paper's manual implementation presumably handles but does not describe. The evaluation would compare the throughput of compiler-split schedules against the paper's hand-split Megatron-LM implementation, and against a baseline where the backward pass is not split, to isolate any overhead from the compiler's split logic. A negative result — if compiler-split schedules show non-trivial recomputation overhead — would clarify the engineering complexity hidden in the paper's approach.

**Sensitivity analysis of schedule quality to profiling noise and timing drift.** The automatic scheduling algorithm depends on profiled `T_F`, `T_B`, `T_W`, and `T_{comm}` values that are point estimates (Table 9) with no reported variance. A follow-up study would systematically perturb these profiled timings (e.g., adding Gaussian noise with standard deviation equal to 5%, 10%, 20% of the mean) and measure the resulting degradation in schedule quality (bubble rate and measured throughput) for the generated schedule. This would answer: how accurate does profiling need to be for the automatic scheduler to produce a near-optimal schedule? The study could also evaluate the effect of timing drift by profiling at the beginning of training and then measuring actual `T_F`, `T_B`, `T_W` values after 1K, 10K, and 100K iterations (when GPU clock frequencies may have changed due to thermal conditions), quantifying how far the schedule departs from optimality as timings drift. A practical outcome would be a recommended re-profiling interval — e.g., "re-profile every 10K iterations to maintain within 2% of optimal throughput." The paper's Figure 6 (comparing planned vs. profiled execution) already hints at residual bubbles from runtime variation; this study would quantify that effect and provide guidance on its mitigation.

**Evaluation on non-transformer architectures to establish generality boundaries for the B/W split.** The paper's FLOP and memory analysis (Table 1) is specific to transformer layers. A critical follow-up would apply B/W splitting to model architectures with substantially different `T_B/T_W` ratios: convolutional neural networks (where `B` may dominate due to transposed convolution operations, and `W` may be relatively cheaper), recurrent neural networks (where the sequential dependence through time steps creates additional scheduling constraints), mixture-of-experts models (where `W` includes expert routing gradients and all-to-all communication that may not be local), and graph neural networks (where the backward pass structure depends on the graph topology). For each architecture, the study would measure the fraction of backward computation that can be decoupled from the cross-stage chain (the `T_W / (T_B + T_W)` ratio) and correlate this with the achieved bubble rate reduction. The hypothesis is that architectures with lower `T_W / (T_B + T_W)` ratios benefit less from B/W splitting because less computation is available to fill bubbles. If confirmed, this would establish a simple pre-screening metric: practitioners can measure the `T_W` fraction on their architecture to estimate potential gains before implementing the split. If disconfirmed (e.g., if scheduling W intelligently provides disproportionate benefits even when `W` is small), it would suggest the gains come more from the reordering flexibility than from the raw amount of slack computation, a more interesting result.

**Integration with activation recomputation to explore the memory-bubble-recomputation trade-off.** The paper operates in the regime where all activations are stored for backward computation. Many production training systems use activation recomputation (checkpointing) to trade computation for memory, storing only a subset of layer activations and recomputing others during the backward pass. This introduces a new dimension: the recomputation cost (`T_F` for each recomputed layer) is additional computation that could potentially be scheduled into bubbles, similar to W. A follow-up study would extend the automatic scheduling algorithm to jointly optimize the placement of (a) B operations, (b) W operations, and (c) recomputation operations within the pipeline schedule, under a combined memory constraint that includes both stored activations and recomputation overhead. The research question is whether B/W splitting provides additional benefit in recomputation regimes, or whether the recomputation operations themselves already fill the bubbles that W would otherwise occupy, making the split redundant. Concretely, for a GPT-3–style model with selective activation recomputation (every second transformer layer recomputed), measure throughput with 1F1B + recomputation, ZB-1p + recomputation, and ZB-2p + recomputation. If ZB methods still provide gains in the recomputation regime, it would establish that B/W splitting and activation recomputation are complementary optimizations. If not, it would narrow the scope of the paper's contribution to non-recomputation training settings.

**Large-scale evaluation on 100+ GPU clusters to test scalability claims.** The paper's largest configuration uses 32 GPUs (Table 3) and claims in the conclusion that ZB methods enable "optimal efficiency with a smaller number of microbatches (typically 3p is enough), which means more microbatches can be partitioned over data parallelism dimension. This brings a better scalability for the training of large models." This claim is not experimentally validated — the paper never tests a combined PP+DP setup where the freed microbatch capacity from ZB scheduling is reallocated to data parallelism. A follow-up study would scale to 128 or 256 GPUs (e.g., `p=32` pipeline stages × 4 or 8 data-parallel replicas), comparing 1F1B (which needs `m` large, leaving fewer microbatches for DP) against ZB-2p (which achieves near-zero bubble at `m ≈ 3p`, freeing microbatches for DP). The experiment would sweep the PP/DP split ratio at fixed total GPU count and measure end-to-end throughput, testing whether ZB scheduling's improved PP efficiency translates to better overall system throughput when DP communication (all-reduce for gradient aggregation) is included. The paper's Appendix A suggests that ZB schedules can overlap DP all-reduce with tail W computations by grouping W passes by parameter — this optimization would be tested at scale to validate whether the theoretical overlap is achievable under real network contention. A null result (where DP communication bottlenecks erase ZB's PP gains) would clarify that ZB scheduling primarily benefits PP-bound workloads, not all distributed training configurations.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Cost-efficient training of mid-scale models on limited GPU clusters.** For organizations training models in the 1–30B parameter range on clusters of 8–32 GPUs (a common configuration in industry research labs and academic groups), ZB-1p provides a 4–15% throughput improvement over the standard 1F1B schedule with **no increase in peak memory** (Table 4). This translates directly to 4–15% lower GPU-hours for the same training run, or equivalently, 4–15% faster iteration on model development. The barrier to adoption is low: ZB-1p uses the same memory as 1F1B, so no hardware upgrade is needed, and the bit-to-bit loss identity (Section 5.1) means no hyperparameter re-tuning is required. The primary engineering cost is implementing the B/W split in the training framework, which the paper's open-source Megatron-LM implementation provides as a reference. For teams already using Megatron-LM, adoption could be a drop-in schedule change.

**High-throughput training with relaxed memory budgets.** When training budgets permit provisioning GPUs with excess memory (e.g., using A100-80GB GPUs for models that would fit in 40GB), ZB-2p can convert that memory headroom into 10–30% higher throughput compared to 1F1B (Table 4, Figure 5). The 28.3B model on 32 GPUs shows the largest gain: ZB-2p achieves 0.99 samples/GPU/second versus 1F1B's 0.76 at `m=96`, a 30% improvement, while using 74 GB versus 43 GB of memory. This is a favorable trade-off for training runs where GPU memory is provisioned conservatively (e.g., to accommodate future model growth or to run multiple experiments simultaneously). The paper's Figure 7 provides guidance on how much memory is needed: bubble rates decrease roughly linearly until `M_limit ≈ 2p M_B`, after which diminishing returns set in. Practitioners can use this to decide how much memory to allocate to activation storage versus other uses (e.g., larger microbatches, activation recomputation avoidance).

**Memory-constrained deployment with ZB-V.** For training configurations where memory is the binding constraint — common when using older GPUs (V100-32GB, A40-48GB), when training very large models relative to GPU capacity, or when co-locating other workloads — ZB-V offers a compelling middle ground: near-ZB-2p throughput at 1F1B-level memory. Table 6 shows ZB-V achieving 1.01–1.06 samples/GPU/second for the 28.3B model on 32 GPUs at 71 GB, compared to ZB-2p's 0.99–1.01 at 74 GB and 1F1B's 0.82–0.95 at 69 GB — ZB-V captures most of ZB-2p's throughput gain while using memory close to 1F1B. The V-shaped interleaving pattern is more complex to implement than ZB-1p (it requires assigning two non-contiguous chunks per device), but the paper provides the algorithmic framework in Section 6. This is the prescription for practitioners who want zero-bubble-like throughput but cannot provision `2p M_B` of activation memory.

**Data parallelism scaling for frontier model training.** For training runs at the scale of hundreds or thousands of GPUs, where data parallelism (DP) and pipeline parallelism (PP) must be combined, ZB methods' ability to achieve near-zero bubble at modest microbatch counts (`m ≈ 3p` rather than `m ≈ 8p` or higher) has a second-order benefit: more of the global batch can be partitioned over the DP dimension, improving DP scalability. The paper's conclusion makes this claim explicitly but does not test it experimentally. In practice, this means that a training run with 256 GPUs could configure `p=32` PP stages and `d=8` DP replicas with `m=96` microbatches (ZB-2p regime, near-zero PP bubble) rather than needing `m=256` microbatches to suppress 1F1B bubbles, which would require `d=32` DP replicas with smaller per-replica microbatches and worse DP scaling efficiency due to increased all-reduce communication. Quantifying this benefit requires integrated PP+DP experiments (see Follow-Up Research above), but the mechanism is sound: ZB scheduling shifts the optimal PP/DP split point, enabling configurations that were previously infeasible due to PP bubble constraints.
