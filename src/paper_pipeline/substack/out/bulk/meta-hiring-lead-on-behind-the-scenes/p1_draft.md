Here are the takeaways from this Austen McDonald interview (Former Meta Hiring Lead, conducted hundreds of interviews, author of a book on behavioral interviewing):

**The full evaluation is relentless — from recruiter chat to post-offer hangout**

Austen lays out the entire hiring assembly line: a sourcer cold-contacts you or processes a referral, screening for years of experience and past titles (but smart enough to heavily down-level inflated titles from non-FAANG companies — "we would take them and downlevel them significantly… sometimes they’d be supporting 50 or 75 people as a manager, and we would give them a team of eight or ten"). A phone screen happens, then a hiring committee of influential engineers and managers reviews the packet to make a hire/no-hire and leveling decision, and finally a company-wide committee of engineering directors does "a gut check on a cross-company hiring bar."

The first call with a recruiter is pitched as "a little chat you don’t need to prepare for," but that’s misdirection. "It is evaluative. You should not just show up to that phone call emotionally disconnected or unprepared to talk about your past experience." Austen’s rule: assume everything is evaluative — the initial sourcer call, the end-of-interview questions, and especially the "meet the hiring manager" chat that gets framed as a formality after a verbal offer. "The hiring manager wants to see, do I want this person on my team? … it is certainly not just a formality or purely for you."

His advice on that first conversation: land your greatest hits immediately. "When you’re having a conversation with a recruiter and you’re talking about your past experience, you have got to land in that first tell-me-about-yourself." The recruiter is listening for business value, technical depth, and organizational complexity — "I organized across 100 engineers, this entire organization, to spend a 12-month performance improvement project." That sets the leveling bucket you enter. If you’re not crisp, delay the call and prepare.

Ryan notes the echoes between this and promo committees. Austen agrees: "Preparing for behavioral interviews will actually make you a better engineer." Most engineers, however, have the opposite problem from lying — "they’re not boosting themselves enough, not talking about the accomplishments."

**Leveling in practice: scope is the North Star**

Levels distill to a cascading hierarchy of ambiguity. "A new grad engineer is doing tasks. A mid-level engineer is doing a feature. A senior engineer is doing projects… A staff person is responsible for some kind of goal which needs multiple projects. A principal engineer is responsible for an entire organization which has many goals." At every stage, the interviewer is pattern-matching: does this candidate’s story show the organizational breadth, technical complexity, and leadership that matches the target level?

For a staff-level packet, Austen’s red-flag-first instinct is "I would go to the behavioral interview. I would want to understand what is the scope that this engineer has operated at." The coding and system-design interviews can’t easily distinguish a rock-solid senior from a staff engineer — you need the behavioral to see cross-team influence, conflict resolution at scale, and mentored growth of others. It’s why staff-and-above loops at Meta add extra behavioral rounds, sometimes cross-functional ones where a PM interviews you. "The behavioral interviewer especially will notice… and then they will reset their mind about what to expect from the interview."

For specialist engineers who aren’t org-spanning leaders, Austen’s framing is: "What would Steve Jobs say about this technological advancement? Why was this project so critical to the company?" You can also pattern-match by describing how others failed. "When I took on this project, I was the third owner, and this is where they had failed." It’s not bragging if you surface the *insight* that made their efforts unsuccessful, which shows the judgment of a senior person.

**Politically pivoting scope mid-process (and negotiating level)**

If you realize during the loop that you’re being assessed too low, "I would push to go ahead and try to get as much of that evaluation process done for the level that you would like to be hired for as much as possible." Anchor every conversation at that higher level in your tell-me-about-yourself. And in the behavioral, "your task as a candidate is to guide the interviewer towards that signal" — choose stories of the highest scope even if the interviewer’s question is more narrow.

He recommends a politician’s pivot when the question doesn’t map to your best story: "I could tell that story, but there is this other story about a longer conflict-resolution experience where I had to collect data and multiple people… that might be a more interesting story. Can I tell that one?" The interviewer may still constrain you, but you’ve flagged the better signal.

On compensation-level negotiation: don’t anchor yourself early. "Early anchoring in any negotiation is dangerous." Get multiple offers to land at a similar time. And if you’re in a jurisdiction like California, the recruiter is required to tell you the salary band — use that.

**The rubric, decode-select-deliver, and the CARL framework**

At Meta, five behavioral signal areas are assessed: driving results, embracing ambiguity, resolving conflicts, growing continuously, and communicating effectively. Every company will have their own variant — check with the recruiter, or reverse-engineer from their public values. Austen’s eight meta-categories cover most of them.

His core behavioral loop: **decode, select, deliver**. Listen to the question and identify what signal area they’re actually probing (often different from the surface question), select a story that hits high scope *and* that area, then deliver it with pace. He dislikes STAR ("I don’t think the difference between a situation and a task is super relevant") and recommends **CARL: context, actions, results, learnings**. The learnings component is critical for senior candidates: "I’m looking to see whether or not you can look at your past experience, understand what made you successful or unsuccessful, and then apply that going forward."

Ryan observes that many candidates talk past the interviewer’s probe, missing the chance to help fill out the rubric. Austen calls it "being a partner with the interviewer. The interviewer is trying to accomplish a task. You need to help them get to the signal."

He also introduces **trunk / branch / leaf** for pacing: a big "tell me about an ambiguous project" is a trunk question — give a longer CARL story. A follow-up like "who did you talk to?" is a branch — give a compressed answer with the relevant action. A specific fact-check is a leaf — answer and move on. "Your ability to detect what kind of question, how long the interviewer wants to listen… is an indicator of seniority."

**Mistakes that down-level senior candidates**

1. **Choosing stories that don’t match the target scope.** Scope outweighs relevance in story selection. "If you stay focused on answering their specific question… maybe you have a story about a conflict with a product manager, but the bigger story is a conflict with the director of engineering." His priority order for story choice: scope first, relevance second, recency third, uniqueness fourth. Recency is flexible — "two to three years for sure is okay; beyond three or four years, you have to have a really good reason."

2. **Giving too much context and not enough action.** "If it’s been 30 seconds since you have told me something you did, rethink that." Work stories aren’t entertainment — keep verbs flowing.

3. **Opening themselves to uncharitable interpretations.** "You’re a senior engineer, codebase had technical debt, so we decided to do X — but if you’re the senior engineer, how did we get this technical debt? It’s your fault." Preemptively explain the trade-off decision that led there.

4. **Neglecting the non-technical parts.** Mentorship, cross-team influence, managing up, aligning roadmaps — these are half the job at staff+. "How did I make the team better? How did I make the org better? Those things are oftentimes forgotten."

5. **Telling long multi-year stories without a table of contents.** Austen suggests front-loading a quick outline: "Here are the five interesting parts — how I initiated it with management, how I aligned stakeholders, the hard technical problems, the complex rollout." It gives the interviewer a map and a way back after interruptions.

**Preparing for specific AI companies**

For OpenAI’s "Feel the AGI" value, you need to project optimism about what AI can do. For Anthropic’s "Hold Light and Shade," you must show you’ve considered both positive and negative implications. So when telling an AI-related project story to Anthropic, "you would really want to mention how you thought about the potential negative implications, how you went about mitigating those. If they don’t hear those things, they’re not going to feel comfortable." Generic behavioral prep isn’t enough — you need to weave in the cultural zeitgeist.

**The subjective, human layer — and how to handle it**

Rapport-building scales with level. "With a more junior candidate… they’re trying to reduce this ‘do I want to have a beer with this person’ vibe by structuring it. But as you get more senior, they’re expecting you to influence other people on the job." This manifests as **casual excellence** — calm yet sharp, able to banter for two minutes then dive into intense technical discussion.

Austen’s internal-work advice: first, change your beliefs. "Tell yourself, give yourself an internal belief structure that makes it okay for you to make mistakes in this environment." Second, believe the interviewer really wants you to be great — "they’re spending their time interviewing you. They really want to find somebody who’s great." That shifts your posture from defensive to collaborative.

Match the interviewer’s energy. If they skip pleasantries and jump straight to "tell me about a time you had an ambiguous problem," you know they want efficiency. If they chat weather and sports for three minutes, mirror that.

First impressions dominate: "I am making a decision within the first 10, 15 minutes of the interview… if I’ve made a decision in the first 10 minutes, it’s pretty hard for you to overcome that." Most of the time it’s because the candidate chose wrong stories, couldn’t communicate, or revealed they hadn’t operated at the expected level.

**The end matters: questions as signal**

The "do you have any questions for me?" block is absolutely evaluative. Don’t ask "day in the life" fluff. Ask what matters to *you* — if career growth is key, ask the hiring manager "tell me about a time you helped someone at my level get to the next level." If product trajectory worries you, ask about that. "The interviewer is assessing you on that. You can leave the impression that you are engaged, you’re excited about the role, you’re really evaluating whether it’s right for you."

**Referrals: the hidden accelerator (especially at the bubble)**

Referrals matter most when you’re on the border. "If you are on the bubble… maybe you flubbed a couple of interviews, having a referral can get you a follow-up." Level and depth of the referrer matter — a VP staking their personal reputation because they directly worked with you carries enormous weight. Austen’s own Meta entry likely hinged on a school friend who vouched for him after he flubbed a screen. When asking for a referral, "write the referral for them. Provide them information they should pass on to the hiring committee." But a referral won’t overturn a unanimous no-hire consensus at IC levels — it’s a nudge, not a bulldozer.

**Coding vs. system design vs. behavioral prep by level**

Most people over-index on coding. For junior engineers, that’s correct — technical signal is paramount. For mid-level, AI is shifting the landscape: "we are looking for mid-level engineers who can own problems end to end, and how do you see that ownership? In the behavioral." At senior and above, "a lot of it’s going to come down to the behavioral interview… really what’s going to get you hired is going to be shining on those behavioral sides." The technical interviews become a bar to clear, not the differentiator. Even a flubbed coding round can be survivable at staff+ because "we don’t expect you to be doing as much day-to-day coding."

**Creating scope as the engine of promotion**

The best advice Austen ever received: "if you want this M2 role, you have to create an M2 scope team. You have to accomplish and solve problems that are of this scope." It’s not just doing your current thing better. He applied this by questioning: "What is your VP saying about your project? If your VP is not going to be talking to any of their peers about what you’re doing, am I doing the right thing?" So work backwards: "what do I want the organization’s leadership to say about what we’ve accomplished, and then go figure out a way to do that thing."

**His own M2 arc and regrets**

Austen’s M2 promo came from solving a deeply ambiguous problem — improving diversity hiring — while also leading iOS and Android recruiting pipelines at scale ("scaling yourself across more than just the teams you’re directly responsible for"). He later realized his biggest regret was the anxiety and bitterness he carried by not being honest about his trade-offs. "I was frustrated that so-and-so got promoted… but I wasn’t honest with myself that I prioritized other things. I prioritized my family, I prioritized community. That has consequences." Once he aligned on his values — asking "what do I want my kids to say about me in 10 years?" — the bitterness subsided. His advice to his younger self: "Don’t give into fear." Imposter syndrome made him avoid industry internships, slowing his understanding of how big orgs and large codebases operate.
