# PRISM: Pre-alignment via Black-Box On-Policy Distillation for Multimodal RL

**ArXiv:** [2604.28123](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.28123)

## 🎯 Pitch

Supervised fine-tuning cripples downstream reinforcement learning in multimodal models by introducing entangled perception and reasoning drift—but sandwiching an adversarial on-policy distillation stage between SFT and RL, using separate discriminator experts for visual grounding and logic, erases this drift and boosts RL gains by +4–6 accuracy points. Crucially, this alignment works *without* teacher logits, reshaping the policy purely through black-box response-level feedback from a Mixture-of-Experts judge.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **PRISM** (PRe-alignment via black-box on-policy dIStillation for Multimodal reinforcement learning), a three-stage post-training pipeline that inserts an explicit distribution-alignment stage between supervised fine-tuning and reinforcement learning to repair the heterogeneous drift—spanning both perception errors and reasoning failures—that SFT introduces in large multimodal models. Validated on Qwen3-VL-4B/8B across seven multimodal benchmarks (MathVista, MathVerse, MathVision, WeMath, MMMU, MMMU-Pro, HallusionBench), PRISM formulates alignment as a black-box adversarial on-policy distillation game using a **Mixture-of-Experts discriminator** with dedicated perception and reasoning experts (evaluating visual descriptions and reasoning traces separately rather than collapsing them into a single scalar reward), providing disentangled corrective signals that steer the post-SFT policy toward the supervision distribution without requiring access to teacher logits. PRISM+GRPO outperforms the standard SFT→GRPO baseline by +4.4 and +6.0 average accuracy points on the 4B and 8B scales respectively, with the gains persisting across multiple RL algorithms (GRPO, DAPO, GSPO), establishing that a dedicated alignment stage unlocks substantially stronger downstream RLVR gains only when the pre-alignment discriminator can decompose multimodal drift into its constituent visual-grounding and logical-reasoning error modes.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: SFT Is a Silent Saboteur of Downstream RL

The standard post-training recipe for large multimodal models (LMMs) follows a seemingly logical two-stage progression: first, supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on curated demonstrations to teach the model what good reasoning looks like; second, reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) to optimize the model toward producing correct final answers. This pipeline has produced state-of-the-art results across reasoning benchmarks and is widely treated as the default approach (Section 1).

PRISM challenges a fundamental assumption baked into this pipeline: **that SFT reliably prepares the model for downstream RL**. The paper argues, drawing on growing evidence in the literature, that SFT can actively *harm* the model's readiness for RL optimization. Specifically, SFT introduces what the authors call **distributional drift** — a mismatch where the post-SFT policy deviates from both (a) the distribution of the supervision data it was trained to imitate, and (b) the model's own original distribution before SFT. The model ends up in a "compromised state" (Section 1) that satisfies neither its native capabilities nor the target behavior it was supposed to learn.

This is not just a theoretical concern. The paper cites recent work showing that SFT can degrade a model's reasoning capabilities when measured against its pre-SFT baseline, particularly for strong models that already possess competent reasoning priors. Kang et al. (2025) and Zhang et al. (2026a) demonstrate that SFT training can displace the model's native strengths rather than augmenting them — the model learns surface-level patterns that mimic the demonstration policy without internalizing faithful reasoning. The result is a policy that *looks* well-trained (high SFT validation scores) but performs poorly when subsequently optimized with RL.

### Why Multimodal Models Suffer Amplified Drift

The problem is especially acute for LMMs because distributional drift in the multimodal setting is **fundamentally heterogeneous** in a way that does not occur in text-only models. The paper identifies two qualitatively distinct failure modes that emerge from SFT (Section 1):

**Perception drift:** The model's visual grounding becomes imprecise. Descriptions of image contents shift toward surface-level approximations — for instance, describing "a star-like outline" instead of "a 6-pointed star," or "five outer pentagonal arms" instead of "five pentagonal arms with visible inner valleys" (Figure 1). These perceptual deviations may seem minor, but they distort the factual premises upon which all subsequent reasoning depends.

**Reasoning drift:** The model's logical deduction becomes inconsistent with the supervision distribution. It may count "visible outer corners" rather than the vertex count that the problem definition requires, leading to a different underlying formula — e.g., deriving $V(n) = (n+1)(n+2)$ instead of the correct $V(n) = (n+2)(n+3)$ (Figure 1). The reasoning *sounds* plausible but follows a subtly wrong logical path.

These two error modes interact in a compounding way: perception errors distort the premises, and reasoning errors then build on those distorted premises, amplifying the overall failure. This is precisely what makes the multimodal case more fragile than the text-only case. In text-only reasoning, the premises (the problem statement) are fixed and unambiguous — drift manifests as a single, relatively uniform degradation in reasoning quality. In multimodal reasoning, the model must *jointly* see and think, and errors in either dimension cascade through the pipeline.

Critically, a standard RLVR stage that only optimizes for final-answer correctness cannot disentangle these two error sources. If a model produces a wrong answer because it misdescribed the image, RLVR sees only "wrong answer" and provides an undifferentiated negative signal. The model may learn to produce correct answers without ever fixing the underlying perceptual imprecision — or worse, learn to guess answers in a way that circumvents faithful visual grounding altogether (a phenomenon documented in related work, e.g., Liu et al., 2025a).

### Where Prior Approaches Fall Short

The paper identifies specific limitations in how the field currently handles the SFT-to-RL transition:

**The standard SFT→RL pipeline ignores the intermediate state entirely.** Most post-training research focuses on improving either SFT (through better data curation, reweighting strategies, or regularization — e.g., Qin & Springenberg, 2025; Zhu et al., 2025) or RLVR (through better algorithms that redesign clipping, advantage estimation, or reward structures — e.g., Shao et al., 2024; Yu et al., 2025; Zheng et al., 2025). The gap *between* these two stages is left unexamined. The assumption is that SFT produces a "good enough" policy, and RLVR will handle whatever deficiencies remain. PRISM's key insight is that this assumption fails: RLVR starting from a drifted SFT checkpoint operates on a distorted foundation, and the gains RL can deliver are fundamentally limited by the quality of that foundation.

**Existing multimodal RL methods add perception rewards but don't fix the drift source.** Recent work has recognized that vanilla RLVR neglects visual perception fidelity, and proposed perception-aware reward signals — using judge LLMs to evaluate whether reasoning is grounded in visual evidence (Xiao et al., 2025), requiring evidence-anchored dual-branch reasoning (Zhang et al., 2025a), or incentivizing differential visual reasoning (Gao et al., 2026). These methods are improvements, but they all operate *within the RL stage*: they modify the reward function to penalize perception failures during RL optimization. They do not address the fact that the policy entering RL may already have systematic perception errors baked in from SFT. PRISM's position is that adding perception rewards during RL is treating symptoms rather than the root cause — the drift itself needs to be repaired before RL begins.

**On-policy distillation exists but has never been used as a pre-RL alignment stage.** On-policy distillation (OPD) was developed to address the train-inference distribution mismatch in knowledge distillation, where a student model trained on teacher-generated outputs (off-policy) behaves differently when generating its own outputs (on-policy). Methods like GKD (Agarwal et al., 2024) and MiniLLM (Gu et al., 2024) train the student on rollouts sampled from its own current policy, using divergence objectives (e.g., reverse KL) to match the teacher's distribution. However, these methods treat distillation as a *terminal* objective — the distilled model is the final product. No prior work has positioned OPD as an intermediate alignment stage whose purpose is to prepare the policy for subsequent RL optimization. This is PRISM's novel framing (Section 1): "the first framework to reposition on-policy distillation as a standalone intermediate alignment stage between SFT and RLVR."

**Existing OPD methods rely on teacher logits or single discriminators, both of which are limiting for multimodal alignment.** Standard OPD requires logit-level access to the teacher model (to compute forward/reverse KL divergences), making it inapplicable when supervision comes from proprietary black-box models like Gemini 3 Flash, which expose only text outputs. Furthermore, even if logit access were available, a single monolithic distillation loss — whether from a divergence measure or a single discriminator — cannot provide the *decoupled* feedback that multimodal drift requires. A single scalar reward collapses perception errors and reasoning errors into one number: if the policy improves visual grounding but regresses on reasoning, the scalar reward may not change, and the gradient signal becomes uninformative. PRISM addresses both limitations: the adversarial formulation requires only response-level samples (no teacher logits), and the MoE discriminator provides separate rewards for visual descriptions and reasoning traces (Section 3.2).

**The closest prior work, VOLD, couples distillation with RL rather than decoupling them.** VOLD (Bousselham et al., 2025) combines GRPO with logit-based on-policy distillation from a text-only teacher into a single unified training objective. PRISM differs in three critical ways (Section 2.2): it separates alignment from RL as a standalone stage (decoupling rather than combining), it operates without teacher logits (adversarial rather than divergence-based), and it provides decoupled feedback through separate experts (MoE rather than single discriminator). The decoupling is not a minor architectural choice — it reflects the paper's core argument that alignment and outcome optimization serve different purposes and should not be conflated. Alignment asks "does this response look like it came from the supervision distribution?" while RL asks "does this response lead to the correct answer?" Conflating them risks the model learning to produce correct answers without matching the supervision distribution's reasoning quality, which may hurt generalization.

### The Data Challenge: Existing Public Multimodal Datasets Are Insufficient for Distribution Alignment

The paper identifies a practical bottleneck that motivates their data curation effort. Distribution alignment requires supervision data where each sample contains not just a correct final answer, but a **complete, high-fidelity reasoning trajectory with precise visual grounding**. The discriminator needs to learn what "good" visual descriptions and reasoning traces look like so it can provide meaningful corrective signals. Existing public multimodal datasets are often inadequate for this purpose: many contain brief answers, incomplete reasoning traces, or imprecise visual descriptions (Section 3.1). If the supervision pool itself is noisy, the discriminator cannot learn to distinguish genuine quality from superficial patterns.

Furthermore, alignment is most valuable when the supervision data targets the *hardest* problems — problems where the model's current policy deviates substantially from the target distribution, and where the gap between perception and reasoning errors is largest. On easy problems, the model already produces supervision-like outputs, so the discriminator's signal is weak and the alignment stage has little work to do. This motivates the paper's specific data strategy: curating 113K demonstrations from Gemini 3 Flash specifically targeting problems with near-zero pass rates under strong contemporary models, with dense visual grounding and step-by-step reasoning explicitly required in the distillation template (Appendix B and C).

The scale question is also carefully considered. The paper supplements this curated corpus with 1.26M publicly available demonstrations from the same Gemini model family (Leng et al., 2025). This is not just about having more data — it reflects an understanding of the relationship between SFT and alignment. SFT needs *breadth*: a large, diverse corpus to broadly initialize the policy toward the target domain. Alignment needs *depth*: high-quality, carefully annotated samples that the discriminator can use to learn fine-grained distinctions. The 107K curated samples go to SFT (plus the 1.26M public), while the 6K highest-quality samples are reserved for the alignment and RL stages (Table 4). This separation of concerns — broad initialization followed by targeted correction — is architecturally analogous to the pipeline structure itself.

### How PRISM Positions Itself

PRISM does not propose a new SFT method or a new RL algorithm. It proposes a **new pipeline architecture** — inserting a third stage between two existing stages that are already widely used. The paper's framing is that both SFT and RLVR are individually well-developed, but their interaction is poorly understood and currently suboptimal.

The central theoretical claim (Section 3.2.1) is that what matters for downstream RL is not just whether the model can produce correct answers after SFT, but whether the *distribution* of the model's rollouts matches the supervision distribution along both perceptual and reasoning dimensions. A model that achieves high SFT accuracy by memorizing surface-level patterns (overfitting to the training distribution) may have worse rollout-distribution alignment than a model with slightly lower SFT accuracy but more faithful reasoning. This is consistent with the broader finding that SFT can produce misleadingly high validation scores while degrading the model's underlying capabilities (Kang et al., 2025).

PRISM's adversarial OPD formulation is designed to optimize exactly this distribution-level match, not token-level accuracy. The discriminator asks "does this rollout look like it came from the supervision pool?" and the policy is optimized to make the answer "yes" — but the discriminator gets to probe both what the model *sees* (visual descriptions) and how it *thinks* (reasoning traces), catching errors that a final-answer-only check would miss. This is a fundamentally different objective from SFT's next-token prediction or RLVR's outcome correctness, and the paper argues it fills a gap that neither stage addresses.

A key part of the positioning is the observation that **the SFT-induced drift is worse for stronger models** (Section 4.2). On Qwen3-VL-8B, SFT degrades the Instruct checkpoint by 5.2 average points (from 63.3 to 58.1), while on 4B the degradation is only 2.9 points (from 59.7 to 56.8). The standard SFT→RLVR pipeline on 8B barely recovers the original Instruct performance (63.3 for both Instruct and SFT→GRPO), while PRISM+GRPO exceeds it by over 5 points. This pattern — larger models suffering more from SFT drift — is consistent with the intuition that strong models have more to lose: their native reasoning distributions are more competent, so token-level imitation of an external demonstration policy is more likely to displace rather than supplement. Weak models gain substantially from SFT because their native distributions are poor; strong models risk degradation. PRISM's alignment stage disproportionately helps the latter case, making it particularly relevant as base models continue to improve.

Finally, the paper explicitly connects to the broader literature on multi-stage post-training pipelines (Yang et al., 2025c), arguing that each stage serves an indispensable role. SFT provides initial competence and enough proximity to the target distribution for adversarial training to be possible. Alignment corrects the residual distributional mismatch that SFT leaves behind, along both perceptual and reasoning axes. RLVR then optimizes for outcome correctness from a well-aligned foundation. Removing any stage causes substantial degradation (Section 4.3, Table 2), supporting the claim that the three-stage architecture is not an incremental tweak but a necessary restructuring.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

PRISM is a **three-stage post-training pipeline** that adds an intermediate "pre-alignment" stage between the standard supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and reinforcement learning (RL) stages for large multimodal models. It solves the problem that SFT introduces **heterogeneous distributional drift** — the model's outputs deviate from the supervision distribution in two qualitatively different ways (perception errors and reasoning errors) — and that standard RL cannot repair this drift because it only optimizes for final-answer correctness. The solution is an **adversarial on-policy distillation game** where a Mixture-of-Experts discriminator with dedicated perception and reasoning experts provides decoupled corrective signals, steering the post-SFT policy's rollout distribution back toward the supervision distribution along both visual-grounding and logical-deduction axes simultaneously, before RL begins its outcome-focused optimization.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The PRISM pipeline consists of five major components connected in a strict three-stage sequence:

1. **SFT Cold-Start Policy** — A multimodal LLM (e.g., Qwen3-VL) fine-tuned on 1.37M curated demonstrations, producing an initial policy `$\pi_{\text{sft}}$` that can generate structured responses (visual caption + reasoning trace + answer) but whose rollout distribution is drifted from the supervision distribution.

2. **Supervision Pool** — A held-out set of 6K high-quality demonstrations (from the same Gemini 3 Flash source) featuring precise visual descriptions and step-by-step reasoning traces. These serve as the "target distribution" that the alignment stage tries to match. Importantly, only the response text is available — no teacher logits are accessible.

3. **MoE Discriminator** — A Mixture-of-Experts model (assembled from four Qwen3-VL-2B modules with top-2 routing) that contains two specialized experts: a **Perception Expert `$D_v$`** that scores the visual description portion of a response for grounding fidelity, and a **Reasoning Expert `$D_r$`** that scores the reasoning trace portion for logical consistency. The discriminator is trained to assign higher scores to supervision responses than to policy rollouts, providing a learned reward signal that differentiates perception quality from reasoning quality.

4. **Alignment Optimizer (GRPO without KL penalty)** — A policy-gradient update mechanism that optimizes the post-SFT policy to maximize the discriminator's combined reward `$r(x,y) = \alpha D_v(x,c) + (1-\alpha)D_r(x,t)$`. The policy samples `$N=16$` rollouts per prompt, computes group-normalized advantages, and updates toward rollouts scored as more supervision-like. Critically, KL regularization against the SFT checkpoint is disabled (`$\beta=0$`) to allow the policy to shift freely toward the target distribution.

5. **RLVR Optimizer** — A standard outcome-based RL stage (GRPO, DAPO, or GSPO) applied to the aligned checkpoint, using deterministic verifiable rewards (answer accuracy + format compliance) on a difficulty-filtered subset of ~2K problems.

**Information flow:** The SFT checkpoint enters Stage 2 (alignment), where each training step alternates between (a) updating the discriminator via Bradley-Terry loss on supervision vs. policy rollouts, and (b) updating the policy via GRPO using the discriminator's MoE reward. After 500 steps, the aligned policy enters Stage 3, where standard RLVR optimizes for correct final answers. The discriminator is discarded after alignment — it serves only to reshape the policy distribution, not to score final answers.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First, the Stage 1 SFT procedure** — what data is used, how it is constructed, and why existing public datasets are insufficient — because SFT provides the initial policy that alignment must correct, and the quality of this initialization determines the difficulty of the alignment task.
- **Second, the MoE discriminator architecture and training** — how the perception and reasoning experts are structured, initialized, and updated via Bradley-Terry loss — because the discriminator is the core mechanism that provides decoupled corrective signals, and its design choices (MoE decomposition, initialization strategy, on-policy updates) are central to PRISM's effectiveness.
- **Third, the adversarial alignment game** — how the policy and discriminator interact, the policy's GRPO objective with group-normalized advantages, and why KL regularization is deliberately removed — because this is the mathematical heart of the paper and where the distribution-level alignment actually happens.
- **Fourth, the Stage 3 RLVR transition** — how the reward switches from learned MoE discriminator to deterministic verifiable reward, and how the RL training set is difficulty-filtered — because this connects the alignment stage's distribution-level objective to the downstream outcome-level objective.
- **Fifth, key hyperparameters and training configurations** — learning rates, temperatures, group sizes, the `$\alpha$` balance weight, training step counts — because these concrete numbers define what "PRISM" actually is in practice and are essential for reproducibility.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is a **pipeline architecture paper** whose core idea is that inserting a distribution-alignment stage between SFT and RLVR — implemented as an adversarial on-policy distillation game with a MoE discriminator that separately evaluates visual descriptions and reasoning traces — repairs the heterogeneous drift that SFT introduces, and that this repaired initialization enables substantially stronger downstream RL gains than the standard two-stage pipeline.

#### Stage 1: Cold-Start Supervised Fine-Tuning

**Purpose and positioning.** Stage 1 of PRISM performs standard supervised fine-tuning to obtain an initial policy `$\pi_{\text{sft}}$` that the alignment stage will subsequently correct. The paper explicitly frames SFT as a necessary but insufficient first step: it "serves as a cold start that equips the model with an initial multimodal reasoning policy" (Section 3.1), narrowing the gap between the base model's outputs and the supervision distribution enough for adversarial training to be viable. Without SFT, the discriminator trivially separates policy rollouts from supervision, and the policy receives no useful gradient — a finding confirmed in the ablation where removing SFT causes a catastrophic 16.8-point average drop (Section 4.3, Table 2).

**Why existing public datasets are inadequate.** The paper identifies a specific data quality requirement that motivates their curation effort: since the same supervision data is later reused in the alignment stage as the reference distribution, every sample must contain "not only a correct final answer but also a complete reasoning trajectory with accurate visual grounding" (Section 3.1). The discriminator learns what "good" perception and reasoning look like by comparing policy rollouts against these reference demonstrations. If the reference demonstrations themselves have imprecise visual descriptions or incomplete reasoning traces, the discriminator learns a noisy target, and the alignment objective becomes unreliable. Existing public multimodal datasets are described as "often inadequate for this purpose, as many contain brief answers, incomplete reasoning traces, or imprecise visual descriptions."

**Curated data construction (113K samples).** The authors construct their own corpus through a multi-stage pipeline targeting the hardest unsolved problems (Section 3.1, Appendix B):

1. **Source preparation:** Problems are sourced from publicly available multimodal reasoning benchmarks spanning mathematical reasoning, scientific diagram understanding, chart interpretation, and spatial reasoning. Problems containing multiple sub-questions bundled under a single image are disambiguated by prompting an LMM to identify which sub-question corresponds to the ground-truth answer, discarding the rest. This ensures clean one-to-one mappings between each problem and its ground truth.

2. **Distillation from Gemini 3 Flash:** Each problem is sent to Gemini 3 Flash with a carefully designed prompt (Appendix C, Figure 7) that requires three output sections in strict XML format: `<caption>` for fine-grained visual description (bullet-point format, covering image type, subjects with counts, attributes including colors and shapes, transcribed text, and spatial relationships), `thinking` for step-by-step logical deduction (bridging visual facts from the caption to the answer, explicitly checking units and formats, referencing specific bullets), and `<answer>` for the concise final answer. The prompt includes explicit rules for each section — for instance, the caption rules require "if details are ambiguous, state 'unclear'. No assumptions," and the thinking rules require "do not simply state the final result until the end."

3. **Multi-stage filtering:** Raw generations pass through three sequential filters. First, a **truncation and failure filter** removes responses that exceed the maximum output length or where the API call fails. Second, a **format filter** verifies that each response contains the required XML structure (`<caption>`, `thinking`, `<answer>`) with non-empty content in each section. Third, a **correctness filter** uses an LLM-as-judge (Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct, with a detailed evaluation prompt shown in Appendix C, Figure 8) to compare the generated answer against the ground truth, discarding responses deemed incorrect.

4. **Iterative regeneration:** Samples that fail any filter are re-submitted to Gemini 3 Flash. Format/truncation failures are regenerated with the same prompt. Correctness failures are regenerated with the ground-truth answer appended to the prompt, guiding the model toward a correct solution while preserving the requirement for detailed reasoning. All regenerated samples pass through the same three-stage filter again. This iterative process "substantially improves the overall yield without sacrificing quality" (Appendix B).

**Data composition and splitting.** From the resulting 113K verified samples, the authors randomly hold out 6K samples for the alignment and RL stages and assign the remaining 107K to the SFT pool (Appendix B, Table 4). The paper notes that the 6K reserved samples "possess the highest annotation quality" (Section 3.1), making them suitable as the supervision reference for distribution alignment — the discriminator needs clean, unambiguous examples to learn meaningful distinctions.

**Supplementing with public data (1.26M samples).** The paper argues that "a policy trained on insufficient data remains far from the target distribution, placing an excessive corrective burden on the downstream alignment stage" (Section 3.1). SFT needs breadth to broadly initialize the model toward the target domain, while alignment needs depth (high-quality samples) to learn fine-grained distributional distinctions. To provide this breadth, the authors supplement their 107K curated samples with 1.26M publicly available demonstrations from the same Gemini model family (Leng et al., 2025), yielding a combined SFT corpus of approximately 1.37M samples. The paper explicitly notes that using demonstrations from the same model family ensures distributional consistency between the SFT data and the alignment reference data.

**SFT training configuration.** Training is performed using LlamaFactory (Zheng et al., 2024) with full-parameter fine-tuning on the language model while freezing the vision tower and multimodal projector (Appendix A, Table 3). The key configuration is: AdamW optimizer, learning rate `$1\times 10^{-5}$`, cosine learning rate schedule with warmup ratio 0.1, training for 1 epoch, cutoff length of 8192 tokens with online stream packing to maximize throughput, and DeepSpeed ZeRO-2 for distributed training. The system prompt (Appendix C, Figure 6) enforces the structured three-part output format across all stages: "Your output must strictly follow this XML format: `<caption>...<caption>` `thinking...` `<answer>...</answer>`."

**What SFT achieves and does not achieve.** After SFT, the model can produce structured responses with visual descriptions and reasoning traces, and its outputs are closer to the supervision distribution than the base model's were. However, as shown in Figure 4 (left panels), the post-SFT response distribution remains "visibly mismatched" from the supervision distribution along both reasoning complexity (number of reasoning steps) and perceptual granularity (number of descriptive items in the caption). In particular, the caption-side proxy tends to overshoot — the post-SFT model produces more descriptive items than the supervision data contains. This is the distributional drift that the alignment stage is designed to repair.

#### MoE Discriminator: Architecture, Initialization, and Training

**Why a Mixture-of-Experts discriminator.** The core challenge is that multimodal distributional drift is heterogeneous: visual grounding errors (e.g., describing "a star-like outline" instead of "a 6-pointed star") and reasoning errors (e.g., counting visible outer corners instead of all vertices) are qualitatively different phenomena. A single monolithic discriminator produces a single scalar reward that collapses both error types into one number. The paper argues this is "too coarse to capture these two error modes simultaneously" (Section 3.2.2) because improvements along one axis can mask regression along the other. For instance, if the policy produces a response with better reasoning but worse visual grounding than the previous iteration, a single scalar reward might not change, providing no useful gradient signal. The MoE design avoids this by letting dedicated experts specialize on their respective domains, providing **decoupled corrective signals** that separately address perception and reasoning drift.

**Discriminator architecture.** The MoE discriminator is constructed by assembling four Qwen3-VL-2B models as expert modules with top-2 routing (Section 4.1). This follows the Qwen3-VL-MoE architecture (Bai et al., 2025). Top-2 routing means that for each input, only two of the four experts are activated, providing sparse computation that scales sub-linearly with total expert count. The two expert scores `$D_v$` and `$D_r$` are obtained by passing different portions of the response through this single MoE model separately: the visual description `$c$` (the `<caption>` section) is routed to produce the perception score `$D_v(x, c)$`, and the reasoning trace `$t$` (the `thinking` section) is routed to produce the reasoning score `$D_r(x, t)$`. The combined discriminator reward is:

$$r(x, y) = \alpha \cdot D_v(x, c) + (1 - \alpha) \cdot D_r(x, t)$$

where `$\alpha$` controls the trade-off between perceptual and reasoning feedback. Here, `$x$` is the multimodal input (image + question), `$y$` is the complete response consisting of visual description `$c$` and reasoning trace `$t$`, `$D_v(x, c)$` is the perception expert's scalar score for how well the visual description is grounded in the image, and `$D_r(x, t)$` is the reasoning expert's scalar score for the logical consistency of the reasoning trace.

**What it computes:** it is a weighted sum of two expert scores, each produced by the same MoE backbone but operating on different portions of the response. The perception expert `$D_v$` receives the image `$x$` and the visual description `$c$` and evaluates whether the description faithfully captures the image content. The reasoning expert `$D_r$` receives the same image `$x$` and the reasoning trace `$t$` and evaluates whether the deduction is logically sound and consistent with the visual evidence. The weight `$\alpha$` interpolates between pure perception feedback (`$\alpha=1$`) and pure reasoning feedback (`$\alpha=0$`). With `$\alpha=0.5$` (the default from Appendix A, Table 3), both experts contribute equally to the policy gradient.

**Why this form:** the additive decomposition is designed to provide separable gradient signals. When the policy's visual description `$c$` improves but its reasoning `$t$` degrades, the perception expert's score increases while the reasoning expert's score decreases. A single monolithic discriminator would see these two effects conflated into one scalar — the reward might stay flat, and the policy would receive no signal that its reasoning needs correction. The MoE decomposition ensures that policy updates can target specific failure modes: the gradient from `$\alpha D_v$` improves visual grounding, and the gradient from `$(1-\alpha)D_r$` improves reasoning, independently. The fixed weighting `$\alpha=0.5$` is a design choice that treats both axes as equally important; exploring learned or dynamic weighting is left to future work.

**Discriminator training objective.** The discriminator is trained to separate supervision responses from policy rollouts using a Bradley-Terry loss applied independently to each expert. For each expert `$k \in \{v, r\}$`:

$$L_{D_k} = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y^+, y^-) \sim \mathcal{T}} \left[ \log \sigma \left( D_k(x, y^+_k) - D_k(x, y^-_k) \right) \right]$$

where `$\mathcal{T}$` denotes the supervision data from which reference pairs are drawn, `$y^+$` is a reference response from the supervision pool with components `$y^+_k$` (the `$k$`-th component: visual description when `$k=v$`, reasoning trace when `$k=r$`), `$y^-$` is a policy rollout sampled from the current policy `$G(\cdot|x)$` with corresponding components `$y^-_k$`, and `$\sigma(\cdot)$` is the sigmoid function.

**What it computes:** for each expert independently, the Bradley-Terry loss measures how well the expert can distinguish the supervision response from the policy rollout. The term `$D_k(x, y^+_k) - D_k(x, y^-_k)$` is the score gap — positive when the expert correctly assigns higher scores to supervision, negative otherwise. The sigmoid `$\sigma(\cdot)$` maps this gap to a probability in `$(0, 1)$`, and `$-\log \sigma(\cdot)$` penalizes the expert when this probability is low. The expectation is over randomly sampled pairs of supervision and policy responses. The result is a non-negative scalar loss for each expert, minimized when the expert consistently assigns higher scores to supervision responses than to policy rollouts.

**Why this form:** the Bradley-Terry loss is the standard objective for training preference-based reward models. It only requires relative comparisons (supervision vs. policy) rather than absolute quality scores, which is appropriate because the absolute "quality" of a response is ill-defined — what matters is whether it looks like it came from the supervision distribution. The independence of the two losses is crucial: each expert learns its own discrimination boundary without interference from the other expert's domain. The perception expert `$D_v$` learns to distinguish supervision-quality visual descriptions from drifted ones, and the reasoning expert `$D_r$` learns to distinguish supervision-quality reasoning traces from drifted ones. Their training signals never mix.

**Discriminator initialization.** The paper identifies a practical challenge: "a pretrained LMM before alignment remains far from the supervision distribution, making its responses trivially separable from reference demonstrations. Under such a large gap, the discriminator can quickly saturate, leaving the policy with uninformative training signals" (Section 3.2.3, citing Goodfellow et al., 2014; Arjovsky et al., 2017). This is a well-known failure mode in GAN training where the discriminator becomes too strong too quickly, providing near-zero reward gradients to the generator.

To prevent this, both experts are initialized from the same pretrained backbone (the same architecture that will be used for the MoE discriminator) and warm-started on their designated components: `$D_v$` on preference pairs constructed from visual descriptions (where the "chosen" description is from supervision and the "rejected" is a policy rollout), and `$D_r$` on preference pairs constructed from reasoning traces. This warm-start narrows the initial capability gap: the discriminator begins training already somewhat calibrated to distinguish supervision-quality from policy-quality outputs, rather than starting from random initialization where the two distributions are trivially separable. An auxiliary load-balancing loss (Fedus et al., 2022) is applied during this warm-start phase to prevent expert collapse — the phenomenon where one expert dominates and the others become unused, defeating the purpose of the MoE design.

**On-policy discriminator updates.** Importantly, the discriminator is not trained once and frozen. Both experts are "optimized jointly with the policy throughout alignment, so that they function as on-policy discriminators that continuously adapt to the evolving rollout distribution" (Section 3.2.4). As the policy improves and its rollouts become more supervision-like, the discriminator must also improve to continue providing meaningful discrimination. If the discriminator were fixed, it would quickly become stale — the policy would learn to produce rollouts that score highly under the outdated discriminator without actually matching the supervision distribution. The alternating update procedure (policy step, discriminator step, policy step, discriminator step, etc.) maintains the adversarial balance.

**Training convergence (Figure 3).** The paper tracks the reward gap — `$D_k(x, y^+_k) - D_k(x, y^-_k)$` for each expert — across training steps. Both experts exhibit "a characteristic rise-then-convergence pattern" that stabilizes around step 500. The perception expert peaks early and converges quickly, while the reasoning expert rises more gradually with greater oscillation before stabilizing. The paper interprets this as evidence that "reasoning alignment involves more nuanced distributional corrections than visual grounding" — perception errors may be more categorical (right/wrong description of objects), while reasoning errors exist on a continuous spectrum of logical quality. Despite these different trajectories, both experts converge to comparable equilibrium levels, confirming that the adversarial game reaches a stable state where the policy has been substantially realigned.

#### Adversarial On-Policy Distillation: The Alignment Game

**Formulation as a minimax game.** The alignment stage is formulated as a two-player adversarial game between the policy `$G_\theta$` (the generator, parameterized by `$\theta$`) and the MoE discriminator `$D_\phi$` (parameterized by `$\phi$`):

$$\min_\theta \max_\phi \mathbb{E}_{(x, y^+) \sim \mathcal{T}, \, y^- \sim G_\theta(\cdot|x)} \left[ r_\phi(x, y^+) - r_\phi(x, y^-) \right]$$

where `$\mathcal{T}$` is the supervision data pool, `$G_\theta(\cdot|x)$` is the current policy distribution over responses given multimodal input `$x$`, `$y^+$` is a reference response sampled from the supervision pool, `$y^-$` is a response sampled from the current policy, and `$r_\phi(x, y) = \alpha D_v(x, c) + (1-\alpha) D_r(x, t)$` is the combined MoE reward.

**What it computes:** the outer minimization over `$\theta$` trains the policy to make its rollouts indistinguishable from supervision by minimizing the reward gap between supervision and policy responses. The inner maximization over `$\phi$` trains the discriminator to maximize this gap — to assign as high a score as possible to supervision responses and as low a score as possible to policy rollouts. At the Nash equilibrium of this game, the policy's rollout distribution matches the supervision distribution (the discriminator cannot distinguish them), and the discriminator provides the most informative gradient signal possible given the current policy. The expectation is over random samples from the supervision pool and random rollouts from the policy, making this a stochastic approximation of the true distributional divergence.

**Why this form:** this is the standard formulation of a Generative Adversarial Network (Goodfellow et al., 2014) adapted to the sequence generation setting. The key property is that the objective is **logit-free** — it only requires samples from the supervision pool, not access to the teacher model's internal probabilities or logits. This makes it applicable when supervision comes from black-box proprietary models like Gemini 3 Flash, which expose only text outputs. Alternative divergence-based distillation methods (reverse KL, forward KL, total variation) all require `$\log p_{\text{teacher}}(y|x)$` to compute the objective, which is inaccessible. The adversarial formulation circumvents this by learning an implicit divergence through the discriminator's score.

**Policy optimization via GRPO.** The policy is updated using GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization; Shao et al., 2024), a policy-gradient algorithm adapted for the alignment stage with a crucial modification: the KL regularization term is removed. For each multimodal input `$x$`, the policy samples `$N=16$` candidate responses `$\{y^-_i\}_{i=1}^N$` (a "group"), evaluates each with the MoE discriminator to obtain rewards `$r(x, y^-_i)$`, and computes group-normalized advantages:

$$A_i = \frac{r(x, y^-_i) - \operatorname{mean}\left(\{r(x, y^-_j)\}_{j=1}^N\right)}{\operatorname{std}\left(\{r(x, y^-_j)\}_{j=1}^N\right)}$$

where `$r(x, y^-_i)$` is the MoE combined reward for the `$i$`-th rollout in the group, `$\operatorname{mean}(\cdot)$` and `$\operatorname{std}(\cdot)$` are computed across the `$N=16$` rollouts within the same group, and `$A_i$` is the resulting normalized advantage for the `$i$`-th rollout.

**What it computes:** the advantage `$A_i$` measures how much better (or worse) the `$i$`-th rollout's reward is compared to the average reward in its group, expressed in units of the group's standard deviation. A rollout with reward one standard deviation above the group mean gets `$A_i \approx 1$`; a rollout one standard deviation below gets `$A_i \approx -1$`. The policy is then updated via the GRPO objective to increase the log-probability of rollouts with positive advantages and decrease the log-probability of rollouts with negative advantages.

**Why this form:** group normalization serves two purposes. First, it makes the advantage signal **baseline-free** — the policy does not need a separate value function or critic to estimate expected reward, since the group mean serves as an implicit baseline. Second, it makes the advantage **scale-invariant** — if the discriminator's scores shift in magnitude over training (e.g., because the discriminator is improving), the advantages remain comparable because they are normalized per group. This is important in the adversarial setting where the discriminator is evolving: the absolute magnitude of `$r(x, y)$` may increase over time, but the relative ranking within each group remains meaningful.

**Why KL regularization is removed.** Standard RLHF and most GRPO implementations include a KL divergence penalty `$\beta \cdot D_{\text{KL}}(\pi_\theta \| \pi_{\text{ref}})$` that penalizes the policy for deviating too far from a reference distribution (typically the SFT checkpoint). This penalty prevents reward hacking and catastrophic forgetting. In PRISM, the paper **deliberately disables this penalty** by setting the KL coefficient to `$\beta = 0.0$` (Section 3.2.4, Appendix A Table 3). The justification is direct: "such a constraint would directly oppose the goal of correcting SFT-induced distributional drift." The whole purpose of the alignment stage is to shift the policy distribution toward the supervision distribution — if KL regularization anchors the policy to the SFT checkpoint, the shift is prevented. This is a calculated risk: without KL regularization, the policy could theoretically drift into degenerate output modes if the discriminator provides poor rewards. The warm-start initialization of the discriminator and the on-policy updates are designed to prevent this failure mode.

**Adversarial training dynamics.** The two objectives define a minimax game: the discriminator maximizes its ability to separate supervision from policy, and the policy minimizes the gap. Training alternates between discriminator updates (minimizing the Bradley-Terry losses for both experts) and policy updates (maximizing the MoE reward via GRPO). The paper runs this for 500 steps, at which point both experts have converged to a stable equilibrium as shown in Figure 3. The resulting checkpoint `$\pi_{\text{align}}$` is the output of Stage 2 and the initialization for Stage 3.

---

#### Stage 3: Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards

**Transition from learned to verifiable reward.** Stage 3 of PRISM applies standard outcome-based RLVR to the aligned checkpoint. The critical change from Stage 2 is the reward function: the learned MoE discriminator reward `$r(x, y)$` is replaced with a deterministic verifiable reward `$r_v(x, y)$`:

$$r_v(x, y) = r_{\text{acc}}(x, y) + r_{\text{fmt}}(x, y)$$

where `$r_{\text{acc}}(x, y)$` is the answer accuracy reward and `$r_{\text{fmt}}(x, y)$` is the format compliance reward.

**What it computes:** `$r_{\text{acc}}$` is a binary or rule-based check: 1.0 if the extracted final answer matches the ground truth (using the two-stage validation process described in Appendix A.2 — first a rule-based validator, then an LLM-as-judge fallback), and 0.0 otherwise. `$r_{\text{fmt}}$` checks whether the response follows the required XML structure (`<caption>`, `thinking`, `<answer>` tags). The two components are weighted with accuracy receiving 0.8 weight and format receiving 0.2 weight (Appendix A, Table 3).

**Why this form:** the switch from learned discriminator reward to verifiable outcome reward reflects the shift in objective from distribution alignment to task performance. The alignment stage asked "does this response look like it came from the supervision distribution?" — a distribution-level question about response quality. The RLVR stage asks "does this response achieve the correct answer?" — an outcome-level question about task success. The verifiable reward is deterministic and does not require a learned model, eliminating the risk of reward hacking that exists with the discriminator. The format reward ensures that the structured output format (which enables the perception/reasoning decomposition) is preserved through RL optimization.

**RL training set construction.** The paper constructs the RL training set by applying a **difficulty-based filter** to the 6K reserved samples (Section 3.3, Appendix B). For each problem in the 6K pool, the aligned policy `$\pi_{\text{align}}$` samples `$N=16$` rollouts, and the pass rate (fraction of rollouts that produce the correct answer) is computed. Only problems whose pass rate falls in the interval `$[0.2, 0.8]$` are retained, yielding a final set of approximately 2K difficulty-matched samples (Table 4).

**Why difficulty filtering:** this is based on the principle that RL is most effective on problems that are "at the frontier" of the policy's current capability — neither trivially easy (pass rate > 0.8, where the model already succeeds and RL provides no learning signal) nor prohibitively hard (pass rate < 0.2, where the model never succeeds and RL has no positive examples to reinforce). The interval `$[0.2, 0.8]$` is a standard heuristic drawn from prior work (Wang et al., 2025b; Zhang et al., 2026b). By filtering to this difficulty range, RLVR trains on problems where the policy sometimes succeeds and sometimes fails, providing a meaningful gradient for improvement.

**RL optimization.** The RL stage uses the same GRPO-style optimization as the alignment stage, with the discriminator reward replaced by `$r_v$`. The policy samples `$N=16$` rollouts per prompt at temperature 1.0, computes group-normalized advantages from the verifiable rewards (using the same mean/std normalization as in Eq. 3), and updates the policy via GRPO. Training runs for up to 1500 steps, with checkpoints saved periodically and the best one selected based on validation performance (Appendix A.2). The global batch size is increased to 32 (from 4 in alignment), and the maximum response length is increased to 8192 tokens (from 6144) to accommodate longer reasoning traces.

**Algorithm-agnostic design.** The paper emphasizes that PRISM "is agnostic to the specific RL algorithm" (Section 3.3). In experiments, this stage is instantiated with three different RL algorithms — GRPO (Shao et al., 2024), DAPO (Yu et al., 2025), and GSPO (Zheng et al., 2025) — and all three show consistent improvements from the PRISM alignment stage over the SFT→RL baseline (Table 1). This is important because it demonstrates that the alignment stage provides a generally better initialization, not one that is specifically tuned to a particular RL algorithm's optimization dynamics. All three algorithms share the same hyperparameters (Appendix A, Table 3): learning rate `$1\times 10^{-6}$`, constant learning rate schedule, AdamW optimizer, weight decay 0.01, rollout temperature 1.0, group size `$N=16$`.

#### Summary of Key Design Choices

**Why adversarial rather than divergence-based distillation?** The supervision model (Gemini 3 Flash) is black-box — only text outputs are available, not logits. Standard OPD methods that minimize `$D_{\text{KL}}(p_{\text{teacher}} \| p_{\text{student}})$` or `$D_{\text{KL}}(p_{\text{student}} \| p_{\text{teacher}})$` require `$\log p_{\text{teacher}}(y|x)$`, which is inaccessible. The adversarial formulation only requires response-level samples from the supervision pool, making it applicable to any black-box teacher.

**Why MoE rather than a single discriminator?** Multimodal drift is heterogeneous: perception errors and reasoning errors are qualitatively different and require different corrective signals. A single discriminator collapses both into one scalar, losing information about which axis needs correction. The MoE design with dedicated experts provides **decoupled gradients** — the perception expert's score drives visual grounding improvement, and the reasoning expert's score drives logical deduction improvement, independently. The ablation (Table 2) confirms this: removing the MoE design (−3.4 average points) degrades performance, with the largest drops on benchmarks requiring faithful perception (WeMath: −6.0, MathVerse: −4.9).

**Why remove KL regularization?** The alignment stage's goal is to shift the policy distribution toward supervision, which is in direct conflict with KL anchoring to the SFT checkpoint. Standard RLHF uses KL regularization to prevent reward hacking and catastrophic forgetting, but in PRISM, the "forgetting" of SFT-induced drift patterns is precisely what is desired. The risk of policy collapse is mitigated by the discriminator warm-start and on-policy updates.

**Why 500 alignment steps?** The training dynamics (Figure 3) show that both discriminator experts converge to a stable equilibrium around step 500. Running longer (the paper extends to 900 steps in Figure 3 to verify convergence stability) does not further narrow the reward gap, indicating that the adversarial game has reached its equilibrium and further training would not improve alignment.

**Why `$\alpha = 0.5$`?** Equal weighting treats perception and reasoning as equally important for distribution alignment. The paper does not ablate `$\alpha$`, so it is unclear whether this is optimal or simply a reasonable default. Exploring learned or dynamic weighting is left to future work.

**Why 1.37M SFT samples instead of only 107K?** The ablation (Table 2, "SFT data scale") shows that using only the curated 107K samples for SFT drops downstream RLVR performance by −3.7 average points compared to using the full 1.37M corpus. SFT provides the broad initialization that narrows the distribution gap enough for adversarial training to begin; insufficient SFT data widens this gap, placing more corrective burden on the alignment stage than it can fully compensate for. The combination of broad public data (breadth) and curated high-quality data (depth) reflects a separation of concerns: SFT needs many samples to broadly initialize, while alignment needs fewer but higher-quality samples to learn fine-grained distinctions.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Reframing SFT→RL as a Pipeline with a Structural Gap, Not a Seamless Chain

The dominant assumption in post-training research — and the unspoken premise behind nearly all work on SFT and RL for LLMs and LMMs — is that the two stages compose cleanly: SFT installs a reasoning prior, and RLVR refines it toward task objectives. The field's attention has been divided into making SFT better (better data, better regularization) and making RL better (better algorithms, better rewards), with the interface between them treated as a solved problem. PRISM challenges this directly. It argues that the interface is itself a site of failure: SFT systematically introduces a form of distributional drift that cannot be assumed away and that RLVR, operating only on final-answer correctness, cannot diagnose or repair.

This is not a small refinement. It is a **reframing of the pipeline architecture from a two-stage process to a three-stage one**, where the new middle stage serves a distinct purpose — distribution alignment — that neither SFT nor RLVR individually addresses. The paper's evidence for this reframing is concrete: the post-SFT checkpoint shows accuracy comparable to the post-alignment checkpoint (Table 1, PRISM row vs. SFT row), yet the downstream RLVR gains from the aligned checkpoint are substantially larger (+4.4 avg. on 4B, +6.0 avg. on 8B, both over SFT→GRPO). This disconnect — similar SFT-level accuracy but dramatically different RLVR outcomes — is the empirical signature that something beyond accuracy matters. The paper's conceptual contribution is naming that "something" as **distribution-level alignment** along the specific axes of visual grounding and logical deduction, and building a pipeline stage that explicitly optimizes it.

Prior work on multi-stage pipelines (e.g., Yang et al., 2025c) had observed that stages are individually important, but had not identified the *nature* of the gap between SFT and RL as a distributional mismatch problem requiring its own optimization stage. The closest prior framing is the observation that SFT can degrade model capabilities (Kang et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026a), but those works diagnosed the problem without proposing a structural solution. PRISM provides both the diagnosis and the architectural fix, making the gap *actionable* rather than merely observable.

### Innovation 2: The Heterogeneous Drift Hypothesis — Why Multimodal Models Need Decoupled Corrective Signals

An important conceptual move in PRISM is the claim that distributional drift in multimodal models is not monolithic but **heterogeneous**: it decomposes into perception drift (imprecise visual descriptions) and reasoning drift (logically inconsistent deductions), and these two error modes follow distinct patterns that a single corrective objective cannot jointly address. This hypothesis is stated in the introduction and substantiated throughout the paper.

The significance of this hypothesis lies in what it rules out. If multimodal drift were uniform — just "the model gets worse at everything" — then a single corrective signal (e.g., a monolithic discriminator, or a unified distillation loss) would suffice. The field's default approach to distribution matching — whether through divergence-based distillation (Gu et al., 2024; Agarwal et al., 2024) or adversarial discriminators (Ye et al., 2025a) — implicitly assumes exactly this uniform-drift model, since it collapses all dimensions of quality into one scalar. PRISM's MoE discriminator is not just an architectural choice; it is the operationalization of the heterogeneous drift hypothesis. The dedicated perception and reasoning experts exist because the hypothesis says these two error modes require distinct feedback, and the paper's ablation results (Table 2) provide evidence for this claim: replacing the MoE with a dense discriminator of equal activated compute causes consistent degradation (−3.4 avg.), and a text-only discriminator that cannot verify visual grounding degrades most sharply on perception-heavy benchmarks (WeMath: −6.0, MathVerse: −4.9).

This hypothesis also explains why prior multimodal RL methods that add perception-aware rewards within the RL stage (Xiao et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025a; Gao et al., 2026) are insufficient: they penalize perception errors during outcome optimization, but they do not *separately* address the visual-grounding component of the policy's distribution. The policy may learn to produce correct answers without ever fixing the underlying perceptual imprecision — the RL reward only cares about final answers. PRISM's heterogeneity hypothesis says that perception and reasoning must be corrected *as distinct distributions*, not just as contributors to a final accuracy score.

The training dynamics in Figure 3 lend further support: the perception expert converges early and quickly, while the reasoning expert rises more gradually with greater oscillation. This asynchronous convergence would be invisible to a single discriminator, and it constitutes evidence that the two alignment processes are genuinely different — one is learning to distinguish accurate from inaccurate descriptions (a more categorical task), while the other is learning to distinguish logically consistent from inconsistent deduction (a more continuous and nuanced task). The MoE design surface this structure; a single discriminator would obscure it.

### Innovation 3: On-Policy Distillation Re-Purposed as Pre-RL Alignment

On-policy distillation was developed to solve the train-inference distribution mismatch in knowledge distillation: a student model trained on teacher-generated outputs behaves differently when generating its own, and OPD corrects this by training on the student's own rollouts (Agarwal et al., 2024; Gu et al., 2024). The standard use case treats OPD as a terminal objective — the distilled checkpoint is the final model. PRISM makes a conceptually different move: it repurposes OPD as an **intermediate alignment stage** whose output is not the final model but a better initialization for downstream RL.

This re-purposing changes the design requirements in ways that the paper navigates carefully. Terminal OPD wants the distilled model to match the teacher as closely as possible; alignment-as-pre-RL wants the model to match the supervision distribution *along the dimensions that matter for subsequent RL optimization*. The paper operationalizes this difference by (a) removing KL regularization from the alignment stage, which would be unacceptable in terminal OPD (it would risk the model forgetting useful behaviors) but is essential here (the goal is precisely to *shift* the distribution away from SFT-induced drift), and (b) using the MoE discriminator to provide decoupled feedback, which is unnecessary in terminal OPD (a single divergence suffices to measure overall match) but is crucial here (different RL-relevant dimensions need separate correction).

The paper also resolves a practical constraint that had limited OPD's applicability: the need for teacher logits. Standard OPD minimizes a divergence like `D_KL(p_teacher || p_student)`, which requires access to `log p_teacher(y|x)`. When the supervision source is a proprietary black-box model like Gemini 3 Flash — precisely the kind of model that produces the highest-quality reasoning traces — this information is unavailable. PRISM's adversarial formulation replaces logit-based divergence with response-level discrimination, making OPD viable for black-box teachers. This is not a minor implementation detail: it expands the set of supervision sources from open-weight models (where logits are accessible) to any model that can produce text outputs, including the strongest proprietary systems. Given that state-of-the-art reasoning models are increasingly closed-source, this is a practically significant expansion.

The paper distinguishes its approach from VOLD (Bousselham et al., 2025), which combines GRPO with logit-based OPD from a text-only teacher into a single objective. PRISM's decoupling — alignment as a separate stage, with separate rewards, followed by RL — is the architectural expression of the paper's core claim that alignment and outcome optimization serve different purposes and should not be conflated. Conflating them (as VOLD does) risks the model optimizing a hybrid objective where the distribution-matching signal and the outcome signal compete or interfere. PRISM's sequential structure ensures that alignment is complete before outcome optimization begins, so RLVR operates on a distributionally clean foundation.

### Innovation 4: The Stronger-Model, Worse-Drift Finding as a Cautionary Principle

A striking empirical pattern emerges from the main results (Table 1) that the paper flags but does not belabor: the SFT-induced degradation is **worse for the stronger model**. On Qwen3-VL-4B, SFT drops the Instruct baseline by 2.9 average points (59.7 → 56.8). On Qwen3-VL-8B, the drop is 5.2 points (63.3 → 58.1). The standard SFT→RLVR pipeline on 8B barely recovers the original Instruct performance (63.3 for both Instruct and SFT→GRPO), while on 4B it exceeds it (61.8 vs. 59.7). PRISM+GRPO exceeds the Instruct baseline by over 5 points on 8B (69.3 vs. 63.3) but by a more modest 6.5 points on 4B (66.2 vs. 59.7).

This pattern suggests a principle: **stronger models have more to lose from token-level imitation of an external demonstration policy, because their native reasoning distributions are more competent and imitation can displace rather than supplement.** Weaker models gain substantially from SFT because their native distributions are poor; strong models risk degradation. PRISM's alignment stage disproportionately helps the strong-model case, which is precisely where the standard pipeline fails most acutely.

This finding has implications beyond the specific PRISM pipeline. It suggests that as base models continue to improve — as they demonstrably are — the standard SFT→RL paradigm will become increasingly problematic, not less. A model like Qwen3-VL-8B already shows that SFT→RL barely recovers the Instruct baseline; a hypothetical Qwen4-VL-70B might show SFT→RL *underperforming* the Instruct baseline. PRISM's three-stage architecture is thus not just an improvement for current models but a necessary structural adaptation for future, stronger models. This is an example of a finding whose significance is **prospective** rather than retrospective: it matters more for what it says about where the field is heading than for what it achieves on current benchmarks.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The paper evaluates on seven multimodal benchmarks spanning two categories. For mathematical reasoning: MathVista (Lu et al., 2023; testmini split), MathVerse (Zhang et al., 2024; testmini split), MathVision (Wang et al., 2024; testmini split), and WeMath (Qiao et al., 2025; testmini split). For general multimodal understanding: MMMU (Yue et al., 2024; val split), MMMU-Pro (Yue et al., 2025a; overall split), and HallusionBench (Guan et al., 2024; no specific split named in the paper). All are standard public benchmarks covering mathematical reasoning with visual contexts, scientific diagram understanding, chart interpretation, spatial reasoning, and visual hallucination detection. The paper does not describe any custom test-set construction — evaluation is entirely on these standard benchmark splits.

- **Base model(s).** All experiments use Qwen3-VL at two scales: 4B and 8B parameters (Bai et al., 2025). These are open-weight multimodal LLMs that accept image and text inputs and produce text outputs. The Instruct variants of both scales serve as the starting point before any post-training. The paper argues that Qwen3-VL is "representative of the capabilities of many contemporary LLMs" (in the spirit of similar claims in the referenced distillation literature), though no ablation across model families is performed. The 8B model is notably stronger than the 4B, with an Instruct baseline average of 63.3 vs. 59.7 (Table 1). The MoE discriminator is assembled from four Qwen3-VL-2B models as expert modules with top-2 routing, following the Qwen3-VL-MoE architecture (Bai et al., 2025).

- **Metrics.** The primary metric is **accuracy (%)** — the fraction of test examples where the model's extracted final answer matches the ground truth. Answers are extracted via a two-stage process: a rule-based validator is applied first to minimize evaluation cost; if the answer cannot be verified by rules, the system falls back to an LLM-as-judge validator powered by Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct (Yang et al., 2025a), using the detailed evaluation prompt shown in Appendix C, Figure 8. The judge prompt handles both multiple-choice and open-ended questions, with specific rules for unit matching, numerical precision ("reasonable precision"), and formatting differences. The primary summary statistic is the **average accuracy across all seven benchmarks**, computed per model scale and method combination. All evaluations use the lmms-eval framework (Zhang et al., 2025b) with generation hyperparameters temperature=1.0, top_p=0.7, top_k=−1.

- **Baselines.** The paper compares against three configuration families, all starting from the same Instruct checkpoint:
  - **Instruct**: The base Qwen3-VL model without any post-training. This establishes the model's native capability before any fine-tuning.
  - **+ SFT**: The Instruct model after supervised fine-tuning on the full 1.37M supervision corpus (107K curated + 1.26M public). This is the intermediate checkpoint before any RL.
  - **+ GRPO**: The standard SFT→RLVR pipeline, where the SFT checkpoint undergoes 1500 additional steps of GRPO with verifiable rewards (Section 3.3). To match the total training budget consumed by PRISM's alignment stage (500 steps), the SFT→GRPO baseline is "trained with additional RLVR steps equal to those of the alignment stage" (Section 4.1). Exactly how this step-count matching works — whether the baseline runs 2000 total RL steps vs. PRISM's 500 alignment + 1500 RL, or whether both run 1500 RL steps after their respective initializations — is not completely specified in the text, but the intent is to control for total optimization budget.
  - **+ DAPO** and **+ GSPO**: The same SFT→RLVR pipeline using alternative RL algorithms DAPO (Yu et al., 2025) and GSPO (Zheng et al., 2025) at the RLVR stage, with identical hyperparameters to GRPO (Appendix A, Table 3).
  - **PRISM** (standalone): The checkpoint after the alignment stage but before RLVR. This measures the immediate effect of distribution alignment, without outcome-based optimization.
  - **PRISM + GRPO / + DAPO / + GSPO**: The full three-stage pipeline with the respective RL algorithm at Stage 3.

  There is no baseline that gives the Instruct model RLVR directly (without SFT), which would test whether SFT is net-harmful to downstream RL compared to skipping SFT entirely. There is also no baseline that runs PRISM alignment on the Instruct model directly (without SFT initialization), though the "w/o SFT" ablation in Table 2 partially addresses this.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper measures compute indirectly through **training steps** rather than FLOPs or wall-clock time. The SFT stage runs for 1 epoch on 1.37M samples. The alignment stage runs for exactly 500 steps (with a global batch size of 2). The RLVR stage runs for up to 1500 steps (global batch size 32). For the SFT→RL baselines, RLVR runs for additional steps equal to the alignment stage (so SFT→RL baselines get 500 more RL steps than PRISM's RL stage, totaling 2000 RL steps vs. PRISM's 1500 RL steps, making this a conservative comparison that favors the baseline). All experiments use 8× NVIDIA H100-SXM5-80GB GPUs. The paper does not report total FLOPs, GPU-hours, or wall-clock time for any configuration. For the RL training set construction, the difficulty-based filter samples N=16 rollouts per problem from the aligned policy to compute the pass rate; this cost is not amortized into any reported metric but represents additional compute at the boundary between Stages 2 and 3.

  All generations during alignment and RLVR use temperature 1.0, group size N=16, and maximum response lengths of 6144 (alignment) and 8192 (RLVR) tokens. The SFT→RL baselines share all these generation hyperparameters during their RLVR stages.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** There is no explicit cross-validation or statistical significance testing for any of the main results. The paper evaluates each model checkpoint once on each benchmark's standard test split and reports the resulting accuracy. Given that the test sets are fixed, publicly available benchmarks (rather than custom splits), and the experiments are deterministic given the same random seeds (no mention of multiple seeds or variance reporting), the reported numbers should be interpreted as point estimates with unknown variance. For the difficulty-based RL training set filter (Section 3.3), the pass rate computation uses N=16 rollouts per problem, which is a Monte Carlo estimate with its own variance, but no confidence intervals are reported. The RLVR stage saves checkpoints periodically and selects the best based on validation performance (Appendix A.2), but the validation set is not described (likely a held-out portion of the difficulty-filtered 2K training problems, but this is not stated explicitly). The paper does not report how many random seeds were used, whether results are averaged across seeds, or what the observed variance was.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Headline: PRISM+GRPO Outperforms SFT→GRPO by +4.4 and +6.0 Average Points

The central result appears in Table 1, which reports accuracy on all seven benchmarks for both Qwen3-VL-4B and Qwen3-VL-8B across all baseline and PRISM configurations. Averaging across the seven benchmarks (rightmost column), the key comparisons are:

**Qwen3-VL-4B:**
- Instruct: 59.7 avg.
- + SFT: 56.8 (−2.9 vs. Instruct)
- + GRPO: 61.8 (+2.1 vs. Instruct)
- PRISM (standalone): 57.2 (−2.5 vs. Instruct)
- PRISM+GRPO: **66.2** (+4.4 vs. SFT→GRPO, +6.5 vs. Instruct)
- PRISM+DAPO: 66.3 (+3.1 vs. SFT→DAPO at 63.2)
- PRISM+GSPO: 65.8 (+4.2 vs. SFT→GSPO at 61.6)

**Qwen3-VL-8B:**
- Instruct: 63.3 avg.
- + SFT: 58.1 (−5.2 vs. Instruct)
- + GRPO: 63.3 (0.0 vs. Instruct — exact recovery)
- PRISM (standalone): 59.3 (−4.0 vs. Instruct)
- PRISM+GRPO: **69.3** (+6.0 vs. SFT→GRPO, +6.0 vs. Instruct)
- PRISM+DAPO: 68.9 (+3.7 vs. SFT→DAPO at 65.2)
- PRISM+GSPO: 68.7 (+5.4 vs. SFT→GSPO at 63.3)

Several patterns emerge from these numbers. First, SFT degrades the Instruct model on both scales, with the 8B model losing more (−5.2 vs. −2.9). Second, the standard SFT→GRPO pipeline on 8B recovers Instruct performance exactly (63.3), meaning that RLVR contributes nothing net over the base model when starting from the drifted SFT checkpoint. On 4B, SFT→GRPO does improve over Instruct (+2.1), but the gain is modest. Third, the PRISM alignment stage on its own (without RLVR) does not improve accuracy over the SFT checkpoint — 57.2 vs. 56.8 on 4B, 59.3 vs. 58.1 on 8B — confirming that alignment is not about immediate accuracy gains but about preparing the distribution for downstream RL. Fourth, PRISM+GRPO exceeds the Instruct baseline by 6.5 points on 4B and 6.0 points on 8B, while SFT→GRPO exceeds it by only 2.1 and 0.0 points respectively, meaning that PRISM roughly triples the net gain from the post-training pipeline on 4B and creates gains where the baseline pipeline produces none on 8B.

The paper does not report whether the differences between PRISM+GRPO and SFT→GRPO are statistically significant, nor does it provide confidence intervals. With 500-question test sets for some benchmarks (MATH has 500; the testmini splits for MathVista, MathVerse, MathVision, and WeMath are typically 1000, though exact sizes vary per benchmark), a +4.4 point average difference is likely meaningful, but the absence of variance reporting is a limitation.

#### Per-Benchmark Breakdown Shows Gains Concentrated on Hardest Benchmarks

Examining individual benchmark results in Table 1:

**MathVision** shows the largest absolute gains from PRISM. On 4B, PRISM+GRPO achieves 45.4 vs. SFT→GRPO's 35.5 (+9.9 points). On 8B, the gain is 52.0 vs. 37.1 (+14.9 points). MathVision is described in prior work as a challenging benchmark testing deep mathematical reasoning with visual contexts; the model's Instruct baseline is only 36.5 on 4B and 43.7 on 8B, suggesting substantial room for improvement. The fact that PRISM unlocks such large gains — while SFT→GRPO barely moves from the SFT baseline (35.5 vs. 31.9 on 4B, 37.1 vs. 32.6 on 8B) — supports the claim that the alignment stage enables RLVR to make progress that would otherwise be impossible.

**WeMath** similarly shows large gains: on 4B, PRISM+GRPO achieves 82.9 vs. SFT→GRPO's 77.8 (+5.1); on 8B, 86.4 vs. 79.7 (+6.7). WeMath tests human-like mathematical reasoning, and the high baseline accuracy (70.7 Instruct on 4B, 71.7 on 8B) indicates that the base model is already competent, yet PRISM still extracts substantial improvements.

**MathVerse** gains are also notable: on 4B, 68.6 vs. 64.5 (+4.1); on 8B, 71.3 vs. 66.9 (+4.4).

**MathVista** gains are more modest: on 4B, 77.9 vs. 75.7 (+2.2); on 8B, 78.3 vs. 75.9 (+2.4). MathVista's high baseline (74.9 Instruct on 4B) suggests that it may contain many easier problems where the model is already near ceiling, leaving less room for any method to improve.

**General benchmarks** (MMMU, MMMU-Pro, HallusionBench) show mixed but consistently positive gains:
- MMMU: 4B: 64.1 vs. 60.1 (+4.0); 8B: 66.6 vs. 62.6 (+4.0)
- MMMU-Pro: 4B: 49.7 vs. 47.3 (+2.4); 8B: 53.3 vs. 48.8 (+4.5)
- HallusionBench: 4B: 74.8 vs. 72.0 (+2.8); 8B: 77.2 vs. 71.9 (+5.3)

HallusionBench tests visual hallucination and illusion detection, making it a direct test of whether the perception expert's alignment improves grounding. The +5.3 point gain on the 8B model is consistent with the paper's claim that alignment corrects visual grounding drift.

#### Algorithm-Agnostic Gains: PRISM Improves DAPO and GSPO Too

The paper demonstrates that PRISM's alignment stage benefits multiple RL algorithms, not just GRPO (Table 1). On 4B:
- PRISM+DAPO: 66.3 vs. SFT→DAPO: 63.2 (+3.1)
- PRISM+GSPO: 65.8 vs. SFT→GSPO: 61.6 (+4.2)

On 8B:
- PRISM+DAPO: 68.9 vs. SFT→DAPO: 65.2 (+3.7)
- PRISM+GSPO: 68.7 vs. SFT→GSPO: 63.3 (+5.4)

All three PRISM+RL configurations outperform their respective SFT→RL baselines, and the absolute performance of all three PRISM variants is clustered tightly — 66.2, 66.3, 65.8 on 4B (range: 0.5 points); 69.3, 68.9, 68.7 on 8B (range: 0.6 points) — suggesting that the alignment initialization, not the specific RL algorithm, is the dominant factor in final performance. This is a strong result because it implies that PRISM's value is general and does not depend on careful tuning of the downstream RL algorithm.

Notably, the baseline RL algorithms rank differently with and without PRISM. Without PRISM, DAPO achieves the highest 4B average (63.2), followed by GRPO (61.8) and GSPO (61.6). With PRISM, the order changes slightly (66.3 for DAPO, 66.2 for GRPO, 65.8 for GSPO), but the differences compress. On 8B, the pattern is similar: the advantage of DAPO over GSPO shrinks from 1.9 points (65.2 vs. 63.3) to 0.2 points (68.9 vs. 68.7). This compression effect could indicate that better initialization reduces the importance of RL algorithm design — RL has less "repair work" to do, so algorithmic differences matter less.

#### Token Efficiency: PRISM+GRPO Achieves Higher Accuracy with Fewer Tokens

Figure 5 presents a three-panel comparison of accuracy and average token usage on three benchmarks (MathVision, MathVerse, MMMU-Pro) for Qwen3-VL-4B across three configurations (Instruct, SFT+GRPO, PRISM+GRPO). The key finding:

- **MathVision**: PRISM+GRPO achieves 45.4% accuracy with 2,889 average tokens, vs. SFT+GRPO's 35.5% with 5,724 tokens, and Instruct's 36.5% with 9,538 tokens. PRISM+GRPO is both more accurate and dramatically more concise (~3.3× fewer tokens than Instruct, ~2.0× fewer than SFT+GRPO).

- **MathVerse**: PRISM+GRPO achieves 68.6% accuracy with 1,785 average tokens, vs. SFT+GRPO's 64.5% with 1,942 tokens, and Instruct's 59.0% with 3,182 tokens.

- **MMMU-Pro**: PRISM+GRPO achieves 49.7% accuracy with 2,257 average tokens, vs. SFT+GRPO's 47.3% with 2,953 tokens, and Instruct's 45.1% with 2,761 tokens.

Across all three benchmarks, PRISM+GRPO is the pareto-optimal configuration: highest accuracy, lowest token count. The paper interprets this as evidence that "the alignment stage encourages more concise and effective reasoning rather than simply producing longer outputs" (Appendix A.3). The Instruct model produces the most tokens but the lowest accuracy — it is verbose and often wrong. SFT reduces verbosity substantially but only modestly improves accuracy. PRISM further reduces verbosity while substantially improving accuracy. This pattern is consistent with the hypothesis that SFT teaches the model a specific reasoning style (structured, with explicit captions and reasoning traces) but leaves it prone to verbose yet imprecise outputs, while alignment sharpens the precision of those outputs within the same structured format.

#### Structural Proxies of Distribution Alignment: Visual Evidence of Drift Repair

Figure 4 presents the paper's primary evidence that the alignment stage actually shifts the response distribution toward supervision, using two structural proxies:
- **Number of reasoning steps** (left panel): A kernel density estimate plot showing the distribution of reasoning step counts for five distributions: Base model, Supervision data, Post-SFT, Post-Alignment, and Post-RLVR.
- **Number of descriptive items in the caption** (right panel): A kernel density estimate plot showing the distribution of descriptive items per visual caption for the same five distributions.

The paper reports that "the base model deviates substantially from the supervision data under both proxies." On the reasoning steps proxy, the Base distribution is left-shifted (fewer reasoning steps) compared to Supervision. On the caption items proxy, the Base distribution is left-shifted (fewer descriptive items) compared to Supervision.

After SFT, "the response distributions shift toward supervision but remain visibly mismatched, particularly because the caption-side proxy tends to overshoot and produce more descriptive items than the supervision data contains." This is the drift that the paper argues harms downstream RL: the model learns to produce *more* description than the supervision, but not necessarily *better* or more accurate description.

After alignment, "the alignment stage substantially reduces this mismatch along both dimensions." The Post-Alignment density curves visibly move closer to the Supervision curves than the Post-SFT curves are, though exact numerical divergences (e.g., KL divergence, Wasserstein distance) are not reported.

After RLVR, "the post-RLVR distributions continue to follow similar trends despite the shift in optimization objective from distributional matching to outcome-based correctness." This is an important finding: the distributional correction from alignment persists through RLVR, meaning the benefits of alignment are not overwritten by subsequent outcome optimization.

The paper acknowledges a limitation of this analysis: "since directly visualizing the full multimodal response distribution is difficult in such high-dimensional output spaces, we instead probe its evolution through interpretable structural proxies" (Section 4.4). The choice of reasoning steps and caption items as proxies is justified by citing prior work (Tang et al., 2016; Mathur et al., 2025; Jiang et al., 2025), but the proxies are necessarily incomplete — a response could match the supervision's structural statistics (same number of steps, same number of description items) while still being semantically different. The paper does not report other proxies (e.g., lexical diversity, semantic embedding distance, n-gram overlap) that could provide additional evidence of distribution-level alignment. The claim that "the alignment stage substantially narrows the distributional gap" is visually supported by Figure 4 but lacks a quantitative metric to formalize the degree of narrowing.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

All ablations are conducted on Qwen3-VL-4B with GRPO as the default RL algorithm, with results in Table 2 (Section 4.3). The full PRISM pipeline (first row) achieves 66.2 average accuracy for reference.

**MoE discriminator vs. dense discriminator (equal activated compute):** Replacing the MoE discriminator with a single dense model of equal activated compute (a single Qwen3-VL-4B model instead of four Qwen3-VL-2B experts with top-2 routing) reduces average accuracy from 66.2 to 62.8 (−3.4 avg.). The degradation is uneven across benchmarks: WeMath drops from 82.9 to 76.9 (−6.0), MathVerse from 68.6 to 63.7 (−4.9), MathVista from 77.9 to 74.6 (−3.3), while HallusionBench is barely affected (74.8 vs. 74.0, −0.8). The pattern — largest drops on perception-heavy math benchmarks, minimal drop on HallusionBench — is consistent with the heterogeneous drift hypothesis: WeMath and MathVerse require precise visual grounding for mathematical diagrams, and the dense discriminator's single scalar reward cannot provide the decoupled corrective signal that the MoE's dedicated perception expert provides. HallusionBench, despite testing visual hallucination, may rely more on high-level reasoning about visual illusions than fine-grained perceptual precision, making the dense discriminator less of a bottleneck.

**Text-only discriminator (no vision):** Replacing the vision-language discriminator with a text-only variant of identical architecture (i.e., the discriminator sees only the text of the policy response and supervision response, without the image) reduces average accuracy from 66.2 to 62.3 (−3.9 avg.). The degradation is again uneven: MathVision drops from 45.4 to 42.8 (−2.6), WeMath from 82.9 to 76.8 (−6.1), MathVista from 77.9 to 74.0 (−3.9), while MMMU-Pro and HallusionBench show smaller drops. The paper interprets this as evidence of "parrot alignment": the text-only discriminator can capture surface-level textual patterns (formatting conventions, reasoning templates, stylistic cues) but cannot verify whether a visual description faithfully depicts the image content. The policy learns to produce text that looks like supervision without actually grounding it in the image. The largest drops occur on WeMath and MathVerse, where accurate visual description is most critical for correct reasoning.

**Removing the alignment stage (w/o Alignment):** Ablating the alignment stage reduces the pipeline to standard SFT→GRPO, dropping average accuracy from 66.2 to 61.8 (−4.4 avg.). This is identical to the SFT→GRPO baseline in the main results (Table 1), confirming that the ablation is correctly implemented. The consistent degradation across all benchmarks (MathVista: 77.9 → 75.7, MathVerse: 68.6 → 64.5, MathVision: 45.4 → 35.5, WeMath: 82.9 → 77.8, MMMU: 64.1 → 60.1, MMMU-Pro: 49.7 → 47.3, HallusionBench: 74.8 → 72.0) demonstrates that the alignment stage's benefit is not specific to any benchmark category.

**Removing the SFT stage (w/o SFT):** Removing SFT entirely — starting from the Instruct checkpoint, applying the alignment stage directly, then RLVR — causes a catastrophic drop from 66.2 to 49.4 (−16.8 avg.). Individual benchmarks: MathVista drops from 77.9 to 62.4, MathVerse from 68.6 to 47.6, MathVision from 45.4 to 25.9, WeMath from 82.9 to 55.7, MMMU from 64.1 to 51.4, MMMU-Pro from 49.7 to 36.5, HallusionBench from 74.8 to 66.1. The paper's interpretation is that "without cold-start initialization, the capability gap between the model and the supervision distribution is too large for adversarial training to function: the discriminator trivially separates the two distributions, and the policy drifts toward a degenerate output mode rather than converging toward the supervision distribution." This is a strong negative result that validates the three-stage architecture: each stage serves an indispensable role, and removing any one causes substantial degradation. Notably, the "w/o SFT" configuration (49.4 avg.) underperforms even the raw Instruct baseline (59.7 avg.) by over 10 points — meaning that direct adversarial alignment from the Instruct model without SFT is actively harmful, not just ineffective.

**SFT data scale (107K vs. 1.37M):** Using only the curated 107K samples for SFT (without the 1.26M public demonstrations) reduces average accuracy from 66.2 to 62.5 (−3.7 avg.). The degradation pattern: MathVista drops from 77.9 to 72.3 (−5.6), MathVerse from 68.6 to 67.0 (−1.6), MathVision from 45.4 to 43.1 (−2.3), WeMath from 82.9 to 76.9 (−6.0), MMMU from 64.1 to 60.6 (−3.5), MMMU-Pro from 49.7 to 49.0 (−0.7), HallusionBench from 74.8 to 68.3 (−6.5). The paper notes that even with only 107K SFT samples, the reduced-data variant (62.5 avg.) still outperforms the SFT→GRPO baseline trained on the full 1.37M corpus but without alignment (61.8 avg.), suggesting that alignment can partially compensate for weaker SFT but cannot fully recover from it. The largest drops occur on MathVista (−5.6), WeMath (−6.0), and HallusionBench (−6.5), suggesting that these benchmarks are particularly sensitive to the breadth of SFT coverage — they may contain question types or visual patterns that are underrepresented in the 107K curated set but covered by the 1.26M public demonstrations.

#### Training Dynamics: Perception and Reasoning Experts Converge Along Distinct Trajectories

Figure 3 tracks the reward gap — defined as D_k(x, y⁺_k) − D_k(x, y⁻_k) for each expert k ∈ {v, r} — across training steps during the alignment stage. The key observations:

**Perception expert:** The reward gap rises quickly in the first ~100 steps, peaks around step 50–100 at a value of approximately 6–7 (reading from the y-axis, which is labeled "Score Gap (Supervision − Policy)"), then gradually declines and stabilizes around 4–5 by step 200, remaining essentially flat through step 900. The rapid early rise and quick convergence suggests that distinguishing supervision-quality visual descriptions from drifted ones is a relatively easy task — the perception expert quickly learns the surface-level features that signal grounding quality.

**Reasoning expert:** The reward gap rises more gradually, oscillating between approximately 2 and 6 through the first 400 steps, before stabilizing around 4–5 by step 500. The oscillation amplitude is larger than for the perception expert, and convergence is slower. The paper interprets this as evidence that "reasoning alignment involves more nuanced distributional corrections than visual grounding" — distinguishing good from poor reasoning is a harder, more continuous task than distinguishing accurate from inaccurate descriptions.

**Convergence:** Both experts converge to similar equilibrium levels (approximately 4–5 on the y-axis) by step 500, and remain stable through the extended 900-step monitoring. This justifies the choice of 500 alignment steps as the stopping point.

The asynchronous convergence is cited as additional evidence for the MoE design: a single discriminator would conflate these two trajectories into one averaged signal, potentially masking the fact that perception alignment is "done" while reasoning alignment is still oscillating. The MoE architecture makes this structure visible and allows each expert to operate on its own learning schedule.

#### Additional Analyses (Not in Table 2)

The paper reports several additional results outside the main ablation table:

**KL regularization removal is deliberate and validated by design:** The alignment stage uses KL coefficient β = 0.0 (Appendix A, Table 3). While not presented as a formal ablation with a β > 0 comparison row, the paper argues that removing KL is essential because the alignment stage's goal is to shift the distribution toward supervision — any KL anchor to the SFT checkpoint would directly oppose this. No empirical comparison of β = 0 vs. β = 0.01 or β = 0.1 is provided, which is a gap: it would be informative to see whether a small amount of KL regularization (to prevent extreme drift) improves or degrades performance, especially since prior work on RLVR generally includes KL penalties as a stability measure.

**α = 0.5 is used without ablation:** The MoE reward weight α is set to 0.5, giving equal importance to perception and reasoning experts. The paper does not report any ablation of α (e.g., α = 0.3 vs. 0.5 vs. 0.7) to test whether the optimal trade-off is problem-dependent or scale-dependent. This is noted as a limitation in Appendix E: "The current MoE design relies on structured response formats... which may limit direct applicability to tasks that do not naturally admit such decomposition."

**PRISM standalone accuracy is comparable to SFT:** As noted in the main results, the PRISM row in Table 1 (post-alignment, pre-RLVR) shows accuracy comparable to the SFT checkpoint: 57.2 vs. 56.8 on 4B, 59.3 vs. 58.1 on 8B. This is presented as a feature, not a bug — alignment optimizes distribution, not answer correctness. However, it also means that the alignment stage's value proposition cannot be evaluated at the alignment checkpoint itself; it can only be measured indirectly through downstream RLVR performance. A practitioner running PRISM would not know whether alignment worked until they completed the full RLVR stage, which introduces a delayed feedback loop that may complicate debugging and hyperparameter tuning.

**Difficulty-based RL training set filter produces ~2K samples from 6K:** The paper reports that after sampling N=16 rollouts per problem from the aligned policy and filtering for pass rates in [0.2, 0.8], approximately 2K of the 6K reserved samples are retained. This means roughly two-thirds of the reserved samples fall outside the desired difficulty range — they are either too easy (pass rate > 0.8) or too hard (pass rate < 0.2) for the aligned policy. No breakdown of the easy vs. hard split is provided, nor is there an ablation testing whether including all 6K samples (i.e., no difficulty filter) changes downstream performance. The choice of the [0.2, 0.8] interval is attributed to prior work (Wang et al., 2025b; Zhang et al., 2026b) but is not itself ablated.

### Critical Assessment

#### Claim 1: "PRISM consistently improves downstream RLVR performance"

The experiments in Table 1 clearly demonstrate that PRISM+GRPO outperforms SFT→GRPO on average across seven benchmarks by +4.4 points (4B) and +6.0 points (8B). The improvement is consistent in the sense that every one of the seven benchmarks shows positive gain for PRISM+GRPO over SFT→GRPO on both model scales (14 out of 14 comparisons). The gains also persist across three different RL algorithms (GRPO, DAPO, GSPO), with PRISM+RL outperforming SFT→RL in all six comparisons (2 scales × 3 algorithms = 6 comparisons). This is strong evidence that the alignment stage provides a genuinely better initialization for downstream RL, rather than being an artifact of a particular RL algorithm or benchmark.

However, several qualifications are necessary. First, the paper compares PRISM's RLVR stage (1500 steps) against SFT→RL baselines that presumably run 2000 RL steps (matching the total training budget: 500 alignment + 1500 RL for PRISM vs. 0 alignment + 2000 RL for the baseline). If the baseline runs only 1500 RL steps, then PRISM has an unfair advantage of 500 extra steps (the alignment stage). The paper states that baselines are "trained with additional RLVR steps equal to those of the alignment stage" (Section 4.1), which implies 2000 steps, but this should be verified. If true, PRISM achieves higher performance with *less* total RL optimization, which actually strengthens the claim.

Second, the results are from a single base model family (Qwen3-VL) at two scales (4B and 8B). There is no evidence that the gains would transfer to other multimodal architectures (e.g., LLaVA-style models, InternVL, or proprietary models). The paper's argument that stronger models benefit more from alignment (because they have more to lose from SFT drift) predicts that PRISM should help *more* at larger scales, but the paper only evaluates up to 8B. A 32B or 72B Qwen3-VL experiment would provide a critical test of this prediction. At 8B, the SFT→GRPO baseline already recovers exactly the Instruct baseline (63.3), meaning RLVR contributes nothing net. It would be valuable to see whether an even larger model shows SFT→GRPO *underperforming* the Instruct baseline — which would make PRISM necessary, not just beneficial.

Third, the lack of any statistical significance testing (standard deviations, confidence intervals, multiple seed results) means we cannot assess whether a +4.4 point average gain is significantly different from noise. With 7 benchmarks, some of which have test splits of a few hundred to a few thousand examples, the standard error per benchmark is likely in the 1–3 percentage point range. A +4.4 point average across seven correlated measurements is probably significant, but the paper should report this.

#### Claim 2: "The MoE discriminator provides decoupled corrective signals that are essential for addressing heterogeneous multimodal drift"

The ablation in Table 2 comparing MoE discriminator vs. dense discriminator (−3.4 avg.) provides evidence that the MoE architecture matters. The further comparison of MoE vs. text-only discriminator (−3.9 avg.) confirms that the vision component is important. Together, these two ablations support the claim that the discriminator needs both (a) visual input to check grounding, and (b) dedicated experts to provide separate perception and reasoning feedback.

However, there is a confound: the dense discriminator ablation replaces the MoE architecture with a single dense model "of equal activated compute." The MoE uses four 2B experts with top-2 routing, meaning 4B activated parameters per forward pass. The dense model is a 4B Qwen3-VL. While the activated compute is matched, the total parameter count is not: the MoE has four 2B experts = 8B total parameters (only 4B activated per input). The dense model has only 4B total parameters. The paper does not clarify whether the dense model was trained from scratch for the alignment task (matching the MoE's warm-start and training steps) or whether an existing 4B model was used. If the MoE benefits from having 8B total parameters (with sparse activation) vs. the dense model's 4B, then the −3.4 point drop could partially reflect the parameter count difference rather than the MoE architecture per se. An ablation with a dense 8B model (or an MoE with 2B total activated parameters) would isolate the architectural contribution.

A more fundamental question: what evidence is there that the two experts are actually providing *decoupled* signals, as opposed to both learning similar things and the MoE simply being a more expressive architecture? The training dynamics in Figure 3 show different convergence trajectories, which is suggestive, but the trajectories could differ simply because perception and reasoning are different tasks (describing images vs. logical deduction) with different learning dynamics, not because the experts are providing decoupled corrective signals to the policy. An analysis that measured the correlation between the perception expert's score changes and the reasoning expert's score changes across training steps, or that showed that ablating one expert selectively degrades one axis of performance (e.g., caption quality vs. reasoning accuracy), would provide stronger evidence of decoupling. The paper does not report such analyses.

#### Claim 3: "The alignment stage substantially narrows the distributional gap left by SFT"

Figure 4 provides the primary evidence for this claim, showing that the post-alignment distribution of reasoning steps and caption items is closer to the supervision distribution than the post-SFT distribution is. The claim is visually supported but quantitatively weak: no divergence measure (KL, Wasserstein, Jensen-Shannon) is reported, no statistical test is applied, and the analysis is limited to two structural proxies.

The choice of proxies — number of reasoning steps and number of descriptive items — captures coarse-grained structural aspects of the response but not semantic content. A response could match the supervision distribution perfectly on both proxies (e.g., same number of steps, same number of description items) while still being factually wrong or logically inconsistent. The paper acknowledges this limitation: "developing model-agnostic alignment metrics remains an open problem" (Appendix E). However, there are existing metrics that could partially address this gap: semantic embedding distance (e.g., cosine similarity of response embeddings vs. supervision embeddings), n-gram overlap statistics (BLEU, ROUGE), or LLM-as-judge evaluations of response quality. The absence of any such semantic-level analysis weakens the claim that distributional alignment — in a meaningful sense beyond structural mimicry — has been achieved.

A stronger test would be to evaluate the post-alignment checkpoint on a held-out set of *explicitly perception-heavy* and *reasoning-heavy* prompts (e.g., prompts that ask "describe the image in detail" vs. "solve this math problem step by step") and show that the perception dimension improves on the former and the reasoning dimension improves on the latter, relative to the post-SFT checkpoint. The current benchmark evaluation (Table 1) conflates both dimensions, since all benchmarks require both perception and reasoning.

#### Claim 4: "PRISM+GRPO achieves higher accuracy with fewer tokens" (token efficiency)

Figure 5 shows that PRISM+GRPO uses fewer tokens than both Instruct and SFT+GRPO on three benchmarks (MathVision, MathVerse, MMMU-Pro) while achieving higher accuracy. This is genuinely striking — PRISM+GRPO uses ~3.3× fewer tokens than Instruct on MathVision (2,889 vs. 9,538) and ~2.0× fewer than SFT+GRPO (2,889 vs. 5,724). The paper interprets this as evidence that alignment encourages more concise and effective reasoning.

However, token count is a double-edged metric. If the model learns to produce shorter responses because it has been aligned to a supervision distribution that favors conciseness, that is positive. If it learns to produce shorter responses because the MoE discriminator penalizes verbose but correct responses (e.g., because verbose responses differ stylistically from the supervision data even when correct), then token reduction could come at the cost of reasoning faithfulness. The paper does not evaluate whether the *content* of PRISM's shorter responses is as complete as the longer SFT+GRPO responses — e.g., do the shorter responses contain all necessary reasoning steps, or are they skipping steps? A human evaluation or automated completeness check would address this.

Furthermore, token count is only reported for three of the seven benchmarks. If the token efficiency result were reproduced across all seven, it would strengthen the claim. If the other four benchmarks show different patterns (e.g., PRISM using *more* tokens on some), the claim would need qualification.

#### Structural Weaknesses and Missing Experiments

**No across-model-family generalization.** All experiments are on Qwen3-VL. Qwen3-VL uses a specific architecture (vision transformer + MLP projector + LLM backbone) and a specific pretraining recipe. Whether the PRISM pipeline would work on LLaVA-NeXT, InternVL2, or proprietary models like GPT-4V is unknown. At minimum, the claim that PRISM is a general pipeline architecture (not a Qwen3-VL-specific trick) would be strengthened by a single experiment on a different model family.

**No scaling beyond 8B.** The paper's own logic — that stronger models suffer more from SFT drift and benefit more from alignment — predicts larger gains at larger scales. The 8B model already shows SFT→GRPO contributing nothing net over Instruct (63.3 vs. 63.3). At 32B or 72B, one might expect SFT→GRPO to *underperform* Instruct, making PRISM's correction even more valuable. The absence of larger-scale experiments is a gap, though GPU constraints may be a practical limitation given the computational cost of training 32B+ models with adversarial alignment.

**No ablation of α (the perception/reasoning weight).** The MoE reward weight is fixed at α = 0.5, giving equal weight to perception and reasoning. It is plausible that the optimal α varies by model scale (larger models may have better native perception, requiring less perception correction), by benchmark (math-heavy benchmarks may need more reasoning weight), or dynamically during training. The paper does not explore any of these.

**No adversarial training dynamics for the "w/o SFT" ablation.** The paper reports that removing SFT causes catastrophic failure, attributing it to the discriminator saturating trivially. However, it does not report the training dynamics (reward gap curves) for this failed run, which would provide direct evidence for the claimed failure mode. Visualizing the discriminator's reward gap and the policy's output distribution for the "w/o SFT" variant would be informative.

**Limited evaluation of the standalone alignment checkpoint.** The PRISM row in Table 1 shows accuracy comparable to SFT, but no other properties of the aligned checkpoint are evaluated: response diversity, calibration, robustness to distribution shift, or performance on perception-only vs. reasoning-only sub-tasks. Since the paper's central claim is that alignment improves the *distribution* rather than the accuracy, a richer evaluation of the aligned distribution would strengthen the case.

**No comparison to alternative drift-mitigation methods.** The paper positions PRISM against the standard SFT→RL pipeline and against VOLD (Bousselham et al., 2025) conceptually, but provides no empirical comparison to VOLD. Other drift-mitigation approaches — such as SFT with KL regularization to the base model, iterative SFT with on-policy data collection, or simply applying RLVR for more steps — are not compared. A reader might reasonably ask: if SFT→GRPO with 2000 RL steps recovers Instruct performance on 8B (63.3 vs. 63.3), would SFT→GRPO with 4000 or 8000 RL steps eventually surpass PRISM? The paper does not explore whether simply running RLVR longer — without alignment — can achieve similar gains. The token efficiency result (Figure 5) hints that PRISM produces qualitatively different (more concise) reasoning than longer RL training might, but this is not directly tested.

**Small and potentially non-representative RL training set.** The RLVR stage trains on only ~2K difficulty-filtered problems. This is a very small training set by modern standards, and its composition depends on the aligned policy's pass rates — if the alignment stage changes which problems fall in the [0.2, 0.8] window, the RL training distribution changes too. The paper does not report whether the difficulty-filtered set differs systematically between the PRISM and SFT→RL pipelines (which would mean the two pipelines are training RL on different data, introducing a confound). It also does not report results with the full 6K un-filtered set, which would control for this.

#### Summary of Assessment

The experiments convincingly demonstrate that PRISM improves downstream RLVR performance over the standard SFT→RL baseline on Qwen3-VL at 4B and 8B scales, across seven diverse multimodal benchmarks and three RL algorithms. The gains are large in absolute terms (+4.4 to +6.0 average points) and consistent across all tested configurations. The ablation studies isolate the contributions of the MoE architecture, the vision component of the discriminator, the alignment stage itself, and the SFT data scale — each removal degrades performance, supporting the paper's architectural claims.

The claim that the alignment stage repairs *distributional* drift specifically (as opposed to improving any property that helps RL) is supported by the structural proxy analysis (Figure 4) but is the weakest link in the experimental chain. The evidence for distributional correction is limited to two coarse-grained structural proxies, lacks quantitative divergence metrics, and does not include semantic-level analysis. The claim that the two MoE experts provide *decoupled* corrective signals is plausible given the training dynamics (Figure 3) and the ablation (Table 2) but lacks direct evidence of decoupling (e.g., selective degradation of perception/reasoning when respective experts are removed, or correlation analysis of expert scores).

The strongest empirical finding — perhaps stronger than the paper emphasizes — is the token efficiency result (Figure 5): PRISM+GRPO achieves substantially higher accuracy with substantially fewer tokens than either Instruct or SFT+GRPO. This suggests that alignment is doing something more fundamental than just "making RL work better" — it is changing the model's reasoning style to be more concise and effective. This result deserves more analysis than it receives (e.g., human evaluation of response quality, decomposition of where the token savings come from, testing on additional benchmarks).

The paper's experimental design is careful in matching compute budgets between PRISM and baselines (the SFT→RL baselines get additional RL steps to match the alignment stage's step count), and the algorithm-agnostic demonstration (GRPO, DAPO, GSPO all benefit) is a strong robustness check. The main limitations are the single model family, the limited scale (max 8B), the absence of statistical significance reporting, the lack of direct distribution-divergence metrics, and the missing comparisons to alternative drift-mitigation approaches (longer RL, KL-regularized SFT, VOLD).

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Cost of Difficulty Estimation for RL Training Set Construction Is Substantial and Unaccounted For

**The assumption or constraint:** The Stage 3 RLVR stage trains on a difficulty-filtered subset of ~2K problems drawn from the 6K reserved supervision samples (Section 3.3, Appendix B). Constructing this filtered set requires sampling N = 16 rollouts from the aligned policy for each of the 6K candidate problems to compute pass rates — a total of 96,000 rollouts — and then retaining only problems whose pass rate falls in `[0.2, 0.8]`. The paper explicitly notes this cost (Appendix B: "we sample N = 16 rollouts per problem from the post-alignment policy and retain only problems whose pass rate falls within `[0.2, 0.8]`"), but this cost is **not amortized into any reported metric**. Total training step counts (500 alignment steps + 1500 RL steps) exclude this difficulty-estimation computation entirely. To put the scale in context: 96,000 rollouts at a maximum response length of 6144 tokens (the alignment stage's response length) represents a non-trivial fraction of the alignment stage's own compute budget (500 steps × batch size 4 = 2000 training prompts, each with 16 rollouts = 32,000 rollouts for alignment). Difficulty estimation could easily double the effective compute cost of the alignment stage, yet the paper's headline comparisons treat the alignment checkpoint as if the difficulty filter comes for free.

**The consequence:** A practitioner deploying PRISM would need to budget for this additional computation, which scales linearly with the size of the candidate RL training pool. The paper reports that roughly two-thirds of the 6K reserved samples are filtered out (~4K samples fall outside `[0.2, 0.8]`), meaning the vast majority of the difficulty-estimation rollouts are "wasted" in terms of downstream RL training data. If a practitioner wanted to construct a larger RL training set (e.g., 20K difficulty-filtered problems), the upfront difficulty-estimation cost would scale proportionally — 16 rollouts × 20K candidates = 320,000 rollouts — potentially exceeding the alignment stage's training cost. Furthermore, because the difficulty filter depends on the aligned policy's specific pass rates, any change to the alignment stage (different hyperparameters, different SFT data, different base model) would require recomputing the difficulty estimates from scratch. This makes the pipeline brittle to hyperparameter exploration: every alignment checkpoint variant would need its own difficulty-filtering pass before RL training could begin.

**What evidence exists in the paper:** The paper does not report the compute cost of difficulty estimation, does not include it in any FLOPs or step-count accounting, and does not ablate whether the difficulty filter is actually necessary (e.g., training RL on the full 6K unfiltered set and comparing performance). The ablation in Table 2 tests removing the alignment stage entirely but does not test removing or modifying the difficulty filter. The paper also does not report whether the difficulty-filtered set differs systematically between the PRISM pipeline and the SFT→RL baseline — if PRISM's alignment stage shifts which problems fall in `[0.2, 0.8]`, the two pipelines are training RL on different problem distributions, which would be a confound in the main comparison.

**Mitigation status:** Not addressed. The paper does not explore cheaper difficulty estimation methods (e.g., using fewer than 16 rollouts, using the PRM or discriminator scores as difficulty proxies, or training a lightweight difficulty predictor). The paper does not test whether simply using all 6K reserved problems for RL training (without filtering) produces comparable or better results. This is left entirely as an implicit cost that future work would need to address. A practitioner would need to either absorb this cost or develop their own difficulty-estimation shortcut when deploying PRISM.

---

### The Method Is Validated on a Single Model Family at a Single Scale Regime

**The assumption or constraint:** All experiments use Qwen3-VL at exactly two scales — 4B and 8B parameters (Section 4.1). The base models, the SFT policy, the MoE discriminator (assembled from four Qwen3-VL-2B experts), and the RLVR policy all belong to the same Qwen3-VL architecture family. The paper does not evaluate on any other multimodal architecture (e.g., LLaVA-NeXT, InternVL2, Molmo, or proprietary models) or at any scale beyond 8B parameters. The paper acknowledges this implicitly by stating that Qwen3-VL is used as the base model (Section 4.1) and by noting in Appendix E that "scaling to larger base models" is future work, but does not frame the single-model-family limitation as a threat to the generality of its claims. The paper also does not test whether the MoE discriminator architecture (four Qwen3-VL-2B experts with top-2 routing) is optimal or even functional when the base policy model uses a different architecture — for instance, whether the discriminator and policy need to share the same vision encoder for the perception expert's judgments to be meaningful.

**The consequence:** The central claim — that inserting an alignment stage with a MoE discriminator between SFT and RLVR consistently improves downstream performance — is currently bounded to Qwen3-VL at 4B–8B scale. Several aspects of PRISM could be architecture-specific. Qwen3-VL uses a particular vision encoder (a ViT variant), a particular multimodal projector (an MLP that maps visual features to the LLM's embedding space), and a particular pretraining recipe. The perception expert's ability to evaluate visual grounding depends on the discriminator's vision encoder being well-aligned with the policy's vision encoder — if a practitioner uses a different model family where the discriminator's and policy's visual representations diverge, the perception expert's scores may become unreliable. Similarly, the structured output format (`<caption>`, `thinking`, `<answer>`) is a Qwen3-VL-specific convention enforced by the system prompt (Appendix C, Figure 6). Models with different instruction-tuning styles may not reliably produce this format, breaking the decomposition that the MoE discriminator depends on. The paper's observation that stronger models suffer more from SFT drift (Qwen3-VL-8B degrades more than 4B under SFT) predicts that PRISM should help more at larger scales, but this prediction is untested — the 8B model already shows SFT→GRPO recovering exactly the Instruct baseline (63.3 vs. 63.3, Table 1), and it is unknown whether a 32B or 72B Qwen3-VL would show SFT→GRPO _underperforming_ the Instruct baseline, making PRISM's correction even more valuable.

**What evidence exists in the paper:** The paper provides no cross-model-family experiments. The main results (Table 1) show consistent gains within Qwen3-VL at 4B and 8B, but this is two data points from one architecture family. The paper does not discuss why Qwen3-VL was chosen, whether the specific architectural properties of Qwen3-VL (e.g., its MoE variant used for the discriminator) are required for the method to work, or whether similar gains would be expected on convolution-based vision backbones, different projector designs, or models with different pretraining objectives.

**Mitigation status:** The paper acknowledges "scaling to larger base models" as future work in Appendix E but does not treat the single-model-family limitation as a current weakness. No cross-architecture experiments are suggested. The Appendix E limitations section frames this as an expansion opportunity rather than a gap in the current evidence. A practitioner using a non-Qwen model family would need to assume that the results transfer, with no empirical basis for that assumption. Given that the discriminator is constructed from the same model family as the policy, transferring PRISM to a new architecture would require retraining the entire discriminator from scratch on that architecture — a significant engineering barrier.

### The Alignment Stage's Success Can Only Be Measured Indirectly Through Downstream RL Performance

**The assumption or constraint:** PRISM's alignment stage optimizes a distribution-matching objective — making the policy's rollouts indistinguishable from supervision — not an accuracy objective. The paper is explicit about this: "the alignment objective is distributional correction, reshaping the model's visual descriptions and reasoning traces to better match the supervision distribution, rather than directly optimizing for answer correctness" (Section 4.2). The consequence is that **there is no direct, stand-alone metric by which to evaluate whether alignment succeeded**. The post-alignment checkpoint (the "PRISM" row in Table 1) shows accuracy comparable to the post-SFT checkpoint: 57.2 vs. 56.8 on 4B, 59.3 vs. 58.1 on 8B. These differences (~0.4 and ~1.2 points respectively) are well within the range of sampling noise on the test sets. The only way to know whether the alignment stage worked is to run the full downstream RLVR stage (1500 additional steps) and compare against the SFT→RLVR baseline — a delayed and expensive feedback loop.

**The consequence:** This creates a severe practical challenge for hyperparameter tuning and debugging. A practitioner running PRISM must choose: the number of alignment steps (500 in the paper), the discriminator learning rate, the MoE weight `α`, the group size N, the rollout temperature, whether to use KL regularization (disabled by default), and the discriminator warm-start configuration. For any of these choices, the immediate post-alignment accuracy is near-identical to post-SFT accuracy regardless of whether the alignment stage is working well, working poorly, or actively harming the policy in ways that will only manifest during RLVR. The only feedback signal is downstream RLVR performance, which itself takes 1500 additional training steps to measure. This makes hyperparameter search combinatorially expensive: exploring K alignment configurations requires K full RLVR training runs, each costing 1500 steps. The training dynamics in Figure 3 provide some intermediate signal (the discriminator reward gap should stabilize rather than saturate), but the paper does not establish that the reward gap at convergence correlates with downstream RL performance — it shows only that the MoE discriminator converges, not that convergence quality predicts RL gains.

Furthermore, this indirect evaluation makes it impossible to isolate _why_ alignment helps. The paper claims it repairs distributional drift, but the evidence for this claim is limited to two structural proxies (Figure 4: reasoning steps and caption items) with no quantitative divergence measures. If a future practitioner runs PRISM and sees no downstream RL improvement, they cannot diagnose whether (a) the alignment stage failed to move the distribution, (b) the distribution moved but the wrong direction, (c) the distribution moved correctly but the specific RL algorithm doesn't benefit from that particular distribution, or (d) the alignment stage overfit to the supervision pool and reduced the policy's diversity in ways that hurt RL exploration. The current pipeline provides no diagnostic tools to distinguish these failure modes.

**What evidence exists in the paper:** The PRISM row in Table 1 shows accuracy nearly identical to SFT on both scales. Figure 4 shows structural proxy distributions shifting toward supervision after alignment, but these are not validated as predictive of downstream RL success. The paper does not report any correlation between the degree of distributional shift (e.g., the KL divergence between post-alignment and supervision distributions on the proxy metrics) and the magnitude of downstream RL improvement. The paper does not test whether early-stopping the alignment stage at different points (e.g., 100, 250, 500 steps) produces different downstream RL outcomes — the 500-step choice is justified by discriminator convergence (Figure 3) but not by downstream RL performance.

**Mitigation status:** The paper does not address this limitation. It does not propose any stand-alone evaluation metric for the alignment stage, any diagnostic tool for practitioners, or any method for predicting downstream RL performance from alignment-stage metrics. The acknowledgement in Appendix E that "developing model-agnostic alignment metrics remains an open problem" is relevant but not connected to the specific practical problem that the PRISM pipeline itself creates. Future work would need to develop intermediate evaluation signals — e.g., held-out discriminator accuracy, distribution divergence measures that correlate with downstream RL gains, or lightweight RL probes that estimate downstream performance from a few dozen steps — to make the pipeline practically tunable.

### Hard Problems Remain Unsolved: Test-Time and Training-Time Compute Cannot Compensate for Fundamental Capability Gaps

**The assumption or constraint:** PRISM operates entirely within the post-training phase of a fixed base model (Qwen3-VL at a given scale). All three stages — SFT, alignment, and RLVR — modify the policy through fine-tuning on a fixed dataset derived from a fixed supervision source (Gemini 3 Flash). The pipeline assumes that the base model already possesses the fundamental capability to produce supervision-quality responses if properly aligned — it just needs its distribution corrected. This assumption breaks down for problems where the base model genuinely lacks the visual recognition or reasoning capacity to approach the supervision quality, regardless of how the post-training budget is allocated. The paper provides indirect evidence of this boundary: the difficulty filter for RL training discards problems with pass rate < 0.2 (Section 3.3), meaning problems where the aligned policy succeeds less than 20% of the time — i.e., problems that are genuinely too hard for the model at its current scale — are excluded from RL training entirely. The paper does not report what fraction of the original 113K curated problems fall into this "too hard" category for each model scale, nor whether this fraction decreases (as expected) when moving from 4B to 8B.

**The consequence:** PRISM's gains are concentrated on problems that the base model can already solve at some non-trivial rate — the alignment stage and RLVR amplify existing capability but do not create new capability from nothing. This is evident in the benchmark-level results: on MathVista, where the Instruct baseline is already high (74.9 on 4B, 76.0 on 8B), PRISM+GRPO achieves more modest gains (+3.0 and +2.3 points over Instruct respectively). On MathVision, where the Instruct baseline is low (36.5 on 4B, 43.7 on 8B), PRISM+GRPO achieves much larger gains (+8.9 and +8.3 points respectively). This inverse correlation between baseline difficulty and absolute gain is expected, but it means PRISM does not solve the hard-problem challenge — it makes the model better at problems it already has some traction on. On the hardest problems (those filtered out of RL training with pass rate < 0.2), PRISM provides no improvement at all because those problems are never used for RL optimization. A practitioner hoping that PRISM will enable a 4B model to solve problems that previously required a 70B model is likely to be disappointed: the pipeline improves within-scale capability but does not cross the fundamental capability boundaries set by model scale and pretraining.

**What evidence exists in the paper:** The paper does not directly measure performance on the hardest held-out problems (the ones filtered out of RL training). The difficulty filter's [0.2, 0.8] criterion is mentioned (Section 3.3) but the paper does not report pass-rate distributions for the full 113K curated set at either model scale, does not compare performance on the filtered-out (< 0.2 pass rate) problems between PRISM and baselines, and does not test whether including those hard problems in RL training (despite low pass rates) would provide any benefit. The benchmark results in Table 1 provide indirect evidence: all benchmarks show positive gains from PRISM+GRPO over Instruct, but the gains vary substantially (from +2.3 on MathVista-8B to +14.9 on MathVision-8B), consistent with the hypothesis that PRISM helps more where the model already has some competence.

**Mitigation status:** The paper acknowledges this limitation implicitly by noting in Appendix E that "extending PRISM to broader task formats" is future work, but does not frame the hard-problem boundary as a fundamental limitation of the approach. The difficulty filter is presented as a _feature_ (focusing RL on productive training examples) rather than as a symptom of a capability ceiling. A practitioner dealing with a problem distribution containing many genuinely hard problems (pass rate near zero) would find that PRISM's training pipeline filters most of them out and provides no mechanism for improving performance on them — the alignment stage doesn't help because the model can't approach the supervision distribution on those problems, and the RL stage never sees them. Scaling the base model (to 8B, 32B, 72B) is the only recourse the paper offers, which is a pretraining-scale solution rather than a post-training one.

### The Alignment Stage Is Fundamentally Architecture- and Format-Dependent, Limiting Applicability to Unstructured Domains

**The assumption or constraint:** PRISM's MoE discriminator relies on a strict structural decomposition of each response into two separable components: a visual description (`<caption>` section) and a reasoning trace (`thinking` section). The perception expert `D_v` evaluates only the caption, and the reasoning expert `D_r` evaluates only the thinking trace (Section 3.2.2). This decomposition is enforced by the system prompt (Appendix C, Figure 6) which mandates the three-part XML output format: "Your output must strictly follow this XML format: `<caption>...</caption>` `thinking...` `<answer>...</answer>`." The discriminator's ability to provide decoupled corrective signals depends entirely on the model reliably producing responses with clearly separable visual-grounding and logical-deduction components. The paper acknowledges this dependency in Appendix E: "the current MoE design relies on structured response formats (explicit visual caption and reasoning trace) to decompose perception and reasoning feedback, which may limit direct applicability to tasks that do not naturally admit such decomposition."

**The consequence:** PRISM is not a general-purpose post-training pipeline for all multimodal tasks — it is specifically designed for tasks where (a) the response can be decomposed into a visual description phase and a reasoning phase, and (b) the model can be prompted to produce responses in this structured format. This excludes several important multimodal task categories. **Visual question answering with short answers:** Tasks where the expected output is a single word or phrase (e.g., "What color is the car?" → "Red") have no separable reasoning trace — the caption _is_ the answer. The MoE discriminator would have empty or degenerate `thinking` sections, breaking the decoupled feedback mechanism. **Open-ended generation tasks:** Image captioning, visual storytelling, and dialogue about images do not naturally decompose into separate caption and reasoning sections — the visual description and the response are intertwined. **Tasks without visual grounding:** Purely textual reasoning tasks (math word problems without images, code generation from text specifications) have no visual component, making the perception expert irrelevant and the MoE design wasteful. **Multi-turn or interactive tasks:** The structured format assumes a single response per prompt; multi-turn conversations where visual grounding and reasoning are interleaved across turns cannot be straightforwardly formatted.

Furthermore, even for tasks that _can_ be formatted, the requirement that both the policy and the supervision data follow the same strict XML structure constrains the data pipeline. The curated 113K dataset (Appendix B) was generated by Gemini 3 Flash using a prompt that enforces this exact format (Appendix C, Figure 7). A practitioner wanting to use a different supervision source — e.g., human-written explanations, textbook solutions, or outputs from a different proprietary model with a different output style — would need to either (a) reformat all supervision data to match the XML structure, potentially losing fidelity, or (b) retrain the discriminator to work with a different decomposition, which may require a different expert architecture. The paper does not explore whether the structured format itself contributes to the gains (e.g., by making the reasoning more explicit and verifiable) independently of the MoE discriminator.

**What evidence exists in the paper:** The ablation replacing the vision-language discriminator with a text-only variant (Table 2) demonstrates that the vision component matters for benchmarks requiring visual grounding (WeMath: −6.0, MathVerse: −4.9), but this ablation still uses the structured format — the text-only discriminator sees the `<caption>` and `thinking` sections, just without the image. There is no ablation testing PRISM on tasks without structured outputs (e.g., training without the system prompt and evaluating whether the MoE discriminator can still provide useful signals from unstructured responses). The paper does not evaluate on any benchmark that does not naturally fit the caption-think-answer format (all seven benchmarks are math/science reasoning tasks with clear visual components).

**Mitigation status:** The paper acknowledges the limitation in Appendix E: "Future work includes extending PRISM to broader task formats by exploring automatic or learned decomposition of discriminator feedback." This suggests that the current manual decomposition (`<caption>` vs. `thinking`) could be replaced with a learned decomposition — e.g., training the discriminator to automatically identify which portions of a response relate to perception vs. reasoning without explicit XML tags. However, this is speculative and no experiments toward this direction are reported. For current deployment, PRISM is restricted to tasks where structured output formatting with explicit visual-grounding and reasoning sections is feasible and natural. A practitioner working on general-purpose multimodal chatbots, open-ended visual understanding, or tasks without clear perception/reasoning separation would need to develop their own decomposition strategy before applying PRISM.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

PRISM makes a conceptual move that repositions how the field should think about the SFT-to-RL transition in post-training pipelines: **the gap between SFT and RLVR is not empty space — it is a distinct optimization problem with its own objective, requiring its own stage.** Prior to this work, the dominant assumption was that SFT installs a reasoning prior and RLVR refines it, with the interface between them treated as solved. PRISM demonstrates that this interface systematically fails in ways that matter for downstream performance, and that the failure is not just a matter of insufficient SFT quality or insufficient RL optimization — it is a *distributional* mismatch that neither stage's objective addresses.

The magnitude of this shift is best characterized as **a reframing with structural consequences**, not a paradigm shift. The individual components — SFT, on-policy distillation, adversarial training, RLVR with verifiable rewards — are all established techniques. What is new is the claim that they must be arranged in a specific three-stage sequence where the middle stage serves a purpose (distribution alignment) that is fundamentally different from both the preceding stage (token-level imitation) and the following stage (outcome optimization). If this claim holds across model families and scales, it implies that the standard two-stage post-training recipe is architecturally incomplete — not just suboptimal, but missing a necessary component. The paper's evidence that the 8B model's SFT→GRPO pipeline recovers *exactly* the Instruct baseline (63.3 vs. 63.3 in Table 1) while PRISM+GRPO exceeds it by 6.0 points is the empirical signature that something essential is missing from the standard recipe.

The paper also resolves a tension in the multimodal RL literature. Prior work had observed that standard RLVR neglects visual perception fidelity and proposed perception-aware reward signals within the RL stage (Xiao et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025a; Gao et al., 2026). These methods implicitly assume that the problem is the *reward* — if the RL objective cared about perception, the model would learn to ground its reasoning. PRISM's finding that the problem is upstream of the reward — in the *distribution* of the policy entering RL — suggests that perception-aware RL rewards are treating symptoms rather than causes. The alignment stage repairs perception drift *before* RL begins, so that subsequent outcome optimization operates on a distribution already aligned with faithful visual grounding. This does not make perception-aware RL obsolete — the two approaches could be complementary — but it redirects research attention from reward engineering to *initialization engineering*: the distributional state of the policy at the start of RL matters more than previously recognized.

The paper's most practically significant shift is the finding that **SFT-induced drift is worse for stronger models** (the 8B model loses 5.2 points under SFT vs. 2.9 for the 4B model in Table 1). This inverts the natural intuition that stronger models are more robust to post-training perturbations. If this pattern generalizes — and the paper provides a plausible mechanism (stronger models have more competent native reasoning distributions that token-level imitation displaces rather than supplements) — then the standard SFT→RL pipeline becomes *increasingly* problematic as base models improve. A hypothetical future model where the Instruct baseline achieves 80% average accuracy might degrade to 70% under SFT and recover only to 75% under RLVR, while a PRISM-style alignment stage could push it to 85%. The paper's contribution is not just a method that works today but a **diagnostic principle** that predicts where and why the standard pipeline will break as models scale.

Research directions that become more attractive:
- **Distribution-level diagnostics for post-training checkpoints.** The paper shows that SFT accuracy and downstream RL performance can be decoupled (post-SFT and post-alignment have similar accuracy but dramatically different RL outcomes). This suggests that the field needs new metrics — beyond validation loss and downstream benchmark scores — that measure the *distributional alignment* of a post-SFT policy. The paper's structural proxies (reasoning steps, caption items in Figure 4) are a crude first step, but they establish the principle that distribution shape matters independently of point estimates.
- **Pipeline architecture design as a first-class research problem.** Most post-training research focuses on improving individual stages (better SFT data, better RL algorithms). PRISM suggests that the *arrangement* of stages — how many, in what order, with what objectives, with what information passed between them — is itself a design space that can yield gains comparable to algorithmic improvements within stages. This opens a new axis of research that is orthogonal to, and potentially multiplicative with, advances in SFT and RL individually.
- **Decoupled feedback for heterogeneous errors.** The paper's MoE discriminator provides separate corrective signals for perception and reasoning errors. This principle — that different error modes require different feedback channels — could generalize beyond the perception/reasoning decomposition. Code generation might decompose into syntax errors vs. algorithmic errors. Translation might decompose into fluency errors vs. fidelity errors. Any domain where errors are heterogeneous in ways that a single scalar reward cannot disentangle is a candidate for MoE-style decoupled feedback.

Research directions that become less attractive:
- **Improving RL algorithms in isolation, without attending to the initialization distribution.** The paper shows that three different RL algorithms (GRPO, DAPO, GSPO) all benefit similarly from PRISM alignment, and that their performance differences compress when starting from a well-aligned initialization. This suggests that, at current levels of algorithmic sophistication, the quality of the RL initialization is a larger lever than the choice of RL algorithm. Research that pushes RL algorithms without simultaneously improving the pipeline architecture may hit diminishing returns.
- **Using off-policy data for distribution matching in multimodal settings.** The paper's ablation showing that SFT alone cannot repair distributional drift (the SFT→GRPO baseline) and that adversarial on-policy distillation succeeds suggests that *on-policy* interaction with the supervision distribution is essential for alignment. Off-policy methods (e.g., training a discriminator once on static data and using it as a fixed reward model) would likely suffer from the staleness problem that PRISM's on-policy discriminator updates avoid.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Direct measurement of distributional drift and its correlation with downstream RL gains.** The paper argues that alignment repairs distributional drift, but the evidence for this repair is limited to two structural proxies (Figure 4) with no quantitative divergence metrics and no demonstration that the degree of drift reduction predicts downstream RL performance. A critical follow-up would measure the KL divergence (or Wasserstein distance, or Jensen-Shannon divergence) between the post-SFT rollout distribution and the supervision distribution — at the level of token sequences, semantic embeddings, and the structural proxies — across multiple SFT checkpoints (varying data, regularization, training steps). The key test: does the checkpoint with the smallest distributional divergence to supervision also produce the best downstream RLVR performance? If yes, distributional divergence becomes a practical early-stopping and model-selection criterion for SFT, decoupled from the delayed RLVR feedback loop. If no, then PRISM's alignment stage is doing something beyond what a simple divergence measure captures, and the mechanism needs further investigation. This experiment requires no new methods, only a systematic sweep of SFT configurations and a consistent divergence measurement protocol.

**Scaling PRISM to larger models to test the stronger-model-worse-drift hypothesis.** The paper's own logic predicts that the SFT→RL pipeline will fail more severely at larger scales, but experiments only go up to 8B. A natural follow-up would apply PRISM to Qwen3-VL-32B and/or Qwen3-VL-72B (or their successors) and test two predictions: (1) SFT→GRPO will *underperform* the Instruct baseline (the 8B model already shows zero net gain; at 32B, the drift costs should dominate any RL gains), and (2) PRISM+GRPO will recover and exceed the Instruct baseline by a larger margin than at 8B (because the alignment stage has more drift to repair). This experiment would also stress-test the MoE discriminator scaling: the paper uses four 2B experts; at 32B or 72B policy scale, the discriminator experts might need to scale proportionally to remain competitive in the adversarial game. An important negative result — if the larger model does *not* show worse SFT drift or *does* show SFT→GRPO outperforming Instruct — would invalidate the paper's central diagnostic principle and suggest that the 8B result is an anomaly or a Qwen3-VL-specific quirk.

**Ablation of the structured response format to test whether the MoE decomposition requires explicit XML tags.** The paper's MoE discriminator depends on responses being parseable into separate `<caption>` and `thinking` sections. Would PRISM work if the model produced free-form responses without explicit structural tags, and the discriminator used a learned mechanism (e.g., an attention-based segmentation, or a separate lightweight model) to identify which spans correspond to visual grounding vs. reasoning? A concrete experiment: train PRISM without the system prompt that enforces structured output, and instead train the discriminator to accept the full response and internally route different tokens to different experts (e.g., using token-level expert routing rather than section-level). Compare against the structured-output PRISM on the same benchmarks. If the unstructured variant performs comparably, PRISM generalizes to tasks where structured output is unnatural. If it fails, the structured format is not just a convenience but a necessary scaffold for the MoE decomposition — informing practitioners about which task formats are compatible with the method.

**Testing whether the alignment stage can be amortized across multiple downstream RL objectives.** PRISM currently runs one alignment stage followed by one RLVR stage on a specific benchmark distribution. An important practical question: if a practitioner wants to deploy the same base model for multiple downstream tasks (math reasoning, chart understanding, scientific diagram analysis), do they need a separate alignment stage for each task, or can one alignment stage prepare the model for multiple RL specializations? Experiment: run PRISM alignment once on a broad supervision pool covering multiple task types, then run separate RLVR stages on task-specific subsets. Compare against (a) running separate PRISM alignment + RL for each task, and (b) running task-specific SFT→RL without alignment. If amortized alignment works, PRISM becomes much more cost-effective because the expensive alignment stage is paid once; if not, the alignment stage's benefit is task-specific, and the cost multiplies with the number of downstream tasks. The paper's finding that alignment improves downstream RLVR across diverse benchmarks (math and general multimodal in Table 1) already provides suggestive evidence for amortization, but the experiment should explicitly compare single-alignment vs. per-task-alignment.

**Replacing the adversarial game with a divergence-based objective when teacher logits are available.** The paper's black-box adversarial formulation is motivated by the inaccessibility of Gemini 3 Flash logits. When the supervision source is an open-weight model (e.g., a larger Qwen model, or a fine-tuned Llama variant), logit-based divergence minimization (reverse KL, forward KL, or a hybrid) becomes feasible. This raises a natural question: is the adversarial formulation *necessary* for the alignment benefits, or would a simpler divergence-based OPD (e.g., GKD-style training with reverse KL on policy rollouts) achieve similar downstream RL gains? Experiment: compare PRISM's adversarial alignment against a variant where the MoE discriminator is replaced with a fixed reward model that computes the log-likelihood ratio log p_supervision(y|x) / log p_policy(y|x) (or similar), both using the same MoE architecture (perception and reasoning components evaluated separately). If the divergence-based variant performs comparably, practitioners with open-weight supervision models can avoid the complexity and instability of adversarial training. If the adversarial variant is substantially better, it suggests that the minimax dynamic provides a benefit beyond what static divergence matching can achieve — perhaps by providing harder negative examples as the policy improves.

**Characterizing what the alignment stage does to the policy's internal representations.** The paper shows that alignment shifts the output distribution (Figure 4), but provides no evidence about *how* the policy's internal computation changes. Does alignment make the model's visual representations more faithful to the image? Does it change how attention is distributed between visual tokens and text tokens during reasoning? A mechanistic follow-up could probe the aligned vs. post-SFT policy using techniques from interpretability: compare attention maps over image regions during caption generation, measure whether the model's hidden states for visual descriptions become more similar to the supervision model's hidden states (if accessible), or use patching experiments to test whether the perception and reasoning components of the policy become more modular (e.g., does ablating the vision encoder affect reasoning accuracy less in the aligned model, because reasoning relies on the caption rather than raw visual features?). This would transform the paper's claim from "the output distribution moves closer to supervision" (a behavioral claim) to "the model learns to see and think more like the supervision model" (a mechanistic claim). Negative results — e.g., the aligned model produces supervision-like outputs through superficial textual patterns without improved visual representations — would reveal important limitations of distribution-level alignment.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Cost-efficient post-training for multimodal reasoning models in industry.** An organization training a multimodal reasoning assistant (e.g., for math tutoring, scientific document analysis, or chart interpretation) typically follows the SFT→RL recipe. If they use a strong open-weight base model (analogous to Qwen3-VL-8B), the paper's results suggest that the standard pipeline will yield *zero net improvement* over the base Instruct model on average (Table 1: SFT→GRPO achieves 63.3, identical to Instruct). The PRISM pipeline, by inserting a 500-step alignment stage, recovers this lost potential and adds +6.0 average points — transforming a failed post-training investment into a successful one. The cost is an extra ~500 training steps (alignment) plus the one-time difficulty-estimation rollouts (~96K rollouts for a 6K candidate pool), but these costs are modest relative to the total post-training budget if RLVR itself runs thousands of steps. The token efficiency gains (Figure 5: ~2× fewer tokens than SFT+GRPO, ~3.3× fewer than Instruct on MathVision, with higher accuracy) translate to lower inference costs in deployment — a model that produces shorter correct responses reduces per-query latency and serving cost, which compounds across millions of queries. The concrete recipe for a practitioner: (1) curate a high-quality supervision pool with structured perception-reasoning decomposition, (2) run SFT on broad data, (3) run PRISM alignment for 500 steps on a held-out high-quality subset, (4) difficulty-filter the RL training set with 16 rollouts per problem from the aligned policy, (5) run RLVR for 1500 steps, and (6) select the best checkpoint based on validation accuracy.

**Improving multimodal reasoning in domains with expensive or scarce human annotation.** The paper's data curation pipeline (Appendix B) uses Gemini 3 Flash as a black-box teacher to generate high-quality reasoning demonstrations from problems with known ground-truth answers. This is a form of *automated supervision* that requires only problem-answer pairs (which can be generated from textbooks, exams, or rule-based generators) and a strong teacher model. A medical imaging lab wanting to train a multimodal model for radiology report generation could adapt this pipeline: collect X-ray images with verified diagnoses (ground-truth answers), prompt a capable multimodal model (Gemini, GPT-4V, or a fine-tuned specialist model) to produce detailed structured reports with visual grounding and diagnostic reasoning, filter for correctness against the verified diagnoses, and use the resulting corpus for PRISM-style post-training of a smaller deployable model. The PRISM pipeline is particularly suitable here because medical imaging requires precise visual grounding (misidentifying an anatomical structure leads to a wrong diagnosis) — exactly the kind of heterogeneous perception-reasoning drift that the MoE discriminator is designed to correct. The alignment stage would help the smaller model's visual descriptions match the quality of the teacher's, and RLVR would optimize for diagnostic accuracy from that aligned foundation. The key adaptation would be defining task-appropriate decomposition (anatomical description vs. diagnostic reasoning instead of generic caption vs. math reasoning) and curating the supervision pool accordingly.

**Self-improvement pipelines where a model iteratively generates its own training data.** The paper's finding that PRISM+GRPO produces more concise reasoning traces with higher accuracy (Figure 5) has implications for self-improvement loops (e.g., STaR, ReST^EM, or iterative rejection sampling). In a typical self-improvement loop, the model generates solutions to training problems, correct solutions are filtered and added to the training set, and the model is retrained. If the model's outputs are verbose and imprecise (as the Instruct and SFT+GRPO models are in Figure 5), the self-improvement data is noisy — it contains unnecessarily long, potentially flawed reasoning that degrades subsequent training iterations. PRISM's alignment stage teaches the model to produce supervision-quality reasoning traces (structured, grounded, concise), which would make the filtered self-improvement data substantially cleaner. A concrete pipeline: (1) use PRISM to produce an aligned policy, (2) use the aligned policy to generate solutions on a large unlabeled problem set, (3) filter for correctness using verifiable rewards, (4) add the filtered solutions to the SFT corpus, (5) retrain the aligned policy on the expanded corpus, and (6) repeat. Each iteration benefits from the alignment stage's conciseness and grounding, potentially avoiding the degeneration that sometimes occurs in iterative self-training (as observed in the paper's negative ReST^EM result in Appendix K, where unaligned revision models degraded).

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper explicitly positions PRISM against the standard two-stage SFT→RL pipeline and implicitly against methods that add perception rewards within RL (Xiao et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025a) or that combine distillation with RL in a single stage (VOLD, Bousselham et al., 2025). The decision rule that emerges from the paper's analysis:

- **Prefer PRISM (three-stage pipeline with MoE alignment) when:**
  - The base model is strong (has a competent native reasoning distribution that SFT might damage). The paper shows SFT degrades the 8B model more than the 4B model, and the standard SFT→RL pipeline recovers nothing on 8B. Stronger models are the primary beneficiaries.
  - The supervision source is a black-box model (no logit access). PRISM's adversarial formulation works with response-level samples only, which is essential when supervision comes from proprietary models like Gemini, GPT-4V, or Claude.
  - The task naturally decomposes into perception and reasoning components that can be formatted as separate response sections. The MoE discriminator requires this decomposition to provide decoupled feedback.
  - Downstream deployment values concise outputs. PRISM+GRPO produces substantially shorter responses than SFT+GRPO while achieving higher accuracy (Figure 5), which reduces inference costs.
  - The post-training compute budget is moderate — the alignment stage adds ~500 steps of adversarial training, which is a non-trivial but manageable overhead. If the total RLVR budget is thousands of steps, the relative cost is small; if the RLVR budget is itself very small (e.g., 100-200 steps), the alignment stage might dominate the total cost.

- **Prefer the standard SFT→RL pipeline (skip alignment) when:**
  - The base model is weak relative to the supervision distribution (SFT provides large capability gains that outweigh any drift costs). The paper does not test this regime directly, but the logic implies that weak models might not suffer the stronger-model-worse-drift problem.
  - The task cannot be decomposed into separable perception and reasoning components (e.g., open-ended visual dialogue, creative image captioning, or tasks where the output is a single short answer without reasoning traces).
  - Latency or total training time is tightly constrained and the 500-step alignment stage overhead is prohibitive.
  - Teacher logits are accessible and a simpler divergence-based distillation method (GKD, MiniLLM) can be applied directly — though the paper does not empirically compare against this alternative, so the tradeoff is speculative.
