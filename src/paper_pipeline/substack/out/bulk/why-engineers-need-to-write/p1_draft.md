Here are the takeaways from this Ryan Peterman interview (the host of The Peterman Post on technical careers, making the case for writing as a core engineering skill):

**Writing is a necessity, not an accessory**

Ryan opens with a confession: he hated writing in high school. It felt subjective, especially compared to math and science, and parsing Shakespeare didn't help. His perspective flipped once he started working full-time as a software engineer. Around the time he became a mid-level engineer and began leading a small workstream of a few engineers, he noticed a shift—he was writing more and more without consciously deciding to. "Soon writing became a significant part of my work outside of coding." The trend accelerated during COVID, when most conversations moved to async chats and word docs, further cementing writing as the primary medium of collaboration.

The core argument is straightforward: "Almost everything software engineers do requires writing." Asking technical questions, commenting on code reviews, creating design docs—all of it is writing. "This is because writing software is collaborative." The corollary is that writing skill is not a nice-to-have; "the better you are at writing, the more effective you will be at building software."

**Scaling yourself through writing**

Beyond being a daily necessity, writing is "the most efficient way for you to scale yourself." Ryan gives a concrete example: a friend who takes incredible notes at work. Those notes become a living wiki of common terminal commands, code pointers, and diagrams for the whole team. "The best part is that his writing helps others move faster without any work on his part after sharing it." This is leverage in its purest form—effort expended once, value extracted repeatedly.

He frames the career math bluntly: "At the end of the day, you can only ship so much code by yourself. You can have much more impact by influencing others to ship what matters most." And "writing is the most scalable way to influence others."

**Writing improves thinking, with or without AI**

Ryan addresses the obvious objection: won't ChatGPT make writing obsolete? His response is that writing's value persists regardless of AI assistance. "Writing your thoughts down forces them into a coherent, logical narrative. Condensing your writing gives you a deeper understanding. This process improves your thinking." Moreover, writing isn't just a reflection of existing ideas—"it generates more of them." When writing design docs, he often discovers optimizations or edge cases he hadn't considered initially. "So long as critical thinking is necessary, writing will not be obsolete."

He closes by observing that "it is no coincidence that the strongest engineers I know are all excellent technical writers." The skill is not orthogonal to engineering effectiveness; it is central to it. That conviction is what prompted his own commitment to writing once a week for his Substack—a practice that is both the medium and the message.
