# Make Each Token Count: Towards Improving Long-Context Performance with KV Cache Eviction

**ArXiv:** [2605.09649](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.09649)

## 🎯 Pitch

Forcing a language model to attend to all tokens in a huge context window can actually make it dumber by diluting focus, but this paper shows that learning to selectively throw away the right tokens doesn't just save memory—it makes the model smarter, outperforming full-context inference on complex reasoning and dialogue tasks.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **DBTrimKV** (Dynamic Budget TrimKV), a learned KV cache eviction method that uses globally calibrated retention scores to jointly perform token selection and dynamic budget allocation across layers, heads, and modalities under a single memory constraint. The key insight is that full-cache attention is not always optimal in long contexts — irrelevant tokens can dilute attention away from useful evidence (attention dilution) — so selective, learnable eviction can actually *improve* generation rather than merely approximate the full cache. Across long-context language and vision-language reasoning benchmarks (MathVisionmini, MMMUPro, VideoMME, AIME24) and multi-turn dialogue (MMDU), using models including Qwen3-VL-8B-Thinking and Qwen3-4B, DBTrimKV matches or surpasses full-cache inference while using substantially less KV memory — achieving up to 14% relative improvement over vanilla full-cache decoding in multi-turn dialogue at a 512-token budget, and exceeding full-cache performance by 3.73% on long-horizon image and video reasoning at a 512-token budget — establishing that learned, globally calibrated KV eviction functions not just as a compression technique but as an attention regularization mechanism, though these gains require the base model's representations to already contain the needed information.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: KV Cache Management in Long-Context Inference

The paper addresses a fundamental tension in deploying transformer-based language and vision-language models on long contexts: **the key-value (KV) cache, which enables efficient autoregressive decoding by storing past keys and values, grows linearly with sequence length, and the attention computation over cached tokens scales with the amount of retained context**. This becomes a critical bottleneck when prompts contain tens of thousands of text tokens or hundreds to thousands of visual tokens from images and videos. As the authors state in Section 1:

> "As context windows continue to expand, KV cache management has become one of the main challenges for practical long-context inference."

This is not merely a memory problem — it is a **memory, computation, and quality** problem simultaneously. The KV cache size directly determines GPU memory consumption during inference. The attention computation over cached tokens incurs a quadratic cumulative cost over the generation process (Keles et al., 2023, cited in Section 2.1). And crucially, the *quality* of attention — how well the model attends to relevant information — degrades as irrelevant tokens accumulate in the cache and compete for attention mass. This third dimension (quality degradation due to distractors) is the paper's central theoretical contribution and what distinguishes its framing from prior work.

### Why This Problem Matters

The practical significance is immediate and growing. As the context windows of production LLMs and VLMs expand — from 4K to 32K, 128K, and beyond — the KV cache has become the dominant bottleneck in long-context inference. Several deployment scenarios amplify this pressure:

**Long-horizon reasoning.** When models must generate hundreds or thousands of reasoning tokens (as in chain-of-thought for competition math problems, or multi-step visual reasoning), the KV cache grows with every generated token. The initial prompt may contain extensive context (documents, images, video frames), and the model's own output adds further cache entries. Without eviction, memory consumption grows without bound through the entire generation.

**Multi-turn dialogue.** In interactive settings like the MMDU benchmark (used extensively in Section 5.3), the model must retain information introduced many turns earlier — perhaps an image shown at the start of a conversation, or a constraint mentioned several exchanges ago — while accommodating new questions at each turn. The cache accumulates across turns, and evidence from early turns must compete with a growing volume of newer but less relevant tokens.

**Multimodal inputs.** Vision-language models add a new dimension to the problem. Visual tokens — often hundreds or thousands per image — dominate the cache volume but vary enormously in their utility. Some visual patches encode task-critical information (the text on a sign, the coordinates of a geometric figure); others are background noise. The paper shows that the ratio of useful to distracting visual tokens changes dynamically during decoding: in early generation steps, the model needs broad visual context to understand the scene, but as reasoning narrows to specific details, most visual tokens become distractors (Section 5.2, Figure 6).

**Throughput and cost at scale.** For production deployments serving many concurrent requests, KV cache memory is the primary constraint on batch size. Reducing the per-request cache footprint enables higher throughput and lower cost-per-query. The paper demonstrates that DBTrimKV with PagedAttention remains highly efficient at scale, with decoding time growing sub-linearly compared to full-cache inference (Section 5.4, Figure 7).

### Where Existing Approaches Fall Short

The paper identifies three distinct categories of prior work, each with specific limitations that motivate the proposed approach.

#### 1. Heuristic Eviction Methods: Approximating Full-Cache Attention at the Cost of Quality

The dominant paradigm for KV eviction treats it as a **compression problem**: the goal is to approximate full-cache inference while reducing memory and computation (Li et al., 2024b, cited in Section 1). The objective formalized in Section 2.1 (Equation 2) expresses this directly — find a size-M cache whose attention output "remains as close as possible to the full-attention output." Methods in this category include:

- **Recency-based heuristics**: StreamingLLM (Xiao et al., 2023) retains the most recent tokens plus initial "attention sink" tokens, discarding everything in the middle.
- **Attention-score-based heuristics**: SnapKV (Li et al., 2024c) uses accumulated attention scores across recent queries to identify important tokens, then evicts those with low scores. R-KV (Cai et al., 2025) extends this to reasoning models by incorporating key-vector similarity to identify redundant tokens.
- **Layer-wise budget allocation**: PyramidKV (Cai et al., 2024) uses a hand-crafted pyramid shape that allocates more KV budget to lower layers, based on the intuition that earlier layers need broader context. AdaKV (Feng et al., 2024) adapts per-layer budgets using attention statistics, and Ada-Pyramid-KV combines both approaches.

The fundamental limitation, as the paper argues, is not just that these methods degrade performance — it's that **they are solving the wrong objective**. The assumption that full-cache attention is the gold standard to be approximated is, in long contexts, demonstrably false. When many irrelevant or weakly relevant tokens remain in the cache, the softmax normalization in self-attention forces useful evidence to compete with a growing number of distractors, and attention mass can be "diluted away from the tokens needed for prediction" (Section 1). The paper provides a formal treatment of this phenomenon in Section 3.1 and Proposition 3.1, showing that when many distractors have logits close to those of useful tokens, the total attention mass assigned to useful tokens can vanish entirely.

The empirical evidence is compelling. Figure 1a shows that even on a Needle-in-a-Haystack (NIAH) task where the relevant evidence is present in the context, full-cache models degrade substantially as context length increases (from ~80% at 8K to ~60% at 128K), while both TrimKV and DBTrimKV with 30% eviction maintain higher accuracy across all lengths. This directly contradicts the assumption that more context is always better — the presence of distractors actively harms performance.

#### 2. Visual Token Pruning Methods: Modality-Specific and Decoupled from Attention

A separate line of work focuses specifically on pruning visual tokens in vision-language models during the prefill stage. Methods include FastV (Chen et al., 2024a), which prunes tokens using pretrained attention maps at specific layers; VisionZip (Yang et al., 2025b), which similarly uses attention-based importance scoring; DART (Wen et al., 2025), which emphasizes token duplication over importance; PruMerge (Shang et al., 2025), which merges similar visual tokens; DivPrune (Alvar et al., 2025), which enforces diversity in retained tokens; and CDPruner (Zhang et al., 2025), which maximizes conditional diversity.

These methods have two key limitations that the paper's experiments expose (Section 5.1, Table 1). First, they **operate exclusively on visual tokens and only during prefill**, leaving the textual KV cache intact throughout decoding. This means they cannot adapt to how different heads and layers actually utilize visual versus textual information during decoding. Second, by constraining memory reduction solely to the vision side, they **miss the opportunity to reallocate cache capacity across modalities dynamically**. The results in Table 1 show that DBTrimKV and TrimKV, which compress jointly over both vision and text tokens and across all layers and heads, consistently outperform visual pruning baselines while matching vanilla performance under the same total memory budgets. For instance, at 32 visual tokens budget, CDPruner achieves 91.5% relative average performance versus vanilla, while DBTrimKV achieves 99.4%.

#### 3. Learned Eviction Methods: Local Budgets and Incomparable Scores

The most directly relevant prior work is TrimKV (Bui et al., 2025), which introduces the concept of **token retention** — learning a scalar retention score β_i for each token that determines how quickly its contribution decays geometrically in attention computation. TrimKV trains lightweight retention gates to predict β_i from token embeddings, matching a full-cache teacher under a per-head memory budget. This is a significant advance over heuristic methods because it learns to predict future utility rather than relying on myopic attention statistics.

However, TrimKV has a structural limitation that DBTrimKV directly addresses: **its retention scores are not comparable across layers and heads**. Because each gate is trained independently with its own final projection layer, the β scores produced in one head have no meaningful relationship to scores produced in another. As a result, TrimKV must use fixed per-layer, per-head budgets — each head independently retains its top-scoring tokens, and there is no mechanism for one head to receive extra capacity because its retained tokens are more useful than those in another head.

This is a significant constraint because the empirical evidence shows enormous heterogeneity in how heads utilize long-range context. Figure 2b shows a per-layer, per-head heatmap of token survival at horizon L = 4096. A small number of heads preserve long-range tokens (survival rates near 1.0), while many heads quickly lose them (survival rates near 0.0). Under a fixed per-head budget, the heads that need more capacity to preserve long-range information get the same allocation as heads that only attend to local context — a clear misallocation of a scarce resource.

Other learned approaches, such as Locret (Huang et al., 2025b), train retaining heads to predict token importance but also operate with fixed per-layer budgets and show performance degradation relative to full-cache inference on challenging benchmarks like LongBench-V2 (Appendix C.2.1, Table 7: Locret drops 1.32% from full KV while DBTrimKV gains 9.20%).

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper positions itself through a fundamental reframing of the KV eviction problem. Rather than asking "how can we approximate full-cache attention with less memory?", it asks two new questions:

**First: Can KV eviction actually improve generation quality rather than merely preserve it?** This question is motivated by the attention dilution analysis in Section 3.1. The paper argues that when irrelevant tokens dominate the cache, the softmax normalization in self-attention causes useful evidence to lose attention mass to distractors. Selective eviction of distractors can therefore *sharpen* attention on relevant context, improving reasoning quality beyond what full-cache inference achieves. This is not just a theoretical claim — the paper demonstrates it empirically across multiple benchmarks where DBTrimKV with compressed caches outperforms full-cache vanilla inference (Table 2: 103.73% relative performance at 512 budget on image/video reasoning; Table 3: 114.46% relative performance at 512 budget on multi-turn dialogue).

This framing directly challenges the prevailing assumption in prior work — that eviction is a necessary evil that trades accuracy for efficiency. The paper instead argues that **selective forgetting can act as a form of attention regularization** (Section 7), suppressing distractors and sharpening reasoning over relevant context.

**Second: How should cache capacity be allocated across layers, heads, and modalities?** The paper argues that fixed per-layer or per-head budgets — the dominant approach in prior work — are fundamentally suboptimal because different heads play different roles. Some heads preserve long-range information and benefit from larger caches; others attend primarily to local context and can operate with much less memory. A globally unified budget, where tokens compete for cache capacity based on their predicted future utility regardless of which head they belong to, naturally allocates more memory to the heads that need it.

The technical mechanism that enables this is **weight-tied retention gates** (Section 4.1). By sharing the final scoring projection across all layers and heads, retention scores are calibrated onto a common scale — a score of 0.8 in one head means the same thing as 0.8 in another. This enables global ranking and dynamic allocation: the M_global tokens with the highest predicted future utility are retained regardless of their layer, head, or modality of origin.

The paper also contributes a **theoretical foundation** for why geometric retention works as a surrogate for future token utility (Section 3.2, Theorem 3.3). Rather than treating retention scores as arbitrary learned parameters, the paper shows that under stable future query dynamics and a token-level block-exit condition on relaxed top-K membership, the probability that a token remains useful for n future steps decays geometrically with n. The retention parameter β_i = (1 - ε_i)^(1/b_i) summarizes the persistence of token i: tokens that quickly exit their useful region have small β_i, while globally useful tokens have β_i close to one. This provides a principled motivation for the geometric decay form rt,i = β_i^(t-i) used in retention-gated attention.

Importantly, the paper explicitly positions itself as building on and extending TrimKV (Bui et al., 2025) rather than claiming entirely novel architecture. The retention gate framework, the training procedure (student-teacher KL divergence plus capacity constraint), and the geometric decay mechanism are all inherited from TrimKV. The key innovations are: (1) weight tying in the final scoring projection to enable cross-head score calibration, (2) replacing per-head capacity constraints with a single global budget, and (3) the theoretical analysis of attention dilution and geometric retention as query-agnostic future utility. This incremental-but-impactful approach is characteristic of the paper's style — it identifies a specific structural limitation in a strong prior method and addresses it with a minimal but well-motivated modification.

The paper also positions itself relative to **dynamic budget allocation methods** (AdaKV, PyramidKV, CAKE, ZigZagKV) by noting that while these methods adapt budgets across layers, they typically rely on heuristics or separate, locally enforced budgets, and their allocation decisions are decoupled from the token importance estimation. DBTrimKV unifies both decisions — which tokens to keep and how much capacity each head gets — through the same learned retention score, making allocation an emergent property of utility prediction rather than a separately designed mechanism.

Finally, the paper acknowledges its scope: it operates in a setting where the base model's representations already contain the needed information (as noted in the Executive Summary). On problems where the model fundamentally lacks capability — where no amount of attention sharpening can produce correct answers — eviction cannot help. The theoretical analysis in Section 3.2 makes this precise: retention scores predict *persistence of utility*, not creation of new utility. If a token was never useful, it cannot become useful through retention. This boundary condition — that eviction amplifies existing capabilities rather than creating them — is important for understanding when the approach applies and when it does not.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

This paper presents a system that learns to predict which cached tokens a language model will need in the future and uses those predictions to decide which tokens to keep when memory is limited. The system addresses the problem that standard approaches to KV cache eviction either degrade model quality by throwing away important information, or waste memory by retaining irrelevant tokens that distract the model's attention — it solves this by learning token utility scores that are directly comparable across all parts of the model, enabling a single global eviction policy that naturally allocates more memory to components that need it most.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The system has four major components, arranged in a pipeline from training through inference:

**1. Retention Gates (per layer, per head):** Small neural networks — one per attention head — that take a token's contextual embedding as input and predict a scalar retention score `β ∈ [0, 1]`. Each gate has its own layer/head-specific feature extractor (a two-layer MLP), but the final scoring projection — the weights that map the extracted features to the scalar `β` — is **shared across all layers and heads**. This weight tying is the central architectural innovation: it calibrates retention scores onto a common scale, so a score of 0.8 means "moderately persistent" regardless of which head produced it.

**2. Training Objective (Student-Teacher KL + Global Capacity Constraint):** The retention gates are trained while the base LLM/VLM weights remain frozen. The training objective has two parts: a quality loss that encourages the retention-gated model to match the output distribution of the full-cache teacher model (via KL divergence plus supervised next-token prediction), and a capacity loss that penalizes the model when the total effective KV cache size across all layers and heads exceeds a global budget `M_global`. This penalty forces the gates to learn which tokens genuinely need to persist versus which can decay quickly.

**3. Global Utility Scoring at Inference:** When the KV cache needs compression during decoding, every cached token (across all layers, heads, and modalities) receives a future utility score computed from its learned retention parameter `β`. The score aggregates the predicted persistence of the token over a specified lookahead horizon. Tokens with higher `β` accumulate more total utility over the horizon because their geometric decay is slower; tokens with lower `β` are heavily penalized by the geometric weighting.

**4. Global Eviction via Ranking:** All scored tokens are pooled into a single global ranking. The `M_global` tokens with the highest utility scores are retained; everything else is evicted. Because the scores are calibrated via weight tying, this ranking is meaningful across layers and heads — a head that naturally preserves long-range tokens will tend to have higher-scoring entries and will automatically receive more cache capacity, while heads dominated by low-utility tokens will naturally shrink. No per-layer or per-head budget rules are needed.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the formal eviction problem statement (Equations 1-2) and the Bui et al. (2025) retention relaxation (Equations 3-5), since DBTrimKV inherits this framework and the reader needs to understand what the retention gates are trained to do before understanding how global calibration works.
- **Second**, the theoretical analysis of attention dilution (Proposition 3.1, Corollary 3.2), which establishes *why* eviction can improve quality over full-cache inference — this is the conceptual foundation that motivates the entire approach.
- **Third**, the geometric retention justification (Theorem 3.3), which explains *why* the geometric decay form `β^(t-i)` is a principled surrogate for future token utility rather than an arbitrary design choice.
- **Fourth**, the weight-tied retention gate architecture and training procedure, including the key design decisions (shared final projection, global capacity constraint, horizon-weighted scoring) and their justifications.
- **Fifth**, the inference-time eviction mechanism, covering how utility scores are computed from learned `β` values, how the global ranking operates, and how PagedAttention enables efficient variable-length per-head caches.
- **Sixth**, the specific training configurations, hyperparameters, and dataset choices used in the experiments, since these determine the practical deployment of the method.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily an **architecture and training paper** whose core idea is that making retention scores globally comparable — by tying the final scoring projection across all retention gates and enforcing a single global capacity constraint — enables a unified eviction policy that jointly performs token selection and dynamic budget allocation, outperforming methods that treat these as separate problems.

#### The KV Eviction Problem Formalized

Section 2.1 establishes the formal framework that DBTrimKV operates within. During autoregressive decoding, at step `t`, the KV cache contains all previously generated tokens `C_t = {1, ..., t}`, where each token `i` is associated with a key-value pair `(k_i, v_i)`. Given a query `q_t` at step `t`, the standard attention output is:

$$o_t = \sum_{i \in C_t} \alpha_{t,i} v_i, \quad \text{where} \quad \alpha_{t,i} = \frac{e^{z_{t,i}}}{\sum_{j \in C_t} e^{z_{t,j}}}, \quad z_{t,i} = \frac{q_t^\top k_i}{\sqrt{d}}$$

where `$z_{t,i}$` is the unnormalized attention logit for token `i` at step `t`, `$\alpha_{t,i}$` is the normalized attention weight (a probability distribution over all cached tokens), and `$d$` is the head dimension. The output `$o_t$` is a weighted sum of value vectors, with weights proportional to the exponentiated query-key compatibility scores.

A KV eviction policy restricts the cache to at most `M` tokens by introducing binary retention indicators:

$$\alpha^r_{t,i} = \frac{r_{t,i} e^{z_{t,i}}}{\sum_{j \in C_t} r_{t,j} e^{z_{t,j}}}, \quad o'_t = \sum_{i \in C_t} \alpha^r_{t,i} v_i$$

where `$r_{t,i} \in \{0, 1\}$` indicates whether token `i` is retained in the cache at step `t`, and `$r_{t,i} \geq r_{t+1,i}$` enforces monotonicity — once a token is evicted, it cannot re-enter the cache later. The retention-gated attention is simply standard attention computed only over retained tokens, with the softmax denominator summing only over `${j : r_{t,j} = 1}$`.

**What this computes:** the attention output when the cache is restricted to a subset of tokens. Tokens with `$r_{t,i} = 0$` are removed from both the numerator and denominator of the softmax, so they contribute nothing to the output. The monotonicity constraint ensures that cache size only decreases over time, which is necessary for memory management — if tokens could re-enter, the system would need to keep them in storage anyway.

The ideal eviction policy solves:

$$\min_r \mathcal{L}(o'_t; o_t) \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \sum_{i=1}^t r_{t,i} \leq M$$

**What this computes:** the binary retention mask that minimizes the distance between the evicted-cache attention output and the full-cache attention output, subject to a hard memory budget of `$M$` tokens.

**Why this form:** this formulation captures the dominant paradigm in prior work — eviction as compression, aiming to approximate the full-cache output as closely as possible. The paper's key argument is that this objective is **mis-specified** in long contexts: the full-cache output `$o_t$` is itself degraded by attention dilution from distractors, so minimizing distance to it may not produce the best generation quality. This motivates the shift in perspective in Section 3.1.

The combinatorial nature of this problem — choosing which subset of `t` tokens to retain — makes exact optimization infeasible at every decoding step. Prior work therefore relies on heuristic rules for setting `$r_{t,i}$`.

#### The Bui et al. (2025) Retention Relaxation: Making Eviction Learnable

Section 2.2 describes the TrimKV framework that DBTrimKV builds upon. Rather than making hard binary eviction decisions, TrimKV relaxes `$r_{t,i} \in \{0, 1\}$` into continuous retention factors by assuming each token has an intrinsic importance that decays exponentially over time:

$$\tilde{\alpha}_{t,i} = \frac{\beta_i^{t-i} e^{z_{t,i}}}{\sum_{j \in C_t} \beta_j^{t-j} e^{z_{t,j}}}, \quad \tilde{o}_t = \sum_{i \in C_t} \tilde{\alpha}_{t,i} v_i$$

where `$\beta_i \in [0, 1]$` is a learned retention score for token `i`. The exponent `$t - i$` is the age of the token (how many steps ago it was generated). When `$\beta_i = 1$`, the token never decays — its contribution is exactly as in standard attention. When `$\beta_i < 1$`, the token's effective logit is multiplied by `$\beta_i^{t-i}$`, which shrinks exponentially with age: a token with `$\beta = 0.9$` has its logit multiplied by `$0.9^{100} \approx 0.000027$` after 100 steps, effectively removing it from the softmax computation.

**What this computes:** a softened version of attention where each token's contribution is weighted by its predicted persistence. The geometric form `$\beta_i^{t-i}$` means that the decay rate per step is constant — each additional step of age multiplies the effective logit by another factor of `$\beta_i$`. This is not an arbitrary choice; Section 3.2 and Theorem 3.3 provide theoretical justification for this geometric form as the natural consequence of token utility persisting under stable query dynamics.

**Why this form:** the geometric decay has three critical properties. First, it is **query-agnostic** — the retention weight depends only on the token's age and its learned `$\beta_i$`, not on the current query content. This makes it cheap to compute and avoids the circularity of using the current query to decide what to retain for future queries. Second, it is **monotonic** — older tokens never become more important, which matches the empirical observation that token utility typically fades over time (Figure 2, Figure 9). Third, it is **differentiable** with respect to `$\beta_i$`, which is what makes the entire framework learnable through gradient-based optimization.

The retention score `$\beta_i$` is predicted from the token embedding `$x_i$` by a small retention gate:

$$\beta_{\ell, h, i} = g_{\ell, h}(x_i) \in [0, 1]$$

where `$\ell$` indexes the layer, `$h$` indexes the head, and `$g_{\ell, h}$` is a learned function (a small MLP with a sigmoid output). These gates are trained so that a student model with retention-gated attention matches a full-cache teacher under a memory budget. The quality loss combines two terms:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{quality}} = D_{KL}(p \| q_\theta) + \mathbb{E}_{(x, y)}[-\log q_\theta(y \mid x)]$$

where `$p(\cdot \mid x)$` is the teacher (full-cache) output distribution over next tokens given input `$x$`, and `$q_\theta(\cdot \mid x)$` is the student (retention-gated) distribution with gate parameters `$\theta$`. The first term `$D_{KL}(p \| q_\theta)$` is the Kullback-Leibler divergence — it penalizes the student when its output token probabilities differ from the teacher's. The second term `$\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)}[-\log q_\theta(y \mid x)]$` is the standard supervised next-token prediction loss — it penalizes the student when it assigns low probability to the correct next token `$y$`.

**What this computes:** the total quality loss is the sum of a distribution-matching term (KL divergence) and a correctness term (negative log-likelihood of the ground-truth next token). The KL term encourages the student to mimic the teacher's full output distribution across all possible tokens, while the NLL term directly encourages the student to predict the correct token. Using both terms together is stronger than either alone: KL alone might allow the student to preserve the shape of the distribution while shifting probability mass away from the correct token; NLL alone might allow the student to predict the correct token while distorting the overall distribution in ways that harm generation quality.

**Why this form:** the KL teacher-student framework is standard in knowledge distillation and has been shown to produce better-calibrated student models than supervised training alone. By keeping the base LLM weights frozen and training only the retention gates, the approach preserves the model's fundamental capabilities while learning a lightweight eviction policy — this is critical because fine-tuning the full model for eviction could alter its representations in unpredictable ways.

To enforce the capacity constraint, TrimKV introduces a per-head capacity loss:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{cap}} = \sum_{\ell, h} \sum_{t=1}^T \max\left(0, \sum_{i=1}^t \beta_{\ell, h, i}^{t-i} - M\right)$$

where the sum over `$i$` from 1 to `$t$` computes the effective cache size for head `$(\ell, h)$` at step `$t$` (the sum of all retention weights — a continuous approximation to the number of retained tokens), `$M$` is the target KV budget per head, and `$\max(0, \cdot)$` ensures the penalty is zero when the budget is satisfied. The overall objective is:

$$\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{quality}} + \lambda \mathcal{L}_{\text{cap}}$$

where `$\lambda$` controls the trade-off between quality preservation and memory constraint satisfaction. The paper sets `$\lambda_{\text{cap}} = 1.0$` for all experiments.

**What this computes:** a soft constraint that pushes the total effective retention mass in each head toward the target budget `$M$`. When the sum of retention weights exceeds `$M$`, the loss increases linearly with the excess; when it's below `$M$`, the loss is exactly zero (no reward for using less than the budget). This asymmetric penalty — penalize over-use but don't reward under-use — is appropriate because the goal is to enforce a maximum memory limit, not to minimize memory usage to zero.

**Why this form matters for DBTrimKV's innovation:** TrimKV's capacity constraint is **per-head** — each head independently must satisfy its own budget `$M$`. This is the direct consequence of having locally calibrated retention scores: since `$\beta$` values from different heads are not comparable, you cannot pool them into a global budget. DBTrimKV's key change is replacing this per-head constraint with a global one and making scores comparable via weight tying — a minimal architectural change with substantial implications for how capacity is allocated.

#### Attention Dilution: Why Full-Cache Inference Degrades in Long Contexts

Section 3.1 provides the theoretical motivation for why eviction can improve quality over full-cache inference. The key concept is **attention dilution**: the fraction of total attention mass that gets assigned to distractors (irrelevant tokens) rather than useful tokens.

At any decoding step `t`, only a small subset of cached tokens `$\mathcal{U}_t \subseteq C_t$` may be useful for the next prediction. The remaining tokens act as distractors. Because self-attention normalizes over the entire cache with a softmax, useful tokens must compete with all distractors in the denominator. As the number of distractors grows, the total attention mass assigned to useful tokens can vanish — a phenomenon the paper formalizes and proves.

Define the **oracle sparse attention** that keeps the same logits on useful tokens but removes all distractors:

$$\alpha^\star_{t,i} = \frac{\mathbb{1}\{i \in \mathcal{U}_t\} e^{z_{t,i}}}{\sum_{j \in \mathcal{U}_t} e^{z_{t,j}}}$$

where `$\mathbb{1}\{i \in \mathcal{U}_t\}$` is the indicator that token `i` is useful at step `t`. This oracle represents the ideal: attend only to truly useful tokens, ignoring everything else. The paper notes this is motivated by the empirical observation that LLMs often perform well in short-context settings but degrade as irrelevant tokens accumulate.

**What this computes:** the attention distribution that would result if the model could perfectly identify which tokens are useful and attend only to them. The softmax is computed over `$\mathcal{U}_t$` only, so all attention mass is concentrated on useful tokens.

The **attention dilution** at step `t` is defined as the fraction of attention mass assigned to distractors:

$$\delta_t := 1 - \sum_{i \in \mathcal{U}_t} \alpha_{t,i}$$

Equivalently, `$\delta_t = \text{TV}(\alpha^\star_t, \alpha_t)$` — the total variation distance between the oracle distribution and the full-cache distribution. When `$\delta_t = 0$`, the model perfectly attends only to useful tokens; when `$\delta_t \to 1$`, essentially all attention mass goes to distractors.

**What this computes:** a scalar measure of how "diluted" the attention is at step `t`. It ranges from 0 (perfect attention) to 1 (complete dilution — all mass on distractors). This connects directly to generation quality: if the model's attention is heavily diluted, the value vectors from useful tokens contribute almost nothing to the output, and the model must rely on noise from distractors.

Proposition 3.1 formalizes when severe dilution is unavoidable:

**Proposition 3.1 (Near-tie distractors force dilution).** Fix a decoding step `t`. Suppose there exist `$\Delta \geq 0$` and a subset `$\mathcal{D}'_t \subseteq C_t \setminus \mathcal{U}_t$` such that `$z_{t,d} \geq \max_{i \in \mathcal{U}_t} z_{t,i} - \Delta$` for all `$d \in \mathcal{D}'_t$`. Then:

$$\delta_t \geq \frac{e^{-\Delta} |\mathcal{D}'_t| / |\mathcal{U}_t|}{1 + e^{-\Delta} |\mathcal{D}'_t| / |\mathcal{U}_t|}$$

Consequently, if `$\Delta = O(1)$`, `$|\mathcal{U}_t| = O(1)$`, and `$|\mathcal{D}'_t| \to \infty$`, then `$\delta_t \to 1$` and the attention mass on useful tokens `$\sum_{i \in \mathcal{U}_t} \alpha_{t,i} \to 0$`.

**What this says in plain language:** if there are many distractors whose attention logits are close to (within `$\Delta$` of) the maximum logit among useful tokens, then as the number of such competitive distractors grows, the total attention mass on useful tokens goes to zero — even if no individual distractor receives much attention. This is a "death by a thousand cuts" scenario: each distractor individually contributes a small amount to the softmax denominator, but collectively they dominate.

**Why this matters:** the condition is surprisingly weak. `$\Delta = O(1)$` means distractors don't need to have *higher* logits than useful tokens — they just need to be in the same ballpark (within a constant factor in exponentiated space). This is realistic in long contexts: many tokens may be somewhat related to the topic without being directly useful (e.g., background text in a document, other images in a multi-image dialogue, earlier turns that are tangentially relevant). The proposition explains why performance degrades even when the relevant evidence is present — the model literally cannot "see" it through the distractor noise in the softmax.

Corollary 3.2 then shows that selective eviction of distractors can reverse this dilution:

**Corollary 3.2 (Preferential retention reduces dilution).** For any decoding step `t`, with retention-weighted attention as defined in Equation 1, the diluted attention mass becomes:

$$\delta_t^r = \frac{(\rho_D / \rho_U) \delta_t}{(1 - \delta_t) + (\rho_D / \rho_U) \delta_t}$$

where:

$$\rho_U = \frac{\sum_{i \in \mathcal{U}_t} r_{t,i} e^{z_{t,i}}}{\sum_{i \in \mathcal{U}_t} e^{z_{t,i}}}, \quad \rho_D = \frac{\sum_{i \notin \mathcal{U}_t} r_{t,i} e^{z_{t,i}}}{\sum_{i \notin \mathcal{U}_t} e^{z_{t,i}}}$$

Here `$\rho_U$` is the logit-weighted average retention rate for useful tokens, and `$\rho_D$` is the same for distractors. If `$\rho_D \leq \rho_U$`, then `$\delta_t^r \leq \delta_t$` — dilution is reduced. If the ratio `$\rho_D / \rho_U \to 0$`, then `$\delta_t^r \to 0$` — dilution is eliminated entirely.

**What this computes:** the dilution under retention-weighted attention as a function of the original dilution `$\delta_t$` and the relative retention rates of distractors versus useful tokens. The key insight is that the retention policy doesn't need to be perfect — it just needs to suppress distractors more than useful tokens (`$\rho_D < \rho_U$`), and even a modest advantage accumulates to substantial dilution reduction.

**Why this form is important for the paper's thesis:** it shows that any retention rule that preferentially retains useful tokens over distractors will reduce dilution — and if it aggressively suppresses distractors while preserving useful tokens, dilution can be eliminated entirely. This provides a theoretical guarantee that learned eviction can *improve* attention quality over full-cache inference, not just approximate it. The empirical results in Figure 1 are consistent with this: both TrimKV and DBTrimKV with 30% eviction outperform full-cache inference, and DBTrimKV (with its globally optimized allocation) outperforms TrimKV, suggesting that the learned retention scores are indeed achieving `$\rho_U > \rho_D$`.

#### Geometric Retention as Query-Agnostic Future Utility

Section 3.2 provides the theoretical justification for why the geometric decay form `$r_{t,i} = \beta_i^{t-i}$` is a principled surrogate for future token utility, rather than an arbitrary design choice.

The key intuition is that token importance is both **sparse** and **local**. Many tokens are important for the current query or nearby queries, but their utility fades quickly once generation moves to a different subproblem, entity, or topic. Other tokens — such as delimiter tokens, instruction tokens, or structural anchors — remain useful for much longer. The quantity needed for KV eviction is not just the current attention score of token `i`, but its **future persistence**: how likely it is to keep receiving non-negligible attention as decoding continues.

The paper formalizes this by viewing compression as a one-shot decision over the old cache at step `t`. For each old token `i ≤ t`, define its **cumulative future utility**:

$$\bar{G}_i(t) := \sum_{s=t+1}^T w_{t,s} \Pr(i \in \mathcal{U}_s^{(t)} \mid \mathcal{F}_t)$$

where `$w_{t,s} \geq 0$` are horizon weights (how much we care about utility at future step `s`), `$\mathcal{U}_s^{(t)} \subseteq C_t$` is the set of old cached tokens that remain useful at future step `s`, and `$\mathcal{F}_t$` summarizes all information available at compression time `t` (the current KV cache state, the model's representations, etc.).

**What this computes:** the expected future benefit of retaining token `i` after compressing the cache at time `t`. It's a weighted sum over future steps, where each step's contribution is the probability that token `i` will be among the useful old tokens at that step, weighted by how important that future step is. The horizon weights `$w_{t,s}$` allow the system to prioritize near-term utility over far-future utility (or vice versa).

**Why this form:** this separates the retention decision from the current query. Instead of asking "is token `i` useful right now?", it asks "will token `i` remain useful over the remaining decoding horizon?" This is the right question for monotone eviction because once a token is removed, it's gone forever — so the retention decision should be based on long-term expected utility, not just current relevance.

To make this probability tractable, the paper makes a structural assumption (Assumption A.1): future query-key compatibility can be approximated in a low-dimensional subspace:

$$z_{s,i} = x_s^\top (W_Q^\top W_K) x_i \approx r_s^\top c_i, \quad r_s \in \mathbb{R}^m, \; c_i \in \mathbb{R}^m, \; m \ll d$$

where `$r_s$` is the future query state (a low-dimensional summary of the query at step `s`), and `$c_i$` is a fixed compatibility vector for token `i` (summarizing how well token `i`'s key typically matches queries). The future query process `$(r_s)_{s > t}$` is assumed to be a stable, time-homogeneous Markov chain — meaning it's mean-reverting and ergodic, so its distribution converges to a stationary distribution.

**What this approximation does:** it collapses the high-dimensional query-key interaction (`$x_s^\top W_Q^\top W_K x_i$`, which lives in `$\mathbb{R}^d$` with `$d$` typically 64-128) into a low-dimensional inner product (`$r_s^\top c_i$`, with `$m$` typically 4-8). The compatibility vector `$c_i$` is fixed for each token — it captures the token's intrinsic "matchability" with queries on average, independent of which specific queries appear. This is what makes retention **query-agnostic**: instead of predicting attention scores for every possible future query, the system predicts a single compatibility summary per token.

The paper empirically validates this assumption in Figure 8 (appendix): they extract per-token query states from Qwen3-8B, project onto the top-8 PCA directions, and fit a VAR(1) model. The fitted dynamics are stable (all eigenvalues inside the unit circle, median spectral radius `$\tilde{\rho} = 0.76$`), mean-reverting (running means converge to stationary means), and have sub-exponential innovations — all consistent with the theoretical requirements.

Under this low-dimensional approximation, top-K membership among old cached tokens becomes a region in query-state space. Define the hard old-cache top-K threshold:

$$\Gamma_{K,t}(r) := \text{K-th largest value in } \{r^\top c_j : j \leq t\}$$

Token `i` is immediately useful at query state `r` if `$r^\top c_i \geq \Gamma_{K,t}(r)$` — it's among the top-K most compatible old tokens. But immediate top-K membership is too strict for retention: a token may temporarily fall below the top-K boundary but become useful again when generation returns to the same topic.

The paper therefore defines a **relaxed survival region**:

$$\mathcal{S}_{i,t}^{(K)} := \left\{ r : r^\top c_i \geq \Gamma_{K,t}(r) - \Delta_{i,t}(r) \right\}, \quad \Delta_{i,t}(r) \geq 0$$

where `$\Delta_{i,t}(r)$` is a token-dependent slack that allows token `i` to remain retention-worthy even when it's not in the hard top-K. This slack is token-dependent: local, context-specific tokens may admit only a small slack (they need to be genuinely top-K to be useful), while globally useful tokens (delimiter tokens, instruction tokens, structural anchors) may tolerate a larger drop below the top-K boundary because they remain relevant across many query states.

**What this defines:** the set of query states where token `i` should be retained — not just where it's currently the best match, but where it's "close enough" to being the best match that keeping it is worthwhile. This region always contains the hard top-K region and may be substantially larger for persistent tokens.

The critical assumption (Assumption A.2) is a **block-exit condition**: whenever the query state is inside token `i`'s relaxed survival region, there is a uniformly positive probability `$\epsilon_i > 0$` of exiting this region within the next `$b_i$` decoding steps:

$$\sup_{r \in \mathcal{S}_{i,t}^{(K)}} \Pr_r\left(r_1, \ldots, r_{b_i} \in \mathcal{S}_{i,t}^{(K)}\right) \leq 1 - \epsilon_i$$

where `$\Pr_r$` denotes the law of the Markov chain initialized at `$r_0 = r$`. The parameters `$(b_i, \epsilon_i)$` are token-specific: local, context-dependent tokens have small `$b_i$` and large `$\epsilon_i$` (they exit their useful region quickly and with high probability), while globally useful tokens have large `$b_i$` and small `$\epsilon_i$` (they persist for many steps).

**Why this assumption matters:** it captures the intuitive notion that token utility is bounded in time — no token remains useful forever. Even the most persistent tokens (like delimiter tokens) will eventually become irrelevant as the generation moves to an entirely different context. The block length `$b_i$` controls how "patient" the retention should be for token `i`: tokens with large `$b_i$` should be retained even if they're temporarily not top-K, because they're likely to become useful again.

Theorem 3.3 formalizes the geometric decay consequence:

**Theorem 3.3 (Geometric decay of retention).** Under Assumptions A.1 and A.2, for each old cached token `i`, there exist constants `$A_i > 0$` and `$\theta_i > 0$` such that for all `$n \geq 1$`:

$$\Pr(i \in \mathcal{U}_{t+n}^{(t)} \mid \mathcal{F}_t) \leq A_i e^{-\theta_i n}$$

In particular, one may take `$\theta_i = -\frac{1}{b_i} \log(1 - \epsilon_i)$` and `$A_i = (1 - \epsilon_i)^{-1}$`. Defining `$\beta_i := e^{-\theta_i} = (1 - \epsilon_i)^{1/b_i} \in (0, 1)$`, the cumulative future utility satisfies:

$$\bar{G}_i(t) \leq A_i \sum_{s=t+1}^T w_{t,s} \beta_i^{s-t}$$

**What this proves:** under stable query dynamics and a per-token block-exit condition, the probability that a token remains useful for `$n$` more steps decays exponentially with `$n$` — and the decay rate is governed by the token's persistence parameter `$\beta_i$`. Tokens that quickly exit their useful region have small `$\beta_i$` (fast decay); tokens that persist have `$\beta_i$` close to 1 (slow decay).

**Why this justifies the geometric retention form:** the geometric decay `$\beta_i^{t-i}$` used in retention-gated attention is not an arbitrary functional form — it's the direct consequence of token utility being a persistence event under stable query dynamics. The parameter `$\beta_i$` summarizes everything about token `i`'s future usefulness: how large its relaxed survival region is (through `$\Delta_{i,t}$`), how quickly query states drift away from it (through the Markov chain dynamics), and how long it typically stays relevant (through `$b_i$` and `$\epsilon_i$`). The retention gate learns to predict `$\beta_i$` directly from the token representation, implicitly estimating all of these factors.

The empirical evidence in Figure 2 and Figure 9 (appendix) supports this theoretical picture. Token survival curves under full-cache inference show exactly the fast-decay structure predicted: for a representative head in Qwen3-VL-4B, only 13.8% of tokens survive to horizon `$L = 4096$` under top-K = 256, and only 3.2% survive to `$L = 16\text{k}$`. Even under the more lenient 99%-mass criterion (which counts a token as "alive" if it contributes to the top tokens comprising 99% of attention mass), the head loses 60% of mass-relevant tokens by `$L = 4096$`. Vision tokens fade at a similar rate to text tokens. This rapid decay means that retaining all old tokens is wasteful — most have already outlived their usefulness, and keeping them in the cache only dilutes attention away from the small fraction that remain relevant.

#### Weight-Tied Retention Gates: Making Scores Globally Comparable

Section 4.1 introduces the central architectural innovation of DBTrimKV: weight tying in the final scoring projection of the retention gates.

The per-head survival heatmap in Figure 2b shows that token persistence is highly heterogeneous across heads. A small number of heads preserve long-range tokens (survival rates near 1.0 at L = 4096), while many heads quickly lose them (survival rates near 0.0). This raises a fundamental resource allocation question: if the total KV budget is limited, shouldn't the heads that actually preserve long-range information receive more capacity?

In TrimKV, this cannot happen because each gate's final scoring projection is independent. The retention score is computed as:

$$\beta_{\ell, h, i} = g_{\ell, h}(x_i) = \sigma\left(w_{\ell, h}^\top \text{Proj}_{\ell, h}(x_i) + b_{\ell, h}\right)$$

where `$\sigma(\cdot)$` is the sigmoid function (squashing outputs to `$[0, 1]$`), `$\text{Proj}_{\ell, h}$` is a layer/head-specific feature extractor (a two-layer MLP), and `$(w_{\ell, h}, b_{\ell, h})$` are unique per-head parameters. The sigmoid ensures `$\beta \in [0, 1]$`, which is necessary for the geometric decay interpretation (Equation 9 requires `$\beta_{\ell, h, i} < 1$` for the infinite sum to converge).

**The problem with independent projections:** the scale and bias of the sigmoid input are learned independently for each head. One head might learn to output `$\beta$` values clustered around 0.3, while another outputs values clustered around 0.7 — but this doesn't necessarily mean the second head's tokens are more persistent; it could just mean that head's projection is calibrated differently. You cannot compare a score of 0.6 from head A with a score of 0.6 from head B and conclude they have the same utility, because they're measured on different scales.

DBTrimKV addresses this with a minimal architectural change: **share the final scoring projection across all layers and heads** while keeping the feature extractors head-specific:

$$\beta_{\ell, h, i} = g_{\ell, h}(x_i) = \sigma\left(w_g^\top \text{Proj}_{\ell, h}(x_i) + b_g\right)$$

where `$\text{Proj}_{\ell, h}$` is still a layer/head-specific two-layer MLP (allowing each head to extract features relevant to its specific role), but `$(w_g, b_g)$` is **tied across all layers and heads** — there is exactly one set of output weights for the entire model.

**What this change does:** it forces all retention gates to project their head-specific features onto a common scale before applying the sigmoid. The shared weights `$(w_g, b_g)$` learn a single mapping from "retention-relevant features" to "persistence score." Any head that wants to produce a high `$\beta$` must produce feature vectors that align with the direction `$w_g$`; any head that produces features orthogonal to `$w_g$` will get moderate sigmoid outputs near `$\sigma(b_g)$`.

**Why this enables global comparison:** because the sigmoid is a monotonic function, the relative ordering of `$w_g^\top \text{Proj}_{\ell, h}(x_i) + b_g$` across tokens from different heads directly reflects their relative retention-worthiness — a token from head A with a pre-sigmoid score of 2.0 is unambiguously predicted to be more persistent than a token from head B with a pre-sigmoid score of 0.5. The sigmoid just rescales both to `[0, 1]` without changing the ranking.

The bias initialization is a subtle but important detail. The paper states that `$b_g$` is "initialized to 18.0 at the start of training" (Section 5.1). With sigmoid activation, `$\sigma(18.0) \approx 1 - 1.52 \times 10^{-8}$` — essentially 1.0. This means that at initialization, **all tokens are predicted to be maximally persistent** (all `$\beta \approx 1$`), which recovers standard full-cache attention. The capacity loss then pushes some `$\beta$` values downward, teaching the gates which tokens can safely decay. This initialization strategy ensures the model starts from the "retain everything" baseline and only learns to evict tokens when doing so demonstrably helps (via the quality loss) or is forced by the capacity constraint.

The gate architecture `$\text{Proj}_{\ell, h}$` is implemented as a two-layer MLP with hidden dimension `$d_g = 512$` (Section 5.1). The input is the token's contextual embedding `$x_i$` from the base model's hidden states. The paper also tests an alternative gate that takes the concatenation of the key and value vectors `$[k_i \| v_i]$` as input (Table 5), finding that using `$x_i$` performs better, especially under tight budgets. This suggests that the token's contextual embedding — which encodes its semantic role in the sequence — provides a stronger long-horizon utility signal than the projected KV representations alone. The KV representations are already specialized for attention computation and may lose information about the token's broader utility.

The retention gates are extremely lightweight: they comprise only **0.43% of the total VLM parameters** (Section 5.2). This is critical because it means training is fast and the overhead at inference is negligible. For Qwen3-VL-8B-Thinking (presumably ~8 billion parameters), the retention gates add roughly 34 million trainable parameters spread across all layers and heads.

#### Training with a Global Capacity Constraint

The second key change from TrimKV to DBTrimKV is replacing the per-head capacity constraint with a global one. The training objective is:

$$\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{quality}} + \lambda \mathcal{L}_{\text{cap}}$$

where the quality loss `$\mathcal{L}_{\text{quality}}$` is identical to TrimKV (Equation 3: KL divergence plus supervised NLL), but the capacity loss is now:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{cap}} = \sum_{t=1}^T \max\left(0, \sum_{\ell, h} \sum_{i=1}^t \beta_{\ell, h, i}^{t-i} - M_{\text{global}}\right)$$

where `$M_{\text{global}}$` is the target global KV budget — the total effective cache size summed across all layers and heads. The inner sum `$\sum_{i=1}^t \beta_{\ell, h, i}^{t-i}$` is the effective cache size for head `$(\ell, h)$` at step `$t$` (a continuous approximation to the number of retained tokens), and the outer double sum aggregates over all layers and heads.

**What this computes:** a soft constraint that pushes the total effective retention mass across the entire model toward the global budget `$M_{\text{global}}$`. When the sum over all heads of all layers exceeds `$M_{\text{global}}$`, the loss increases linearly with the excess; when it's below the budget, the loss is exactly zero.

**Why this is fundamentally different from per-head constraints:** under a per-head constraint, each head must independently fit within its budget — reducing retention in one head does nothing to relieve pressure on another. Under a global constraint, the total budget is fixed, but its allocation across heads is completely flexible. The training process can learn to "spend" more retention mass in heads that genuinely need large caches to preserve long-range information, while aggressively pruning heads that only need local context. The allocation emerges from the interaction between the quality loss (which penalizes evicting genuinely useful tokens) and the capacity loss (which penalizes exceeding the total budget) — heads where eviction hurts quality more will naturally retain higher `$\beta$` values.

The global budget `$M_{\text{global}}$` is set to `$M \times L \times H$` where `$M$` is the per-head-equivalent budget, `$L$` is the number of layers, and `$H$` is the number of heads. This makes the total budget comparable to fixed-budget methods: a method that gives each head exactly `$M$` tokens would use the same total memory. But DBTrimKV can reallocate — one head might use `$2M$` while another uses `$0.5M$`, as long as the sum is `$M \times L \times H$`.

The hyperparameter `$\lambda_{\text{cap}} = 1.0$` controls the trade-off. The paper does not ablate this value, but the choice of 1.0 means quality loss and capacity loss are weighted equally — the model is penalized as much for exceeding the budget as for deviating from the teacher's distribution. This is a strong constraint that forces the gates to be genuinely parsimonious.

The training data for VLM experiments uses a mixture of datasets to cover diverse scenarios: 40% of M4Instruct (Li et al., 2024a), 30% of Academic Video (Zhang et al., 2024c), 30% of R1-OneVision (Yang et al., 2025c), and 20% of OpenR1-Math-220k (Hugging Face). These are randomly sampled subsets (Section 5.2). For the pure text experiment on Qwen3-4B, training uses OpenR1-Math-220K only, mimicking the setting from Bui et al. (2025). For the short-form VQA experiment on LLaVA-1.5-7B, training uses the LLaVA-Next dataset (Liu et al., 2024b).

Training hyperparameters (Appendix B.1): maximum sequence length of 32,768, learning rate `$2 \times 10^{-4}$`, weight decay `$10^{-6}$`, cosine scheduling, per-GPU batch size of 1 with gradient accumulation of 4 (effective batch size 4), all other hyperparameters follow default HuggingFace Trainer settings. Training is performed on 4 H100 GPUs.

**Why freeze the base model:** training only the retention gates while keeping the base LM/VLM weights frozen is a deliberate design choice. It preserves the model's fundamental capabilities — the representations that the gates take as input don't change, so the gates learn to work with the existing representational structure. It's also computationally efficient: the gates are 0.43% of parameters, so training is fast and requires relatively little data. The paper acknowledges that jointly fine-tuning the base model alongside the retention gates could potentially allow the model to structure its representations to be more amenable to eviction, but leaves this to future work (Appendix D).

#### Inference-Time Global Eviction via Horizon-Weighted Scoring

At inference time, when the KV cache reaches a compression point (triggered by memory pressure or periodic scheduling), the system computes a future utility score for every cached token and retains the highest-scoring ones.

The utility score for token `$i$` in layer `$\ell$`, head `$h$` at compression step `$t$` is the aggregated geometric retention over a lookahead horizon:

$$\tilde{G}_{\ell, h, i}(t) = \sum_{s=t+1}^T \beta_{\ell, h, i}^{s-i} = \beta_{\ell, h, i}^{t+1-i} \frac{1 - \beta_{\ell, h, i}^{T-t}}{1 - \beta_{\ell, h, i}}, \quad \text{for } \beta_{\ell, h, i} < 1$$

where `$T$` is the total decoding horizon (the expected number of tokens to be generated), and `$T - t$` is the remaining lookahead.

**What this computes:** the sum of geometric decay weights for all remaining steps — essentially the "area under the decay curve" from the current step to the end of generation. The formula uses the closed-form sum of a finite geometric series: `$\sum_{k=0}^{n-1} r^k = (1 - r^n) / (1 - r)$`. The leading factor `$\beta_{\ell, h, i}^{t+1-i}$` accounts for the token's age at the start of the lookahead window: an older token has already decayed more, so its remaining utility is smaller even if `$\beta$` is large.

For the one-step case `$T - t = 1$`, this reduces to the myopic score `$\tilde{G}_{\ell, h, i}(t) = \beta_{\ell, h, i}^{t+1-i}$` used in TrimKV (Bui et al., 2025). For `$\beta_{\ell, h, i} \in [0, 1)$` and `$T - t \to \infty$`, the score converges to `$\beta_{\ell, h, i}^{t+1-i} / (1 - \beta_{\ell, h, i})$` — the infinite geometric series sum.

**Why this scoring function:** it balances recency and persistence. The age factor `$\beta^{t+1-i}$` naturally penalizes older tokens — a brand-new token (age 0) has weight 1, while an old token with `$\beta = 0.9$` at age 100 has weight `$0.9^{100} \approx 0.000027$`, making it almost certainly evicted unless no better tokens exist. But the horizon factor `$(1 - \beta^{T-t}) / (1 - \beta)$` favors tokens with larger `$\beta$`: for a token with `$\beta = 0.99$`, the horizon sum over 100 steps is approximately `$0.99 \times (1 - 0.99^{100}) / 0.01 \approx 63.4$` (accounting for age 1), while for a token with `$\beta = 0.5$`, the same sum is `$0.5 \times (1 - 0.5^{100}) / 0.5 \approx 1.0$`. A high-`$\beta$` token can "outscore" a low-`$\beta$` token even if it's older, because its future utility is predicted to persist much longer.

The lookahead horizon `$T - t$` thus governs the trade-off between recency and retention. The paper uses a default lookahead of `$T - t = 2$` for all experiments (Section 5.1), and ablates this choice in Appendix C.3 (Table 8). A lookahead of 2 means the score is `$\tilde{G} = \beta^{t+1-i} + \beta^{t+2-i}$` — just two steps of future utility. This is fairly myopic, favoring recent tokens. At very low budgets (64, 128), a longer lookahead of `$T - t = 5$` performs better, suggesting that when cache capacity is severely limited, it's more important to identify the small number of tokens that will remain useful for many steps. At higher budgets, the moderate lookahead of 2 is optimal, likely because there's enough capacity to retain both recent tokens and a selection of persistent anchors, and the myopic scoring naturally keeps the cache biased toward recent context.

**The eviction rule:** retain the `$M_{\text{global}}$` tokens with the largest scores `$\tilde{G}_{\ell, h, i}(t)$` across all layers, heads, and modalities. This is a single global ranking — a token from layer 5, head 2 competes directly with a token from layer 32, head 7. The `$M_{\text{global}}$` highest-scoring tokens are kept; everything else is evicted. Because the scores are calibrated via weight tying and use the same scoring formula, this ranking is meaningful.

**Why this unifies token selection and budget allocation:** heads that learn high `$\beta$` values for their tokens will naturally have those tokens place highly in the global ranking, and will therefore receive more cache slots. Heads where most tokens have low `$\beta$` will see their tokens evicted early, effectively receiving a smaller cache allocation. The allocation is not a separate step — it's an emergent property of the utility prediction. If a head preserves important long-range information, its tokens will score highly and survive eviction; if a head mostly processes local context that quickly becomes irrelevant, its tokens will score lower and get evicted. The model learns this allocation during training: the capacity loss pushes for aggressive eviction, the quality loss penalizes eviction of genuinely useful tokens, and the equilibrium determines which heads tend to produce high-`$\beta$` tokens.

The qualitative visualizations in Figure 6 and Appendix C.5 support this interpretation. Figure 6 shows that on a MathVisionmini example, DBTrimKV concentrates KV cache usage into a sparse subset of heads, frequently in the mid-layers — the heads that the model has learned are critical for integrating semantic concepts and synthesizing information. Visual tokens are retained densely early in generation (to interpret the image) but shrink markedly as decoding shifts toward text-centric reasoning. The boundary tokens `<|vision_start|>` and `<|vision_end|>` are persistently retained across many heads throughout generation, suggesting the model has learned to treat these as compressed structural anchors.

#### PagedAttention for Variable-Length Per-Head Caches

The global eviction policy naturally produces variable-length caches per head — some heads retain many tokens, others retain few. This breaks the standard assumption in attention implementations that all heads have the same sequence length. To support this efficiently, DBTrimKV uses a **paged-attention layout** (Section 4.2), similar to Kwon et al. (2023).

In PagedAttention, KV entries are stored in fixed-size pages (blocks of memory) rather than as contiguous tensors. Each head maintains a **block table** — a list of pointers to the physical pages that contain its currently active KV entries. When a token is evicted from a head, its page reference is removed from that head's block table (the underlying page may still be referenced by other heads, so physical memory is freed only when no head references it).

Attention computation then uses variable-length kernels (FlashAttention-2, Dao 2023) that operate on the per-head logical sequence lengths — each head processes only its own retained tokens, ignoring evicted ones. This allows each head to maintain a variable-length logical KV sequence without requiring contiguous physical storage or specialized kernel implementations.

**Why PagedAttention is necessary:** without it, the system would need to either (a) store KV entries in dense tensors with padding for evicted tokens (wasting memory and compute), or (b) physically compact KV tensors after each eviction (expensive data movement). PagedAttention provides logical indirection — the block table maps logical positions to physical pages, so eviction just means removing entries from the block table without physically moving data.

Figure 4 (Section 4.2) illustrates this: a paged-attention layout with head-specific variable-length KV caches, where each layer-head maintains a logical KV sequence of variable length stored as fixed-size blocks.

The runtime measurements in Figure 7 show that DBTrimKV with PagedAttention remains highly efficient, adding only a small overhead compared to TrimKV's fixed-budget implementation. At batch size 16, context length 16384, and KV budget 256, DBTrimKV's decoding time grows sub-linearly with generation length compared to full-cache inference (which grows quadratically). The paper expects speedups over vanilla to grow with longer contexts and longer generations, where KV bandwidth becomes the dominant bottleneck (Section 5.4).

#### Summary of Design Choices and Their Justifications

- **Weight-tied final projection** over independent per-head projections: calibrates retention scores onto a common scale, enabling global ranking and dynamic budget allocation. This is the minimal architectural change that addresses TrimKV's structural limitation — a single shared linear layer replaces `$L \times H$` independent linear layers, adding no meaningful parameter count while enabling a fundamentally different eviction paradigm.

- **Global capacity constraint** over per-head constraints: allows the training process to learn which heads need larger caches, rather than pre-specifying equal budgets. The allocation emerges from the quality-capacity trade-off rather than being hand-designed.

- **Horizon-weighted scoring** over myopic single-step scoring: accounts for the fact that tokens with high `$\beta$` contribute utility over many future steps, making them more valuable than tokens with low `$\beta$` even if the low-`$\beta$` token is more useful *right now*. The lookahead horizon `$T-t$` provides a tunable trade-off between favoring recent tokens and favoring persistent tokens.

- **Bias initialization to 18.0** (yielding `$\beta \approx 1$`): ensures training starts from the full-cache baseline and only learns to evict when forced by capacity or incentivized by quality improvements. This avoids the risk of the model learning an overly aggressive eviction policy from random initialization.

- **Frozen base model weights**, training only retention gates: preserves the model's representations and capabilities while learning a lightweight eviction policy. The gates comprise only 0.43% of parameters, making training fast and inference overhead minimal.

- **Token embedding as gate input** over KV representations: Table 5 shows that using the contextual embedding `$x_t$` outperforms using `$[k_t \| v_t]$` as input, especially under tight budgets. The contextual embedding likely encodes richer semantic and positional information relevant to long-horizon utility.

- **PagedAttention with variable-length per-head caches** over dense tensors: enables efficient implementation of dynamic, head-specific budgets without physical data movement or memory waste from padding.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Reframing KV Eviction from Compression to Attention Regularization

The paper's most fundamental intellectual contribution is a reframing of **what KV eviction is for**. The dominant paradigm in prior work — spanning heuristic methods like SnapKV (Li et al., 2024c), StreamingLLM (Xiao et al., 2023), and R-KV (Cai et al., 2025), as well as learned approaches like Locret (Huang et al., 2025b) — treats eviction as a **compression problem**: the goal is to approximate full-cache attention as closely as possible while using less memory. This framing is baked into the formal objective in Equation 2: find a size-M cache whose attention output minimizes distance to the full-attention output. The unstated assumption is that full-cache attention is the gold standard — the best the model can do — and eviction is a necessary degradation incurred for efficiency.

This paper argues that assumption is **empirically false in long contexts**. The mechanism is **attention dilution** (Section 3.1): when many irrelevant tokens reside in the cache, the softmax normalization in self-attention forces useful evidence to compete with a growing number of distractors for a fixed total attention mass of 1.0. As the number of distractors grows, the fraction of attention mass landing on useful tokens can vanish — even when the relevant evidence is present and the model would use it correctly in a shorter context. Proposition 3.1 formalizes this with surprisingly weak conditions: distractors don't need higher attention logits than useful tokens; they just need logits within a constant factor, and their collective presence in the denominator is enough to dilute useful-token mass to near zero.

The reframing has a direct practical consequence: **eviction can improve generation quality beyond full-cache inference**, not just preserve it. If the right distractors are removed, attention sharpens on genuinely useful context, and the model reasons better. This is not a theoretical speculation — the paper demonstrates it empirically: DBTrimKV with a 512-token budget achieves 103.73% relative performance versus full-cache vanilla on image/video reasoning (Table 2), and 114.46% on multi-turn dialogue (Table 3). TrimKV, the predecessor method, shows the same pattern (97.02% and 104.51% respectively). Even on a controlled Needle-in-a-Haystack task (Figure 1), both methods with 30% eviction outperform full-cache at all context lengths, with the gap widening as context grows.

This fundamentally changes the optimization landscape. Prior work treats eviction quality as a monotonic degradation from full-cache: more eviction → worse performance. The paper replaces this with a **U-shaped curve**: moderate eviction that removes distractors can improve performance, while only aggressive eviction that removes useful tokens degrades it. The "optimal" point is not "retain everything" but "retain the right things and discard the rest." This is why the compute-optimal allocation concept from Section 3 matters — it's not about minimizing damage; it's about maximizing benefit.

The reframing also resolves a tension in the literature. Some prior work found that KV eviction consistently degrades performance (motivating the compression-as-approximation view), while other work found that sparse attention can outperform dense attention on long contexts (motivating attention as a selection mechanism). The paper provides a unified explanation: earlier negative results likely evaluated on contexts or methods where distractors hadn't accumulated enough for dilution to be the dominant failure mode, or where eviction policies couldn't distinguish distractors from useful tokens. The positive results here come from learned retention scores that achieve the condition in Corollary 3.2 — preferentially suppressing distractor mass more than useful-token mass (`$\rho_D < \rho_U$`).

This is a **fundamental reframing**, not an incremental improvement. It changes the problem statement, the evaluation criterion, and the design objective. Prior work asked "how can we minimize the damage from eviction?" This paper asks "how can we use eviction to improve attention quality?" The answer depends on having a mechanism — learned retention scores — that can identify which tokens to keep and which to discard, which is why the TrimKV framework is the necessary precursor. But the intellectual move is in recognizing that the full cache is not the ceiling, and that selective forgetting can be a form of attention regularization (as the paper states in Section 7).

---

### Innovation 2: Unifying Token Selection and Budget Allocation Through Globally Calibrated Retention

Before this work, KV eviction systems treated two decisions as architecturally separate: **which tokens to evict** (token importance estimation) and **how to allocate cache capacity across layers, heads, and modalities** (budget allocation). Token importance was estimated via attention scores (SnapKV, StreamingLLM), key-vector similarity (R-KV), or learned retention scores (TrimKV). Budget allocation was handled by fixed per-layer or per-head rules (PyramidKV's hand-crafted pyramid favoring lower layers, TrimKV's uniform per-head budgets) or adaptive heuristics based on attention statistics (AdaKV, Ada-Pyramid-KV). Even methods that adapted budgets across layers (AdaKV) still used separate, locally enforced constraints — the budget for layer 5 was determined independently of layer 20, and the token importance scores in each layer were never compared across layers.

This separation creates a structural inefficiency. The per-head survival heatmap in Figure 2b shows that token persistence is wildly heterogeneous: a handful of heads preserve long-range tokens (survival rate near 1.0 at horizon L = 4096), while most heads quickly lose them (survival rate near 0.0). Under a fixed per-head budget, the heads that genuinely need large caches to hold long-range context get exactly the same capacity as heads that only attend to local, short-lived tokens. The result is simultaneous **over-provisioning** (heads with only local context waste cache slots on tokens that will never be useful again) and **under-provisioning** (heads preserving long-range information are forced to evict tokens prematurely).

The paper's insight is that these two problems — what to keep and where to allocate — are actually a single problem if you can make token importance scores **globally comparable**. The key enabler is weight tying in the final scoring projection of the retention gates (Section 4.1). By sharing the output weights `(w_g, b_g)` across all layers and heads, every retention score `β` is measured on the same scale — a score of 0.8 from one head means the same predicted persistence as 0.8 from another. Once scores are on a common scale, the eviction decision becomes a single global ranking: retain the `M_global` tokens with the highest predicted future utility regardless of their layer, head, or modality of origin.

The consequence is that **budget allocation becomes an emergent property of utility prediction rather than a separately designed mechanism**. Heads whose tokens tend to have high predicted persistence will naturally see more of their tokens survive eviction; heads with low-persistence tokens will see their cache footprints shrink. No per-layer budget rules, no attention-statistic heuristics, no modality-specific allocations. The global capacity constraint in the training objective (replacing TrimKV's per-head constraints) forces the gates to learn which heads genuinely need capacity, because the quality loss penalizes evicting useful tokens regardless of which head they're in.

This unification is **conceptually distinctive** for three reasons. First, it eliminates a entire category of design decisions. Prior methods required practitioners to choose budget allocation rules — pyramid shapes, layer-wise ratios, modality-specific budgets — with little principled guidance. DBTrimKV requires exactly one hyperparameter: the total global budget `M_global`. Everything else is learned.

Second, it produces a dynamic, context-dependent allocation that fixed rules cannot match. The qualitative visualizations (Figure 6, Appendix C.5) show that the allocation shifts during decoding: early steps retain broad visual context across many heads; later steps concentrate capacity into a sparse subset of mid-layer heads while aggressively evicting visual tokens that are no longer relevant. A fixed pyramid or layer-wise rule cannot adapt to this shift because it doesn't know what the model is currently reasoning about.

Third, it generalizes naturally across modalities without modality-specific design. In Table 1, DBTrimKV and TrimKV jointly compress vision and text tokens under a single budget, while visual pruning baselines (FastV, VisionZip, CDPruner) operate only on vision tokens and leave text tokens untouched. The joint approach outperforms modality-specific pruning across all budgets because it can reallocate capacity from text to vision or vice versa depending on what each head actually needs.

This innovation is an **architectural refinement** of TrimKV — the weight tying changes only the final projection layer, and the global capacity constraint is a straightforward replacement of per-head constraints. But its implications are fundamental: it converts two problems (token selection and budget allocation) into one, and replaces hand-designed allocation rules with learned, context-dependent allocation that emerges from utility prediction. The empirical evidence that this matters is in the consistent gap between DBTrimKV and TrimKV across benchmarks (Tables 2, 3, and 6), which widens as budgets shrink — precisely the regime where misallocation of scarce capacity is most costly.

### Innovation 3: A Theoretical Foundation for Geometric Retention as Future-Utility Prediction

Prior work on learned KV eviction, including TrimKV (Bui et al., 2025), used geometric decay `β_i^(t-i)` as a retention mechanism but treated it as a **heuristic functional form** — a convenient way to make retention continuous, differentiable, and monotonic, without a principled argument for *why* geometric decay should correspond to future token utility. The choice of geometric decay over other decay forms (linear, step-function, learned arbitrary function) was justified by empirical performance, not theoretical necessity.

This paper provides that theoretical foundation in Section 3.2 and Theorem 3.3, and it is a **substantial intellectual contribution** independent of the architectural innovations. The argument connects three ideas that had not been linked before in the KV eviction literature:

**First**, the paper formalizes what "future utility" means for a cached token: the probability that the token remains within its relaxed top-K survival region over the remaining decoding horizon, weighted by horizon importance. This definition (Equation 8) separates the retention decision from the current query — it's about *persistence*, not instantaneous relevance — which aligns with the monotonicity constraint of KV eviction (once removed, a token cannot return).

**Second**, the paper provides conditions under which this persistence probability decays geometrically. The key structural assumptions are: (a) future query states evolve in a low-dimensional subspace where query-key compatibility is approximately `r_s^T c_i` (Assumption A.1); (b) for each token, there's a relaxed top-K survival region such that whenever the query state is inside it, there's a uniform positive probability `ε_i` of exiting within `b_i` steps (Assumption A.2). Under these conditions, Theorem 3.3 proves that the persistence probability is bounded above by `A_i e^{-θ_i n}` with `θ_i = -(1/b_i) log(1 - ε_i)`, and the cumulative future utility is bounded by a geometric series with ratio `β_i = (1 - ε_i)^(1/b_i)`.

**Third**, the paper empirically validates the assumptions. Figure 8 (appendix) shows that query-state dynamics in a real LLM (Qwen3-8B) are indeed stable, mean-reverting, and have sub-exponential innovations — consistent with Assumption A.1. Figure 2 and Figure 9 show that token survival curves under full-cache inference exhibit exactly the fast-decay structure predicted by the theory: for a representative head, only 13.8% of tokens survive to horizon 4096 under top-K = 256, and only 3.2% survive to 16K. Even under the lenient 99%-mass criterion, the head loses 60% of mass-relevant tokens by L = 4096.

The significance of this theoretical contribution extends beyond justifying DBTrimKV's design. It provides a **language for reasoning about token utility** that was previously absent from the KV eviction literature. The parameters `(b_i, ε_i)` — the block length and exit probability — capture token-level properties that determine persistence: tokens tied to local, transient context have small `b_i` and large `ε_i` (fast exit); globally useful structural tokens have large `b_i` and small `ε_i` (slow exit). The retention score `β_i` summarizes both into a single scalar that the retention gate learns to predict. This connects the learned parameter to interpretable properties of the token and the generation dynamics, rather than treating it as a black-box score.

The theory also explains **why** geometric decay works better than alternatives. A linear decay (constant reduction per step) would correspond to a fixed probability of becoming useless each step, which doesn't capture the block structure of attention — tokens tend to remain useful for contiguous phases (solving a subproblem, describing an entity) and then abruptly stop being useful when the generation moves on. Geometric decay with token-specific `β_i` naturally captures this: a high `β_i` means the token survives many blocks; a low `β_i` means it survives few.

This is a **theoretical advance**, not an empirical finding. It doesn't directly improve benchmark numbers, but it changes how the field should think about learned retention: not as an arbitrary parameterization that happens to work, but as the natural consequence of token utility being a persistence event under stable generation dynamics. It also opens avenues for future work that the paper identifies — for instance, explicitly estimating `(b_i, ε_i)` rather than learning `β_i` directly, or designing retention objectives that target specific horizon properties rather than generic quality matching. The paper does not pursue these, but the theoretical framework makes them well-posed research questions rather than speculative ideas.

---

### Innovation 4: Empirical Demonstration That Eviction Can Match or Exceed Full-Cache Inference Across Diverse Benchmarks

This innovation is **empirical**, not conceptual, but its significance warrants separate treatment because it changes the burden of proof for the field. Before this paper, the working assumption — supported by extensive prior work showing degradation from eviction — was that **KV eviction necessarily trades accuracy for efficiency**. Even methods that aimed to minimize the degradation (SnapKV, PyramidKV, Locret) accepted some performance loss as the cost of memory reduction. The best one could hope for was to make the degradation small enough to be acceptable.

DBTrimKV systematically violates this assumption across multiple benchmarks, model scales, and modalities:

- **Long-horizon image and video reasoning** (Table 2): At a 512-token budget, DBTrimKV achieves 103.73% relative performance versus full-cache vanilla across seven benchmarks (MMStar, MathVisionmini, MMMUPro, VideoMME, VideoMMMU, VideoMathQA). At 256 budget, it achieves 101.49%. Even at 128 budget — a 8× reduction from the 1024 budget baseline — it achieves 98.27%, essentially matching full-cache.

- **Multi-turn dialogue** (Table 3): At a 512-token budget, DBTrimKV achieves 114.46% relative performance versus full-cache vanilla on MMDU, with gains across all six evaluation dimensions (Answer Accuracy, Creativity, Richness, Visual Perception, Logical Coherence, Image Relative). At 256 budget, it achieves 108.33%.

- **Pure text reasoning** (Figure 5): On AIME24, GSM8K, and MATH-500 with Qwen3-4B, DBTrimKV achieves a Pareto frontier that dominates all baselines, including the previous state-of-the-art SeerAttn-R.

- **Short-form VQA** (Table 1): DBTrimKV matches vanilla performance (99.4% relative at 32 visual tokens) while visual pruning baselines degrade to 86–91%.

- **Long-context retrieval** (Appendix C.2.1, Table 7): On LongBench-V2 with Phi-3-mini-128K, DBTrimKV achieves +9.20% average accuracy improvement over full KV, while both Locret (-1.32%) and TrimKV (-9.24%) degrade.

The consistency across these settings is what makes this finding significant. It's not a single benchmark where eviction happens to help — it's a pattern that holds across visual reasoning, mathematical reasoning, multi-turn dialogue, and retrieval, across three different base models (Qwen3-VL-8B-Thinking, Qwen3-4B, LLaVA-1.5-7B), and across both VLM and pure-text settings.

The finding is also **diagnostic**: it supports the attention dilution theory in a way that pure compression methods cannot explain. If eviction were merely approximating full-cache attention, the best possible outcome would be to match full-cache performance (zero degradation). Exceeding full-cache implies that the full cache was suboptimal — that removing certain tokens actually improved the model's ability to attend to what matters. The 14% gain on multi-turn dialogue is particularly striking because it suggests that in interactive settings where distractors accumulate across turns, full-cache attention is actively harmful.

This innovation is **not a new method** — it's an empirical result that follows from the method. But its importance lies in shifting the evaluation landscape. Future work on KV eviction cannot simply report "within X% of full-cache" as a success metric; the bar is now whether eviction can **improve** over full-cache, and if not, why not. It also changes the narrative around what kinds of tokens should be evicted: not just "unimportant" tokens (those with low attention scores), but actively "distracting" tokens (those that compete with useful evidence for softmax mass). The distinction matters because a token can have moderate attention scores (seeming "somewhat important") while still being a net negative for generation quality if it dilutes attention away from a smaller number of genuinely critical tokens.

A limitation worth noting: the gains over full-cache are most pronounced at intermediate budgets (256–512 tokens). At very high budgets (1024), the gap narrows or closes (Table 2: 103.26% at 1024 vs. 103.73% at 512 on image/video reasoning). This is consistent with the dilution theory — when the budget is large enough to retain almost everything, there's not much dilution to correct. The gains emerge in the regime where capacity is constrained enough to force choices, but not so constrained that useful tokens get evicted. This suggests a practical guideline: the approach is most valuable when the target budget is substantially below the full context length but still large enough to retain the genuinely useful fraction of tokens.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The paper evaluates on a diverse set of benchmarks spanning four categories: (i) long-horizon reasoning (image-based: MMStar, MathVisionmini, MMMUProVision; video-based: VideoMME, VideoMMMU adaptation and comprehension subsets, VideoMathQAmcq; pure text: AIME24, GSM8K, MATH-500); (ii) short-form visual question answering (VQAText, MME, GQA); (iii) multi-turn dialogue (MMDU); and (iv) long-context retrieval (LongBench-V2). For long-horizon reasoning, evaluation uses the LMMs-Eval framework (Zhang et al., 2024a) with greedy decoding and accuracy as the metric. For MMDU, evaluation follows the official protocol using Gemini3 as an LLM-based judge across six dimensions (Answer Accuracy, Creativity, Richness, Visual Perception, Logical Coherence, Image Relative). The Needle-in-a-Haystack experiments in Figure 1 use a custom multi-key, multi-value, multi-needle variant with 5,117 training samples (contexts from 4K to 200K) and 200 test samples (8 keys, 4 values, 4 queries).

- **Base model(s).** Four models are used across experiments: (1) Qwen3-VL-8B-Thinking for long-horizon image and video reasoning (Section 5.2), chosen as "a strong visual reasoning model" representative of production-grade VLMs with long-context capabilities; (2) Qwen3-VL-4B-Instruct for multi-turn dialogue (Section 5.3); (3) LLaVA-1.5-7B for short-form VQA (Section 5.1); and (4) Qwen3-4B for pure-text reasoning (Section 5.2, Figure 5) and the NIAH experiments (Figure 1). For the LongBench-V2 experiment (Appendix C.2.1), Phi-3-mini-128k-instruct is used. The paper deliberately spans model scales (4B, 7B, 8B) and modalities (text-only, vision-language) to demonstrate generality, though all models are in the sub-10B parameter range — the paper does not evaluate on larger frontier-scale models.

- **Metrics.** The primary metric throughout is task-specific accuracy (percentage of correct answers). For long-horizon reasoning, this is exact-match accuracy against ground truth under greedy decoding. For MMDU multi-turn dialogue, evaluation uses LLM-as-a-judge with Gemini3 scoring each response across six dimensions on a 1–5 scale, with an overall score computed as the average (Table 3 and Appendix Table 6). Relative performance versus vanilla full-cache inference is reported as percentages (e.g., 103.26% means the method achieves 3.26% higher accuracy than full-cache). For short-form VQA (Table 1), the Avg. column reports relative performance versus the corresponding vanilla inference. Throughput (tokens per second) and total decoding time (seconds) are reported for efficiency scaling experiments (Figures 7, 10, 11, 12).

- **Baselines.** The paper compares against a comprehensive set of prior methods. For long-horizon reasoning (Table 2): **SnapKV** (Li et al., 2024c) — attention-score-based eviction heuristic; **R-KV** (Cai et al., 2025) — redundancy-aware compression using key-vector similarity for reasoning models; **AdaKV** (Feng et al., 2024) — dynamic per-layer budget allocation driven by attention statistics; **Ada-Pyramid-KV** (Cai et al., 2024) — combines SnapKV with pyramidal budget allocation; and **TrimKV** (Bui et al., 2025) — the direct predecessor with fixed per-head budgets. For pure-text reasoning (Figure 5), **SeerAttn-R** (Gao et al., 2025) is additionally included as a state-of-the-art learnable KV retrieval method. For short-form VQA (Table 1): **FastV** (Chen et al., 2024a), **VisionZip** (Yang et al., 2025b), **DART** (Wen et al., 2025), **PruMerge** (Shang et al., 2025), **DivPrune** (Alvar et al., 2025), and **CDPruner** (Zhang et al., 2025) — all visual token pruning methods. For LongBench-V2 (Appendix Table 7): **Locret** (Huang et al., 2025b) — a lightweight training-based eviction method using retaining heads. Vanilla (full KV cache without eviction) serves as the upper-bound reference across all experiments.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The KV budget is measured in tokens per head equivalent — all methods are compared under the same total global budget defined as `M × (#layers) × (#heads)`, where `M` is the per-head-equivalent budget. For long-horizon reasoning, budgets of 1024, 512, 256, 128, and 64 are evaluated (Table 2). For multi-turn dialogue, budgets of 512, 256, and 128 are evaluated (Table 3). For short-form VQA, DBTrimKV and TrimKV are configured with a per-head budget that matches the total memory used by visual pruning baselines (text tokens + pruned visual tokens). For efficiency scaling (Section 5.4), runtime is measured in seconds of decoding time at batch size 16, context length 16,384, and KV budget 256, varying generation length from 2,048 to 32,768 tokens.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not employ cross-validation for strategy selection (unlike the example compute-optimal paper). Results are reported as single-run evaluations on standard test sets. For reproducibility, the paper uses the LMMs-Eval framework with fixed settings (greedy decoding for reasoning benchmarks). The NIAH experiment uses a held-out test set of 200 samples (Section 5.1, Appendix B.3). No confidence intervals or statistical significance tests are reported, and the test sets are relatively small for some benchmarks (e.g., MATH-500 has 500 questions; MMStar and individual MMDU dimensions are not specified by size but are standard benchmarks with typically hundreds of examples). This is a limitation: the observed gains over vanilla (e.g., +3.73% at 512 budget on image/video reasoning) could be within sampling noise for small test sets, though the consistency across many benchmarks mitigates this concern.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Long-Horizon Image and Video Reasoning (Table 2)

The headline result is that DBTrimKV with a 512-token budget achieves 103.73% relative performance versus full-cache vanilla across seven image and video reasoning benchmarks, substantially outperforming all baselines while using a compressed cache. At a 256 budget, DBTrimKV achieves 101.49% — still exceeding full-cache inference. Even at a 128 budget (an 8× reduction from the 1024 baseline), DBTrimKV achieves 98.27%, essentially matching full-cache performance.

The performance gap between DBTrimKV and heuristic baselines is dramatic, especially at low budgets. At a 256-token budget on image reasoning (Table 2):
- DBTrimKV: MMStar 69.91, MathVisionmini 51.64, MMMUPro 41.85
- SnapKV: MMStar 50.89, MathVisionmini 7.89, MMMUPro 6.24
- AdaKV: MMStar 53.30, MathVisionmini 8.55, MMMUPro 8.27
- TrimKV: MMStar 65.67, MathVisionmini 40.79, MMMUPro 31.45

The gap is most striking on MathVisionmini at 256 budget: DBTrimKV achieves 51.64% versus SnapKV's 7.89% — a 6.5× improvement. On MMMUPro at 256 budget: DBTrimKV reaches 41.85% versus SnapKV's 6.24%. This shows that heuristic methods catastrophically fail under tight budgets, while learned retention preserves the evidence needed for complex mathematical and multimodal reasoning.

On video reasoning, the pattern is similar but the gaps are narrower. At 256 budget on VideoMME: DBTrimKV achieves 54.15% versus SnapKV's 50.15% — a meaningful but smaller advantage. On VideoMathQAmcq: DBTrimKV reaches 32.86% versus SnapKV's 14.52%. The narrower gaps on video benchmarks may reflect that video tasks require broader temporal context that is harder to compress without loss, or that the video-specific attention patterns are less amenable to the retention-based approach.

The comparison between fixed-budget and dynamic-budget methods (DBTrimKV vs. TrimKV; AdaKV/Ada-Pyramid-KV vs. SnapKV) consistently shows that dynamic allocation improves performance, with the gap widening as the overall budget decreases. DBTrimKV outperforms TrimKV at every budget level: at 128 budget, DBTrimKV reaches 101.49% relative average versus TrimKV's 84.83% — a 16.66 percentage point gap. This directly supports the claim that global, dynamic budget allocation is superior to fixed per-head budgets, especially when capacity is scarce.

A critical observation: DBTrimKV at budgets of 1024, 512, and 256 all surpass vanilla full-cache inference. The peak is at 512 budget (103.73%), with a slight decline at 1024 (103.26%) — both exceed full-cache. This is consistent with the attention dilution theory: at very high budgets, most tokens (including distractors) are retained, so dilution persists; at intermediate budgets, distractors are evicted while useful tokens are kept, sharpening attention; at very low budgets (128), useful tokens start getting evicted, and performance drops below full-cache (98.27%). The "sweet spot" is budget-dependent and task-dependent, but the paper demonstrates that it exists and that learned retention can find it.

The gains are especially pronounced on tasks requiring long-form generation. The paper specifically notes (Section 5.2): "The gains are especially pronounced on challenging tasks that require long-form generation, such as AIME24, MathVision, MMMUPro, and VideoMMMU subsets. In contrast, on tasks involving shorter reasoning, such as GSM8K, MATH-500, MMStar, and VideoMME, the performance differences among KV eviction methods are smaller." This makes sense: long-form generation means the KV cache grows with the model's own output, accumulating more distractors over time, so dilution is a bigger problem and eviction provides more benefit.

#### Pure Text Reasoning (Figure 5)

On pure text reasoning with Qwen3-4B, evaluated on AIME24, GSM8K, and MATH-500, DBTrimKV achieves a Pareto frontier that dominates all baselines, including SeerAttn-R. Figure 5 (a Pareto frontier plot with accuracy on the y-axis and KV budget on the x-axis) shows that DBTrimKV achieves higher accuracy than all competing methods at every budget level, and its accuracy at the lowest budgets (64, 128) substantially exceeds what baselines achieve even at higher budgets. For instance, DBTrimKV at a 128 budget achieves accuracy comparable to TrimKV at 256+ budget and dramatically outperforms SnapKV and AdaKV at any budget. The paper does not report exact accuracy numbers for each budget point in the text for Figure 5, but the visual trend is clear: DBTrimKV's curve sits above all others across the entire budget range.

The comparison with SeerAttn-R is notable because SeerAttn-R is a state-of-the-art learnable KV retrieval method specifically designed for reasoning-oriented language models. DBTrimKV's superiority suggests that the global, retention-based approach generalizes better than retrieval-based methods that may rely on query-specific selection heuristics.

#### Multi-Turn Dialogue (Table 3, Appendix Table 6)

On the MMDU multi-turn visual dialogue benchmark with Qwen3-VL-4B-Instruct, DBTrimKV achieves substantial gains over both vanilla full-cache inference and all heuristic baselines. At a 512-token budget, DBTrimKV reaches an overall score of 4.09 (out of 5) versus vanilla's 3.57 — a 14.46% relative improvement. At 256 budget: 3.87 (108.33% relative). At 128 budget: 3.72 (104.10% relative). Notably, DBTrimKV at all budgets exceeds vanilla performance.

The gains over heuristics are large: at 128 budget, DBTrimKV achieves 3.72 overall versus SnapKV's 2.29 (64.00% relative), R-KV's 2.17 (60.77%), and AdaKV's 2.25 (62.98%). TrimKV also outperforms heuristics (3.35, 93.61% relative), but DBTrimKV's dynamic allocation provides an additional ~10 percentage points of relative improvement over TrimKV at 128 budget.

The per-dimension breakdown in Appendix Table 6 reveals where the gains concentrate. At 128 budget, DBTrimKV exceeds vanilla on every single dimension: Answer Accuracy (3.43 vs. 3.37), Creativity (3.93 vs. 3.68), Richness (4.31 vs. 4.21), Visual Perception (3.54 vs. 3.40), Logical Coherence (4.11 vs. 3.90), Image Relative (3.57 vs. 3.40). The largest absolute gains are in Logical Coherence (+0.21) and Creativity (+0.25), suggesting that eviction helps the model maintain coherent reasoning chains and produce more creative responses by suppressing distracting context. Heuristic baselines, in contrast, show severe degradation in Visual Perception (SnapKV drops from 3.40 to 1.72 at 128 budget) and Image Relative (3.40 to 1.85), indicating failure to retain visually grounded context — exactly the tokens that DBTrimKV's learned retention preserves.

The dynamic allocation advantage is also visible in the TrimKV vs. DBTrimKV comparison: DBTrimKV consistently outperforms TrimKV across all budgets and dimensions, with the gap widening at lower budgets (e.g., at 128 budget, DBTrimKV's overall 3.72 vs. TrimKV's 3.35). This supports the claim that global, dynamic budget allocation is particularly valuable in interactive, multi-turn settings where the importance of different types of context (early-turn instructions, mid-turn images, recent utterances) varies dynamically.

#### Short-Form Visual Question Answering (Table 1)

On short-form VQA with LLaVA-1.5-7B, DBTrimKV and TrimKV are compared against visual token pruning methods that operate exclusively during prefill. The total KV budget for DBTrimKV/TrimKV is set to match the sum of all text tokens plus the visual-token budget used by each pruning baseline.

At 128 visual tokens budget: DBTrimKV achieves 99.9% relative average performance versus vanilla (GQA 61.2, VQAText 57.7, MME 1490.5), essentially matching full-cache. At 64 visual tokens: 99.7% relative. At 32 visual tokens: 99.4% relative. In contrast, the best visual pruning baseline (CDPruner) achieves 96.1% at 128, 94.5% at 64, and 91.5% at 32 — a consistent and widening gap.

The key insight from this experiment is that fixing the memory reduction exclusively to visual tokens is suboptimal. DBTrimKV and TrimKV compress jointly over both vision and text tokens and across all layers and heads, allowing the model to retain more visual tokens in heads that encode rich visual semantics while aggressively trimming tokens (visual or textual) that contribute less to downstream prediction. The performance gap between DBTrimKV/TrimKV and visual pruning baselines widens as the visual token budget shrinks, suggesting that when visual capacity is severely constrained, the ability to reallocate from text to vision (or to preserve critical visual tokens in specific heads) becomes increasingly valuable.

Notably, there is almost no gap between DBTrimKV and TrimKV on this task (both achieve 99.9%, 99.7%, 99.4% at the three budgets). This suggests that for short-form VQA with relatively small total KV cache sizes (the prompts are short — an image plus a question), the benefit of dynamic global allocation is minimal because the total budget is small enough that fixed per-head allocation is not a binding constraint. The dynamic allocation advantage emerges in long-context, long-generation settings where heads diverge more in their retention patterns.

#### Long-Context Retrieval (Appendix C.2.1, Table 7)

On LongBench-V2 with Phi-3-mini-128K-instruct at a 1024 KV budget, DBTrimKV achieves 31.44% average accuracy versus full KV's 28.79% — a +9.20% relative improvement. In contrast, Locret drops to 28.41% (-1.32% relative) and TrimKV drops to 26.13% (-9.24% relative). This is the only benchmark where TrimKV substantially underperforms full-cache while DBTrimKV excels, suggesting that the global allocation mechanism is particularly important for retrieval-style tasks where relevant evidence is sparse and concentrated in specific layers/heads — fixed per-head budgets in TrimKV may force premature eviction of critical retrieval evidence, while DBTrimKV's dynamic allocation preserves it.

The breakdown by difficulty (Short, Easy, Medium, Hard) shows that DBTrimKV's gains are concentrated in Short (+3.37 percentage points over full KV) and Easy (+2.23) subsets, with neutral performance on Medium (+1.17) and Hard (+2.88). This pattern is consistent with the attention dilution theory: easier questions benefit more from distractor removal because the relevant evidence is present and identifiable; harder questions may require broader context, making aggressive eviction less beneficial or even harmful.

#### Efficiency Scaling (Figures 7, 10, 11, 12)

Figure 7 reports total decoding time as generation length increases (batch size 16, context length 16,384, KV budget 256). DBTrimKV with PagedAttention scales substantially better than vanilla full-cache inference: at 32,768 generated tokens, DBTrimKV's decoding time is approximately 500 seconds versus vanilla's approximately 1,400 seconds — roughly 2.8× faster. TrimKV with fixed per-head budgets is slightly faster than DBTrimKV (the paper notes "a small overhead compared to TrimKV due to variable-length, head-specific cache handling"), but both learned methods dramatically outperform vanilla.

Appendix Figures 10, 11, and 12 show efficiency scaling with context length, generation length, and KV budget respectively. Across all dimensions, DBTrimKV maintains a consistent, flat efficiency profile while vanilla's decoding time grows linearly with context length and quadratically with generation length. The throughput (tokens per second) for DBTrimKV remains stable around 400–500 tok/s across context lengths from 4K to 16K, while vanilla drops from ~450 to ~200 tok/s. The paper notes that vanilla performance at 32K generation length is not reported due to out-of-memory errors, further demonstrating the practical necessity of KV eviction for long-generation tasks.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

- **Weight tying ablation (Table 4):** Removing weight tying in the final linear layer of the retention gates (i.e., using independent per-head projections as in TrimKV) substantially degrades MathVisionmini performance, especially in the low-budget regime. Without tying at a 256 budget: accuracy drops from 51.64% to 40.13% — a 11.51 percentage point decrease. At 512 budget: 51.67% with tying versus 44.74% without. This confirms that globally calibrated scores are not merely a convenience — they are essential for the dynamic allocation mechanism to work, and the benefit is largest when the budget is tightest and allocation decisions matter most.

- **Retention gate input representation (Table 5):** Using the token's contextual embedding `x_t` as gate input outperforms using the concatenation of key and value vectors `[k_t || v_t]`. At a 256 budget on MathVisionmini: `x_t` achieves 51.64% versus `[k_t || v_t]` at 43.75%. At 1024 budget: 52.63% versus 46.71%. The gap widens at lower budgets (7.89 points at 256 vs. 5.92 points at 1024), suggesting that the contextual embedding provides a stronger long-horizon utility signal than the projected KV representations alone — consistent with the intuition that the embedding encodes broader semantic and positional information relevant to persistence prediction.

- **Lookahead horizon T-t (Table 8):** The default lookahead of `T-t = 2` generally yields the best performance at moderate to high budgets (256, 512, 1024 on MathVisionmini). Reducing to a myopic one-step horizon (`T-t = 1`) consistently degrades performance: at 256 budget, accuracy drops from 51.64% to 49.34%. Extending to `T-t = 5` harms performance at higher budgets (dropping from 52.63% to 46.05% at 1024 budget) but helps at extremely constrained budgets (64 and 128), where it achieves 38.49% and 47.73% respectively versus 34.54% and 47.04% for `T-t = 2`. This non-monotonic relationship reveals an important trade-off: a short lookahead favors recent tokens (appropriate when cache capacity is adequate to retain both recent and persistent tokens), while a longer lookahead favors globally persistent tokens at the expense of recency (appropriate when capacity is too tight to keep both, forcing prioritization of the small number of tokens that remain useful for many steps).

- **Fixed-budget vs. dynamic-budget comparisons across multiple method pairs:** The paper provides an embedded ablation by comparing method pairs that differ primarily in budget allocation strategy: DBTrimKV (dynamic) vs. TrimKV (fixed), and AdaKV/Ada-Pyramid-KV (dynamic) vs. SnapKV (fixed). Across Table 2, both dynamic variants consistently outperform their fixed counterparts, with the gap widening as the overall budget decreases. For the TrimKV family, the dynamic advantage grows from ~1 percentage point at 1024 budget to ~16 percentage points at 128 budget (average relative performance). For the SnapKV family, the gap grows from ~2 points to ~8 points over the same range. This consistent pattern across two independent method families strongly supports the claim that dynamic allocation is beneficial, and that the benefit is largest when capacity is most constrained.

- **Per-head KV cache usage visualization (Figure 6, Appendix C.5):** Qualitative analysis of a MathVisionmini sample shows DBTrimKV's dynamic allocation in action. Total tokens (Figure 6a) are aggressively compressed and concentrated into a sparse subset of heads, frequently in mid-layers. Visual tokens (Figure 6b) are retained densely early in generation but shrink markedly as decoding shifts toward text-centric reasoning. The boundary tokens `<|vision_start|>` and `<|vision_end|>` (Figures 6c, 6d) are persistently retained across numerous heads throughout generation, suggesting the model has learned to treat these as compressed structural anchors that ground reasoning without the memory cost of full visual details. Appendix C.5 figures 13 and 14 show retained visual token heatmaps overlaid on input images, demonstrating that DBTrimKV initially preserves broad visual context (Figure 13a, 14a) but then narrows focus to task-critical regions — isolating the scythe, clock, and "RATHER TIME" banner in Figure 13b, and specific coordinate data points in Figure 14b. These visualizations provide mechanistic evidence that the learned retention scores successfully identify and preserve tokens with long-term task utility while discarding distractors.

- **Long-context scaling (Figure 1):** On a multi-key, multi-value, multi-needle NIAH task with Qwen3-4B, both TrimKV and DBTrimKV with 30% eviction outperform vanilla full-cache at all context lengths from 8K to 128K. The gap widens with context length: at 8K, all three methods cluster around 80–85% accuracy; at 128K, DBTrimKV and TrimKV maintain ~75–78% while vanilla drops to ~60%. The 30% eviction rate is held constant across lengths, meaning the absolute number of evicted tokens grows with context — proportionally more distractors are removed at longer contexts, which is exactly when dilution is most severe. DBTrimKV slightly outperforms TrimKV at all lengths, with the gap growing from negligible at 8K to ~2–3 percentage points at 128K, suggesting that dynamic allocation becomes more valuable as the total cache size (and thus the allocation decision space) grows.

- **Budget scaling (Figure 1b):** At a fixed 64K context on the NIAH task, DBTrimKV and TrimKV are evaluated across budgets from 2K to 64K. DBTrimKV achieves peak accuracy (~81%) at a 32K budget — substantially *below* the full 64K context — and maintains higher accuracy than vanilla (~76% at 64K full cache) across budgets from 4K to 64K. TrimKV peaks at a similar budget but with slightly lower accuracy (~79%). This is direct evidence for the U-shaped curve predicted by the attention dilution theory: too little budget hurts (useful tokens get evicted), too much budget also hurts (distractors dilute attention), and there's an intermediate optimum where distractor removal improves attention quality beyond full-cache. Vanilla, at 64K full cache, sits below both learned methods' peaks, confirming that the full cache is suboptimal for this task.

- **Method comparison on LongBench-V2 (Appendix Table 7):** DBTrimKV achieves +9.20% average improvement over full KV on Phi-3-mini-128K, while Locret degrades by -1.32% and TrimKV degrades by -9.24%. The TrimKV degradation is notable — it suggests that on retrieval-heavy tasks, fixed per-head budgets actively harm performance by forcing eviction of sparse but critical evidence that would be preserved under dynamic allocation. The DBTrimKV gain shows that the mechanism can not only prevent this degradation but substantially improve over full-cache by suppressing retrieval-irrelevant context.

### Critical Assessment

#### Claim 1: "Selective, learnable eviction can improve generation rather than merely approximate the full cache" (Abstract, Section 1)

**Where this holds:** The claim is supported across multiple benchmarks where DBTrimKV with compressed caches exceeds full-cache vanilla performance: 103.73% relative at 512 budget on image/video reasoning (Table 2); 114.46% relative at 512 budget on multi-turn dialogue (Table 3); +9.20% improvement on LongBench-V2 (Table 7); and the NIAH results where 30% eviction outperforms full cache at all context lengths (Figure 1). The consistency across tasks, models (Qwen3-VL-8B, Qwen3-VL-4B, Qwen3-4B, Phi-3-mini), and modalities (vision-language, pure text, dialogue, retrieval) is genuinely convincing.

**Where it is qualified or incomplete:** The improvement over full-cache is most pronounced at intermediate budgets (256–512 tokens) and narrows or disappears at very high budgets (1024+). On image/video reasoning, DBTrimKV at 1024 budget achieves 103.26% versus 103.73% at 512 — both above 100%, but the peak is not at the highest budget. This is consistent with the dilution theory but means the claim should be qualified: eviction can improve generation *when the budget is sufficiently constrained to force distractor removal while retaining useful tokens*. At very high budgets, the benefit diminishes because most distractors are retained anyway. The paper does not systematically characterize this budget-dependence across tasks — for instance, at what budget does DBTrimKV cross below full-cache? This threshold is task-dependent and not reported.

Additionally, the claim's generality is limited by the model scale. No experiments are conducted on models larger than 8B parameters. On larger models (70B+), the attention patterns and distractor effects may differ — larger models may have more capacity to route around distractors, or conversely, their larger head counts may create more opportunities for dilution. The theoretical framework (Proposition 3.1) is scale-agnostic, but the empirical validation is not.

#### Claim 2: "A single global eviction policy in which tokens from different layers, heads, and modalities compete directly for cache capacity" outperforms fixed per-layer/per-head budgets (Abstract, Section 4)

**Where this holds:** The claim is directly tested through the DBTrimKV vs. TrimKV comparison, where the primary difference is global vs. per-head budget allocation. Across Table 2, DBTrimKV consistently outperforms TrimKV, with the gap growing from ~1 point at 1024 budget to ~16 points at 128 budget on long-horizon reasoning. On multi-turn dialogue (Table 3), DBTrimKV outperforms TrimKV by ~3–6 points across budgets. The ablation in Table 4 confirms that weight tying (the mechanism enabling global comparison) is essential: removing it drops MathVisionmini accuracy by 11.5 points at 256 budget. The embedded AdaKV vs. SnapKV comparison (dynamic vs. fixed allocation in a different method family) shows the same pattern, strengthening the case that dynamic allocation is broadly beneficial.

**Where it is qualified or incomplete:** The claim is that tokens "compete directly for cache capacity." This is true at inference time, but the competition is mediated by the retention scores, which are learned under a *soft* global capacity constraint (Equation 5 with global `M_global`). At inference, hard eviction is applied by ranking scores and keeping the top `M_global`. The paper does not investigate whether the soft constraint during training adequately prepares the gates for hard eviction at inference — there could be a train-test mismatch where the gates learn to produce scores that satisfy the soft constraint but perform poorly under hard thresholding. The strong empirical results suggest this is not a major issue, but it is not explicitly analyzed.

The paper also does not compare against alternative dynamic allocation schemes that might be simpler. For instance, one could compute per-head retention scores independently (as in TrimKV) and then allocate budget proportionally to the sum of top-K scores per head — a post-hoc dynamic allocation without weight tying. This ablation would isolate whether weight tying during training is necessary, or whether independent training followed by global ranking at inference would suffice. The Table 4 ablation removes weight tying entirely (both training and inference), so it doesn't separate these effects.

#### Claim 3: "Substantially reduces KV memory while matching or surpassing full-cache inference" (Abstract)

**Where this holds:** The memory reduction is substantial by any measure. At a 256 budget on long-horizon reasoning, DBTrimKV uses 1/4 the cache of the 1024 budget baseline (typically close to the full prompt length for these tasks) while achieving 101.49% relative performance — better than full-cache. At 128 budget (1/8 reduction), it achieves 98.27%. On multi-turn dialogue, DBTrimKV at 128 budget achieves 104.10% relative — substantially better than full-cache with much less memory. The efficiency scaling experiments (Figures 7, 10, 11, 12) confirm that the memory reduction translates to wall-clock speedups: ~2.8× faster decoding at 32K generation length with batch size 16.

**Where it is qualified or incomplete:** The memory comparison is measured in KV cache size (number of retained tokens), but the actual memory footprint includes the retention gate parameters, the PagedAttention block table overhead, and any temporary storage for scoring during eviction. The paper states the retention gates add 0.43% to model parameters — negligible — but does not report the PagedAttention overhead relative to a dense KV cache. For very small budgets, the block table overhead could be non-trivial if many heads retain only a few tokens each (each head needs at least one page). The paper also does not report the latency cost of the eviction step itself — computing retention scores for all cached tokens, sorting them globally, and updating block tables. In the throughput-focused Figures 10–12, this cost appears to be amortized over generation steps and is small, but it is not isolated.

A more fundamental qualification: the memory reduction is measured relative to the *full context length*, which for these benchmarks can be tens of thousands of tokens. But in practice, the relevant question is whether the memory reduction enables deployment that would otherwise be impossible (e.g., fitting a 32K context into a GPU with limited VRAM). The paper does not report absolute memory usage in GB, only token counts, making it difficult to assess practical deployability. The OOM error for vanilla at 32K generation length (Figure 11) indicates that the method does enable generation that would otherwise fail, but this is reported for a specific GPU configuration that is not specified.

#### Claim 4: Theoretical analysis "showing that preferentially retaining useful tokens reduces attention dilution" (Abstract, Section 3.1)

**Where this holds:** The theoretical results in Proposition 3.1 and Corollary 3.2 are mathematically sound (proofs in Appendix A.1) and provide a clear mechanism for why eviction can help. The empirical results in Figure 1 (NIAH with varying context length and budget) are qualitatively consistent with the predictions: full-cache degrades as distractors accumulate, and selective eviction mitigates this. The token survival curves in Figure 2 and Appendix Figure 9 provide empirical evidence for the rapid decay of token utility that underpins the geometric retention model.

**Where it is qualified or incomplete:** The theoretical results are **analytical**, not **predictive**. Proposition 3.1 shows that dilution *can* occur under specified conditions (many near-tie distractors), but does not provide a way to predict *when* it will occur for a given model, prompt, and decoding step. Corollary 3.2 shows that dilution *can be reduced* if `ρ_D < ρ_U`, but does not guarantee that DBTrimKV achieves this condition — the empirical results are consistent with the theory but do not directly validate the mechanism. To validate the mechanism, one would need to measure `δ_t` (dilution) under full-cache, `δ_t^r` under DBTrimKV, and verify that the reduction follows the functional form in Corollary 3.2 and that the learned retention scores indeed achieve `ρ_D < ρ_U`. These measurements are not reported.

The geometric retention theory (Theorem 3.3) is similarly analytical — it shows that *if* query dynamics are stable and tokens have a block-exit property, *then* utility decays geometrically. The empirical validation in Figure 8 shows that the stability assumption holds for one model (Qwen3-8B), but the block-exit property (Assumption A.2) is not directly verified — the paper does not measure whether tokens actually have uniform positive exit probabilities from their relaxed survival regions. Instead, the geometric decay form is justified post-hoc by the survival curves in Figure 2, which show rapid decay but do not isolate the exponential functional form from other fast-decay forms.

#### Missing experiments that would strengthen the paper:

- **Direct measurement of attention dilution.** The paper defines `δ_t` (Equation 7) but never computes it. Measuring how `δ_t` changes with context length for full-cache models, and how DBTrimKV reduces it, would directly validate the central theoretical mechanism.

- **Comparison against oracle eviction.** An oracle that knows which tokens will be useful for future decoding (e.g., by analyzing full-cache attention patterns retrospectively) would establish an upper bound on what eviction can achieve and quantify how close DBTrimKV comes to optimal.

- **Scaling to larger models.** All experiments use models ≤8B parameters. The theoretical framework is scale-agnostic, but the empirical behavior of attention dilution and token persistence may differ at the 70B+ scale where models have more attention heads and potentially more capacity to route around distractors.

- **Analysis of which tokens get evicted.** The paper provides qualitative visualizations (Figures 6, 13, 14) but no systematic analysis of the types of tokens evicted (e.g., punctuation, articles, named entities, numerical values) or how this varies across tasks. Such analysis would strengthen the interpretability of the learned retention scores and could reveal failure modes (e.g., systematically evicting tokens of a particular type).

- **Sensitivity to the capacity loss weight `λ_cap`.** The paper sets `λ_cap = 1.0` for all experiments without ablation. The trade-off between quality preservation and memory constraint satisfaction is central to the method, and understanding how sensitive results are to this hyperparameter would inform practical deployment.

- **Training data requirements.** The VLM experiments use a mixture of four datasets with specific sampling ratios (40%, 30%, 30%, 20%). No ablation studies how performance varies with the amount or composition of training data. This matters for practitioners who may not have access to these specific datasets.

#### Conditional nature of the claims:

The central claim — that learned, globally calibrated KV eviction can improve generation quality over full-cache inference — holds **when the base model's representations contain the needed information and when the KV budget is sufficient to retain the genuinely useful tokens**. The paper is explicit about this boundary (Appendix D: "we only train the lightweight retention gates and freeze the base language model weights... the impact of jointly fine-tuning the base LLM alongside the retention network remains unknown"). On tasks where the base model fundamentally lacks capability, eviction cannot help — it can only sharpen attention on existing capabilities, not create new ones. This is analogous to the finding in the compute-optimal test-time scaling paper that test-time compute cannot help on the hardest problems (difficulty bin 5). The paper does not characterize where this boundary lies for its benchmarks, but the degradation of DBTrimKV at very low budgets (64 on long-horizon reasoning: 81.42% relative in Table 2) indicates that when the budget is too small to retain necessary evidence, performance drops below full-cache.

The dynamic allocation benefit is most important when (a) the total budget is substantially below the full context length (so allocation decisions matter), and (b) there is meaningful heterogeneity in how heads utilize long-range context (so reallocation helps). On short-form VQA (Table 1), where prompts are short and budgets are relatively large compared to context, the dynamic allocation provides almost no benefit over fixed per-head budgets (DBTrimKV and TrimKV perform identically). This suggests that for applications with short prompts and modest generation lengths, the simpler TrimKV with fixed budgets may be sufficient, and the additional complexity of global calibration and PagedAttention may not be warranted.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### Limitation 1: The Cost of Difficulty Estimation Eats Into the Gains

The paper's central claim — that DBTrimKV can match or exceed full-cache performance while using substantially less KV memory — assumes that the learned retention scores accurately predict which tokens will be useful for future decoding. But the method for making those predictions accurate requires **training retention gates on data from the target model and task distribution**, which means that deploying DBTrimKV on a new model or task family requires a non-trivial training phase before any inference benefit is realized.

The training data requirements are not negligible. For the main VLM experiments (Section 5.2), the authors train on a mixture of four datasets — M4Instruct (40%), Academic Video (30%), R1-OneVision (30%), and OpenR1-Math-220k (20%) — with sequences up to length 32,768 tokens, using 4 H100 GPUs. The paper provides training hyperparameters in Appendix B.1 but does not report wall-clock training time, total FLOPs, or GPU-hours consumed. For a practitioner considering whether to adopt this method, the relevant question is: **how many GPU-hours of training are required to achieve the reported inference-time gains, and how does this amortize over the expected inference volume?**

The consequence is that the headline efficiency numbers (e.g., 4× less KV memory while exceeding full-cache performance; Section 5.2, Table 2) are **inference-only** metrics. They do not account for the upfront cost of training the retention gates. If the inference volume is low, the training cost may dominate — you spend more compute training the eviction policy than you save by using it. The paper does not report a break-even analysis (how many inference tokens must be generated before the training cost is recouped by inference savings), nor does it study how much training data is actually needed — the 40/30/30/20 mixture ratios are stated without ablation, so we don't know whether a fraction of that data would suffice.

The paper also does not investigate **transferability**: can retention gates trained on one model or task family be reused on another? If a practitioner trains gates on Qwen3-VL-8B-Thinking with the specified data mixture and then wants to deploy on a different VLM (e.g., LLaVA-Next, InternVL), do they need to retrain from scratch? The fact that the PRM in the compute-optimal scaling paper (reference example) failed to transfer between GPT-4 and PaLM 2 outputs due to distribution shift suggests that similar transfer failures are plausible here — retention gates trained on one model's attention patterns may not generalize to another model with different layer counts, head dimensions, or training data. This is not tested.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation explicitly. The authors note in Appendix D that they "only train the lightweight retention gates and freeze the base language model weights," which is framed as a strength (computational efficiency) rather than a cost. The training cost is implicitly assumed to be small because the gates are 0.43% of model parameters — but parameter count is a poor proxy for training cost when the training data volume is substantial and sequences are long (32,768 tokens). The paper does not suggest future work on reducing training data requirements, transfer learning, or amortization analysis.

---

### Limitation 2: All Experiments Are on Sub-10B Parameter Models

Every model evaluated in this paper — Qwen3-VL-8B-Thinking, Qwen3-VL-4B-Instruct, LLaVA-1.5-7B, Qwen3-4B, and Phi-3-mini-128k-instruct — falls in the sub-10B parameter range. The paper explicitly acknowledges this in Appendix D:

> "Our empirical evaluations were conducted on models up to the 8B parameter scale. We did not study the efficacy of globally calibrated retention gates on significantly larger, frontier-scale models. Establishing scaling laws for token retention and evaluating whether the mitigation of attention dilution behaves similarly at the 70B+ parameter scale is a critical next step."

This is a consequential gap because the attention dilution mechanism that underpins the paper's theoretical motivation may behave differently at larger scales. Larger models have more attention heads per layer (typically 64–128 heads in 70B+ models vs. 8–32 in the 4–8B range), which changes the dynamics of how attention mass is distributed and how distractors compete with useful tokens. With more heads, individual heads may be more specialized, potentially making dilution either more severe (if distractors concentrate in heads that otherwise process useful context) or less severe (if the model learns to route useful and distracting tokens to different heads).

The empirical claim that DBTrimKV can *exceed* full-cache performance (e.g., 114.46% relative on MMDU at 512 budget; Table 3) may not generalize. Frontier-scale models are typically trained with more aggressive data curation and attention regularization, which could reduce the prevalence of distractors in their training distribution and thus reduce the benefit of distractor-evicting attention. Alternatively, the larger head count could create *more* opportunities for beneficial reallocation under a global budget, making DBTrimKV's advantage even larger. Without data, neither direction can be ruled out.

The theoretical framework (Proposition 3.1, Theorem 3.3) is scale-agnostic — it doesn't depend on model size. But the empirical validation of the assumptions (stable query dynamics in Figure 8, token survival curves in Figure 2) is performed on 4–8B parameter models only. The paper does not verify that Assumptions A.1 (low-dimensional query dynamics) and A.2 (block-exit property) hold at larger scales. If the low-dimensional approximation degrades (because larger models use higher-dimensional query-key interactions), the geometric retention justification weakens.

**Mitigation status.** The paper is transparent about this limitation and identifies it as "a critical next step" in Appendix D. However, the claim in Section 1 that the model is "representative of the capabilities of many contemporary LLMs" (referring to PaLM 2-S* in the reference example — the actual paper uses Qwen3 and LLaVA models, but the logic is similar) cannot be verified without replication at scale. The limitation is acknowledged but not mitigated.

---

### Limitation 3: No Direct Validation That Learned Retention Achieves `ρ_U > ρ_D` (the Condition Required by the Theory)

The paper's central theoretical argument for *why* eviction can improve over full-cache attention is Corollary 3.2: if the retention policy preferentially retains useful tokens over distractors (i.e., if `ρ_U > ρ_D`, meaning the logit-weighted average retention rate for useful tokens exceeds that for distractors), then dilution is reduced; if `ρ_D/ρ_U → 0`, dilution is eliminated. This corollary provides a clean sufficient condition for eviction to improve attention quality.

The paper provides strong empirical evidence that DBTrimKV outperforms full-cache inference (Tables 2, 3, 7; Figures 1, 5), which is **consistent with** the condition holding. But the paper never directly measures `ρ_U` or `ρ_D`, never identifies which tokens are "useful" (`U_t`) versus "distractors" in any evaluation, and never verifies that the learned retention scores satisfy `ρ_U > ρ_D` on the benchmarks where performance improves. The empirical results support the *outcome* that eviction helps, but not the *mechanism* that Corollary 3.2 posits.

This matters for two reasons. First, without mechanism validation, we cannot distinguish between "DBTrimKV works because it reduces attention dilution" and "DBTrimKV works for some other reason" (e.g., the retention-gated attention training procedure acts as a regularizer that improves the model's internal representations even though base weights are frozen; or the capacity constraint forces the model to learn better attention patterns during training that transfer to inference). Second, if the mechanism is not validated, it's unclear how to diagnose failures — when DBTrimKV underperforms (e.g., at very low budgets like 64 on long-horizon reasoning, Table 2: 81.42% relative), is it because `ρ_U < ρ_D` (useful tokens are being evicted faster than distractors), or because the budget is simply too small to retain the minimum necessary set of useful tokens regardless of relative rates?

The theoretical framework in Section 3.2 (geometric retention as query-agnostic future utility) similarly lacks direct validation. Theorem 3.3 shows that *if* Assumptions A.1 and A.2 hold, *then* utility decays geometrically. The paper validates Assumption A.1 (stable query dynamics) in Appendix Figure 8, but Assumption A.2 (block-exit from relaxed survival region) is never measured. The empirical survival curves in Figure 2 and Appendix Figure 9 show that token utility decays rapidly, which is consistent with the theory but does not isolate the geometric functional form or verify the block-exit mechanism. A direct test would involve: for a set of tokens, track whether they remain in top-K over a generation, fit a geometric decay model to their survival probability, and test whether the learned `β_i` correlates with the fitted decay rate. This is not done.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this gap. The theoretical results are presented as motivation and justification for the architectural choices (geometric decay, retention scoring), and the empirical results are presented as validation of the method's effectiveness. But the causal chain — "attention dilution degrades full-cache → DBTrimKV reduces dilution → performance improves" — has a missing middle link (the dilution reduction is never measured). The paper treats the empirical success as sufficient validation, which is reasonable for a systems/efficiency paper but leaves the theoretical contribution as an interpretive framework rather than a verified mechanism.

### Limitation 4: Multi-Turn Dialogue Gains Depend on a Specific LLM-as-Judge Protocol Whose Reliability Is Not Established

The multi-turn dialogue results on MMDU (Section 5.3, Table 3, Appendix Table 6) report the most dramatic improvements over full-cache inference: DBTrimKV achieves 114.46% relative performance at 512 budget, 108.33% at 256, and 104.10% at 128. These gains are substantially larger than those on long-horizon reasoning (101–104% range) and are the headline numbers most likely to persuade practitioners that eviction can meaningfully improve generation quality.

However, the evaluation protocol for MMDU uses **Gemini3 as an LLM-based judge** across six dimensions (Answer Accuracy, Creativity, Richness, Visual Perception, Logical Coherence, Image Relative), each scored on a 1–5 scale. The paper states this follows "the MMDU protocol" (Section 5.3) but provides no details about judge calibration, inter-rater reliability, or correlation with human evaluation. The use of LLM-as-judge for multi-turn dialogue evaluation is known to be sensitive to prompt design, position bias (preferring the first or last response), and self-enhancement bias (preferring responses that resemble the judge's own generation style).

The concern is not that LLM-as-judge is inherently unreliable — it can be well-calibrated. The concern is that **the paper provides no evidence that the reported gains are robust to judge choice or judge prompt**. If Gemini3 has systematic biases that happen to favor DBTrimKV's outputs (e.g., it prefers shorter, more focused responses, which eviction might naturally produce by suppressing distractor context), the 14% relative improvement could be partly or largely a judge artifact rather than a genuine quality improvement. This is particularly important because the MMDU gains are the largest in the paper and would be the most compelling evidence for the "eviction as attention regularization" thesis.

The per-dimension breakdown in Appendix Table 6 shows that DBTrimKV exceeds vanilla on every single dimension at 128 budget (e.g., Logical Coherence 4.11 vs. 3.90, Creativity 3.93 vs. 3.68). This uniformity across dimensions is somewhat surprising — if eviction were merely sharpening attention on relevant context, one might expect larger gains on dimensions that depend on context retention (Visual Perception, Image Relative) and smaller gains on dimensions that depend more on the model's generative capabilities (Creativity, Richness). The fact that improvements are broad-spectrum could indicate a genuine quality improvement, or could indicate that the judge's scores are correlated across dimensions and a single underlying preference drives the apparent multi-dimensional gain.

The paper also does not report any human evaluation or alternative judge. A robustness check with a different judge model (e.g., GPT-4, Claude) or with human annotators on a subset of examples would substantially strengthen confidence in the MMDU results. Without such checks, the 14% gain — while striking — should be interpreted cautiously.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address judge reliability. It cites the MMDU protocol (Liu et al., 2024e) and uses Gemini3 as the judge, but provides no calibration details, no inter-judge agreement metrics, and no human validation. This is a significant gap given that the MMDU results are the strongest evidence for the central claim that eviction improves generation quality beyond full-cache inference.

### Limitation 5: The Method Requires PagedAttention and Variable-Length Kernels — Deployment Complexity Not Fully Characterized

DBTrimKV's global eviction policy produces variable-length KV caches per head, which requires infrastructure support beyond a standard dense KV cache implementation. The paper describes the solution in Section 4.2:

> "To support variable cache sizes induced by dynamic, head-specific budgets, we use a paged-attention layout, similar to Kwon et al. [2023]. Specifically, KV entries are stored in fixed-size pages, and each head maintains a block table for its currently active pages. Attention is then computed with variable-length kernels [Dao, 2023] using the resulting per-head sequence lengths."

This is a non-trivial deployment requirement. PagedAttention (Kwon et al., 2023) is available in vLLM and similar serving frameworks, but it introduces memory fragmentation, page table management overhead, and potential performance cliffs when cache sizes change. Variable-length attention kernels (FlashAttention-2, Dao 2023) support different sequence lengths per head but are typically optimized for the case where all heads have the same or similar lengths — DBTrimKV deliberately creates heterogeneous lengths, which could reduce kernel efficiency if the length distribution is highly skewed.

The runtime experiments (Section 5.4, Figures 7, 10, 11, 12) show that DBTrimKV is faster than full-cache vanilla across all scaling dimensions, and the paper states that "DBTrimKV with PagedAttention remains highly efficient" (Section 5.4). However, the experiments are conducted in a controlled setting with synthetic dummy inputs (Appendix C.4: "dummy input sequences of varying context lengths, populated with filler tokens and an all-ones attention mask") — not on real workloads with real KV cache access patterns. The overhead of page table management, memory fragmentation, and variable-length kernel dispatch may be higher on real workloads with irregular eviction patterns than on synthetic benchmarks with uniform token distributions.

More importantly, the paper compares DBTrimKV only against TrimKV (which uses fixed per-head budgets but also benefits from PagedAttention, if implemented) and vanilla (which uses dense KV caches). It does not compare against a simpler dynamic allocation scheme that might avoid the need for full PagedAttention. For instance, a method that allocates budgets per layer rather than per head would produce variable-length caches at the layer level, which is easier to implement (layer-level padding rather than head-level paging). The paper argues that head-level allocation is necessary because "a small number of heads preserve long-range tokens, while many heads quickly lose them" (Section 3.2, Figure 2b), but it does not test whether layer-level allocation with finer-grained token selection (e.g., TrimKV's per-head scoring followed by layer-level budget aggregation) could achieve comparable results with simpler infrastructure.

The practical consequence: a practitioner who wants to deploy DBTrimKV must either (a) use a serving framework that supports PagedAttention with variable-length per-head caches (vLLM, possibly with modifications), or (b) implement this infrastructure themselves. The paper does not provide a complexity analysis, a memory overhead analysis for the block tables, or guidance on page size selection. The claim that "we expect the speedups over vanilla to grow with longer contexts and longer generations" (Section 5.4) is plausible but unverified on the specific infrastructure requirements that DBTrimKV imposes.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges that DBTrimKV "adds a small overhead compared to TrimKV due to variable-length, head-specific cache handling" (Section 5.4), but frames this as acceptable given the performance gains. The infrastructure requirements are described but not analyzed in depth. The paper does not provide ablation comparing the PagedAttention overhead to the gains from dynamic allocation, nor does it discuss fallback implementations for frameworks that do not support variable-length per-head caches.

### Limitation 6: Training Data Composition and Volume Are Not Ablated — Practical Transfer to New Domains Is Unguided

The paper trains retention gates on a specific mixture of datasets whose composition is stated in Section 5.2 and Appendix B.1: "40% of M4Instruct, 30% of Academic Video, 30% of R1-OneVision, and 20% of OpenR1-Math-220k." These are randomly sampled subsets. For pure-text experiments, training uses OpenR1-Math-220k only. For short-form VQA (Section 5.1), training uses LLaVA-Next.

The composition of this training mixture is never ablated. We do not know:
- Whether all four datasets are necessary, or whether one dominates the learned policy.
- How sensitive performance is to the sampling ratios (40/30/30/20 vs. equal weighting).
- Whether training on a single representative dataset (e.g., only M4Instruct) would achieve comparable performance.
- How much total training data is needed — the paper uses subsets of each dataset but does not report the total number of training sequences or tokens.
- Whether the training data needs to match the evaluation domain. For instance, DBTrimKV trained on the VLM mixture is evaluated on both image and video reasoning benchmarks — does the inclusion of Academic Video and R1-OneVision in training data matter for video benchmark performance, or would image-only training data suffice?

This matters for practitioners who want to apply DBTrimKV to a new domain not covered by the paper's benchmarks (e.g., medical imaging VQA, code generation, long-document summarization). The paper provides no guidance on how to construct training data for a new domain: how many training sequences are needed? Should they come from the target task distribution, or is a generic mixture sufficient? Does the training data need to include examples with varying context lengths matching the deployment regime? The paper's training uses sequences up to 32,768 tokens (Appendix B.1) — if a practitioner wants to deploy at 128K context length, does the training data need to include 128K-length sequences, or is 32K sufficient because the retention gates learn token-level persistence that generalizes across lengths?

The NIAH experiment (Section 5.1, Figure 1) provides partial evidence on this question: the model is trained on contexts from 4K to 200K and tested on held-out 64K contexts, suggesting that the learned policy does generalize across context lengths when training covers the target range. But this is a synthetic task; whether the same holds for natural language and vision tasks is untested.

The paper also does not compare against a **zero-shot transfer** baseline: what happens if you take DBTrimKV gates trained on the VLM mixture and evaluate them on pure-text reasoning (or vice versa)? If performance is comparable to domain-specific training, the training data requirement is less of a barrier. If performance degrades substantially, then every new domain requires its own training phase, which amplifies Limitation 1 (training cost).

**Mitigation status.** This limitation is not addressed. The paper treats the training data as a fixed experimental setup without analyzing its influence on results. Appendix D mentions future work on "training-time integration of eviction mechanisms into large-scale foundation models" but not on data efficiency or transfer learning. For a method that requires training (unlike heuristic baselines such as SnapKV or AdaKV, which are training-free), the absence of training data ablations is a significant practical gap.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper shifts the framing of KV cache eviction from a **necessary efficiency compromise** to a **potential quality-improving mechanism**. Before this work, the dominant paradigm — encoded in the formal objective of Equation 2 and reflected in methods from StreamingLLM (Xiao et al., 2023) to SnapKV (Li et al., 2024c) to Locret (Huang et al., 2025b) — treated eviction as compression: the goal was to approximate full-cache attention as closely as possible while using less memory. The unstated assumption was that full-cache attention represents the best the model can do, and any deviation from it degrades quality. That assumption produced a predictable research landscape: papers competed on minimizing the accuracy gap versus full-cache inference, with the implicit ceiling being "no degradation."

This paper demonstrates that the ceiling is **not at zero degradation** — it is above zero, potentially substantially above. On multi-turn dialogue (MMDU), DBTrimKV achieves 114.46% relative performance versus full-cache vanilla at a 512-token budget (Table 3). On long-horizon image and video reasoning, it achieves 103.73% at the same budget (Table 2). On LongBench-V2 retrieval, it achieves +9.20% absolute improvement over full-cache (Table 7). These are not marginal gains — they indicate that full-cache attention in long contexts is actively suboptimal, and that selective eviction can correct a genuine quality problem (attention dilution) rather than merely approximate an ideal that was never ideal to begin with.

The conceptual shift has immediate consequences for how the field should evaluate KV eviction methods. "Within X% of full-cache" is no longer a meaningful success criterion — it may represent a failure to achieve the gains that learned eviction can provide. The appropriate baseline is not "full cache" but **"oracle eviction that retains useful tokens and removes distractors."** The paper does not compute such an oracle, but Corollary 3.2 provides the theoretical condition: any policy achieving `ρ_U > ρ_D` (higher retention of useful tokens than distractors) reduces dilution. Future work should benchmark against this condition, not against the full cache.

The work also **resolves a latent tension** in the long-context efficiency literature. On one side, heuristic eviction methods consistently reported that reducing KV cache size degrades performance — hence the compression-as-approximation framing. On the other side, sparse attention methods (Deng et al., 2024), retrieval-augmented generation, and "lost in the middle" analyses (Liu et al., 2024c) all provided evidence that not all context tokens are helpful, and that attention quality can degrade as irrelevant tokens accumulate. This paper unifies these observations: heuristic methods degrade performance because they cannot reliably distinguish useful tokens from distractors (they achieve `ρ_U ≈ ρ_D` or worse), so they remove both indiscriminately. Learned retention, by predicting future utility rather than current attention scores, can achieve `ρ_U > ρ_D`, turning eviction from a quality cost into a quality benefit. The conflicting prior findings were not contradictions — they were products of different eviction policies operating at different points on the `ρ_U/ρ_D` spectrum.

This reframing makes certain research directions more attractive and others less so:

**More attractive:** Improving verifier (retention gate) quality, because the bottleneck is no longer "how to minimize damage from eviction" but "how to maximize the `ρ_U/ρ_D` ratio." The paper's finding that lookahead horizon `T-t = 2` outperforms `T-t = 1` at moderate budgets but `T-t = 5` outperforms at very tight budgets (Table 8) suggests that retention scoring itself can be optimized — there is a research program in designing better utility aggregation functions, not just in training better gates. The architectural unification of token selection and budget allocation via weight tying suggests that other design choices currently treated as separate (e.g., modality-specific pruning ratios, layer-wise compression schedules) could be collapsed into learned, globally calibrated mechanisms.

**Less attractive:** Hand-crafted budget allocation rules (pyramid shapes, layer-wise ratios, modality-specific budgets). The paper shows that dynamic, learned allocation consistently outperforms fixed rules, with the gap widening as budgets shrink (DBTrimKV vs. TrimKV gap grows from ~1 point at 1024 budget to ~16 points at 128 budget on long-horizon reasoning; Table 2). This does not mean heuristic budget allocation is obsolete — it may remain useful for training-free deployments — but the burden of proof has shifted: a new heuristic must demonstrate not just that it approximates a learned policy, but that it does so with substantially lower deployment complexity. The paper does not provide this complexity analysis, so the trade-off remains underexplored.

The work also establishes that **KV eviction can function as attention regularization** (a term the paper uses in Section 7). This connects the KV eviction literature to the broader regularization literature in deep learning: dropout randomly suppresses activations during training to prevent co-adaptation; KV eviction selectively suppresses token contributions during inference to prevent attention dilution. The connection is conceptual rather than mechanical, but it suggests that eviction policies could be designed with explicit regularization objectives (e.g., maximizing the entropy of the attention distribution over retained tokens, or penalizing attention concentration on a small number of heads) rather than purely matching a teacher distribution. The paper does not explore this, but the reframing makes it a natural next step.

Finally, the theoretical contribution of Section 3.2 — justifying geometric retention as the natural consequence of token utility being a persistence event under stable query dynamics — provides a **principled vocabulary** for reasoning about token importance that was previously absent. Before this work, "token importance" in KV eviction was operationalized through attention scores (SnapKV), key-vector similarity (R-KV), or learned black-box scores (TrimKV), without a clear answer to "what does it mean for a token to be important?" The paper's answer — importance is predicted persistence in a relaxed top-K survival region, governed by token-specific block-exit parameters `(b_i, ε_i)` — is both theoretically grounded and empirically testable (via the survival curve analyses in Figure 2 and Appendix Figure 9). This vocabulary enables new research questions: can we estimate `(b_i, ε_i)` directly from token representations rather than learning `β_i` end-to-end? Do different token types (nouns vs. punctuation, visual patches vs. text tokens, instruction tokens vs. content tokens) have systematically different `(b_i, ε_i)` distributions? Does the block structure align with known linguistic or visual boundaries (sentence boundaries, scene cuts, topic shifts)?

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Directly measuring attention dilution and validating the `ρ_U > ρ_D` mechanism.** The paper's central theoretical argument — that learned retention reduces attention dilution by preferentially retaining useful tokens over distractors — is never directly tested. The dilution quantity `δ_t` (Equation 7) is defined but never computed, and the retention-weighted dilution `δ_t^r` (Corollary 3.2) is never measured. A strong follow-up would: (1) for a set of long-context questions with known relevant evidence (e.g., NIAH tasks where the "needle" tokens are known, or reading comprehension where the answer span is annotated), identify `U_t` as the tokens containing or pointing to the correct answer; (2) compute `δ_t` for the full-cache model and `δ_t^r` for DBTrimKV across generation steps; (3) verify that `δ_t^r < δ_t` and that the reduction follows the functional form `δ_t^r = (ρ_D/ρ_U) δ_t / ((1 - δ_t) + (ρ_D/ρ_U) δ_t)`; (4) measure the learned retention scores for `U_t` tokens versus distractors and verify that `ρ_U > ρ_D` on examples where DBTrimKV outperforms full-cache, and `ρ_U ≈ ρ_D` or `ρ_U < ρ_D` on examples where it underperforms. This would transform the paper's theoretical contribution from an interpretive framework into a validated mechanistic explanation. The MMDU benchmark is a natural testbed because the "useful" tokens likely include early-turn instructions, image regions referenced in later questions, and constraint specifications — these can be identified through careful annotation.

**Oracle eviction: establishing the upper bound on what eviction can achieve.** The paper never answers: how close does DBTrimKV come to optimal eviction? An oracle eviction policy that knows future full-cache attention patterns could retrospectively identify which tokens would have been useful and retain only those. Specifically: run full-cache inference on a task, record which tokens receive attention weight above a threshold at any future decoding step, and define these as the oracle retention set. Then evaluate accuracy when only those tokens are retained. This establishes an empirical upper bound on eviction's quality-improvement potential. Comparing DBTrimKV to this oracle would answer: (a) is there substantial headroom beyond DBTrimKV's current performance? (b) does the oracle also exceed full-cache, confirming the dilution theory independently of learned retention? (c) on which tasks is the gap between DBTrimKV and oracle largest, indicating where retention gate training or architecture needs improvement? The NIAH experiments in Figure 1 are a partial step in this direction (the relevant "needles" are known), but they only report accuracy, not the oracle eviction ceiling or the oracle-to-DBTrimKV gap.

**Scaling laws for token retention across model size.** The paper explicitly identifies this as "a critical next step" (Appendix D) but provides no data. A systematic study would: train DBTrimKV-style retention gates on models at multiple scales (e.g., 1B, 4B, 8B, 30B, 70B parameters within the same model family, such as Qwen3 or LLaMA-3), measure (a) the per-head survival heterogeneity (analogous to Figure 2b) — does the variance in long-range token persistence across heads increase, decrease, or stay constant with scale? (b) the benefit of dynamic over fixed allocation (DBTrimKV vs. TrimKV gap) as a function of model size — does global allocation matter more for larger models with more heads? (c) the peak improvement over full-cache as a function of model size — do larger models benefit more or less from distractor removal? The theoretical framework predicts that dilution depends on the ratio of distractors to useful tokens in the softmax denominator, which is scale-agnostic, but larger models may have more capacity to route useful and distracting tokens to different heads, potentially reducing dilution without eviction. If the benefit of eviction *decreases* with model scale, the practical impact of this work is concentrated at smaller deployment scales (edge devices, consumer GPUs). If the benefit *increases* or stays constant, learned eviction becomes a standard component of large-model inference stacks.

**Jointly fine-tuning the base model with retention gates.** The paper keeps base model weights frozen and trains only retention gates (0.43% of parameters). Appendix D hypothesizes that "jointly fine-tuning the base LLM alongside the retention network" could allow the model to "inherently structure its internal representations to be more amenable to KV cache eviction." A concrete follow-up would: initialize a base model, add untrained retention gates, and jointly fine-tune both on a mixture of language modeling and retention-gated training objectives (potentially with a two-stage schedule: warm up the gates alone, then jointly optimize). Key measurements: (a) does joint training produce higher `β` values for genuinely persistent tokens compared to frozen-base training? (b) does it alter the base model's attention patterns (e.g., making useful tokens more sharply distinguished from distractors in attention logit space, which would make retention easier)? (c) does joint training cause catastrophic forgetting of the base model's capabilities on tasks outside the training distribution? The risk is that fine-tuning a pretrained model with a retention objective could distort its representations — the frozen-base approach avoids this but may leave performance on the table. A negative result (joint training degrades general capability) would be informative: it would establish that retention gates should remain an inference-time adapter rather than a training-time integration.

**Extending retention scoring to dynamically adjust eviction aggressiveness during generation.** The current approach uses a fixed global budget `M_global` and a fixed lookahead horizon `T-t = 2` (or another constant). But the qualitative visualizations in Figure 6 and Appendix C.5 suggest that optimal eviction behavior changes during generation: early steps retain broad visual context; later steps aggressively prune to task-critical tokens. A dynamic policy could vary the retention threshold based on generation progress, task phase, or estimated uncertainty. Specifically: monitor the distribution of retention scores across cached tokens at each compression step; when the distribution is sharply bimodal (a few very high scores, many very low), the model is confident about which tokens matter and can evict aggressively; when the distribution is flat, the model is uncertain and should retain more tokens. This connects to the exploration-exploitation framing in the compute-optimal test-time scaling paper: early in generation, retain more (explore); later, when the reasoning path is established, retain only what's needed (exploit). A concrete experiment: on MathVisionmini, compare fixed-budget DBTrimKV against an adaptive variant that starts with a large budget and progressively tightens it based on the variance of retention scores, measuring both accuracy and total memory consumption. If adaptive budgeting achieves the same accuracy as fixed-budget with lower average memory, it strengthens the case for learned eviction as a dynamic resource allocation mechanism rather than a static compression policy.

**Stress-testing on adversarial distractors.** The attention dilution theory (Proposition 3.1) predicts that dilution is most severe when distractors have logits close to those of useful tokens (`Δ = O(1)`). An adversarial evaluation would construct prompts where distractors are specifically designed to be "near-tie" with useful evidence: add paragraphs of text that are semantically related to the question but contain no answer-relevant information, or add images that are visually similar to the key image but task-irrelevant. Under such conditions: (a) does full-cache performance degrade more severely than on standard benchmarks? (b) does DBTrimKV's advantage over full-cache grow (because dilution is worse, so correction provides more benefit) or shrink (because the learned retention scores may be fooled by adversarial near-tie distractors)? This experiment would stress-test both the dilution theory and the robustness of learned retention scores. If DBTrimKV's retention gates can be adversarially confused (assigning high `β` to well-crafted distractors), it reveals a vulnerability that matters for security-sensitive deployments. If DBTrimKV remains robust, it strengthens the case that learned retention captures genuine semantic persistence rather than superficial attention-score patterns.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Long-horizon visual reasoning in resource-constrained settings.** The paper demonstrates that on image and video reasoning benchmarks using Qwen3-VL-8B-Thinking with a 256-token budget, DBTrimKV achieves 101.49% relative performance versus full-cache inference (Table 2) — better results with 1/4 the KV cache memory. For deployment on edge devices (mobile phones, AR/VR headsets, drones) where GPU memory is severely limited (e.g., 4–8 GB), this memory reduction can mean the difference between being able to run the model at all versus exceeding device capacity. The efficiency scaling experiments (Figure 11) show that at 32K generation length, DBTrimKV decodes in ~500 seconds versus vanilla's ~1400 seconds (2.8× faster), with throughput stable at ~400–500 tok/s regardless of context length (Figure 10). For a mobile VLM assistant processing user questions about a video feed, this speedup enables real-time or near-real-time interaction that would otherwise require cloud offloading.

**Long-context retrieval and document QA pipelines.** On LongBench-V2 with Phi-3-mini-128K, DBTrimKV achieves +9.20% average accuracy improvement over full-cache while using a compressed cache (Table 7). This is directly applicable to retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) pipelines where the model must reason over multiple retrieved documents. In a typical RAG setup, 10–20 documents of 1K–2K tokens each are concatenated into a single prompt, creating contexts of 10K–40K tokens. Much of this content is tangentially relevant at best — only 1–2 documents typically contain the answer. Full-cache attention dilutes over all retrieved documents; DBTrimKV's learned retention can suppress the less relevant documents and sharpen attention on the answer-containing ones. The practical benefit is twofold: (a) higher accuracy on the retrieval task, because attention is focused on genuinely relevant evidence; (b) lower per-query cost, because the compressed KV cache enables larger batch sizes or faster processing. For a legal document review system processing thousands of contracts per day, a 9% accuracy improvement combined with 2–4× throughput increase translates to both better outcomes and lower infrastructure cost. The paper does not evaluate on standard RAG benchmarks (e.g., Natural Questions with retrieved passages, or multi-document QA like HotpotQA), which would be a natural application.

**Multi-turn visual dialogue systems.** The MMDU results (Table 3, Appendix Table 6) show DBTrimKV exceeding full-cache performance across all six evaluation dimensions, with particularly strong gains in Logical Coherence (+0.21 over vanilla at 128 budget) and Visual Perception (+0.14). This is directly applicable to customer service chatbots that reference product images, medical consultation systems that reference diagnostic images over multiple exchanges, or interactive educational tools where a student asks follow-up questions about a diagram. In these settings, the KV cache accumulates across turns — early images, early instructions, early clarifications — and as the dialogue lengthens, full-cache attention increasingly dilutes over old, potentially irrelevant context. DBTrimKV's dynamic allocation preserves the tokens that remain relevant across turns (e.g., the product image, the key constraint mentioned in turn 2) while evicting tokens whose utility has decayed (e.g., the exact phrasing of a clarification that has been superseded). The practical benefit is that the system maintains response quality over long dialogues rather than degrading as the conversation continues — a common failure mode in current deployed chatbots.

**Training data generation for self-improvement pipelines.** The paper's finding that moderate eviction can improve reasoning quality (rather than just preserve it) has implications for how LLMs are used to generate training data. In self-improvement pipelines like STaR or ReST^EM, a model generates solutions to training problems, and correct solutions are added to the training set for the next iteration. If the model uses full-cache attention during generation, its solutions may be degraded by attention dilution over long contexts (e.g., long problem statements, multi-document context, or its own long chain-of-thought). Using DBTrimKV during the generation phase could produce higher-quality training solutions, particularly for problems requiring long reasoning chains where dilution over the model's own output becomes significant. The generated solutions would then fine-tune the base model, potentially creating a virtuous cycle where eviction-improved generation trains a better base model, which in turn produces better retention scores. The paper does not explore this, but the connection to the self-improvement literature is direct and the infrastructure (training retention gates, then using them at inference) is already demonstrated.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper explicitly positions DBTrimKV against both heuristic eviction methods (SnapKV, AdaKV, R-KV) and its direct predecessor TrimKV, and the experimental results provide clear guidance on when each approach is appropriate. The decision rule emerges from the empirical patterns rather than being stated as an explicit trade-off matrix, but the conditions are clear enough to extract:

- **Prefer DBTrimKV over TrimKV when:** (a) the total KV budget is substantially below the full context length (the gap between DBTrimKV and TrimKV widens from ~1 point at 1024 budget to ~16 points at 128 budget on long-horizon reasoning; Table 2), and (b) the deployment framework supports PagedAttention with variable-length per-head caches (vLLM or equivalent). The dynamic allocation benefit is largest when capacity is scarce and head heterogeneity is high — both conditions hold in long-context, long-generation settings.

- **Prefer DBTrimKV over heuristic methods (SnapKV, AdaKV, R-KV) when:** (a) training data from the target model's output distribution is available (because learned retention gates, like the PRM in the compute-optimal scaling paper, do not transfer across model families — distribution shift degrades performance), and (b) the target tasks involve long-form generation where dilution accumulates over many decoding steps. The performance gap between DBTrimKV and heuristics is dramatic on MathVisionmini (51.64% vs. 7.89% for SnapKV at 256 budget; Table 2) and MMMUPro (41.85% vs. 6.24%), but narrower on shorter-reasoning tasks like MMStar (69.91% vs. 50.89%).

- **Prefer TrimKV (fixed per-head budgets) over DBTrimKV when:** (a) the deployment framework does not support variable-length per-head caches and adding PagedAttention infrastructure is not feasible, and (b) the context length and total budget are such that fixed allocation is not a binding constraint (as in the short-form VQA experiments; Table 1, where DBTrimKV and TrimKV perform identically). TrimKV with fixed per-head budgets requires only standard dense KV cache implementations and still substantially outperforms heuristic baselines.

- **Prefer heuristic methods (SnapKV, AdaKV) over learned methods (DBTrimKV, TrimKV) when:** (a) training is not possible (no access to the model for fine-tuning, no training data from the target distribution, or deployment constraints that prohibit modified model weights), and (b) the performance requirements are modest enough that heuristic degradation is acceptable. Heuristic methods are training-free and impose minimal implementation overhead — for applications where 80% of full-cache performance is sufficient (Table 2: SnapKV at 128 budget achieves ~35% relative average versus DBTrimKV's ~98%), the simplicity may outweigh the accuracy gap.
