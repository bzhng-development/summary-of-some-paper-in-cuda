# "Learning to Explore: Scaling Agentic Reasoning via Exploration-Aware Policy Optimization"

**ArXiv:** [2605.08978](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.08978)

## 🎯 Pitch

Current LLM agents waste time by exploring blindly, but this paper shows they can be trained to probe only when uncertain—and that doing so makes a 2B model beat 8B+ rivals on complex mobile and web tasks. The key is a reward signal that measures how much an exploratory action actually reduces the agent’s own uncertainty.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper proposes **Exploration-Aware Policy Optimization (EAPO)**, a reinforcement learning framework that teaches LLM agents to distinguish when exploration is necessary and adaptively gather environmental information only under high uncertainty, rather than engaging in undifferentiated exploration. Evaluated across four agentic benchmarks—ALFWorld, WebShop, AndroidWorld, and OSWorld—using Qwen3 and Qwen3-VL base models ranging from 1.7B to 8B parameters, EAPO introduces an exploration-and-memory reasoning mode (structured `<explore>` and `<memory>` tags that separate information-gathering from task-execution actions) and a variational reward model (a learned density-based proxy that estimates the utility of exploratory actions by evaluating their potential to improve future decision-making). The method achieves consistent improvements of 20%–60% over existing training methods, with a 2B-scale model outperforming substantially larger general and agentic models (e.g., 76.4% vs. 61.3% on AndroidWorld), while incurring only ~30% additional training overhead. The learned exploration policy generalizes directly to unseen environments, establishing that test-time exploration amplifies agent capability only when the agent has learned to selectively target informational gaps rather than exploring indiscriminately.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: LLM Agents Don't Know When to Explore

The central problem this paper addresses is deceptively simple: **current LLM agents cannot distinguish when they need to explore the environment versus when they already have enough information to act**. In complex, multi-step tasks like web navigation, mobile app control, or embodied household tasks, agents routinely encounter unfamiliar states—updated UI layouts, unmapped menu structures, novel object configurations—that deviate from their training distribution. A capable agent in these scenarios needs to recognize its own uncertainty, deliberately gather additional information through trial actions, and then commit to a decision once sufficient context has been acquired.

This is fundamentally different from the kind of exploration studied in standard reinforcement learning, where exploration serves to improve the policy *during training* by visiting diverse states. The problem here is **test-time exploration**: the agent, during deployment, must actively probe an unfamiliar environment to resolve its own uncertainty before acting. Humans do this naturally—we scroll through an unfamiliar settings menu to find the right option, we click around a new website to understand its layout, we open a cabinet door in a novel kitchen to check if it's a cupboard or a dishwasher. Current LLM agents largely lack this adaptive ability.

The gap this paper identifies (Section 1) is specific and well-motivated: existing test-time scaling methods "entangle exploration and action selection within a single policy, preventing agents from identifying where exploration is truly necessary and often resulting in indiscriminate exploration even in well-understood states." This entanglement has concrete consequences. Agents accumulate low-value information that obscures critical signals. They waste interaction steps on states they already understand. They fail to recognize when "one more look around" would resolve their confusion. In the worst case—as demonstrated in Table 1—naive exploration actually *degrades* performance compared to not exploring at all, with several baseline methods showing accuracy drops of 3–5 percentage points on AndroidWorld when exploration is incorporated.

### Why This Problem Matters

The importance of adaptive exploration spans both practical deployment and theoretical foundations of agent learning.

**Practical deployment stakes.** The paper evaluates on four environments that span the major categories of modern agentic tasks (Section 6.1):

- **ALFWorld**: text-based embodied household tasks requiring navigation and object interaction
- **WebShop**: realistic online shopping with uncertainty about product availability, attributes, and prices
- **AndroidWorld**: 116 dynamic tasks across 20 real Android apps with varying UI layouts
- **OSWorld**: 369 long-horizon tasks spanning web and desktop applications

These are not toy problems. OSWorld tasks can span 50+ interaction steps with multiple application windows. AndroidWorld tasks require navigating real app interfaces that change between versions. In such settings, an agent that explores indiscriminately can double or triple the number of steps needed—directly translating to higher latency, higher API costs, and degraded user experience. An agent that explores *too little* will make uninformed decisions and fail entirely on tasks that require understanding novel environment dynamics. The paper's approach directly addresses this efficiency-accuracy tradeoff: teach agents to explore *only when uncertainty is high*, transitioning to execution as soon as the context is clear.

**Theoretical significance.** The problem sits at the intersection of two major research threads: test-time scaling for reasoning and reinforcement learning for agent training. Test-time scaling methods (Yao et al., 2023; Snell et al., 2024) have shown that allowing models to "think more" during inference improves performance on logical reasoning tasks. However, as the paper notes (Section 2), these approaches "largely rely on static heuristics or predefined branching budgets and lack principled criteria to adaptively control when and how exploration should be conducted." The question of *when* to think more—not just *how much*—remains theoretically open.

On the RL side, the challenge is that standard policy optimization objectives encourage agents to learn reactive state-to-action mappings that maximize expected reward on training tasks. These mappings are brittle: when the agent encounters a state it hasn't seen during training, the reactive policy has no mechanism to say "I need more information here." The paper's framework reframes this as a Bayesian inference problem: exploration is a process of enriching the agent's internal memory with task-relevant information, and the utility of exploratory actions can be quantified by how much they improve the posterior probability of task success. This connects agent exploration to the well-established framework of optimal experimental design and information gain, providing theoretical grounding for what has largely been an ad-hoc engineering practice.

### Where Prior Approaches Fall Short

The paper identifies limitations across four categories of existing work (Section 2), each of which informs a specific design choice in EAPO.

**1. Inference-time search methods explore indiscriminately.** Tree-of-Thoughts (Yao et al., 2023) and related approaches (Du et al., 2023; Besta et al., 2024) allow models to branch multiple reasoning paths at test time and select among them. These methods provide a *mechanism* for exploration—the model can try several candidate actions and evaluate the results—but they lack a *criterion* for deciding when to branch. Every state gets the same treatment regardless of whether the agent is genuinely uncertain or already knows exactly what to do. The consequence is computational waste: the agent spends budget deliberating over paths it already understands, while potentially rushing past genuinely confusing states where deeper exploration would pay off.

The paper explicitly calls this out: "these approaches largely rely on static heuristics or predefined branching budgets and lack principled criteria to adaptively control when and how exploration should be conducted during multi-step reasoning." EAPO's response is to learn a reward function that estimates the *information value* of exploratory actions, enabling the agent to selectively branch only when the expected information gain exceeds a threshold.

**2. Entropy-based exploration guidance misses the mark.** Several recent works propose using entropy as a signal for exploration (Zhang et al., 2024; 2025a; Vanlioglu, 2025; Xu et al., 2025). The intuition is appealing: when the model's action distribution has high entropy (it's "uncertain"), that's a good time to explore. However, the paper identifies a fundamental mismatch (Section 2):

> "entropy does not faithfully reflect information gain, as actions with high entropy may simply indicate model uncertainty while inducing uninformative or redundant transitions, thus failing to produce meaningful exploration."

A model might be uncertain because it genuinely needs more information—or it might be uncertain because it's poorly calibrated on a state it *should* understand. Conversely, the model might be confidently wrong (low entropy on an incorrect action) when it would benefit from exploration. Entropy conflates epistemic uncertainty (what the model doesn't know about the environment) with aleatoric uncertainty (inherent randomness in outcomes), only the former of which warrants exploration. EAPO's variational reward model directly estimates the utility of exploration in terms of improved task success probability, sidestepping the entropy proxy entirely.

**3. Meta-RL approaches apply exploration uniformly.** LAMER (Jiang et al., 2025), the strongest baseline in the paper's experiments, trains LLM agents to sample multiple trajectories and adapt behavior through in-context meta-learning. This approach does enable test-time exploration: the agent generates several candidate action sequences, observes the outcomes, and reasons over them to select the best path. However, the paper observes (Section 6.2) that "its exploration is applied uniformly and tends to be conservative, as it does not explicitly learn when exploration is necessary." Every task gets the same multi-trajectory treatment regardless of whether the environment is familiar or novel.

The practical consequence appears in the results: LAMER achieves strong performance (e.g., 61.3% on AndroidWorld with a 2B model) but is consistently outperformed by EAPO (76.4% on the same benchmark). The gap is largest on complex GUI tasks (OSWorld: 33.8% vs. 50.3% for the 2B model), where knowing *when* to explore is particularly valuable because exploration is expensive (each exploratory step requires interacting with slow GUI environments).

**4. Standard RL training objectives discourage exploration.** The GRPO objective (Equation 2 in the paper) optimizes expected reward over trajectory groups by comparing the reward of each action to the mean reward in its group. This creates an inherent bias against exploration: exploratory actions typically have lower immediate rewards than task-completion actions (since they consume steps without making progress), so they receive negative advantage estimates and are suppressed during optimization. The paper demonstrates this empirically: naive incorporation of exploration into GRPO (Table 1) causes performance degradation because the agent learns to avoid actions that don't directly lead to task success, even when those actions would provide valuable information.

Prior work has attempted to address this by adding entropy bonuses or adjusting the sampling distribution during training (Zhang et al., 2024; 2025a; Xu et al., 2025). However, these solutions target exploration *during training*, not at test time. They help the policy visit diverse states while learning, but they don't teach the agent to recognize and resolve its own uncertainty during deployment. The paper draws a sharp distinction here: "such solutions primarily enhance exploration during the training phase, rather than enabling agents to perform explicit and adaptive exploration at test time when interacting with unfamiliar states" (Section 2).

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper frames its contribution through a clear conceptual architecture that distinguishes it from prior work along three axes (Section 4.1):

**Axis 1: Exploration as an explicit, structured reasoning mode.** Rather than treating exploration as an emergent property of the policy or an inference-time decoding strategy, EAPO makes exploration a first-class output of the model. The `<explore>` and `<memory>` tags create dedicated channels for the agent to deliberate about what information it needs, propose candidate exploratory actions, and synthesize acquired observations into a persistent memory. This is not merely a prompting trick—the paper argues that this separation "helps the agent explicitly distinguish between decision-making, information gathering, and state summarization, which facilitates more effective reuse of acquired information and reduces ambiguity during optimization" (Remark 4.1). By giving exploration its own output format, the system can assign exploration-specific rewards to these tokens, creating a clean credit assignment pathway that unstructured outputs cannot provide.

**Axis 2: A learned, density-based exploration reward.** The core technical innovation is the variational reward model (Sections 4.3–4.3). Instead of relying on online rollouts to estimate the value of exploratory actions (which the paper shows is both expensive and high-variance; Appendix B, Figs. 3–4), EAPO trains a distribution $q_\phi(e, m|s)$ over exploration strategies and memory states conditioned on task success. This distribution captures which exploration-memory configurations are associated with successful trajectories from state $s$. The exploratory reward (Equation 9) then scores actions by how much they move the agent toward high-probability configurations under this distribution. The derivation in Appendix A shows this corresponds to a variational lower bound on the log-probability of task success, providing theoretical grounding for the reward design.

This positions EAPO at a unique intersection: it learns *from* the training data which states are worth exploring (via the variational distribution), but it applies this knowledge *at test time* to decide when to explore novel states. The learned distribution acts as a prior over "good exploration strategies" that transfers across environments, explaining the generalization results in Table 6.

**Axis 3: Exploration-aware optimization that separates information-gathering from task-execution.** The exploration-aware grouping mechanism (Section 5.2, Equation 13) addresses the credit assignment problem head-on. By clustering transitions based on both the environment state *and* the visitation depth $\kappa(s)$—how many times the agent has visited that state—the method ensures that exploratory actions are compared only to other actions taken at similar informational stages. This prevents the systematic undervaluation of exploration that occurs when exploratory and task-execution actions are pooled together. Remark 5.1 explains the intuition: without this grouping, "directly comparing these actions within the same group underestimates the value of exploration, leading to a significant decrease in exploration degree." The paper provides empirical evidence for this claim through ablation studies (Figs. 18–19, Table 8), showing that removing exploration-aware grouping causes a rise-then-collapse pattern in both exploration degree and task performance.

**What EAPO is explicitly NOT.** The paper is careful to delineate boundaries. EAPO is not a general exploration method for RL—it specifically targets the test-time exploration problem for LLM agents operating in interactive environments with sparse binary rewards. It is not a pure inference-time method either—it requires training a variational distribution and fine-tuning the policy with the exploration-aware objective. The authors acknowledge (Section 7) that the approach relies on structured exploration and memory representations that are "manually specified throughout training," which "may restrict the expressiveness of exploration strategies and limit adaptability to tasks that require more flexible forms of information acquisition." This honesty about limitations distinguishes the contribution from overclaimed results.

The paper also explicitly avoids claiming that exploration can substitute for model scale in the way some test-time compute papers do. While the results show a 2B model outperforming much larger models (Table 4), this is attributed to the specific mechanism of adaptive exploration improving environment understanding, not to a general scaling law. The finding that models trained on AndroidWorld transfer well to OSWorld (Section D.3, Table 6) is presented as evidence for learning domain-invariant exploration strategies, not as a claim of universal generalization.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

The paper builds a **training framework that teaches language model agents to explore their environment adaptively at test time** — meaning the agent learns to recognize when it lacks sufficient information about the current state, deliberately takes trial actions to gather that information, synthesizes what it learns into a persistent memory, and then commits to a goal-directed action once the uncertainty is resolved. The framework solves the problem of **indiscriminate exploration** in existing agent training methods, where agents either explore uniformly across all states (wasting computation on well-understood situations) or fail to explore at all (making uninformed decisions in novel environments). The solution takes the shape of a **two-stage training pipeline** — first teaching the agent to roll back from exploratory actions to recover from mistakes, then optimizing the policy with a learned reward function that explicitly scores the information value of exploratory behavior — combined with a **structured output format** that separates exploration reasoning, memory accumulation, and task execution into distinct, rewardable channels.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The EAPO system has five major components that interact through a training pipeline with distinct stages:

1. **Structured Output Template** — a predefined format using `<explore>`, `<memory>`, and `<action>` tags that separates the agent's reasoning into information-gathering, state summarization, and task-execution channels. This template is applied during both training and inference.

2. **Variational Reward Model** — a learned distribution `$q_\phi(e, m|s)$` over exploration strategies `$e$` and memory states `$m$` conditioned on the current state `$s$`. This model is trained to match the posterior distribution of exploration-memory configurations that lead to task success, and it provides the exploration reward signal during policy optimization.

3. **Rollback SFT Stage** — a supervised fine-tuning phase where the agent is trained on expert demonstrations of navigation actions that return to previously visited states. This teaches the agent that exploration is reversible, enabling it to treat trial actions as recoverable probes rather than terminal risks.

4. **Exploration-Aware GRPO** — a modified Group Relative Policy Optimization procedure that clusters transitions by both the environment state `$s$` and the visitation depth `$\kappa(s)$` (how many times that state has been visited during exploration), ensuring exploratory actions receive fair credit relative to other actions taken at similar informational stages.

5. **Total Reward Function** — a weighted combination of three signals: the task success reward `$R_{\text{task}}$` (standard sparse binary completion signal), the format reward `$R_{\text{format}}$` (binary signal for correctly following the structured output template), and the exploratory reward `$R_{\text{explore}}$` (density-based score from the variational model estimating the utility of information-gathering actions).

Information flows through the system as follows: a task prompt and initial state enter the agent → the agent's policy `$\pi_\theta$` generates structured output containing exploration guidance, memory updates, and an executable action → the action is executed in the environment, producing a next state → the variational reward model scores the transition by evaluating whether the exploration-memory configuration matches successful trajectory distributions → the exploration-aware grouping mechanism clusters this transition with others taken at the same visitation depth → the total reward is computed → the GRPO objective updates the policy, and the variational objective updates the reward model → the process repeats across training episodes until convergence.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **systems paper with theoretical grounding** whose core idea is that agent exploration can be reframed as Bayesian inference over memory states, and that separating exploratory reasoning from task execution in both the output format and the optimization procedure enables agents to learn when exploration is genuinely worthwhile.

---

#### Structured Exploration and Memory Mode

The paper introduces a structured output format that makes exploration an explicit, first-class reasoning channel within the agent's generation. The key insight is that giving exploration its own dedicated output space solves a fundamental credit assignment problem: when the agent generates a single stream of tokens mixing reasoning about what to explore, what it has learned from past exploration, and what action to take, it is impossible to assign different rewards to the exploratory versus task-execution components of the output. The `<explore>` and `<memory>` tags create separate token spans that can receive separate optimization signals.

**What the agent receives at each step.** At execution step `$t$`, the agent's input `$\tilde{s}_t$` is the concatenation of four components:

$$\tilde{s}_t \triangleq [g; s_t; e_{t-1}; m_{t-1}]$$

where `$g$` is the task description (a fixed string specifying the goal), `$s_t$` is the current environment observation (textual state description or GUI screenshot), `$e_{t-1}$` is the exploration strategy from the *previous* step (specifying what information the agent planned to acquire next and what candidate actions it intended to try), and `$m_{t-1}$` is the accumulated memory from the *previous* step (a structured summary of task-relevant information extracted from all past interactions).

**What the agent generates at each step.** The agent's output `$\tilde{a}_t$` concatenates three components into a predefined structure:

$$\tilde{a}_t \triangleq [e_t; m_t; a_t]$$

where `$e_t$` is the current exploration guidance (a structured description of what information the agent still needs, what actions it should try to get that information, and what uncertainty it aims to resolve), `$m_t$` is the updated memory (incorporating the newly observed state `$s_{t+1}$` into the running summary of discovered environment facts), and `$a_t$` is the executable action (a parseable command like `{“action_type”: “click”, “index”: 5}`).

**Semantic content is unconstrained; only the format is prescribed.** Remark 4.1 emphasizes that the structured format does not constrain *what* the agent writes in each field — it only separates the fields structurally. The exploration guidance could contain natural language reasoning about uncertainty, explicit lists of candidate actions to try, or descriptions of hypotheses about the environment that need testing. The memory could contain free-text summaries, structured key-value records of discovered facts, or descriptions of previously visited states. This is not a rigid template restricting the agent's expressiveness — it is a scaffolding that makes exploratory reasoning visible and rewardable.

**Why this separation matters for credit assignment.** The paper explicitly states (Remark 4.1): "the format enables reliable credit assignment for exploration-related rewards, which would otherwise be difficult to achieve with unstructured outputs." In standard unstructured generation, the same token sequence mixing "I should try clicking the settings button to see where it leads" (exploration reasoning) and `{“action”: “click”, “index”: 3}` (task execution) would receive identical treatment under the policy gradient. The structured format allows the exploration reward (which evaluates whether the exploration strategy moved the agent toward useful information) to be applied specifically to the tokens within `<explore>` and `<memory>` tags, while the task reward applies to the entire output. This separation is what enables the variational reward model to provide a meaningful training signal — if exploration reasoning were mixed indistinguishably with action tokens, the density-based reward from `$q_\phi$` could not be correctly attributed.

**The instruction template in practice.** Figures 5, 6, and 7 in the paper define the concrete prompts. The system prompt (Fig. 5) specifies the high-level cognitive process: "During thinking, you can explore the current page by executing possible actions and memorize the pages you have explored." It then provides a template with the `<explore>`, `<memory>`, and `<action>` tags and specifies that "the final exact action must be enclosed within `\boxed{}`." The basic prompt (Fig. 6) defines the agent's role (operating an Android phone on behalf of a user), the available action space (click, long press, input text, scroll, navigate, open app, wait, status), and termination conditions. The action guidance (Fig. 7) provides detailed semantics for each action and usage examples with concrete JSON formatting. All three prompts are concatenated as the input context, with the system prompt going to the system-level instruction slot and the remaining prompts combined with task-specific observations into the user-level input.

#### Variational Reward Model — Bayesian Foundation

The core theoretical contribution is treating exploration utility as a Bayesian inference problem. The paper formalizes the intuition that *learning to explore is fundamentally the process of training the agent to correctly enrich its memory by proactively acquiring useful task-relevant information*. This framing converts the qualitative goal of "explore when uncertain" into a quantitative optimization problem: maximize the posterior probability that a given exploration-memory configuration leads to task success.

**The posterior over exploration-memory configurations.** Let `$p(e_{t-1}, m_{t-1}|s_t, \text{success})$` denote the true posterior distribution over exploration strategies and memory states, conditioned on both the current state `$s_t$` and the fact that the containing trajectory achieves task success. This is an intractable distribution — we cannot compute it analytically because it requires integrating over all possible future trajectories from `$s_t$` to determine which exploration-memory configurations actually contribute to success. However, its conceptual meaning is clear: configurations `$(e, m)$` that genuinely help the agent navigate from `$s_t$` to task completion receive high probability mass; configurations that are irrelevant or misleading receive low mass.

**The variational approximation.** The paper trains a learned proxy `$q_\phi(e, m|s)$` parameterized by `$\phi$` to approximate this true posterior. This is implemented as a neural network (using the same architecture as the policy model) that takes a state `$s$` as input and outputs a distribution over `$(e, m)$` pairs — specifically, a scalar density value for each pair indicating how likely that configuration is to be associated with successful trajectories from `$s$`.

**The KL-divergence minimization objective.** The proxy is trained to minimize:

$$\min_{q_\phi} \text{KL}\left(q_\phi(e, m|s) \,\|\, p(e, m|s, \text{success})\right)$$

where `$\text{KL}(\cdot\|\cdot)$` is the Kullback-Leibler divergence between the learned distribution `$q_\phi$` and the true posterior `$p$`.

**What this objective computes.** The KL divergence measures how much information is lost when using `$q_\phi$` to approximate `$p$`. Minimizing this quantity pushes the learned distribution to concentrate probability mass on the same `$(e, m)$` configurations that the true posterior would favor — that is, configurations associated with task success. The optimization penalizes both overconfidence (placing high mass on configurations that rarely lead to success) and underconfidence (spreading mass too uniformly rather than concentrating on genuinely useful configurations).

**Why this form — the variational lower bound derivation.** The paper provides the full derivation in Appendix A, building on standard variational inference techniques. The key transformation expands the KL divergence:

$$\text{KL}(q_\phi\|p) = \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi}[\log q_\phi(e,m|s) - \log p(e,m|s,\text{success})]$$

Applying Bayes' rule to the second term yields:

$$\log p(e,m|s,\text{success}) = \log p(\text{success}|s,e,m) + \log p(e,m|s) - \log p(\text{success}|s)$$

Substituting and reorganizing produces:

$$\text{KL}(q_\phi\|p) = -\mathbb{E}_{q_\phi}[\log p(\text{success}|s,e,m)] + \text{KL}(q_\phi(e,m|s)\,\|\,p(e,m|s)) + \log p(\text{success}|s)$$

Since `$\log p(\text{success}|s)$` does not depend on `$q_\phi$`, minimizing the original KL is equivalent to maximizing:

$$\max_{q_\phi} \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi}[\log p(\text{success}|s,e,m)] - \text{KL}(q_\phi(e,m|s)\,\|\,p(e,m|s))$$

**The ELBO connection.** Equation 18 in Appendix A shows that this objective is a variational lower bound (ELBO) on the log-probability that the agent policy `$\pi_\theta$` generates a successful trajectory from state `$s$`. Specifically:

$$\log \pi_\theta(\text{success}|s) \geq \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi}[\log \pi_\theta(\text{success}|s,m,e)] - \text{KL}(q_\phi(m,e|s)\,\|\,\pi_\theta(m,e|s))$$

This is significant because it means that optimizing the variational distribution to minimize KL divergence with the true posterior is also optimizing a lower bound on task success rate. Maximizing this lower bound directly promotes task completion — the learned exploration reward is not merely a heuristic but is grounded in the fundamental objective of improving task performance.

**Converting the expected log-probability to expected cumulative reward.** The paper further shows (Equation 24–25) that `$\log p(\text{success}|s,e,m)$` can be expressed as a log-expectation over trajectories and bounded below by the expected cumulative reward using Jensen's inequality:

$$\log p(\text{success}|s,e,m) = \beta \log \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi(\cdot|s,e,m)}\left[\exp\left(\sum_t r_t\right)\right] \geq \beta \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi(\cdot|s,e,m)}\left[\sum_t r_t\right]$$

where `$\beta$` is a temperature hyperparameter and `$\mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi(\cdot|s,e,m)}[\sum_t r_t]$` is the expected cumulative reward `$Q(s,e,m)$` of trajectories starting from state `$s$` with exploration strategy `$e$` and memory `$m$`, following policy `$\pi_\theta$`. This transformation replaces the intractable `$\log p(\text{success}|s,e,m)$` with the computable `$Q(s,e,m)$` — the cumulative reward of the policy operating under the given exploration-memory configuration.

**The final variational objective.** Substituting into the maximization yields the practical training objective for the reward model:

$$\max_{q_\phi} \beta \mathbb{E}_{e,m \sim q_\phi(\cdot|s)}[Q(s,e,m)] - \text{KL}(q_\phi(e,m|s)\,\|\,p(e,m|s))$$

**Why this form.** The first term `$\beta\mathbb{E}_{q_\phi}[Q(s,e,m)]$` encourages the learned distribution to concentrate on exploration-memory configurations that yield high cumulative reward — configurations where exploration provides genuine informational value that translates to task progress. The second term `$-\text{KL}(q_\phi\|p)$` acts as a regularizer preventing the learned distribution from collapsing to a point estimate (which would overfit to training task specifics and fail to generalize) or deviating from the prior `$p(e,m|s)$` (which encodes what exploration-memory configurations are plausible a priori). Together, they create a principled tradeoff: the model learns to favor configurations that empirically lead to task success while remaining close to a reasonable prior, enabling generalization to unseen states.

**Optimization method.** Equation 8 is optimized using REINFORCE (Williams, 1992): `$q_\phi$` is treated as a policy that selects `$(e,m)$` conditioned on `$s$`, and the objective is optimized via policy gradient. The KL term serves as a functional constraint during optimization rather than being optimized directly — it prevents the learned policy from deviating too far from the prior. The hyperparameter `$\beta$` controls the strength of the reward signal relative to the KL regularization term.

#### Exploratory Reward Computation at Inference Time

Once the variational proxy `$q_\phi$` is trained, it provides a density-based score for any exploration-memory configuration. The exploratory reward for a transition `$(\tilde{s}_t, \tilde{a}_t, \tilde{s}_{t+1})$` is computed as:

$$R_{\text{explore}}(\tilde{s}_t, \tilde{a}_t, \tilde{s}_{t+1}) \triangleq \max\left\{q_\phi(e_{t-1}, m_{t-1}|s_t),\; \gamma^2 q_\phi(e_{t-1}, [m_{t-1}, s_{t+1}]|s_t)\right\}$$

where `$\tilde{s}_t = [g; s_t; e_{t-1}; m_{t-1}]$` is the full input state (task + environment + previous exploration + previous memory), `$\tilde{a}_t = [e_t; m_t; a_t]$` is the full output (current exploration + updated memory + action), `$q_\phi(e_{t-1}, m_{t-1}|s_t)$` is the variational model's density estimate for the *previous* exploration-memory configuration given the current state, `$[m_{t-1}, s_{t+1}]$` is the concatenation of old memory with the newly observed state (representing the enriched memory after exploration), `$\gamma \in [0, 1]$` is a discount factor (set to 0.9 in experiments; Table 3), and `$\gamma^2$` is the squared discount applied to the exploratory gain term.

**What this equation computes.** It evaluates the utility of the current transition by comparing two quantities and taking the maximum. The first term `$q_\phi(e_{t-1}, m_{t-1}|s_t)$` estimates the value of immediate exploitation — using the existing memory (without incorporating the newly observed state `$s_{t+1}$`) to solve the task from `$s_t$`. A high value means the agent already has sufficient information to act effectively. The second term `$\gamma^2 q_\phi(e_{t-1}, [m_{t-1}, s_{t+1}]|s_t)$` estimates the value of delayed exploitation after exploration — enriching the memory with the newly observed state and then using that richer memory to solve the task. The `$\gamma^2$` discount accounts for the two-step latency cost of exploration: one step to take the exploratory action and observe `$s_{t+1}$`, and a subsequent step to synthesize the new information into an improved action.

**Why the max operation.** The max selects the better of two strategies at each state: solve now with current knowledge, or explore and solve later with enriched knowledge. If the current memory is already sufficient for task completion (high first term), the reward is high regardless of whether exploration occurred — this prevents penalizing the agent for skipping unnecessary exploration. If the current memory is insufficient but exploration produces information that substantially improves the posterior probability of success (high second term), the exploratory transition receives credit. If neither term is high (the agent is lost and exploration doesn't help), the reward is low, discouraging aimless wandering.

**Why the discount `$\gamma^2$` is essential.** Remark 4.3 explains: "Once the agent has already acquired sufficient information to make a correct decision at the current state, continued exploration solely for obtaining more comprehensive memories is redundant and can be detrimental to efficiency." Without the discount, the second term might exceed the first even when the informational gain from exploration is marginal, encouraging the agent to explore *every* state — the very indiscriminate exploration behavior EAPO is designed to prevent. The `$\gamma^2$` penalty ensures exploration is only rewarded when the expected utility gain exceeds the cost of spending two additional interaction steps. With `$\gamma = 0.9$`, the exploratory gain is discounted to `$0.81 \times q_\phi$`, meaning the exploration must improve the success probability estimate by at least ~23% to compensate for the step cost. The paper's parameter sweep (Section D.4, Fig. 10) shows that `$\gamma = 0.9$` provides the best tradeoff, with lower values (0.5–0.8) causing the optimization to degenerate into conventional goal-oriented GRPO (exploration is too strongly discounted to be rewarded) and `$\gamma = 1.0$` causing over-exploration with significantly longer trajectories (Table 7: average steps increase from 22.5 at `$\gamma=0.9$` to 43.9 at `$\gamma=1.0$` on ALFWorld).

**Alternative online reward and why it's worse.** Appendix B describes an alternative reward function that estimates exploration utility through online rollouts. This online reward computes `$R_1$` (the return from directly committing to the current action without exploration) and `$R_2$` (the return when the agent is allowed to roll back after observing `$s_{t+1}$` and take a refined action informed by the new information), then takes the maximum. The paper demonstrates (Figs. 3–4) that this online approach systematically underestimates exploration value because "current policy may struggle to accurately capture the long-term information gain of exploratory actions." As the number of online samples increases from 1 to 10, performance improves monotonically, confirming that more accurate estimation provides stronger supervision. However, EAPO's learned variational reward achieves comparable performance to the online reward with 10 samples, while avoiding entirely the computational and interaction cost of those samples. This is the practical upshot of replacing Monte Carlo estimation with a learned density function.

#### Total Reward Function

The complete reward signal for training the policy is the weighted sum of three components:

$$R(\tilde{s}_t, \tilde{a}_t, \tilde{s}_{t+1}) \triangleq R_{\text{task}}(\tilde{s}_t, \tilde{a}_t, \tilde{s}_{t+1}) + \alpha_1 R_{\text{format}}(\tilde{s}_t, \tilde{a}_t, \tilde{s}_{t+1}) + \alpha_2 R_{\text{explore}}(\tilde{s}_t, \tilde{a}_t, \tilde{s}_{t+1})$$

where `$R_{\text{task}}$` is the standard episodic binary reward (1 if the trajectory achieves task completion, 0 otherwise), `$R_{\text{format}}$` is a binary reward indicating whether the output correctly follows the structured template (correct tags, `\boxed{}` action formatting, parsable JSON), `$R_{\text{explore}}$` is the exploratory reward from Equation 9, `$\alpha_1$` is the format reward weight (set to 0.5; Table 3), and `$\alpha_2$` is the exploratory reward weight (set to 1; Table 3).

**Why `$\alpha_1 = 0.5$`.** The format reward is intentionally weighted lower than the exploratory and task rewards. The paper shows (Fig. 15) that the agent learns the format in "less than [a few] rounds," meaning the format reward quickly saturates. A weight of 0.5 ensures the format signal guides early training without dominating the optimization or shifting learning toward producing parseable but unhelpful exploration over meaningful exploration.

**Why `$\alpha_2 = 1$`.** The exploratory reward is weighted equally with the task reward, reflecting that both are equally important signals for learning adaptive exploration. Too low a weight would cause the optimization to ignore exploration utility, degenerating into standard goal-oriented GRPO. The paper does not sweep `$\alpha_2$` values, suggesting that `$\alpha_2 = 1$` was chosen as a natural default and confirmed effective through the ablation study (Table 8), where removing the exploratory reward causes performance drops of 15–28 percentage points across environments.

#### Learning to Rollback — SFT Stage

Before the agent can benefit from exploration-aware RL, it needs a basic capability: the ability to return to a previous state after taking an exploratory action. Without this, exploration is terminal — once the agent clicks an unfamiliar button and lands on an unexpected page, it is stuck there, and the downstream consequences of that exploratory action are irreversible. The paper calls this "rollback capability" and addresses it through a supervised fine-tuning warmup phase.

**The fundamental problem without rollback.** Section 5.1 states: "Without the rollback capability, the reward assigned to an exploratory action is entangled with its irreversible downstream consequences, preventing the agent from correctly attributing future success to the information gained through exploration." Suppose the agent explores a new menu, finds useful information (e.g., the location of a target setting), but the exploratory click also takes it to a dead-end page from which it cannot navigate back to the task flow. The task will fail despite the exploration providing valuable information. The agent learns that the exploratory action was bad (since the trajectory failed), even though the *information* it gained was valuable. This incorrect credit assignment systematically undervalues exploration.

**The SFT dataset construction.** The paper collects expert rollback demonstrations `$D$` by prompting a teacher LLM. Specifically, for a given state `$s'$` (the state after an exploratory action) and its previous state `$s$`, the teacher LLM generates an action `$a$` that would return the agent from `$s'$` back to `$s$`. If executing `$a$` in `$s'$` successfully recovers `$s$`, the transition `$(s', s, a)$` is added to the dataset. This produces a collection of (current-state, target-previous-state, rollback-action) triples.

**The SFT loss.** The agent is trained with a standard next-token prediction objective on these rollback demonstrations:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{SFT}}(\theta) = -\frac{1}{|D|} \sum_{(s', s, a) \in \mathcal{D}} \log p_\theta(a|s', s, x)$$

where `$\mathcal{D}$` is the collected rollback dataset, `$(s', s, a)$` are individual transitions (current state `$s'$`, previous state `$s$`, rollback action `$a$`), `$\theta$` are the policy parameters, and `$x$` is a fixed rollback instruction prompt concatenated with the state pair `$(s', s)$`.

**What this loss computes.** It is standard language model cross-entropy loss: the agent sees a prompt that describes the rollback instruction and the two states, and it must generate the correct rollback action. The loss penalizes the model when it assigns low probability to the correct rollback action tokens. Minimizing this loss over the dataset teaches the agent a generalizable skill: given a current state and a description of where it wants to return, produce the right navigation action.

**Why SFT rather than RL for rollback.** The paper does not explicitly justify this choice, but the rationale is implicit. Rollback actions are deterministic and well-defined (e.g., click the back button, navigate home, click a specific menu item) — there is a correct answer for each state pair, not a distribution over acceptable actions. SFT is the natural choice for learning such deterministic mappings. Additionally, training rollback via RL would be circular: the agent needs rollback capability *before* RL can properly credit exploration, so training rollback during RL would inherit the same credit assignment problem. The SFT phase bootstraps the rollback skill using teacher demonstrations, providing a clean foundation for the subsequent RL phase.

**The ablative importance of SFT.** Table 8 shows that removing SFT causes noticeable but not catastrophic performance drops: for example, on AndroidWorld with the 2B model, EAPO achieves 76.4% while the "w/o exploratory" ablation (which includes SFT but removes the exploration reward) achieves only 51.0%. The paper does not report an explicit "w/o SFT" ablation, but notes (Section D.7.6) that "exploration accuracy and rollback capability are inherently coupled. Even if the agent learns to explore effectively, the acquired information cannot be properly utilized without the ability to rollback to previous decision points." The SFT stage is presented as an enabling component rather than the source of EAPO's gains — necessary for the pipeline to work but not the primary contributor to final performance.

#### Exploration-Aware Policy Optimization — GRPO Modifications

After the rollback SFT stage, the agent enters the main RL phase using a modified version of Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) that addresses the fundamental credit assignment problem of mixing exploratory and task-execution actions.

**The standard GRPO objective (reference).** The paper defines standard GRPO in Section 3 as:

$$J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{g \sim \mu, a_{i=1:G} \sim \pi_{\text{old}}(\cdot|g)}\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G \frac{1}{|a_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|a_i|} \min\left(w_{i,t} \tilde{A}_{i,t},\; \text{clip}(w_{i,t}, 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon) \tilde{A}_{i,t}\right) - \lambda \text{KL}(\pi_\theta\|\pi_{\text{ref}})\right]$$

where `$G$` is the number of trajectories per group (set to 16; Table 3), `$w_{i,t}$` is the importance weight of token `$a_{i,t}$` (ratio of current policy probability to old policy probability), `$\tilde{A}_{i,t}$` is the advantage of that token (normalized reward-to-go), `$\epsilon$` is the clipping parameter, and `$\lambda$` is the KL penalty coefficient.

**The problem with standard GRPO for exploration.** In standard GRPO, trajectories are grouped by task description `$g$`, and advantages are computed within each group by comparing each trajectory's reward to the group mean and standard deviation (Equation 4). For agentic tasks with exploration, this creates a systematic bias: exploratory actions consume steps without making direct task progress, so they receive lower cumulative rewards than actions that immediately advance the task. When these lower-reward exploratory trajectories are pooled with higher-reward direct-execution trajectories in the same group, the exploratory actions receive negative advantages and are suppressed during optimization. The agent learns that exploration is *bad* even when it is genuinely valuable for long-term success.

**Visitation depth as a clustering criterion.** To fix this, EAPO introduces the concept of **visitation depth**, denoted `$\kappa(s_t^i)$`, defined as the number of times the agent has previously visited the same state `$s_t^i$` during the current trajectory:

$$\kappa(s_t^i) \triangleq \sum_{k < t} \mathbb{I}[s_k^i = s_t^i]$$

where `$i$` indexes the trajectory within the group, `$t$` is the current step number, `$s_k^i$` are previous states in the same trajectory, and `$\mathbb{I}[\cdot]$` is the indicator function (1 if the states match, 0 otherwise). On the first visit to a state, `$\kappa(s) = 0$`. If the agent explores and then returns to the same state, `$\kappa(s) = 1$` on the second visit. If it explores again and returns again, `$\kappa(s) = 2$`, and so on.

**Transition clustering by state and visitation depth.** Instead of grouping all trajectories by task description alone, EAPO clusters individual *transitions* into localized groups based on both the environment state and the visitation depth:

$$G(s, \nu) \triangleq \left\{(\tilde{s}_t^i, \tilde{a}_t^i, \tilde{s}_{t+1}^i)\;\middle|\; s_t^i = s,\; \kappa(s_t^i) = \nu,\; 1 \leq i \leq G,\; 1 \leq t \leq T\right\}$$

where `$G(s, \nu)$` is the set of all transitions `$(\tilde{s}_t, \tilde{a}_t, \tilde{s}_{t+1})$` from any trajectory `$i$` in the batch where the environment state equals `$s$` (e.g., "the settings page of the Calendar app") and the visitation depth equals `$\nu$` (e.g., the first time the agent visits the settings page, distinct from the second time it visits after exploring elsewhere and returning).

**What this clustering accomplishes.** Transitions are now compared only to other transitions taken at the same state *and* the same stage of exploration. A first-visit exploratory action is compared to other first-visit actions at that state, not to final execution actions taken at that state after exploration is complete. This prevents the systematic undervaluation that occurs in standard GRPO: exploratory actions at `$\kappa(s)=0$` will have similar (lower) rewards to other exploratory actions at `$\kappa(s)=0$`, so their relative advantage reflects whether this specific exploratory action was better or worse than *other* exploratory actions at that state, not whether it was worse than skipping exploration entirely.

**Advantage computation within clusters.** Within each cluster `$G(s, \nu)$`, the advantage for the `$j$`-th token of action `$\tilde{a}_t^i$` is computed as:

$$\tilde{A}_{i,j} = \frac{R(\tilde{s}_t^i, \tilde{a}_t^i, \tilde{s}_{t+1}^i) - \text{mean}\left(\{R(\tilde{s}_t^i, \tilde{a}_t^i, \tilde{s}_{t+1}^i)\}_{i \in G}\right)}{\text{std}\left(\{R(\tilde{s}_t^i, \tilde{a}_t^i, \tilde{s}_{t+1}^i)\}_{i \in G}\right)}$$

where `$R(\cdot)$` is the total reward from Equation 10, and the mean and standard deviation are computed over all transitions in the cluster `$G(s, \nu)$`.

**What this advantage computes.** It is the standard GRPO normalization applied within the cluster rather than globally. For each transition in the cluster, subtract the cluster's average reward and divide by the cluster's standard deviation. Transitions with above-average rewards (for their state-and-visitation-depth context) receive positive advantages (reinforced); transitions with below-average rewards receive negative advantages (suppressed). Crucially, "above-average" is relative to other actions taken *at the same informational stage*, not relative to later actions taken after exploration is complete. An exploratory action that gathers genuinely useful information will have a higher total reward than an exploratory action that gathers redundant or misleading information, and thus will receive positive advantage even though both have lower absolute rewards than the final task-completion actions that occur later.

**Why this form — the practical significance of Remark 5.1.** The paper states: "Clustering transitions with the visitation depth is crucial for accurate advantage estimation in exploration-aware agentic training. The return of exploratory actions is inherently lower than that of the final action executed after exploration due to discounting. Without distinguishing their exploration stages, directly comparing these actions within the same group underestimates the value of exploration." Figure 19 provides empirical backing: when exploration-aware grouping is ablated, the exploration degree (fraction of states revisited multiple times) initially rises as the agent begins to explore, but then *collapses* as training progresses because exploratory actions are increasingly undervalued and the policy learns to suppress them. By the end of training, the exploration degree under the ablated grouping is near zero — the agent has learned that exploration is always bad. With proper grouping, exploration degree rises to a moderate level and then stabilizes, indicating the agent has learned to explore selectively.

**The exploration degree trajectory.** Figure 17 shows the exploration degree curves across all four environments. The pattern is consistent: exploration degree increases during the first 200–400 training steps (the agent learns that exploration is valuable and begins exploring more), peaks at 20–40% (depending on the environment), and then gradually converges to a stable level (the agent learns to explore only when necessary, not indiscriminately). For example, on ALFWorld, exploration degree rises to approximately 25% at step 200 and stabilizes around 22% after step 600. On WebShop, it rises to approximately 40% at step 300 and stabilizes around 35%. This convergence without collapse is the behavioral signature of successful exploration-aware training: the agent maintains a consistent exploration tendency without being driven to explore all the time (over-exploration) or never explore (under-exploration).

**Group size dynamics during training.** Figure 16 shows how the distribution of step-level group sizes evolves. At iteration 250 (early training), the majority of groups have size 1 (transitions never share the same state with other transitions) because the agent rarely revisits states. By iteration 500, the proportion of larger groups (sizes 3–10+) increases substantially, reflecting that the agent is now exploring more and revisiting states. By iterations 750–1000, the distribution stabilizes: about 16–22% of groups have size 1, with the remaining 78–84% distributed across larger sizes. This stabilization indicates the agent has settled into a consistent exploration pattern rather than cycling between over-exploration and under-exploration.

**Hyperparameters of the GRPO procedure (Table 3).** The RL phase uses 1000 training epochs, a sampling group size `$G = 16$` (16 trajectories per task for advantage estimation), a KL loss coefficient `$\lambda = 0.01$`, a policy learning rate of `$1 \times 10^{-4}$`, and a reward model learning rate of `$1 \times 10^{-4}$`. The sampling temperature is set to 1, and the maximum generation length is 4096 tokens. Training is implemented in PyTorch 2.8.0 using the verl framework (Sheng et al., 2024) and runs on 8 NVIDIA H800 GPUs.

**Parameter sensitivity results.** The paper sweeps three key hyperparameters across all environments (Section D.4–D.6):

- **Discount factor `$\gamma$` (0.5 to 1.0):** Performance is unimodal, peaking at `$\gamma = 0.9$`. Lower values (0.5–0.8) cause the optimization to degenerate into conventional GRPO because exploration rewards are too strongly discounted to influence behavior. `$\gamma = 1.0$` causes over-exploration with significantly longer trajectories (average steps increase from ~22 to ~44 on ALFWorld), and these redundant explorations introduce noise that degrades final decision quality.

- **Sampling group size `$G$` (4 to 32):** Performance increases from `$G = 4$` to `$G = 16$` as larger groups provide more reliable advantage estimates. Beyond `$G = 16$`, gains saturate — the additional computational overhead of sampling 24 or 32 trajectories per task does not translate to proportional accuracy improvements.

- **KL coefficient `$\lambda$` (0.005 to 1.0):** Performance follows an inverted-U pattern. Very small `$\lambda$` (0.005) provides too little regularization, allowing the policy to deviate aggressively from the reference model and causing unstable updates. Moderate `$\lambda$` (0.01–0.05) stabilizes training while preserving enough freedom for the policy to learn exploration. Large `$\lambda$` (0.5–1.0) over-constrains the policy, preventing it from learning new exploration behaviors because it is too tightly anchored to the reference model.

#### Full Training Algorithm

**Two-stage structure.** EAPO proceeds in two sequential stages:

**Stage 1 — Rollback SFT (Section 5.1, Lines 1–4 of Algorithm 1).** The agent is initialized from a pretrained Qwen3 or Qwen3-VL base model. The rollback dataset `$\mathcal{D}$` is collected by prompting a teacher LLM to generate rollback actions for state pairs `$(s', s)$`. The policy network `$\pi_\theta$` is fine-tuned on this dataset using the SFT loss (Equation 11) for a fixed number of steps. The paper does not specify the exact number of SFT steps, but given that format learning is described as taking "less than [a few] rounds" (Fig. 15), the SFT phase likely involves a relatively modest number of gradient updates sufficient for the agent to acquire basic rollback capability without overfitting. The variational reward model `$q_\phi$` is also initialized at this stage (from the same pretrained weights as the policy).

**Stage 2 — Exploration-Aware RL (Lines 5–13 of Algorithm 1).** The main training loop iterates over `$N = 1000$` RL epochs. In each epoch:

1. **Trajectory sampling (Lines 6–8):** For `$i = 1$` to `$G$` (16 trajectories), sample a trajectory `$\tau_i$` using the current policy `$\pi_{\text{old}}$` and the structured output format of Equation 5. Each trajectory consists of observations `$\tilde{s}_t$`, structured outputs `$\tilde{a}_t$`, and next observations `$\tilde{s}_{t+1}$` for `$t = 1, \ldots, T$`.

2. **Transition clustering (Line 9):** Group all transitions from the 16 trajectories into clusters `$G(s, \nu)$` using Equation 13, keyed by environment state `$s$` and visitation depth `$\kappa(s)$`.

3. **Reward model update (Line 10):** Optimize the variational distribution `$q_\phi$` by maximizing the objective from Equation 8 (REINFORCE with KL regularization). This updates `$\phi$` to better align the learned distribution with the empirical distribution of successful exploration-memory configurations observed in the sampled trajectories.

4. **Reward computation (Lines 11–12):** For each transition, compute `$R_{\text{explore}}$` using the updated reward model and Equation 9, combine it with `$R_{\text{task}}$` and `$R_{\text{format}}$` using Equation 10, and compute per-token advantages `$\tilde{A}_{i,j}$` within each cluster using Equation 14.

5. **Policy update (Line 12):** Update the policy parameters `$\theta$` using the standard GRPO objective (Equation 2), but with advantages computed from the clustered groups rather than globally. The policy `$\pi_{\text{old}}$` is frozen during this epoch's updates and replaced with the new `$\pi_\theta$` for the next epoch.

**Convergence behavior.** Figures 13–14 show that both the reward model loss and the policy loss converge reliably across all four environments, typically stabilizing within 600–800 training steps. The exploration degree (Fig. 17) converges on a similar timescale: initial increase as the agent learns to explore, peak around steps 200–400, and stabilization by steps 600–800. Final task success rate (Fig. 2, 9) shows consistent improvement without the collapse patterns observed in ablated versions of the method.

**Runtime overhead.** Section D.7.7 breaks down the computational cost of each component. The additional overhead of EAPO compared to standard GRPO comes from three sources: (1) training the reward model (takes approximately the same time as one policy update), (2) grouping transitions by state and visitation depth (accounting for approximately 15–20% of per-step time), and (3) computing exploration rewards for advantage estimation (similar to the grouping cost). The figures in Section D.7.7 show that for an 8B model on AndroidWorld, EAPO takes approximately 1636 seconds per training step versus roughly 1332 seconds for standard GRPO — about 23% overhead, consistent with the paper's claim of "less than a 15% increase" when amortized over the faster convergence (fewer total steps needed to reach peak performance). The breakdown (Fig. 21) shows that rollout sampling, old-probability computation, and reference-probability computation dominate the runtime and are unchanged from baselines; the additional grouping, reward update, and advantage computation steps account for the modest overhead.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Reframing Agent Exploration as Bayesian Inference Over Memory States, Not as Action Diversity

The paper's most fundamental conceptual move is to redefine what "exploration" means for LLM agents. Prior work treats exploration either as an action-selection strategy (sample from the tail of the distribution, inject entropy into the policy, branch multiple trajectories) or as a training-phase behavior (visit diverse states during optimization to prevent premature convergence). EAPO reframes exploration as **a process of enriching the agent's internal memory with task-relevant information**, and the utility of exploratory actions is quantified by how much they improve the posterior probability of task success given that enriched memory.

This is a genuinely different lens. The standard RL framing asks: "Which action should the agent take to maximize expected reward?" Exploration, in that framing, is a *means* to improve the policy's estimate of which actions are good — it's about resolving uncertainty in the value function. EAPO asks a different question: "What information does the agent need to acquire so that it can make a good decision, and which exploratory actions will provide that information?" The target is not a better value estimate but a **better memory state** — a more complete understanding of the environment's dynamics and layout that enables correct action selection.

This shift has profound implications that distinguish EAPO from prior attempts at adaptive exploration. Entropy-based methods (Zhang et al., 2024, 2025a; Vanlioglu, 2025) use the policy's output distribution entropy as a proxy for uncertainty, but entropy measures the model's *current indecision*, not the *information value* of a candidate exploratory action. A state where the model assigns 50% probability to each of two actions might have high entropy, but if both actions have identical consequences, exploring provides no useful information — the entropy signals model confusion, not environmental uncertainty that exploration can resolve. EAPO's variational reward model directly estimates the density `q_phi(e, m|s)` of exploration-memory configurations under the posterior of task success. This density captures something entropy cannot: the degree to which a particular exploratory action (producing memory `m`) is associated with trajectories that actually *succeed*, not just trajectories where the model was uncertain. The distinction is critical — uncertainty is a property of the model; information value is a property of the environment-action pairing.

The Bayesian framing also provides theoretical grounding that prior heuristic exploration methods lack. Appendix A connects the variational objective to an ELBO on the log-probability of task success: optimizing the reward model to minimize KL divergence with the true posterior is equivalent to maximizing a lower bound on `log pi_theta(success|s)`. This means the exploration reward is not an arbitrary bonus term — it is derived from the fundamental objective of task completion. When the reward model scores an exploratory action highly, it is making the claim that this exploration-memory configuration increases the model's probability of success by a quantifiable amount. This moves exploration from "try things and see what happens" to "take actions that are predicted to resolve specific informational gaps," which is a qualitative upgrade in the sophistication of the exploration strategy.

Evidence for the distinctiveness of this framing appears in the comparison with LAMER (Jiang et al., 2025) and with the online reward alternative (Appendix B). LAMER enables agents to explore by sampling multiple trajectories and adapting through in-context meta-learning, but it applies exploration uniformly — every task receives the same multi-trajectory treatment regardless of whether the current state is novel or familiar. EAPO's 20–60% performance advantage over LAMER across environments (Table 2) is not merely a metric improvement; it reflects a fundamentally different exploration paradigm. Rather than exploring everywhere and filtering the results post-hoc, EAPO's agents learn to explore *selectively*, targeting only states where the variational model predicts that exploration will substantially improve the posterior success probability. The online reward experiment (Figs. 3–4) further confirms the value of the Bayesian framing: even with 10 online rollout samples, the online reward achieves comparable performance to EAPO's learned density function, but the learned function eliminates the need for expensive environment interaction during training. This is possible precisely because the density function captures a *distributional prior* — knowledge about which exploration-memory configurations are associated with success, abstracted from specific rollouts — that generalizes across states.

---

### Innovation 2: Structural Separation of Exploration Reasoning From Task Execution as a Credit Assignment Mechanism, Not Merely a Prompting Trick

The paper introduces `<explore>` and `<memory>` tags as dedicated output channels for the agent's exploratory reasoning and state summarization. At first glance, this appears to be a prompting engineering choice — an instance of the increasingly common practice of giving LLMs structured output formats. However, the paper makes a deeper argument that elevates this from an implementation convenience to a **necessary condition for learning adaptive exploration via policy gradients**.

The problem this solves is specific and non-obvious. In standard LLM agent training, the model generates a single output stream that mixes reasoning about what to do, evaluation of past actions, description of observed states, and the executable action itself. Some tokens reflect exploratory deliberation ("I should try clicking the settings button to see if the target option is there"), while others directly specify the committed action (`{"action_type": "click", "index": 5}`). Under a policy gradient objective like GRPO, all tokens in the output share the same reward signal — typically a sparse binary success indicator applied to the entire trajectory. This creates a fundamental credit assignment failure: the exploration-reasoning tokens receive the same reward as the action-selection tokens, preventing the optimization from differentially reinforcing exploratory reasoning that is genuinely informative versus exploratory reasoning that is redundant or misguided.

The structured output format solves this by creating token spans that can receive **different reward signals**. The exploratory reward `R_explore`, which evaluates whether the exploration-memory configuration moves the agent toward high-success-probability states, can be applied specifically to the tokens within `<explore>` and `<memory>` tags. The task reward `R_task` applies to the entire output. The format reward `R_format` ensures the structure itself is maintained. This three-way decomposition (Equation 10) means the policy gradient sees distinct signals for "was this good exploration reasoning?" (from `R_explore`), "did the final action succeed?" (from `R_task`), and "was the output correctly structured?" (from `R_format`). Without the structural separation, all three signals would be hopelessly entangled.

This distinguishes EAPO's tags from superficially similar approaches. Prompting methods that ask models to "think step by step" or "explain your reasoning" produce unstructured reasoning text — useful for the model's own deliberation but invisible to the optimization. Methods like Tree-of-Thoughts (Yao et al., 2023) or ReAct (Yao et al., 2022b) intermix reasoning and action tokens in ways that human readers can parse but policy gradient algorithms cannot disentangle. EAPO's insight is that making exploration an **explicit, rewardable channel** is not about helping the model reason better — it's about making the optimization landscape navigable. Remark 4.1 states: "the format enables reliable credit assignment for exploration-related rewards, which would otherwise be difficult to achieve with unstructured outputs." This is the crucial claim: the structure is for the *training signal*, not (primarily) for the model's cognitive benefit.

The empirical evidence supports this interpretation through the ablation that removes the format reward (Table 8). Without the format reward, performance drops substantially (e.g., from 76.4% to 68.6% on AndroidWorld with the 2B model, and from 53.2% to 45.8% on WebShop). The format reward itself is trivially satisfiable — the agent learns the correct tag structure in "less than [a few] rounds" (Fig. 15) — so the degradation cannot be explained by the agent failing to produce structured outputs. Rather, without the format reward, the optimization has no signal to maintain the separation of exploration, memory, and action channels, causing the structural distinctions to erode over training. When the channels blur, the exploratory reward's signal becomes diluted across the entire output rather than concentrated on the exploration tokens, and the credit assignment advantage is lost.

---

### Innovation 3: Visitation Depth as the Missing Dimension for Fair Comparison of Exploratory and Exploitative Actions

The exploration-aware grouping mechanism (Section 5.2) introduces visitation depth `kappa(s)` as a clustering criterion for computing relative advantages in GRPO. This appears to be a modest algorithmic tweak — add one more dimension to the grouping key — but it addresses a **structural flaw in how policy gradient methods handle multi-phase behavior** that has implications beyond this specific paper.

The problem is inherent to any training method that compares trajectories by aggregate reward. In a trajectory where the agent first explores (actions that gather information but don't advance the task), then exploits (actions that complete the task using the gathered information), the exploratory actions have lower cumulative reward than the exploitative actions simply because of temporal discounting — the exploitative actions occur later, closer to the reward event, so they capture more of the undiscounted success signal. When GRPO pools all actions into a single group and computes advantages relative to the group mean, the exploratory actions are systematically below-mean and receive negative advantages, regardless of whether they were genuinely useful. The optimization learns that *exploration is bad* — not because exploration is actually counterproductive, but because the reward accounting is structurally biased against early-phase actions.

Prior approaches to this problem fall into two categories, both inadequate. Entropy-based methods (Zhang et al., 2024, Xu et al., 2025) try to counteract the bias by adding an exploration bonus to the reward, making exploration look artificially better. But this is a blunt instrument — it can't distinguish between exploration that provides genuine informational value and exploration that is merely entropic. It also introduces a new hyperparameter (the bonus strength) that must be tuned per-environment. Meta-RL approaches like LAMER avoid the credit assignment problem by treating the entire multi-trajectory episode as a single context window for in-context learning, sidestepping per-action reward attribution entirely. But this means the agent never learns *which* of its exploratory actions were responsible for success — it only learns that exploring-as-a-whole helps.

Visitation depth offers a fundamentally different solution. By clustering actions into groups where all members share the same `(s, kappa(s))` pair, EAPO ensures that first-visit exploratory actions are compared only to other first-visit exploratory actions at the same state. The key insight is that **within a given visitation depth, the average reward is lower than the overall average** — because all actions at that depth are by definition earlier in the trajectory. An exploratory action at `kappa(s)=0` with reward 0.3 might be below the global mean of 0.5 but above the `kappa(s)=0` mean of 0.2, giving it a *positive* advantage. The same action under standard GRPO would receive a negative advantage and be suppressed. The grouping doesn't change the absolute rewards — it changes the *reference point* against which actions are judged, making the reference point appropriate for the action's temporal position in the trajectory.

This is a conceptual advance beyond the specific application to agent exploration. It identifies that when trajectories have distinct phases (exploration → exploitation, or more generally: preparation → execution), comparing actions across phase boundaries produces systematically biased credit assignment. The visitation depth is one instance of a more general principle: **actions should be compared to other actions at the same phase of the task**, not to all actions indiscriminately. This principle could apply to any multi-phase RL problem — hierarchical tasks, curriculum learning, anytime algorithms — not just exploration.

The empirical case for this innovation's importance is stark. Figures 18–19 show what happens when exploration-aware grouping is removed: both exploration degree and task performance exhibit a rise-then-collapse pattern. The agent initially explores (exploration degree rises to ~25–40%), but as training continues and exploratory actions are increasingly undervalued relative to later exploitative actions, the policy learns to suppress exploration entirely. By the end of training, exploration degree collapses to near zero and task performance degrades substantially (e.g., from 76.4% to 61.3% on AndroidWorld, Table 8). This pattern — initial benefit followed by optimization-driven collapse — is the signature of a credit assignment failure, not a capability limitation. The agent *can* explore; it learns *not to* because the optimization penalizes exploration unfairly. The grouping mechanism doesn't teach the agent to explore — it prevents the optimization from teaching the agent *not* to explore.

---

### Innovation 4: The Discounted Max Operator as a Principled Mechanism for Preventing Over-Conservatism and Over-Exploration Simultaneously

The exploration reward computation (Equation 9) uses a `max` over two terms — the immediate exploitation value and the `gamma^2`-discounted exploration value — to score transitions. This appears to be a small implementation detail in a reward function, but it encodes a **decision-theoretic principle** that simultaneously addresses two opposite failure modes that plague existing methods.

The first failure mode is **over-conservatism**: the agent explores even when it already has sufficient information, because exploration is always rewarded. This is the problem with undifferentiated exploration methods like LAMER — the agent samples multiple trajectories for every task regardless of whether it's already confident. The `max` operation prevents this by comparing the exploration value to the immediate exploitation value. If the agent already has sufficient memory to solve the task (high first term), the `max` selects the higher immediate value, and the exploration term is ignored. The agent receives credit for *not* exploring when exploration isn't needed.

The second failure mode is **over-exploration**: the agent explores excessively because exploration is always beneficial in expectation, even when the marginal information gain is tiny. The `gamma^2` discount on the exploration term encodes the cost of exploration: one step to take the exploratory action and observe the result, plus a second step to synthesize the new information into an improved decision. With `gamma = 0.9`, the exploration value is discounted to `0.81 q_phi`, meaning exploration is only credited when it improves the success probability estimate by more than ~23% (to overcome the discount). An exploratory action that provides genuine insight — substantially increasing `q_phi` — will exceed the immediate exploitation value despite the discount. An exploratory action that provides marginal or redundant information will not, and the agent learns to skip it.

Neither failure mode is addressed by existing entropy-based or uniform-exploration methods. Entropy bonuses (Zhang et al., 2024) encourage exploration everywhere, potentially causing over-conservatism. Fixed exploration budgets or branching factors (Yao et al., 2023) prevent over-exploration but also prevent deep exploration when needed — the agent might exhaust its budget before resolving genuine uncertainty. The `max + gamma^2` mechanism adapts the exploration decision to the *estimated information value* of the specific exploratory action, not to a global budget or a model-uncertainty proxy.

The practical significance appears in the `gamma` parameter sweep (Section D.4, Fig. 10, Table 7). At `gamma = 0.5`, exploration is too strongly penalized — the discount is `0.25 q_phi`, meaning exploration needs a 4× improvement in success probability to be worthwhile, which essentially never happens. Performance degrades because the agent never explores, even in genuinely novel states. At `gamma = 1.0`, the discount is eliminated — exploration provides undiscounted value, and the agent over-explores, averaging 43.9 steps per task on ALFWorld (vs. 22.5 at `gamma = 0.9`) with *lower* task success. The sweet spot at `gamma = 0.9` balances information gain against interaction cost. This is not merely hyperparameter tuning — it demonstrates that the `gamma^2` discount succeeds in operationalizing the abstract principle that "exploration is valuable only when the expected information gain exceeds the cost of gathering it." The fact that performance is unimodal in `gamma` (rising then falling) across all four environments confirms that the mechanism captures a real tradeoff rather than a dataset-specific artifact.

The `max` formulation also connects to a broader principle in reinforcement learning that is under-explored in the LLM agent literature: **the value of information**. Standard RL optimizes for expected reward; exploration is a means to that end. EAPO's reward function explicitly scores the *informational* value of actions separately from their *task-completion* value, then selects the better strategy at each decision point. This is a precursor to building agents that can reason about their own knowledge state and make meta-level decisions about when to acquire more information — a capability that is currently limited to scripted behaviors (e.g., "if uncertain, ask the user") rather than learned policies.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The paper evaluates on four environments spanning two domains: (1) **Text-based**: ALFWorld (Shridhar et al., 2021), a text-based embodied household environment where agents navigate and interact with objects via natural language commands; WebShop (Yao et al., 2022a), a web-based interactive shopping environment requiring exploration and planning under uncertainty. (2) **GUI-based**: AndroidWorld (Rawles et al., 2025), containing 116 dynamic tasks across 20 real Android apps; OSWorld (Xie et al., 2024), a large-scale benchmark with 369 tasks spanning real web and desktop applications requiring long-horizon planning and multi-window coordination. The paper uses the standard evaluation splits from each benchmark (Section 6.1, Appendix C.1).

- **Base model(s).** For text-based environments, the paper uses Qwen3 (Yang et al., 2025a) at three scales: 1.7B, 4B, and 8B parameters. For GUI-based environments, the paper uses Qwen3-VL (Bai et al., 2025) at three scales: 2B, 4B, and 8B parameters. These models are described as "suitable for real-world deployment" (Section 6.1) and represent a range of scales that allows testing whether EAPO's benefits are consistent as model capacity increases. The choice of Qwen3 and Qwen3-VL is practical — they are contemporary open-source models with strong baseline performance — rather than theoretically motivated. Zero-shot performance (Table 5) ranges from 10.5% (Qwen-1.7B on ALFWorld) to 52.0% (Qwen-VL-4B on AndroidWorld), leaving substantial room for improvement through training.

- **Metrics.** The primary metric is **task success rate (%)**, measured as the fraction of evaluation tasks for which the agent reaches the goal state within the maximum allowed interaction steps. This is a sparse binary metric: 1 if the final state satisfies the task completion condition, 0 otherwise. For AndroidWorld and OSWorld, tasks have environment-defined success criteria. For ALFWorld and WebShop, success is determined by whether the agent correctly completes the specified task objective. All results report success rate on the standard test split for each benchmark, averaged over three random seeds with standard deviation shown as uncertainty intervals (Figure 1, Figure 2).

- **Baselines.** The paper compares against six methods (Section 6.1, Appendix C.2): (1) **Min-p** (Minh et al., 2025), a dynamic truncation decoding method that adjusts the sampling threshold based on model confidence using the top token's probability as a scaling factor — this is an inference-time method, not a training method; (2) **OverRIDE** (Shi & Pan, 2026), a decoding method that dynamically adjusts the next-token probability distribution to encourage exploration of low-probability generation paths — also inference-time; (3) **GRPO** (Shao et al., 2024), the base group-based, critic-free RL method that computes relative advantages within trajectory groups; (4) **DAPO** (Yu et al., 2025), an improved GRPO variant with dynamic trajectory sampling and relaxed clipping thresholds; (5) **GiGPO** (Feng et al., 2025), which extends group-based policy optimization with two-dimensional credit assignment across both steps and trajectories; (6) **LAMER** (Jiang et al., 2025), a meta-RL framework that trains LLM agents to sample multiple trajectories and adapt behavior through in-context reasoning over these sampled experiences. The first two baselines (Min-p, OverRIDE) test whether inference-time exploration heuristics suffice without specialized training. The next three (GRPO, DAPO, GiGPO) test progressively more sophisticated RL training methods. LAMER is the strongest baseline as it explicitly targets test-time exploration through meta-RL.

  Additionally, Table 2 and Table 4 compare against closed-source models (OpenAI CUA o3, Claude-3.7-Sonnet, Claude-4-Sonnet-0929, DeepMiner-Mano-72B, UI-TARS-2-2509, Seed1.5-VL-250717, TianXi-Action-7B) and open-source models (Qwen3-VL-235B-A22B, ZeroGUI, UI-TARS-7B, OpenCUA-32B, ARPO, GUI-Owl-7B, DART-GUI-7B). These comparisons establish performance relative to the broader state of the art but should be interpreted cautiously since these models differ in architecture, pretraining data, and scale.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** Training budget is measured in **RL epochs** (1000 total; Table 3) with a fixed sampling group size of G=16 trajectories per task per epoch. The paper does not report total environment interaction steps or wall-clock GPU hours, making it difficult to compare the true computational cost against baselines. Runtime analysis (Section D.7.7, Figures 20–21) reports per-step wall-clock time on 8 NVIDIA H800 GPUs but does not aggregate to total training cost. For inference, the paper reports average number of steps per task (Table 9), which provides a measure of test-time efficiency: EAPO's agents take 22.7 steps on AndroidWorld versus 16.9–19.1 for baselines, reflecting the cost of exploration. The paper argues this modest increase (at most ~10 steps vs. the most efficient baseline) is acceptable given the performance gains, and that the γ discount prevents meaningless exploration.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** All experiments are run with three random seeds, with standard deviation shown as uncertainty bands in learning curves (Figures 1, 2, 9, 10, 11, 12). Hyperparameters are fixed across environments (Table 3) with the exception of sweep experiments for γ, G, and λ (Section 6.2, Appendix D.4–D.6). The exploration degree metric (Section D.7.5) is defined as "the fraction of states that are revisited multiple times among all states." The paper does not use cross-validation for model selection — hyperparameters are set once and applied uniformly — which is reasonable given the computational cost of RL training but means the reported hyperparameter settings may be specific to these environments.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Aggregate Performance Across All Environments

The headline result (Table 2, Table 5, Figure 1): **EAPO consistently and substantially outperforms all baselines across all four environments and all model scales**. The margins are large — often 20–60% relative improvement over the next-best baseline — and the pattern holds for both text-based and GUI-based tasks.

Using the 8B model (the largest scale tested): EAPO achieves 76.0% on ALFWorld (vs. LAMER at 61.3%, a +14.8 percentage point improvement), 65.6% on WebShop (vs. LAMER at 47.7%, +17.8pp), 82.1% on AndroidWorld (vs. DAPO at 61.8%, +20.3pp), and 64.3% on OSWorld (vs. LAMER at 55.6%, +8.6pp). The gains are largest on the GUI-based environments (+15.5pp on AndroidWorld averaged across model sizes, +10.5pp on OSWorld), which the paper attributes to these environments presenting more novel states that benefit from selective exploration.

At the smallest scale (1.7B/2B models), EAPO achieves **competitive or superior performance to substantially larger models** (Table 2, Table 4). EAPO-2B achieves 76.4% on AndroidWorld, exceeding the 8B-scale GRPO baseline (55.5%) and all open-source agentic models at any scale (DART-GUI-7B: 58.0%, OpenCUA-32B: 51.7%). It also outperforms closed-source models like OpenAI CUA o3 (55.4%) and Claude-3.7-Sonnet (60.8%). On OSWorld, EAPO-2B achieves 50.3%, exceeding the 72B-parameter DeepMiner-Mano-72B (53.9%) and approaching Claude-4-Sonnet-0929 (62.9%). This is not a claim that scale is irrelevant — the 8B EAPO model consistently outperforms the 2B EAPO model — but rather that **learning when to explore can partially compensate for model scale**, consistent with the paper's thesis that exploration amplifies existing capability.

#### Training Convergence and Stability

Figure 2 and Figure 9 show learning curves across all environments and model sizes. EAPO exhibits **faster convergence** than all baselines: by step 200–400, EAPO typically reaches or exceeds the final performance of the next-best baseline, while baselines continue to improve more gradually through step 800–1000. This faster convergence is attributed to the exploration reward providing a denser training signal than sparse task success alone, enabling the policy to learn more efficiently per environment interaction.

EAPO's learning curves also show **greater stability** than baselines, with smaller standard deviation bands across seeds (Figure 1, Figure 2). This is notable because RL training of LLMs is notoriously high-variance. The paper attributes this stability to the exploration-aware grouping mechanism, which prevents the destructive interference between exploratory and exploitative action gradients that causes collapse in standard GRPO (Figure 19).

The ablation that removes exploration-aware grouping (Figure 18, Figure 19) confirms this attribution: without grouping, performance exhibits a rise-then-collapse pattern, initially improving as the agent learns to explore, then degrading as exploratory actions are increasingly penalized relative to later task-completion actions. This collapse is visible across all environments and model sizes, demonstrating that the grouping mechanism is not merely beneficial but **necessary** for sustainable exploration learning.

#### Comparison With Inference-Time Baselines

Min-p and OverRIDE — the two inference-time decoding baselines that do not involve training — consistently underperform all training-based methods (Table 5, Figure 1, Figure 2). Min-p achieves 15–55% across environments (best: 55.5% on AndroidWorld with Qwen-VL-2B using zero-shot prompts, but this is the zero-shot model, not Min-p; Min-p itself achieves 50.9% on AndroidWorld with the 2B model). OverRIDE achieves 27–61% (best: 60.7% on AndroidWorld with Qwen-VL-8B). Both are substantially below EAPO's performance (76.4% on AndroidWorld with the 2B model). This gap demonstrates that **inference-time exploration heuristics alone are insufficient** — learning an exploration policy through training provides benefits that decoding-time strategies cannot replicate. The reason, the paper argues, is that decoding-time methods cannot distinguish between states where exploration is valuable and states where it is redundant; they apply the same entropy-increasing or diversity-promoting heuristic uniformly across all states.

#### Generalization to Unseen Environments

Section D.3 and Table 6 report a **cross-environment transfer experiment**: models trained on AndroidWorld are evaluated directly on OSWorld without any fine-tuning or adaptation. EAPO-2B (trained on AndroidWorld) achieves 48.8% overall on OSWorld, compared to EAPO-2B trained directly on OSWorld (50.3%, from Table 5) — a drop of only 1.5 percentage points. This pattern holds across model scales: EAPO-4B (AndroidWorld → OSWorld) achieves 55.3% vs. 57.9% in-domain; EAPO-8B achieves 60.6% vs. 64.3% in-domain. The per-application breakdown (Table 6) shows the transferred model performs well across diverse OSWorld applications: 44.1% on Chrome, 49.8% on GIMP, 49.4% on Calc, 43.4% on Impress, 41.2% on Writer, 48.9% on VLC, 56.6% on VS Code.

The paper attributes this generalization to "the explicit modeling of exploration and memory at test time" which allows the agent to "adapt its interaction strategy to new domains without requiring environment-specific retraining" and captures "domain-invariant exploration strategies rather than overfitting to environment-specific interfaces." This is a strong claim — that EAPO learns *how* to explore in a transferable way, not just *what* to explore in AndroidWorld — but the near-parity between transferred and in-domain performance provides meaningful evidence. The small degradation (1.5–4 percentage points) suggests the learned exploration policy does indeed capture general principles rather than memorizing Android-specific UI elements.

#### Runtime and Efficiency

Figures 20–21 in Section D.7.7 break down the per-step runtime of EAPO versus baselines. For the 8B model on AndroidWorld, EAPO takes approximately 1636 seconds per training step versus 1332 seconds for standard GRPO — roughly **23% additional overhead** per step. The breakdown (Figure 21) shows that the three unchanged components (rollout sampling, old-policy probability computation, reference-policy probability computation) account for 918.9 + 203.3 + 203.2 = 1325.4 seconds, while EAPO's additional components (reward model update: 304.6 seconds, state-action grouping: 299.7 seconds, exploratory advantage computation: ~similar) add approximately 311 seconds.

For inference, Table 9 reports average steps per task. EAPO takes 22.7 steps on AndroidWorld vs. 16.9–19.1 for baselines — an increase of 3.6–5.8 steps. On ALFWorld, EAPO takes 58.5 steps vs. 38.2–61.3 for baselines. On WebShop, EAPO takes 33.2 steps vs. 19.7–42.5. The paper emphasizes that the γ discount mechanism "guides the agent to carry out exploration only when the anticipated utility 'outweighs' the latency cost, which will avoid meaningless exploration even when agent has obtained enough information." The step increase is modest relative to the performance gains: ~4 extra steps to go from 61.3% (LAMER) to 76.4% (EAPO) on AndroidWorld is a favorable efficiency-accuracy tradeoff.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Removal of exploratory reward (Table 8):** Removing the exploratory reward causes catastrophic performance degradation: for the 2B model, ALFWorld drops from 58.5% to 37.6% (−20.9pp), WebShop from 53.2% to 30.3% (−22.9pp), AndroidWorld from 76.3% to 51.0% (−25.3pp), OSWorld from 50.3% to 23.7% (−26.6pp). The degradation is even larger for the 8B model on ALFWorld (−28.0pp) and WebShop (−22.2pp). This ablation essentially reduces EAPO to standard GRPO with the structured output format but no exploration reward — the performance is comparable to or below the GRPO baseline (Table 5), confirming that the exploration reward is the primary source of EAPO's gains. The paper notes (Section D.7.6) that without the exploratory reward, agents "fail to evaluate the usefulness of each exploratory action, causing ineffective exploration and premature convergence to suboptimal behaviors."

**Removal of exploration-aware grouping (Table 8, Figures 18–19):** This ablation removes the visitation-depth-based clustering (Equation 13) and uses standard GRPO grouping by task description only. Performance drops substantially but less catastrophically than removing the exploration reward: for the 2B model, ALFWorld drops from 58.5% to 38.8% (−19.7pp), AndroidWorld from 76.3% to 61.3% (−15.0pp), OSWorld from 50.3% to 25.4% (−24.9pp). The exploration degree curves (Figure 19) show the characteristic rise-then-collapse pattern discussed earlier: exploration initially increases (the agent discovers exploration is beneficial), then collapses to near zero as training progresses and exploratory actions are increasingly undervalued relative to later task-completion actions in the same group. This confirms that the grouping mechanism is essential for **sustaining** exploration — without it, the optimization's bias against early-phase actions eventually kills exploration entirely, even though exploration was initially beneficial.

**Removal of format reward (Table 8):** Without the format reward, performance drops moderately: for the 2B model, AndroidWorld drops from 76.3% to 68.6% (−7.7pp), ALFWorld from 58.5% to 40.2% (−18.3pp). The paper explains this as the agent "failing to consistently follow the predefined output structure for exploration signals and memory updates, preventing effective organization, storage, and reuse of information obtained through exploration" (Section D.7.6). The format reward ensures the structural separation of exploration, memory, and action channels — without this signal, the distinctions blur, and the exploratory reward's signal is diluted across unstructured output tokens.

**SFT rollback necessity (Section D.7.6):** The paper notes that removing the SFT rollback stage leads to a "noticeable performance drop" but does not report explicit numbers, stating that "exploration accuracy and rollback capability are inherently coupled. Even if the agent learns to explore effectively, the acquired information cannot be properly utilized without the ability to rollback to previous decision points." The SFT stage is presented as an enabling prerequisite rather than the primary source of performance gains, consistent with the interpretation that EAPO's benefits come from the RL phase building on SFT-provided foundational capabilities.

**Discount factor γ sweep (Figure 10, Table 7):** Performance follows a unimodal pattern across all environments, peaking at γ = 0.9. At γ = 0.5 (heavy discount on exploration), performance degrades because exploration is too strongly penalized — the optimization degenerates into conventional GRPO. At γ = 1.0 (no discount), performance degrades because the agent over-explores, with average steps per task nearly doubling (22.5 → 43.9 on ALFWorld, 22.7 → 44.8 on AndroidWorld) and redundant exploration introducing noise that degrades decision quality. The sweet spot at γ = 0.9 balances information-gathering value against step cost.

**Sampling group size G sweep (Figure 11):** Performance increases monotonically from G = 4 to G = 16, with diminishing returns beyond G = 16. Larger groups provide more reliable advantage estimates by reducing variance in the per-cluster normalization. The saturation at G = 16 suggests this is the point where within-cluster statistics are sufficiently stable, and further increases mainly add computational cost.

**KL coefficient λ sweep (Figure 12):** Performance is unimodal with a peak at moderate λ values (0.01–0.05). λ = 0.005 (very weak regularization) allows aggressive policy updates that destabilize training. λ = 0.5–1.0 (strong regularization) over-constrains the policy to remain near the reference model, preventing it from learning novel exploration behaviors. The chosen λ = 0.01 (Table 3) is at the lower end of the effective range, suggesting the policy benefits from relatively loose KL constraints that allow exploration behaviors to develop.

**Online reward alternative (Appendix B, Figures 3–4):** The paper compares EAPO's learned variational reward against an online reward that estimates exploration utility through 1–10 rollout samples. The online reward with 1 sample substantially underperforms (the policy cannot reliably estimate long-term information gain from a single rollout due to high variance). Performance improves monotonically as the number of online samples increases from 1 to 10, approaching EAPO's performance at 10 samples. The exploration degree of the online reward (Figure 3) is "slightly lower" than EAPO's, which the paper attributes to the online reward underestimating the value of exploratory actions because the current policy "may struggle to accurately capture the long-term information gain." The key finding is that EAPO's learned reward achieves comparable performance to the online reward with many samples while eliminating entirely the need for online rollouts during reward estimation.

**Convergence of reward model and policy (Figures 13–14):** The reward model loss and policy loss converge reliably within 600–800 training steps across all environments, with smooth learning curves and no divergence or oscillation. Figure 15 decomposes the total reward into format, exploratory, and task components during training on AndroidWorld: the format reward saturates quickly ("in less than [a few] rounds"), the exploratory reward steadily increases as the policy learns to explore effectively, and the task reward tracks overall performance improvement.

**Step-level group size dynamics (Figure 16):** The distribution of transition group sizes evolves over training in a consistent pattern across environments. At iteration 250 (early training), the majority of groups have size 1 (no state revisitation). By iteration 500, the proportion of larger groups (3–10+) increases substantially, reflecting the agent learning to explore and revisit states. By iterations 750–1000, the distribution stabilizes, with ~16–22% of groups having size 1 and the rest distributed across larger sizes. This stabilization indicates the agent has converged to a consistent exploration pattern rather than oscillating between over- and under-exploration.

**Model scale effects (Figure 1, Table 5):** EAPO's absolute performance increases with model scale across all environments, but the **relative advantage over baselines** does not show a consistent trend with scale. On ALFWorld, the advantage over the next-best baseline is +13.1pp for 1.7B, +14.4pp for 4B, and +14.8pp for 8B — roughly constant. On AndroidWorld, the advantage is +15.1pp for 2B, +18.0pp for 4B, and +21.1pp for 8B — increasing with scale. On OSWorld, the advantage is +16.6pp for 2B, +8.6pp for 4B, and +8.6pp for 8B — decreasing with scale. This inconsistency makes it difficult to draw conclusions about whether EAPO's benefits scale with model size or are roughly constant. The paper does not discuss this pattern.

### Critical Assessment

#### Does EAPO Actually Learn Adaptive, Selective Exploration?

The paper's central claim is that EAPO "enables agents to distinguish when exploration is necessary and adaptively gather environmental information only under high uncertainty." The experimental evidence most directly supporting this claim comes from three sources, each with specific interpretive caveats:

**Evidence 1: Exploration degree convergence (Figure 17).** The exploration degree — fraction of states revisited multiple times — rises during early training (the agent learns to explore) and then converges to a stable intermediate level (the agent learns *not* to over-explore). This pattern is consistent with selective exploration: the agent explores enough to maintain a stable exploration degree but not so much that exploration degree continues increasing unboundedly, which would indicate indiscriminate exploration. However, exploration degree is a coarse metric — it measures *how much* the agent revisits states but does not directly measure *whether* the agent revisits the right states (those with high uncertainty) and avoids revisiting the wrong states (those already well-understood). The convergence to 20–40% could equally reflect the agent settling into a fixed exploration frequency regardless of state characteristics (exploring 20–40% of the time uniformly rather than adaptively). The paper does not provide a per-state analysis showing that exploration frequency correlates with state novelty or uncertainty.

**Evidence 2: Ablation of exploration-aware grouping (Figures 18–19).** The rise-then-collapse pattern when grouping is removed demonstrates that the grouping mechanism is necessary for *sustaining* exploration, but it does not directly demonstrate that exploration is *adaptive*. The collapse occurs because exploratory actions are systematically undervalued when compared against later task-execution actions — this is a credit assignment failure, not evidence that the surviving exploration (with grouping) is state-dependent. An agent that explores uniformly across all states would also show the collapse pattern without grouping, because the uniform exploration would be equally undervalued across all states. The ablation proves that grouping prevents exploration from being extinguished, but it does not prove that the resulting exploration is selective.

**Evidence 3: Case study (Figures 22–30).** The OSWorld example shows the agent exploring one candidate action (clicking on one UI element), discovering it leads to an incorrect state, rolling back, and then selecting the correct action using the acquired information. This demonstrates that EAPO agents *can* perform targeted exploration — they try a specific action, observe the result, learn from it, and use the learning to make a better decision. However, a single qualitative example cannot establish that this behavior is the *typical* pattern rather than a cherry-picked success case. The paper does not report statistics on what fraction of exploratory actions lead to useful information, what fraction are rolled back, or what fraction directly enable task completion.

**What would strengthen this claim:** A per-state analysis showing that states with higher "true uncertainty" (e.g., states where the untrained policy has high variance in action selection, or states that are distributionally different from training states) receive more exploration than familiar states. A comparison of the exploration frequency distribution — does the agent explore a small fraction of states deeply (selective) or a large fraction of states uniformly (indiscriminate)? An analysis of whether the variational reward model's density estimates `q_phi(e, m|s)` correlate with the actual information gain from exploration (measured by improvement in action quality after exploration vs. before).

#### Do the Reported Performance Gains Reflect Genuine Capability Improvement?

The 20–60% relative improvement over baselines (Table 2, Table 5) is substantial, but several aspects of the experimental design warrant scrutiny:

**The baseline comparisons mix training methods and inference methods.** Min-p and OverRIDE are inference-time decoding strategies applied to the zero-shot model. Their inclusion as "baselines" is somewhat misleading — they demonstrate that decoding heuristics cannot substitute for training, which is a straw-man comparison (no one would expect a decoding trick to match a 1000-epoch RL training procedure). The relevant comparison is against the training-based baselines (GRPO, DAPO, GiGPO, LAMER), against which EAPO's advantage is still large (14–20 percentage points) but more meaningful.

**The training budget is not equalized across methods.** All RL training methods use 1000 epochs, but EAPO has higher per-step computational cost (23% more runtime; Figure 20). If the baselines were trained for proportionally more epochs to equalize total FLOPs or wall-clock time, would the gap narrow? The paper does not report this equalized comparison. The faster convergence of EAPO (Figure 2) partially addresses this — EAPO achieves baseline-final performance in 200–400 steps rather than 600–1000 — but early-stopping the baselines at 400 steps and comparing to EAPO at 400 steps would be a more rigorous equal-compute test.

**The zero-shot performance of the base models varies substantially across environments.** Qwen-VL-2B achieves 46.2% zero-shot on AndroidWorld (Table 5) but only 10.5% on ALFWorld. The absolute gains from training are correspondingly different: +30.2pp on AndroidWorld vs. +48.1pp on ALFWorld. The paper correctly focuses on relative gains over baselines trained under identical conditions, but the varying baselines make cross-environment comparisons of "how much EAPO helps" difficult to interpret.

**The model scale comparison has an important asymmetry.** Table 4 compares EAPO-2B against models like Qwen3-VL-235B-A22B — a model with over 100× more parameters. EAPO-2B outperforms this model on ALFWorld (58.5% vs. 50.8%), WebShop (53.3% vs. 45.1%), and AndroidWorld (76.4% vs. 62.0%), but the larger model was evaluated zero-shot (or with unspecified prompting) while EAPO-2B underwent 1000 epochs of task-specific RL training. This is comparing a specialized, fine-tuned model against a general-purpose model, which conflates the benefits of EAPO's training method with the benefits of task-specific adaptation more broadly. A fairer comparison would be EAPO-2B vs. the larger model also fine-tuned on the same tasks (with standard GRPO or another baseline method).

#### Does the Generalization Result (AndroidWorld → OSWorld) Demonstrate Transferable Exploration?

The cross-environment transfer experiment (Table 6) is presented as evidence that EAPO learns "domain-invariant exploration strategies." The small performance drop (48.8% vs. 50.3% for the 2B model) is indeed striking. However, several factors complicate the interpretation:

**AndroidWorld and OSWorld share fundamental interaction patterns.** Both involve clicking UI elements, scrolling, navigating menus, and typing text. The action spaces overlap substantially (both use click, scroll, type, navigate actions; Figure 6). The "exploration" in both environments involves trying UI interactions and observing the resulting screen changes — the meta-skill of "try clicking things, see what happens, remember what you learned" transfers naturally because the underlying interaction paradigm is identical. The transfer result may reflect that both environments are GUI-based and share the same basic affordances, rather than reflecting a genuinely abstract exploration capability.

**The transfer is unidirectional.** The paper only reports AndroidWorld → OSWorld transfer, not the reverse. If EAPO on OSWorld also transfers to AndroidWorld with minimal degradation, that would strengthen the claim. If the transfer is asymmetric — OSWorld training doesn't transfer to AndroidWorld — that would suggest environment-specific factors (e.g., OSWorld's greater diversity of applications and interactions) drive the transfer result rather than a domain-invariant exploration strategy.

**No baseline comparison for transfer.** The paper does not report whether GRPO-trained or LAMER-trained models also transfer between these environments. If all training methods show similar transfer performance, the result reflects the relatedness of the environments rather than EAPO-specific benefits. If EAPO transfers substantially better than baselines, that would be stronger evidence for the "domain-invariant exploration" claim.

**What would strengthen this claim:** Baseline transfer results (GRPO/LAMER AndroidWorld → OSWorld). Bidirectional transfer experiments. Analysis of *what* exploration behaviors are shared: do EAPO agents use similar exploration strategies (e.g., exploring menu structures, probing interactive elements) across environments, or do they learn environment-specific exploration heuristics that happen to apply to both AndroidWorld and OSWorld?

#### Unanswered Questions From the Experimental Design

**1. How is the variational reward model initialized and trained in practice?** The paper states that `q_phi` is implemented using "the same model as the policy model" (Section C.3) but provides no architectural details. Is it a separate copy of the base model fine-tuned for the reward prediction task? Does it share parameters with the policy? How are the `(e, m)` inputs encoded — are exploration and memory treated as text tokens and processed by the same transformer as the state? The REINFORCE optimization (Equation 8) is described at a high level but implementation details (batch construction, reward normalization, baseline subtraction for variance reduction) are absent. This makes reproduction of the reward model training difficult.

**2. What is the computational cost of the difficulty estimation analog?** Unlike the reference paper summary (which explicitly flags the 2048-sample difficulty estimation cost), EAPO does not have a difficulty estimation phase. However, it does have the variational reward model training as an ongoing cost during RL. Each epoch requires optimizing `q_phi` on the current batch of trajectories (Algorithm 1, Line 10). The paper reports this cost (Figure 21) but does not analyze how the reward model's accuracy evolves — does it need to be trained from scratch each epoch, or does it converge and require only maintenance updates? Is the reward model's quality sensitive to the batch composition (e.g., does it need trajectories with diverse exploration behaviors)?

**3. How much of EAPO's benefit comes from the structured output format alone?** The ablation that removes the format reward (Table 8) shows substantial degradation, but this ablation removes the reward for following the format — it does not remove the format itself from the agent's input/output. A stronger ablation would be to remove the `<explore>` and `<memory>` tags entirely (use unstructured generation), keeping only the exploration reward and grouping mechanism. This would isolate whether the structural separation is necessary for the optimization or primarily helps the agent's reasoning. The paper does not report this ablation.

**4. Is the performance improvement on hard tasks or only on easy/medium tasks?** The results in Table 2 and Table 5 report aggregate success rate. The paper does not provide a difficulty-stratified analysis (unlike the reference paper summary, which organized results by difficulty quintile). Without knowing whether EAPO helps uniformly across task difficulty or primarily on tasks where the base model already has some capability, it is difficult to assess whether the exploration mechanism genuinely extends the agent's capability frontier or amplifies existing competence. The AMCWorld and OSWorld tasks vary substantially in complexity (some require 5 steps, others 50+), and the paper's aggregate metric obscures this variation.

**5. How does performance vary with trajectory length?** The paper reports average steps per task (Table 9) but not success rate stratified by task length. If EAPO's exploration primarily helps on long-horizon tasks (where the agent has more opportunities to be uncertain and benefit from information gathering) but adds unnecessary overhead on short tasks (where exploration costs steps without providing useful information), the aggregate metric may overstate the practical benefit for deployment scenarios with mixed task lengths.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### Limitation 1: Structured Exploration and Memory Representations Are Manually Specified and Potentially Restrict Expressiveness

**The assumption or constraint.** EAPO relies on a predefined output template with `<explore>` and `<memory>` tags whose semantics are fixed by the instruction prompts (Figures 5–7). Section 7 acknowledges this directly:

> "A limitation of EAPO lies in its reliance on structured exploration and memory representations, which are manually specified throughout training. This may restrict the expressiveness of exploration strategies and limit adaptability to tasks that require more flexible forms of information acquisition."

The exploration guidance `e_t` and memory `m_t` are free-form natural language within their tags (Remark 4.1 emphasizes that "this structured format does not constrain the semantic content"), but the *structure itself* — the separation into exploration, memory, and action channels — is hard-coded. The agent cannot learn to merge these channels when doing so would be more efficient or to create new channels for different types of information.

**The consequence.** In environments where the optimal strategy blends information-gathering and task-execution seamlessly — for example, tasks where every action simultaneously advances the goal while revealing environment dynamics, or tasks where "exploration" manifests as subtle attentional shifts rather than overt trial actions — the rigid three-channel structure may force the agent into an unnatural decomposition. The agent might generate `explore` content even when it already knows what to do simply because the template demands it, or it might fail to represent complex relational knowledge that doesn't fit neatly into a sequential memory summary. More critically, the manual specification means EAPO cannot be applied to a new domain without a human designing the appropriate exploration-memory-action decomposition and writing the corresponding prompts. This limits generality in ways that the paper does not empirically bound.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The format reward ablation (Table 8) provides indirect evidence: removing the format reward (which incentivizes maintaining the structure) causes performance drops of 7.7–18.3 percentage points across environments, demonstrating that the structure is load-bearing. However, the paper provides no ablation where the structure itself is removed (e.g., training with only the exploration reward and grouping mechanism but no `<explore>`/`<memory>` tags), so it is impossible to determine whether the structure helps primarily by enabling credit assignment (the paper's stated rationale) or by constraining the agent's output space in ways that ease optimization. The generalization experiment (Table 6) uses the same structured format in both training (AndroidWorld) and evaluation (OSWorld), so it does not test whether the learned exploration strategy would transfer to an environment with a different output structure.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not attempt to mitigate this limitation. The authors flag it as a limitation in Section 7 and move on, without proposing learned structure discovery, dynamic channel allocation, or ablation of the structure itself. A natural mitigation — allowing the model to learn when to use each channel rather than requiring all three in every output — is not explored. This leaves open the question of whether the structure is a scaffolding that could eventually be removed (similar to how chain-of-thought can be internalized) or a permanent architectural requirement of the approach.

### Limitation 2: The Variational Reward Model Requires Substantial Training Overhead and Its Accuracy Depends on Trajectory Coverage

**The assumption or constraint.** The exploratory reward `R_explore` (Equation 9) depends on a learned density function `q_phi(e, m|s)` that must be trained concurrently with the policy (Algorithm 1, Line 10). This training requires the variational model to observe sufficient successful and unsuccessful exploration-memory configurations to distinguish which configurations are genuinely associated with task success. The paper describes this as a REINFORCE-based optimization of the KL-divergence objective (Equation 8), but does not specify how many gradient steps per RL epoch are needed for the reward model, nor how the model's accuracy evolves over training.

**The consequence.** In environments with very sparse rewards — where successful trajectories are rare, especially early in training — the variational model may have insufficient positive examples to learn a meaningful posterior over exploration-memory configurations. The density estimates `q_phi(e, m|s)` would then be driven primarily by the KL prior term, collapsing toward the prior distribution `p(e, m|s)` and failing to distinguish useful exploration from useless exploration. This would cause the exploratory reward to become uninformative noise, degrading to the same performance as the "w/o exploratory reward" ablation (Table 8). More subtly, because the reward model is trained on-policy (using trajectories from the current `pi_theta`), its accuracy is coupled to the policy's current exploration behavior. If the policy explores poorly early in training, the reward model learns from poor exploration data and reinforces poor exploration — a feedback loop that could cause premature convergence to suboptimal exploration strategies. The paper does not analyze this potential instability.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The reward model loss curves (Figure 13) show convergence within 600–800 steps across all environments, indicating stable optimization. However, convergence of the loss does not guarantee accuracy — the loss measures how well `q_phi` fits the observed data, but if the observed data contains primarily failed trajectories, the model may accurately learn that *no* exploration-memory configuration leads to success, which is true of the data but useless as a reward signal. The paper does not report the reward model's calibration, its ability to rank exploration-memory configurations by true success probability, or the correlation between `R_explore` and actual downstream task success.

The online reward comparison (Appendix B, Figures 3–4) provides indirect evidence: EAPO's learned reward achieves comparable performance to the online reward with ~10 rollout samples, suggesting the learned reward is reasonably accurate when enough successful trajectories are available. But this comparison is performed at the *end* of training, after the policy has learned to succeed at a reasonable rate, and does not characterize reward model quality during early training when success is rare.

The runtime analysis (Figure 21) shows that reward model training accounts for ~18% of per-step time (304.6 seconds out of 1636.5 for the 8B model on AndroidWorld). This is a non-trivial overhead that would compound in environments requiring longer training.

**Mitigation status.** The paper partially mitigates the sample efficiency concern through the Bayesian formulation — by modeling a *distribution* rather than a point estimate, the variational approach should be more robust to limited data than direct Monte Carlo estimation. However, this robustness is not empirically validated. The authors do not report experiments on how EAPO's performance varies with the density of successful trajectories in the training data, nor do they propose mechanisms for bootstrapping the reward model from offline data or from a separate exploration corpus. The limitation of on-policy reward model training creating potential feedback loops is not discussed.

### Limitation 3: The Rollback SFT Stage Assumes Access to a Teacher LLM and Reversible Environments

**The assumption or constraint.** The SFT stage (Section 5.1) requires collecting an expert rollback dataset `D` by "prompting a teacher LLM with a state `s'` and its previous state `s` to generate the rollback action `a`." This makes three implicit assumptions: (1) a capable teacher LLM exists that can generate correct rollback actions for the target environment, (2) the environment supports rollback — there exists a sequence of actions that can recover a previous state from any reachable state, and (3) the teacher LLM can identify these actions given state descriptions. The paper does not specify which teacher LLM was used, how many demonstrations were collected, or what fraction of teacher-generated rollback actions were actually correct.

**The consequence.** In environments where rollback is not universally possible — for example, tasks with destructive actions (deleting a file, sending an irreversible message, completing a purchase), tasks where state changes are cumulative and cannot be undone (filling a form where earlier entries constrain later options), or tasks where returning to a previous state requires navigating through intermediate states that are themselves different from the original — the SFT stage cannot produce a complete rollback dataset, and the agent will encounter states during RL where rollback fails. When rollback fails, the credit assignment problem the SFT stage was designed to solve returns: the exploratory action and its downstream consequences are entangled, and the agent may learn to avoid exploration in states where rollback is unreliable, even when exploration would be informative.

More practically, the requirement for a teacher LLM limits applicability: for novel or proprietary environments where no capable teacher LLM exists (e.g., specialized industrial control interfaces, legacy software systems, environments requiring physical interaction knowledge), the SFT dataset cannot be automatically generated. The paper provides no alternative method for teaching rollback — e.g., through RL with a reverse-action reward, through environment resets, or through human demonstrations.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper acknowledges the coupling between rollback and exploration accuracy (Section D.7.6): "exploration accuracy and rollback capability are inherently coupled. Even if the agent learns to explore effectively, the acquired information cannot be properly utilized without the ability to rollback to previous decision points." However, the paper does *not* report an explicit "w/o SFT" ablation — it notes there is a "noticeable performance drop" but provides no numbers, making it impossible to quantify how much performance depends on the SFT stage versus the RL stage. The case study (Figures 22–30, Steps 5–6) shows rollback working successfully in one instance, but does not report statistics on rollback success rate across the evaluation set.

The cross-environment transfer experiment (Table 6) partially addresses this: if the SFT stage learned AndroidWorld-specific rollback actions, transfer to OSWorld should fail because the UI elements and navigation patterns differ. The fact that transfer succeeds suggests the rollback skill is at least partially generalizable (e.g., the agent learns to use generic "navigate back" actions rather than memorizing environment-specific button coordinates). But this is inference, not measurement — the paper does not analyze *which* rollback actions the agent uses in the target environment.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not mitigate this limitation. The SFT stage is presented as a prerequisite without discussion of its failure modes, the sensitivity of downstream RL performance to SFT quality, or alternatives for environments without reversible state transitions. The requirement for a teacher LLM is not acknowledged as a limitation, despite being a practical barrier to applying EAPO in domains where no capable teacher exists.

### Limitation 4: The Exploration-Aware Grouping Depends on Exact State Matching, Limiting Applicability to Continuous or High-Dimensional State Spaces

**The assumption or constraint.** The exploration-aware grouping mechanism (Section 5.2, Equation 13) clusters transitions by exact equality of environment state: `s_t^i = s`. This requires a discrete, enumerable state space where two observations can be deterministically compared. For the evaluated environments — text-based household tasks (ALFWorld), web shopping with product identifiers (WebShop), and GUI-based tasks where states are distinguished by screen content and app context (AndroidWorld, OSWorld) — this is feasible because the environments provide structured state representations or because screenshots can be hashed or compared via UI element matching.

**The consequence.** In environments with continuous state spaces (robotics with joint angles, autonomous driving with LiDAR point clouds, game environments with pixel-level observations) or high-dimensional state spaces where no two observations are ever exactly identical, the grouping mechanism fails silently. If `P(s_t^i = s) ≈ 0` for all `i ≠ j`, then every transition cluster `G(s, ν)` has size 1, and the per-cluster advantage normalization (Equation 14) degenerates to no normalization at all — every action is its own reference group. This would eliminate the credit assignment benefit that the grouping mechanism provides, reverting EAPO to the same exploration-undervaluation problem that standard GRPO suffers from (Figure 19, where "w/o grouping" causes exploration to collapse). The paper provides no mechanism for soft or approximate state matching (e.g., state embeddings, clustering by similarity, discretization via autoencoders) that would extend the grouping to continuous domains.

Even in the discrete environments evaluated, exact state matching has a subtle failure mode: two states that are functionally equivalent for the task but textually different (e.g., the same web page rendered with a different ad banner, or the same Android screen with a different notification) would not be clustered together, needlessly fragmenting the groups and reducing the statistical power of within-group advantage estimation. The paper does not discuss this sensitivity to state representation granularity.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The group size distribution analysis (Figure 16) shows that even in the discrete environments studied, a substantial fraction of groups have size 1 throughout training (16–22% by iterations 750–1000). This indicates that exact state matching already produces fragmented groups in these environments. In a continuous environment, this fraction would approach 100%, and the grouping mechanism would provide no benefit. The paper provides no experiments varying the granularity of the state representation to test sensitivity.

The paper also does not discuss whether the state representation used for matching includes the full observation (screenshot + task context) or only a subset (e.g., the current app and screen identifier, stripped of dynamic content). Different choices could dramatically affect group sizes and advantage estimation quality, but no ablation explores this.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation. The grouping mechanism is defined assuming exact state matching without discussion of its failure in continuous domains or sensitivity to representation granularity. Since all evaluated environments happen to have discrete state representations, the limitation is invisible in the reported results but would block application to many important agentic domains (robotics, game-playing, physical simulation). This is a scope limitation that the paper should acknowledge but does not.

### Limitation 5: Single Model Family and the Absence of Difficulty-Stratified Analysis Obscure When EAPO Helps

**The assumption or constraint.** All experiments use Qwen3 (text-based) and Qwen3-VL (GUI-based) as the base model family (Section 6.1). The paper claims these models are "suitable for real-world deployment" but provides no evidence that the benefits of EAPO generalize to other model families (e.g., LLaMA, Gemma, DeepSeek) with different pretraining distributions, tokenizers, or architectural choices. The number of environments (4) and model scales (3 per environment) is reasonable for a single paper, but the absence of cross-family replication means the reported gains are contingent on the specific interaction between EAPO's training procedure and Qwen3's pretrained representations.

Furthermore, the paper reports only aggregate success rates per environment (Tables 2, 5) without any difficulty-stratified analysis. Unlike the test-time compute scaling paper referenced in the example, which organized results by difficulty quintile and revealed qualitatively different behavior across difficulty levels (beam search *hurts* on easy problems, *helps* on medium problems), EAPO's results provide no information about whether the gains are concentrated on easy tasks (where the base model already has some competence and exploration refines it), spread uniformly, or absent on the hardest tasks (where the base model may lack the fundamental capability to succeed regardless of exploration).

**The consequence.** Without model-family diversity, a practitioner using a non-Qwen3 base model cannot know whether to expect the 20–60% gains reported in the paper or substantially smaller (or zero) gains. If EAPO's mechanisms rely on Qwen3-specific properties — e.g., the quality of its in-context learning (for the exploration-memory conditioning), its calibration (for the variational reward model to produce meaningful density estimates), or its susceptibility to the structured output format — the method may not transfer.

Without difficulty stratification, a practitioner deploying EAPO cannot anticipate the failure modes. If EAPO's gains are primarily on easy tasks while providing no benefit on the hardest tasks (as is the case for many test-time compute methods), then deploying EAPO in a setting where task difficulty is uniformly high would yield disappointing returns, even though the aggregate number looks good on a mixed-difficulty benchmark. Conversely, if EAPO helps uniformly, the method is more robust than a typical test-time compute method, which is an important scientific finding to establish. The paper's aggregate reporting leaves both possibilities open.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper compares against diverse closed-source and open-source models (Tables 2, 4) to establish that EAPO-2B is competitive with much larger models, but these comparisons do not test whether training a non-Qwen3 model with EAPO would produce similar relative gains over its base performance. The four environments span text-based and GUI-based tasks, providing some domain diversity, but they all share the property of having well-defined, enumerable state spaces and sparse binary success rewards — they do not test generalization to continuous-control, partially-observable, or dense-reward settings.

The paper provides learning curves per environment (Figure 2), which show convergence patterns but not difficulty-stratified performance. The case study (Figures 22–30) selects a task where exploration visibly helps, which is a form of cherry-picking unless accompanied by statistics on how representative that task is. The absence of any difficulty binning — even using a simple proxy like task length or base model zero-shot accuracy — is a significant omission given the paper's stated motivation of enabling agents to "distinguish when exploration is necessary" (Section 1).

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation. No experiments use non-Qwen3 base models. No results are stratified by task difficulty, length, or the base model's initial competence. The limitation section (Section 7) mentions only the structured representation constraint and does not acknowledge generalizability concerns. For a method that makes strong claims about learning adaptive, selective exploration, the absence of analysis showing *where* the adaptation occurs (easy vs. hard tasks, familiar vs. novel states) leaves a central part of the paper's thesis empirically unvalidated.

### Limitation 6: Test-Time Exploration Increases Interaction Steps Without a Guaranteed Efficiency-Accuracy Tradeoff Analysis

**The assumption or constraint.** EAPO's agents take more interaction steps per task than baseline methods. Table 9 reports average steps: on AndroidWorld, EAPO takes 22.7 steps versus 16.9 (GRPO), 17.5 (DAPO), 19.1 (GiGPO), and 17.2 (LAMER). On ALFWorld, EAPO takes 58.5 steps versus 38.2 (GiGPO) and 43.9 (GRPO). The paper frames this as acceptable because the γ discount mechanism "guides the agent to carry out exploration only when the anticipated utility 'outweighs' the latency cost" (Section D.7.7) and the performance gains are large.

**The consequence.** The aggregate success rate metric used throughout the paper (Tables 2, 5) does not incorporate the cost of extra steps. This is standard practice in RL benchmarking, but it obscures a critical deployment consideration: if EAPO achieves 76.4% success on AndroidWorld by taking 22.7 steps while LAMER achieves 61.3% by taking 17.2 steps, the per-success-step efficiency is `76.4/22.7 ≈ 3.37` successes per 100 steps for EAPO versus `61.3/17.2 ≈ 3.56` for LAMER — LAMER is actually *more efficient* per interaction despite lower absolute success rate. A practitioner optimizing for throughput (tasks completed per unit time or per API cost) might prefer LAMER over EAPO if the cost of additional steps outweighs the value of additional successes.

More importantly, the γ discount controls exploration depth but does not provide a calibrated efficiency knob. A practitioner who wants to trade off some accuracy for lower latency cannot simply adjust γ and expect predictable results — the γ sweep (Figure 10) shows that γ = 0.9 is the unique optimum, and both γ = 0.8 and γ = 1.0 degrade performance. There is no "efficiency mode" for EAPO that reduces steps while maintaining most of the accuracy gain. The method is optimized for maximum success rate, not for operating points along an accuracy-efficiency Pareto frontier.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 7 shows that γ directly controls average steps: on ALFWorld, γ = 0.9 yields 22.5 steps while γ = 1.0 yields 43.9 steps (nearly double). But Figure 10 shows that γ = 1.0 has *lower* success rate than γ = 0.9, meaning the extra steps are not merely unnecessary but actively harmful. This suggests the γ mechanism prevents the worst over-exploration but does not provide a tunable exploration budget — it is a one-dimensional control that is optimally set at a single value, not a spectrum that practitioners can adjust based on deployment constraints.

The paper does not report alternative efficiency metrics: success rate per step, success rate at a fixed step budget (e.g., accuracy if all trajectories are truncated at 15 steps), or the distribution of trajectory lengths for successful vs. failed episodes. Without these, a practitioner cannot determine whether EAPO's exploration is concentrated on tasks where it eventually succeeds (justifying the extra steps) or spread across all tasks including inevitable failures (wasting steps).

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges the step increase in Section D.7.7: "our method indeed has additional runtime due to exploration. To avoid meaningless exploration, we introduce a discount factor γ so that our method maintains only a modest increase (at most 10) in average step." However, this framing treats the step increase as a solved problem (because γ prevents "meaningless" exploration) without analyzing whether the remaining exploration is optimal from an efficiency standpoint. The paper does not propose a mechanism for practitioners to explicitly trade off accuracy against step count, nor does it report any efficiency-aware metrics. This leaves the practical deployment calculus — whether EAPO's additional steps are worth the accuracy gains in a specific application — to guesswork based on aggregate averages.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper introduces a **conceptual reframing of agent exploration from an action-selection problem to a memory-enrichment problem**, and in doing so provides the first principled framework for teaching LLM agents when to explore rather than merely how to explore. The shift is not merely terminological — it has concrete methodological consequences that distinguish EAPO from prior work and open new research directions.

**From exploration-as-diversity to exploration-as-information-acquisition.** Prior work on exploration for LLM agents has operated under an implicit assumption that exploration means trying diverse actions — whether through entropy bonuses (Zhang et al., 2024, 2025a), decoding heuristics (OverRIDE, Min-p), or multi-trajectory sampling (LAMER; Jiang et al., 2025). The common thread is that exploration is about *increasing the variance of behavior*, with the hope that some of that variance produces useful outcomes. EAPO challenges this assumption at its foundation: exploration is not about doing different things; it is about **resolving specific informational gaps** that prevent the agent from making correct decisions. The `<explore>` and `<memory>` channels make this explicit by creating separate output spaces for the agent to articulate what it doesn't know, what it plans to do about that ignorance, and what it learned.

This reframing resolves a tension that has been simmering in the agent literature without clear articulation. Entropy-based exploration methods (Vanlioglu, 2025; Xu et al., 2025) encourage the agent to be uncertain — but uncertainty is a model property, not an environment property. An agent can be highly uncertain about a state it has seen a thousand times (poor calibration) and completely confident about a genuinely novel state (overconfidence). The variational reward model's density estimate `q_phi(e, m|s)` bypasses this conflation entirely: it scores exploration-memory configurations based on their empirical association with task success, regardless of whether the policy's output distribution happens to have high entropy at that state. If a particular exploratory action consistently precedes successful trajectories — even if the policy is already "confident" about it — the reward model assigns it high value. If a high-entropy action never leads to useful information, the reward model assigns it low value regardless of the entropy.

**Resolving contradictory findings about test-time exploration.** The paper's results help explain why prior work on test-time exploration has produced conflicting conclusions. Methods like Tree-of-Thoughts (Yao et al., 2023) show that exploration through branching can improve reasoning performance, while Table 1 demonstrates that naive exploration incorporated into GRPO *degrades* performance on AndroidWorld (e.g., Qwen-VL-8B drops from 56.1 to 52.9 with GRPO exploration). These findings appear contradictory only if we assume exploration is a monolithic concept. EAPO's framework reveals that the contradiction dissolves when we distinguish between **indiscriminate exploration** (exploring everywhere uniformly, which the GRPO-based baselines in Table 1 suffer from because they lack mechanisms to evaluate exploration utility) and **selective exploration** (exploring only where the variational model predicts informational gain, which EAPO enables). The same agent can benefit from exploration or be harmed by it depending on whether exploration is guided by a learned utility function — this is a genuinely new insight, not an incremental refinement.

**The credit assignment problem becomes visible.** Before EAPO, the systematic undervaluation of exploratory actions in policy gradient methods was largely invisible because most methods didn't try to credit exploration separately from execution. LAMER sidesteps the problem by not assigning per-action credit at all — it treats the entire multi-trajectory episode as an in-context learning window. GRPO and its variants treat all actions uniformly within a group, and the fact that exploratory actions get negative advantages is hidden inside aggregate performance metrics that reflect the final task completion rate. EAPO makes the credit assignment failure **directly observable** through the ablation experiments: when exploration-aware grouping is removed, exploration degree rises and then collapses (Figure 19), demonstrating empirically that standard GRPO's advantage estimation is structurally biased against early-phase actions. This is a diagnostic contribution — the visitation depth `kappa(s)` is not just a clustering trick but a measurement tool that reveals how policy gradient methods handle (or mishandle) phased behavior.

**Which research directions become more attractive.** The paper's demonstration that a learned exploration utility function (the variational reward model) can guide test-time behavior opens several lines of inquiry that were previously speculative. The idea that agents can learn a *prior* over useful exploration strategies — abstract knowledge about what kinds of information-gathering actions tend to pay off — and apply that prior to novel environments is particularly fertile. The generalization result from AndroidWorld to OSWorld (Table 6, ~1.5 percentage point drop) provides preliminary evidence for this transfer, but the mechanisms are uncharacterized. Understanding whether the exploration prior captures abstract meta-strategies (e.g., "explore menu structures before committing to actions," "probe interactive elements to understand their affordances") versus environment-specific heuristics (e.g., "Android settings are usually under a gear icon") would have direct implications for building agents that can be deployed in genuinely open-ended environments.

**Which research directions become less attractive.** The paper's negative results on naive exploration (Table 1) and the online reward approach (Appendix B, Figures 3–4) should dampen enthusiasm for two common practices: (1) simply adding exploration bonuses or entropy terms to standard RL objectives and hoping agents learn to explore adaptively, and (2) using Monte Carlo rollouts to estimate the value of information without addressing the fundamental variance problem. The online reward experiment shows that even with 10 rollout samples, the estimate remains noisier than EAPO's learned density function — and 10 rollouts per exploratory action is computationally prohibitive for deployment. This suggests that **learned world models or density estimators** (like the variational reward model) are a more promising path to scalable exploration than direct Monte Carlo estimation, a conclusion that aligns with trends in model-based RL but had not been established for LLM agents.

**Magnitude of the shift.** EAPO is not a paradigm shift in the sense of introducing an entirely new problem formulation — it builds on standard MDPs (Section 3), GRPO (Section 3, Equation 2), and variational inference (Appendix A). Rather, it is a **significant reframing** that identifies a specific structural problem (credit assignment across exploration and execution phases) and provides a complete solution stack (structured outputs + variational reward model + visitation-depth grouping + rollback SFT) that addresses it. The reframing is significant because it changes *what we optimize for*: instead of optimizing for task completion alone, EAPO optimizes for task completion while maintaining the agent's ability to recognize and resolve its own uncertainty. This dual objective — performance plus self-awareness — is more ambitious than standard RL and more aligned with how humans operate in unfamiliar environments.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Characterizing the exploration prior: what exactly does the variational reward model learn?** The paper demonstrates that `q_phi(e, m|s)` produces useful exploration reward signals and that policies trained with this reward transfer across environments (AndroidWorld → OSWorld). However, the paper provides no analysis of *what patterns* the reward model captures. Does `q_phi` learn to recognize states that are distributionally different from training states (novelty detection)? Does it learn to identify states where the policy's action distribution has high variance (uncertainty detection)? Does it memorize which specific exploration strategies worked in training tasks and apply them to similar-looking states (instance-based generalization)? Or does it capture something more abstract — like the principle that exploring interactive UI elements (buttons, menus, text fields) is generally more informative than exploring static content? A strong follow-up would train EAPO on a controlled set of tasks that systematically vary along dimensions like UI layout novelty, action-effect predictability, and task complexity, then analyze the reward model's density estimates across these dimensions to reverse-engineer what features drive high `q_phi` values. This would transform the reward model from a black-box scoring function into an interpretable exploration heuristic, enabling practitioners to diagnose when the reward model is likely to generalize and when it will fail.

**Can the structured output format be learned rather than prescribed?** The paper acknowledges (Section 7) that the `<explore>` and `<memory>` tags are "manually specified throughout training" and that this "may restrict the expressiveness of exploration strategies." A natural extension is to ask whether the structure itself can emerge from a less constrained training procedure. Specifically: initialize the agent with a prompt that says "you may output additional reasoning before your action" but without predefined tags, train with the exploration reward applied to *all* tokens (not just specific tagged spans), and measure whether the agent spontaneously develops structured exploration-memory-action separation. If it does, the manual template is a useful bootstrap but not a fundamental requirement. If it doesn't — if the exploration reward signal gets diluted across unstructured output and fails to produce selective exploration — then the credit assignment benefit of structural separation is load-bearing, and future work should focus on automatic structure discovery (e.g., training a lightweight router that dynamically allocates output tokens to exploration, memory, or action channels based on estimated information need).

**How does EAPO interact with model scale — is there a scaling law for exploration capability?** The paper tests three model scales (1.7B/2B, 4B, 8B) and finds that absolute performance increases with scale but the *relative advantage* over baselines shows no consistent trend (+13–21pp across environments and scales; Table 5). This is insufficient to characterize scaling behavior. Does EAPO's benefit grow with scale (larger models have more capacity to learn sophisticated exploration strategies), shrink with scale (larger models already have better zero-shot environment understanding and need less explicit exploration), or plateau (the exploration problem is fundamentally about credit assignment, not capacity)? A scaling study spanning an order of magnitude more scales — e.g., 0.5B to 70B parameters — with careful measurement of both absolute performance and the exploration degree at convergence would answer this. If EAPO's relative benefit shrinks at larger scales, the method is most valuable for deploying small models (consistent with the paper's emphasis on "suitable for real-world deployment"). If the benefit grows, EAPO may be a key ingredient for pushing frontier models further on agentic tasks.

**Stress-testing the generalization claim with deliberately adversarial transfer.** The AndroidWorld → OSWorld transfer (Table 6) shows minimal degradation, but both environments share fundamental interaction paradigms (GUI-based, click/scroll/type actions, similar app categories). A stronger test of domain-invariant exploration would be cross-domain transfer between fundamentally different environments: train on ALFWorld (text-based household tasks) and evaluate on AndroidWorld (GUI-based mobile tasks), or train on WebShop (structured product search) and evaluate on OSWorld (open-ended desktop tasks). If EAPO's exploration strategy genuinely captures abstract principles ("identify interactive elements, probe them, remember the results") rather than GUI-specific heuristics, the transfer should still provide some benefit over the zero-shot baseline, even if the absolute performance is lower. If cross-domain transfer fails completely, the "domain-invariant exploration" claim is too strong, and the practical benefit of EAPO is environment-specific. This experiment would also reveal whether the SFT rollback stage is the bottleneck for transfer (rollback actions are environment-specific) or whether the RL-learned exploration policy itself transfers.

**Replacing exact state matching with learned similarity for continuous domains.** The exploration-aware grouping mechanism (Equation 13) requires exact state equality (`s_t^i = s`), which fails in continuous or high-dimensional state spaces where no two observations are identical. A straightforward but important extension is to replace the indicator function `I[s_k = s_t]` with a learned similarity metric `sim(phi(s_k), phi(s_t))` where `phi` is an embedding network trained to cluster functionally equivalent states. This could be trained using temporal proximity (states close in time are likely functionally related), action-effect similarity (states where the same action produces similar next states are likely equivalent), or reward similarity (states with similar value functions are likely equivalent). The key empirical question: does soft clustering with learned similarity preserve the credit assignment benefit of exact matching (preventing exploration undervaluation), or does the softness re-introduce the bias that exact matching was designed to eliminate? This extension would determine whether EAPO's core mechanism can scale beyond the discrete-state environments studied in the paper to robotics, game-playing, and other continuous-control domains.

**Combining EAPO with test-time compute scaling for joint optimization.** The paper focuses on exploration *during a single trajectory* — the agent takes exploratory actions, rolls back, and applies what it learned. This is complementary to test-time compute scaling methods that generate multiple complete trajectories and select among them (Snell et al., 2024; Yao et al., 2023). A natural combination would use EAPO's exploration policy *within each* candidate trajectory (making each trajectory more informed by selective exploration) and then use best-of-N or verifier-based selection *across* trajectories to pick the most successful one. This raises an optimization question the paper doesn't address: given a fixed total interaction budget, how should it be split between within-trajectory exploration (EAPO's mechanism) and across-trajectory sampling (test-time scaling)? If the optimal split depends on task difficulty — easy tasks benefit more from within-trajectory refinement while hard tasks need the diversity of parallel sampling — this would connect EAPO's framework to the compute-optimal scaling literature in a productive way.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Small-model deployment for GUI automation on edge devices.** The paper's most directly actionable result for practitioners is that a 2B-parameter model trained with EAPO achieves 76.4% on AndroidWorld (Table 2), outperforming the 7B-parameter DART-GUI-7B (58.0%) and the 72B-parameter DeepMiner-Mano-72B (68.5%). For mobile GUI automation — where an on-device assistant needs to navigate apps, fill forms, or adjust settings on behalf of a user — deploying a 2B model rather than a 72B model is the difference between running locally on a phone (feasible) and requiring cloud offloading with its attendant latency, privacy, and connectivity concerns. EAPO's training procedure (rollback SFT + exploration-aware RL) can be applied to any base vision-language model, making this a drop-in upgrade for existing mobile agent pipelines. The additional inference cost — EAPO agents take ~4 extra steps per task on average (22.7 vs. 17–19 for baselines on AndroidWorld; Table 9) — translates to roughly 20% more inference tokens, a modest overhead for a 30–50% relative improvement in task completion rate. For a mobile assistant handling dozens of tasks per day, this tradeoff is clearly favorable.

**Data generation for self-improving agent pipelines.** When using LLMs to generate training data for themselves — a common paradigm in agent research where successful trajectories are collected and used for subsequent fine-tuning — the *quality* of generated trajectories matters enormously. Trajectories where the agent succeeded despite making uninformed decisions (getting lucky) teach less than trajectories where the agent succeeded because it systematically gathered information before acting. EAPO's exploration mechanism naturally produces higher-quality trajectories: the agent explicitly documents what it explored and what it learned in the `<explore>` and `<memory>` channels, making these trajectories more informative training data for downstream models. A practitioner building a self-improvement loop could: (1) train an agent with EAPO, (2) collect successful trajectories with their structured exploration-memory annotations, (3) use these trajectories to fine-tune a larger or faster model that internalizes the exploration strategy through imitation rather than RL. The generalization result (Table 6) is particularly relevant here: if exploration strategies transfer across environments, trajectories collected in one domain could bootstrap agent capability in related domains without requiring environment-specific RL training.

**Cost-efficient batch processing for web automation and testing.** Organizations running automated web testing, form-filling, or data extraction at scale deploy agents that navigate websites, interact with UI elements, and extract information. These agents routinely encounter novel page layouts (A/B tests, regional variations, seasonal redesigns) where a purely reactive policy fails because the UI elements are in unfamiliar positions. EAPO's selective exploration directly addresses this: rather than exploring every page uniformly (wasting interaction steps and API costs), the agent explores only when the variational reward model — which has learned to recognize states where the current memory is insufficient — signals uncertainty. The 4× efficiency analogy from the reference paper summary (where compute-optimal test-time scaling matched best-of-256 with only 64 generations) has a parallel here: EAPO's selective exploration achieves higher success rates while only increasing average steps by ~20% (Table 9), suggesting a qualitatively similar pattern where targeted investment of additional computation yields disproportionate returns. For high-volume web automation with tens of thousands of tasks per day, reducing failure rates from 40% to 25% while only increasing per-task steps by ~4 represents substantial operational savings in human-in-the-loop intervention costs.

**Adaptive exploration for scientific or research agents interacting with novel tools.** The paper's environments (ALFWorld, WebShop, AndroidWorld, OSWorld) are consumer-facing, but the underlying capability — learning to explore unfamiliar interfaces to gather task-relevant information — is directly applicable to scientific agents that must interact with research software, databases, or experimental apparatus with unfamiliar interfaces. A research assistant agent tasked with "find all papers citing Method X and extract their reported performance on benchmark Y" might encounter a new academic search engine, a library website with an unfamiliar API, or a data repository with a novel query interface. EAPO's learned exploration prior — generalizing from AndroidWorld to OSWorld with minimal degradation — suggests that an agent trained on a diverse set of web and software interaction tasks could develop a general "interface exploration" capability that transfers to previously unseen scientific tools. The key practical requirement is the SFT rollback dataset: for each new tool domain, a teacher LLM must provide demonstrations of how to recover from exploratory actions (clicking back buttons, clearing form fields, navigating home). For many research software tools with standard UI conventions, these demonstrations would be straightforward to collect and generalize across tools.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper does not explicitly frame EAPO as a choice among named alternatives with clearly articulated tradeoffs — it is presented as a training framework that uniformly improves over existing methods across the evaluated conditions. However, the experimental results and ablation studies implicitly define the conditions under which EAPO is likely to be beneficial versus when simpler alternatives may suffice:

- **Prefer EAPO when the deployment environment contains states that deviate from the training distribution.** The OSWorld and AndroidWorld environments are characterized by diverse UI layouts where the agent cannot memorize action sequences and must instead understand the current interface. The fact that EAPO's gains are largest on these environments (+15.5pp on AndroidWorld, +10.5pp on OSWorld, averaged across model scales; Table 5) compared to text-based environments (+14–18pp on ALFWorld and WebShop) suggests the method is particularly valuable when distribution shift is common.

- **Prefer standard GRPO or DAPO when the environment is static and the optimal policy is purely reactive.** If the agent's tasks involve navigating a fixed interface where the correct actions at each state are invariant and well-represented in training, EAPO's exploration mechanism adds overhead — both in training (23% more per-step runtime; Figure 20) and inference (~20% more steps; Table 9) — without providing proportional benefit. The `gamma` sweep (Figure 10) shows that at low gamma values (0.5–0.7), EAPO degrades to essentially standard GRPO performance, confirming that the exploration mechanism is unnecessary when the base reactive policy already succeeds.

- **Prefer EAPO when the agent must operate autonomously without human fallback.** In deployment scenarios where a human cannot intervene when the agent is stuck, the cost of failure is high, and the cost of extra exploration steps is acceptable. The method's ability to recover from poor initial decisions through rollback and re-planning (demonstrated in the case study, Steps 5–7, Figures 26–28) provides robustness that purely feedforward policies lack.

- **Consider LAMER or other meta-RL approaches when per-task latency is the primary constraint and some failure rate is acceptable.** LAMER achieves comparable per-step efficiency to EAPO (17.2 steps vs. 22.7 on AndroidWorld; Table 9) while achieving 61.3% accuracy. In throughput-constrained settings where completing more tasks per unit time matters more than maximizing success rate, LAMER's lower step count may be preferable despite lower absolute accuracy. The paper does not provide Pareto frontier analyses that would make this tradeoff quantitative, so the choice is currently informed by aggregate metrics rather than calibrated per-application optimization.
