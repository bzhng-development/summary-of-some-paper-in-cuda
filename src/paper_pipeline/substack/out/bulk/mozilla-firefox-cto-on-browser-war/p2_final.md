Here are the takeaways from this Bobby Holley interview (went from intern to CTO of Mozilla Firefox, originally led the effort to uplift Servo’s CSS engine):

**Starting at Mozilla and the early open-source culture**

Bobby’s first day at Mozilla was a shock after a structured Nvidia internship. His manager, platform director Damon Secor—who “embodied the dude from the Big Lebowski in all of the best ways”—told him to “get on IRC and get on Bugzilla and start figuring out some things to do.” This was deliberate. Mozilla emerged as an open-source project where the process selected for a particular type of person who “really just needed the bare minimum and was very motivated to just go in and figure things out.” The management philosophy was “let’s get the right people together and let’s just get out of the way.” He was inspired by contributors who had started working on the browser from their college dorm rooms just to make it crash less. One figure loomed especially large: Boris Zbarsky, one of Mozilla’s first distinguished engineers. Bobby only knew him on IRC for years, but Boris seemed to own “like half” of the roughly 10-million-line Netscape-inherited codebase, acting as the person of last resort who would simultaneously carry on deep technical conversations across five different IRC channels. “He was just a machine.”

**The browser wars: from allies to “a hundred oopses”**

When Bobby started in June 2008, it was the peak of Google and Mozilla’s partnership. Mozilla interns got badges to eat at Google’s campus; snacks in the kitchen were rumored to be delivered by Google facilities; and the search deal made Mozilla financially sustainable. “There was really this sense that Google and Mozilla together were working to unlock the future of the open web.” Shortly thereafter, Google announced Chrome. Initially framed as a friendly move to take share from Internet Explorer, the relationship soured gradually—not through a single villainous decision, but through organizational dynamics. Google poured hundreds of millions (effectively billions, counting the effective cost of ads on google.com) into Chrome distribution. Jonathan Nightingale, a Firefox VP, later called it “the year of a hundred oopses”—Google would show Firefox users a banner on google.com saying “Download Chrome, it’s better,” always claiming it was unintentional, then fixing it a release later after the metrics had already been won. Simultaneously, Firefox fell behind on architecture (multi-process, a robust plugin architecture for Flash, native video playback) and Mozilla made a big, resource-diverting bet on a mobile OS (Firefox OS) that ultimately didn’t work—exactly when those resources were most needed to compete with Chrome.

**Why people worked for free**

Before the Google funding, Mozilla was sustained by people working nights and weekends. Bobby chalks it up to three factors. First, at that time there were simply far fewer opportunities to contribute to something major and meaningful in open source. Second, the codebase itself was “an amazing intellectual challenge”—Boris Zbarsky, a math major at MIT, found it “in some ways, like even harder.” Third, and most important, was the mission: the idea that the internet should be “a global public resource that is open and accessible to all” was not a given during the Microsoft-dominated nineties. Mozilla wasn’t just an advocacy organization; it was actually building a browser that gave users control—popup blocking, tab browsing, ad blocking—and “creating that reality and making the world better in that way was very inspiring.”

**Biggest projects: security, Slaughterhouse, and the Bugzilla breach**

Bobby was drawn to “the hardest and gnarliest parts of the code.” The ownership model was simple: “as soon as you show up and you start working on something and you fix one thing, you start looking around and you’re like, ‘Hey, who owns this?’ And they’re like, ‘you do now.’” He gravitated toward the security-sensitive DOM bindings code (XPConnect), responsible for isolating webpages from each other and from the browser chrome. There was a constant cat-and-mouse game with an external community of security researchers, including an anonymous figure named “MozBugRa4” who, rumor had it, lived in a country where security research was illegal. Bobby launched a large cross-functional effort he called **Slaughterhouse**—a joke about “chrome object wrappers” they needed to kill—to fix a class of vulnerabilities they couldn’t robustly patch. The value of getting ahead of security became literal when Bugzilla was compromised by a threat actor (likely via a credential leak before two-factor auth). The attacker exfiltrated roughly 45 confidential security bugs—but because Slaughterhouse had just wrapped up, “we had fixed all but two of them.” In a surreal twist, Bobby had recognized an in-the-wild exploit’s “fingerprint” as belonging to one of their own trusted researchers and emailed him accusing him of double-crossing them—only to discover an hour later that Mozilla itself had been the leak. The researcher, convinced his devices were deeply compromised, had already fled off-grid; they eventually got a call from “a bowling alley in upstate New York” to explain it was Mozilla’s bad and help him resurface.

**No performance reviews and the bottom-up culture**

In the early days, Mozilla didn’t really have levels or formal performance reviews. The title Bobby chose for himself was “Negative Entropy.” The governance was built around a module ownership system with access control called “Despot,” designed to insulate against executive power. It was “very much a notion of distributed and decentralized decision making.” The model worked well when caliber and dedication were high, but if someone was doing a poor job owning a piece of code, “everybody just kind of lived with that.” Bobby has spent recent years figuring out how to preserve the best parts of that technical culture while addressing its shortcomings—preserving the bottom-up initiative and sense of ownership while adding necessary structure.

**Rust, Servo, and leapfrogging Chrome**

By the early 2010s, Mozilla had structural problems that incremental work couldn’t fix. Two in particular were rooted in C++: memory-safety vulnerabilities (any one of which was a potential “webpage takes over your computer” scenario) and the impossibility of safe multi-threading to exploit the rising number of CPU cores. Mozilla sponsored research that became Rust, a language with static compile-time guarantees against memory-safety issues and data races. Rust co-evolved with Servo, a parallel browser engine intended to “leapfrog Chrome” by being both memory-safe and concurrent. But a team of a dozen people on Servo was competing with Google’s hundreds of engineers continuously improving Chromium. Every year, there was more and more for Servo to catch up with. Around 2014-2015, the decision was made to uplift pieces of Servo into Firefox rather than wait for a full engine replacement. Bobby led the project to integrate Servo’s parallel CSS engine into Gecko—having Rust code traverse a C++ DOM with “no tooling for it; we had to build it all ourselves.” The result, shipping as part of Firefox Quantum, improved rendering time on amazon.com by about 25% and remains “the fastest CSS engine in the market.” Every step of the way felt like “that’s definitely never gonna work”—right up until it did. “If we just coordinate and we do all the technical effort and we make the right bets, we can win.”

**Career progression and the model of impact**

When Bobby started, Mozilla didn’t really have levels; by the time of Firefox Quantum, he had reached senior staff. The successful launch brought him to principal engineer—one of fewer than five at the time. His long-term dream was to be like Boris. His model for impact was to “contribute to as many parts of the code as possible and just be prolific”—moving from DOM bindings to media playback to multi-process to Rust/CSS to WebRender to mobile, always guided by “whatever felt like the biggest problem.” At one point his manager, Joe Hildebrand, told him he wanted Bobby to spend one day a week without opening his terminal. Bobby was shocked: “What are you asking me to do here? You’re asking me to not work for an entire day.” The point was to broaden his impact beyond direct code contributions, though that wasn’t his natural mode.

**On promos: impact over title**

Bobby has strong opinions on the two common promo philosophies: “I am very much of the mind that it is important to focus on impact and not the title.” He compares promo-hacking—pinning your management chain down on exactly what boxes to check—to a child asking for a reward before doing a chore: “It just does not land well with me.” When people focus on checking boxes, they end up doing the work “in a way that was very sort of focused on checking the box as opposed to having the impact.” Impact is ultimately what matters. For quieter engineers who need to share their work, Bobby reframes the activity: don’t think of it as “I am trying to brag more about me”; think of it as “I am trying to communicate and coordinate and create visibility for work that is important.” The same action, framed differently, produces different outcomes—and people can tell the difference. He also emphasizes giving visibility to others and thanking the team as part of those updates. His advice on ambition: “Think about your ambition in terms of the impact that you want to have as opposed to the status you want to achieve.” The best career conversations are not “How do I get promoted?” but “How do I grow my career and have more impact? Am I working on the most important thing?” And on a practical level: “You don’t want the people in leadership to find you to be annoying.”

**The distinguished engineer rejection that redefined impact**

When Bobby thought he was on a DE trajectory through sheer codebase mastery, CTO Eric Rescorla sat him down and told him that for the first time in nearly a decade Mozilla was making new distinguished engineers—and Bobby would not be one of them. The old Mozilla DE archetype was the prolific, codebase-spanning contributor (Boris, who could own half the code). But Rescorla was looking for something different: **industry impact**—changing the world at the scale of the internet itself. Bobby’s reaction wasn’t resentment; it was the feeling of discovering a muscle he didn’t know he had that was “extremely weak.” It reoriented how he directed his efforts. He describes three DEs promoted at that time as archetypes: Martin Thomson, whose name is on the author line of protocols like HTTP/2, QUIC, Web Push, WebRTC, and Oblivious HTTP—designing faster protocols that also leaked less metadata, so the entire industry had a business incentive to adopt them and increase privacy/security by default. Luke Wagner, who was “the most single-handedly responsible for the development of WebAssembly”—starting from Emscripten, evolving through ASM.js, and eventually navigating both the technical and political dimensions to get all browsers to adopt a new sandboxed bytecode format (Ryan notes Figma and a tens-of-billions-of-dollars industry sit on top of that work). And Tim Terriberry, the codec lead who developed the open, royalty-free Opus audio codec and the Daala video codec that was incorporated into AV1, now the industry standard powering video across an alliance of Mozilla, Google, Apple, and Netflix.

**Advice for aspiring distinguished engineers: writing**

Bobby’s single most actionable piece of advice is to work on writing. He references an internal (now public) Mozilla memo called **Writing Things Down**, which argues that writing is both about the artifact for coordination and feedback, and also about the process itself: “The process of writing is the process of thinking and refining your ideas.” Clarity of thinking is one of the most important things, and “you only can really get that by forcing yourself to write down your ideas.” His corollary: do not use an LLM to produce text for you. You’ll lose the opportunity to do the thinking yourself, and “that thinking that happens when you write is one of the most important types of thinking for growth.” On how to write well: it doesn’t need to be any longer than what you have to say. If you run out of things to say, “you either need to refine your ideas more or you’re just done.” Fluff is never useful. And substance matters more than rhetoric—non-native English speakers shouldn’t worry about flowery words: “It is about clarity of thought, and clarity of thought, once you have it, is relatively easy to express in written form if you practice.”

**The AI browser wars and why Firefox’s engine matters**

Bobby draws a sharp distinction: most of the “new browsers” announced monthly by AI companies are not browsers—they are “different front ends, different window dressing and monetization keys on top of Chrome.” A browser is defined by its engine, and Firefox is now the only independent browser with no engine dependency on anyone else. Safari uses WebKit; everyone else (Opera, Brave, Edge, Arc, Perplexity) uses Chromium. Chrome is making an aggressive play to control the AI stack by leveraging its dominance, including proprietary LLM-access APIs where the model behavior is fundamentally unspecified—but as the dominant browser, Google has the market power to get websites to tune their usage to what Gemini expects. Mozilla, which isn’t building its own foundation model, is fighting to keep the space open so that “a multi-stakeholder environment where no single entity controls the evolution of the web platform” survives AI. The web uniquely provides user agency and re-interpretable content (e.g., ad blockers); if it becomes a backend detail consumed by ChatGPT or Operator, much is lost. Maintaining an independent engine is a “giant cost center” with no bottom-line justification, but it gives Mozilla a seat at the standards table—crucial in a world where the remaining alternatives are a bilateral Apple-Google standoff. He cites **Speedometer 3**, a shared browser benchmark Mozilla helped broker between Google and Apple: in a bilateral relationship, a shared benchmark “would predictably go nowhere” because each side would tilt it. Mozilla’s presence kept everyone focused on making the web faster for users rather than making any particular browser look good. The result: both Firefox and Chrome roughly doubled their scores, translating to about a 30% improvement in real-world page-load performance. “I think it is reasonable to say that having this benchmark has made the web 30% faster. And that is something that benefits everyone and I don’t think it’s something that would’ve happened without Mozilla in the room.”

**Biggest technical regret: vibes-based architecture decisions**

When Bobby was excited about uplifting Servo’s WebRender graphics backend, he advocated heavily for it—based not on deep graphics expertise but on “a vibes thing of this is where it’s going.” That enthusiasm caught on with management. Senior graphics engineers who had strong doubts tried to raise concerns; one engineering leader responded, “C++ is not the future.” The “vibes-based enthusiasm for a risky technical direction over the concerns of the people who knew it the best ended up being very toxic.” Several of Mozilla’s most senior and impactful graphics engineers left. The team that remained did eventually build WebRender into a fantastic, performant, safe engine, and Bobby is proud of what they built. But “pushing it on the team when the team wasn’t ready and wasn’t aligned on it had collateral damage that I regret.”

**Wide intellectual consumption over book-completionism**

Bobby considers himself an avid reader but confesses that many books that profoundly impacted his thinking are ones he never actually read cover-to-cover. “Reading a whole book on a topic is a depth-first approach,” and his style is wider. He read a three-page review of *Good Strategy, Bad Strategy* that permanently changed how he evaluates strategy documents; *Turn the Ship Around* and *The Innovator’s Dilemma* shaped his thinking on empowering teams and industry dynamics without his having finished them. He also encourages looking “wide, not just within tech,” citing the geopolitics and national-security space as an area full of thoughtful writing about what happens on a battlefield—concepts that “rhyme” with big-tech battles and team motivation.

**Advice to his younger self: be humble**

“At the end of the day, to be humble is the most important.” Early in his career, Bobby had an attitude that engineering was the center of the universe. He’s since realized there are many other important functions and perspectives in an organization, and that developing “an appreciation and empathy for all of the different players within the organization and within the industry” is critical. “Everybody has a point of view and a place that they’re coming from and working to understand where people are coming from is the way to make progress.”

---

Bobby’s journey from code-fixing intern to browser-engine steward is undergirded by an evolution in what “impact” means—first defined as raw **prolificacy across the codebase**, then reoriented by his DE rejection toward **industry impact** that changes the internet at scale. His **Slaughterhouse** project embodied his security philosophy of getting ahead of the problem, while his later championing of **Speedometer 3** shows how an independent engine turns a bilateral stalemate into a 30% faster web for everyone. The thread connecting them is the **Writing Things Down** conviction: clarity of thought, refined through the discipline of writing, is the force multiplier for both code-level leadership and standards-table diplomacy. His regret around **vibes-based architecture decisions**—and his closing advice to stay humble—reveals that the hardest engineering problem is often aligning the humans in the room before the code.
