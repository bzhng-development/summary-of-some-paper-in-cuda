Here are the takeaways from this **Ryan Peterman** newsletter on career strategy and interviewing:

**Why You Should Interview Even When You're Happy**

Ryan opens with a striking anecdote: an engineer with one of the fastest-growing careers he's ever encountered—**IC3 to IC8 in 5 years at Meta**—shared a counterintuitive practice. Despite having worked at Meta for over a decade, this engineer interviews at other companies once a year. "Interviewing and choosing to stay gave him confidence that he was always in the best spot." Ryan later heard similar advice from a Staff (IC6) mentor, cementing the insight as a recurring theme among high-performers.

The core logic revolves around **abundance and leverage**. "The worst time to look for a new job is when you need one." Desperation breeds anxiety, which harms interview performance and weakens negotiating power—if your current alternative is bad, you have no fallback and less leverage to push for better terms. But the deeper reason is strategic: "Access to the right opportunity becomes the limiting factor for career growth eventually." Regularly scanning the landscape is simply an effective way to surface those opportunities before you're forced to hunt.

**The Efficient Approach to Interviewing While Content**

Ryan tackles the obvious objection head-on: most engineers don't interview when they're happy because it feels like a waste of time—"a contrived process that doesn't make you a better engineer." He flips this by arguing that interviewing *from a position of happiness* is actually the most time-efficient approach, for two reasons.

First, **you apply for fewer roles**. Since you genuinely like your current job, your bar for what constitutes an improvement is high, and the list of potential roles shrinks dramatically. Second, **you don't need to overprepare**. Because there's no desperation attached to the outcome, "you can just spend the 20% of the time that gets you 80% of the benefit"—a light touch on preparation that avoids the grinding, anxiety-fueled cramming of someone who *must* land the job.

The asymmetry is clean: "Worst case, you remain happy in your current role and you're sharper in case you need to interview later. Best case, you find an opportunity that is better than what you have." The practice doubles as a hedge—keeping interview skills warm and calibrated—and an exploratory tool for discovering unexpectedly better moves.
