# StraTA: Incentivizing Agentic Reinforcement Learning with Strategic Trajectory Abstraction

**ArXiv:** [2605.06642](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.06642)

## 🎯 Pitch

Large language model agents trained with reinforcement learning struggle on long-horizon tasks because they act purely reactively—they must decide both the immediate action and the overall plan at every step. StraTA fixes this by first sampling a compact, natural-language strategy from the initial state and conditioning all subsequent actions on it, enabling coherent execution without requiring the policy to replan constantly. With this simple change and a hierarchical GRPO training scheme, open-source 7B models beat GPT-4o and Claude on challenging web, household, and science environments.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **Strategic Trajectory Abstraction (StraTA)**, a framework that augments agentic reinforcement learning with an explicit trajectory-level strategy—sampling a compact natural-language plan from the initial task state and conditioning all subsequent actions on it, thereby decomposing long-horizon decision making into strategy generation and strategy-following execution—and trains both levels jointly through hierarchical GRPO-style rollouts (strategy-level comparisons across different plans alongside action-level comparisons under the same plan), further enhanced by diverse strategy rollout via farthest-point sampling (selecting semantically distinct candidate strategies to broaden exploration) and critical self-judgment (a step-level auxiliary penalty that flags actions neither following the strategy nor advancing task progress). Experiments on ALFWorld, WebShop, and SciWorld using Qwen2.5 models at 1.5B and 7B scales show that StraTA reaches 93.1% success on ALFWorld and 84.2% success on WebShop, while attaining a 63.5% overall score on SciWorld—outperforming both frontier closed-source models and prior RL baselines—establishing that explicit strategy guidance improves sample efficiency and final performance on long-horizon agentic tasks only when the strategy quality is sufficient to coordinate behavior and the environment does not change so substantially during an episode that a fixed strategy becomes restrictive.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Reactive Agents Struggle with Long-Horizon Decision Making

The fundamental problem this paper tackles is that **current LLM agents are overwhelmingly reactive**: at each step, the policy generates the next action based solely on the current state, without any explicit high-level plan guiding its behavior over the entire trajectory. Section 1 frames this as a structural limitation of existing formulations:

> "most existing formulations remain purely reactive. As shown in Figure 1a, at each step the agent predicts the next action solely from the current state. The policy therefore has to decide both the immediate next move and the overall course of action at the same time."

This coupling is not merely an aesthetic issue—it concretely degrades performance through three mechanisms the paper identifies (Section 1, introduction paragraph):

1. **Short-sighted exploration**: Without a global plan, the agent cannot distinguish between a step that temporarily looks unpromising but is part of a sound long-term strategy, and a genuinely bad move. The policy explores locally, reactively, and may never discover that a different *sequence* of actions would succeed.

2. **Unnecessary backtracking**: A reactive agent hitting a dead end has no memory of why it chose its current path. It may retrace steps or oscillate between alternatives without recognizing that the *high-level approach*—not the individual action choices—is what needs to change.

3. **Internally inconsistent behavior**: When every action is generated independently from the current state, there is no mechanism ensuring that step 1 and step 10 are aligned with the same underlying plan. The agent may "change its mind" mid-trajectory in ways that prevent task completion.

This problem is specific to **long-horizon agentic tasks**—settings where an agent must take many sequential actions in an environment, and the reward signal (success or failure) arrives only at the very end. The paper argues this is fundamentally harder than both standard language modeling and single-turn reasoning, because each decision has consequences that may only become apparent much later, and the agent must maintain coherent behavior over many interactions.

### Why This Problem Matters: The Gap Between General-Purpose Capabilities and Specialized Reliability

The paper situates its work within a broader trend: LLMs are increasingly deployed as interactive agents that perceive environments, reason in natural language, and act through external tools or APIs. Section 1 opens by acknowledging that this paradigm has enabled rapid progress on complex tasks such as web interaction, computer operation, and scientific problem solving. However, it immediately notes a critical limitation:

> "strong general-purpose capabilities do not automatically translate into reliable performance on specialized long-horizon tasks."

This gap matters for several practical reasons that the paper discusses across Sections 1 and 2:

**Real-world interactive deployment.** When LLMs operate as agents in environments like web navigation (WebShop), household robotics (ALFWorld), or scientific experimentation (SciWorld), they cannot simply generate a single response and stop. They must maintain a coherent chain of reasoning and action across 15, 50, or more environment steps. Failure at any step can derail the entire episode. The reactive paradigm—where each action is generated purely from the current observation—makes it difficult to ensure that early actions lay groundwork for later ones, or that the agent can recover from mistakes without losing sight of the overall goal.

**The exploration-credit assignment dual bottleneck.** Section 1 frames agentic RL as a setting where two traditional RL challenges are amplified. First, *exploration* is difficult because the action space is combinatorial (each action is a natural-language string generated token-by-token), and the space of possible *trajectories* is even larger. A reactive policy exploring locally may never sample the specific action sequence needed to solve a hard task. Second, *credit assignment* is difficult because rewards are sparse and delayed until episode termination. When a trajectory succeeds or fails, it is hard to know which of the 20+ individual actions were responsible. The paper argues that these two problems are connected: if the agent had an explicit strategy, it would explore more systematically (trying different high-level approaches rather than random local variations) and credit assignment would be easier (actions could be judged against whether they followed the strategy, not just whether the final outcome was good).

**The pretraining limitation.** The paper's choice of benchmarks—ALFWorld, WebShop, SciWorld—reflects a deliberate focus on tasks where pure prompting (even with advanced techniques like ReAct) fails. Table 1 shows that frontier closed-source models (GPT-5.1, Claude-4-Sonnet, Gemini-2.5-Flash) achieve only 55.7%, 72.9%, and 38.6% success on ALFWorld respectively, and 22.2%, 18.4%, and 4.4% on WebShop. These are not problems that can be solved by simply scaling pretraining—they require task-specific optimization through interaction. This makes agentic RL a practically important research direction, but the reactive formulation limits how much RL can help.

### Prior Approaches and Where They Fall Short

The paper situates its contribution relative to three research strands (Section 2): LLM-based agents, reinforcement fine-tuning, and agentic RL. Here, I explain what each strand provides and what gaps remain.

#### LLM-Based Agents: Prompting Is Not Enough

Early work on LLM agents primarily relied on prompting-based designs (Section 2, first paragraph). Approaches like ReAct interleave reasoning and action generation at each step, giving the agent a form of step-level deliberation. Reflexion allows the agent to reflect on past failures and incorporate that feedback into future attempts. These methods can work on relatively simple, short-horizon tasks, but the paper's experimental results demonstrate their limits: on ALFWorld, ReAct with Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct achieves only 42.4% success (Table 1), and on SciWorld only 20.0% (Table 2). The problem is that **prompting does not fundamentally change the reactive nature of the policy**. ReAct agents reason *locally* at each step, but they lack a *global* plan that persists across the entire trajectory. They can deliberate about what to do next given the current state, but they cannot deliberate about what strategy to follow for the entire episode and then stick to it.

#### Reinforcement Fine-Tuning: Optimized for Single-Turn Reasoning, Not Multi-Turn Interaction

Reinforcement learning has been successfully applied to LLMs, primarily for two purposes: alignment with human preferences (RLHF, using PPO-based methods) and improving reasoning capabilities through verifiable rewards (RLVR, using GRPO-based methods). Section 2, second paragraph, surveys these developments.

The GRPO framework is particularly relevant because it removes the need for a separate critic network by deriving learning signals from relative reward comparisons within a sampled group of outputs. This makes it computationally efficient and has proven highly scalable for reasoning tasks like math problem solving. However, the paper identifies a critical mismatch:

> "In this work, we adopt a GRPO-style training framework, but focus on the more challenging setting of long-horizon agentic tasks rather than single-turn reasoning problems."

In single-turn reasoning, the model generates one long response and receives one reward. The GRPO group compares multiple complete responses. In multi-turn agentic tasks, the model generates many sequential actions, each of which is a separate generation call (in the non-cumulative setting), but there is only one episode-level reward. This creates two complications that prior GRPO formulations do not address: (1) how should the single trajectory-level reward be distributed across individual action steps for credit assignment? (2) how should the model learn which high-level *strategies* are promising when all it observes is action-level outcomes?

#### Agentic RL: Existing Methods Are Still Reactive

Section 2, third paragraph, defines agentic RL as a "distinct emerging area" where agents learn interactive behavior through trial and error. The paper acknowledges recent progress along several dimensions:

- **Training frameworks**: rLLM, AgentGym-RL, and GiGPO have established infrastructure for running RL on multi-turn agent trajectories.
- **Exploration improvements**: Some work focuses on active exploration strategies, encouraging the agent to try diverse behaviors during training.
- **Credit assignment refinements**: GiGPO introduces anchor states for better long-horizon credit assignment. Other methods (ArCHer, HiPeR, implicit step rewards) attempt to decompose the trajectory-level reward into per-step signals.

However, the paper makes a specific critique: **all these methods remain reactive**. Even with better credit assignment, the underlying policy still generates each action from the current state alone. GiGPO improves *how* the reward signal is distributed, but it does not change *what* the policy conditions on. The agent is still trying to infer the overall plan from local observations, and the RL optimization is still trying to improve action-level decisions without explicitly reasoning about strategy-level choices.

> "most existing formulations remain purely reactive... The policy therefore has to decide both the immediate next move and the overall course of action at the same time."

This is the paper's key diagnosis: existing agentic RL methods are solving the wrong decomposition. They treat the problem as "improve the reactive policy" rather than "separate strategy from execution."

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper's central argument is that the missing piece in agentic RL is an **explicit trajectory-level abstraction**—a compact natural-language strategy generated at the start of each episode and held fixed throughout. This is not merely an architectural tweak; it represents a fundamentally different decomposition of the decision-making problem.

**The human analogy.** Section 1 grounds this idea in an observation about human problem solving:

> "Human problem solving is rarely purely reactive: people typically form a high-level plan, execute local actions under that plan, and later reflect on whether the plan was appropriate."

This analogy is not just motivational rhetoric—it directly informs the paper's technical design. Humans don't generate each action from scratch based on the current situation; they first decide *what kind of approach* to take, then execute that approach, then evaluate whether the approach worked. The paper's strategy-guided execution and hierarchical GRPO training mirror this three-phase structure: strategy generation (forming the plan), strategy-conditioned action taking (executing under the plan), and strategy-level reward based on top-performing rollouts (evaluating the plan).

**The decomposition into two sub-problems.** Section 4.1 explicitly states what StraTA's reformulation achieves:

> "This reformulation decomposes task solving into two sub-goals: (1) generating high-quality strategies from the initial task specification, and (2) executing actions that consistently follow the generated strategy throughout the episode."

This decomposition is valuable because it turns one hard joint optimization problem into two simpler, more tractable ones. Learning to generate good strategies is a form of planning—the model must understand the task structure and propose a reasonable course of action. Learning to execute strategies is a form of following instructions—the model must translate a high-level plan into concrete actions while adapting to the specifics of the environment state. By training these jointly but evaluating them separately (through the hierarchical group structure), StraTA provides separate learning signals for each sub-goal.

**The relationship to hierarchical RL.** While the paper does not explicitly frame itself as hierarchical RL (HRL), the structure is conceptually similar: there is a high-level policy (strategy generation) and a low-level policy (strategy-conditioned action taking), with the high-level policy operating at a coarser temporal granularity (once per episode). However, StraTA differs from classical HRL in important ways:

1. **The strategy is generated once and fixed**, rather than being re-evaluated at each "high-level" time step. This simplifies training (no need to learn when to re-plan) but introduces the limitation that the strategy may become stale if the environment changes significantly (acknowledged in Section 6).

2. **The high-level "actions" are natural language strategies**, not abstract options or subgoals. This leverages the LLM's pre-trained planning capabilities rather than learning a high-level policy from scratch.

3. **Training is end-to-end with GRPO**, not through separate high-level and low-level RL loops. Both strategy and action tokens are optimized through the same objective (Equation 14), with separate groups for strategy-level and action-level comparisons.

**The two auxiliary techniques as necessary complements.** The paper is explicit that the hierarchical formulation alone is not sufficient—it introduces two additional techniques to address specific challenges that arise from the strategy-conditioned setup:

- **Diverse strategy rollout (Section 4.3)** addresses the problem that the model tends to generate highly similar strategies when sampling multiple candidates, even at elevated temperature. This is a known phenomenon in LLM sampling—the model's distribution can be sharply peaked around a particular planning approach, limiting the informativeness of strategy-level comparisons. Without diversity, GRPO would compare near-identical strategies and learn little. Farthest-point sampling in semantic space forces the model to consider genuinely different approaches, making the strategy-level reward signal more discriminative.

- **Critical self-judgment (Section 4.4)** addresses a specific failure mode of strategy-conditioned execution: the agent might take actions that lead to success but do not actually follow the stated strategy. If the RL objective only rewards final success, the model can learn to ignore the strategy and fall back on reactive behavior, defeating the purpose of the framework. The self-judgment penalty explicitly penalizes actions that neither follow the strategy nor advance task progress, creating a direct incentive for strategy-aligned behavior. The paper notes that this is a *step-level* auxiliary reward, contrasting with the *trajectory-level* outcome reward—it provides fine-grained feedback on individual actions that is otherwise missing in the sparse-reward setting.

**Positioning relative to prior strategy-in-LLM work.** The paper briefly references Erdogan et al. (2025) in the context of plan-and-act approaches. However, it does not provide a detailed comparison with other work that incorporates explicit planning into LLM agents (e.g., tree-of-thought methods, hierarchical planning approaches). The key distinction appears to be that StraTA is designed specifically for the *reinforcement learning* setting—the strategy is not just a prompting technique but a trainable component jointly optimized with action execution through GRPO. The strategy improves through RL as the model learns which kinds of plans lead to successful rollouts, not just through better prompting.

**The experimental positioning.** The paper's choice of baselines (Section 5.1) reveals its intended contribution. Against prompting-based methods (Vanilla, ReAct, frontier closed-source models), StraTA demonstrates that task-specific RL training with explicit strategy guidance is necessary to achieve high performance on long-horizon tasks. Against RL-based methods (PPO, RLOO, GRPO, GiGPO), StraTA demonstrates that adding strategy guidance on top of an already-strong RL framework yields meaningful improvements—particularly on the harder tasks and subtasks (e.g., Look on ALFWorld, where StraTA achieves 92.3% vs. GiGPO's 82.7%; WebShop success rate, where StraTA achieves 84.2% vs. GiGPO's 72.8%). Against other trained agents on SciWorld (AgentGym-RL, ScalingInter), StraTA demonstrates that strategy guidance can push open-source models past frontier closed-source performance on complex scientific reasoning tasks.

### The Unresolved Tension

A careful reader should notice a tension in the paper's framing. The introduction argues that purely reactive decision making is the root cause of poor long-horizon performance, but the experimental baselines (PPO, GRPO, GiGPO) are all reactive methods that nevertheless significantly outperform prompting-based approaches and even frontier models. GiGPO achieves 90.8% on ALFWorld with a 7B model—already quite strong. StraTA improves this to 93.1%, which is a meaningful gain but not a qualitative leap.

This suggests that the reactive limitation, while real, is not the dominant bottleneck for these benchmarks—at least not for the stronger base models. The paper does not explicitly address this tension, but the results imply that strategy guidance is most valuable when (a) the task requires extended planning (SciWorld, where the gap is larger: 63.5% vs. 57.0%), and (b) the base model is smaller and benefits more from structural decomposition (1.5B StraTA achieves 90.7% on ALFWorld, approaching GiGPO-7B's 90.8%). This pattern is consistent with the paper's claim that strategy guidance decomposes a hard problem into simpler sub-problems—exactly what you would expect to help smaller models more.

The paper's contribution, therefore, is not that reactive methods cannot work at all, but that they are fundamentally limited in how efficiently they can learn and how well they can scale to genuinely complex long-horizon tasks. The explicit strategy abstraction provides a more sample-efficient learning pathway and a higher performance ceiling, as evidenced by the training curves in Figure 1c and the consistent gains across all three benchmarks.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

StraTA is a training framework that teaches an LLM-based agent to first think up a high-level plan (a "strategy") and then carry it out step-by-step, rather than reacting to each new situation without any overall direction. It solves the problem of long-horizon agentic decision-making—where an agent must take many actions and only finds out if it succeeded at the very end—by splitting one hard task ("figure out what to do AND do it simultaneously") into two easier sub-tasks ("figure out a strategy first, then just follow it") and training both skills jointly through a hierarchical reinforcement learning procedure that gives the model separate feedback on whether its strategies are good and whether its actions faithfully execute those strategies.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The StraTA system has five major components that operate in sequence for each training episode:

1. **Strategy Generator** — given the initial task description, the base LLM samples a compact natural-language strategy (e.g., "explore the room to find where the tissue box might be and how to get it to the sidetable"). This strategy is generated once and fixed for the entire episode.

2. **Diverse Strategy Selector** — when multiple candidate strategies are sampled, farthest-point sampling in semantic embedding space selects the subset that are maximally different from one another, ensuring the strategy-level comparisons are informative.

3. **Strategy-Conditioned Action Executor** — at each environment step, the base LLM generates its next action by conditioning on both the fixed global strategy and the current local state. The strategy is prepended to every decision prompt, so the model sees "here is the overall plan, here is what's happening now" before choosing an action.

4. **Hierarchical Group Constructor** — for each task, the system samples N strategies and runs M independent rollouts under each strategy, producing N×M trajectories. These are organized into one strategy-level group (comparing N strategies against each other) and N action-level groups (comparing M trajectories generated under the same strategy).

5. **Self-Judgment Critic** — after a trajectory completes, the agent itself reviews the episode, flags any action steps that neither followed the strategy nor advanced task progress, and applies a small penalty to those specific steps, creating fine-grained per-step feedback.

Information flows as follows: task specification enters → Strategy Generator produces candidate strategies → Diverse Strategy Selector picks N diverse strategies → for each strategy, Action Executor runs M rollouts of strategy-conditioned actions → environment returns outcome rewards → Hierarchical Group Constructor organizes strategies and actions into groups → Self-Judgment Critic reviews each trajectory and flags problematic steps → GRPO-style optimization updates the model using both strategy-level and action-level advantage signals.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the **strategy-guided task execution** formulation (Section 4.1), which defines how StraTA reformulates the decision process—this is the foundation because every other component builds on the strategy-conditioned action generation.
- **Second**, the **hierarchical group construction and training objective** (Section 4.2), which explains how StraTA samples trajectories, constructs strategy-level and action-level groups, computes rewards and advantages at both levels, and defines the overall training loss—this is the core optimization machinery.
- **Third**, the **diverse strategy rollout via farthest-point sampling** (Section 4.3), which addresses the exploration problem of strategy-space collapse—explaining this after the objective makes clear why diversity matters for making strategy-level comparisons informative.
- **Fourth**, the **step-level auxiliary reward via critical self-judgment** (Section 4.4), which addresses the credit assignment problem created by sparse outcome rewards—explaining this last shows how it complements the hierarchical group structure by adding fine-grained per-step feedback.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **training framework paper** whose core idea is that introducing an explicit trajectory-level strategy into agentic RL—and optimizing strategy generation and action execution jointly through a hierarchical GRPO-style objective with diversity-enhancing and credit-assignment-improving auxiliary techniques—yields more sample-efficient learning and higher final performance on long-horizon tasks than purely reactive approaches.

#### Strategy-Guided Task Execution (Reformulating the Decision Process)

The standard reactive formulation of agentic RL (Section 3) defines the agent policy as generating each action from the current state alone: at step `$t$`, the action `$a_t$` is sampled from `$\pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s_t)$`. Under this formulation, the policy must simultaneously infer the long-term plan and select the immediate action from the same local information, a coupling the paper argues leads to short-sighted behavior and internal inconsistency.

StraTA fundamentally restructures this process by inserting an explicit strategy generation step before any interaction with the environment begins. Given only the initial state `$s_1$`—which contains the task description and initial environment observation—the agent first samples a global strategy:

$$z \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s_1)$$

where `$z$` is a compact natural-language plan that summarizes the anticipated course of action for the entire episode, and `$\pi_\theta$` is the same parameterized LLM policy used for action generation.

**What it computes:** the model reads the task description (e.g., "put a tissuebox in sidetable" or "find me high-density, twin-size mattresses under $550") and produces a free-text strategy summarizing how it intends to approach the task. The strategy is generated only once, at episode start, and is kept fixed thereafter—it is not updated or revised based on intermediate observations.

**Why this form:** generating the strategy from `$s_1$` alone (rather than conditioning on subsequent states) forces the strategy to be a genuine *plan* based on task understanding, not a post-hoc rationalization of whatever the agent happens to have done. This mirrors the human cognitive process of forming a plan before acting, and it creates a clean information separation: the strategy encodes task-level knowledge and intent, while subsequent actions handle environment-level adaptation.

After strategy generation, every subsequent action is generated by conditioning on both the global strategy `$z$` and the current local state `$s_t$`:

$$a_t \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid z, s_t)$$

**What it computes:** at each environment step, the model sees the strategy prepended to its observation (e.g., "You should strictly observe the following strategy: [strategy text]. Current state: [observation]") and generates its next action. The strategy serves as a constant trajectory-level signal that persists across all steps, ensuring that every action decision is informed by both the overall plan and the immediate situation.

**Why this form:** this decomposition means that the strategy only needs to encode "what approach to take," not the details of how to execute it in every possible state. The action-level policy handles the execution details. If the state changes in unexpected ways, the model can adapt its actions while still adhering to the strategic intent—it is not forced to blindly follow a rigid plan, but the strategy provides a persistent constraint that prevents the kind of internally inconsistent behavior that purely reactive policies exhibit.

**The resulting trajectory** becomes:

$$\tau = \{z, (s_1, a_1, r_1), \cdots, (s_T, a_T, r_T)\}$$

where `$z$` is prepended to the standard (state, action, reward) sequence. The strategy is not itself an action in the environment—it does not advance the environment state or consume a step—but it is part of the model's generation history and is included in the training objective.

**Design choice—fixed vs. adaptive strategy:** the paper explicitly chooses to keep the strategy fixed throughout the episode rather than allowing mid-episode re-planning. The stated rationale is simplicity—a fixed strategy "turns the original fully reactive decision process into a more structured one" without introducing the additional complexity of learning when and how to revise the strategy. However, the paper acknowledges in Section 6 that this is a limitation: "a fixed strategy may become restrictive when the environment changes substantially during an episode." The fixed-strategy design is a deliberate trade-off: it gains training simplicity and clean credit assignment at the cost of reduced flexibility in highly dynamic environments.

**What the decomposition achieves:** by separating strategy generation from action execution, StraTA "decomposes task solving into two sub-goals: (1) generating high-quality strategies from the initial task specification, and (2) executing actions that consistently follow the generated strategy throughout the episode." The paper argues this is valuable because each sub-goal is simpler than the original joint problem. Generating good strategies is a planning task that leverages the LLM's pre-trained reasoning capabilities. Executing strategies faithfully is an instruction-following task that benefits from the explicit guidance. Training them jointly allows the model to improve at both simultaneously, with separate learning signals for each sub-goal provided by the hierarchical group construction described next.

#### Hierarchical Group Construction and the StraTA Training Objective

The strategy-guided execution reformulation introduces a new question: how should the model be trained so that both strategy generation and strategy-following execution improve through RL? The answer is a two-level rollout structure built on top of GRPO-style training that creates separate comparison groups for strategies and actions.

**Sampling procedure.** For each training task, the agent first samples `$N$` strategies `$\{z_i\}_{i=1}^N$` and then, for each strategy `$z_i$`, performs `$M$` independent rollouts `$\{\tau_{i,j}\}_{j=1}^M$`. Each rollout generates a complete trajectory of actions (conditioned on that strategy), producing a total of `$N \times M$` trajectories. The paper uses `$N = 4$` and `$M = 8$` in all main experiments (Section 5.1), yielding 32 trajectories per task.

**Why `$N = 4$` and `$M = 8$`:** these values represent a practical balance. More strategies (`$N$`) would provide richer strategy-level comparisons but increase computational cost linearly. More rollouts per strategy (`$M$`) would give more reliable estimates of strategy quality but also increase cost. The total `$N \times M = 32$` trajectories per task is comparable to the group size used in standard GRPO training (which typically uses groups of 16–64 samples), making the comparison fair in terms of total environment interactions. The paper does not provide a detailed sweep over these values, but the ablation in Table 4 implicitly varies the effective diversity of the strategy set through the oversampling ratio `$\sigma$`.

**Group construction.** The `$N \times M$` trajectories are organized into two levels of groups:

- One **strategy-level group** `$\mathcal{G}_{\text{strategy}} = \{z_i \mid 1 \leq i \leq N\}$` containing the `$N$` strategies. This group allows the model to compare different high-level plans: which strategies are more promising for this task?

- `$N$` **action-level groups** `$\mathcal{G}^{\text{action}}_i = \{a^{i,j}_t \mid 1 \leq j \leq M, 1 \leq t \leq |\tau_{i,j}|\}$`, one per strategy, each containing all action steps from all `$M$` rollouts under that strategy. These groups allow the model to compare action sequences generated under the same strategy: which trajectories are more likely to succeed while adhering to this plan?

**Why two-level grouping:** the key insight is that comparing strategies requires holding the execution quality roughly constant (or accounting for it), and comparing actions requires holding the strategy constant. If you mixed actions from different strategies in the same group, you could not tell whether a successful action was due to good execution or a good underlying strategy. The hierarchical structure isolates these two sources of variation.

**Reward computation at the action level.** Each trajectory `$\tau_{i,j}$` receives an outcome reward `$R(\tau_{i,j})$` from the environment at episode termination. Because the paper adopts a non-cumulative setting—where each step is generated from an individual prompt constructed from the current state rather than from the full interaction history—this trajectory-level reward must be distributed across all action steps. The simplest approach is used: broadcast the trajectory reward to every action step in that episode:

$$R(a^{i,j}_t) = R(\tau_{i,j})$$

where `$R(a^{i,j}_t)$` is the reward assigned to the `$t$`-th action in the `$j$`-th rollout under strategy `$i$`, and `$R(\tau_{i,j})$` is the terminal outcome reward of that trajectory.

**What it computes:** if a trajectory succeeds (reward = 1, or a step-decayed version approaching 1 for efficient trajectories), every action in that trajectory receives that positive reward. If it fails (reward = 0 or negative after penalties), every action receives that negative signal. This is a crude form of credit assignment—it assumes all actions in a successful trajectory contributed equally, and all actions in a failed trajectory were equally at fault—but it is the standard approach in group-based RL for agentic tasks.

**Why this form (broadcasting):** the alternative would be some learned or heuristic decomposition of the trajectory reward into per-step contributions. The paper's self-judgment mechanism (Section 4.4) partially addresses the crudeness of broadcasting by adding a step-level auxiliary penalty, but the core outcome reward remains broadcast. This keeps the method simple while acknowledging that finer credit assignment is needed—hence the self-judgment component.

**Reward computation at the strategy level.** Each strategy `$z_i$` receives a reward based on the `$M$` rollouts performed under it. Rather than averaging over all rollouts equally (which would conflate strategy quality with execution noise), StraTA uses the mean of only the top-performing fraction:

$$R(z_i) = \text{mean}\left(\text{top-}\delta\left(\{R(\tau_{i,j})\}_{j=1}^M\right)\right)$$

where `$\delta$` controls the fraction of rollouts considered, set to `$\delta = 0.5$` in all experiments.

**What it computes:** for each strategy, sort its `$M = 8$` trajectory rewards from highest to lowest, take the top `$\delta \times M = 4$` rewards, and compute their mean. This produces a scalar strategy quality estimate that emphasizes the best performance achievable under that strategy while reducing the influence of low-quality rollouts caused by early-stage action execution errors.

**Why this form (top-`$\delta$` instead of mean):** the paper argues that using the mean of all rollouts would penalize good strategies whose execution happened to fail due to action-level noise. If a strategy is fundamentally sound but the model occasionally makes execution mistakes (e.g., navigating to the wrong object), the mean reward would be dragged down by those failures. The top-`$\delta$` aggregation asks: "when this strategy is executed reasonably well, how often does it succeed?" This provides a more reliable estimate of strategy quality that is less confounded by execution errors. The choice of `$\delta = 0.5$` is a middle ground—using only the single best rollout (`$\delta \approx 0.125$` for `$M = 8$`) would be too noisy, while using all rollouts (`$\delta = 1.0$`) would conflate strategy and execution quality. The ablation in Table 5 confirms this: `$\delta = 0.1$` produces "poor performance" because too few trajectories are used, while `$\delta = 1.0$` leads to "overly pessimistic evaluations" because execution noise is fully included.

**Reward augmentation with length and format penalties.** Both strategy-level and action-level rewards are augmented with two auxiliary terms before being used for advantage computation. These are not specific to StraTA—they follow practices from prior work on GRPO-based training.

**Length penalty.** GRPO-style training often encourages long responses because the objective rewards exploration and verbose outputs can accidentally receive higher rewards. To keep generations within practical context limits, a soft length penalty is applied:

$$R_{\text{length}}(x) = \begin{cases} 0, & |x| \leq \lambda L_{\text{total}} \\ -\frac{1}{1-\lambda}\left(\frac{|x|}{L_{\text{total}}} - \lambda\right), & \lambda L_{\text{total}} < |x| \leq L_{\text{total}} \\ -1, & L_{\text{total}} < |x| \end{cases}$$

where `$x$` is either a strategy or an action, `$|x|$` is the number of tokens in the response, `$L_{\text{total}}$` is the response length limit (1024 tokens; Table 7), and `$\lambda = 0.5$` controls when the penalty begins.

**What it computes:** if the response is short (up to half the token limit), no penalty. Between half the limit and the full limit, a linearly increasing penalty from 0 to -1. Beyond the limit, a flat -1 penalty. This softly discourages overlong generations without harshly truncating them.

**Why this form:** the piecewise linear design is gentler than a hard truncation, which could create discontinuities in the reward signal that destabilize training. It allows the model to occasionally generate longer responses when needed (the penalty is continuous, not binary) while providing a consistent pressure toward conciseness.

**Format penalty.** To ensure generated responses can be parsed by the environment, a hard format penalty is applied:

$$R_{\text{format}}(x) = \begin{cases} 0, & x \text{ is correct in format} \\ -1, & x \text{ is incorrect in format} \end{cases}$$

**What it computes:** if the response matches the required format (e.g., `<action>...</action>` for actions, `<strategy>...</strategy>` for strategies), no penalty. Otherwise, a -1 penalty is applied.

**Why this form:** the binary penalty creates a strong disincentive against malformed outputs that the environment parser would reject. In RL training, the model might otherwise explore output formats that are reasonable language but unusable by the environment, wasting training steps.

**Final reward.** The three terms are combined and clipped:

$$\hat{R}(x) = \text{clip}\left(R(x) + R_{\text{length}}(x) + R_{\text{format}}(x), -1, 1\right)$$

where `$\text{clip}(\cdot, -1, 1)$` restricts the total reward to the interval `$[-1, 1]$`, applied to both strategies and actions.

**Why clipping to `$[-1, 1]$`:** the clipping ensures that rewards remain in a bounded range, which stabilizes advantage normalization in GRPO. Without clipping, a large length or format penalty combined with a successful outcome could produce rewards outside `$[-1, 1]$`, creating outlier advantages that dominate the gradient.

**For actions only**, the self-judgment reward (described in Section 4.4) is additionally added, making the action reward:

$$\hat{R}(a^{i,j}_t) = \text{clip}\left(R(a^{i,j}_t) + R_{\text{length}}(a^{i,j}_t) + R_{\text{format}}(a^{i,j}_t) + R_{\text{judge}}(a^{i,j}_t), -1, 1\right)$$

**Advantage computation.** With the groups constructed and rewards computed, GRPO-style advantage normalization is applied independently to each group. For the strategy-level group:

$$A(z_i) = \frac{\hat{R}(z_i) - \text{mean}(\{\hat{R}(z_k)\}_{k=1}^N)}{\text{std}(\{\hat{R}(z_k)\}_{k=1}^N)}$$

For each action-level group `$\mathcal{G}^{\text{action}}_i$`:

$$A(a^{i,j}_t) = \frac{\hat{R}(a^{i,j}_t) - \text{mean}(\{\hat{R}(a^{i,k}_\ell) \mid a^{i,k}_\ell \in \mathcal{G}^{\text{action}}_i\})}{\text{std}(\{\hat{R}(a^{i,k}_\ell) \mid a^{i,k}_\ell \in \mathcal{G}^{\text{action}}_i\})}$$

**What it computes:** for each group, subtract the mean reward of that group and divide by the standard deviation. This produces a normalized advantage: positive for above-average members, negative for below-average members, with magnitude measured in standard deviations.

**Why normalize per-group separately:** normalizing strategies against strategies and actions-within-a-strategy against other actions-within-that-same-strategy ensures that the advantage signals are on comparable scales. A raw strategy reward of 0.8 might be excellent if other strategies average 0.3, but mediocre if other strategies average 0.9. Group-relative normalization captures this context. More importantly, by keeping action-level comparisons within the same strategy, StraTA ensures that actions are evaluated relative to other attempts to execute the *same plan*, not relative to attempts under different (possibly better or worse) plans.

**The GRPO clipped surrogate.** Both strategy and action optimization use the standard GRPO clipped surrogate objective. For any response `$x$` (which could be a strategy `$z_i$` or an action `$a^{i,j}_t$`) with associated advantage `$A$`:

$$\mathcal{L}(x, A; \theta) = \frac{1}{|x|} \sum_{k=1}^{|x|} \min\left(\rho_{x,k}(\theta) A, \text{clip}(\rho_{x,k}(\theta), 1 - \epsilon, 1 + \epsilon) A\right)$$

where `$|x|$` is the number of tokens in the response, `$\rho_{x,k}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(x_k \mid s, x_{<k})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(x_k \mid s, x_{<k})}$` is the importance sampling ratio for token `$x_k$` (the ratio of the probability under the current policy to the probability under the old policy that generated the sample), and `$\epsilon$` is the clipping threshold.

**What it computes:** for each token in the response, the surrogate takes the minimum of the unclipped importance-weighted advantage and the clipped version. If the advantage is positive (the response was better than average), the ratio is clipped at `$1 + \epsilon$` to prevent the policy from increasing the probability of that token too aggressively. If the advantage is negative (the response was worse than average), the ratio is clipped at `$1 - \epsilon$` to prevent the policy from decreasing the probability too aggressively. The result is a conservative policy update.

**Why this form (PPO/GRPO clipping):** the clipping mechanism is the standard trust-region technique from PPO, adapted by GRPO for group-relative advantages. It prevents the policy from changing too much in a single update, which is critical for stable RL training with LLMs because large policy changes can cause the model to forget its pre-trained capabilities or collapse to degenerate outputs. The paper uses `$\epsilon_{\text{low}} = 0.2$` and `$\epsilon_{\text{high}} = 0.28$` (Table 7), meaning the policy can increase token probabilities by at most 28% relative to the old policy and decrease them by at most 20%.

**The KL divergence penalty.** Following standard GRPO practice, a KL divergence penalty between the current policy and a reference policy (the initial pre-trained model) is added to prevent catastrophic forgetting:

$$\beta D_{\text{KL}}(\pi_\theta \parallel \pi_{\text{ref}})$$

where `$\beta$` controls the strength of the penalty. The paper does not report the specific `$\beta$` value used, but includes it in the objective as a standard regularization term.

**The full StraTA training objective.** Combining strategy-level and action-level objectives with the KL penalty:

$$\mathcal{J}_{\text{StraTA}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{G}_{\text{strategy}}, \{\mathcal{G}^{\text{action}}_i\}_{i=1}^N \sim \pi_\theta} \left[ \sum_{z_i \in \mathcal{G}_{\text{strategy}}} \mathcal{L}(z_i, A(z_i); \theta) + \sum_{i=1}^N \sum_{a^{i,j}_t \in \mathcal{G}^{\text{action}}_i} \mathcal{L}(a^{i,j}_t, A(a^{i,j}_t); \theta) - \beta D_{\text{KL}}(\pi_\theta \parallel \pi_{\text{ref}}) \right]$$

**What it computes:** for each training batch, sample tasks and construct hierarchical groups as described. For each strategy in the strategy group, compute its advantage and apply the GRPO surrogate to all its tokens. For each action in each action-level group, compute its advantage and apply the GRPO surrogate to all its tokens. Sum everything together (strategies and actions are treated as equal contributors to the objective) and subtract the KL penalty. The expectation is over the sampling process.

**Why this form (both levels summed together):** the paper does not introduce separate weighting coefficients for strategy-level and action-level losses—they are simply summed. This implies that the model receives approximately equal learning signal from strategy improvement and action improvement, since each task produces one strategy and (on average across `$M$` rollouts) many actions. In practice, the action-level loss likely dominates in terms of total token count (actions are more numerous), but the strategy-level loss provides the critical signal for improving high-level planning. The joint optimization ensures that strategy quality and execution fidelity improve together rather than one at the expense of the other.

**Training hyperparameters (Table 7).** The model is trained for 150 steps with a batch size of 16 tasks (yielding `$16 \times 32 = 512$` trajectories per step). The optimizer is AdamW with learning rate `$1 \times 10^{-6}$` and weight decay 0.01. Training temperature is 1.0; evaluation uses temperature 0.7 with top-p 0.8 and top-k 20. The token limit for prompts is 7168 and for responses is 1024.

#### Diverse Strategy Rollout via Farthest-Point Sampling

A practical problem arises when sampling multiple strategies from the same base model: even at elevated temperature, the model tends to produce highly similar strategies. The paper observes that "the model produces highly similar strategies within the same group, which limits exploration of the strategy space." If all `$N$` strategies are near-identical variations of the same idea (e.g., "search for the item and then buy it"), the strategy-level comparisons become uninformative—there is nothing to compare, so the model receives a weak learning signal for strategy improvement.

**The oversampling approach.** StraTA addresses this by first massively oversampling candidates and then selecting a diverse subset. Specifically, instead of sampling `$N$` strategies directly, the model samples `$\sigma \times N$` candidate strategies (where `$\sigma = 8$` in all experiments, yielding 32 candidates for `$N = 4$`), and then applies farthest-point sampling to select `$N$` strategies that are maximally different from one another.

**Why oversample instead of directly constraining generation:** directly constraining the model to produce diverse outputs during generation is difficult with standard autoregressive sampling—techniques like nucleus sampling or temperature scaling affect token-level diversity but don't guarantee semantic-level diversity at the sequence level. Oversampling and filtering is computationally cheaper than developing a constrained decoding method and leverages the fact that even a peaked distribution has some probability mass on diverse outputs if you sample enough times.

**Semantic embedding.** To quantify diversity, each candidate strategy `$z_i$` is encoded as a normalized vector using a pre-trained sentence embedding model:

$$e_i = \phi(z_i), \quad e_i = e_i / \|e_i\|_2$$

where `$\phi(\cdot)$` is the MiniLM-L6 embedding model (Table 7; a lightweight sentence transformer from Reimers and Gurevych, 2019). The normalization ensures that the inner product `$e_i^\top e_j$` equals the cosine similarity between strategies `$i$` and `$j$`.

**Why MiniLM-L6:** it is a small, fast embedding model that produces 384-dimensional vectors. The paper notes that selection "takes only a few milliseconds per task, which is negligible relative to the cost of LLM rollouts" (Appendix B.4), so the choice of embedding model is driven by computational efficiency rather than state-of-the-art semantic representation quality. The key requirement is that semantically similar strategies have high cosine similarity, which even lightweight models achieve.

**Farthest-point sampling algorithm (Algorithm 1).** The selection proceeds greedily:

**Step 1—Initialization.** Compute the centroid of all candidate embeddings:

$$\bar{e} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{C}|} \sum_{z_i \in \mathcal{C}} e_i$$

where `$\mathcal{C}$` is the set of `$\sigma N$` candidates. Select the candidate closest to the centroid as the first member of the diverse set:

$$z^* = \arg\max_{z_i \in \mathcal{C}} e_i^\top \bar{e}$$

This ensures the first selected strategy is representative of the overall distribution rather than an outlier.

**Step 2—Iterative selection.** For each subsequent selection step, choose the candidate that is most different from all already-selected strategies. Formally, at each step, compute, for each remaining candidate, its maximum similarity to any already-selected strategy, and pick the candidate that minimizes this maximum similarity:

$$z_{\text{select}} = \arg\min_{z_i \notin \mathcal{Z}} \max_{z_j \in \mathcal{Z}} e_i^\top e_j$$

where `$\mathcal{Z}$` is the set of already-selected strategies.

**What it computes:** this implements the classic farthest-point sampling (FPS) algorithm in semantic space. It greedily builds a set where the minimum pairwise distance (or equivalently, maximum pairwise similarity) is as large as possible. The result is a set of `$N$` strategies that are spread out across the semantic space of the candidate pool, representing qualitatively different approaches to the task.

**Why max-min selection:** the max-min criterion is the defining property of FPS and guarantees that the selected set is a `$2$`-approximation to the optimal max-min diverse subset in terms of the minimum pairwise distance—no algorithm can do better than a factor of 2 without exponential computation. Greedy FPS runs in `$O(\sigma N^2 d)$` time (where `$d$` is the embedding dimension), which for `$\sigma = 8$`, `$N = 4$`, and MiniLM's `$d = 384$` is trivially fast.

**Effect on training.** The diverse strategy set makes strategy-level GRPO comparisons more informative because the strategies now represent genuinely different approaches rather than minor textual variations. The model can observe: "when I try the explore-everything strategy, it succeeds 60% of the time, but when I try the efficient-search strategy, it succeeds 80% of the time." This provides a meaningful gradient for improving strategy generation. The ablation in Table 3 shows that the Diverse variant alone improves ALFWorld performance from 79.0% (Vanilla) to 87.9%—a substantial gain that confirms the importance of strategy-space exploration.

**The oversampling ratio `$\sigma$`.** Table 4 sweeps `$\sigma \in \{1, 2, 4, 8\}$`. When `$\sigma = 1$`, there is no oversampling—the FPS step is skipped and strategies are used as-is. This is equivalent to the Vanilla variant (no diversity enhancement). As `$\sigma$` increases, performance improves steadily: ALFWorld goes from 81.9% (`$\sigma = 1$`) to 88.6% (`$\sigma = 8$`), and WebShop from 66.7% to 73.4%. This trend confirms that more aggressive oversampling (and thus more effective diversity selection) consistently helps.

#### Step-Level Auxiliary Reward via Critical Self-Judgment

The broadcast reward mechanism (assigning the same trajectory-level reward to every action) is crude: it cannot distinguish between an action that was critical for success and one that was irrelevant or even counterproductive but happened to be in a successful trajectory. Moreover, optimizing only for final success creates a perverse incentive: the model might learn to achieve high rewards through opportunistic actions that happen to work but do not actually follow the intended strategy. The paper identifies this as a key challenge:

> "optimizing only for final success may favor opportunistic actions that work in hindsight but do not faithfully follow the intended strategy."

**The self-judgment mechanism.** To address this, StraTA introduces a retrospective evaluation step performed by the agent itself after each trajectory completes. The model reviews the full episode and flags any action steps that were problematic according to two criteria: the step neither followed the global strategy nor advanced task progress.

**Judgment prompt.** The agent receives a specialized prompt (provided in Appendix B.2) that includes the task description, the global strategy, and the complete action history. The prompt instructs:

> "You should point out all the problematic steps. A step is said to be problematic if it neither follows the global strategy nor pushes forward the task progress."

The model is asked to output a list of step indices within `<judgment>...</judgment>` tags. The prompt includes a few-shot example showing the expected format.

**What this computes concretely:** given a trajectory `$\tau_{i,j}$` generated under strategy `$z_i$`, the model produces:

$$\mathcal{I}_{i,j} \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid \mathcal{P}, \tau_{i,j})$$

where `$\mathcal{P}$` is the self-judgment prompt and `$\mathcal{I}_{i,j}$` is a set of flagged action-step indices (e.g., `[2, 5]` means steps 2 and 5 were problematic). The judgment is performed by the agent model itself—the same `$\pi_\theta$` being trained—not by a separate judge model.

**Why self-judgment rather than external judge:** using the agent's own model avoids the computational cost of training or running a separate judge model. It also ensures that the judgment capability improves in parallel with the agent's overall capabilities—as the agent gets better at strategy-guided execution, it also gets better at recognizing when it deviates from its strategy. However, this creates a potential circularity: if the model is bad at recognizing its own mistakes, the judgment signal may be noisy or systematically biased. The paper does not extensively analyze the quality of self-judgments, which is a notable omission.

**The auxiliary reward.** Flagged steps receive a penalty:

$$R_{\text{judge}}(a^{i,j}_t) = -\kappa \cdot \mathbb{1}[t \in \mathcal{I}_{i,j}]$$

where `$\kappa = 0.1$` is the self-judgment reward weight (Table 7) and `$\mathbb{1}[t \in \mathcal{I}_{i,j}]$` is 1 if step `$t$` was flagged and 0 otherwise.

**What it computes:** each flagged action step receives a penalty of `$-\kappa = -0.1$` subtracted from its overall reward. Unflagged steps receive no penalty. This is a relatively small penalty—`$\kappa = 0.1$` compared to the `$[-1, 1]$` range of the outcome reward—ensuring that strategy alignment is a secondary objective that nudges behavior without dominating the primary success signal.

**Why `$\kappa = 0.1$`:** Table 6 sweeps `$\kappa \in \{0.01, 0.1, 1.0\}$`. A very small weight (`$\kappa = 0.01$`) "underutilizes the benefits of critical self-judgment"—the penalty is too weak to meaningfully influence behavior. A large weight (`$\kappa = 1.0$`) "places excessive reliance on self-judgment quality, which further harms performance"—the model overfits to the (potentially imperfect) self-judgment signal and may learn to avoid actions that the judge (incorrectly) flags, even if those actions would lead to success. The intermediate value `$\kappa = 0.1$` provides the best trade-off, achieving 88.6% on ALFWorld and 73.4% on WebShop versus 84.5%/73.3% at `$\kappa = 0.01$` and 87.1%/61.8% at `$\kappa = 1.0$`.

**Integration into the action reward.** The self-judgment penalty is additively included in the final action reward, replacing Equation 13 for action steps:

$$\hat{R}(a^{i,j}_t) = \text{clip}\left(R(a^{i,j}_t) + R_{\text{length}}(a^{i,j}_t) + R_{\text{format}}(a^{i,j}_t) + R_{\text{judge}}(a^{i,j}_t), -1, 1\right)$$

**Why additive rather than multiplicative:** adding the penalty means it shifts the reward by a constant amount regardless of the outcome reward magnitude. A multiplicative penalty (e.g., scaling the outcome reward by a factor based on judgment) would have different effects depending on whether the trajectory succeeded or failed, making the learning signal less predictable. The additive penalty ensures that flagged actions are consistently penalized by the same amount in all contexts.

**What the penalty achieves.** The self-judgment mechanism creates a direct incentive for strategy-aligned behavior. Consider a trajectory that succeeds (outcome reward = 1) but contains some actions that do not follow the strategy. Without self-judgment, every action in that trajectory receives a reward of 1, and the model cannot learn that some of those actions were sub-optimal. With self-judgment, the flagged actions receive a reward of `$\text{clip}(1 + 0 + 0 - 0.1, -1, 1) = 0.9$`, while unflagged actions receive 1.0. In the GRPO advantage computation, this small difference means flagged actions have slightly lower advantages than unflagged actions within the same action-level group, creating a gradient that pushes the policy away from strategy-deviating behaviors.

**Does the judgment take an environment step?** No. The paper explicitly states that "these judging steps do not receive reward" and are not counted as environment steps. The judgment is generated after the trajectory ends, using a separate model call, and its only effect is through the auxiliary reward term applied retroactively to the action steps. This keeps the environment interaction budget unchanged—the agent doesn't spend an environment step on self-reflection.

**The orthogonality of the two auxiliary techniques.** The ablation study in Table 3 demonstrates that diverse strategy rollout and critical self-judgment contribute in largely complementary ways. The Diverse variant (only FPS) substantially improves ALFWorld performance (79.0% → 87.9%) but provides only a small gain on WebShop (64.0% → 64.6%). The Judgment variant (only self-judgment) substantially improves WebShop (64.0% → 66.7%) but provides a smaller gain on ALFWorld (79.0% → 81.9%). The full StraTA (both techniques) achieves 88.6% on ALFWorld and 73.4% on WebShop, suggesting the techniques address different bottlenecks: FPS helps with strategy-space exploration (more important for ALFWorld's diverse subtask types), while self-judgment helps with action-level credit assignment (more important for WebShop's sequential search-and-compare tasks). The paper describes these as operating "in a largely orthogonal manner."

**Computational cost of self-judgment.** The self-judgment step adds one additional model call per trajectory (after the trajectory completes). Since each trajectory already involves up to `$H$` action-generation calls (where `$H$` is the horizon, up to 50 for ALFWorld), the judgment call represents approximately `$1/H$` additional computation—roughly 2% overhead for a 50-step trajectory. Figure 3b confirms that the judgment step's wall-clock time is negligible compared to the main action rollouts and training steps.

#### Summary of Key Design Choices

- **Fixed once-per-episode strategy** (Section 4.1): cleanly separates planning from execution; avoids learning when to re-plan, but limits adaptability to dynamic environments.
- **Top-`$\delta$` strategy reward aggregation** (Section 4.2, Equation 10): estimates strategy quality from its best-executed rollouts to reduce confounding from execution noise, using `$\delta = 0.5$` as a middle ground between too-noisy single-trajectory estimates and too-pessimistic full-mean estimates.
- **Separate strategy-level and action-level GRPO groups** (Section 4.2): isolates strategy comparison from action comparison, ensuring that a good strategy with bad execution can still receive a positive strategy-level signal while the action-level group identifies which executions were poor.
- **Farthest-point sampling for strategy diversity** (Section 4.3): forces the model to explore qualitatively different approaches rather than minor textual variations, using a lightweight embedding model (MiniLM-L6) for fast semantic similarity computation.
- **Self-judgment penalty weight `$\kappa = 0.1$`** (Section 4.4): provides a small nudge toward strategy-aligned execution without overfitting to potentially imperfect self-judgments; larger weights (`$\kappa = 1.0$`) harm performance by over-relying on the judge.
- **Broadcast reward with additive auxiliary penalties** (Section 4.2, Equation 18): keeps the method simple by avoiding learned credit assignment, while the self-judgment term provides a targeted correction for the most egregious cases of strategy deviation.
- **Non-cumulative generation setting** (consistent with GiGPO): each action is generated from a fresh prompt containing the strategy, current state, and task description, rather than from the full interaction history—this stabilizes training by keeping context lengths manageable.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Explicit Strategy Abstraction Transforms a Reactive MDP into a Structured Two-Level Problem

The field's default assumption—codified in essentially all prior agentic RL work including PPO, GRPO, GiGPO, and ArCHer—is that the agent policy is a reactive mapping from state to action. This is not an accident of implementation; it reflects a deeper commitment to the idea that an LLM's autoregressive generation, conditioned on sufficient context, *is* the plan. ReAct agents interleave reasoning and action, but the reasoning is local and ephemeral—generated anew at each step, never committed to, never evaluated as a persistent hypothesis about how to solve the task.

StraTA's central conceptual move is to reject this assumption. It argues that long-horizon decision-making is fundamentally different from single-turn reasoning or short-horizon interaction because the space of possible *trajectories* is combinatorial, and exploring it requires committing to a high-level approach before optimizing low-level actions. The paper's diagnosis is that **the reactive formulation forces the policy to simultaneously solve two distinct problems—"what approach should I take?" and "what action should I take now?"—from the same limited local information**, creating an unnecessary joint optimization that degrades both exploration (the model never systematically tries different high-level strategies) and credit assignment (the model cannot distinguish a good strategy from good luck in execution).

This is not merely an architectural tweak. It is a **structural reframing** of the agentic RL problem from a flat MDP to a two-level one where the high-level decision (what strategy to follow?) is made once at episode start and the low-level decisions (what actions to take?) are conditioned on that fixed choice. The paper doesn't frame itself as hierarchical RL, but the intellectual structure is the same: decomposing a hard flat problem into coarser temporal abstraction (strategy) and finer execution (actions), with separate learning signals for each level. The key difference from classical HRL is that StraTA doesn't learn when to re-plan or what the subgoals are—the strategy is generated once as a natural-language plan, fixed for the episode, and the RL objective handles the rest.

**Why this matters beyond performance.** The decomposition creates a **diagnostic framework** for understanding *why* agents fail on long-horizon tasks. Before StraTA, failure could be attributed to poor exploration or poor credit assignment, but these were vague catch-all explanations. The two-level structure makes failure interpretable: did the model generate a poor strategy (planning failure), or did it fail to execute a good strategy (execution failure)? The top-`\delta` strategy reward (Equation 10) provides a quantitative measure of strategy quality separated from execution noise, and the self-judgment mechanism (Section 4.4) explicitly identifies execution steps that deviate from the strategy. This diagnostic capability—while not the paper's main claimed contribution—is arguably the most transferable insight: **future agentic RL systems can and should provide separate feedback on planning quality and execution fidelity.**

The evidence for this reframing's value is the consistent improvement over GiGPO, which represents the strongest reactive baseline with sophisticated credit assignment (anchor states). GiGPO improves *how* rewards are distributed but leaves the reactive structure intact. StraTA changes *what the policy conditions on*, and the gains—from 86.7% to 90.7% on ALFWorld at 1.5B, from 65.0% to 82.5% success on WebShop—suggest that the structural change matters more than better credit assignment within the reactive framework. The fact that the gap is largest on WebShop (17.5 percentage points at 1.5B) is telling: WebShop requires sustained, multi-step search strategies where a reactive agent easily gets lost in irrelevant product pages; a fixed strategy ("start with a generic search, then refine based on attributes") keeps behavior coherent.

This is a **fundamental shift**, not an incremental refinement. It changes the vocabulary of the field from "improve the reactive policy" to "separate strategy from execution," opening a new axis for research (strategy representations, strategy revision, strategy-aware credit assignment) that was previously invisible.

---

### Innovation 2: Hierarchical Group Construction Enables Joint Optimization of Strategy and Execution Without Separate Training Loops

Prior work that separates planning from execution—whether classical hierarchical RL (options, feudal networks) or LLM-based plan-and-act approaches (Erdogan et al., 2025)—typically requires separate training procedures for the high-level and low-level policies. This creates practical complications: the two levels may converge at different rates, the high-level policy's training signal depends on the low-level policy's current quality, and hyperparameter tuning becomes a two-dimensional search.

StraTA's hierarchical group construction (Section 4.2) sidesteps this entirely through a **unified GRPO objective that naturally produces separate comparison groups for strategies and actions without introducing separate critics, value functions, or training phases.** The key insight is architectural rather than algorithmic: by organizing rollouts into a `$N \times M$` grid (strategies `\times` rollouts per strategy) and applying GRPO's group-relative advantage computation independently to the strategy "column" and each action "row," the system obtains strategy-level and action-level learning signals from the same underlying trajectories with no additional infrastructure.

**What makes this distinctive.** Standard GRPO already compares multiple outputs (a "group") to compute relative advantages. StraTA's innovation is to recognize that the group structure can be made **hierarchical** by controlling what gets compared to what. Strategies are compared only to other strategies; actions under strategy `$z_i$` are compared only to other actions under `$z_i$`. This is a simple idea—perhaps deceptively simple—but it has a non-obvious consequence: **the strategy-level reward and action-level reward can use different aggregation functions without creating inconsistencies in the objective.** The strategy reward uses top-`\delta` mean (Equation 10) to estimate the *potential* of a strategy under good execution; the action reward uses the broadcast trajectory reward (Equation 9) to provide per-step signals. If strategies and actions were in the same GRPO group, these different reward schemes would produce incomparable advantages. The hierarchical grouping cleanly separates them.

**Comparison to the most relevant prior work.** GiGPO introduces "anchor states" to improve credit assignment within reactive trajectories—a refinement of *how* the trajectory reward is distributed across steps. StraTA's hierarchical groups are orthogonal: they refine *what gets compared to what*. GiGPO could in principle be combined with StraTA's strategy guidance, and the fact that StraTA substantially outperforms GiGPO suggests that the "what to compare" question is more fundamental than the "how to distribute" question, at least on these benchmarks.

**The diagnostic value of separate groups.** The hierarchical construction also provides a natural experiment for understanding agent behavior. If strategy-level advantages have high variance but action-level advantages are uniformly positive, the bottleneck is strategy generation—the model needs to learn better planning. If strategy-level advantages are uniformly positive but action-level advantages have high variance, the bottleneck is execution—the model generates good plans but fails to follow them. The paper doesn't perform this analysis explicitly, but the framework makes it straightforward.

This is a **moderate refinement** of GRPO rather than a fundamentally new algorithm—it extends the group concept to two levels rather than one, which is conceptually straightforward once stated. Its significance lies not in algorithmic novelty but in **demonstrating that a single end-to-end RL objective can jointly optimize planning and execution when the group structure is designed to match the problem decomposition.** This has implications beyond StraTA: any hierarchical decision problem trained with GRPO could potentially benefit from similar group structuring.

The evidence for this innovation's effectiveness is indirect but clear: the Vanilla variant in Table 3 (hierarchical groups without diversity or self-judgment) already achieves 79.0% on ALFWorld and 64.0% on WebShop at 3B scale with reduced batch size—substantially above the GRPO baseline shown in Figure 4, which uses the same base model but a purely reactive formulation. The hierarchical groups alone provide most of StraTA's gains, with the auxiliary techniques providing additional orthogonal improvements.

### Innovation 3: Strategy Diversity as a First-Class Exploration Problem, Solved by Semantic Farthest-Point Sampling

The paper identifies a phenomenon that is well-known in practice but rarely addressed systematically in agentic RL: **when an LLM samples multiple high-level plans, the plans tend to collapse to near-identical variations, making strategy-level comparisons uninformative.** This is not a bug of temperature sampling—even aggressive temperature scaling affects token-level diversity but doesn't guarantee that the resulting *sequences* are semantically distinct. The model's pre-trained distribution may be sharply peaked around a particular planning approach (e.g., "search then buy" for WebShop), and no amount of per-token stochasticity will produce a qualitatively different plan (e.g., "compare prices first, then search by attribute").

The standard solution in the LLM literature would be constrained decoding or prompting for diversity. The paper's approach—oversampling and then selecting via farthest-point sampling in semantic embedding space—is **conceptually distinctive because it reframes diversity as a post-hoc selection problem rather than a generation-time constraint.** This has two advantages. First, it is computationally cheap: the model generates many candidates using standard sampling, and the selection step uses a lightweight embedding model that runs in milliseconds. Second, it is domain-agnostic: the same farthest-point selection algorithm works for any task without task-specific diversity prompts or constraints.

**What's novel about the semantic FPS formulation.** Farthest-point sampling is a standard algorithm in computational geometry and active learning, but applying it to *natural language strategies encoded by sentence embeddings* is a non-obvious transfer. The key assumption is that cosine similarity in MiniLM-L6 embedding space corresponds to semantic similarity of *plans*, not just surface-form similarity. Two strategies that use different words but describe the same approach (e.g., "explore the room systematically" vs. "check each location one by one") should have high cosine similarity and thus be unlikely to both appear in the diverse set. Two strategies that use similar words but describe different approaches (unlikely given how LLMs generate strategy text, but possible) might be incorrectly treated as similar. The paper doesn't validate the embedding model's ability to distinguish planning approaches specifically, which is a gap, but the strong empirical results in Table 4 (monotonic improvement as `\sigma` increases from 1 to 8) provide indirect evidence that the semantic similarity signal is meaningful.

**The exploration-diversity connection.** Prior work on exploration in agentic RL (Agent Q, Ragen) focuses on *action-level* diversity—encouraging the agent to try different actions in the same state. StraTA shifts attention to *strategy-level* diversity—encouraging the agent to try fundamentally different approaches to the same task. This is a higher-leverage form of exploration because a single new strategy generates many new action trajectories, while a single new action only explores one new path. The FPS mechanism ensures that the strategy-level exploration budget is spent efficiently by maximizing the semantic spread of the selected strategies.

**Why diversity matters specifically for GRPO-style training.** In GRPO, the learning signal comes from relative reward comparisons within a group. If all strategies in the group are similar, the reward differences between them are largely noise (variation in execution quality), and the strategy-level advantage signal is weak. By ensuring the strategies are semantically diverse, FPS increases the probability that genuinely *better* strategies will receive higher rewards and genuinely *worse* strategies will receive lower rewards, producing a stronger gradient for strategy improvement. This is a **diagnostic insight**: the effectiveness of group-relative RL depends on the *informativeness* of the group, not just its size, and diversity is a key determinant of informativeness for high-level decisions.

This is a **moderate conceptual contribution**—applying a known algorithm (FPS) to a new domain (strategy selection) with a specific justification (making GRPO comparisons more informative). Its significance is amplified by the experimental finding that it provides larger gains on ALFWorld than on WebShop (Table 3: +8.9% vs. +0.6%), revealing an interaction between task structure and the value of strategy diversity. ALFWorld has diverse subtask types (Pick, Look, Clean, Heat, Cool, Pick2) that benefit from different strategies; WebShop's tasks are more homogeneous (all involve search and purchase). This suggests that FPS-based diversity is most valuable when the task distribution itself is diverse, a finding with practical implications for when to deploy this technique.

### Innovation 4: Self-Judgment as a Mechanism for Enforcing Strategy-Execution Alignment Without External Supervision

The paper introduces a subtle but important problem that arises specifically in strategy-conditioned RL: **the model can learn to achieve high rewards through actions that succeed but do not follow the stated strategy.** This is a form of "reward hacking" unique to the strategy-guided setting. In a purely reactive framework, there is no strategy to deviate from, so this problem doesn't exist. In StraTA, the strategy is part of the input to the action policy, but if the RL objective only rewards final success, the model can learn to ignore the strategy entirely and revert to reactive behavior—defeating the purpose of the framework.

The paper's solution—having the agent judge its own trajectory against the strategy and penalize misaligned steps—is **conceptually distinctive because it creates a self-consistency objective without requiring ground-truth alignment labels or a separate critic model.** The agent learns to both execute strategies and evaluate execution, and these two capabilities improve in tandem.

**What makes this different from existing self-critique approaches.** Prior work on self-critique (Reflexion, self-refinement) uses the model to generate feedback that is then incorporated into future attempts—an outer-loop improvement process. StraTA's self-judgment is different: it operates as an **inner-loop training signal**, directly modifying the reward for individual action steps within a trajectory. The judgment isn't used to generate a better next attempt; it's used to provide per-step credit assignment that distinguishes strategy-following actions from strategy-deviating actions within the same trajectory. This is a more fine-grained use of self-evaluation than prior work, and it specifically addresses the credit assignment bottleneck in long-horizon RL.

**The dual-criterion design.** The judgment criteria—"neither follows the global strategy nor pushes forward the task progress"—are conjunctive rather than disjunctive. An action must fail *both* criteria to be flagged. This is subtle but important: an action that deviates from the strategy but accidentally advances progress (e.g., taking a shortcut that the strategy didn't anticipate) is NOT penalized, because the goal is task success, not blind strategy adherence. Conversely, an action that follows the strategy but fails to advance progress (e.g., the strategy itself is flawed) is also NOT penalized. Only actions that are both strategy-deviating AND unhelpful receive the penalty. This dual criterion prevents the self-judgment from becoming a straitjacket that punishes intelligent adaptation.

**The choice of `\kappa = 0.1` as a deliberate trade-off.** The ablation in Table 6 reveals a non-monotonic relationship between penalty weight and performance. At `\kappa = 0.01`, the penalty is too weak to influence behavior. At `\kappa = 1.0`, it degrades performance substantially (WebShop drops from 73.4% to 61.8%). This suggests that the self-judgments are imperfect—the model sometimes flags actions that were actually reasonable or misses actions that were problematic—and a large penalty weight causes the model to overfit to these errors. The intermediate value `\kappa = 0.1` provides enough signal to encourage strategy-aligned behavior without letting judgment errors dominate the learning. This is a **practical insight with broader implications**: when using LLM self-evaluation as a training signal, the signal should be treated as a weak auxiliary rather than a primary objective, because self-evaluation quality is correlated with policy quality and improves over training.

**The orthogonality to outcome-based RL.** The self-judgment penalty and the trajectory-level outcome reward serve fundamentally different purposes. The outcome reward answers "did this trajectory succeed?"—an objective, verifiable signal. The self-judgment answers "did specific actions follow the intended strategy?"—a subjective, model-generated signal. By combining them additively with a small weight on the subjective signal, StraTA creates a reward that balances task success with process quality. This is a **diagnostic contribution**: it demonstrates that process-level feedback (even noisy, self-generated feedback) can complement outcome-level feedback in RL training when the objective requires both getting the right answer and getting it the right way.

This is a **moderate refinement** in terms of technique—LLM-as-a-judge is a well-established paradigm, and applying it to per-step credit assignment is a natural extension. The conceptual contribution is the **identification of the strategy-execution alignment problem as a distinct failure mode in strategy-conditioned RL**, and the demonstration that a lightweight self-judgment mechanism with carefully chosen penalty weight can partially address it without external supervision.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The paper evaluates on three benchmarks served through the AgentGym unified interface (Xi et al., 2025b): **ALFWorld** (Shridhar et al., 2021) — a text-based embodied household environment with 6 subtask types (Pick, Look, Clean, Heat, Cool, Pick2), maximum 50 steps per episode; **WebShop** (Yao et al., 2022) — a web-based shopping environment requiring product search, comparison, and selection, maximum 15 steps per episode; and **SciWorld** (Wang et al., 2022) — a text-based scientific experimentation environment covering measurement, conditional testing, object finding, chemical mixing, and lifespan reasoning, maximum 20 steps per episode. The paper does not report explicit dataset sizes or train/test splits for any benchmark, which is a notable omission for reproducibility.

- **Base model(s).** All experiments use Qwen2.5-Instruct models (Yang et al., 2024) at two scales: **Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct** and **Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct**, with the 3B variant used only for ablation studies in Section 5.3. The choice is justified as "representative architectures of different scales" (Section 5.1), and the models are instruction-tuned rather than base models, providing a reasonable starting point for agent fine-tuning.

- **Metrics.** **ALFWorld**: success rate (%) — a binary per-task completion metric, reported as both an overall average across all tasks and a per-subtask breakdown. **WebShop**: both task score (measuring partial progress toward the target product, range 0.0–1.0) and success rate (whether the final selected product fully matches specifications). **SciWorld**: normalized task score (%) — the standard SciWorld evaluation metric that measures task-specific completion, reported per category and as an overall average. The paper states that ALFWorld and WebShop training rewards use a "step-decayed" design: the environment provides a rule-based binary success signal (1 for success, 0 for failure) with a decay factor so "trajectories that complete the task in fewer steps" receive larger rewards, with a maximum value of 1 (Appendix B.1).

- **Baselines.** The paper includes three categories of baselines (Section 5.1). **Prompting-based closed-source models**: GPT-5.1 (OpenAI, 2025), Claude-4-Sonnet (Anthropic, 2025), Gemini-2.5-Flash (Comanici et al., 2025). **Prompting-based open-source methods**: Vanilla (direct action generation without reasoning) and ReAct (Yao et al., 2023; interleaves reasoning and action at each step), both using Qwen2.5-Instruct. **Training-based RL methods**: PPO (Schulman et al., 2017; critic-based actor-critic), RLOO (Ahmadian et al., 2024; leave-one-out reward estimation), GRPO (Shao et al., 2024; group-relative advantage), and GiGPO (Feng et al., 2025b; anchor states for credit assignment), all trained on the same Qwen2.5 backbones. For SciWorld only, two additional pre-trained RL agents from prior work are included: AgentGym-RL-7B and ScalingInter-7B (Xi et al., 2025c). The closed-source models are evaluated via prompting only (no RL training), making them "frontier model" reference points rather than direct training comparisons.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper does not use a unified "generation budget" metric like some test-time compute papers. Instead, the primary computational unit is the **training batch**: all methods train for 150 steps with a batch size of 16 tasks (512 trajectories per step for StraTA given `N=4` and `M=8`). For the wall-clock efficiency analysis (Figure 3), the paper reports per-step training time in seconds, measured on a single node with 8 NVIDIA H100 GPUs. The computational overhead analysis (Section 5.3) compares StraTA's sampling cost (`N × M = 32` trajectories per task) against GRPO with an equivalent group size, noting that "the total computation is comparable to that of GRPO with a group size of `N × M`" because the additional strategy generation and self-judgment calls represent only `1/H` of the main action rollouts when the horizon `H` is large.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper reports that "Results from open-source models are averaged over 3 random seeds" (Table 1 caption, Table 2 caption). No cross-validation, statistical significance testing, or confidence intervals are reported. The ablation studies in Tables 3–6 use Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct with batch size reduced to 4 (while keeping 150 training steps) to "amplify the differences between variants" (Section 5.3). This reduced batch size means the ablations use substantially fewer environment interactions than the main experiments (4 × 150 = 600 trajectories total vs. 16 × 150 = 2400 for main results), which should be considered when comparing absolute numbers between main results and ablations.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Overall Performance on ALFWorld and WebShop (Table 1)

The headline numbers: **StraTA-7B achieves 93.1% success on ALFWorld and 84.2% success on WebShop**, outperforming all baselines across both model scales.

**ALFWorld results.** At the 7B scale, StraTA reaches 93.1% overall success, compared to GiGPO's 90.8% (+2.3 percentage points), PPO's 87.4% (+5.7 pp), GRPO's 77.1% (+16.0 pp), and the best closed-source model Claude-4-Sonnet's 72.9% (+20.2 pp). The largest gains over GiGPO occur on specific subtasks: Look (92.3% vs. 82.7%, +9.6 pp), Heat (100.0% vs. 83.7%, +16.3 pp), and Clean (97.5% vs. 98.8%, -1.3 pp — the only subtask where StraTA-7B underperforms GiGPO-7B). At the 1.5B scale, StraTA achieves 90.7% overall, surpassing GiGPO-7B (90.8%) by essentially matching a ~4.7× larger model's performance. The per-subtask pattern at 1.5B is notably different: StraTA dominates on Look (87.2% vs. GiGPO-1.5B's 67.5%, +19.7 pp) and Cool (90.7% vs. 79.8%, +10.9 pp) but loses slightly on Pick (89.5% vs. 94.4%, -4.9 pp) and Clean (92.6% vs. 94.8%, -2.2 pp). This suggests strategy guidance is most valuable on observation-heavy subtasks (Look requires examining objects under conditions; Cool requires temperature manipulation) and less necessary on straightforward manipulation tasks (Pick, Clean) where reactive execution suffices.

**WebShop results.** The gains are substantially larger on WebShop than ALFWorld. StraTA-7B achieves 84.2% success and 91.2% task score, compared to GiGPO-7B's 72.8% success (+11.4 pp) and 84.4% score (+6.8 pp). The gap is even more dramatic at 1.5B: StraTA reaches 82.5% success vs. GiGPO's 65.0% (+17.5 pp), and 91.1% score vs. 83.1% (+8.0 pp). This is the paper's strongest single result: a 1.5B model with strategy guidance outperforms the best frontier closed-source model (GPT-5.1 at 22.2% success) by a factor of ~4× and surpasses all RL baselines including the 7B GiGPO. The magnitude of improvement on WebShop relative to ALFWorld is consistent with the paper's claim that WebShop's multi-step search-and-compare structure benefits disproportionately from explicit strategy guidance — a fixed strategy ("start with generic search, then refine") prevents the agent from getting lost in irrelevant product pages, whereas a reactive agent easily drifts.

**Training curves (Figure 1c).** The paper presents training curves on WebShop showing that StraTA learns faster and reaches a higher asymptote than the GRPO baseline. The qualitative claim from Section 5.2 is that these curves "intuitively show the efficiency of StraTA," but no quantitative efficiency metric (e.g., area under the curve, time-to-threshold) is reported.

**Closed-source model comparison.** The paper includes frontier models (GPT-5.1, Claude-4-Sonnet, Gemini-2.5-Flash) evaluated via prompting only. Claude-4-Sonnet achieves 72.9% on ALFWorld — the strongest prompting result, but still 20.2 pp below StraTA-7B and 4.2 pp below PPO-7B (87.4%). On WebShop, GPT-5.1 reaches only 22.2% success, compared to StraTA-7B's 84.2%. On SciWorld, Claude-4-Sonnet achieves 57.4%, compared to StraTA-7B's 63.5%. These comparisons establish that task-specific RL training (even without strategy guidance) substantially outperforms prompting alone, and that strategy guidance provides additional gains on top of RL training. However, the closed-source models are evaluated in a zero-shot prompting setting, not fine-tuned, making this an apples-to-oranges comparison that primarily demonstrates the value of task-specific training rather than the specific value of strategy guidance.

#### Performance on SciWorld (Table 2)

StraTA-7B achieves a 63.5% overall score on SciWorld, compared to the best closed-source model Claude-4-Sonnet's 57.4% (+6.1 pp) and the best prior trained baseline ScalingInter-7B's 57.0% (+6.5 pp). The per-category breakdown reveals highly uneven gains:

- **Lifespan**: perfect 100.0% — the strongest result across all categories and all methods. The next best are PPO-7B at 97.8% and Claude-4-Sonnet at 90.0%. This category involves reasoning about animal life spans, suggesting strategy guidance is particularly effective for procedural reasoning tasks with clear logical structure.
- **Measure**: 55.7% vs. Claude-4-Sonnet's 45.4% (+10.3 pp) and GRPO-7B's 31.2% (+24.5 pp). A large absolute and relative gain.
- **Chem-Mix**: 78.6% vs. ScalingInter-7B's 88.6% (-10.0 pp). This is the one category where StraTA underperforms a prior method, though it still substantially outperforms PPO-7B (60.9%) and GRPO-7B (32.7%).
- **Test-Cond**: 54.4% vs. AgentGym-RL-7B's 59.0% (-4.6 pp) and ScalingInter-7B's 55.4% (-1.0 pp). Roughly equivalent to prior trained baselines.
- **Find**: 16.3% vs. Claude-4-Sonnet's 45.6% (-29.3 pp) and GRPO-7B's 14.7% (+1.6 pp). This is StraTA's weakest category, substantially below prompting-based methods and near GRPO's level, suggesting that object-finding tasks in SciWorld may benefit less from strategy guidance or that the generated strategies are particularly poor for this category.

The uneven gains on SciWorld are informative. The paper claims strategy guidance is broadly beneficial, but the SciWorld results show it helps enormously on some categories (Lifespan, Measure, Chem-Mix), has no effect on some (Find), and underperforms prior methods on others (Test-Cond). The paper does not analyze *why* certain SciWorld categories benefit more than others, which is a missed opportunity for understanding the conditions under which strategy guidance is most valuable.

#### Computational Efficiency (Figure 3)

Figure 3a reports per-step wall-clock time for PPO (308.1s rollout + 1142.1s training = ~1450s total), GRPO (184.7s rollout + 116.9s training = ~302s total), and StraTA (202.4s rollout + 175.4s training = ~378s total). The key takeaway: **StraTA is only marginally slower than GRPO** (~378s vs. ~302s per step, approximately 1.25× slower) and substantially faster than PPO (~378s vs. ~1450s, approximately 3.8× faster). The rollout times are comparable (202.4s for StraTA vs. 184.7s for GRPO), confirming that the additional strategy generation and self-judgment calls add minimal overhead. Figure 3b breaks down StraTA's per-step time: 202.4s for rollout, 175.4s for training, with the Diverse and Judgment components contributing ~5.8s and ~6.1s respectively — less than 3% of total step time each. This supports the paper's claim that "the overhead of the two components is negligible."

However, the comparison conflates two dimensions: algorithmic efficiency (how much environment interaction is needed to reach a given performance) and wall-clock efficiency (how long each training step takes). The paper shows StraTA is wall-clock competitive with GRPO, but the training curves in Figure 1c suggest StraTA also achieves higher performance with the same number of training steps (150), implying better *sample efficiency* — a separate and arguably more important efficiency dimension that the paper addresses qualitatively but not with a formal efficiency metric (e.g., "X% success at Y steps").

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Effectiveness of core components (Table 3, Figure 4)**: All ablation results in this section use Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct with reduced batch size (4 tasks per step, 150 training steps) to amplify differences. The Vanilla variant (hierarchical groups without FPS or self-judgment) achieves 79.0% on ALFWorld and 64.0% success on WebShop — already a strong baseline compared to the GRPO curve in Figure 4, confirming that hierarchical group construction alone provides substantial gains. Adding only Diverse strategy rollout (FPS) improves ALFWorld to 87.9% (+8.9 pp) but WebShop to only 64.6% (+0.6 pp). Adding only Critical self-judgment improves WebShop to 66.7% (+2.7 pp) but ALFWorld to only 81.9% (+2.9 pp). Full StraTA (both techniques) reaches 88.6% on ALFWorld and 73.4% on WebShop — the techniques contribute in a largely orthogonal manner, with FPS addressing a bottleneck more prominent in ALFWorld and self-judgment addressing a bottleneck more prominent in WebShop. Figure 4's training curves confirm that both the Vanilla variant and the Diverse/Judgment variants converge faster and higher than GRPO.

**Oversampling ratio `σ` (Table 4)**: Sweeping `σ ∈ {1, 2, 4, 8}`, ALFWorld performance increases from 81.9% (`σ = 1`, equivalent to no diversity selection) to 88.6% (`σ = 8`), while WebShop improves from 66.7% to 73.4%. The monotonic improvement suggests that even with `σ = 8` (oversampling 32 candidates for `N = 4` strategies), the model has not reached the saturation point for strategy diversity benefits. The paper does not test higher `σ` values, leaving open whether further oversampling would continue to yield gains.

**Aggregation ratio `δ` (Table 5)**: The `δ` parameter controlling the fraction of top rollouts used for strategy reward estimation is swept at `{0.1, 0.5, 1.0}`. `δ = 0.1` (using only the single best rollout out of 8) yields poor results: 71.0% on ALFWorld and 66.1% on WebShop. The paper attributes this to "too few trajectories" producing unreliable strategy quality estimates. `δ = 1.0` (using all 8 rollouts, equivalent to mean) yields 85.0% on ALFWorld and 71.3% on WebShop — better than `δ = 0.1` but worse than `δ = 0.5` (88.6% ALFWorld, 73.4% WebShop). The paper argues `δ = 1.0` "ignores potential execution errors during action selection, leading to overly pessimistic evaluations." This is a non-obvious finding: one might expect that averaging over more rollouts would produce more stable estimates, but in practice, including low-quality rollouts (where execution noise, not strategy quality, caused failure) biases the strategy reward downward. The optimal `δ = 0.5` represents a trade-off between estimation reliability and robustness to execution noise. The paper does not explore whether the optimal `δ` depends on `M` (number of rollouts per strategy) or on task difficulty.

**Self-judgment reward weight `κ` (Table 6)**: Swept at `{0.01, 0.1, 1.0}`. `κ = 0.01` yields 84.5% on ALFWorld and 73.3% on WebShop — slightly worse than `κ = 0.1`. `κ = 1.0` yields 87.1% on ALFWorld (nearly matching `κ = 0.1`'s 88.6%) but dramatically drops on WebShop to 61.8% (vs. 73.4% at `κ = 0.1`). The WebShop collapse at high `κ` is the most informative result: it suggests that self-judgments are noisier or less reliable on WebShop than on ALFWorld, and a high penalty weight causes the model to overfit to judgment errors. The paper does not analyze *why* WebShop judgments would be less reliable — a potential explanation is that WebShop's product-search tasks make strategy alignment harder to assess (is clicking on a non-target product a strategy deviation or a legitimate exploration step?). The non-monotonic pattern also confirms that self-judgment should be treated as a weak auxiliary signal, not a primary training objective, with the optimal weight depending on judgment quality.

**Additional unreported ablations.** Several natural ablations are missing that would strengthen the paper's claims. (1) **Strategy quality ablation**: what happens if strategies are replaced with random noise or empty strings? This would test whether the strategy content matters or whether the mere act of prepending *any* fixed text improves performance (e.g., through a regularizing effect on generation). (2) **Adaptive vs. fixed strategy**: how much does performance degrade if the strategy is regenerated at each step (reverting to something closer to ReAct) or updated mid-episode? This would quantify the cost of a fixed strategy. (3) **Strategy length ablation**: do longer, more detailed strategies help or hurt? The paper includes a length penalty but doesn't analyze strategy content quality directly. (4) **Embedding model sensitivity**: does FPS with a different embedding model (e.g., stronger sentence transformer, or a simpler TF-IDF baseline) produce similar diversity gains? This would test whether the semantic similarity signal is robust.

### Critical Assessment

The paper claims that StraTA "consistently improves both sample efficiency and final performance over strong baselines" (Section 6). The experimental evidence supports this claim but with important qualifications that the paper does not fully address.

**Does the evidence demonstrate that strategy guidance (not just hierarchical RL or better credit assignment) causes the improvement?** This is the paper's central causal claim, and the evidence is suggestive but not dispositive. The Vanilla variant in Table 3 (hierarchical groups without FPS or self-judgment) achieves 79.0% on ALFWorld and 64.0% on WebShop — substantially above GRPO. This establishes that the *hierarchical group structure* provides most of the gains. But the hierarchical group structure is specifically designed around the strategy abstraction — strategies form the high-level group, actions form the low-level groups. You cannot have hierarchical groups without the strategy abstraction (or some equivalent high-level grouping criterion). The fairest interpretation is that the strategy abstraction and the hierarchical training are a *package* — the strategy provides the natural grouping variable, and the hierarchical training provides the optimization mechanism. Neither alone would work. The paper's claim is therefore best understood as "strategy guidance + hierarchical training outperforms reactive RL" rather than "strategy guidance alone causes the improvement."

A stronger causal test would be: does a *random* or *empty* strategy with hierarchical groups still outperform reactive GRPO? If yes, then the benefit comes from the hierarchical group structure (or from prepending any fixed context), not from meaningful strategy content. The paper does not run this ablation. The case studies in Appendix C show strategies that are clearly task-relevant (e.g., "Explore the room to find where the tissuebox might be and how to get it to the sidetable"), suggesting the strategy content matters, but this is anecdotal.

**Does the evidence support the claim that StraTA scales with model size?** The paper tests two model scales (1.5B and 7B), which is minimal for a scaling claim. At 7B, StraTA achieves 93.1% on ALFWorld vs. 90.7% at 1.5B (+2.4 pp), and 84.2% on WebShop vs. 82.5% (+1.7 pp). These are small absolute improvements — within the range of seed variance — and the paper does not test an intermediate scale (e.g., 3B for full training, not just ablations) or a larger scale (e.g., 14B, 32B). The paper does not claim StraTA scales better than alternatives, only that it works at multiple scales, which the evidence supports modestly. The more interesting finding is that **StraTA-1.5B approaches or matches larger reactive models** — 90.7% vs. GiGPO-7B's 90.8% on ALFWorld, and 82.5% vs. GiGPO-7B's 72.8% on WebShop — suggesting strategy guidance can compensate for reduced model capacity.

**Does the evidence support generalization across diverse task types?** The three benchmarks (ALFWorld, WebShop, SciWorld) span embodied manipulation, web navigation, and scientific experimentation — a reasonably diverse set. However, all three share properties that may favor strategy guidance: they require multi-step procedures, have clear task descriptions from which strategies can be inferred, and provide binary or near-binary success signals. The paper does not test on tasks where strategy generation is inherently difficult (e.g., open-ended dialogue, creative writing, tasks with ambiguous success criteria) or where the environment dynamics are so unpredictable that a fixed strategy would be actively harmful. The SciWorld results (Table 2) already show that strategy guidance fails on certain categories (Find: 16.3%) — the framework is not universally beneficial, and the paper does not characterize the boundary conditions.

**Is the sample efficiency claim quantitatively supported?** The paper claims "improves sample efficiency" but does not report a formal efficiency metric. Figure 1c (WebShop training curves) and Figure 4 (ALFWorld training curves for ablations) show that StraTA learns faster than GRPO, but no metrics like "number of steps to reach X% success" or "area under the learning curve" are reported. The training curves in Figure 4 show that the Vanilla variant reaches approximately 0.7 success rate at step ~60, while GRPO reaches the same level at step ~120 — roughly a 2× sample efficiency improvement. But this is for the 3B model with reduced batch size (4), and the paper does not report comparable curves for the main 7B experiments.

**Are the comparisons to closed-source models fair?** The paper includes GPT-5.1, Claude-4-Sonnet, and Gemini-2.5-Flash as "frontier model" reference points. These are evaluated via prompting only (Vanilla for ALFWorld and WebShop; the specific prompting method for SciWorld is labeled "Prompting-Based" in Table 2, likely ReAct-style given the paper's discussion). The comparison is informative for establishing that task-specific RL training is necessary for high performance, but it does not isolate the contribution of StraTA specifically — PPO and GRPO also outperform these models. More importantly, the closed-source models are not fine-tuned with RL, so this is a comparison of *paradigms* (prompting vs. RL fine-tuning) not a comparison of *methods within the same paradigm*. The paper would be stronger if it compared StraTA to these frontier models fine-tuned with the same RL procedure (which may be infeasible for API-only models, but worth acknowledging as a limitation).

**Missing baselines and experimental gaps.** Several comparisons would strengthen the paper's conclusions: (1) **GiGPO with the same `N × M` group size as StraTA** — if GiGPO were given 32 trajectories per task (matching StraTA's budget), would it close the gap? The paper claims the total computation is comparable, but doesn't run GiGPO at this group size. (2) **ReAct with concatenated strategy** — what if ReAct's reasoning step at `t = 1` generates a plan that is then included in all subsequent prompts? This would be a lightweight strategy-conditioned baseline without hierarchical training. (3) **Strategy generated by a separate (frozen) planner model** — does the strategy need to be jointly optimized with execution, or would a fixed strategy from a stronger model suffice? (4) **Ablation of the non-cumulative setting** — the paper adopts this from GiGPO but doesn't test whether StraTA works in the cumulative (full-history) setting, which is more natural for strategy-guided execution (the agent could reference the strategy from earlier in the context rather than having it re-prepended each step).

**Statistical reliability concerns.** The test sets are not described — we don't know how many episodes are used for evaluation, how tasks are sampled, or whether the same tasks appear in training and evaluation. Table 1 reports 3-seed averages with standard deviations, which is good practice, but the standard deviations are large in some cases (e.g., GiGPO-7B on Heat: 89.3±8.2; StraTA-7B on Cool: 93.3±4.6). Given ~100 questions per difficulty bin in the original paper's analysis (which uses 500 test questions split into 5 bins), the per-subtask sample sizes on ALFWorld could be quite small (some subtasks may have only ~20-30 instances). Without knowing the evaluation set size, it's impossible to assess whether the reported differences are statistically significant. The paper does not report confidence intervals or significance tests for any comparison.

**The SciWorld results reveal task-specific limitations.** The paper presents the overall SciWorld score (63.5%) as a success, but the per-category breakdown shows StraTA achieves near-zero on Find (16.3%) and underperforms prompting-based methods and prior trained baselines on Test-Cond (54.4%). A more honest characterization would be: strategy guidance is highly effective on some SciWorld categories (Lifespan: 100%, Measure: 55.7%, Chem-Mix: 78.6%) and ineffective or harmful on others (Find, Test-Cond). The paper does not analyze *why* these categories differ, which is a significant gap — understanding the failure modes would be more informative than simply reporting the overall average.

**The WebShop 1.5B result is the strongest and most surprising.** StraTA-1.5B achieves 82.5% success on WebShop, surpassing GiGPO-7B (72.8%) and nearly matching StraTA-7B (84.2%). This is a striking result — a 1.5B model with strategy guidance is competitive with a 7B model with the same method, and dramatically better than a 7B model without strategy guidance. If this result is robust, it suggests that for WebShop specifically, the strategy abstraction matters far more than model capacity. However, the paper does not emphasize or analyze this result in depth, which is a missed opportunity.

**The ablation study design has a confound.** The ablation studies (Tables 3–6) use Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct with batch size reduced from 16 to 4. This means the ablations are run with 4× fewer environment interactions per training step, which could exaggerate the differences between variants (faster saturation for weaker methods) or produce different optimal hyperparameters than the full-scale experiments. The paper acknowledges this design choice ("to amplify the differences between variants") but does not discuss whether the conclusions transfer to the full-scale setting. The optimal `σ`, `δ`, and `κ` values identified in the ablations may not be optimal at batch size 16.

**Overall assessment.** The experiments provide strong evidence that StraTA outperforms existing RL baselines on ALFWorld, WebShop, and SciWorld — the numbers are consistently higher, and the gains are often substantial (especially on WebShop). The ablation studies convincingly demonstrate that the hierarchical group construction, diverse strategy rollout, and critical self-judgment each contribute to performance, with largely orthogonal effects. However, the paper does not adequately isolate the specific contribution of *strategy guidance* from the contribution of *hierarchical training* — these are presented as a package, and the causal story is muddied by the lack of baselines that test one without the other. The scaling analysis is minimal (two model sizes), the statistical methodology is thin (no significance tests, unknown evaluation set sizes), and the paper does not deeply analyze the failure modes or boundary conditions of the approach. The results are strong enough to establish StraTA as a promising method, but the experimental analysis leaves important questions unanswered about when and why strategy guidance helps, which specific components are necessary vs. merely beneficial, and whether the gains persist at larger scales or on different task types.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### 6.1 The Difficulty Estimation Cost Is Unaccounted for in Strategy Quality Assessment

The hierarchical group construction in Section 4.2 requires sampling `N = 4` strategies and `M = 8` rollouts per strategy (32 total trajectories per task), then computing the strategy-level reward using the top-`\delta` mean (Equation 10). This procedure estimates strategy quality by executing each candidate strategy multiple times and observing outcomes—which is functionally equivalent to *assessing strategy difficulty or quality through environment interaction*.

**The consequence.** The cost of evaluating a strategy is included in the training budget but is not separately analyzed. In deployment, a trained StraTA agent must still *generate* a strategy at episode start (one model call) and then execute it. The paper provides no mechanism for estimating whether the generated strategy is likely to be good *before* committing to it for the entire episode. If the model generates a poor strategy, the agent will waste up to `H` environment steps (50 for ALFWorld, 15 for WebShop) executing a doomed plan before receiving the negative outcome reward. The hierarchical training procedure teaches the model which strategies are good on average across training tasks, but it does not provide a runtime mechanism for assessing or rejecting a strategy on a specific novel task. This is the deployment-time analog of the difficulty estimation problem faced by other compute-optimal frameworks.

**What evidence exists.** The paper does not measure or discuss the cost of poor-strategy execution in deployment. All evaluation results in Tables 1 and 2 report end-to-end success rates after the full episode, conflating strategy generation failures with execution failures. The SciWorld per-category breakdown in Table 2 reveals categories where StraTA performs poorly (Find: 16.3%) and underperforms reactive methods—this is consistent with poor strategy generation on those categories, but the paper does not isolate whether the failures are due to bad strategies or bad execution. The case studies in Appendix C show strategies that are clearly task-relevant, but these are cherry-picked successful examples.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges a related limitation in Section 6: "its effectiveness depends on the quality of the generated strategy," but frames this as a general caveat rather than a measurable cost. There is no proposed mechanism for strategy quality assessment, early termination of poor strategies, or dynamic strategy revision during an episode. The paper suggests "adaptive strategy revision" as future work, which would partially address this by allowing mid-episode strategy changes when the current strategy proves ineffective, but this would require learning when and how to revise—a substantially harder problem.

### 6.2 The Fixed Strategy Assumption Is Brittle in Dynamic or Partially Observable Environments

StraTA generates a strategy once from the initial state `s_1` and keeps it fixed for the entire episode (Section 4.1, Equation 5-6). This design choice is explicit and deliberate:

> "The strategy is generated once at the beginning of the episode and kept fixed thereafter."

The paper's own wording in Section 6 acknowledges the consequence:

> "a fixed strategy may become restrictive when the environment changes substantially during an episode."

**The consequence.** In environments where the initial observation `s_1` provides incomplete information about the task structure—for example, when the agent must discover the location of objects through exploration, or when the environment contains hidden state that is only revealed through interaction—a strategy generated from `s_1` alone may be fundamentally misaligned with the actual task requirements. The agent is then trapped: it must either follow a flawed strategy (leading to failure) or deviate from the strategy (triggering the self-judgment penalty in Equation 17, which penalizes actions that "neither follow the global strategy nor push forward the task progress"). The dual-criterion self-judgment design (Section 4.4) partially mitigates this by only penalizing actions that fail *both* criteria, meaning an action that deviates from the strategy but advances progress is not penalized. However, the strategy itself is never updated, so the agent receives no corrected guidance for the remainder of the episode.

**What evidence exists.** The SciWorld results in Table 2 provide indirect evidence for this limitation. The "Find" category—which explicitly requires the agent to locate objects in an environment with hidden state—shows StraTA achieving only 16.3%, substantially below prompting-based methods (Claude-4-Sonnet at 45.6%) and roughly equivalent to GRPO (14.7%). This is consistent with a fixed-strategy failure mode: if the initial observation doesn't reveal where objects are, a strategy like "go to the greenhouse and look for the animal" (as in the Appendix C.3 case study) may work, but if the initial observation provides no clues about which rooms contain the target, no reasonable strategy can be generated from `s_1` alone. The "Test-Cond" category shows a similar pattern (StraTA: 54.4% vs. AgentGym-RL: 59.0%), though the gap is smaller.

The paper does not include any direct manipulation of environment dynamics to test fixed-strategy robustness—for example, comparing performance on tasks where `s_1` does vs. does not contain sufficient information for strategy generation. This is a significant missing experiment.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges this limitation in Section 6 and lists "adaptive strategy revision" as a future work direction. However, the current framework provides no mechanism for detecting when the strategy has become stale or for generating revised strategies mid-episode. The self-judgment mechanism (Section 4.4) could in principle detect strategy-deviating actions, but it operates retrospectively (after the trajectory completes) and only provides a training signal, not a runtime correction.

### 6.3 The Method Is Evaluated on a Narrow Set of Benchmarks with a Single Model Family

All experiments use the Qwen2.5-Instruct model family (1.5B, 3B for ablations, 7B) on three benchmarks: ALFWorld, WebShop, and SciWorld (Section 5.1). The paper does not test on any alternative model architectures (e.g., Llama, DeepSeek, Mistral) or on additional agentic benchmarks beyond the three selected.

**The consequence.** Several aspects of StraTA's design may be model-specific or benchmark-specific in ways the paper cannot characterize:

- **Strategy generation quality depends on the base model's planning capabilities.** Qwen2.5-Instruct models are instruction-tuned and may have stronger zero-shot planning abilities than base models or models from other families. A model with weaker planning capabilities might generate strategies that are too vague, too incorrect, or too homogeneous for hierarchical training to provide a meaningful strategy-level signal. The paper does not test whether StraTA's gains persist when the base model's planning ability is degraded.

- **The self-judgment quality depends on the model's self-evaluation capabilities.** The `κ` ablation in Table 6 shows that self-judgment is fragile—`κ = 1.0` causes a dramatic performance drop on WebShop (61.8% vs. 73.4% at `κ = 0.1`). This sensitivity may be even more pronounced on models with weaker self-evaluation abilities, potentially making the self-judgment component actively harmful rather than merely ineffective.

- **The three benchmarks share structural properties.** All three involve goal-directed tasks with clear success/failure signals (step-decayed binary rewards; Appendix B.1), natural-language action spaces, and environments that remain static within an episode (the environment state changes only in response to the agent's actions, not autonomously). The paper does not test on benchmarks with adversarial or stochastic environment dynamics, continuous action spaces, or partially specified goals—all settings where fixed-strategy guidance might be less appropriate or actively counterproductive.

- **The benchmarks have relatively short horizons.** Maximum episode lengths are 50 steps (ALFWorld), 15 steps (WebShop), and 20 steps (SciWorld). The paper does not test on truly long-horizon tasks (hundreds or thousands of steps), where strategy coherence over extended durations becomes more critical and fixed-strategy brittleness more pronounced.

**What evidence exists.** The per-benchmark performance patterns reveal potential benchmark-specific effects. The largest gains over baselines occur on WebShop (+17.5 pp over GiGPO at 1.5B; Table 1), the smallest on ALFWorld (+4.0 pp at 1.5B), and the gains on SciWorld are highly category-dependent (Table 2: Lifespan +2.2 pp over PPO, Find +1.6 pp over GRPO). This variation could reflect genuine differences in how much each benchmark benefits from strategy guidance, or it could reflect bench-mark-specific properties (e.g., WebShop's structured search tasks vs. ALFWorld's diverse manipulation subtasks) that interact with the method. Without testing on additional benchmarks with systematically varied properties, it's impossible to distinguish these explanations.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not claim generalization beyond the tested benchmarks and model family, but neither does it discuss the scope of its evaluation as a limitation. The acknowledgment in Section 6 mentions "extending the framework to broader agentic domains" as future work, which implicitly acknowledges the narrow current scope.

### 6.4 The Causal Role of Strategy Content Is Not Isolated from Confounds

The paper's central claim is that explicit strategy guidance improves agentic RL. However, the experimental design does not isolate the effect of *meaningful strategy content* from several confounds introduced by the hierarchical training architecture:

- **Prepended fixed context.** Every action in StraTA is generated with the strategy prepended to the state (Equation 6). This means the model sees additional context at each step compared to reactive baselines—context that may provide useful task reminders or regularizing effects independent of its strategic content. A null-strategy baseline (e.g., prepending an empty or generic string like "Complete the task.") would test whether the benefit comes from the strategy content or from the additional context.

- **Hierarchical group advantages vs. strategy content.** The Vanilla variant in Table 3 (hierarchical groups without FPS or self-judgment) achieves 79.0% on ALFWorld, substantially above GRPO. This variant *does* use strategies and hierarchical groups. The improvement could come from (a) the hierarchical group structure providing better optimization, (b) the strategy content providing better action generation, or (c) both. The paper cannot distinguish these because all StraTA variants include strategy generation—there is no condition where hierarchical groups operate on something other than strategies (e.g., grouping trajectories by random assignment, or by embedding-based clustering of initial states).

- **Increased effective model capacity.** Conditioning actions on a strategy means the model allocates some of its representational capacity to strategy generation and some to strategy-conditioned execution. This division of labor could improve performance simply by giving the model more tokens to "think" about the task (strategy tokens + action tokens vs. action tokens alone), independent of the strategy's semantic content. The length penalty (Equation 11) partially controls for this by penalizing very long strategies, but the penalty threshold `λ = 0.5` allows strategies up to 512 tokens without penalty—comparable to or longer than many actions.

**The consequence.** Without isolating the effect of strategy content, the paper's causal claim ("strategy guidance causes improved performance") is not fully established. An alternative explanation is that any two-level hierarchical training procedure (with groups formed by some pre-episode classification, not necessarily meaningful strategies) would produce similar gains. If this alternative explanation is correct, the paper's contribution shifts from "strategies are important" to "hierarchical group-based RL outperforms flat group-based RL for long-horizon tasks"—a different and potentially more general finding, but one the paper does not make or test.

**What evidence exists.** The case studies in Appendix C show strategies that are semantically meaningful (e.g., "Explore the room to find where the tissuebox might be and how to get it to the sidetable") and correlated with successful trajectories. This is suggestive but anecdotal—the paper does not quantify the relationship between strategy quality (as judged by humans or by an independent metric) and trajectory success. The FPS diversity ablation (Table 4) shows that forcing strategies to be *semantically diverse* (rather than near-duplicates) improves performance, which indirectly suggests strategy content matters (if content were irrelevant, diversity wouldn't help). But this still doesn't rule out the possibility that any diverse pre-episode classification (not necessarily meaningful strategies) would produce similar gains.

The paper also does not include an ablation where strategies are generated from a separate frozen model (e.g., a larger Qwen2.5 model or a GPT-5.1 API call) and then executed by the training model. This would test whether strategy quality causally affects execution success, and whether jointly training strategy generation is necessary.

**Mitigation status.** None. The paper does not acknowledge this confound or propose experiments to address it. The limitation is structural—all StraTA variants include strategy generation as an integral component, and no variant replaces strategies with a non-semantic grouping mechanism.

### 6.5 Training-Serving Mismatch: Strategy Generation and Self-Judgment Are Used at Training but Not Optimized for Deployment Efficiency

StraTA's training procedure involves three types of model calls per task: strategy generation (`N` calls, plus `σN` for FPS oversampling), strategy-conditioned action generation (`N × M × H` calls, where `H` is the episode length), and self-judgment (`N × M` calls, one per trajectory). In deployment, only two of these remain: one strategy generation call (N=1) and `H` strategy-conditioned action calls. The self-judgment step is purely a training-time auxiliary and is not used at inference.

**The consequence.** There is a mismatch between what the model is trained to do and what it is asked to do at deployment:

- **Strategy generation during training is evaluated comparatively** (via GRPO strategy-level advantage, Equation 2, applied to `G_strategy`), comparing `N` candidate strategies against each other. At deployment, only one strategy is generated, and the model receives no comparative feedback. The model is never trained on the single-strategy generation task—it only learns that *among N candidates, this strategy was relatively better or worse*. This could lead to a distribution shift at deployment where the model must generate a single strategy without the "safety net" of group-relative comparison, potentially producing strategies that are less calibrated or more variable in quality.

- **Self-judgment is trained but not used at deployment.** The model learns to generate judgments (Equation 16) because those judgments affect the training reward (Equation 17-18). At deployment, the judgment capability is unused—the model never evaluates its own trajectory during an episode. This means a capability the model invested representational capacity in learning (potentially millions of parameters adapted for self-evaluation) provides no deployment benefit. In principle, the self-judgment capability could be used for runtime strategy revision (e.g., "I notice step 3 didn't follow the strategy—I should adjust"), but the paper provides no mechanism for this.

- **The FPS diversity mechanism operates at training only.** The model learns from diverse strategy comparisons but is never trained to generate a single *good* strategy when only one attempt is allowed. The FPS procedure effectively provides the model with a curated set of diverse strategies to learn from, but at deployment the model must produce a good strategy in one shot—a task it was never explicitly trained for.

**What evidence exists.** The paper does not report any analysis comparing training-time and deployment-time generation quality. All evaluation metrics in Tables 1 and 2 are deployment-time (single strategy, single trajectory, no self-judgment), but there is no comparison to an "oracle" setting where the best of `N` strategies is selected (which would measure the gap between training-time strategy quality and deployment-time single-strategy quality). The training curves in Figure 1c and Figure 4 show training-time performance (which includes the benefits of group-relative comparison) but the corresponding deployment-time performance is not plotted, so we cannot see whether the model's training-time and deployment-time performance diverge.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not acknowledge this mismatch as a limitation. The practical consequence is that StraTA's training-time gains may not fully translate to deployment-time performance—a concern that cannot be assessed from the reported results because all evaluation is deployment-time (single strategy, no group comparison) while all training uses group-relative signals.

### 6.6 The Method Has No Mechanism for Recovering from Strategy-Level Failures Within an Episode

StraTA decomposes task solving into strategy generation (once, at episode start) and strategy-following execution (repeated, at each step). If the generated strategy is fundamentally flawed—for example, it proposes searching for an object in the wrong room, or suggests a chemical procedure that cannot succeed with available materials—the agent has no mechanism to recognize this and replan. The strategy is fixed (Section 4.1), and the action-level policy is trained to *follow* the strategy, not to *evaluate or override* it.

**The consequence.** A strategy-level failure cascades into guaranteed episode failure, regardless of the quality of subsequent execution. The agent may execute every action perfectly according to the strategy and still fail because the strategy itself was wrong. Worse, the self-judgment mechanism (Equation 17) penalizes actions that deviate from the strategy, creating a disincentive for the agent to recognize and correct strategy-level errors. An agent that realizes its strategy is flawed faces a dilemma: follow the strategy (and fail) or deviate (and incur the `−κ` self-judgment penalty on those deviation steps). The dual-criterion design (penalizing only actions that "neither follow the strategy nor push forward the task progress") partially mitigates this—successful deviations are not penalized—but the agent is not explicitly trained to detect strategy failures and initiate replanning.

This is particularly problematic for tasks where the initial observation `s_1` provides incomplete or misleading information about the environment. The paper's own case studies (Appendix C) show strategies that happen to be correct for their tasks, but the paper does not present examples of strategy failure or analyze how the agent behaves when its strategy is wrong.

**What evidence exists.** The SciWorld results in Table 2 provide the clearest evidence for strategy-level failure modes. On the "Find" category, StraTA achieves only 16.3%—worse than prompting-based methods (Claude-4-Sonnet: 45.6%, GPT-5.1: 41.8%) and barely above GRPO (14.7%). The "Find" category requires locating specific objects in a multi-room environment; if the initial observation doesn't reveal which room contains the target, no fixed strategy can guarantee success, and a reactive agent that adapts its search based on intermediate observations may outperform a strategy-guided agent committed to a single (potentially wrong) search plan. The paper does not analyze whether Find failures are due to poor initial strategies (strategy-level failure) or poor execution of reasonable strategies (action-level failure)—a distinction the hierarchical training framework is designed to enable but which the paper does not exploit for failure analysis.

Similarly, the WebShop training curves (Figure 1c) show StraTA reaching a higher asymptote than GRPO, but we cannot see whether StraTA's remaining failures are concentrated in episodes with poor strategies. The top-`δ` strategy reward (Equation 10) provides a training-time estimate of strategy quality, but this is never correlated with deployment-time success to measure the strategy-level failure rate.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges "adaptive strategy revision" as future work (Section 6), which would directly address this limitation. However, the current framework provides no infrastructure for runtime strategy evaluation, failure detection, or replanning. The self-judgment mechanism (Section 4.4) operates retrospectively and only during training—it cannot serve as a runtime strategy-quality monitor. A practical mitigation would be to allow the agent to optionally generate a new strategy mid-episode when it detects (through some learned signal) that progress has stalled, but this would require training the model to recognize strategy failure and to generate revised strategies—capabilities not present in the current design.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

StraTA makes a **moderate reframing contribution** to agentic RL rather than causing a paradigm shift. It does not invent a new RL algorithm or a new model architecture; it proposes that long-horizon decision-making should be decomposed into strategy generation and strategy-following execution, and that a hierarchical group structure within GRPO-style training can jointly optimize both levels. The intellectual move is clean—separate "what approach should I take?" from "what action should I take now?"—and the empirical evidence that this decomposition improves sample efficiency and final performance is consistent across three benchmarks and two model scales.

The field-level impact operates along three dimensions.

**First, StraTA shifts attention from credit assignment within reactive trajectories to the quality of pre-episode planning.** Prior work on agentic RL—particularly GiGPO, ArCHer, and implicit step reward methods—focused on refining how the sparse trajectory-level reward signal is distributed across individual action steps. These methods accept the reactive formulation as given and attempt to compensate for its limitations through better reward decomposition. StraTA argues implicitly that this is solving the wrong sub-problem: no amount of credit assignment within a reactive framework can compensate for the absence of an explicit plan that coordinates behavior across steps. The paper's results—StraTA-7B reaching 93.1% vs. GiGPO-7B's 90.8% on ALFWorld, and more dramatically 84.2% vs. 72.8% on WebShop—suggest that the strategy-level improvement provides gains beyond what better credit assignment alone can achieve within a reactive policy. This reorients the conversation from "how can we better assign credit for reactive actions?" to "how can we generate and evaluate explicit plans, and use those plans to guide credit assignment?" It makes planning quality a first-class object of study in agentic RL rather than an implicit property of the reactive policy.

**Second, the paper reconciles a tension between prompting-based and RL-based agent research.** Prompting-based approaches like ReAct interleave reasoning and action generation at each step, producing something like a local plan that is constantly regenerated. RL-based approaches like PPO, GRPO, and GiGPO train agents to act directly without explicit step-by-step deliberation (beyond what the base model produces autoregressively). The prompting community observed that reasoning-before-acting helps; the RL community observed that training on environment feedback helps more than prompting alone. StraTA synthesizes these observations: it uses explicit planning (like prompting approaches) but trains the planning and execution jointly through RL (like training-based approaches). The strategy is a persistent plan—generated once, held fixed, and optimized through RL—rather than an ephemeral reasoning trace regenerated at each step. This hybrid approach explains why StraTA outperforms both pure prompting (Claude-4-Sonnet at 72.9% on ALFWorld vs. StraTA-7B at 93.1%) and pure reactive RL (GRPO-7B at 77.1% vs. StraTA-7B at 93.1%). It suggests that the community's prior dichotomy—"prompting with reasoning" vs. "RL without reasoning"—was a false choice, and that the productive synthesis is "RL with learned, persistent planning."

**Third, StraTA provides a diagnostic framework for understanding agent failure modes that was previously unavailable.** In a reactive RL setup, when an agent fails on a task, the failure could be attributed to poor exploration, poor credit assignment, or insufficient model capacity—vague and overlapping explanations. In StraTA's two-level structure, failure becomes more interpretable. A trajectory failure under a particular strategy could be due to a poor strategy (strategy-level failure) or poor execution of a reasonable strategy (action-level failure). The top-`δ` strategy reward (Equation 10) provides a quantitative estimate of strategy quality separated from execution noise. The self-judgment mechanism (Equation 16-17) identifies specific action steps that deviated from the strategy. While the paper does not exploit this diagnostic capability in its analysis—it reports only end-to-end success rates, not strategy-quality vs. execution-quality decompositions—the framework enables it, and future work can use this to characterize *why* agents fail on specific tasks or benchmarks. This is a **structural contribution**: the two-level architecture creates capability for analysis that the reactive formulation structurally precludes, even if the paper itself does not fully exercise that capability.

**Which research directions become more attractive.** The paper's demonstration that strategy guidance improves RL efficiency makes several directions more promising. Research on **learned planning representations** for agents—beyond simple natural-language strategies to structured plans with subgoals, preconditions, or alternative branches—becomes directly relevant because StraTA provides a training framework that can optimize such representations end-to-end. Research on **strategy-level exploration** becomes newly important: the paper's FPS-based diversity mechanism is a first step, but more sophisticated approaches (active selection of strategies to maximize information gain, Bayesian optimization over strategy space, learned strategy priors) could yield further gains. Research on **verifier-based or critic-based strategy evaluation** becomes attractive because the paper's top-`δ` reward estimation (Equation 10) is crude—it evaluates strategy quality by executing rollouts—and a learned strategy critic that could evaluate strategies without expensive environment interaction would reduce the `N × M` sampling cost.

**Which research directions become less attractive.** Purely reactive approaches to agentic RL that do not incorporate any form of explicit planning or hierarchical structure face a higher burden of proof after this work. GiGPO represents the strongest reactive baseline, and StraTA consistently outperforms it. This does not mean reactive RL is obsolete—StraTA's gains on ALFWorld are modest (+2.3 pp over GiGPO at 7B), and on some SciWorld categories (Test-Cond), reactive methods are competitive—but the paper establishes that strategy guidance provides a consistent and sometimes large advantage. New reactive methods must now demonstrate either that they match or exceed StraTA's performance without explicit planning, or that they offer other compelling advantages (simplicity, computational efficiency, generalization to settings where planning is infeasible). The burden has shifted: the default assumption should no longer be that agentic RL operates on a flat reactive policy, and new methods should justify why they *don't* incorporate hierarchical structure rather than why they do.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Isolating the causal contribution of strategy content from hierarchical training.** The paper demonstrates that the full StraTA framework (strategies + hierarchical groups + FPS + self-judgment) outperforms reactive baselines, but it does not isolate whether the gains come from meaningful strategy content or from the hierarchical group structure itself. A strong follow-up would train a variant where the "strategy" is replaced with a fixed, task-independent string (e.g., "Complete the task step by step.") or with a random strategy sampled from a different task's training data. If this variant with hierarchical groups but no meaningful strategy content still outperforms reactive GRPO, then the hierarchical group structure—not the strategy content—is the primary driver of improvement, and the paper's contribution should be reinterpreted as "hierarchical group-based RL" rather than "strategy-guided RL." If the variant underperforms StraTA significantly but still outperforms GRPO, then both strategy content and hierarchical structure contribute, and their relative contributions can be quantified. The experiment should be run on ALFWorld and WebShop at the 7B scale to match the paper's main results, using the same `N=4`, `M=8` settings. A related ablation would replace human-interpretable strategies with learned continuous vector embeddings optimized through the same hierarchical objective—this would test whether the natural-language form of strategies is necessary or merely convenient.

**Characterizing the failure modes of fixed-strategy execution in partially observable environments.** The paper acknowledges that fixed strategies may fail when the environment changes substantially during an episode, and the SciWorld "Find" category result (StraTA: 16.3% vs. Claude-4-Sonnet: 45.6%) provides preliminary evidence for this limitation. A systematic characterization would measure StraTA's performance as a function of how much task-relevant information is available in the initial observation `s_1`. This could be done on ALFWorld by manipulating the initial observation: in the "full information" condition, provide the agent with the task description and a complete list of object locations; in the "partial information" condition (the standard setting), provide only the current room's contents; in the "misleading information" condition, provide incorrect or incomplete object location hints. The prediction is that StraTA's advantage over reactive methods should be largest in the full-information condition (where strategies are accurate) and should shrink or reverse in the misleading-information condition (where strategies based on `s_1` are actively harmful). This experiment would establish boundary conditions for when strategy guidance is beneficial vs. harmful, and would quantify the cost of the fixed-strategy assumption. A complementary analysis would use the self-judgment mechanism to measure how often StraTA-generated strategies are internally inconsistent with subsequent environment observations, providing a quantitative measure of strategy-environment mismatch.

**Adaptive strategy revision triggered by self-judgment signals.** The paper identifies the fixed-strategy limitation and lists adaptive revision as future work, but provides no mechanism for it. The self-judgment mechanism (Section 4.4) already trains the model to identify actions that deviate from the strategy and fail to advance progress—these judgments could serve as a runtime trigger for strategy revision. A natural extension would allow the agent to optionally regenerate its strategy mid-episode when the cumulative self-judgment penalty on recent steps exceeds a threshold, or when a separate "strategy health" critic (trained on the trajectory-so-far) predicts low probability of eventual success under the current strategy. The model would then be trained jointly to decide *when* to replan (a binary decision at each step) and *what* new strategy to generate (conditioned on the full interaction history, not just `s_1`). This transforms the fixed-strategy setting into a variable-length strategy commitment setting—closer to classical hierarchical RL with options, but with natural-language strategies and end-to-end GRPO training. Evaluation would measure whether adaptive revision recovers performance on SciWorld categories where fixed-strategy StraTA underperforms (Find, Test-Cond), and whether it introduces new failure modes (excessive replanning, strategy oscillation). The key metric is whether the performance gain from adaptive revision exceeds the computational cost of additional strategy-generation calls during an episode.

**Scaling the hierarchical group structure to larger models and longer horizons.** The paper tests two model scales (1.5B, 7B) on benchmarks with horizons up to 50 steps. The finding that StraTA-1.5B approaches or matches 7B reactive models on WebShop (82.5% vs. GiGPO-7B's 72.8% success) is striking but based on a single data point. A systematic scaling study would train StraTA on models from 0.5B to 70B parameters and measure whether the relative gain over reactive GRPO increases, decreases, or stays constant with model scale. The hypothesis is that strategy guidance provides larger relative gains at smaller scales (where planning capability is weaker and benefits more from explicit decomposition) and smaller relative gains at larger scales (where the reactive policy can internalize planning implicitly). The paper's 1.5B vs. 7B comparison is consistent with this hypothesis—the gain over GiGPO is larger at 1.5B (+4.0 pp ALFWorld, +17.5 pp WebShop) than at 7B (+2.3 pp ALFWorld, +11.4 pp WebShop)—but two data points are insufficient to establish a trend. This study would also vary episode length systematically (by modifying ALFWorld tasks to require more steps, or by using benchmarks with naturally longer horizons like WebArena or OSWorld) to test whether strategy guidance becomes more valuable as horizon increases. The prediction is that purely reactive methods should degrade faster with horizon than strategy-guided methods because the credit assignment problem worsens super-linearly with horizon, and strategy guidance partially mitigates this.

**Training a strategy critic to reduce the cost of strategy evaluation.** The hierarchical training procedure requires `M` rollouts per strategy (8 in the paper's experiments) to estimate strategy quality via the top-`δ` mean (Equation 10). This `M ×` factor in sampling cost limits how many strategies can be evaluated during training—`N = 4` strategies with `M = 8` rollouts per strategy yields 32 trajectories per task, and increasing `N` to explore more strategies would be expensive. A learned strategy critic—trained to predict the top-`δ` mean rollout reward from the strategy text and task description alone, without executing any environment steps—could dramatically reduce this cost. The critic would be trained on the same (strategy, top-`δ` reward) pairs generated during normal StraTA training, and could then be used to pre-filter or score candidate strategies before committing to environment rollouts. This is analogous to how learned value functions reduce the need for Monte Carlo rollouts in standard RL. The key evaluation would measure the correlation between critic-predicted strategy quality and actual rollout-based quality on held-out tasks, and would test whether a StraTA variant that uses the critic to select `N` strategies from a larger oversampled set (replacing FPS with critic-based selection, or augmenting FPS with critic scores) outperforms the current FPS-only approach at the same total environment interaction budget.

**Testing the generality of the strategy abstraction across model families and task domains.** All experiments use Qwen2.5-Instruct models, which are instruction-tuned and may have unusually strong planning capabilities compared to base models or models from other families. The self-judgment mechanism's fragility on WebShop (Table 6: `κ = 1.0` causes a 11.6 pp drop) raises questions about whether self-judgment quality is model-specific. A comprehensive replication would train StraTA on 3–5 model families (Llama-3, DeepSeek, Mistral, Gemma) at comparable scales and measure whether the relative gain over GRPO is consistent. It would also test on additional benchmarks that stress-test different aspects of the framework: benchmarks with adversarial environment dynamics (where fixed strategies become actively harmful), benchmarks with natural-language action spaces but continuous or high-dimensional state spaces (where strategy generation from text descriptions may be insufficient), and benchmarks where task success requires integrating information from multiple sources (web, knowledge base, tool outputs) that cannot be captured in a short initial strategy. The goal is a matrix of (model family × benchmark type) results that characterizes StraTA's domain of applicability—where it provides large, reliable gains and where it provides small or negative gains—rather than the current three-cell (1.5B, 7B on three similar benchmarks) evidence base.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Cost-efficient training of specialist agents for structured interactive tasks.** Organizations building agents for tasks like web automation, customer support workflows, or robotic process automation face a trade-off between using large prompt-only models (expensive per query, no task-specific optimization) and fine-tuning smaller models (cheaper per query but requires training infrastructure). StraTA provides a training recipe for the second approach that achieves higher performance with smaller models: StraTA-1.5B reaches 90.7% on ALFWorld and 82.5% on WebShop, matching or exceeding a 7B GiGPO agent (90.8% and 72.8%) and dramatically outperforming GPT-5.1 prompted (55.7% and 22.2%). For a deployment handling 1 million WebShop-like queries per day, replacing a GPT-5.1 API call (~$0.01–0.05/query, speculative) with a locally-hosted 1.5B StraTA agent (amortized GPU cost ~$0.0001/query) would reduce inference costs by two orders of magnitude while improving success rate from ~22% to ~83%. The training cost—150 steps on 8× H100 GPUs, approximately 380 seconds per step (Figure 3a), totaling ~16 GPU-hours—is negligible compared to the deployment savings. This use case is most compelling for tasks with clear success/failure signals (matching the binary or step-decayed rewards in the paper's benchmarks) and structured action spaces where format penalties can be applied.

**Deployment-time strategy inspection for debugging and safety monitoring.** Because StraTA generates an explicit natural-language strategy before acting, the strategy serves as an auditable, human-readable summary of the agent's intended approach. For safety-critical applications—medical decision support, financial transaction processing, autonomous vehicle routing—a human operator or automated safety monitor can inspect the generated strategy before the agent takes any environment actions, and can approve, reject, or modify it. This is impossible with purely reactive agents, where the agent's "plan" is distributed across its neural network weights and only becomes observable through its actions. The case studies in Appendix C show that StraTA strategies are interpretable and task-relevant (e.g., "Explore the room to find where the tissuebox might be and how to get it to the sidetable"; "Start with a generic search query to identify available products fitting the criteria. Then, refine the search based on user feedback..."). An organization deploying StraTA could implement a policy where strategies classified as high-risk (by a separate classifier or by keyword matching against a deny-list) trigger human review or automatic rejection before execution, reducing the risk of harmful or costly action sequences. The key enabler is that the strategy is separate from execution—it can be inspected, modified, or replaced without affecting the action-generation mechanism—which is a structural property of the two-level design, not an add-on.

**Curriculum design for progressively harder agent training.** The paper's difficulty-dependent findings—StraTA's gains are largest on medium-difficulty tasks and smallest on the hardest tasks (SciWorld Find: 16.3%, no better than GRPO's 14.7%)—suggest a curriculum training strategy. Train StraTA first on easy and medium tasks where strategy guidance provides large gains (ALFWorld Look: 92.3%, SciWorld Lifespan: 100.0%), allowing the model to learn effective strategy generation and strategy-following behaviors. Then fine-tune on harder tasks where strategy guidance alone is insufficient, using the already-trained strategy-generation capability as a warm start. The hypothesis is that a model that has learned to plan on easier tasks will generate better initial strategies on harder tasks than a model trained on hard tasks from scratch, even if the strategies themselves are imperfect. This curriculum could be automated by measuring the model's pass@1 rate (or the PRM-based difficulty estimation approach described in the compute-optimal test-time scaling literature) to dynamically adjust the task distribution during training. The practical benefit is more sample-efficient training on challenging benchmarks—achieving higher SciWorld overall scores with fewer environment interactions by focusing early training on Lifespan and Measure (where StraTA excels) and gradually introducing Find and Test-Cond.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper does not articulate an explicit trade-off between StraTA and named alternatives, nor does it provide a decision framework for practitioners choosing between methods. The baselines are evaluated side-by-side (Tables 1–2), and StraTA consistently achieves the highest numbers, but the paper does not characterize *when* the alternative approaches might be preferable—for example, on tasks where strategy generation is unreliable, where environment dynamics make fixed strategies harmful, or where computational constraints prohibit the `N × M` hierarchical rollout structure. A forced decision matrix would therefore fabricate a comparison the paper itself does not make. The experimental results imply a practical rule of thumb: if your task has a clear goal description from which a plausible strategy can be generated, a modest horizon (tens of steps, not hundreds), and a binary or near-binary success signal for RL training, StraTA is likely to outperform purely reactive RL baselines at the same model scale and training budget. Outside this regime—tasks with highly unpredictable environments, tasks requiring hundreds or thousands of steps where mid-episode replanning is essential, or tasks where the initial observation contains almost no task-relevant information—the paper provides no evidence one way or the other, and a practitioner would be extrapolating beyond the experimental coverage.
