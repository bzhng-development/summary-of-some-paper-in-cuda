Here are the takeaways from this Ryan Peterman interview (career-coach and Meta engineer, on why focusing on ratings is a trap for career growth):

**The hunger that started it all**

Ryan joined Meta as a new graduate, but he'd already spent eight months working at Amazon, and that made him feel like he was starting from behind. "This made me hungry to grow since I felt behind." That hunger sharpened further when he learned about Meta's rating system and the top-tier "Redefines Expectations" designation. It felt like a trophy worth chasing, and he was ready to pour all his energy into capturing it.

**The counterintuitive truth from his manager**

A single conversation with his manager flipped his perspective entirely. "He told me that high ratings wouldn't necessarily help me grow." The logic was simple but not obvious: ratings measure how much impact you had *relative to what's expected for your current level*. If you get lucky—or simply work a crushing number of hours—a junior engineer can generate more raw impact than a senior engineer. That doesn't mean they're ready for the next level. "That alone won't help you grow."

**Promotions demand behaviors, not just output**

The core distinction Ryan draws is between **ratings** and **behaviors**. For promotion, you need to demonstrate the *behaviors* of the next level. Without them, there's a real risk of being promoted too early and then struggling to meet the new bar. He paints a concrete picture: imagine a junior engineer who works "an obscene amount" and lands three times as many features as their peers. Their rating will be stellar—but they might not have learned any new skills. "What you've shown is that you're a strong junior engineer, but it's not clear that you could sustain what is expected at the next level." If their insane hours dropped back to normal, the raw output advantage would vanish, and any gaps in their ability to lead others or tackle ambiguity would be exposed.

**Behaviors > Ratings: the durable case**

Ryan builds a multi-pronged argument for why chasing behaviors of the next level yields a much better return than chasing ratings. First, **behaviors are portable**: if you learn the behaviors of a senior engineer, those capabilities follow you to any company—they aren't tied to one employer's calibration curve. Second, **behaviors are sustainable; ratings are a grind**: the highest ratings demand outsized impact, which often requires heroic hours. Behaviors, by contrast, are compounding skills that let you produce *more* impact in the *same* amount of time. Third, the raw economics: he cites Meta pay numbers from levels.fyi to illustrate that a promotion from IC4 to IC5 yields roughly $133,000 per year in pay increase, while a "Redefines" rating at IC4 (which multiplies your bonus by 3x) nets about $75,000 as a one-time bonus. Refreshers might edge up slightly with a top rating, but the recurring promo pay still dominates. And finally, a personal note: **larger scope is more satisfying**. Grinding out repetitive work within your current level gets old.

**The playbook: career conversations as a lever**

His tactical takeaway is to refocus career conversations with your manager entirely on learning next-level behaviors. "Don't worry about ratings so long as you're on track for meeting expectations at your level." He adds a practical reassurance: in practice, next-level behaviors tend to produce solid ratings as a side effect anyway—but the ratings themselves aren't the goal.

**A through-line: agency over resume anxiety**

The thread running underneath the whole argument is a quiet push against credential anxiety (that Amazon head start he felt behind on) and toward **agency**: you can't control how a calibration committee rates your output on a curve, but you *can* control which skills you deliberately practice and which stretch assignments you chase. The real career asset isn't a "Redefines" badge stashed in a perf packet—it's a durable toolkit of behaviors you carry forward.
