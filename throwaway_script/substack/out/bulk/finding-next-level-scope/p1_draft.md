Here are the takeaways from this Ryan Peterman article on finding next-level scope for engineering career growth:

**Two paths to larger-scope projects**

Ryan opens with a clean, two-bucket model for finding projects that match the expectations of the next level. The first is **receiving scope**: someone hands you an opportunity. People often assume this only applies to junior roles, but the problems sitting on an engineering director or CTO's plate are themselves massive in scope, and being handed one of those can drive senior-level promotions. The second is **creating scope for yourself**: identifying improvements in your team's imperfect systems and turning them into projects that make promotion cases straightforward.

**Receiving scope: the straightforward and the less-obvious paths**

The most direct way to receive scope is simply asking your management chain or tech lead. Engineering managers know their teams' top problems, and tech leads often have problems they don't have time for that would make excellent growth opportunities. The less obvious path is increasing the probability that someone comes to you by building a track record and becoming known in a specific technical area. This played a large role in Ryan's promotions to Staff. He walks through three examples from his own career: at the **promotion to Mid-Level (L4)**, after finishing several features faster than expected, his tech lead asked him to take on a larger project, which became the main reason for the promotion. At **promotion to Senior (L5)**, after demonstrating strong ownership over his team's backend, his manager positioned him as the lead on a new initiative, providing leadership experience and building an L5 track record. At **promotion to Staff (L6)**, Ryan was known in his org for getting things done to the point where his Director knew his work. The Director handed him two major initiatives to lead, and while they weren't the main reason for the promotion, they "helped strengthen my packet."

**Creating scope: domain expertise and discovering significant problems**

For growth to more senior levels, creating scope becomes a baseline expectation and carries a major structural advantage: no one needs to trust you with the projects because you create them yourself, which "drives much faster growth trajectories since building trust takes time." Ryan offers two high-level approaches. The first is **leveraging domain expertise**, where ideas for improvement flow naturally from a strong end-to-end understanding of a system. His own experience in video processing taught him how such pipelines should be built. When he dug into the ads video pipeline, he realized "there were a ton of opportunities to improve it," which turned into a large collaboration with the ads team and got him promoted to Senior (L5). The second approach is **discovering significant problems** in less mature areas, where major problems surface just by digging around. The project that drove his Staff promotion came from noticing, in the relatively immature space of video compute efficiency, that the company was "running out of compute." Chasing that problem down turned into a massive initiative yielding savings large enough that "even Zuck wrote about it," and it propelled his fast promotion to Staff (L6).

**Always keeping a few options warm and using mentors**

Done well, this approach means you'll always have a few candidate projects to choose from. Ryan's practice is to then ask a manager or trusted mentors for their perspective on where to spend time — a discipline of **strategizing with mentors** that he credits as significantly helpful in his growth to Staff. The underlying framework is documented in his level-by-level expectations guides for L4 through L7, which serve as the target for the scope you aim to find or create.
