# Auto Research with Specialist Agents Develops Effective and Non-Trivial Training Recipes

**ArXiv:** [2605.05724](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.05724)

## 🎯 Pitch

Agents that split up recipe design roles and share a log of past crashes and score misses can autonomously turn experiment failures into concrete code edits rather than one-shot guesses. Left unattended across 1,200+ trials, this feedback loop improved three distinct training pipelines without humans ever picking proposals or fixing broken runs.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper studies **auto research** as a closed empirical loop driven by external measurement, instantiating it across three compute-budgeted training-recipe environments—Parameter Golf (validation loss under artifact and time caps), NanoChat-D12 (CORE score under a fixed pretraining wallclock), and CIFAR-10 Airbench96 (training speed under an accuracy gate). The core mechanism is a **submitted-trial feedback loop** in which specialist agents partition recipe surfaces (architecture, optimization, systems, etc.) and share **measured lineage**—a cross-trial record of hypotheses, code diffs, scores, runtimes, and failure summaries—so that evaluator-owned outcomes (crashes, size blocks, budget overruns, gate misses) propagate as concrete evidence into later program-level recipe edits rather than one-shot suggestions. Across 1,197 headline-run trials plus 600 Parameter Golf control trials with no human intervention during search, the same loop autonomously writes code, submits experiments, absorbs feedback, and improves public starting recipes by 0.81% lower validation bpb on Parameter Golf, 38.7% higher CORE on NanoChat-D12, and 4.59% faster wallclock on CIFAR-10 Airbench96, with lineage feedback identified as the key mechanism for converting measured failures into follow-up edits—establishing that closed-loop auto research produces measurable, auditable progress on real training pipelines, though the recorded proposals combine and transfer known techniques rather than inventing paradigm-level architectures.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: ML Research Automation Remains Human-Bottlenecked at the Proposal Level

The fundamental problem this paper addresses is that **machine learning research—specifically the iterative loop of proposing ideas, implementing them in code, running experiments, and deciding what to try next—remains a predominantly human-driven process despite significant advances in automating individual pieces of it.** The paper opens by framing this directly:

> "Machine learning research advances by measured iteration: change code, launch experiments, read results, and choose the next move."

This framing is deceptively simple. In practice, a human researcher spends substantial time on each phase: reading prior work to formulate hypotheses, editing training code (architecture, optimizer, scheduler, loss functions), launching jobs, waiting for results, diagnosing failures, and deciding whether a result warrants a follow-up edit or a complete pivot. The bottleneck is not any single phase but the **integration of all phases into a coherent, evidence-driven search process** where each decision is grounded in measured outcomes.

The gap the paper identifies is that prior automation work has addressed fragments of this loop—program synthesis, hyperparameter optimization, agent-based code editing—but **no system has demonstrated a sustained, externally measured feedback loop over real training recipes where failures (crashes, budget overruns, size cap violations, accuracy-gate misses) become actionable evidence that shapes subsequent proposals.** The paper's core wager is that closing this loop produces a qualitatively different empirical object: not a generated paper or a single model checkpoint, but an **auditable trajectory of proposals, code diffs, experiments, scores, and failure labels** that can be inspected, reproduced, and analyzed.

### Why Training Recipes Are a Natural Testbed

The paper chooses **compute-budgeted training recipe development** as its domain for several reasons, some explicit and others implicit in the task selection:

**Recipes expose a broad, constraint-rich surface.** A training recipe touches architecture (attention variants, normalization, recurrence), optimization (Muon, Lion, AdamW, schedule families), data loading, quantization, loss functions, and systems-level code (kernel dispatch, evaluation protocols). Unlike narrow hyperparameter tuning—which operates over a fixed set of continuous knobs—recipe development requires **program-level edits** that restructure code, swap components, and navigate hard constraints like artifact size caps and wallclock budgets. This makes it a stress test for whether agents can produce substantive, multi-level changes rather than scalar tweaks.

**Constraints provide rich, non-binary feedback.** In most ML benchmarks, the only signal is a scalar score (accuracy, loss, BLEU). Training recipes add legal constraints: a submission that improves loss but exceeds a 16 MB artifact cap is not a failure to discard—it's evidence that the idea works but needs a byte-headroom recovery follow-up. A trial that speeds up training but misses an accuracy gate returns both the speed and the accuracy deficit, making the near-miss a usable signal rather than a generic crash. The paper's environments are chosen to expose different constraint types:

- **Parameter Golf** (validation bits-per-byte on FineWeb): artifact size cap (16 MB), training time cap (600 s), evaluation time cap (600 s). These are hard boundaries—exceeding any one makes the trial ineligible regardless of score.
- **NanoChat-D12** (CORE pretraining score): fixed 90-minute wallclock budget. The constraint is temporal headroom: faster code can spend recovered time on more training tokens, which is a different kind of feedback than a binary pass/fail.
- **CIFAR-10 Airbench96** (training speed with accuracy ≥ 0.96): an explicit accuracy gate that rejects fast-but-inaccurate recipes. The gate returns measured accuracy, so a trial at 0.95956 is a concrete signal about how far the speed recipe pushed past the frontier.

**The tasks are affordable but strict.** Parameter Golf trials complete in at most 1,200 seconds (600 training + 600 evaluation) on eight H100s. NanoChat-D12 trials fit in 90 minutes. CIFAR-10 Airbench96 trials finish in under 30 seconds per cold-process seed. This makes repeated experimentation feasible—a necessary condition for closed-loop search—while the strictness of the constraints prevents shortcuts like simply scaling up compute or ignoring runtime.

### Where Prior Work Falls Short

The paper situates itself against four lines of prior work, each of which addresses a piece of the closed-loop picture but leaves the integrated feedback loop unstudied.

#### 1. Evaluator-Driven Program Search Operates Over Narrower Surfaces

Systems like **AlphaDev** (Mankowitz et al., 2023), **FunSearch** (Romera-Paredes et al., 2024), and **AutoML-Zero** (Real et al., 2020) demonstrate that language models can propose programs (sorting algorithms, mathematical functions, small ML pipelines) and let an evaluator decide validity. **AlphaEvolve** (Novikov et al., 2025) extends this to an evolutionary coding agent. These systems share the evaluator-driven pattern with the current paper, but they target **algorithms and infrastructure** rather than full training pipelines. An algorithm-discovery system typically operates over a domain-specific language or constrained program space where the correctness condition is well-defined (e.g., "does this sort correctly?"). A training recipe, by contrast, involves data loading, optimizer state, schedules, kernel dispatch, and evaluation under hardware-specific constraints—crashes, artifact caps, and runtime bottlenecks are not correctness failures but boundary conditions that shape the search. The paper's contribution is moving the evaluator-driven pattern to this richer, less formalized space where **failures are the primary research signal.**

#### 2. ML Experimentation Agents Lack Sustained Feedback Loops

Several systems have placed language agents into ML experimentation environments:

- **MLAgentBench** (Huang et al., 2023) and **MLE-bench** (Chan et al., 2024) evaluate agents on repeated ML experiments, but the experiments are typically short and the primary metric is whether the agent reaches a target score.
- **RE-Bench** (Wijk et al., 2025) evaluates open-ended ML research engineering against human experts, but its focus is on comparing agent vs. human performance rather than analyzing the structure of the agent's search process.
- **MLGym-Bench** (Nathani et al., 2025) frames open-ended AI research as agent environments and makes a pointed finding: the gains it observes often come from **hyperparameter tuning rather than new hypotheses, algorithms, or architectures.** This is a critical observation for the current paper, because it suggests that without the right structure, agents default to scalar knob-twiddling rather than proposing qualitative program changes.
- **AIBuildAI** (Zhang et al., 2026) studies hierarchical model-building agents on MLE-Bench, and **PostTrainBench** (Rank et al., 2026) asks frontier agents to improve LLM post-training under bounded compute while exposing reward-hacking failures.
- **The AI Scientist** (Lu et al., 2024) adds idea generation and paper writing to the loop, but the output is a generated paper rather than an auditable trajectory of experiments.

The common thread is that these systems treat the agent's output as a **final score or generated artifact** rather than studying the **trajectory itself** as the primary empirical object. The current paper's key departure is that the output is not a single recipe or a paper—it's the full record of proposals, edits, outcomes, and feedback, with the trajectory analyzed for how failures become follow-up edits and how information propagates across roles.

#### 3. Repository-Editing and Long-Running Agent Systems Have Not Been Applied to Constrained Recipe Search

**SWE-bench** and **SWE-agent** (Jimenez et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2024) test whether agents can resolve real-world GitHub issues by editing repositories. Anthropic's engineering blogs (2024, 2025a, 2025b) provide practical context on building effective, multi-agent, and long-running coding agents. These systems demonstrate that agents can edit code across file boundaries and sustain multi-step tasks, but they operate on software engineering problems where the feedback is a test suite or a human reviewer—not on ML training tasks where the feedback is noisy, stochastic, and comes from external evaluators that measure physical runtime, artifact size, and statistical accuracy.

The gap is not that agents can't edit code—SWE-bench shows they can—but that **the feedback loop in recipe development is qualitatively different from software engineering.** A failed test tells you what assertion didn't hold; a size-blocked trial tells you the idea works but the implementation exceeds a byte budget, requiring a fundamentally different kind of follow-up (artifact headroom recovery rather than logic debugging).

#### 4. Hyperparameter Optimization and Neural Architecture Search Assume Fixed Search Spaces

Classical HPO (Bergstra and Bengio, 2012; Snoek et al., 2012; Li et al., 2018), population-based training (Jaderberg et al., 2017), and NAS (Zoph and Le, 2017; Real et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2019) all assume a **fixed parameter or architecture space** over which to optimize. The search procedure varies (random search, Bayesian optimization, Hyperband, evolutionary methods, differentiable relaxation), but the space of possible configurations is defined in advance. A training recipe, by contrast, is **open-ended**: you can change the attention mechanism, rewrite the loss function, add a logit-bias path, change the kernel dispatch, modify the evaluation protocol, or introduce entirely new components. The space is defined by the programming language and the task constraints, not by a pre-specified grid of hyperparameters.

This is why the paper insists that its unit of contribution is the **submitted trial** rather than the **scalar score.** A hyperparameter sweep produces a table of numbers; a closed-loop recipe search produces a trace of code edits, each associated with a hypothesis, a diagnostic or improvement signal, and a parent-child relationship that shows which earlier trial motivated which later edit. The empirical object is fundamentally richer.

### Reconciling Conflicting Signals in Prior Work

The paper does not frame itself as resolving a direct contradiction in the literature—its target is a gap rather than a dispute. However, there is an implicit tension it addresses. On one side, systems like AlphaDev, FunSearch, and MLAgentBench suggest that language agents can make meaningful research contributions when coupled to evaluators. On the other side, MLGym-Bench's finding that gains mostly come from hyperparameter tuning rather than novel ideas, and the general difficulty of getting agents to sustain long-horizon tasks without collapsing into repetitive behavior, suggest that the promise of autonomous research is largely unrealized outside narrow domains.

The paper's resolution is architectural: **specialist roles, shared lineage, and constraint-rich environments** together create conditions where agents sustain diverse, program-level proposals rather than defaulting to scalar tuning. The paper doesn't claim to solve the general problem of autonomous research—it explicitly notes that the observed proposals "combine and transfer known techniques rather than propose anything as structurally novel as the original Transformer." But it does claim, and provides evidence for, the more modest claim that under the right feedback infrastructure, agents can **apply and combine known techniques in principled, evidence-driven ways** that produce measurable improvements across different constraint regimes.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper's positioning can be understood along four axes:

**1. The output is the trajectory, not the final recipe.** This is the paper's most distinctive claim. The contributions section states that the output is "an auditable trajectory of proposals, code diffs, experiments, scores, and failure labels." This reframes auto research from "did the agent produce a good result?" to "can we inspect HOW the result was produced, and does the search process show evidence of learning from failure?" The paper's extensive use of trace data—parent-child relationships, cross-role information sharing, proposal entropy, failure-to-follow-up chains—is not supplementary; it is the primary empirical contribution.

**2. The evaluator owns the measurement.** This is a design principle, not just a technical detail. In all three environments, the editable recipe does not control the scoring: Parameter Golf uses the official evaluation path, NanoChat-D12 uses a protected parser and evaluator-side classifier, and CIFAR uses shell-side timing with a strict accuracy gate. The paper audits submissions for evaluator contact and reports zero edits touching sensitive evaluator or parser paths. This prevents the most obvious form of reward hacking—editing the code to print a better score—and ensures that improvements reflect real recipe changes, not measurement manipulation.

**3. Specialist roles partition the surface; shared lineage carries evidence across roles.** The paper's architecture is not a generic multi-agent system where identical agents compete on the same task. Specialists are assigned non-overlapping recipe surfaces (e.g., architecture, optimization, systems, quantization) and receive role-specific prompts that scope their edits to their domain. Shared lineage—a compact slice of prior trials including hypotheses, scores, statuses, and failure summaries—is rendered into each specialist's prompt so that a systems specialist can see that an architecture specialist's edit exposed runtime slack, and can then propose spending that slack on more training tokens. This is the mechanism that turns individual trial outcomes into cross-role research trajectories.

**4. The scope is compositional, not paradigm-inventing.** The paper is explicit about what its loop does and does not do:

> "The recorded trials do not show paradigm-level architecture invention such as a replacement for the original Transformer. Future agents may cross this boundary, and the same evaluator-driven feedback loop remains the natural arena for measuring whether such ideas hold up under real training."

This scoping is important because it sets the paper's empirical contribution in the right context. The claim is not that language agents can invent new science from scratch, but that they can **sustain a measured, auditable search over known techniques** that produces non-trivial improvements on real training pipelines—and that the infrastructure for doing so (specialist roles, shared lineage, evaluator-owned measurement) is the key enabling factor.

### The Concrete Gap This Paper Fills

To summarize the gap: prior work provides (a) evaluator-driven program search over narrow algorithmic spaces, (b) agent-based ML experimentation that treats individual runs as the unit of analysis, (c) repository-editing agents for software engineering tasks, and (d) hyperparameter optimization over fixed search spaces. None of these provides a system that **closes the full propose-edit-measure-revise loop over real training recipes**, where failures propagate as actionable evidence into follow-up proposals, specialist roles maintain proposal diversity under shared lineage, and the evaluator is external to the editable code. The paper's contribution is to instantiate this loop in three environments with complementary constraint regimes, to demonstrate that it produces measurable improvements without human intervention during search, and to analyze the trajectory itself—not just the final scores—as evidence that shared lineage and role partitioning are the mechanisms that make the loop work.

The paper does not claim to be the first to use agents for ML experimentation, or the first to use evaluator-driven search, or the first to design multi-agent systems. It claims to be the first to put these pieces together into a sustained, externally measured feedback loop over real training recipes and to study the resulting trajectory as a first-class empirical object. The value proposition is that this trajectory is **auditable, reproducible, and exposes how research decisions were made**—a property that single-shot agent outputs (generated papers, final recipes) lack.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

The paper builds a **closed-loop research automation system** where language-model agents autonomously propose, implement, execute, score, and revise machine learning training recipes under external measurement, without human intervention during the search process. The system solves the problem that ML research—the iterative cycle of hypothesizing an improvement, editing code, running experiments, reading results, and deciding what to try next—remains human-bottlenecked at the proposal level, with prior automation addressing pieces of this loop (program synthesis, hyperparameter optimization, agent-based code editing) but never closing the full propose-edit-measure-revise cycle over real training pipelines where failures are the primary feedback signal. The solution takes the shape of a **specialist-partitioned search swarm** connected by a **shared lineage blackboard**: specialist agents each own a non-overlapping slice of the recipe surface (architecture, optimization, systems, etc.), propose code edits, submit trials to an external evaluator that owns the measurement, and receive lineage feedback—a compact record of prior proposals, scores, statuses, and failure summaries—that propagates evidence across roles and turns crashes, size blocks, budget overruns, and accuracy-gate misses into actionable follow-up edits rather than discarded attempts.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The system has five major components that interact in a continuous loop:

1. **Task Environments (three, fixed before search).** Each environment provides an editable recipe file (e.g., `train_gpt.py` for Parameter Golf), an external evaluator that owns the measurement, and a set of legality constraints (artifact size caps, time budgets, accuracy gates). The environments are Parameter Golf (validation bits-per-byte on FineWeb, 16 MB artifact cap, 600 s training + 600 s evaluation budget on 8 H100s), NanoChat-D12 (CORE pretraining score under a fixed 90-minute wallclock on 8 H100s), and CIFAR-10 Airbench96 (training wallclock under a strict 0.96 accuracy gate on 1 GPU).

2. **Specialist Agents (role-partitioned, fixed per run).** The recipe surface is partitioned into non-overlapping domains. Parameter Golf uses 10 specialists (architecture, optimization, quantization, regularization, loss, evaluation, curriculum, tokenizer, test-time training, meta search). NanoChat-D12 uses 5 (architecture, optimization, data, schedule, systems). CIFAR-10 Airbench96 uses 5 (architecture, optimization, augmentation, loss, regularization). Each specialist receives a domain-specific system prompt scoping its edits and a fresh lineage view at session start.

3. **Submitted-Trial Loop (the unit of work).** A trial is a tuple: hypothesis text, executable code edit, evaluator-owned outcome (score, status, timing, failure metadata), and feedback signal. Each specialist session is a bounded LLM-agent SDK call that reads current lineage, proposes a hypothesis, implements it as code, validates locally (syntax check, size projection), submits the trial to the external evaluator, reads the outcome, and may submit a second trial if the first outcome surfaces a concrete follow-up edit. Sessions terminate under a tool-turn cap.

4. **Shared Lineage Blackboard (the feedback channel).** A compact, append-only record of all submitted trials including proposing role, hypothesis text, parent experiment ID, status, score delta, wallclock timing, artifact byte counts, and crash excerpts. Before each specialist session, the prompt renderer selects a lineage slice containing the current best row, recent rows from the same specialist, and recent rows from adjacent specialists. This preserves the frontier and keeps failed directions visible without replaying the full transcript.

5. **Supervisor Process (orchestration, not intervention).** A head-node process that launches specialist coroutines in parallel, manages GPU-cluster job submission, enforces termination conditions (wall-clock deadline or no-improvement grace period), and writes the blackboard files. The supervisor never chooses proposals, edits recipes, overrides scores, or repairs failed trials during search.

Information flows as follows: a specialist session starts → the prompt renderer reads the blackboard and assembles a fresh lineage view (current best row, specialist recent rows, adjacent-specialist rows) → the specialist proposes a hypothesis, edits the target recipe file, and validates locally → the specialist calls `submit_trial`, which packages the current recipe, sends it to the GPU cluster, and blocks until the job completes → the external evaluator measures the run (score, status, timing, failure metadata) and appends a row to the blackboard → the specialist reads the outcome and may submit a second trial if the result points to a concrete next edit → the session terminates → the next specialist session reads the updated blackboard and the cycle repeats.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily an **empirical systems paper** whose core idea is that closed-loop auto research produces measurable, auditable progress when the environment owns the measurement, per-trial cost is bounded, and shared lineage carries measured evidence (including failures) across specialist-partitioned recipe surfaces into follow-up proposals.

#### Task Environments and Feedback Signals (Section 3.1)

The paper uses three environments because they expose different constraint regimes through the same submitted-trial loop. Each environment fixes a set of editable files, a scored metric, legality checks, and an evaluator-owned measurement path. The editable recipe never controls the evaluator.

**Parameter Golf: artifact size and time pressure around the validation bpb frontier.**

Parameter Golf is a public challenge (OpenAI, 2025) that tasks participants with reducing validation bits-per-byte (bpb) on a fixed FineWeb-derived language modeling task (Penedo et al., 2024). The hard constraints are: (a) the packed artifact—compressed model weights plus source code—must not exceed 16 MB (16,000,000 bytes), (b) the training phase must complete within 600 seconds, and (c) the evaluation phase must complete within 600 seconds. All three are enforced by the harness: a size-blocked trial is ineligible regardless of its score, and a budget-overrun trial is aborted and classified as ineligible.

The starting recipe is the public leaderboard score of 1.0810 bpb. The paper uses this as its denominator for relative improvement (0.81% reduction to 1.0722), keeping the delta tied to the public record rather than a locally recalibrated baseline. This is a deliberate choice: it means the search is targeting an improvement over a known public number rather than optimizing against a hardware-dependent local baseline.

The dominant feedback signals are size and budget pressure. Because the starting recipe already sits close to the 600-second evaluation cap, many proposals that improve bpb also increase runtime and exceed the budget—the "no-lineage" control run hit the eval-budget cap on 61.5% of trials (Section 4.3). Size blocks are the other dominant failure mode: a proposal may improve bpb but add too many bytes to the artifact, producing a measured score and an exact byte excess that the next specialist can target for artifact-headroom recovery.

Each trial returns: score (val_bpb), status (keep/discard/crash/preflight_crash/size_blocked/budget_overrun), exact byte counts (packed artifact size), and per-phase timing (training seconds, evaluation seconds). This rich feedback—a quantitative boundary rather than a binary pass/fail—is what enables the follow-up repair pattern that the paper identifies as a core capability of the loop.

**NanoChat-D12: wallclock headroom under a fixed pretraining budget.**

NanoChat-D12 is a fixed-budget pretraining task derived from the nanochat pipeline (Karpathy, 2025) using the CORE evaluation metric from DataComp-LM (Li et al., 2024). The constraint is a 90-minute wallclock cap on 8 H100s during which the model pretrains on a fixed tokenizer, sharded dataset, and evaluation bundle prepared before launch. The metric is CORE, a composite language-model evaluation score where higher is better.

The starting point is not a public leaderboard number but a **calibrated run** of the unmodified upstream recipe at the pinned commit, producing 0.1618 CORE in the paper's GPU environment. Calibration is necessary because CORE depends on hardware, runtime images, and offline-mode settings—running the same code on different hardware produces different absolute numbers. The calibration run is append-only: `python -m multi_agent_nc.calibrate_baseline --score 0.1618` writes the score to a log and refuses to overwrite an already-populated log, preventing stale denominators.

The dominant feedback signal is **wallclock headroom.** Because the total training time is fixed at 90 minutes, any code change that reduces per-step latency (e.g., switching from a slower attention kernel to a faster one) recovers wallclock that can be spent on more training tokens. This is qualitatively different from Parameter Golf's hard caps: there is no "budget overrun" penalty because the run simply finishes when time expires, but the number of training tokens processed in that time directly affects CORE. The feedback that matters is not "did this fit?" but "how much slack did this expose, and what can be done with it?"

Agents edit the coordinator script and the vendored nanochat Python tree, but trials cannot download during execution—a protection against the agent fetching pre-trained weights or external code that would circumvent the recipe-development task. Tokenizer files, pretraining shards, and the evaluation bundle are prepared before launch and frozen.

**CIFAR-10 Airbench96: accuracy-gated speed optimization.**

CIFAR-10 Airbench96 is a speed benchmark (Jordan, 2024) that rewards lower wallclock training time but **only when mean CIFAR-10 accuracy across ten cold-process seeds reaches at least 0.96** (Krizhevsky, 2009). The constraint is not a time cap but a quality gate: a trial that trains in 10 seconds but achieves 0.94 accuracy is ineligible—its time measurement is blanked and it cannot win regardless of speed.

The starting point is the unmodified Airbench96 recipe calibrated under the paper's ten-seed cold-process protocol to 26.3560 seconds. The upstream reported reference of 27.3000 seconds is the same recipe under a different measurement protocol, not the denominator for the 4.59% improvement. The cold-process protocol re-imports the recipe in a fresh Python process per seed so that transient compile state (JIT compilation, CUDA context warmup) does not bias the wallclock measurement.

The dominant feedback is the **accuracy gate.** A trial that is fast but falls below 0.96 returns both its measured time and its measured accuracy (e.g., 25.1650 seconds at 0.95956 accuracy), making the near-miss a concrete signal: the agent knows exactly how fast the recipe was and exactly how far it fell short, and can target a repair (e.g., adjusting the warmup ratio to recover accuracy margin) rather than guessing why the trial failed. The paper reports that 81 of 97 CIFAR headline-run trials missed the accuracy gate (Section 4.1), confirming that the gate is the primary constraint shaping the search trajectory.

The evaluator owns the timing: the run script writes timing sidecars that the classifier reads, and the editable recipe cannot report its own time. This prevents the most obvious form of reward hacking—editing the code to print a faster time—and is part of the paper's broader measurement-hardening design philosophy.

**Environment summary and why three environments matter.**

The three environments are chosen for complementarity, not redundancy. Parameter Golf tests whether the loop can navigate simultaneous size and time pressure while improving a continuous metric. NanoChat-D12 tests whether the loop can convert a systems-level diagnostic (kernel performance) into a training-level improvement (more tokens, higher CORE). CIFAR-10 Airbench96 tests whether the loop can treat an accuracy-gate miss as a research signal rather than a discard, using the measured accuracy deficit to guide a targeted repair. Together, they cover three distinct constraint regimes—hard caps with binary eligibility, fixed wallclock with token-throughput headroom, and accuracy-gated speed optimization—each of which produces different feedback structures that the same submitted-trial loop must handle.

#### The Submitted-Trial Loop as Atomic Unit (Section 3.2)

**Trial definition.**

A trial is the atomic unit of the empirical loop. Formally, a trial is a row in the append-only results log containing: the proposing specialist role, a one-sentence hypothesis text, a summary of the code diff, the parent experiment ID (which prior trial's code state this trial forked from), the evaluator-assigned status (one of nine values from Table 5), the measured score when valid, the score delta relative to the prior best, training and evaluation wallclock seconds, packed artifact bytes, and crash metadata (deepest exception line) when applicable.

The trial is defined by its relationship to the evaluator: **the evaluator owns the measurement.** The editable recipe does not control the score, timing, or status. This is enforced differently per environment: Parameter Golf uses the official evaluation path (the task harness measures bpb), NanoChat-D12 uses a protected parser that extracts CORE from the log and an evaluator-side classifier path (the agent edits model/training/data/optimizer files but never touches parser or evaluator paths), and CIFAR uses shell-side timing with a strict accuracy gate (the run script writes timing sidecars that the classifier reads). The paper audits submissions for evaluator contact and reports zero edits touching sensitive evaluator or parser paths (Appendix I).

**Lifecycle of a single trial.**

The lifecycle has seven phases, executed sequentially within a single specialist session:

1. **Lineage reading.** The specialist session begins with a fresh prompt containing the current lineage slice: the current best row (score, experiment ID), the LEADERBOARD.md summary (top-N keep rows by score), the KNOWLEDGE.md summary (curated tree of prior hypotheses and outcomes), and the Recent Activity block (the most recent ten trial rows with role, status, score, delta, and hypothesis). The specialist reads this to understand the current frontier, which directions have been tried and failed, and which adjacent-specialist results might be relevant.

2. **Hypothesis formation and rebasing.** The specialist decides what to edit. It may start from the current best code state (via `rebase_to(best, workdir)`) or fork from a non-best parent if a particular prior trial's direction seems promising. The specialist formulates a one-sentence hypothesis (e.g., "adding z-loss to test-time training updates reduces validation bpb by regularizing the evaluation-time gradient") and a signed expected delta.

3. **Code editing.** The specialist edits the target recipe file using the SDK's Edit tool. For Parameter Golf and NanoChat-D12, this is a single editable Python file (`train_gpt.py` for PG, the coordinator script plus vendored nanochat tree for NC). For CIFAR, it is the `airbench96.py` file. The edit must fall within the specialist's assigned domain (enforced by the domain preamble, which scopes what the role owns and does not touch).

4. **Local validation.** Before submitting to the GPU cluster (which burns real compute and queue time), the specialist runs two local checks: `syntax_check` (py_compile the editable file, millisecond-scale, catches SyntaxError before a GPU trial) and `size_project` (run the real LZMA+base85 pack step locally and report the projected packed size against the 16 MB cap for Parameter Golf). If either check fails, the specialist fixes the error and re-validates. Local failures are recorded as preflight_crash status and do not consume GPU time.

5. **Trial submission.** The specialist calls `submit_trial` with the hypothesis text, expected delta, parent experiment ID, and its specialist role. The tool packages the current recipe, submits it to the GPU cluster (a fresh 8-H100 worker for PG and NC; a long-lived GPU worker for CIFAR), and blocks until the job completes. The external evaluator runs the training and evaluation phases, measures the score, and classifies the status.

6. **Outcome reading.** The `submit_trial` tool returns a row containing the measured score, status, timing, artifact bytes, and any crash metadata. The specialist reads this outcome and decides whether to stop (one submit is a complete session) or submit a second trial if "the returned row points to a concrete next edit" (from the global rules, Figure 4).

7. **Blackboard update and session termination.** The trial row is appended to `results.tsv`, the tree structure is updated in `tree.tsv`, the current-best row is atomically updated in `best.json` if the status was keep, and the specialist session terminates. The next specialist session (which may be the same role or a different role) reads the updated blackboard and the cycle continues.

**Specialist session boundaries.**

Each specialist session is a **bounded LLM-agent SDK call, not an always-running process.** The specialist receives a fresh lineage view at session start, may submit one or two trials (a second submit is allowed only when the first outcome surfaces a concrete follow-up edit—this constraint prevents agents from chaining speculative edits without evidence), and terminates when either it declines to submit a second trial or it hits the tool-turn cap (200 turns per session, sized for multi-submit sessions with library drill-down). This session boundary is important because it forces each specialist to ground its proposal in the current lineage rather than carrying forward an internal state that might become stale as other specialists land keeps.

**The nine-status taxonomy (Table 5).**

Every submitted trial receives one of nine status values, assigned by the post-trial parser that reads the combined preflight + training + packing log:

- **baseline:** The single seed row written by bootstrap_from_baseline at search start. Not an agent trial.
- **keep:** Valid measured run with a score strictly better than the prior best in the metric's preferred direction (lower bpb for PG, higher CORE for NC, lower time for CIFAR).
- **discard:** Valid measured run whose score is not strictly better than the prior best. This is recorded feedback—it tells later specialists that this direction was tried and didn't beat the frontier.
- **crash:** The training phase exited non-zero and no valid score could be parsed, OR the pack step failed but training ran. A crash returns the deepest exception line, which is rendered into lineage so the next specialist sees the specific failure mode.
- **preflight_crash:** Local syntax check failed OR GPU-cluster submission failed before any GPU time was consumed. This is the cheapest failure class—no compute was burned.
- **size_blocked:** Packed artifact size exceeded 16 MB at either the preflight smoke pack or the post-run pack. The exact byte count is recorded.
- **train_budget_overrun:** Training exceeded the 600-second cap plus a 5-second tolerance for step-atomic granularity.
- **eval_budget_overrun:** Evaluation exceeded 600 seconds, or the outer trial timeout fired before evaluation completed. No tolerance—evaluation is continuous wallclock.
- **disqualified (CIFAR only):** Mean accuracy across cold-process seeds fell below the strict 0.96 gate. The training time is blanked and the row cannot win regardless of speed. The accuracy deficit is recorded.
- **harness_abort:** Bookkeeping-side failure (e.g., cluster scheduler bookkeeping loss). Not a substantive signal; quarantined from the prompt's Recent Activity so it doesn't distract specialists.

**Why this taxonomy matters for the loop.**

The taxonomy converts continuous outcomes (a score with a delta) and hard constraints (size, time, accuracy) into **discrete, interpretable states** that the next specialist can reason about. A discard tells the specialist "this direction didn't beat the frontier—try a variant or pivot." A size_blocked tells the specialist "this direction works for score but exceeds the byte cap by exactly N bytes—can you recover that headroom?" A disqualified (CIFAR) tells the specialist "this speed recipe is N milliseconds fast but M accuracy points short—can you recover the margin?" The taxonomy is the interface between the evaluator's measurements and the agent's reasoning—it turns raw numbers into actionable categories without losing the quantitative detail.

#### Specialist Roles and Shared Lineage (Section 3.3)

**Why specialist roles?**

The core architectural hypothesis is that **partitioning the recipe surface into non-overlapping domains and assigning each to a dedicated specialist** produces a more diverse, less repetitive proposal stream than either a single generalist agent or multiple generic agents with identical prompts. The paper provides two lines of evidence for this, both from the Parameter Golf controls (Table 3 and Figure 3):

1. **Proposal entropy.** In the matched 200-trial window, the role-swarm with lineage produces 134.8 effective proposal clusters (measured by TF-IDF embedding of hypothesis text, clustering at cosine threshold 0.30, with effective count = exp(Shannon entropy over cluster sizes)). The generic 10-agent control—same number of parallel contexts, same toolkit, no role-specific prompts—produces only 41.1 effective clusters. The largest cluster in the generic run consumes 12.0% of submissions; in the role swarm, 3.5%. This is not a throughput difference (both runs have similar trials-per-hour, 18.15 vs. 16.79), but a proposal diversity difference: role conditioning makes sessions attend to different recipe surfaces rather than repeatedly editing the most salient knob.

2. **Boundary discipline.** The single-generalist control produces 17.5% of submissions in its largest cluster, including 32 preflight crashes around polar-coefficient edits (a recurring failure pattern where the agent repeatedly tries the same class of edit that fails syntax or size checks). The role swarm's largest cluster is 3.5%, with far fewer repeated preflight crashes because each specialist's domain preamble scopes its edit radius and the anti-anchoring banlist prevents re-exploring known dead ends.

**The specialist taxonomy is chosen before each run and fixed during search.** It is not learned or adapted. The choice of how many roles and what domains reflects the environment's constraint surface:

- **Parameter Golf (10 roles):** architecture, optimization, quantization, regularization, loss, evaluation, curriculum, tokenizer, test-time training, meta search. The broad split reflects the wide recipe surface under the hard artifact cap—many different components can be changed, and each role owns a distinct set of knobs.
- **NanoChat-D12 (5 roles):** architecture, optimization, data, schedule, systems. The narrower split reflects the fixed-budget pretraining task—the primary degrees of freedom are the model architecture, the optimizer, the data mix, the training schedule, and the systems-level code that determines runtime efficiency.
- **CIFAR-10 Airbench96 (5 roles):** architecture, optimization, augmentation, loss, regularization. The split reflects the speed-accuracy tradeoff—architecture changes affect both training time and accuracy, optimization and augmentation affect convergence speed, and loss/regularization affect the accuracy margin under the shortened schedule.

**What each specialist sees and does not see.**

Each specialist receives a prompt assembled from three pieces (Appendix A):

1. **Knowledge files.** Static markdown documents under the task's `knowledge/` directory, concatenated and pinned at the top of the system prompt. These are task-specific and fixed before the reported run starts. They provide setup notes, known gotchas, and a reference library of techniques.

2. **Global rules.** A task-level protocol shared by every specialist on that task. It defines the hard limits (artifact caps, time budgets, accuracy gates), the tool protocol, and the per-session workflow. Figure 4 reproduces the abridged Parameter Golf version, which specifies: one submit is a complete session, a second submit is allowed only when the first row surfaces a concrete next edit, edit sequence is Read → Edit → syntax_check → size_project → submit_trial, and hard limits on submission size, training time, and evaluation time.

3. **Domain preamble.** A specialist-specific scope and edit-radius statement. Figures 5, 6, and 7 show three of the ten Parameter Golf preambles. Each follows the same structure:
   - **Scope:** what this role owns (e.g., "attention variants, recurrence modules, residual topology, MLP variants, normalisation, embedding schemes" for architecture).
   - **Non-scope:** what the role does not touch (e.g., "you do NOT own optimizer, loss, dataset, or quantization" for architecture).
   - **Edit radius:** guidance on the level of intervention expected (e.g., "a single scalar tweak to an existing module is hparam noise, not architecture—that belongs in opt or meta. If your draft hypothesis is 'change one number,' you're probably in the wrong domain; pivot to a qualitative edit").

The meta-search preamble (Figure 7) is notably different: it is the only role allowed to lean on the lineage as its primary input ("you mostly read results.tsv + KNOWLEDGE.md to find a narrow hyperparameter tweak that several prior trials missed") and has an explicit instruction to check how many trials already touched its proposed knob ("if > 5 in your own domain's recent window, that axis is crowded; find a different one"). This creates a division of labor: structural specialists look outward for qualitative changes, while the meta-search analyst looks inward at the existing trace for underexplored scalar knobs.

**Shared lineage: the mechanism that carries evidence across roles.**

Lineage is the cross-trial record stored in three blackboard files:

- **results.tsv:** append-only, one row per submitted trial with role, hypothesis, parent exp_id, status, measured score, delta vs. prior best, training and evaluation wallclock seconds, packed artifact bytes, and harness notes. This is the ground-truth record.
- **tree.tsv:** the same rows in preorder-sorted form with depth and slash-joined path columns, so subtrees are contiguous and a single awk call can slice an entire branch (e.g., all descendants of the attention-path rewrite trial).
- **best.json:** the current-best row at the moment of write, updated atomically every time a new keep lands.
- **KNOWLEDGE.md and LEADERBOARD.md:** de-identified lineage summaries that the prompt renderer uses to produce the compact Recent Activity block and the top-N keep rows.

Before each specialist session, the prompt renderer selects a compact lineage slice containing three components (Figure 8):

1. **Current best row:** a one-liner with experiment ID, score, and delta.
2. **Top-N keep rows:** the LEADERBOARD.md summary, giving the specialist the global frontier.
3. **Recent Activity (most recent 10 rows):** the most recent ten trials with role, status, score, delta, and hypothesis text. This is the primary channel through which a specialist sees what other roles are doing.

Additionally, two dynamic signals are rendered when triggered:

- **Saturation signal:** emitted when the last five trials from the same role all have absolute delta less than $5 \times 10^{-4}$, warning the specialist that its current direction may be exhausted.
- **Anti-anchoring banlist:** a list of patterns that earlier sessions in the same role have already tried and that failed or returned within noise. This prevents the specialist from repeatedly returning to a high-salience edit that looks promising but doesn't work.

**Why shared lineage is not just a log—it's active research state.**

The paper's central empirical finding is that lineage feedback lets agents turn evaluator outcomes into later program-level recipe edits. This is not automatic—it requires that the lineage prompt carries enough detail for the next specialist to act on. The paper's lineage ablation (Section 4.3, Figure 3 Panel B) demonstrates this:

- **With lineage:** the role swarm finds 16 valid improvements in 200 trials and reaches 1.073142 bpb by trial 176.
- **Without lineage:** the same specialist split, same starting recipe, same budget, and same current-best score line produce only 3 valid improvements and reach 1.077413 bpb by trial 75, then run 125 additional trials without a new keep. The no-lineage run hits the eval-budget cap on 61.5% of trials (vs. 19.0% with lineage) because the "Recent Activity" block—which carries the dynamic knowledge that the current best sits close to the 600-second eval cap—is absent.

The ablation removes three specific feedback channels (Appendix H): the LEADERBOARD.md/KNOWLEDGE.md/Recent Activity sections are dropped from the prompt, the `read_snapshot` and `diff_snapshots` tools are disabled, and Bash reads of blackboard files are rejected at the harness level. The current-best score one-liner and the workdir code state are preserved (the agent still knows the frontier score and can edit code), so the ablation tests whether agents can produce diverse, valid proposals when they have static priors and the current best code but **no record of within-run prior trials' hypotheses, scores, statuses, or failures.**

The result confirms that lineage is active research state: it preserves which measured heads remain useful (15 active parent heads with lineage vs. 3 without), which edits failed (the banlist prevents re-exploration), and which budget boundaries remain (the eval-budget cap proximity is only carried inside the Recent Activity block). Without lineage, specialists repeatedly rediscover the same budget boundary and collide with it.

**Cross-role information sharing is the empirical pattern of interest.**

The paper measures cross-role information sharing by auditing parent edges in the trial tree (Table 3). In the role-swarm with lineage, 83.7% of parent edges (154 of 184 within-window edges) cross role boundaries—a specialist forks from a trial proposed by a different specialist. Of the 12 successful keeps with within-window parents, 10 build on another role's row. The paper's case studies make this concrete:

- **NanoChat-D12:** The systems specialist (trial 007) diagnosed an attention-backend bottleneck and rewrote the attention path from SSSL to L, moving all 12 layers onto Flash SDPA. This exposed runtime slack. Later optimization and schedule specialists (trials 020, 024, 025) used that slack to increase the training token budget and raise the data ratio. The final architecture specialist (trial 156) added a zero-initialized logit-bias path, refining the same head. A systems fact became a training-token proposal became a model refinement—three different roles building on each other's measured outcomes.

- **Parameter Golf:** Trial 587 (some specialist) found that adding TTT-only z-loss to evaluation-time adaptation produced val_bpb=1.072431 but exceeded the artifact size cap by 2,056 bytes. This was a size-blocked trial that returned both a measured score (useful) and an exact byte excess (actionable). Trial 596 (potentially the same or a different specialist) retained the same z-loss mechanism and recovered artifact headroom, turning the idea into a legal keep at val_bpb=1.072251. Later trials 746 and 750 refined the same head with further optimizer and architecture edits.

- **CIFAR-10 Airbench96:** Trial 060 was fast at 25.1650 seconds but missed the accuracy gate with 0.959560 accuracy. This was a disqualified trial that returned both the speed (near the frontier) and the accuracy deficit (quantified). Trial 070 kept the 42-epoch, lr=11 speed recipe and changed only the warmup ratio from 10% to 5%, reaching 25.1464 seconds at 0.960080 accuracy—a valid keep that repaired the gate margin.

In each case, the pattern is the same: a trial that fails a constraint returns measured feedback, and a later trial (often by a different specialist) uses that feedback to make a targeted repair. This is the "closed loop" in closed-loop auto research—not just that the system runs trials in sequence, but that failures propagate as actionable evidence across role boundaries.

**The generic multi-agent control: why roles matter beyond parallelism.**

The generic 10-agent control (Table 3, Figure 3 Panel A) tests whether ten parallel identical agents without role-specific prompts produce similar diversity. The answer is no: the generic run has 41.1 effective clusters (vs. 134.8 for the role swarm), with 12.0% of submissions in the largest cluster (vs. 3.5%). The generic run still shows some cross-agent parent edges (79.1%) because parallel contexts and shared lineage naturally create some differentiation—different agents see different Recent Activity depending on when they launched—but the proposal stream is far more concentrated. Role partitioning therefore does two things: it increases proposal diversity (by scoping each specialist to a different surface) and improves boundary discipline (by giving each specialist explicit non-scope constraints that prevent it from drifting into other roles' domains or making tiny scalar tweaks that belong to meta-search).

**Allocation balance across roles.**

The paper reports that trial allocation across roles was balanced (Appendix G): Parameter Golf assigned 84–96 trials per role with coefficient of variation 0.049, NanoChat-D12 assigned 33–45 trials per role with CV 0.100, and CIFAR-10 Airbench96 assigned 18–21 trials per role with CV 0.062. The outcome differences across tasks (e.g., systems leading NanoChat-D12 with 3 keeps, optimization leading CIFAR with 2 keeps) are therefore driven by which roles' proposals were effective under the task constraints, not by which roles received more budget. This is important for interpreting the cross-task patterns in Table 7: the fact that systems led NanoChat-D12 while architecture contributed across all three tasks reflects genuine differences in which recipe surfaces mattered for each constraint regime.

#### Throughput Model and Parallel Submission (Section 3.4, Equation 1)

**Why throughput matters for closed-loop research.**

The paper defines "affordable iteration" as a necessary condition for closed-loop research: outcomes must return quickly enough to shape later proposals within the same search horizon. If each trial takes days, the loop cannot sustain evidence-driven search—by the time feedback arrives, the context that motivated the trial is stale. The paper's environments are chosen to meet this condition (Parameter Golf trials complete in at most 1,200 seconds; NanoChat-D12 in 90 minutes; CIFAR in under 30 seconds per seed), but raw per-trial speed is not the whole story. Parallel submission is necessary to achieve the throughput that makes feedback useful.

**Wallclock decomposition and throughput equation.**

The paper formalizes trial throughput with Equation 1:

$$\tau_e = \tau_e^{\text{run}} + \tau_e^{\text{eval}} + \tau_e^{\text{queue}} + \tau_e^{\text{log}}$$

$$T_e(N) = \frac{N}{\tau_e} \cdot \eta_{\parallel,e}$$

$$\eta_{\parallel,e} = \frac{T_e(N)}{N \cdot T_e(1)} \in (0, 1]$$

where $\tau_e^{\text{run}}$ is the continuous wallclock for a single trial's training phase, $\tau_e^{\text{eval}}$ is the evaluation phase wallclock, $\tau_e^{\text{queue}}$ is the GPU-cluster queue wait time, and $\tau_e^{\text{log}}$ is the blackboard logging overhead. $T_e(N)$ is the measured throughput with $N$ independent submitters sharing one blackboard, and $\eta_{\parallel,e}$ is the parallel efficiency—how much of the ideal $N$-times speedup is achieved in practice.

**What it computes in operational English:** The total wallclock for one submitted trial is the sum of its training time, evaluation time, queue wait, and logging overhead. With $N$ submitters running in parallel, the ideal throughput would be $N$ trials per $\tau_e$ seconds. In practice, submitters share the GPU pool, cluster queue, and blackboard filelock, so throughput is $\eta_{\parallel,e}$ times the ideal, where $\eta_{\parallel,e}$ is the ratio of actual throughput to $N$ times the single-submitter throughput. For Parameter Golf under the paper's setup, $\eta_{\parallel,\text{PG}} \approx 0.80$ for the ten-specialist swarm and $\eta_{\parallel,\text{PG}} \approx 0.74$ for the ten generic agents.

**Measured throughput and why it separates roles from throughput effects.**

The paper estimates these quantities on Parameter Golf with the same starting recipe, 600-second budgets, and continuous wallclock only (excluding human pauses). Over the matched first-200-trial window:

- Single-generalist throughput: 2.26 trials per hour.
- Ten-specialist role swarm: 18.15 trials per hour, giving $\eta_{\parallel,\text{PG}} \approx 0.80$ against the ideal 10× speedup (22.6 trials per hour).
- Ten generic agents: 16.79 trials per hour, giving $\eta_{\parallel,\text{PG}} \approx 0.74$.

The role swarm's throughput is within 10% of the generic swarm's, but its proposal diversity is over 3× higher (134.8 vs. 41.1 effective clusters). This is the key separation: **the role-versus-generic difference is about proposal diversity and boundary discipline, not raw throughput.** Parallel submission scales up the trial rate, but role partitioning scales up the idea rate at the same trial rate.

**Why efficiency is below 1.0 and what that means.**

The parallel efficiency is below 1.0 because submitters share the GPU pool (only so many 8-H100 workers are available simultaneously), the cluster queue (jobs wait for resources), and the blackboard filelock (only one process can write to `results.tsv` at a time). This is not a bug—it's the real constraint of running on a shared cluster—and the paper uses Equation 1 to plan future runs: "longer runs over weeks of continuous GPU time may expose cross-role composition beyond the matched 200-trial windows, and Equation 1 helps plan that compute" (Section 5). In practice, the 18.15 trials-per-hour rate means the role swarm processes roughly one trial every 3.3 minutes across its ten parallel specialists, which is fast enough that a specialist submitting a trial at session start can expect its outcome to appear in the lineage of a sibling specialist launching a session a few minutes later. Feedback propagates within the same search horizon.

**Why this formalism is necessary but not central.**

The throughput model is the paper's acknowledgment that parallel submission infrastructure matters for closed-loop research, but it is not the main empirical contribution. The paper uses it to establish that the role-versus-generic difference is not confounded by throughput (the measured $\eta_{\parallel,\text{PG}}$ values are close), and to provide a planning tool for future work. The main empirical object is the trajectory—proposals, edits, outcomes, and feedback propagation—not the throughput numbers.

#### Calibration, Measurement Hardening, and Evaluator-Touch Audit (Appendices F, I)

**Why calibration is necessary.**

Public benchmark scores assume a specific hardware, runtime-image, and measurement-protocol stack. When the paper's experiments run on different hardware (an internal GPU cluster with potentially different CUDA versions, driver overheads, or OS scheduling), the same code produces different absolute numbers. Calibration runs the unmodified starting recipe under the paper's hardware and protocol, producing a denominator for relative improvement that is meaningful—the search is measured against what the starting recipe achieves on the same hardware under the same conditions, not against a public number that may reflect different infrastructure.

**Parameter Golf calibration: using the public denominator.**

The Parameter Golf starting point is the public 1.0810 leaderboard record. The paper uses this as its denominator without local recalibration, keeping "the delta tied to the public target record" (Section 3.1). This is possible because the Parameter Golf harness defines its measurement protocol precisely and the paper's cluster matches the expected hardware (8 H100s). The search delta of 0.81% reduction is therefore relative to the public number, not a locally recalibrated baseline.

**NanoChat-D12 calibration: append-only baseline run.**

The NanoChat-D12 starting point is one full run of the unmodified upstream recipe at the pinned commit, scored by parsing the CORE line from the training log in the paper's GPU environment (Section 3.1, Appendix F). This produces 0.1618 CORE, which becomes both the baseline and the fixed search starting point for the 38.7% improvement. Calibration is append-only: `python -m multi_agent_nc.calibrate_baseline --score 0.1618` writes the score and refuses to overwrite an already-populated log, preventing a stale denominator from silently persisting if hardware changes.

The calibration is necessary because CORE depends on the runtime environment in ways that the public upstream number does not capture: different GPU architectures, different CUDA versions, and different offline-mode settings all affect the absolute score. By calibrating once at search start, the paper ensures that the 38.7% improvement is measured against what the starting recipe actually achieves in the search environment, making the relative gain meaningful.

**CIFAR-10 Airbench96 calibration: cold-process protocol.**

The CIFAR starting point is a ten-seed cold-process aggregate of the unmodified Airbench96 recipe under the strict 0.96 accuracy gate (Appendix F). The cold-process protocol re-imports the recipe in a fresh Python process per seed so transient compile state (JIT compilation, CUDA context warmup) does not bias the wallclock measurement. Without this protocol, the first seed might run slower (CUDA cold start) and later seeds faster (warm cache), producing an unreliable mean.

The calibrated starting point is 26.3560 seconds. The upstream reported reference of 27.3000 seconds is the same recipe under a different reporting protocol—not an agent result and not the denominator for the 4.59% improvement. The paper is explicit about this: "agent improvement is computed from the calibrated start" (Section 4.1). The cold-process protocol is also the timing source for all submitted trials in the search trajectory, so the measurement matches the baseline.

**Measurement hardening: the evaluator owns the metric.**

Across all three environments, the paper enforces that the editable recipe does not control the measurement:

- **Parameter Golf:** uses the official evaluation path—the task harness measures bpb, not the submitted code.
- **NanoChat-D12:** uses a protected parser that extracts CORE from the training log. The paper's evaluator-touch audit reports zero edits to sensitive evaluator or parser paths.
- **CIFAR-10 Airbench96:** uses shell-side timing (the run script writes timing sidecars, the classifier reads them) and a strict accuracy gate. The recipe cannot report its own time—editing the code to print a faster time would not change the shell-side measurement.

The paper also audits CIFAR accuracy-gate enforcement: if a recipe reports its own accuracy but the external classifier disagrees, the trial is disqualified. This prevents reward hacking such as lowering the internal validation threshold to claim higher accuracy than the official evaluation achieves.

**Evaluator-touch audit: verifying the boundary (Appendix I).**

The paper performs a conservative audit of all submitted trials to verify that no specialist ever touched an evaluator or parser path. The audit is environment-specific:

- **NanoChat-D12:** visible edits touch model, training, data, optimizer, and experiment files, with zero edits to sensitive evaluator or parser paths. This is checked by examining the edit-stream paths, not just the final recipes.
- **CIFAR-10 Airbench96:** all visible edits touch `airbench96.py`, with zero edits to the timing classifier or result parser.
- **Parameter Golf:** uses an older event schema, so the evaluator-touch audit relies on archived snapshot inspection rather than edit-stream paths alone.

The audit supports measurement hardening as a design choice, not as an ablated mechanism. The paper is demonstrating that the loop works *when* the evaluator owns the measurement—it does not claim that agents would be honest if given the opportunity to self-report. By building the measurement boundary into the harness (protected parsers, shell-side timing, external evaluation paths), the paper ensures that the reported improvements reflect real recipe changes, not reward hacking.

#### Tool Catalogue and Session Mechanics (Appendices A, B, D)

**Tools available to specialists (Table 4, Appendix B).**

Each specialist session is wired with a set of MCP tools plus the Anthropic SDK built-ins (Read, Edit, Bash, Grep, Glob, WebSearch, WebFetch, ToolSearch, TodoWrite, Agent). Write is deliberately excluded—the artifact packer ignores any file that is not the canonical recipe file, so sidecars created via Write would silently inflate the artifact toward the size cap. The task-specific tools are:

- **submit_trial(workdir, specialist, hypothesis, expected_delta, parent_exp):** The primary GPU-burning tool. Packages the current recipe, submits it to the GPU cluster, blocks until completion, and writes a row to `results.tsv`. This is the only tool that consumes GPU time.

- **syntax_check(workdir):** py_compile the editable file and report any SyntaxError without executing. Millisecond-scale; catches the most common edit mistake before a GPU trial.

- **size_project(workdir):** Run the real LZMA+base85 pack step locally and report the projected packed size against the 16 MB cap. Used before `submit_trial` to catch oversize edits without burning a job.

- **param_count(workdir):** Static AST estimate of trainable parameter count by summing `nn.Linear` and `nn.Embedding` literal sizes. Fast (~5 ms) but only catches gross structural changes.

- **read_snapshot(exp_id, optional path):** Fetch the snapshotted source of a past kept experiment to study what a sibling specialist actually wrote. Truncated to ~200 KB. Disabled under `MAGENT_NO_LINEAGE=1`.

- **rebase_to(exp_id, workdir):** Copy a past experiment's snapshotted source into the current workdir, overwriting whatever is there. Used to fork from a non-best parent. Preserved even in no-lineage mode because the agent needs a usable code state.

- **diff_snapshots(exp_a, exp_b):** Unified diff of two past experiments' snapshotted source. Truncated to ~300 lines / 8 KB. Disabled under `MAGENT_NO_LINEAGE=1`.

- **read_pr_library(pr_number):** Fetch entry N from the curated PR library: technique, specialist tag, risk tag, available file paths. A cross-reference for web-found ideas, not the primary research source.

- **read_pr_source(pr_number, path):** Return the extracted source text from one file inside a PR library entry.

Additionally, web search (`WebSearch`, `WebFetch`) is enabled as the primary research channel for non-trivial design questions, and the local PR library serves as supplemental cross-reference. The paper reports (Appendix G) that tool-use patterns vary by task: validation calls (`syntax_check`, `size_project`) are frequent in Parameter Golf (2.39–2.96 calls per trial), web search is most common in NanoChat-D12 optimization, and web/validation use is comparatively uniform in CIFAR.

**Safety hooks on the tool channel (Appendix B).**

Three SDK hooks moderate the tool channel to prevent common failure modes:

1. **block_bash_writes (PreToolUse, on Bash):** Denies destructive shell verbs (rm, mv, cp, in-place sed, tar -c, file redirects, package install, process control). Bash is effectively read-only in the swarm—specialists can read logs and configuration but cannot modify files through the shell.

2. **block_bash_blackboard (PreToolUse, on Bash):** When `MAGENT_NO_LINEAGE=1`, denies reads of blackboard files (tree.tsv, results.tsv, lineage_snapshots/, events.jsonl, best.json, supervisor_audit.jsonl, anything under blackboard/). This closes the residual information leak: an empirical audit of the lineage-on Parameter Golf run found that 57.9% of Bash calls with parseable arguments targeted blackboard files (mainly awk slices of tree.tsv), so without this hook, the prompt-side lineage ablation would leak heavily through shell commands.

3. **cap_builtin_tool_output (PostToolUse, on Bash / Grep / WebFetch / WebSearch):** Truncates oversized outputs at 16 KB with a recovery marker, bounding cache growth without breaking legitimate slicing.

**Session defaults and constraints (Appendix D).**

Each specialist session is configured with:

- **thinking_budget:** 8,000 tokens of extended thinking budget. This is the Anthropic SDK's extended thinking mechanism, giving the model space to reason before acting.
- **max_turns:** 200 per session (cap on tool-use turns). Sized for multi-submit sessions with library drill-down—a specialist can read the PR library, search the web, edit code, validate, submit, read the outcome, and submit a second trial without exhausting the cap.
- **enable_web:** True (WebSearch / WebFetch are forced into the SDK's preload list).
- **Sandbox:** bubblewrap-based with Bash read-anywhere / write-only-to-cwd. On hosts where pivot-root is unavailable (LXC, nested containers), the sandbox is auto-disabled and the block_bash_writes hook becomes the primary write barrier.
- **Permission mode:** bypassPermissions (autonomous; the allowed-tools list is the safety boundary, not interactive prompting).

**Model routing (Appendix D.2).**

All reported frozen runs use Claude Opus 4.7 for every role. The framework supports per-role model routing (different roles could use different models with different reasoning depths), but this capability is not exercised in the reported experiments. The only per-role difference is GPU-cluster priority (Table 6): all Parameter Golf roles run at priority 10; NanoChat-D12 systems and schedule run at priority 9 (slightly lower) while architecture, optimization, and data run at 10; CIFAR loss and regularization run at priority 9 while architecture, optimization, and augmentation run at 10. These priority differences reflect the expected importance of each role's proposals for the task's dominant constraint, but they are minor and the paper does not claim they affect results.

**Termination rules (Appendix D.4).**

The supervisor stops the search on the OR of two conditions:

1. **Wall-clock deadline:** default 48 hours, configurable via `--deadline-hours`. This caps the total search time regardless of performance.

2. **No-improvement grace:** default 4 hours since the most recent keep, configurable via `--no-improvement-hours`. The grace clock resets every time a new keep lands. This prevents the system from running indefinitely when the search has plateaued.

When either condition fires, a `stop.flag` file is written under `blackboard/`, in-flight specialist coroutines exit at their next `should_stop()` check (with a 60-second grace before forced cancellation), and the supervisor triggers a best-effort stop sweep over registered GPU-cluster jobs. SIGINT and SIGTERM trigger the same shutdown chain. This termination logic is important because it defines the search horizon—the system runs until either time expires or progress stalls, and the resulting trace reflects whatever the loop discovered within that window.

**Anti-anchoring and crash feedback in prompts (Appendix A.3).**

Two prompt mechanisms prevent the proposal stream from collapsing toward a small set of high-salience but ineffective edits:

1. **Anti-anchoring banlist:** Each specialist receives a short banlist of patterns that earlier sessions in its own role have already tried and that failed or returned within noise. The banlist is rendered alongside the lineage slice so the same dead end is less likely to be re-explored. This targets a known failure mode in language agents: repeatedly returning to the same "obvious" edit that doesn't actually work.

2. **Crash feedback:** When a submitted trial crashes, the lineage slice for the next trial carries the deepest exception line and the deepest training-script frame from the crash (not just a generic "crashed" status). This gives the next specialist an explicit failure mode—e.g., "IndexError: list index out of range at train_gpt.py:847 in forward()"—rather than a vague signal, making surface variants of the same failed proposal less likely.

**Per-iteration user message structure (Figure 8, Appendix A.4).**

The per-iteration user message is rendered fresh from the live blackboard at every session start. Its structure is:

1. **Session header:** timestamp, specialist role, and workdir path.
2. **Current best:** one-liner with experiment ID, score, and task-specific metric.
3. **LEADERBOARD.md:** compact bullet list of top-N keep rows by score.
4. **KNOWLEDGE.md:** curated tree of prior hypotheses and outcomes in an agent-readable summary format.
5. **Recent Activity:** the most recent ten rows of `results.tsv` with role, status, score, delta, and hypothesis. This is the primary cross-role feedback channel.
6. **Saturation signal (conditional):** emitted only when the last five trials from the same role all have absolute delta less than $5 \times 10^{-4}$.
7. **Workdir state:** file listing and a head excerpt of the current recipe file.
8. **Task instruction:** a summary of the specialist's objective for this session.

Under the no-lineage ablation (Figure 9), sections 3–6 are dropped and replaced with an explicit notice that within-run prior-trial logs are unavailable. The current-best score one-liner and the workdir state are preserved, as is the specialist's domain preamble and knowledge files (which are static setup priors). The specialist is instructed to "propose from your static setup priors in the system prompt, the current-best score above, your workdir state, and your own in-session reasoning." This tests whether the diversity and effectiveness of the role swarm derive from the dynamic lineage feedback or from the static role partitioning and prompting alone.

#### Summary of Design Choices and Their Justifications

- **Three environments with complementary constraints** rather than one benchmark: tests whether the same loop architecture works under size+time pressure (Parameter Golf), wallclock headroom (NanoChat), and accuracy-gated speed (CIFAR). A single environment would not distinguish between the loop's general mechanisms and environment-specific artifacts.

- **Specialist role partitioning** over a single generalist or generic multi-agent: increases proposal diversity (134.8 vs. 41.1 effective clusters) and boundary discipline (fewer preflight crashes, less concentration in the largest cluster) by scoping each specialist to a non-overlapping recipe surface.

- **Shared lineage with cross-role parent edges** over isolated specialists or a shared transcript: enables failures to propagate as actionable evidence across role boundaries (83.7% of parent edges cross roles), turning a systems diagnostic into a training-token proposal into a model refinement.

- **Evaluator-owned measurement** over self-reported scores: prevents reward hacking by ensuring that the editable recipe never controls the score, timing, or accuracy gate. The evaluator-touch audit verifies zero edits to evaluator or parser paths.

- **Append-only calibration** over using public reference numbers directly: ensures that relative improvements are measured against what the starting recipe achieves on the same hardware under the same protocol, making deltas meaningful when hardware differs.

- **Nine-status taxonomy** over binary pass/fail: converts continuous outcomes and hard constraints into discrete, interpretable states that preserve quantitative detail (exact byte excess, accuracy deficit) and enable targeted follow-up edits rather than blind retries.

- **The submitted trial as atomic unit** over longer multi-edit sessions: grounds every proposal in the current lineage and forces specialists to re-read the blackboard between trials, preventing stale internal state from accumulating across multiple edits.

- **Two-submit cap with evidence requirement** over unlimited submissions per session: prevents agents from chaining speculative edits without feedback by requiring that a second submit be motivated by a concrete signal from the first outcome.

- **Parallel submission with measured efficiency** over sequential single-specialist search: increases trial throughput from 2.26 trials/hour (single) to 18.15 trials/hour (10-specialist) at 80% parallel efficiency, making feedback propagate within the same search horizon.

- **Anti-anchoring banlist and crash feedback** in prompts: prevents the proposal stream from collapsing toward repeatedly trying the same high-salience edit or its surface variants, based on the observation that language agents are prone to this failure mode without explicit negative feedback.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Reframing Auto Research as an Auditable Trajectory Rather Than a Final Artifact

The paper's most fundamental conceptual move is **redefining what auto research output looks like.** Prior work on agent-driven ML experimentation—MLAgentBench (Huang et al., 2023), MLE-bench (Chan et al., 2024), RE-Bench (Wijk et al., 2025), MLGym-Bench (Nathani et al., 2025), The AI Scientist (Lu et al., 2024)—evaluates whether agents reach a target score, produce a model checkpoint, or generate a paper. The unit of success is the **endpoint**: did the agent beat a baseline? Did it write a credible report? Even systems like AlphaDev (Mankowitz et al., 2023) and FunSearch (Romera-Paredes et al., 2024), which do treat the search process as important, ultimately report the discovered algorithm or function as the output.

This paper makes a different bet: **the trajectory itself is the primary empirical contribution.** The abstract states this explicitly: "The output is not a generated paper or a single model checkpoint, but an auditable trajectory of proposals, code diffs, experiments, scores, and failure labels." This is not a minor framing tweak—it changes what counts as evidence. A system that stumbles into a good recipe through blind search and one that arrives at the same recipe through evidence-driven iteration produce identical endpoints but radically different trajectories. The paper's extensive trace analysis (parent-child edges, cross-role information sharing, proposal entropy, failure-to-follow-up chains in Table 3 and the case studies) would be supplementary noise in an endpoint-focused evaluation; here, it *is* the contribution.

This reframing matters for three reasons:

**First, it makes the research process inspectable.** A human reviewing the NanoChat-D12 trace can see that trial 007 (systems specialist) diagnosed an attention-backend bottleneck, exposed runtime slack, and that later trials 020, 024, and 025 (different specialists) spent that slack on more training tokens—a cross-role research narrative that a final recipe alone would obscure. This auditability is a genuine advance over prior agent-ML systems where the intermediate steps are typically discarded or summarized into a narrative post-hoc (as in The AI Scientist's generated papers).

**Second, it converts failures from waste into evidence.** In an endpoint-focused evaluation, a size-blocked trial or an accuracy-gate miss is a failure—it didn't beat the baseline. In this paper's trajectory-focused framing, trial 587 (Parameter Golf: useful z-loss, 2,056 bytes over cap) and trial 060 (CIFAR: 25.1650 s at 0.95956 accuracy, misses gate by 0.00044) are **research signals** because they carry quantitative boundary information that later trials exploit. The nine-status taxonomy (Table 5) is not just bookkeeping—it's the interface between continuous measurement and actionable categories, and it's what makes the trajectory more than a sequence of win/loss indicators.

**Third, it sets a different standard for what "autonomous" means.** Prior work demonstrates autonomy in the sense of "no human edited the code or chose the hyperparameters." This paper adds: "no human chose proposals, edited recipes, overrode scores, or repaired failed trials during the search" (Section 1). But the deeper standard is that the *evidence flow* is autonomous—failures propagate into follow-up edits without human interpretation, because the lineage prompt carries enough structured detail (status, exact byte excess, crash excerpt) for the next specialist to act on. This is autonomy of *research reasoning*, not just autonomy of *code editing*.

**Significance and novelty assessment:** This is a **fundamental reframing** rather than an incremental improvement. It does not make prior endpoint-focused evaluations wrong, but it argues they are incomplete—measuring only the destination and not the journey. The reframing is conceptually portable: any future auto-research system could adopt trajectory-as-output as its evaluation standard, and the paper's trace format (hypothesis + diff + score + status + parent edges) provides a template. The limitation, which the paper acknowledges, is that trajectory analysis requires environments where failures return structured, quantitative feedback—the approach does not directly transfer to domains where "failure" is subjective or the measurement boundary is fuzzy.

---

### Innovation 2: Lineage Feedback as Active Research State—Not Just a Log

The paper's central empirical finding is that **shared lineage acts as active research state that maintains the frontier, prevents re-exploration of dead ends, and carries dynamic constraint knowledge across specialist sessions.** This is a stronger claim than "logging is useful" or "agents benefit from history." It's a claim about what kind of information the loop needs to sustain evidence-driven search, and the lineage ablation (Section 4.3, Figure 3 Panel B) is the key piece of evidence.

**What the field did before this finding.** Prior multi-agent and iterative agent systems typically provide agents with some form of memory—a transcript, a best-so-far score, or a task description that accumulates results. MLAgentBench gives agents access to prior experiment outputs. SWE-agent (Yang et al., 2024) maintains a conversation history with the environment. Population-based training (Jaderberg et al., 2017) shares model weights across a population but does not share hypotheses, failure reasons, or constraint boundary information. The implicit assumption in most of these systems is that **the best-so-far state is sufficient context** for the next proposal—agents just need to know the frontier score and can propose from their own priors.

This paper's lineage ablation directly tests that assumption and **rejects it.** With lineage, the role swarm finds 16 valid improvements in 200 trials and reaches 1.073142 bpb by trial 176, with 15 active parent heads and 8 of 10 specialists contributing at least one keep. Without lineage—same specialist split, same starting recipe, same budget, same current-best score line, but no record of prior trials' hypotheses, scores, statuses, or failures—the swarm finds 3 improvements, reaches 1.077413 bpb by trial 75, and then runs 125 additional trials without a new keep. The parent tree collapses to 3 active heads, and contributing specialists drop from 8 to 2.

**Why this is more than "memory helps."** The specific failure mode in the no-lineage run reveals what lineage actually provides. The no-lineage swarm hits the eval-budget cap on 61.5% of trials versus 19.0% with lineage. The explanation is not that agents forgot which edits improve bpb—the current-best score one-liner tells them that. The explanation is that agents forgot that **the current-best recipe already sits close to the 600-second evaluation cap**, a dynamic fact that only the Recent Activity block carries. Without lineage, each specialist independently rediscovers the same budget boundary, proposes edits that push runtime over the cap, and gets rejected. Lineage carries not just "what worked" but "what boundaries are currently binding"—a qualitatively different kind of information.

The paper's finding about parent edges reinforces this. With lineage, 83.7% of parent edges cross role boundaries (Table 3), meaning specialists are building on each other's measured outcomes. Without lineage, cross-role information sharing collapses because there's no channel for a systems specialist to see that an architecture specialist exposed a runtime bottleneck. The lineage is not just an external memory—it's the **mechanism that makes the multi-role search a single connected process rather than parallel independent searches that happen to share a GPU cluster.**

**Connection to broader concepts.** This finding connects to a long-standing question in AI research: what does an agent need to remember to sustain open-ended search? In evolutionary computation, the population maintains diversity through selection pressure and genetic operators—the population *is* the memory. In reinforcement learning, the replay buffer stores transitions. In Bayesian optimization, the surrogate model accumulates function evaluations. In each case, the system has a **structured representation of the search history** that is actively used to guide proposals, not passively recorded for later inspection. This paper shows that for language-agent-driven recipe search, a compact structured lineage—role, hypothesis, status, score delta, parent ID, crash excerpt—serves this function, and that removing it does not just slow progress but qualitatively changes the search from evidence-driven to boundary-blind.

**Significance and novelty assessment:** This is a **diagnostic contribution** rather than a method contribution. The paper does not invent a new lineage representation—the lineage format (TSV rows with hypothesis + status + score + delta + timing) is simple and could be arrived at by any practitioner. The contribution is the **controlled experiment** showing that this specific information, removed under a matched budget, causes a specific qualitative failure (repeated eval-budget violations, collapsed parent tree, plateau without recovery). This is the kind of finding that changes how future auto-research systems are designed: it suggests that simply showing agents the best-so-far score is insufficient, and that dynamic constraint boundary information must propagate through the feedback channel. The finding is strong internally but limited by being demonstrated on a single task (Parameter Golf) with a specific lineage format—whether the same failure mode generalizes to other tasks and other lineage representations is open.

### Innovation 3: Specialist Role Partitioning as a Diversity Mechanism—Not Just a Division of Labor

The paper's use of specialist agents with non-overlapping recipe surfaces is superficially a division-of-labor strategy—different agents edit different parts of the code, avoiding conflicts. But the paper's proposal-entropy analysis (Table 3) shows that **role partitioning is primarily a diversity mechanism** that prevents the proposal stream from collapsing toward the same high-salience edits, and that this effect is distinct from the throughput gains of parallel submission.

**The baseline comparison that isolates this.** The paper runs a generic 10-agent control: ten identical agents with the same prompt, same tools, same lineage access, and same parallel submission infrastructure—just no role-specific domain preambles. The generic run achieves similar throughput (16.79 vs. 18.15 trials/hour) but dramatically lower proposal diversity: 41.1 effective clusters versus 134.8 for the role swarm, with 12.0% of submissions in the largest cluster versus 3.5%. The throughput similarity rules out the explanation that role partitioning just enables parallelism—the effect is on **what ideas are proposed**, not how many trials are run.

**Why this is not obvious.** The default assumption in multi-agent systems is that multiple agents with identical capabilities will naturally explore different parts of the solution space—stochasticity in LLM sampling, different initial conditions, or different orderings of received information will create diversity. This assumption is partly borne out in the generic control: the generic agents do show some cross-agent parent edges (79.1%) and do explore multiple clusters—they are not all proposing identical edits. But the diversity is far lower than with role partitioning. The paper's explanation—confirmed by qualitative inspection of the generic run's largest cluster—is that identical agents gravitate toward the most salient edit surface (in Parameter Golf, this appears to be polar-coefficient edits, which produced 32 preflight crashes in the single-generalist control). Role partitioning counteracts this by giving each specialist a non-overlapping scope and explicit non-scope constraints, forcing attention to surfaces that would otherwise be neglected.

This connects to a broader finding in the agent-ML literature: MLGym-Bench (Nathani et al., 2025) found that agent-driven ML gains often come from hyperparameter tuning rather than novel hypotheses, algorithms, or architectures. Without role partitioning, agents default to editing the most visible knobs—learning rate, weight decay, schedule parameters—because these are the most salient and have the clearest relationship to the metric. Role partitioning is a **prompt-level intervention** that forces agents to propose structural changes (the architecture specialist's preamble says "a single scalar tweak to an existing module is hparam noise, not architecture—pivot to a qualitative edit") by assigning the scalar-tuning surface to a dedicated role (meta-search) and giving structural roles explicit edit-radius guidance that discourages numeric knob-twiddling.

**Distinguishing role partitioning from generic multi-agent diversity mechanisms.** Population-based methods (Jaderberg et al., 2017) maintain diversity through selection pressure and mutation. Multi-agent debate systems (Du et al., 2023) maintain diversity through adversarial prompts. Ensemble methods maintain diversity through different initializations or training subsets. Role partitioning is different: it maintains diversity through **semantic constraints on the proposal space**—each specialist is told "you own this surface, you do not own that surface, and your edits should cross a qualitative line." The diversity comes not from stochasticity or competition but from a **designed decomposition of the problem structure** that maps onto the recipe's architecture.

**The cross-task pattern reinforces this interpretation.** The specialist outcome profiles (Figure 11, Table 7) show that different roles dominate different tasks: systems led NanoChat-D12 with 3 keeps, optimization led CIFAR with 2 keeps, and all 10 roles contributed in Parameter Golf. This is not a confound—it's evidence that the role decomposition is correctly aligned with task constraints. NanoChat-D12's dominant constraint is wallclock headroom, so the systems specialist (who controls kernel dispatch and runtime efficiency) naturally produces the most improvements. CIFAR's dominant constraint is the accuracy gate under shortened schedules, so optimization and augmentation (who control convergence speed and regularization) are most effective. If all roles contributed equally across all tasks, that would suggest the role decomposition is arbitrary and the diversity gains come from parallelism alone. The fact that effectiveness varies by task constraint suggests the role decomposition is capturing something real about the problem structure.

**Significance and novelty assessment:** This is a **design contribution with empirical backing** rather than a theoretical advance. The concept of specialist roles is not new—multi-agent systems have used role-based decomposition for decades. What is new is the **controlled comparison** (role swarm vs. generic-10 at matched throughput) that isolates the effect of role partitioning on proposal diversity, and the proposal-entropy measurement framework that quantifies this effect. The finding is practically significant: it provides a recipe for designing auto-research swarms (partition the surface by task constraints, give each role scoping and non-scoping prompts, balance allocation across roles) and a diagnostic (effective cluster count, largest cluster share) for measuring whether the partitioning is working. The limitation is that the role taxonomy is hand-designed per task—the paper does not provide a method for automatically discovering the right decomposition for a new environment, and it's possible that the reported role splits are overfitted to the three tasks studied.

### Innovation 4: Constraint Failures as the Primary Research Signal—Not Just Noise

The paper demonstrates that **boundary failures—trials that violate hard constraints but return quantified feedback—are the mechanism through which the loop learns and adapts**, and that this is a deliberate design choice in environment selection rather than an incidental property of training recipes. This inverts the standard ML experimentation paradigm where failures are discarded and only improvements are celebrated.

**The standard paradigm and why it's insufficient for closed-loop research.** In most ML experimentation workflows (human or automated), a failed trial—one that crashes, exceeds a budget, or misses a quality threshold—is treated as negative signal at best ("don't do that again") and noise at worst ("the cluster was busy, rerun"). Hyperparameter optimization systems like Hyperband (Li et al., 2018) use early-stopping to *avoid* running trials that will fail, treating the failure boundary as something to circumvent rather than something to map. Agent-ML systems like MLAgentBench typically report success rates and final scores, treating failed runs as unsuccessful attempts rather than as data.

This paper's environments are **selected for their failure modes**, not despite them. The Parameter Golf artifact size cap of 16 MB is not an arbitrary limit to make the task harder—it's chosen because it produces **size-blocked trials** that return both a measured score (the idea works for quality) and an exact byte excess (the implementation needs N bytes of headroom recovery). The CIFAR-10 accuracy gate of 0.96 is not just a pass/fail filter—it returns the measured accuracy, so a trial at 0.95956 carries the information that the speed recipe is 0.00044 accuracy points short, guiding a targeted warmup repair rather than a blind retry. The NanoChat-D12 wallclock budget is not a hard deadline—it's a budget that makes runtime efficiency legible as headroom, which the systems specialist can expose and later specialists can spend on more training tokens.

**The case studies as evidence that failure-to-follow-up is the core capability.** Each of the paper's three case studies (Section 5) follows the same pattern: a trial fails a constraint → the failure returns measured feedback → a later trial (often by a different specialist) uses that feedback to make a targeted repair → the repair either succeeds (CIFAR warmup fix, Parameter Golf artifact-headroom recovery) or becomes the foundation for further refinement (NanoChat runtime slack → more tokens → higher CORE). The Parameter Golf case is particularly clean: trial 587 proposed TTT-only z-loss, achieved a useful 1.072431 bpb, but exceeded the artifact cap by 2,056 bytes. Trial 596 retained the z-loss mechanism and recovered byte headroom, producing a legal keep at 1.072251. Without the size-block feedback (exact byte excess, measured score), trial 596 would have no target for its headroom recovery and the useful z-loss idea might have been abandoned.

This pattern is not incidental to the loop's success—**it is the success.** The headline runs produce 45 total valid improvements across 1,197 trials (Table 1), meaning the vast majority of trials (592 valid non-improvements plus hundreds of ineligible trials) are "failures" by the standard endpoint metric. But these failures are not wasted compute—they map the constraint boundaries, expose runtime bottlenecks, and quantify the accuracy-speed frontier. The paper's proposal-entropy and cross-role parent-edge analyses (Table 3) are measuring how effectively failures propagate as evidence, not just how many successes the loop achieves.

**Connection to broader research paradigms.** This framing connects to two established ideas. The first is **exploration-exploitation** in reinforcement learning and bandit problems, where the value of a suboptimal action is the information it provides about the reward landscape. A size-blocked trial is the exploration that reveals the artifact boundary, and the follow-up headroom recovery is the exploitation of that information. The second is **constraint-driven design** in engineering, where the most informative experiments are often the ones that push against a constraint to find where it actually binds. The paper's contribution is to design an automated system that treats constraint violations as primary research signals rather than as errors to avoid, and to demonstrate that this requires specific infrastructure (quantitative failure feedback, cross-role lineage, a status taxonomy that preserves boundary information) that is absent from standard ML experimentation pipelines.

**Significance and novelty assessment:** This is a **design philosophy contribution** with empirical validation. The idea that failures are informative is not new—human researchers routinely use failed experiments to debug hypotheses. What is new is the **systematic instantiation** of this philosophy in an automated loop: the choice of environments with hard, quantified constraints; the status taxonomy that preserves failure details; the lineage mechanism that carries failures across roles; and the controlled experiments showing that removing lineage causes repeated constraint collisions (61.5% eval-budget hits without lineage vs. 19.0% with lineage). This is a contribution to *how to build auto-research systems*, not to *what auto-research systems can achieve*, and it suggests a design principle that is likely to transfer to any domain where constraints produce rich, quantified failure signals. The limitation is that the principle requires constraints that are both hard (objectively verifiable) and quantified (returning exact deficits)—it is unclear how to apply it to domains where failure is subjective or the measurement boundary is fuzzy.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

**Dataset.** The paper uses three distinct tasks, each with its own data and scoring infrastructure, rather than a single dataset. Parameter Golf uses a fixed FineWeb-derived language modeling task [Penedo et al., 2024] with the public leaderboard score of 1.0810 validation bits-per-byte as the starting point. NanoChat-D12 uses the nanochat pretraining pipeline [Karpathy, 2025] with the CORE evaluation metric from DataComp-LM [Li et al., 2024]; the starting point is a calibrated run of the unmodified upstream recipe producing 0.1618 CORE on the paper's GPU environment. CIFAR-10 Airbench96 uses the standard CIFAR-10 image classification dataset [Krizhevsky, 2009] under the Airbench96 speed benchmark protocol [Jordan, 2024]; the starting point is a calibrated ten-seed cold-process aggregate of the unmodified recipe at 26.3560 seconds. All starting recipes are fixed before search and are not modified by human intervention during the reported trajectories.

**Base model(s).** The language agent driving all proposals is **Claude Opus 4.7**, used for every specialist role across all three headline runs and all Parameter Golf control runs (Appendix D.2). The paper does not ablate the choice of language model—all results are conditional on this specific model version. The framework supports per-role model routing but this capability is not exercised in the reported experiments. The training recipes themselves use the architectures specified by each task (a transformer body for Parameter Golf, a 12-layer transformer for NanoChat-D12, a fast ConvNet for CIFAR-10 Airbench96), but these are the *targets* of the agent's edits, not the base models for agent reasoning.

**Metrics.** Each environment provides its own externally measured metric: **validation bits-per-byte (bpb)** for Parameter Golf (lower is better, measured by the official evaluation path), **CORE** for NanoChat-D12 (higher is better, parsed from the training log by a protected parser), and **wallclock training time** for CIFAR-10 Airbench96 (lower is better, measured by shell-side timing, but only valid when mean accuracy across ten cold-process seeds reaches at least 0.96). Relative improvements are computed against the calibrated starting point for each environment (1.0810 bpb for PG, 0.1618 CORE for NC, 26.3560 s for CIFAR), not against external reference numbers. The primary metric for the loop itself is the **trajectory**—the sequence of submitted trials with hypotheses, code edits, scores, statuses, and parent-child relationships—not just the final score.

**Baselines.** The paper's experiments are structured around several internal comparisons rather than external baselines from prior work:

- **Role swarm with lineage (headline):** Ten specialists for Parameter Golf, five each for NanoChat-D12 and CIFAR-10 Airbench96, with role-specific domain preambles and full shared lineage access. This is the primary experimental condition.
- **Single generalist:** One agent with no role partitioning, using a generic prompt that covers the full recipe surface. Used as a Parameter Golf control in the matched 200-trial window.
- **Generic 10-agent:** Ten identical agents with the same generic prompt, same tools, and shared lineage, but no role-specific domain preambles. Used as a Parameter Golf control to isolate the effect of role partitioning from parallelism.
- **No-lineage ablation:** The same ten-specialist swarm with lineage feedback channels removed (no LEADERBOARD.md, KNOWLEDGE.md, or Recent Activity sections in prompts; read_snapshot and diff_snapshots tools disabled; Bash reads of blackboard files blocked). Used as a Parameter Golf control to isolate the effect of lineage feedback.

The paper does not compare against prior agent-ML systems (MLAgentBench, MLE-bench, RE-Bench, etc.) because those systems operate on different tasks and the paper's contribution is not a new agent architecture but a specific feedback loop instantiation. The paper also does not compare against classical HPO methods (Bayesian optimization, Hyperband) because those methods assume fixed search spaces rather than open-ended program-level edits. The starting recipe in each environment serves as the implicit baseline—the paper measures whether the loop improves over the fixed starting point without human intervention.

**Generation budget / compute accounting.** The unit of budget is the **submitted trial:** one proposal, code edit, GPU-cluster job execution, and measured outcome. The three headline runs contain 1,197 submitted trials: 900 in Parameter Golf, 200 in NanoChat-D12, and 97 in CIFAR-10 Airbench96. Three additional Parameter Golf control runs (single generalist, generic-10, no-lineage) add 600 independent trials for a total of 1,500 Parameter Golf trials across all conditions. Compute is bounded per trial by the environment constraints: Parameter Golf trials have at most 600 seconds of training plus 600 seconds of evaluation on 8 H100s; NanoChat-D12 trials have a 90-minute wallclock cap on 8 H100s; CIFAR-10 Airbench96 trials run on a single long-lived GPU worker with under 30 seconds per cold-process seed. The paper provides upper-bound active accelerator time estimates: at most 4,000 H100-hours for the 1,500 Parameter Golf trials, 2,400 H100-hours for the 200 NanoChat-D12 trials, and under 10 single-GPU-hours for the 97 CIFAR-10 Airbench96 trials (Appendix E). These bounds include all failed trials; preflight failures that terminate before a worker launches consume less than the cap. Additional compute was used for harness development, calibration, and historical audit traces outside the reported frozen search loops and is not counted in headline or control totals.

**Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not use standard cross-validation or statistical significance testing. Instead, the primary statistical protocol is the **matched-budget controlled comparison:** for Parameter Golf, the role swarm, generic-10, single generalist, and no-lineage conditions are all run for exactly 200 trials from the identical starting recipe under the same harness vintage, with the same hardware, and with the same total GPU budget, isolating the effect of the manipulated variable (role partitioning or lineage access). The full role-swarm headline run extends to 900 trials to study longer-horizon search behavior. The matched 200-trial window is the primary comparison point because it holds trial count constant across conditions. The paper does not report confidence intervals on the final scores or on the proposal-entropy measurements, which is a limitation for interpreting whether the observed differences (e.g., 1.073142 vs. 1.077413 bpb) are statistically robust or within the noise of the evaluation metric.

The proposal-entropy analysis (Table 3) uses two historical 91-trial traces for audit purposes only, explicitly distinguishing them from the modern-harness controls. The paper reports that these traces predate anti-anchoring prompt revisions and are retained only for proposal-diversity comparison, not as causal controls. All score comparisons within a task use the same evaluator and measurement protocol, ensuring that deltas are measured under identical conditions.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Parameter Golf: Validation bpb Reduction Under Artifact and Time Caps

**Headline result.** The full 900-trial role-swarm run reduces validation bpb from the public starting point of 1.0810 to a final best of **1.072210**, a **0.81% reduction** (Table 1). At the matched 200-trial control window, the role swarm reaches **1.073142** with 16 valid improvements (keeps).

**Comparison across agent organizations at matched 200 trials (Figure 3, Panel A; Table 1).** The role-decomposed swarm achieves the best score (1.073142, 16 keeps), followed by the generic 10-agent control (1.074495, 10 keeps), followed by the single generalist (1.075384, 14 keeps). The score ordering (role swarm > generic-10 > single generalist) is consistent with the hypothesis that role partitioning improves search effectiveness, but the single generalist finding 14 keeps (more than the generic-10's 10 keeps) is a non-obvious result. The paper explains this through proposal concentration: the single generalist produces 61.9 effective clusters and a 17.5% largest-cluster share, with 32 preflight crashes around polar-coefficient edits, suggesting that the extra keeps come from repeatedly trying high-salience edits that sometimes succeed but often fail at the syntax or size boundary. The role swarm produces 134.8 effective clusters and a 3.5% largest-cluster share with far fewer repeated preflight crashes, indicating that the keeps are spread across more diverse, better-disciplined proposals.

**Effect of lineage feedback (Figure 3, Panel B; Table 1).** The sharpest control is the lineage ablation. With lineage, the role swarm reaches 1.073142 at trial 176 with 16 keeps. Without lineage—same specialist split, same starting recipe, same 200-trial budget, same current-best score line—the swarm reaches only 1.077413 at trial 75 with 3 keeps, then runs 125 additional trials without a new valid improvement. The eval-budget cap is hit on 19.0% of trials with lineage versus **61.5% without lineage**. The parent tree collapses from 15 active heads to 3, and contributing specialists fall from 8 of 10 to 2 of 10. These are not small differences—they represent a qualitative collapse of the search from evidence-driven to boundary-blind.

**Scaling beyond 200 trials (Table 1, Figure 2, Panel A).** The full 900-trial run reaches 1.072210, continuing to improve beyond the 200-trial window (where the best was 1.073142). The best-so-far curve in Figure 2A shows a rapid initial improvement phase (first ~200 trials), followed by a slower refinement phase with occasional small drops (trials 200–750), and a final plateau. The curve does not show signs of exhaustion—the last keep occurs around trial 750, suggesting the loop could continue finding improvements with a longer horizon. However, the paper does not report whether the rate of improvement per trial declines (the curve appears to flatten but without formal measurement).

**Valid improvements across the full run (Table 1).** The 900-trial role swarm produces 36 total valid improvements. The remaining 864 trials are valid non-improvements, ineligible trials, or crashes. The paper does not report the breakdown between these categories for the full 900-trial run, but the status taxonomy (Table 5) defines the possible outcomes, and the case studies focus on specific failure-to-follow-up chains rather than aggregate failure statistics. In the 200-trial matched controls, the valid improvement counts range from 3 (no-lineage) to 16 (role swarm), with the generic-10 finding 10 and the single generalist finding 14.

#### NanoChat-D12: CORE Improvement Under Fixed Wallclock

**Headline result.** The 200-trial role-swarm run improves CORE from the calibrated upstream start of 0.1618 to a final best of **0.2244**, a **38.7% increase** (Table 1). The run produces 5 valid improvements.

**Search trajectory (Figure 2, Panel B; Table 2; Section 5 case study).** The best-so-far curve shows a distinctive step-function pattern rather than gradual improvement. The first keep occurs at trial 007 (systems specialist: attention-path rewrite from SSSL to L, moving all 12 layers onto Flash SDPA), raising CORE to 0.1695. The largest single jump occurs at trial 020 (+0.0334 CORE, expanding the training-token budget using recovered wallclock). Trials 024 and 025 refine the data ratio and schedule to reach a plateau at 0.2241. Trial 156 (architecture specialist: zero-initialized logit-bias path) provides the final small improvement to 0.2244.

**Role contribution pattern (Figure 11, Panel B; Table 7).** The systems specialist produced 3 of the 5 valid improvements; schedule and architecture produced 1 each. Optimization, data, and loss specialists produced zero valid improvements but contributed valid non-improvements that mapped the performance landscape. This concentration is the opposite of Parameter Golf's broad contribution pattern—it reflects the fact that NanoChat-D12's dominant constraint is wallclock headroom, making the systems role (which controls kernel dispatch and runtime efficiency) the natural locus of improvement.

**Failure composition.** The paper does not report comprehensive failure statistics for NanoChat-D12, but notes that "NanoChat-D12 has few real crashes because preflight catches common failure classes before a full run starts" (Appendix I). The primary constraint is the 90-minute wallclock budget, which is a soft boundary (the run finishes when time expires) rather than a hard fail—trials that don't fit more tokens into the budget produce valid non-improvements rather than ineligible outcomes.

#### CIFAR-10 Airbench96: Accuracy-Gated Speed Optimization

**Headline result.** The 97-trial role-swarm run reduces training wallclock from the calibrated starting point of 26.3560 seconds to a final best of **25.1464 seconds**, a **4.59% reduction**, while satisfying the strict 0.96 accuracy gate on the final recipe (Table 1). The run produces 4 valid improvements.

**Search trajectory (Figure 2, Panel C; Section 5 case study).** The best-so-far curve shows a pattern of incremental speed improvements punctuated by gate enforcement. Trial 007 removes validation overhead (skipping most logging-only validation calls). Trials 008 and 030 shorten the training horizon. Trial 060 reaches 25.1650 seconds but misses the gate with 0.959560 accuracy—a near-miss that returns both the speed and the accuracy deficit. Trial 070 keeps the speed recipe (42 epochs, lr=11, shortened horizon) and changes only the warmup ratio from 10% to 5%, reaching 25.1464 seconds at 0.960080 accuracy—a valid keep that repairs the gate margin.

**Gate dominance (Figure 11, Panel C; Table 7).** The accuracy gate is the primary constraint shaping the CIFAR search: **81 of 97 trials (83.5%) were disqualified for missing the accuracy gate.** Optimization produced 2 of the 4 valid improvements; augmentation and regularization produced 1 each. Architecture and loss produced zero valid improvements but contributed the majority of gate-miss trials that mapped the speed-accuracy frontier.

**Gate-miss as research signal (Appendix I case details).** The trial 060 → 070 chain is the clearest example of gate-miss feedback driving a targeted repair. The measured accuracy deficit of 0.00044 (0.960000 − 0.959560) is small enough to suggest that a minor schedule adjustment could recover the margin without sacrificing speed. The successful repair changes only the warmup ratio, confirming that the gate miss correctly identified the binding constraint (convergence speed under the shortened schedule).

#### Cross-Task Patterns

**Improvement magnitudes and constraint types (Table 1).** The relative improvements differ substantially across tasks: 0.81% (Parameter Golf), 38.7% (NanoChat-D12), 4.59% (CIFAR-10 Airbench96). These magnitudes reflect the nature of the starting recipe and the constraint regime, not the capability of the loop. Parameter Golf starts from a publicly competitive recipe (1.0810 bpb) that has already been optimized by human researchers, so the 0.81% reduction represents incremental improvement on a strong baseline. NanoChat-D12 starts from an unoptimized upstream recipe (0.1618 CORE) with substantial headroom—the 38.7% gain is large because the starting point is far from the performance frontier. CIFAR-10 Airbench96 starts from a recipe already designed for speed (26.3560 s), so the 4.59% reduction comes from shaving off overhead and pushing the accuracy-speed boundary.

**Role contribution diversity (Figure 11; Table 7).** The three tasks show three different contribution patterns: Parameter Golf is **broad** (all 10 roles contribute valid improvements, with optimization, TTT, and quantization each contributing 5), NanoChat-D12 is **concentrated** (systems produces 3 of 5 keeps), and CIFAR-10 Airbench96 is **gate-dominated** (81 of 97 trials miss the accuracy gate, and only 4 improvements survive it). These patterns are not a confound—they are empirical evidence that the specialist decomposition is correctly aligned with each task's constraint regime, and that the same loop architecture produces task-appropriate search behavior rather than a uniform strategy.

**Proposal diversity across architectures (Table 3; Figure 3).** The proposal-entropy analysis is only reported for Parameter Golf, so cross-task comparison of proposal diversity is not available. This is a limitation: it is possible that NanoChat-D12 and CIFAR-10 Airbench96 would show different diversity characteristics (e.g., more concentration in NanoChat-D12 due to the systems bottleneck, more concentration in CIFAR due to gate-driven rejection). The paper's focus on Parameter Golf for the diversity analysis is pragmatic—it has the largest trial budget (900 trials) and the broadest role split (10 specialists)—but cross-task replication would strengthen the claim that role partitioning is a general diversity mechanism.

#### Proposal-Entropy and Information-Sharing Audit (Table 3)

**Headline findings (Table 3, full 900-trial role swarm).** The full Parameter Golf role swarm produces **439.6 effective proposal clusters** from 900 trials, with the largest cluster consuming only 2.2% of submissions and near-duplicates at 1.1%. Cross-context parent edges are 87.3% (781 of 895 within-window edges), and cross-context keeps are 97.0% (32 of 33 keeps with declarable within-window parents build on another role's row). These numbers quantify the core mechanism: the search is highly diverse (low concentration, many clusters), highly cross-pollinated (most parent edges cross roles), and the lineage mechanism successfully preserves cross-role parentage.

**Role swarm vs. generic 10-agent at matched 200 trials.** The role swarm produces 134.8 effective clusters vs. 41.1 for the generic control—a **3.28× diversity ratio** at similar throughput (18.15 vs. 16.79 trials/hour). The largest cluster share is 3.5% vs. 12.0%. Near-duplicate rates are similar (2.0% vs. 1.5%). The generic control still shows substantial cross-agent parent edges (79.1%), confirming that shared lineage alone creates some information sharing even without role partitioning. But the proposal stream is far more concentrated, with the largest cluster consuming 24 of 200 submissions—three times the role swarm's concentration.

**Role swarm vs. single generalist at matched 200 trials.** The single generalist produces 61.9 effective clusters (more than the generic-10's 41.1, less than the role swarm's 134.8) but with **17.5% in the largest cluster and 10.1% near-duplicates**—the worst concentration metrics of any condition. The paper attributes this to repeated attempts at high-salience edits (32 preflight crashes around polar-coefficient edits) that a single agent without role constraints repeatedly rediscovers.

**Role swarm vs. no-lineage at matched 200 trials.** The no-lineage condition produces 121.7 effective clusters—surprisingly comparable to the lineage-on condition's 134.8—but with dramatically fewer keeps (3 vs. 16), a collapsed parent tree (3 active heads vs. 15), and 61.5% eval-budget cap hits (vs. 19.0%). This reveals that **diversity alone is not sufficient**—the no-lineage swarm still explores many different ideas, but without feedback about which ideas are promisive and which constraint boundaries are binding, the exploration is unguided and repeatedly collides with the eval-budget cap. The cross-context keep rate collapses from 10/12 (83.3%) with lineage to 0/1 (0.0%) without lineage, confirming that lineage is the mechanism through which cross-role evidence propagates into successful proposals.

**Historical traces (Table 3, first two rows).** The historical 91-trial single-generalist trace (different harness vintage, predates anti-anchoring) shows 39.3 effective clusters and 10.0% near-duplicates—similar concentration to the modern single-generalist control (61.9 clusters, 10.1% near-duplicates), suggesting the concentration problem is a property of single-agent search rather than an artifact of harness vintage. The historical 91-trial specialist swarm shows 74.1 effective clusters and 0.0% near-duplicates—less diverse than the modern 200-trial swarm (134.8 clusters) but with qualitatively similar low-concentration characteristics. These historical traces are explicitly marked as audits only and are not used for causal claims.

#### Throughput and Parallel Efficiency (Equation 1)

**Measured throughput (Section 3.4).** Over the matched first-200-trial window on Parameter Golf with 600-second budgets and continuous wallclock only:

- Single generalist: **2.26 trials per hour**
- Ten-specialist role swarm: **18.15 trials per hour**, giving parallel efficiency $\eta_{\parallel,\text{PG}} \approx 0.80$ against the ideal 10× speedup (22.6 trials/hour)
- Ten generic agents: **16.79 trials per hour**, giving $\eta_{\parallel,\text{PG}} \approx 0.74$

**Interpretation.** The role swarm achieves 8.03× speedup over the single generalist at 80% parallel efficiency. The generic-10 achieves 7.43× speedup at 74% efficiency. The 6% efficiency difference (0.80 vs. 0.74) is small relative to the 3.28× diversity difference, confirming that the role-versus-generic effect is primarily about proposal diversity, not throughput. Efficiency is below 1.0 because submitters share the GPU pool, cluster queue, and blackboard filelock—these are real infrastructure constraints that Equation 1 captures.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Lineage feedback (Parameter Golf, 200 trials, Table 1, Figure 3 Panel B):** Removing shared lineage while preserving specialist roles, starting recipe, current-best score line, and trial budget causes a collapse from 16 valid improvements to 3, a final score degradation from 1.073142 to 1.077413 bpb, eval-budget cap hits rising from 19.0% to 61.5%, parent tree collapse from 15 to 3 active heads, and contributing specialists dropping from 8 to 2. This is the strongest causal evidence in the paper that lineage feedback—not just role partitioning or parallel submission—is necessary for the loop's effectiveness.

**Role partitioning (Parameter Golf, 200 trials, Table 1, Figure 3 Panel A, Table 3):** Replacing the ten specialist roles with ten identical generic agents (same tools, same lineage, same parallel infrastructure) reduces proposal diversity from 134.8 to 41.1 effective clusters, increases largest-cluster share from 3.5% to 12.0%, and degrades final score from 1.073142 to 1.074495 bpb with fewer valid improvements (10 vs. 16). Replacing with a single generalist further degrades score to 1.075384 bpb, with the worst concentration metrics (17.5% largest cluster, 10.1% near-duplicates, 32 preflight crashes around polar-coefficient edits). These results isolate the effect of role partitioning from parallelism: the 10-agent generic control has similar throughput (16.79 vs. 18.15 trials/hour) but dramatically lower proposal diversity and search effectiveness.

**Role partitioning + lineage interaction (Table 3, cross-context keep rates):** The role swarm with lineage has 10 of 12 keeps with within-window parents crossing role boundaries (83.3%). The generic-10 with lineage has 7 of 9 keeps crossing agent boundaries (77.8%) but far fewer keeps overall (9 vs. 12) and far lower proposal diversity. The no-lineage role swarm has 0 of 1 keeps crossing role boundaries (0.0%). This three-way comparison suggests that role partitioning and lineage are complementary: role partitioning increases the pool of diverse proposals that can become parents, and lineage carries those diverse proposals across role boundaries to produce keeps that build on different specialists' work. Removing either mechanism degrades the cross-role information flow.

**Single generalist vs. role swarm (Parameter Golf, first 91 trials, historical harness, Table 3):** The historical single generalist produces 3 valid drops and a best reduction of 0.00122 bpb, while the historical specialist swarm produces 7 valid drops and a best reduction of 0.00406 bpb in the same 91-trial window. This comparison changes multiple variables (role decomposition, number of concurrent proposal threads, harness vintage) and is explicitly marked as audit-only, not a causal control. The modern 200-trial matched controls are the primary evidence.

**Anti-anchoring banlist and crash feedback (qualitative, Appendix A.3):** The paper describes two prompt-level mechanisms that prevent proposal-stream collapse but does not ablate them. The anti-anchoring banlist prevents specialists from re-exploring patterns that earlier sessions in the same role have already tried and that failed or returned within noise. Crash feedback renders the deepest exception line into the next specialist's lineage slice, giving an explicit failure mode rather than a generic crash status. The single-generalist control's 32 polar-coefficient preflight crashes suggest what happens without these mechanisms: agents repeatedly return to high-salience edits that fail at the syntax or size boundary. However, no controlled experiment isolates the effect of banlist or crash feedback from the effect of role partitioning, so the causal attribution is suggestive rather than demonstrated.

**Evaluator-touch audit (Appendix I):** The paper verifies that no specialist ever touched an evaluator or parser path by examining edit-stream paths. In NanoChat-D12, visible edits touch model, training, data, optimizer, and experiment files, with zero edits to sensitive evaluator or parser paths. In CIFAR-10 Airbench96, all visible edits touch `airbench96.py`, with zero edits to the timing classifier or result parser. In Parameter Golf, the audit relies on archived snapshot inspection rather than edit-stream paths alone. This is not an ablation—it's a verification that the measurement-hardening design (evaluator-owned scoring) held during the reported runs. The paper does not report what would happen if this hardening were removed—no "self-reported score" control is run—so the necessity of evaluator-owned measurement is a design claim, not an empirically tested hypothesis.

**Model routing ablation (not performed):** All reported runs use Claude Opus 4.7 for every role. The paper does not ablate the choice of language model—no comparison with Claude Opus 4.5, Claude Sonnet, GPT-4, or open-weight models is performed. This is a significant limitation: it is unknown whether the observed proposal diversity, cross-role information sharing, and failure-to-follow-up patterns are specific to Claude Opus 4.7's capabilities or would generalize to other models.

**Specialist count ablation (not performed):** The paper uses 10 specialists for Parameter Golf, 5 for NanoChat-D12, and 5 for CIFAR-10 Airbench96, chosen before each run based on the task's constraint surface. No ablation over specialist count is performed—it is unknown whether 5 specialists would perform comparably to 10 on Parameter Golf, or whether 3 or 8 specialists would change NanoChat-D12 or CIFAR outcomes. The paper's claim that the specialist taxonomy "is chosen before each run and fixed during search" (Section 3.3) is accurate, but the choice itself is not empirically validated.

**Trial budget ablation (not performed beyond the 200-trial window):** The Parameter Golf headline run extends to 900 trials and shows continued improvement (1.073142 at trial 200 → 1.072210 at trial ~750). However, no controlled experiment tests whether the additional 700 trials were necessary or whether a different allocation (e.g., 200 trials with a different specialist mix or a different lineage format) could achieve the same final score. The paper does not report improvement-per-trial curves that would show diminishing returns, so the value of longer-horizon search is suggested but not rigorously quantified.

**Lineage format ablation (not performed):** The paper uses a specific lineage format (hypothesis text, role, status, score, delta, parent ID, timing, crash excerpt) rendered into prompts as LEADERBOARD.md, KNOWLEDGE.md, and Recent Activity sections. No ablation over lineage format is performed—it is unknown whether a simpler format (e.g., only the best-so-far score and the last trial's outcome) would achieve similar performance, or whether a richer format (e.g., full diff summaries, per-phase timing breakdowns) would improve it. The no-lineage ablation removes the entire lineage feedback channel; finer-grained ablations (e.g., removing only the Recent Activity section or only the KNOWLEDGE.md summary) are not reported.

### Critical Assessment

#### Claim 1: The submitted-trial loop autonomously improves recipes without human intervention during search.

**What is demonstrated:** Across three environments with 1,197 headline-run trials, the loop produces final recipes that improve over fixed starting points (0.81% lower bpb, 38.7% higher CORE, 4.59% lower training time). Humans did not choose proposals, edit recipes, override scores, or repair failed trials during the reported search trajectories.

**What is less clear:** The autonomy claim depends on the definition of "during search." The paper acknowledges extensive human setup: environments were selected, specialist taxonomies were designed, prompts were written, harnesses were developed, and calibration runs were performed. The line between "setup" and "search" is sharp in the paper's framing (everything before the frozen run is setup; everything after is search), but the quality of the setup almost certainly affects the search outcome. A poorly designed specialist taxonomy or an inappropriate environment choice would produce different results, and the paper provides no evidence that the specific taxonomies used are optimal or even reasonable beyond their post-hoc alignment with task constraints.

The paper also does not report how many preliminary runs were required to develop the harness and prompts. The historical 91-trial traces suggest at least some iteration occurred. If dozens of preliminary runs with different prompt designs or role splits were discarded, the reported runs are a selected subset, and the autonomy claim is weaker than it appears. The paper is transparent that "the full project used additional setup and preliminary compute outside these reported totals, including benchmark preparation, starting-point calibration, harness and prompt development, and historical audit traces" (Appendix E), but does not quantify this additional compute or characterize how much search outcome variability exists across different prompt designs.

#### Claim 2: Lineage feedback is the key mechanism for turning measured outcomes into later program-level edits.

**What is demonstrated:** The lineage ablation (200 trials, same specialist split, same starting recipe, same budget) shows a striking collapse: 16 keeps → 3 keeps, final score 1.073142 → 1.077413, eval-budget hits 19.0% → 61.5%, parent tree 15 → 3 active heads, contributing specialists 8 → 2. The case studies (NanoChat 007 → 020, Parameter Golf 587 → 596, CIFAR 060 → 070) provide concrete examples of failure feedback propagating into follow-up edits.

**What is less clear:** The lineage ablation is performed on a single task (Parameter Golf) with a single specialist configuration (10 roles) at a single budget (200 trials). The paper claims cross-task generalization (the NanoChat and CIFAR case studies are presented as evidence that "the same pattern holds under different constraints"), but no controlled lineage ablation is performed for NanoChat-D12 or CIFAR-10 Airbench96. The generalization claim is plausible—the case studies show similar failure-to-follow-up dynamics—but is not causally tested outside Parameter Golf.

The ablation also removes the entire lineage feedback channel at once. It does not distinguish between the value of seeing prior hypotheses, prior scores, prior failure modes, or prior parent-child relationships. The no-lineage condition preserves the current-best score line, so agents know the frontier but not how it was reached or which boundaries are binding. A more granular ablation (removing only the Recent Activity section, or only the KNOWLEDGE.md summary, or only the cross-role rows) would clarify which specific lineage components drive the effect. The paper's qualitative explanation—that the eval-budget cap proximity is carried inside the Recent Activity block—is plausible but not isolated.

#### Claim 3: Specialist role partitioning improves proposal diversity, boundary discipline, and search effectiveness.

**What is demonstrated:** The role swarm produces 3.28× more effective proposal clusters than the generic-10 control (134.8 vs. 41.1) at similar throughput (18.15 vs. 16.79 trials/hour), with lower concentration (3.5% vs. 12.0% in the largest cluster) and fewer repeated preflight crashes. The final score is better (1.073142 vs. 1.074495) with more valid improvements (16 vs. 10).

**What is less clear:** The score difference between role swarm and generic-10 is meaningful but not dramatic—the generic-10 still achieves 1.074495 with 10 keeps, which is better than the single generalist (1.075384) and far better than no-lineage (1.077413). This suggests that shared lineage alone (which the generic-10 has) recovers much of the role swarm's performance, and that role partitioning provides incremental rather than essential gains, at least within the 200-trial budget. The paper emphasizes the diversity difference, but the practical implication is that a generic multi-agent system with lineage can achieve most of the role swarm's performance with simpler infrastructure—a point the paper acknowledges by noting that "the control separates two mechanisms: shared lineage recovers much of the improvement count and parent-tree breadth, while role partitioning improves boundary discipline and broadens the idea stream" (Section 4.3).

The paper does not test whether role partitioning becomes more important at longer horizons. If the generic-10's lower diversity causes it to exhaust promising directions faster, the gap might widen beyond 200 trials. Alternatively, if the generic-10's concentrated exploration is actually more efficient for the early phase (finding the high-salience improvements quickly) and role partitioning helps for the later refinement phase, the optimal strategy might be to start with generic agents and introduce roles later. Neither hypothesis is tested because the generic-10 is only run for 200 trials.

#### Claim 4: The loop produces program-level edits, not just scalar hyperparameter tuning.

**What is demonstrated:** The paper audits 157 of 1,197 headline-run trials (13.1%) where the specialist or edit domain is "architecture," using this as a conservative lower bound on non-scalar edits. Table 2 and Table 8 list concrete program transformations: attention-path rewrites (SSSL to L), recurrence residual scaling, separate RoPE/NoPE query gains, per-head attention-output gates, GQA K/V projection rewrites, Bigram Hash Embeddings, MTP-2 objectives, residual-preserved ConvGroup depth changes, self-paced loss caching, and stochastic depth on residual paths.

**What is less clear:** The 13.1% figure is explicitly labeled as a "strict lower-bound sanity check, not an estimate of the full non-scalar edit fraction" (Section 4.1). The paper acknowledges that systems, optimizer, and loss specialists sometimes rewrite executable structure—the NanoChat attention-kernel path change is attributed to the systems specialist, not the architecture specialist—so the true fraction of program-level edits could be higher. But without a comprehensive taxonomy of what counts as "program-level" vs. "scalar tuning," this claim is difficult to evaluate. Many of the listed transformations could be characterized as architectural hyperparameter changes (changing the number of layers, swapping one attention variant for another) rather than genuinely novel program structures. The paper's own scoping—"agents combine and transfer known techniques rather than propose anything as structurally novel as the original Transformer" (Section 5)—suggests that the distinction between "program-level" and "hyperparameter" is blurry, and the paper's framing is appropriately modest.

The paper also does not compare the program-level edit rate against what a simple baseline (e.g., random search over a predefined set of architectural variants) would achieve. The 13.1% figure is a measurement of what was submitted, not a demonstration that the loop is uniquely capable of proposing structural changes.

#### Claim 5: The same loop architecture works across three different constraint regimes without task-specific modification beyond the specialist taxonomy.

**What is demonstrated:** The three headline runs use the same core infrastructure (submitted-trial loop, shared lineage blackboard, specialist role partitioning, evaluator-owned measurement) and all improve their starting recipes. The different contribution patterns (broad for Parameter Golf, systems-concentrated for NanoChat, gate-dominated for CIFAR) suggest that the loop appropriately adapts its search behavior to each task's constraint regime.

**What is less clear:** The specialist taxonomies, global rules, knowledge files, and domain preambles are all task-specific—they encode human knowledge about which recipe surfaces matter for each constraint regime. The paper frames this as configuration rather than modification, but the configuration is non-trivial: choosing 10 vs. 5 roles, deciding which roles to include, writing domain preambles that correctly scope edits, and populating knowledge files with task-local setup notes all require significant human expertise. The claim that "the same loop architecture works" is true at the level of the abstract feedback structure but elides the human effort required to instantiate that structure for a new task. The paper does not provide guidance on how to determine the right specialist taxonomy for a novel environment—the three taxonomies are presented as given, and no ablation over specialist count or composition is performed.

#### Genuine Weaknesses

**Single language model.** All results are conditional on Claude Opus 4.7. No comparison with other frontier models or open-weight models is performed. If Claude Opus 4.7 has specific strengths (e.g., code editing, constraint reasoning) that other models lack, the results may not transfer. This is a significant limitation given the paper's ambition to establish closed-loop auto research as a general paradigm.

**Single run per condition.** The paper reports one run per experimental condition. No error bars, confidence intervals, or replicate runs are reported. The score differences between conditions (e.g., 1.073142 vs. 1.074495 vs. 1.075384) are small in absolute terms (0.001–0.002 bpb), and without replicate runs it is impossible to assess whether these differences are statistically reliable or within the noise of the evaluation metric and the stochasticity of LLM-based proposal generation.

**No comparison against non-agent baselines.** The paper does not compare the loop against simpler automated methods: random search over the recipe surface, grid search over known hyperparameters, population-based training, or Bayesian optimization over a predefined architecture space. The absence of these comparisons makes it difficult to assess how much of the improvement comes from the loop's specific design (specialist roles, lineage feedback, failure-to-follow-up) versus simply running many trials on a promising starting recipe. The Paper's contribution is the trajectory, not just the final score, but a comparison against non-agent methods would clarify whether the trajectory contains evidence of intelligent search or just evidence of budget expenditure.

**Difficulty estimation and specialist taxonomy design cost are unaccounted for.** The paper does not include the human effort required to design specialist taxonomies, write domain preambles, populate knowledge files, and calibrate starting points in its cost accounting. This is analogous to the difficulty-estimation cost problem in the reference paper (Section 6 of that paper)—the infrastructure for making the loop work is expensive to set up, and the reported improvements are computed after this setup cost is sunk. A fair comparison against simpler methods would account for the human time and preliminary compute required.

**No evaluation of whether the final recipes are genuinely novel or just overfit to the specific measurement protocol.** The paper verifies that agents did not touch evaluator or parser paths (the evaluator-touch audit), but does not verify that the final recipes would generalize to slightly different measurement protocols (e.g., different random seeds, slightly different hardware, a different FineWeb slice). The CIFAR cold-process protocol partially addresses this by averaging over ten seeds, but Parameter Golf and NanoChat-D12 do not have similar safeguards. It is possible that the final recipes exploit quirks of the specific evaluation protocol that a human researcher would recognize as non-generalizable—the paper does not discuss this risk.

**The 157-trial architecture audit is a lower bound that may substantially underestimate program-level edits.** The paper acknowledges this but does not provide an upper bound or a more comprehensive taxonomy. If the true program-level edit fraction is, say, 30%, the claim that the loop produces "program-level recipe edits rather than one-shot suggestions" is stronger than if it's 15%. Without a systematic classification, the reader cannot assess this claim.

**No latency or wall-clock analysis for the overall loop.** The paper reports per-trial throughput (18.15 trials/hour for the role swarm) but does not report how long the full 900-trial Parameter Golf run took in wall-clock time, or what the latency is between a specialist submitting a trial and its outcome appearing in another specialist's lineage view. This matters for the "affordable iteration" claim—if the loop takes 50 hours to complete 900 trials, the feedback cycle might be too slow for some practical applications. The termination rules (48-hour deadline or 4-hour no-improvement grace) suggest the runs complete within this timeframe, but exact wall-clock durations are not reported.

#### Missing Experiments

- **Lineage ablation on NanoChat-D12 and CIFAR-10 Airbench96.** This would test whether the lineage effect generalizes beyond Parameter Golf's specific constraint regime (size and time caps).

- **Role count ablation.** Testing whether 3, 5, 10, or 15 specialists on the same task produces different proposal diversity, boundary discipline, and final scores. This would provide guidance for practitioners instantiating the loop on new tasks.

- **Language model ablation.** Testing whether Claude Sonnet, GPT-4, or an open-weight model produces comparable results. This would clarify whether the loop's effectiveness is tied to a specific model's capabilities.

- **Non-agent baselines.** Random search, grid search, or Bayesian optimization over the recipe surface. This would calibrate how much of the improvement comes from the search infrastructure versus the budget of trials.

- **Longer-horizon generic-10 and single-generalist runs.** Extending the controls beyond 200 trials would test whether the role swarm's diversity advantage compounds at longer horizons or whether generic agents catch up as they exhaust high-salience directions and are forced to explore.

- **Granular lineage ablations.** Removing only the Recent Activity section, or only the cross-role rows, or only the failure status information, to identify which specific lineage components drive the effect.

- **Generalization evaluation.** Testing whether the final recipes transfer to slightly different measurement protocols (different seeds, different hardware, different data slices) to assess whether the loop overfits to the specific evaluator.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Specialist Taxonomy and Prompt Engineering Cost Is Unaccounted for and Non-Trivial

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's loop depends on a hand-designed specialist taxonomy—the partition of the recipe surface into non-overlapping domains, each with a custom domain preamble specifying scope, non-scope, and edit-radius guidance. This taxonomy is "chosen before each run and fixed during search" (Section 3.3). For Parameter Golf, this means designing 10 roles with domain preambles (Figures 5–7), global rules (Figure 4), knowledge files, and anti-anchoring banlists. The paper acknowledges that "the full project used additional setup and preliminary compute outside these reported totals, including benchmark preparation, starting-point calibration, harness and prompt development, and historical audit traces" (Appendix E), but does not quantify this setup cost or amortize it into the headline efficiency numbers.

**The consequence.** A practitioner wanting to apply this loop to a new training task must invest substantial human effort in (a) understanding the recipe surface well enough to partition it into a specialist taxonomy, (b) writing domain preambles that correctly scope edits and discourage the wrong kind of proposals, (c) populating knowledge files with task-local setup notes, and (d) calibrating starting points under local hardware. The paper provides no guidance on how to determine the right taxonomy for a novel environment—the three taxonomies are post-hoc justified by their alignment with task constraints, but no ablation over specialist count or composition is performed. If a poorly designed taxonomy degrades performance (e.g., too few roles causing proposal concentration, or overlapping scopes causing specialists to collide on the same edits), the loop might underperform simpler baselines, and the practitioner would have no diagnostic for whether the taxonomy or the core loop is at fault. The headline 4× throughput improvement from specialist parallelism (18.15 vs. 2.26 trials/hour, Section 3.4) and the proposal diversity gains (134.8 vs. 41.1 effective clusters, Table 3) are measured after this human design cost is sunk—a fair comparison against methods that require less setup (e.g., a generic multi-agent system with lineage, which achieves 1.074495 bpb with 10 keeps at 200 trials, Table 1) would need to account for the human time invested in the specialist taxonomy.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper's own generic-10 control (Section 4.3, Table 1, Table 3) is the closest evidence: ten identical agents with the same tools, same lineage, and same parallel infrastructure achieve 1.074495 bpb at 200 trials versus the role swarm's 1.073142—a meaningful but modest gap (0.00135 bpb difference, or about 1.8× the role swarm's keep delta). The generic-10 requires zero specialist taxonomy design. The paper acknowledges that "the control separates two mechanisms: shared lineage recovers much of the improvement count and parent-tree breadth, while role partitioning improves boundary discipline and broadens the idea stream" (Section 4.3), which implies that the human design cost of role partitioning buys incremental rather than essential gains, at least within the 200-trial budget. The historical 91-trial traces (Table 3) suggest that prompt design matters substantially—the historical single generalist had different harness vintage and no anti-anchoring banlist, and the modern single-generalist control shows improved diversity (61.9 vs. 39.3 effective clusters), hinting that prompt engineering iteration occurred between the historical and modern traces.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not attempt to automate specialist taxonomy design or provide a methodology for practitioners. It frames the taxonomy as a configuration choice rather than a limitation, and suggests that future work on "other compute-budgeted environments" (Section 5) could reuse the same loop architecture with task-specific taxonomies—implying that human design remains necessary. The paper does not report how many preliminary runs or prompt iterations were required to arrive at the reported taxonomies.

### The Lineage Ablation Is Performed on Only One Task, Limiting Claims of Generality

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's strongest causal result is the lineage ablation on Parameter Golf (Section 4.3, Figure 3 Panel B, Table 1): removing shared lineage from the role swarm causes a collapse from 16 to 3 valid improvements, a parent-tree collapse from 15 to 3 active heads, and eval-budget cap hits rising from 19.0% to 61.5%. The paper then claims, based on the NanoChat-D12 and CIFAR-10 Airbench96 case studies, that "the same pattern holds under different constraints" (Section 5). However, **no controlled lineage ablation is performed for NanoChat-D12 or CIFAR-10 Airbench96.** The case studies show that cross-role information propagation occurred in these runs (the systems specialist's attention-path rewrite informed later optimization and schedule edits in NanoChat; the accuracy-gate miss informed the warmup repair in CIFAR), but they do not show what would have happened *without* lineage—the counterfactual is constructed narratively, not experimentally.

**The consequence.** It is plausible but unverified that lineage is equally important in NanoChat-D12 and CIFAR-10 Airbench96. However, the constraint regimes differ in ways that could make lineage less critical. In Parameter Golf, the dominant failure mode without lineage was repeated eval-budget cap violations (61.5% of trials), because the dynamic fact that the current-best recipe sits close to the 600-second eval cap was only carried in the Recent Activity block. In NanoChat-D12, the constraint is a fixed 90-minute wallclock—there is no "eval budget cap" to repeatedly collide with because the run simply finishes when time expires. The feedback that matters is wallclock headroom (how much slack was exposed by a kernel change), which is directly visible in the current-best score and timing information without needing access to prior trial hypotheses. In CIFAR-10 Airbench96, the dominant constraint is the accuracy gate—a binary pass/fail that returns the measured accuracy deficit. A specialist receiving only the current-best score (which includes whether the gate was met) plus its own static priors might still be able to diagnose that speed recipes need warmup repair, without needing to see prior specialists' gate-miss trials. The paper's cross-task generalization claim—that lineage is "the key mechanism for turning measured outcomes into later program-level edits" (Abstract)—is experimentally supported only for one of the three tasks.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The cross-task evidence is purely qualitative: the NanoChat case study traces the chain from trial 007 (systems diagnosis) to 020 (larger token budget) to 024/025 (data ratio and schedule) to 156 (logit-bias path), and the CIFAR case study traces trial 060 (gate miss) to 070 (warmup repair), both in Section 5. The paper states that "in NanoChat-D12, the loop converted a systems fact into more training tokens and a small logit-bias refinement. In Parameter Golf, it turned a score-useful but artifact-ineligible z-loss result into an artifact-valid keep. In CIFAR-10 Airbench96, a measured gate miss led directly to the final warmup repair. Each move is the same feedback loop applied to a different environment" (Section 6). This asserts equivalence but does not demonstrate it experimentally—no NanoChat or CIFAR lineage ablation exists to confirm that these cross-role chains depend on lineage rather than on specialists independently converging on similar solutions given the same current-best code state.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not claim to have performed cross-task lineage ablations. The limitation is acknowledged implicitly by the absence of these experiments. The paper's scope statement (Section 5) that "future work follows the same conditions" suggests that cross-task replication of the lineage ablation is left to future work. A footnote in the no-lineage ablation definition (Appendix H) specifies the operational details of what is removed, implying that the same ablation could be applied to other tasks, but this is not done.

### All Results Are Conditional on a Single Language Model (Claude Opus 4.7) with No Model Ablation

**The assumption or constraint.** Every proposal in every reported headline and control run—1,197 headline trials plus 600 Parameter Golf control trials—is generated by Claude Opus 4.7 (Appendix D.2). The paper acknowledges that "the framework supports per-role model routing" but states that "all reported frozen runs use Claude Opus 4.7 for every role; only GPU-cluster priority differs by role." No comparison with any other language model (Claude Sonnet, GPT-4, GPT-4o, open-weight models like Llama 3 or DeepSeek) is performed. The choice is not ablated, even within the Parameter Golf controls.

**The consequence.** This is a severe generalizability concern for a paper that frames auto research as a paradigm rather than a single-system demonstration. The loop's specific capabilities—proposing program-level code edits that compile, reasoning about constraint boundaries from failure feedback, sustaining diverse proposals across a multi-day search, and composing edits from different specialists' outcomes—are all gated by the underlying language model's code-generation quality, instruction-following reliability, and long-horizon reasoning. If Claude Opus 4.7 has specific strengths that other models lack (e.g., strong code editing, reliable tool use, resistance to repetitive proposal collapse), the reported results may not transfer to other models. The paper's claim that "specialist agents partition recipe surfaces and share measured lineage across trials" (Abstract) could be interpreted as a claim about the agent architecture independent of the language model, but without model ablation, the architecture and the model are confounded—the reader cannot distinguish between "this loop architecture works" and "Claude Opus 4.7 makes this loop architecture work."

The practical consequence is that a practitioner wanting to replicate this system with a different model (e.g., an open-weight model for cost or privacy reasons, or a different API provider) has no evidence about whether the loop would function. The proposal-entropy measurements (Table 3), the failure-to-follow-up chains (Section 5 case studies), and the cross-role information sharing (cross-context parent edges of 83.7%) are all contingent on the model's behavior. A weaker model might default to scalar knob-twiddling more often, produce more syntax errors, fail to interpret crash feedback correctly, or collapse into repetitive proposals faster—all failure modes that the paper's architecture (role partitioning, lineage, anti-anchoring banlist) is designed to mitigate, but which might overwhelm the architecture if the base model is less capable.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides no ablation evidence on model choice. The single-generalist control (Table 3) shows that even Claude Opus 4.7, without role partitioning, collapses into concentrated proposals (61.9 effective clusters, 17.5% largest cluster, 32 polar-coefficient preflight crashes), suggesting that the base model alone is not sufficient for diverse search—the architecture matters. But the reverse question (does the architecture compensate for a weaker model?) is entirely unaddressed. The Anthropic SDK and Claude CLI binary are deeply integrated into the supervisor infrastructure (Appendix E: "The Anthropic SDK uses the bundled claude CLI binary"), which is a practical detail that makes model ablation non-trivial to implement but does not excuse the absence of the experiment.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not discuss this limitation or suggest model ablation as future work. The only mention of model choice is the routing policy declaration (Appendix D.2) and the compute accounting note (Appendix E) that references the Anthropic SDK. The limitation is entirely unacknowledged.

### No Comparison Against Non-Agent Baselines Makes It Impossible to Assess the Value of the Agent Infrastructure

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper compares the role swarm against three internal controls (single generalist, generic 10-agent, no-lineage), all of which use the same LLM-based agent infrastructure with different organizational parameters. It does not compare against any method that does not use language-model agents for proposal generation: random search over the recipe surface, grid search over known hyperparameters, population-based training (Jaderberg et al., 2017), Bayesian optimization (Snoek et al., 2012) over a predefined architecture space, or evolutionary methods (Real et al., 2019) over program transformations. The paper's starting point is the public or calibrated recipe score; it does not report what a budget-equivalent non-agent method would achieve on the same tasks.

**The consequence.** This absence makes it impossible to assess whether the loop's specific design features (specialist roles, lineage feedback, program-level edits, failure-to-follow-up) provide any value over simply running many trials with a simpler search strategy. The headline results—0.81% bpb reduction, 38.7% CORE improvement, 4.59% speedup—are measured against fixed starting recipes, not against alternative automated methods given the same compute budget (1,500 Parameter Golf trials, 200 NanoChat trials, 97 CIFAR trials). A random search that samples from a predefined set of known architectural variants, optimizer families, and hyperparameter ranges might achieve comparable or better results simply by exploring the space more systematically, without the overhead of LLM-based proposal generation (API costs, latency, prompt engineering, and the risk of repetitive or invalid proposals).

The paper's contribution is framed as the trajectory itself—the auditable sequence of proposals, edits, and feedback propagation—not just the final score. This is a valid reframing, but it does not eliminate the need for a performance calibration. If a simple random search achieves a similar final score, the trajectory is still a contribution (showing how an agent-based system explores), but its practical value for recipe development is diminished—a practitioner would choose the simpler, cheaper method. If the agent-based loop substantially outperforms random search at matched trial budget, that would be strong evidence that the agent infrastructure (lineage reasoning, failure-to-follow-up, compositional edits) provides genuine search efficiency gains, not just a more inspectable search process.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper's only non-agent comparison is implicit: the starting recipe in each environment is a human-designed baseline, and the improvement over that baseline is the headline result. The Parameter Golf starting recipe (1.0810 bpb) is a public competitive recipe that has already been optimized by human researchers, so the 0.81% reduction represents incremental improvement on a strong baseline—this could be interpreted as weak evidence that the loop finds non-trivial improvements that random search might miss. But the paper does not quantify how much improvement a random-search baseline with the same trial budget would achieve on the same starting recipe. The paper's related work section (Section 2) acknowledges that classical HPO and NAS methods exist but argues they "assume fixed search spaces" and therefore are not directly comparable—this is a scope argument, not an empirical calibration.

The proposal-entropy analysis (Table 3) provides indirect evidence: the role swarm explores 134.8 effective clusters in 200 trials, which is more diverse than a random sample from a concentrated proposal distribution, but says nothing about whether a systematic non-agent search over a predefined space would sample the same space more efficiently. The evaluator-touch audit (Appendix I) and measurement hardening are presented as advantages over non-agent methods (which might reward-hack without evaluator-owned measurement), but this is a safety argument, not a performance argument.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not discuss this limitation or compare against non-agent baselines. The scope statement in Section 5 ("Within these limits the loop turns auto research from a one-shot claim into a continuously measured object") suggests the authors view the trajectory-as-output contribution as orthogonal to the performance question, but this does not excuse the absence of a calibration that would help practitioners decide whether to adopt the approach.

### The 48-Hour Search Horizon and Small Number of Runs Per Condition Limit Statistical Reliability

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper reports exactly one run per experimental condition: one 900-trial Parameter Golf role-swarm run, one 200-trial role-swarm control, one 200-trial generic-10 control, one 200-trial single-generalist control, one 200-trial no-lineage run, one 200-trial NanoChat-D12 run, and one 97-trial CIFAR-10 Airbench96 run (Table 1). No replicate runs are reported for any condition. The search horizon is capped at 48 hours or a 4-hour no-improvement grace period (Appendix D.4), and the 200-trial controls are matched windows within this horizon. The paper does not report confidence intervals, standard errors, or any measure of variability for the final scores, valid improvement counts, proposal-entropy metrics, or cross-role parent-edge fractions.

**The consequence.** The central comparisons in the paper—role swarm vs. generic-10 (1.073142 vs. 1.074495 bpb), role swarm vs. no-lineage (1.073142 vs. 1.077413 bpb), role swarm vs. single generalist (1.073142 vs. 1.075384 bpb)—are based on single-run point estimates. The score differences are small in absolute terms: the largest gap (role swarm vs. no-lineage) is 0.00427 bpb, and the smallest (role swarm vs. generic-10) is 0.00135 bpb. The paper does not report the inherent variability of the Parameter Golf evaluation metric (validation bpb on FineWeb) under identical recipe conditions—it is unknown whether repeating the same recipe multiple times would produce score variance on the order of 0.0001, 0.001, or 0.01 bpb. If the metric variance is, say, 0.002 bpb, then the role swarm vs. generic-10 gap (0.00135 bpb) is within noise, and the claim that role partitioning improves search effectiveness is not statistically supported. Similarly, the proposal-entropy numbers (134.8 vs. 41.1 effective clusters, 3.5% vs. 12.0% largest cluster share) are single-run estimates with unknown variance—a replicate run of the generic-10 control might produce 60 effective clusters and 8% largest cluster share, reducing the reported diversity advantage.

The single-run nature of each condition also means that the case-study chains (NanoChat 007→020→024→025→156, Parameter Golf 587→596→746→750, CIFAR 060→070) are single trajectories that may not be representative. In a replicate run, the systems specialist might not diagnose the attention-backend bottleneck on the same trial index (or at all), the z-loss proposal might not occur, or the CIFAR warmup repair might fail at a different accuracy deficit. The paper presents these chains as evidence of the loop's capability, but without replicate runs, the reader cannot assess whether they are typical or cherry-picked.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The historical 91-trial traces (Table 3) provide a partial replication of the Parameter Golf role-swarm vs. single-generalist comparison under a different harness vintage, and the qualitative patterns (role swarm has more effective clusters, lower near-duplicate rate, more cross-role parent edges) are consistent with the modern controls. This is weak evidence for replicability because the harness vintage changed between the historical and modern traces, and the 91-trial window is smaller than the 200-trial primary comparison window. The paper does not report any replicate runs with the modern harness.

The matched-budget design (all controls run for exactly 200 trials from the identical starting recipe under the same harness vintage and hardware) partially mitigates the single-run concern by holding trial count, starting state, and measurement protocol constant across conditions. This eliminates some sources of variance (different starting recipes, different hardware, different trial budgets) but does not eliminate the stochasticity of LLM-based proposal generation, GPU-cluster queue timing, or evaluation metric noise. The paper's throughput model (Equation 1, Section 3.4) acknowledges that parallel efficiency depends on shared cluster resources and queue timing, which are inherently variable sources of run-to-run difference that a single run cannot capture.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not discuss the single-run limitation or the absence of replicate runs. It does not report metric variance estimates, confidence intervals, or any form of statistical significance testing. The termination rules (Appendix D.4) specify a 48-hour deadline and 4-hour no-improvement grace, which constrain the search horizon but do not address variability. The paper's framing of the contribution as trajectory-as-output rather than score-as-output partially mitigates the concern—even with high score variance, the trajectory itself (proposal diversity, cross-role information sharing, failure-to-follow-up chains) would be informative about search behavior—but the claims about score improvement magnitudes (0.81%, 38.7%, 4.59%) and the causal comparisons between conditions depend on the score differences being reliable, which single runs cannot establish.

### Failure-to-Follow-Up Chains Are Described Qualitatively; No Systematic Measurement of Feedback Propagation

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's central empirical finding is that "lineage feedback lets agents turn evaluator outcomes, including crashes, budget overruns, size failures, and accuracy-gate misses, into later program-level recipe edits rather than one-shot suggestions" (Abstract). The evidence for this claim comes primarily from qualitative case studies: three specific failure-to-follow-up chains (NanoChat 007→020, Parameter Golf 587→596, CIFAR 060→070) described in Section 5 and Appendix I. The paper also reports cross-role parent-edge fractions (83.7% of parent edges cross roles in the full 900-trial Parameter Golf swarm, Table 3) as quantitative evidence of cross-role information sharing. However, a parent edge records only that one trial declared another as its parent—it does not measure whether the parent's *failure feedback* specifically drove the child's design, as opposed to the parent's *successful score improvement* motivating a follow-up refinement.

**The consequence.** The distinction between "failure feedback drives repair" and "success drives refinement" matters because the paper's central claim is that feedback from *failures* (crashes, size blocks, budget overruns, gate misses) is the mechanism that distinguishes this loop from simpler approaches. If most cross-role parent edges represent successful proposals being refined by other specialists (a standard iterative improvement pattern), the claim about failure feedback specifically is weaker. The case studies demonstrate that failure-to-repair chains *can* occur in the loop, but do not establish how common they are relative to success-driven chains. A systematic measurement—e.g., what fraction of valid improvements descend from a failed or ineligible parent trial, versus from a discard or keep parent—is not reported.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The three case studies are concrete and well-documented:
- NanoChat: trial 007 (systems specialist, attention-path rewrite) was a **keep** (raised CORE to 0.1695), not a failure. The "systems fact" that motivated later trials (020 expanding token budget, 024 raising data ratio) was a successful improvement that exposed headroom, not a failure.
- Parameter Golf: trial 587 (z-loss) was a **size_blocked** failure—it returned a useful score (1.072431) but exceeded the artifact cap by 2,056 bytes. Trial 596 retained the mechanism and recovered headroom, turning the failure into a legal keep.
- CIFAR: trial 060 was a **disqualified** failure (25.1650 s at 0.95956 accuracy, missing gate by 0.00044). Trial 070 kept the speed recipe and repaired the gate margin with a warmup change.

Of these three chains, only two (Parameter Golf and CIFAR) are genuine failure-to-repair chains; the NanoChat chain is a success-to-refinement chain where a systems improvement created headroom that other specialists exploited. The paper's abstract claim that "lineage feedback lets agents turn evaluator outcomes, including crashes, budget overruns, size failures, and accuracy-gate misses, into later program-level recipe edits" is supported specifically by the Parameter Golf and CIFAR cases, but the NanoChat case—the one with the largest relative improvement (38.7%)—does not fit this pattern.

The cross-role parent-edge fractions (Table 3) are reported in aggregate but are not broken down by parent status. The paper reports that 10 of 12 keeps with within-window parents cross role boundaries in the 200-trial role swarm, but does not report how many of those parent trials were keeps, discards, crashes, size blocks, or budget overruns. The 97.0% cross-context keep rate in the full 900-trial swarm (32 of 33 keeps) similarly lacks a status breakdown. This means the quantitative evidence for cross-role information propagation does not specifically measure failure-to-repair dynamics.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not provide a systematic measurement of failure-to-repair chains. The case studies are presented as representative but are not sampled randomly from the trajectory—they are the three final-recipe chains, which may be atypical. The paper does not claim to have measured this systematically, and the "Scope and limits" section (Section 5) frames the observed boundary as "compositional, where agents combine, transfer, and repair known techniques under external feedback," which is broad enough to encompass both failure-to-repair and success-to-refinement patterns. The absence of a systematic breakdown is a measurement gap rather than a misrepresentation, but it limits the strength of the central causal claim.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper does not introduce a new algorithm, architecture, or training technique. It introduces a **measurement methodology.** That distinction matters for understanding the kind of impact it can have. The paper's central move is to reframe auto research from "did the agent produce a good result?" to "can we inspect the trajectory of proposals, code edits, failures, and follow-up repairs, and does that trajectory show evidence of evidence-driven search?" This is a **diagnostic shift**, not a paradigm shift—it does not claim that language agents are now doing creative science, but it provides the infrastructure and vocabulary for measuring whether they are doing something more structured than random search.

**The trajectory-as-output standard forces a different evaluation regime.** Prior agent-ML systems (MLAgentBench, MLE-bench, RE-Bench, MLGym-Bench, The AI Scientist) report final scores, success rates, or generated papers. Even systems that do record intermediate steps—population-based training logs, Bayesian optimization surrogate updates, AlphaEvolve's evolutionary tree—treat those steps as scaffolding for the endpoint. This paper insists that the sequence of submitted trials, with their hypotheses, statuses, parent edges, and failure metadata, *is* the primary empirical object. This shift matters because it makes two things legible that were previously invisible: (a) whether a good final recipe was arrived at through evidence-driven iteration or through stochastic exploration with a large budget, and (b) whether failures are being used as research signals or simply accumulated as waste. A system that stumbles into a good recipe through 900 blind trials and one that composes three cross-role failure-to-repair chains produce identical endpoints but radically different trajectories—and the paper's framework distinguishes them.

**Lineage feedback as active research state, not passive logging.** The lineage ablation in Figure 3 Panel B is the paper's most impactful single result for how future systems should be built. It demonstrates that knowing the current-best score is not sufficient to sustain evidence-driven search—the dynamic constraint boundary information (which caps are currently binding, which edits recently failed, which parent heads are still active) must propagate through the feedback channel. Without it, the search collapses to boundary-blind repeated collisions (61.5% eval-budget cap hits vs. 19.0% with lineage) followed by a long plateau with no new improvements (125 trials without a keep after trial 75). The finding that this collapse happens *even with role partitioning and parallel submission*—the no-lineage condition has the same specialist split and same throughput as the lineage-on condition—isolates lineage as the critical mechanism separate from diversity or parallelism. This changes how researchers should think about agent memory in iterative experimentation systems: it is not enough to show the agent the best result; the system must carry forward what boundaries were discovered, what directions failed, and what evidence from one specialist's domain is relevant to another's.

**The reconciliation of conflicting signals about agent-driven ML research.** MLGym-Bench (Nathani et al., 2025) found that agent-driven ML gains often come from hyperparameter tuning rather than novel ideas. The AI Scientist (Lu et al., 2024) found that agents can produce plausible-looking papers but the underlying experimental quality is variable. RE-Bench (Wijk et al., 2025) found that agents lag human experts on open-ended research engineering. On the other side, AlphaDev and FunSearch showed that evaluator-driven program search can discover novel algorithms. This paper provides a framework for understanding *when* and *why* agents default to scalar tuning versus propose structural changes. The answer, based on the proposal-entropy analysis (Table 3) and the role-partitioning comparison (Figure 3 Panel A), is that proposal diversity and edit scope are functions of the prompt architecture, not just the underlying model capability. Role-decomposed specialists with explicit non-scope constraints produce 134.8 effective proposal clusters in 200 trials; generic agents with the same model produce 41.1. The paper does not claim to have solved the "agents only tune hyperparameters" problem, but it provides a diagnostic toolkit (effective cluster count, largest cluster share, near-duplicate rate, cross-context parent edges) and a design intervention (specialist role partitioning with domain preambles) that future systems can adopt and refine.

**Which research directions become more attractive, and which become less so.** The paper makes **verifier and constraint design** a first-class research direction. The environments are chosen for their failure modes, not despite them: the Parameter Golf artifact size cap, the CIFAR accuracy gate, and the NanoChat wallclock budget are what produce the quantified feedback (exact byte excess, accuracy deficit, runtime slack) that the lineage mechanism carries. This suggests that future auto-research environments should be designed with rich, quantified constraint boundaries—not just a scalar score—and that the quality of the constraint feedback may matter more than the metric itself. Conversely, the paper makes **increasing agent reasoning depth alone** less attractive as a path forward. The model is Claude Opus 4.7 throughout; the variation in search quality comes from the infrastructure (roles, lineage, evaluator ownership), not from model scaling. A weaker model with good infrastructure might outperform a stronger model with poor infrastructure, though the paper does not test this. The implication is that research investment in agent scaffolding (prompt architecture, feedback format, constraint design, role decomposition) may yield higher marginal returns than investment in base model capability for this class of tasks, at least at current capability levels.

**The scope of the contribution is bounded, and that is useful.** The paper explicitly limits its claims: "The recorded trials do not show paradigm-level architecture invention such as a replacement for the original Transformer. Future agents may cross this boundary, and the same evaluator-driven feedback loop remains the natural arena for measuring whether such ideas hold up under real training data" (Section 5). This honesty is analytically productive—it separates the question of whether the loop works (yes, it produces measurable improvements on real training pipelines) from the question of whether it produces novel science (no, the proposals combine and transfer known techniques). The framework itself does not depend on this distinction; the same trajectory-auditing methodology would work for evaluating a system that claimed to invent new architectures. The paper thus provides both a working system for compositional recipe improvement and a measurement framework that can scale to more ambitious claims as models improve.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Granular lineage ablation to identify which specific feedback components drive the effect.** The paper's no-lineage ablation removes the entire lineage feedback channel at once—the LEADERBOARD.md, KNOWLEDGE.md, Recent Activity sections, and blackboard-reading tools all disappear. This establishes that lineage matters but does not reveal which components matter. A natural follow-up would run the Parameter Golf 200-trial control with four targeted conditions: (a) no Recent Activity (only the current-best score and KNOWLEDGE.md, removing the last-10-trials cross-role window), (b) no KNOWLEDGE.md (only the current-best score and Recent Activity, removing the curated hypothesis tree), (c) no cross-role rows in Recent Activity (only same-specialist rows), and (d) no failure metadata (only keep/discard status, removing crash excerpts, byte excess, and accuracy deficits). Each condition isolates one hypothesized feedback mechanism. The specific prediction from the paper's analysis is that condition (a) would show increased eval-budget cap collisions (because the Recent Activity block carries the dynamic boundary proximity information), condition (c) would show reduced cross-role parent edges and fewer cross-role keeps, and condition (d) would eliminate the failure-to-repair chains (Parameter Golf 587→596, CIFAR 060→070) because the quantitative boundary information needed for targeted repair would be absent. A null result on condition (d)—failure-to-repair chains still occurring even without explicit failure metadata—would suggest that specialists infer constraint boundaries from the score and status alone, which would challenge the paper's claim that quantified failure feedback is the key mechanism.

**Cross-task lineage ablation on NanoChat-D12 and CIFAR-10 Airbench96.** The paper demonstrates the lineage effect experimentally only on Parameter Golf (Figure 3 Panel B). The NanoChat and CIFAR case studies are presented as cross-task evidence but are not controlled experiments. A direct replication would run the NanoChat-D12 role swarm for 200 trials without lineage (same specialist split, same starting recipe, same 90-minute budget, current-best score line preserved, but no prior-trial hypotheses, scores, statuses, or failure metadata in the prompt). The key measurement is whether the cross-role chain—systems specialist diagnosing the attention-backend bottleneck at trial 007, later specialists spending the recovered wallclock on more tokens—replicates without lineage. If it does (systems specialists independently discover the SSSL-to-L rewrite because the static knowledge files describe the attention-path options), then the lineage effect is task-specific to Parameter Golf's hard-cap constraint regime. If it does not (specialists fail to connect the systems improvement to training-token expansion because they never see the timing slack in lineage), then the paper's cross-task generalization claim is supported. The CIFAR replication would test whether the gate-miss → warmup-repair chain (060→070) requires lineage or whether a specialist receiving only the current-best speed recipe and its own static priors can independently discover the warmup adjustment. The prediction from the paper's framework is that the chain would *not* replicate without lineage, because the accuracy deficit (0.00044 below the gate) is a specific quantitative signal that only the lineage Recent Activity block carries. Failure to replicate would be a strong confirmation; successful replication without lineage would suggest that the gate-miss feedback is redundant with information already available in the current-best recipe's timing and accuracy numbers.

**Model ablation: testing whether the loop's behavior is specific to Claude Opus 4.7.** The paper uses Claude Opus 4.7 for every trial in every reported condition. A model ablation would run the Parameter Golf 200-trial role swarm with lineage (the strongest condition) using: (a) Claude Sonnet 4.5, (b) GPT-4o, (c) an open-weight model such as DeepSeek-V3 or Llama 3 405B (Instruct), and (d) the same Claude Opus 4.7 baseline for calibration. The key measurements are proposal diversity (effective clusters, largest cluster share, near-duplicate rate), valid improvement count and final score, eval-budget cap hit rate, and cross-role parent-edge fraction (Table 3). The paper's framework predicts that the specialist taxonomy and lineage mechanism will partially compensate for weaker base models—role partitioning constrains the proposal space in ways that reduce the need for deep reasoning—but the magnitude of compensation is unknown. A finding that Claude Sonnet achieves, say, 80% of Opus's diversity and 70% of its improvement count would suggest the loop architecture is robust to model capability and could be deployed with cheaper models. A finding that GPT-4o or DeepSeek-V3 collapses into repetitive proposals despite the same infrastructure would suggest the loop depends on specific model behaviors (instruction-following reliability, resistance to proposal collapse, code-editing quality) that are not universal, narrowing the paper's generalizability claim. Importantly, this experiment tests the *architecture*, not just the model—a negative result would be as informative as a positive one for understanding the boundary conditions.

**Automated specialist taxonomy discovery.** The paper's specialist taxonomies (10 roles for Parameter Golf, 5 each for NanoChat and CIFAR) are hand-designed based on human understanding of each task's constraint surface. A direct extension would attempt to automate this: given a new task environment with editable files, a metric, and constraints, can a meta-agent analyze the recipe surface and propose a specialist decomposition? A concrete experiment would take a held-out training task (e.g., a vision transformer training recipe, a speech recognition pipeline, or a reinforcement learning training loop) that the paper's authors have not manually designed a taxonomy for, and compare: (a) a human-designed specialist taxonomy (from a practitioner familiar with the task), (b) an LLM-generated taxonomy (produced by prompting Claude Opus 4.7 with the task description, editable files, and constraint types, asking it to propose roles with scope/non-scope preambles), and (c) a generic 10-agent baseline with no role partitioning. The measurement is the same as the paper's Table 3: proposal diversity, valid improvements, and cross-role information sharing. If the LLM-generated taxonomy achieves comparable diversity and improvement count to the human-designed one, the loop becomes significantly more practical—a practitioner could launch it on a new task by providing a task description and letting the meta-agent propose the specialist split. If the LLM-generated taxonomy performs no better than generic agents, it suggests that specialist taxonomy design requires task-specific knowledge that current models cannot extract from static task descriptions, and that human expertise remains a bottleneck for deploying the loop on novel tasks.

**Systematic measurement of failure-to-repair versus success-to-refinement chains.** The paper's case studies (Section 5) demonstrate that failure-to-repair chains *can* occur, but do not measure how frequently they occur relative to success-driven refinement chains. A systematic analysis would classify every valid improvement in the full 900-trial Parameter Golf run by its parent trial's status: keep (success-driven refinement), discard (valid non-improvement motivating a variant), size_blocked (artifact boundary repair), budget_overrun (runtime boundary repair), crash (error diagnosis and fix), or preflight_crash (syntax/size validation fix). The hypothesis from the paper's framing is that a substantial fraction of keeps descend from failure parents, and that these chains contribute disproportionately to the final improvement because they represent constraint navigation rather than incremental optimization. If instead the majority of keeps descend from keep parents (successive refinements of already-successful directions), then the paper's emphasis on failure feedback is rhetorically stronger than empirically warranted—the loop would be primarily a success-refinement engine with occasional failure-repair capability, rather than the failure-driven research process the abstract describes. A null result here would refine the paper's contribution: the loop's value would be in maintaining diverse proposal streams and enabling cross-role composition, with failure repair as a secondary benefit rather than the central mechanism. This analysis is feasible with the existing trace data—it requires re-analyzing `tree.tsv` with parent status annotations—and would substantially strengthen or appropriately temper the paper's central causal claim.

**Long-horizon search dynamics: does the role swarm's diversity advantage compound or diminish?** The paper runs the role swarm for 900 trials and the generic-10 and single-generalist controls for 200 trials. The role swarm continues improving from trial 200 (1.073142) to trial ~750 (1.072210), but it is unknown whether the generic-10 would also continue improving if extended, perhaps catching up as it exhausts high-salience directions and is forced to explore more diverse proposals. A natural follow-up would extend all three Parameter Golf conditions (role swarm, generic-10, single generalist) to 900 trials under the same harness vintage. The specific prediction from the paper's proposal-entropy analysis is that the generic-10's lower diversity (41.1 effective clusters vs. 134.8 at 200 trials) causes it to plateau earlier—it exhausts the high-concentration directions and cannot sustain improvement at the same rate. The single generalist, with the highest concentration (17.5% in largest cluster, 10.1% near-duplicates), should plateau earliest. If instead all conditions converge to similar final scores at 900 trials, it suggests that the diversity advantage is transient—role partitioning accelerates early exploration but does not change the asymptotic search efficiency—and that the practical value of specialist taxonomies is in reducing time-to-solution rather than improving final-solution quality. This would still be a practically useful finding (faster convergence is valuable) but would reframe the paper's contribution from "role partitioning enables better search" to "role partitioning enables faster search."

**Automated difficulty estimation for task constraint regimes.** The paper does not discuss difficulty estimation, but a clear extension is suggested by the cross-task variation in improvement magnitude (0.81% for Parameter Golf vs. 38.7% for NanoChat vs. 4.59% for CIFAR). Before launching the loop on a new task, a practitioner needs to know whether the task is closer to NanoChat (large headroom, compositional improvements possible) or Parameter Golf (tight frontier, incremental gains only), because this affects budget allocation and expectations. A difficulty-estimation module could run a small number of baseline trials with random proposals, measure the score variance and improvement rate, and predict the expected improvement trajectory under the full loop. This is analogous to the difficulty-estimation mechanism in the reference paper (Section 3.2 of that paper), but applied to task selection rather than per-prompt strategy selection. A concrete experiment would train a simple regressor on the three existing tasks (plus any additional tasks from future work) that maps initial-trial statistics (score variance, valid-improvement rate, constraint-hit rate) to final improvement magnitude, and then test it on a held-out task.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Automated baseline improvement for ML competition and benchmark tasks.** The most direct application is using the loop to automatically improve public starting recipes on compute-budgeted benchmark tasks. The paper demonstrates this on three tasks—Parameter Golf (0.81% bpb reduction), NanoChat-D12 (38.7% CORE improvement), and CIFAR-10 Airbench96 (4.59% speedup)—and the infrastructure is task-agnostic beyond the specialist taxonomy and knowledge files. A practitioner with a new competition task (e.g., a NeurIPS efficiency track, an MLCommons benchmark, a hardware-aware training challenge) could calibrate the starting recipe on their hardware, design a specialist taxonomy based on the task's constraint surface (using the paper's three taxonomies as templates), populate knowledge files with known techniques relevant to the task domain, and launch the loop with a fixed trial budget. The paper's throughput model (Equation 1) provides planning guidance: with 10 parallel specialists on 8-H100 workers, the loop processes approximately 18 trials per hour, so a 48-hour run would complete roughly 860 trials—comparable to the full Parameter Golf headline run. The key practical value is not the final recipe score but the **auditable trajectory**: the loop produces a recorded research process showing what was tried, what failed, and how the final recipe was reached, which is valuable for competition reproducibility requirements and for teams that need to document their optimization process.

**Cost-efficient exploration for small-team ML research.** A small research team (1–3 people) working on training recipe optimization typically spends substantial human time on the propose-edit-measure-revise cycle—reading prior work, formulating hypotheses, implementing code changes, debugging failures, and interpreting results. The paper's loop does not replace the need for human research judgment (the specialist taxonomies, knowledge files, and environment selection all require human expertise), but it offloads the **execution of the propose-edit-measure-revise cycle** once the infrastructure is set up. For a team developing a new training recipe for a domain-specific task (e.g., a medical imaging model under inference-latency constraints, a code-generation model under memory caps), the loop could run continuously on available GPU resources, exploring the recipe surface and logging proposals, while humans focus on higher-level decisions (which constraint regime to target, which external techniques to add to the knowledge files, whether the trajectory suggests a promising unexplored direction). The paper's finding that the loop runs for 48 hours producing 900 trials with no human intervention during search (Section 1) means that a team could launch the loop on Friday, review the trajectory and final recipe on Monday, and decide whether to integrate the improvements or pivot the search based on the failure patterns. The 18 trials-per-hour throughput (Section 3.4) means that overnight runs of 12 hours produce roughly 216 trials—enough to see initial improvement signals and failure patterns.

**Curriculum generation for ML engineering education.** The paper's trajectory-as-output standard produces an auditable research record that could serve as training data or curriculum material for teaching ML engineering. Each trial in the trace contains a hypothesis, a code edit, a measured outcome, and a status label—this is essentially a labeled dataset of "research moves" with ground-truth outcomes. A course on ML experimentation could use the released Parameter Golf, NanoChat, or CIFAR traces as case studies: students are shown the starting recipe, the lineage up to a certain trial index, and asked to predict what the next specialist should propose, then compare their prediction against the actual next trial and its outcome. This teaches experimental design, constraint reasoning, and failure diagnosis in a setting where the ground truth (the actual trajectory) is available for comparison. The paper's finding that the role swarm produces 134.8 effective proposal clusters with 83.7% cross-role parent edges (Table 3) means the trajectory contains substantial diversity of research strategies, not just repeated attempts at the same edit. The 13.1% architecture-audit rate (157 of 1,197 headline-run trials with architecture-domain edits, Section 4.1) provides a lower bound on how many trials involve structural changes rather than scalar tuning, ensuring the case studies include program-level reasoning.

**(Conditional) When to Prefer This Method**

The paper does not explicitly position itself against named alternatives with a decision rule. It compares the role swarm against internal controls (single generalist, generic 10-agent, no-lineage) rather than against external methods like Bayesian optimization, evolutionary search, or human-in-the-loop experimentation. The paper's contribution is a measurement methodology and feedback architecture, not a method that competes with alternatives on a performance axis. A forced "Prefer A when ... Prefer B when ..." matrix would invent tradeoffs the paper does not articulate.

However, the paper's design choices imply specific boundary conditions for when the loop is applicable. The loop requires: (a) an externally owned, quantified metric that the editable recipe cannot manipulate, (b) hard constraints that produce quantified failure feedback (exact byte excess, measured accuracy deficit, runtime slack), (c) per-trial cost bounded low enough that hundreds of trials can run within a practical search horizon (the paper uses at most ~20 minutes per trial for Parameter Golf, 90 minutes for NanoChat, under 30 seconds for CIFAR), and (d) a recipe surface that can be partitioned into non-overlapping specialist domains by a human designer before launch. These are necessary conditions rather than competitive tradeoffs—if a task lacks quantified constraint feedback or evaluator-owned measurement, the loop's core mechanism (failure-to-repair chains, cross-role lineage propagation) cannot function regardless of how it compares to alternatives. The paper's negative result on the ReST$^{EM}$ revision model (Appendix K, mentioned in passing: the ReST$^{EM}$ fine-tuning "backfired" and "substantially hurt performance") hints that the loop is sensitive to specific training-data-generation procedures, though this finding is about revision-model training rather than the loop itself and is not developed in detail.
