Here are the takeaways from this Ryan Peterman post on how software engineers can improve their writing:

**Background: code and media as leverage**

In 2018, Naval Ravikant wrote a famous thread about how to get rich without getting lucky. One idea from it changed how Ryan thinks about impact: **business leverage**—having more impact with less effort. To him, code and media stood out as the most actionable forms. He was already coding a lot, but he hadn’t thought much about creating media. The most relevant media for a software engineer is writing, because of its importance in our careers. That’s why he started caring more about it, and it’s part of why he writes his newsletter (which just hit 41,000 readers).

**Step 1: Spend extra time on the outline**

He’s thrown away many rough drafts that went off-track, and each time the root cause was unclear thinking in the outline. "Measure twice, cut once"—the outline is "the most simple and malleable form of our writing." It’s worth spending extra time here, while you can still make efficient changes, to make sure the draft will actually make sense.

**Step 2: Don’t sweat the rough draft**

It’s common to get blocked because the writing seems bad during the rough-draft phase. That’s expected—it’s *rough*. The best way to fight writer’s block is to "get all the words down without thinking about quality yet."

**Step 3: Polish until perfect**

Consider the read-to-write ratio: in most cases, your writing will be read orders of magnitude more times than it’s written. All those future readers benefit from your effort to polish. Ryan spends about **80% of his writing time on the final draft**, reading it repeatedly to "reduce clutter and make it easier to read." He uses three tools: **Grammarly** for typos and grammar, **Hemingway** for readability (he aims for ~6th-grade level), and **ChatGPT** for rewordings. It’s also more satisfying to produce something you can be proud of.

**The ultimate goal: be understood by the reader**

The writing process itself is just an implementation detail. What matters is being understood, which has two components. First, *make the reader want to read*—write in a way that’s interesting to your audience; knowing your audience is a critical skill. Second, *make your writing easy to read*: be concise ("don’t waste your reader’s mental capacity on needless words") and write simply ("don’t pick words that are more complicated than they need to be"). He recommends Paul Graham’s famous essay on writing, which explores simplicity well.

**Leading advice**

The best way to improve is to write more and ask for feedback from someone who writes well. Over time, "this feedback loop will make you an excellent writer."

---

Ryan’s entire method is a direct application of the **business leverage** idea he opens with: by front-loading clarity into the outline and back-loading effort into the 80% polish phase, he treats writing as a system where a single author’s hours pay dividends across thousands of reader-hours. His focus on the read-to-write ratio and on tools like Hemingway and Grammarly is, at bottom, an engineer’s instinct to instrument and optimize for the true KPI—being understood—rather than for the vanity metric of having written.
