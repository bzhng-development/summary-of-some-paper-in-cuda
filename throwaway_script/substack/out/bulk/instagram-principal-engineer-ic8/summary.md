Here are the takeaways from this Jake Bolam interview (grew from Staff to Principal Engineer at Instagram, led the backend rewrite affecting hundreds of teams):

**From struggle to Staff (IC6) and the "go where you're valued" lesson**

Jake joined Meta at the Staff (IC6) level and initially struggled. The first team he landed on didn't value his frontend skillset, and he found himself underwater. His advice to his younger self crystallizes this experience: **go where you're valued**. When he switched to a different Instagram team that genuinely needed and appreciated his expertise, his career trajectory changed dramatically. He went from barely treading water to thriving, and ever since, his growth accelerated through leading a massive backend rewrite. The lesson is blunt: you can be the same engineer with the same skills, but the domain and team context determines whether you'll be seen as underperforming or exceptional. The mismatch wasn't about his ability—it was about fit—and finding a team that *needed* his specific strengths unlocked everything that followed.

**The Instagram backend rewrite and the IC8 promo**

Jake was entrusted with a migration that initially spanned a handful of teams. That scope then grew progressively until it involved moving *all of Instagram's backend* to an entirely new backend system, affecting hundreds of teams across the company. The blast radius of this work was enormous—touching critical paths that, if broken, would take down core Instagram functionality for millions of users. ("This backend handled everything. You break it, you break Instagram.") Delivering the migration successfully despite that risk earned him his promotion to Principal Engineer (IC8). The pattern is clear: the promo wasn't about a single clever technical insight but about taking on a project whose *failure radius* was so large that succeeding in spite of it proved his ability to operate at the next level. He led the coordination, architecture, and execution across a sprawling organization, and the promo recognized the scope and risk rather than a particular line of code.

**The diff review philosophy: bias toward accepting**

Jake brings a deliberate, somewhat provocative philosophy to code review. His default stance on diffs is to figure out "how can I accept this diff" rather than "how can I reject this diff." If the diff isn't touching a critical path or introducing significant risk, he'll prioritize speed and give a relatively rudimentary review to keep projects moving. He's explicitly willing to **accept diffs that break prod** in non-critical areas—because those breaks surface quickly, get reverted fast, and the learning from the occasional incident is worth more than the accumulated drag of over-cautious review cycles. The principle is that unless he receives clear feedback that he's moving too fast and causing unacceptable damage, he constantly pushes the envelope on project speed. This isn't recklessness; it's a calculated trade where the cost of slowness is weighed against the cost of recoverable failures, and he biases toward velocity. He frames reviews around "can we move fast on this?" rather than an adversarial hunt for every possible edge case.

**Systems for sustainable output at IC8**

Despite the enormous responsibility of a Principal Engineer role spanning hundreds of teams, Jake works roughly 40 hours a week. He credits this to systems he's developed over his entire career, not something he hacked together reactively at the staff-plus level. The core of these systems is aggressive **meeting minimization and focus-time blocking**—he pushes back on meetings far more than the average engineer does, guarding large uninterrupted blocks of time for deep work. The framing isn't about laziness or work-life balance as an indulgence; it's about sustainability and leverage. At his level, every hour spent in a meeting has a massive opportunity cost in architectural direction, code, or mentorship that only he can provide. The discipline to protect his calendar is itself a form of senior leadership—modeling that the most impactful people are often the hardest to schedule. He treats his calendar as a finite resource and defends it with the same rigor he'd apply to a critical system.

**The VS Code note-taking system: his own personal Wikipedia**

Jake's personal knowledge management is a deliberately unstructured system that lives entirely inside a single instance of VS Code. He keeps a large collection of simple text files with no formal organization, no folder hierarchies, no tagging system. Navigation is purely keyword-based: he knows certain phrases or terms exist in certain files and jumps to them via search. Over time, he links files to each other, creating what he describes as **his own personal Wikipedia**—a web of interconnected notes that grows organically without curation overhead. The philosophy is anti-perfectionist: the energy cost of organizing notes defeats their actual purpose, which is to serve as a rapid-access external brain. The system's value increases with volume and time, as the link graph becomes denser and keyword-search more likely to surface relevant historical context. He never sits down to "build a knowledge base"; he just writes notes, links when it feels natural, and trusts that search will find what he needs later.

---

Jake's career trajectory is a loop: his **"go where you're valued"** lesson from the early struggle directly fuels the **bias toward accepting diffs** velocity philosophy—both are about removing friction and placing yourself (and your team) in contexts where impact can compound rather than fight headwinds. The **IC8 promo through blast-radius ownership** and his **meeting-minimization systems** form a complementary pair: he takes on work with enormous failure radius but defends the focused time required to execute it sustainably. Even the **VS Code personal Wikipedia** mirrors the same instinct—build a lightweight, searchable, anti-perfectionist system that grows with you rather than demanding upfront structure, trusting that speed of access beats curation. Taken together, his approach is a unified stance on engineering leadership: find the placement where your strengths count, cut everything that slows you down, own the scary thing, and keep a loose, fast external brain to stay oriented while you do it.
