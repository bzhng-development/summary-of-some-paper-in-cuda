# Distributed Speculative Inference (DSI): Speculation Parallelism for Provably Faster Lossless Language Model Inference

**ArXiv:** [2405.14105](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.14105)

## 🎯 Pitch

This paper introduces **distributed speculative inference (DSI)**, a novel inference algorithm that parallelizes speculative inference (SI) by leveraging **speculation parallelism (SP)** — a new type of task parallelism that overlaps verification with drafting across multiple instances of target and drafter models, eliminating the blocking draft-then-verify sequence of standard SI.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **distributed speculative inference (DSI)**, a novel inference algorithm that parallelizes speculative inference (SI) by leveraging **speculation parallelism (SP)** — a new type of task parallelism that overlaps verification with drafting across multiple instances of target and drafter models, eliminating the blocking draft-then-verify sequence of standard SI. Using realistic simulations across off-the-shelf LMs (StarCoder-15B, Phi3-14B, Vicuna-13B/7B) on standard benchmarks (HumanEval, MBPP, Alpaca, CNN-DM), DSI achieves 1.29–1.92× speedup over SI in single-node multi-GPU setups — and unlike SI, which slows below standard autoregressive inference when drafters are too slow or inaccurate, DSI is provably never slower than non-SI and is strictly faster than both SI and non-SI in expectation for any drafter configuration, establishing that the sequential verification bottleneck of SI can be entirely eliminated given sufficient parallelism.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Speculative Inference Has a Sequential Bottleneck That Makes It Fail Under Many Practical Conditions

This paper addresses a specific, well-defined gap in the speculative inference (SI) literature: **SI can be slower than standard autoregressive inference (non-SI) when drafters are insufficiently fast or accurate, because its draft-then-verify loop is fundamentally sequential and blocks on every target forward pass.** The paper's central observation is that this is not an implementation detail but a structural limitation of the SI paradigm that had not been recognized or solved prior to this work.

The mechanism of this failure is straightforward. SI works by having a fast drafter model propose several tokens ahead, then having the target model verify all those proposals in parallel using batching. If the drafter is accurate, many draft tokens are accepted, reducing the number of expensive target forward passes needed. The speedup formula is:

> "As long as the total drafting latency is less than the saved latency from reduced target forwards, SI offers a speedup over non-SI."

The problem is what happens when this condition is violated. If the drafter is too slow (e.g., 30%+ of target latency) or too inaccurate (low acceptance rate), the overhead of generating draft tokens — which are then rejected — outweighs the savings from fewer target forwards. In these regimes, SI is **worse than doing nothing** — i.e., slower than standard autoregressive decoding. The paper puts this precisely (Section 3):

> "SI with sufficiently slow or inaccurate drafters is slower than non-SI, even if reducing the number of target forwards."

This failure mode is not a corner case. Many realistic deployment scenarios involve drafters with substantial relative latency or modest acceptance rates. For example, when using a smaller model from the same family as a drafter, the drafter may still be 30–70% of the target's latency (as shown in Table 2 for Phi3-4B vs. Phi3-14B, where the drafter is 65–67% of the target latency). SI offers no speedup — or a slowdown — in such configurations, which means the technique has a narrow practical operating window that constrains which drafter-target pairs can be used.

### Why This Problem Matters: Broader Applicability, Guaranteed Acceleration, and Hardware Utilization

This gap has three significant implications that motivate the paper:

**First, it restricts which LMs can benefit from speculative inference.** Prior work on SI has focused almost exclusively on configurations where drafters are extremely fast (1–5% of target latency) and moderately to highly accurate (60%+ acceptance). The paper's own survey of prior empirical results in SI (Leviathan et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2023; Miao et al., 2024) reports speedups in precisely this regime. But many realistic model pairs fall outside it. A user who wants to accelerate inference of a 14B-parameter model with a 4B-parameter drafter from the same family might find that SI offers no benefit because the drafter's relative latency (~65% in the Phi3 case from Table 2) is too high to overcome verification overhead. The paper explicitly frames this as a **practical adoption barrier**:

> "SI relies on a sequential draft-then-verify process, where each verification must be completed before drafting new tokens. As a result, SI is beneficial only if the drafters are sufficiently fast and accurate."

**Second, SI's failure in slow-drafter regimes means that existing hardware is underutilized.** When SI is slower than non-SI, the rational user simply falls back to autoregressive decoding, leaving additional GPUs idle. Multi-GPU setups are increasingly common (single nodes with 4–8 GPUs are standard in cloud instances and research clusters), but SI in its standard form cannot productively use more than two GPUs — one for the target, one for the drafter — and even that pair may yield a net slowdown. The paper identifies this as a resource allocation problem: available parallelism is not being converted into latency reduction.

**Third, the sequential verification bottleneck limits theoretical guarantees.** Prior SI methods provide no guarantee of speedup — they offer empirical speedup only under favorable conditions. The absence of any worst-case or expected-case speedup guarantee means that deploying SI always carries a risk of making inference slower, not faster, which is problematic for latency-sensitive production systems. The paper positions this as a gap between the theoretical understanding of SI and its practical deployment: we understand *when* SI works empirically, but we lack a paradigm that *guarantees* it never hurts and always helps.

### Prior Approaches and Where They Fall Short

The paper situates itself within a rich ecosystem of SI research and adjacent techniques. Understanding why each prior approach fails to address the sequential bottleneck is essential to appreciating DSI's contribution.

**Standard speculative inference (Leviathan et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2023; Miao et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2025; Timor et al., 2025).** These works all share the same fundamental orchestration pattern: draft $k$ tokens, verify them in one batch on the target, accept some prefix, repeat. The key structural property is that **the next iteration cannot begin until the current verification completes**. This is the "blocking" nature of SI that the paper identifies as the root cause of the failure mode. Prior work in this family has improved various aspects — dynamic lookahead selection (Mamou et al., 2024), tree-based verification (Miao et al., 2024), lossless verification for heterogeneous vocabularies (Timor et al., 2025), block verification for efficiency (Sun et al., 2025) — but none has altered the fundamental sequential structure. The drafter is always idle during verification, and the target is always idle during drafting. This idle time is the resource DSI exploits.

**PEARL (Liu et al., 2025).** This is the closest prior work to DSI and the only SI variant that attempts to overlap drafting with verification. PEARL drafts the next iteration's tokens while the current iteration is being verified, using a heuristic to control whether to verify only the first draft token or more. The paper identifies three critical limitations of PEARL that DSI addresses:

1. **PEARL is still fundamentally sequential in its scope of parallelism.** It can only process tokens belonging to the *next* SI iteration, not tokens at arbitrary future positions. DSI, in contrast, can process tokens at any future position because it maintains multiple concurrent verification tasks across many iterations (Section 5):

> "PEARL remains a sequential algorithm because it can only process tokens of the next SI iteration, unlike DSI, which can process tokens of any future iteration."

2. **PEARL provides no theoretical speedup guarantees.** It uses a heuristic for when to parallelize and offers no proof that it is faster than SI or non-SI. In fact, PEARL shares SI's failure mode: it can be slower than non-SI if drafters are too slow or inaccurate. DSI is proved to be strictly faster than both SI and non-SI in expectation.

3. **PEARL cannot scale beyond one target instance and one drafter instance.** It can only orchestrate two GPUs total, leaving additional hardware completely unutilized. DSI can orchestrate an arbitrary number of GPUs (≥2) by tuning the `lookahead` hyperparameter.

In essence, PEARL takes a partial step toward overlapping verification and drafting, but it doesn't fully parallelize the verification process, doesn't provide guarantees, and doesn't generalize to more than two processing units. DSI addresses all three limitations.

**Model parallelism (TP, PP, DP).** These techniques (tensor parallelism, pipeline parallelism, data parallelism) speed up individual forward passes by distributing the computation of each forward across multiple processors. The paper distinguishes these from speculation parallelism (SP) along a critical dimension: MP methods are constrained by Amdahl's law on the inherently sequential portion of autoregressive generation. Since each token depends on all previous tokens, the autoregressive loop itself cannot be parallelized by model parallelism — only the computation *within* each forward pass can be distributed. SI attacks a different part of the problem (reducing the *number* of target forwards) and DSI goes further by hiding the latency of the target forwards that remain. The paper explicitly notes:

> "Since the portion of the inference that could be parallelized is small (if any) in typical LMs, the potential speedup of MP methods is limited, by Amdahl's law."

Critically, DSI is orthogonal to MP — it orchestrates *which models run when*, not *how each forward pass is computed internally*. They can be combined (Section 3.1):

> "DSI could be naturally combined with MP to accelerate the underlying forwards, requiring no changes to the algorithm, because DSI offers an orchestration algorithm agnostic to the underlying computation of forwards."

**Model compression and architectural modifications.** The paper surveys a broad category of techniques — pruning, quantization, knowledge distillation, low-rank factorization, early exiting, alternative architectures — that make models run faster by changing the model itself. These techniques have two fundamental limitations compared to DSI: they typically **degrade output quality** (compression is lossy), and they often **require retraining or architectural changes**. DSI, like all SI variants, is **lossless** — it preserves the target model's output distribution exactly — and operates on **frozen models** without any modification. The paper positions these as complementary categories: compression reduces per-forward latency, SI/DSI reduces the number of forwards or hides their latency.

**Kernel optimizations (FlashAttention, etc.).** These accelerate individual attention operations through hardware-aware implementations. Like MP, they speed up each forward pass but do not address the sequential structure of autoregressive generation. They are also hardware-specific.

### How DSI Positions Itself Relative to Existing Work

DSI's contribution is best understood not as a new verification method or a new drafter training technique, but as a **new orchestration layer** that sits *above* existing SI methods and transforms them from sequential to parallel algorithms. The paper draws an explicit analogy to Amdahl's law to illustrate the theoretical opportunity:

> "Assume the drafter is perfectly accurate. In that case, our method hides all the verification latency such that the overall end-to-end latency remains only the drafting latency. For drafters of 1-5% latency and lookahead ∈ [2, 5], our proposed parallelization leads to a theoretical speedup of 4x-50x compared to SI."

This is an upper bound, but it illustrates the scale of the gap that DSI closes. The actual empirical speedups (1.29–1.92× over SI in Table 2) are smaller because real drafters have modest acceptance rates and moderate relative latencies, but the key point is that DSI achieves these gains *in configurations where SI itself provides little or no benefit*.

The paper introduces **speculation parallelism (SP)** as a first-class parallelism type, on par with tensor parallelism and pipeline parallelism, but addressing a different bottleneck (Section 3.1):

> "DSI offers a new type of task parallelism we name speculation parallelism (SP) that orchestrates instances of the target and drafters to overlap in time."

This framing is significant because it establishes a new **tradeoff between computational resources and latency**. Before DSI, adding more GPUs to an inference setup offered diminishing returns for latency reduction (because the autoregressive loop is sequential). DSI changes this: with sufficient SP degree, verification latency can be completely hidden, and adding more GPUs directly reduces end-to-end latency. The paper formalizes this tradeoff through Equation 1, which relates target latency, drafter latency, lookahead, and SP degree:

$$\left\lceil \frac{\text{(target latency)}}{\text{(lookahead)} \cdot \text{(drafter latency)}} \right\rceil \leq \text{SP}$$

This inequality determines the minimum lookahead needed to avoid verification tasks waiting for a target server, given a fixed SP degree (number of available GPUs minus one for the drafter). It establishes DSI as a **principled orchestration framework** rather than an ad-hoc parallelization heuristic — unlike PEARL's heuristic approach.

Finally, the paper positions DSI as closing a theoretical gap that had gone unaddressed despite the rapid practical adoption of SI. The existence of SI algorithms that are "lossless in expectation" (preserve the target distribution) was well-established by prior work (Leviathan et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2023). DSI extends this line by proving that losslessness can be combined with a *guaranteed* speedup: DSI is never slower than non-SI (Theorem 1) and is strictly faster than both SI and non-SI in expectation (Theorem 2, Proposition 1). These are not empirical observations but formal proofs under stated assumptions, marking a qualitative advance from "SI sometimes helps" to "DSI always helps."

The paper's positioning can be summarized as: **DSI is to SI what SI is to non-SI** — a meta-level acceleration that can be applied on top of any existing SI method, requiring no model changes, preserving losslessness, and converting the speculative speedup from a conditional empirical benefit into an unconditional theoretical guarantee.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation (Approachable Technical Breakdown)

This paper develops a **distributed orchestration algorithm** — a set of rules for scheduling computation across multiple GPUs — that accelerates speculative inference by running verification and drafting concurrently instead of one after the other. The problem it solves is that standard speculative inference (SI) blocks on verification: drafting cannot resume until the target model finishes checking all proposed tokens, which means SI is *slower* than autoregressive inference when drafters are too slow or inaccurate, and idle hardware accumulates during the blocking periods. The solution's shape is a **non-blocking version of SI** where multiple instances of the target model run verification tasks in parallel with ongoing drafting, so that a verification finishing late never stalls new drafts — effectively hiding the verification latency behind useful computation.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The system has four major components:

1. **A target model instance (`$f_m$`)** — the large language model whose output distribution must be preserved. This is the authoritative source of correct tokens. Multiple copies of `$f_m$` run concurrently on separate GPUs, forming a **target server pool**.
2. **Drafter model instances (`$f_1, \ldots, f_{m-1}$`)** — faster, approximate models that propose likely next tokens. These run continuously on dedicated GPUs, never waiting for verification to complete.
3. **A thread orchestration layer** — the core of DSI (Algorithm 1). It spawns computation threads, labels one thread as the "current verifier" (the authoritative thread whose output defines the correct token), and terminates threads whose draft tokens don't match the verifier's output.
4. **A speculation parallelism (SP) degree controller** — a mechanism that, given a fixed number of available GPUs, selects the `lookahead` hyperparameter (number of draft tokens per verification batch) to ensure that verification tasks never queue for a target server, enforcing the inequality `$\lceil \frac{\text{target latency}}{\text{lookahead} \cdot \text{drafter latency}} \rceil \leq \text{SP}$`.

Information flows as a continuously expanding tree: a prompt enters → all `$m$` models (drafters + target) generate candidate first tokens concurrently → for each candidate token, new threads are spawned to generate the next token from all `$m$` models → the verifier thread (always a target model instance) establishes the ground-truth token → threads producing different tokens are terminated along with their descendants → a new verifier is designated → the process repeats, with drafting and verification overlapping in time.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the formal thread model and notation, because the entire algorithm is expressed in terms of concurrent threads and their parent-child relationships — understanding this notation is prerequisite to reading Algorithm 1.
- **Second**, Algorithm 1 (the orchestration core) in detail, walking through each numbered line, because the algorithm is the paper's central contribution and everything else (lookahead tuning, theoretical guarantees, empirical results) builds on it.
- **Third**, speculation parallelism (SP) and the lookahead mechanism, because these are the practical knobs that make DSI deployable on a fixed number of GPUs — without them, Algorithm 1 assumes infinite hardware — and they establish the resource-latency tradeoff.
- **Fourth**, the acceptance and termination mechanics, because DSI's correctness (losslessness) and speedup both depend on precisely how and when incorrect threads are killed.
- **Fifth**, the relationship to model parallelism (MP), because this clarifies why SP is a genuinely new parallelism type rather than a rebranding of existing techniques.
- **Sixth**, the KV cache management story, because DSI's token tree structure requires careful cache handling that prior work (SpecInfer) has already solved, and understanding this confirms DSI's deployability.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily an **algorithm design paper** whose core idea is that the verification step in speculative inference can be fully parallelized by running multiple target model instances concurrently, transforming SI from a sequential draft-then-verify loop into a non-blocking algorithm where verification latency is hidden behind ongoing drafting.

#### The Thread Model and Notation

DSI is expressed in a formal concurrent computation model. The paper defines a **thread** as "a hardware component capable of executing threads" — essentially, a unit of computation that runs a forward pass of a model and potentially samples a token. Threads can be initiated, terminated, and can spawn child threads. Multiple threads run concurrently and may overlap in time.

A thread is identified by a tuple `$J = (j_1, \ldots, j_k)$` that encodes the sequence of model indices used to reach that thread's computation. For example, `$C_{(1)}$` is the thread computing `$f_1(x_{\leq 0})$` — the first drafter's output token given the initial prompt. `$C_{(1,2)}$` is the thread computing `$f_2(x_{\leq 0} \oplus x_1^1)$` — the second model's output token given the prompt plus the first drafter's token. In general, `$C_{(j_1, \ldots, j_k)}$` computes `$f_{j_k}$` on the prompt concatenated with tokens generated by models `$j_1, \ldots, j_{k-1}$` in sequence.

Each thread has three key properties:

- **`$C_J[\text{prompt}]$`** — the input sequence (prompt plus all previously generated tokens along this thread's ancestry).
- **`$C_J[\text{prob}]$`** — the output probability vector after computing the forward pass (the result of applying softmax to the logits).
- **`$C_J[\text{new}]$`** — a token sampled from `$C_J[\text{prob}]$`.
- **`$C_J[\text{return}]$`** — the concatenation of `$C_J[\text{prompt}]$` and `$C_J[\text{new}]$`, which is the thread's final output.

A thread that finishes and is not terminated "returns" its output. A new thread spawned by `$C_J$` is denoted `$C_{J \oplus (j)}$`, where `$J \oplus (j)$` is the tuple `$J$` with `$j$` appended. The set of all threads originating from `$C_J$` is `$\{C_{J \oplus J'} : J' \text{ is a nonempty tuple}\}$`. Critically, terminating a thread also terminates all its descendants — this cascading termination is what keeps DSI from wasting computation on already-rejected token paths.

Time is measured as **wall time**:

$$T_{\text{wall}}[\{C_J\}_{J \in \mathcal{J}}] = \max_{J \in \mathcal{J}} (\text{Timepoint } C_J \text{ finishes}) - \min_{J \in \mathcal{J}} (\text{Timepoint } C_J \text{ starts})$$

where `$\mathcal{J}$` is a set of thread tuples. This is the standard definition: wall time for a set of concurrent threads is the interval from the earliest start to the latest finish. For a single thread, we write `$T_{\text{wall}}[C_J]$`.

Three formal assumptions underpin the theoretical results:

**Assumption 1 (bounded computation, zero sampling):** There exists a universal constant `$c > 0$` such that for any prompt and model index, `$T_{\text{wall}}[\text{computing } f_j(x_{\leq 0})] \in (0, c)$` and `$T_{\text{wall}}[\text{sampling } x \sim f_j(x_{\leq 0})] = 0$`. In plain language: every forward pass takes some finite, bounded time; token sampling from the output distribution is instantaneous relative to computation.

**Assumption 2 (drafters are strictly faster):** For all drafter indices `$j \in [m-1]$`, the maximum time to compute `$f_j$` on any input is less than or equal to the minimum time to compute the target `$f_m$` on any input. Formally: `$\max_{x_{\leq 0}} T_{\text{wall}}[\text{computing } f_j(x_{\leq 0})] \leq \min_{x_{\leq 0}} T_{\text{wall}}[\text{computing } f_m(x_{\leq 0})]$`. This guarantees that a drafter's output for a given position is available before the target's output for the same position — a necessary condition for the verifier-based termination to be meaningful.

**Assumption 3 (no scheduling delays):** Computing a sequence of tokens `$x_k^{j_1,\ldots,j_k}$` takes exactly the sum of the times to compute each token sequentially, with no delays between them. Formally: `$T_{\text{wall}}[\{C_{(j_1,\ldots,j_i)}\}_{i=1}^k] = \sum_{i=1}^k T_{\text{wall}}[C_{(j_1,\ldots,j_i)}]$`. This abstracts away real-world thread management overhead (context switching, scheduling) to isolate the algorithmic properties.

These assumptions define the idealized setting in which the theoretical guarantees hold. The empirical evaluation relaxes them by running actual multithreading systems that incur real OS-level overhead.

#### Algorithm 1: The DSI Orchestration Core

Algorithm 1 is the central contribution. It computes `$N$` output tokens autoregressively from target model `$f_m$` given prompt `$x_{\leq 0}$`, using drafter models `$f_1, \ldots, f_{m-1}$`. The algorithm is presented as an event-driven procedure: actions trigger when any thread finishes computing a token.

**Initialization (Lines 1–3).**

The algorithm begins by setting a counter `$v = 1$` (which tracks how many target-verified tokens have been generated) and spawning `$m$` concurrent threads `$C_{(1)}, \ldots, C_{(m)}$`. Thread `$C_{(j_1)}$` generates the token `$x_1^{j_1} \sim f_{j_1}(x_{\leq 0})$` — that is, each of the `$m$` models independently computes its prediction for the first output token given the prompt. Thread `$C_{(m)}$` (the target model) is labeled as the **current verifier**. The verifier is the authoritative thread: its output defines the "correct" token against which all other threads are checked.

This initialization establishes the fundamental pattern: all models (drafters and target) run the same computation in parallel, but only the target's output is trusted as ground truth.

**Main event loop (Lines 4–20).**

The algorithm enters an event-driven loop: **"ONCE any thread `$C_{J \oplus (j)}$` finishes to generate a token"** triggers a block of logic. The thread has completed both its forward pass and token sampling, producing `$C_{J \oplus (j)}[\text{new}] \sim f_j(C_{J \oplus (j)}[\text{prompt}])$`.

**Spawning descendants (Lines 5–6).** If the current token position is not the final one (`$|J| + 1 < N$`), the algorithm immediately spawns `$m$` new threads: `$C_{J \oplus (j,1)}, C_{J \oplus (j,2)}, \ldots, C_{J \oplus (j,m)}$`. Each of these new threads generates a token from one of the `$m$` models, taking the concatenation of the parent thread's prompt and its newly generated token as input. This spawning happens **regardless of whether the parent is a drafter or the target** — every token spawns `$m$` speculative continuations.

This is the key departure from SI. In standard SI, drafting pauses while verification runs, and new drafts are only spawned after verification completes. In DSI, new threads are spawned immediately upon any token generation, creating a continuously expanding tree of concurrent computations. The tree grows breadth-wise (`$m$` children per node) and depth-wise (new tokens appended to accepted paths). The tree is pruned only by termination (Lines 8 and 10), not by waiting.

**Verifier-based termination (Lines 7–11).** If the finishing thread `$C_{J \oplus (j)}$` is the current verifier thread, the algorithm performs two cleanup operations:

1. **Exact-match termination (Line 8):** Terminate all threads `$C_{J \oplus (j')}$` (and their descendants) that sampled a different token than the verifier. That is, if `$C_{J \oplus (j')}[\text{new}] \neq C_{J \oplus (j)}[\text{new}]$`, kill `$C_{J \oplus (j')}$` and everything that descends from it. These threads were computing continuations based on an incorrect token and are now wasted work.

2. **Redundancy elimination (Line 10):** Among threads that did match the verifier's token, keep only the one with the smallest model index. Formally, let `$j^* = \arg\min_{j' \in [m]} \{j' \mid C_{J \oplus (j')}[\text{new}] = C_{J \oplus (j)}[\text{new}]\}$`. Terminate all matching threads with higher indices. This prevents duplicate identical computations: if both `$C_{(1)}$` and `$C_{(3)}$` produced the correct token `$x_1$`, only `$C_{(1)}$` survives. The index ordering ensures deterministic pruning.

**Verifier reassignment (Lines 11–15).** After pruning, thread `$C_{J \oplus (j^*, m)}$` is labeled as the new current verifier. This is the thread that takes the accepted token path (indexed by the smallest matching model `$j^*$`) and then calls the target model `$f_m$` on that path. Since `$j^*$` was verified to produce the correct token at this position, and `$f_m$` is the target model, `$C_{J \oplus (j^*, m)}$`'s output will be the correct next token — so it is the natural next authority.

The counter `$v$` increments to `$v + 1$`, tracking that another position has been verified.

Line 13 handles an important edge case: if `$C_{J \oplus (j^*, m)}$` has **already finished** by the time it is labeled as verifier (because the target forward for that path was spawned earlier and completed during the verification of the current position), the algorithm immediately jumps back to the verifier logic at Line 7 with this new verifier. This "cascading verification" handles the scenario where multiple target forwards have completed concurrently, allowing the algorithm to verify several positions in rapid succession without waiting.

**Termination condition (Lines 17–19).** If the finishing thread has reached position `$N$` (the `$|J| + 1 = N$` case) and it is a target model thread (`$j = m$`), the algorithm returns the thread's output. This is the final verified sequence of `$N$` tokens.

**The algorithmic invariant.** Throughout execution, exactly one thread is labeled as the current verifier. This thread's output, when it finishes, defines the correct token at its position. All other threads are speculative: they *might* produce the correct token (in which case they and their descendants survive), or they might diverge (in which case they are terminated). The invariant ensures that termination is always clean — the algorithm never has to "undo" accepted tokens — and that the final output is guaranteed to match what `$f_m$` would have produced autoregressively.

**Why this design.** The core insight is that verification does not need to block drafting. In standard SI, the target forward determines which draft tokens are accepted, and only then can the next iteration's drafts begin — because those drafts build on the accepted prefix. DSI breaks this dependency by allowing drafts to be spawned *speculatively on unverified tokens*. If a draft was built on a token that later gets rejected, that draft and all its descendants are simply terminated. The cost of this speculation is wasted computation on incorrect paths; the benefit is that correct paths can be explored concurrently, without waiting for verification. Since drafters are faster than the target (Assumption 2), many draft tokens are generated in the time it takes for one verification, so the "pipeline" stays full.

#### Speculation Parallelism and the Lookahead Hyperparameter

Algorithm 1 as presented spawns a new target verification thread (`$C_{J \oplus (j, m)}$`) every time *any* thread finishes generating a token — including drafter threads. With `$\text{lookahead} = 1$` (the implicit setting in Algorithm 1), this means every single draft token generates a verification task that must be processed by a target server. If drafters are much faster than the target, verification tasks would accumulate faster than target servers can process them, requiring an unbounded number of target servers (SP degree → ∞).

**The lookahead mechanism** addresses this by batching multiple draft tokens into each verification task. For `$\text{lookahead} = k > 1$`, Lines 2 and 6 of Algorithm 1 are modified to:

- Spawn `$m-1$` drafter threads that each generate `$k$` tokens (not 1) and 1 target thread that generates 1 token.

In operational terms: instead of verifying every single draft token individually, the target verifies a batch of `$k$` draft tokens at once. This is the same batching optimization used in standard SI, but with a new purpose: controlling the rate at which verification tasks are sent to target servers.

**The resource constraint inequality.** Given a fixed SP degree (number of available target servers), the minimum `lookahead` that ensures verification tasks never wait is:

$$\left\lceil \frac{\text{target latency}}{\text{lookahead} \cdot \text{drafter latency}} \right\rceil \leq \text{SP}$$

where target latency is the time for one target forward pass, drafter latency is the time for one drafter forward pass, and `$\lceil \cdot \rceil$` is the ceiling function.

**What this inequality computes:** For a given `lookahead`, the ratio `$\frac{\text{target latency}}{\text{lookahead} \cdot \text{drafter latency}}$` is the number of verification tasks that can be issued in the time it takes *one* verification task to complete. For example, if target latency is 30ms, drafter latency is 3ms, and `lookahead = 5`, then `$\text{lookahead} \cdot \text{drafter latency} = 15\text{ms}$`, and `$\frac{30}{15} = 2$`. This means that, on average, 2 verification tasks are issued during each verification. If SP ≥ 2, there will always be a free target server when a new verification task is ready — no queuing. If SP = 1, verification tasks would pile up, and the algorithm would degrade to sequential SI.

**Why this form:** The ceiling ensures integer SP degree. The inequality direction ensures that the number of concurrent verification tasks in flight never exceeds the number of available target servers. The `lookahead` in the denominator captures the key tradeoff: larger `lookahead` means fewer, larger verification batches, reducing the required SP degree but also reducing the frequency of verification (which means rejections are detected later, wasting more speculative work on incorrect paths).

**Selecting the optimal lookahead.** The paper states:

> "selecting the minimum lookahead value that satisfies Equation 1 is the optimal choice, allowing DSI to detect rejections (line 8) earlier."

Smaller `lookahead` means verification happens more frequently, catching incorrect drafts sooner and minimizing wasted computation on descendants of rejected tokens. The optimal configuration is therefore: **given your available GPUs, compute the SP degree (GPUs minus one for the drafter), then select the smallest `lookahead` satisfying the inequality.** If more GPUs are available than needed, you can reduce `lookahead` to detect rejections faster.

**Maximum useful SP degree.** There is a natural ceiling: using more target servers than `$\lceil \frac{\text{target latency}}{\text{drafter latency}} \rceil$` provides no further speedup because there won't be enough verification tasks to keep all servers busy. Beyond this point, additional GPUs should be used for model parallelism on the target or drafter, or left idle.

**Example from the paper.** The paper provides a concrete configuration: drafter at 5% latency, SP = 4, `lookahead = 5`. The inequality: `$\lceil \frac{1}{5 \cdot 0.05} \rceil = \lceil 4 \rceil = 4 \leq 4$` ✓. The maximum number of processing units required is `$1 + \lceil \frac{1}{5 \cdot 0.05} \rceil = 1 + 4 = 5$`. With 8 GPUs available, you could reduce `lookahead` to 3: `$\lceil \frac{1}{3 \cdot 0.05} \rceil = \lceil 6.67 \rceil = 7 \leq 7$`, using 8 total GPUs and detecting rejections roughly 40% faster (every 3 drafts instead of every 5).

**Resource contention.** The paper addresses a practical concern: if multiple verification tasks complete simultaneously and compete for the same target server, resource contention (memory bandwidth, data transfer, CPU orchestration) could degrade performance. The inequality with minimal `lookahead` naturally avoids this because verification requests are sent at staggered times (each triggered by a drafter completing `lookahead` tokens), so responses arrive staggered — "the verification requests are sent to the target server at different times, and the responses are expected in staggered timings" (Section 3.1).

#### Acceptance Rate, Rejection, and Termination Mechanics

The term **acceptance rate** is central to understanding DSI's speedup. Given a target, a drafter, and an input prompt, the acceptance rate is the probability that a draft token matches what the target would have generated at that position. The paper uses exact-match verification (token identity) as the baseline: a draft token is accepted if it equals the verifier's token, rejected otherwise.

**How acceptance determines speedup.** When a draft token is accepted, the target forward pass that *would* have been needed to generate that token is saved — the drafter's output was correct, so we got a token without paying target latency. In standard SI, every accepted draft token reduces the number of target forwards by one. In DSI, an accepted draft token means the target forward that verified it was executed *concurrently* with ongoing drafting, so its latency was hidden — it didn't contribute to end-to-end wall time. The expected fraction of verification work that DSI hides is `$p^{\text{lookahead}}$`, where `$p$` is the acceptance rate. This is because a verification task of `lookahead` tokens only affects latency if *all* `lookahead` tokens were accepted (otherwise the rejection triggers a synchronization that makes the verification latency visible). For `$\text{lookahead} = 2$` and `$p = 0.8$`, only `$1 - 0.8^2 = 36\%$` of target forwards contribute to latency (Section 3.1).

**The termination cascade (why DSI remains lossless).** Lines 8 and 10 implement a strict correctness mechanism. When the verifier thread finishes, it establishes the authoritative token at that position. Any thread that diverged from this token (Line 8) or that duplicated it with a suboptimal model index (Line 10) is terminated, along with all its descendants. This means:

- **No incorrect token ever appears in the final output.** The verifier is always a target model thread, so its output is by definition the token `$f_m$` would have produced.
- **Wasted computation is bounded.** Threads built on incorrect drafts are killed as soon as the verifier detects the divergence. The computation invested in those threads is lost, but it never corrupts the output.
- **The tree is pruned to a single path.** At each position, exactly one thread survives (the one with the smallest model index among those matching the verifier). This means the final output is a single linear sequence, not a branching structure.

**Relaxed rejection and losslessness in expectation.** The paper notes that strict exact-matching can be replaced with **relaxed rejection methods** from prior work (Leviathan et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2023; Miao et al., 2024; Timor et al., 2025) that increase the acceptance rate while maintaining the target distribution — i.e., lossless in expectation. These methods accept draft tokens probabilistically based on the ratio of target and drafter probabilities, rather than requiring exact token match. The DSI orchestration algorithm is agnostic to which rejection criterion is used; any lossless verification procedure can be plugged into Lines 8 and 10. The paper's empirical evaluation uses exact-match acceptance rates for simplicity, but the framework supports more sophisticated schemes.

**Acceptance rate estimation for experiments.** The paper estimates acceptance rates via a separate experiment: for each `(target, drafter, dataset)` triple, generate 256 tokens from both models, count the longest common prefix, and fit a geometric distribution:

$$\text{acceptance rate} = 1 - \frac{1}{1 + \bar{n}}$$

where `$\bar{n} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N n_i$` is the average number of accepted draft tokens per prompt across `$N$` prompts.

**What this computes:** Given the empirical mean `$\bar{n}$` of a geometrically distributed random variable with success probability `$p$` (where "success" is rejection and "failure" is acceptance), the mean of a geometric distribution counting failures before first success is `$\frac{1-p}{p}$`. Setting `$\bar{n} = \frac{1-p}{p}$` and solving for `$p$` gives the formula. In plain language: if on average 4 draft tokens are accepted before one is rejected, the acceptance rate is `$1 - \frac{1}{1+4} = 0.8$` (80%).

**Why this form:** The i.i.d. assumption (each token acceptance is independent with constant probability) is a standard simplification in the SI literature. The paper cites Mamou et al. (2024) for empirical evidence that the number of accepted tokens per iteration actually follows a geometric distribution in practice, validating the assumption.

#### Relationship to Model Parallelism

The paper carefully distinguishes speculation parallelism (SP) from model parallelism (MP), establishing SP as a genuinely new category of parallelism.

**Model parallelism** (TP, PP, or combinations) speeds up individual forward passes by distributing the computation of *one* forward pass across multiple processors. For example, tensor parallelism (TP) splits weight matrices across GPUs so that matrix multiplications run in parallel. Pipeline parallelism (PP) splits layers across GPUs so that different layers process different micro-batches simultaneously.

**Speculation parallelism** does something qualitatively different: it runs *multiple independent forward passes concurrently*, not parts of a single forward pass. Each target server in DSI computes a complete forward pass of `$f_m$` on a different input (different prompt prefix corresponding to a different speculative path in the token tree). These forwards are computationally independent — they share no intermediate results, require no communication during the forward pass, and can be scheduled completely independently.

**Why SP can outperform MP at equal hardware.** The paper provides a concrete comparison: drafter at 10% latency, `lookahead = 2`, SP = 5 (6 GPUs — 5 target servers + 1 drafter). With 80% acceptance rate, only `$1 - 0.8^2 = 36\%$` of target forwards contribute to latency. This means DSI effectively eliminates 64% of the target forward latency without any acceleration of individual forwards. To match this with MP alone (MP = 5), each target forward would need to be accelerated by `$\frac{1}{1-0.64} \approx 2.78\times$`. However, "MP is ineffective for certain hardware setups, model architectures and sizes, while DSI remains effective" — MP speedup is limited by communication overhead and the fraction of computation that is inherently sequential (Amdahl's law on the forward pass itself), while DSI's speedup comes from hiding latency through concurrent work, which has no such theoretical ceiling.

**Combining SP and MP.** The paper emphasizes that DSI is an orchestration layer *above* the forward pass computation. "DSI could be naturally combined with MP to accelerate the underlying forwards, requiring no changes to the algorithm." Each target server could itself use TP or PP across multiple GPUs. In a multi-node setup, DSI could orchestrate across nodes, with each node using internal MP for its model instances. The two parallelism types are orthogonal and multiplicative in their effects.

#### KV Cache Management

DSI generates a **token tree** — a branching structure where each node is a token, each edge is a model's prediction, and different paths represent different speculative futures. At runtime, this tree is continuously expanded (new tokens are drafted) and pruned (incorrect branches are terminated). The key implementation challenge is managing the KV cache for this tree efficiently across multiple GPUs.

The paper's position is that this is a **solved problem**, not a new challenge introduced by DSI. The tree-based KV cache management developed in SpecInfer (Miao et al., 2024) handles exactly this scenario: "tree paths can share common prefixes." When multiple speculative paths share the same prefix, the KV cache entries for that prefix are computed once and reused across all paths. Only the divergent suffixes require separate cache entries.

In DSI, each server (target or drafter) maintains its own KV cache. Servers collaboratively process the token tree, sharing common prefixes. Synchronization points occur only at draft rejections — when a rejection happens, the affected branches are pruned, and the KV cache entries for those branches can be freed. Between rejections, each server operates independently on its assigned speculative path.

The paper states that "practitioners can apply SpecInfer's KV cache management as-is to achieve the expected speedups reported in this paper" and characterizes the KV cache issue as requiring "some engineering effort" but being a "solved research problem" with "negligible latency" overhead. This is a pragmatic stance: the paper's contribution is the orchestration algorithm, and it defers the implementation-level KV cache optimization to known techniques rather than reinventing them.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Speculation Parallelism as a New Parallelism Category That Eliminates the Sequential Bottleneck of Speculative Inference

Prior to DSI, the field organized parallelism for language model inference around two axes: **model parallelism** (TP, PP, DP — distributing the computation *within* each forward pass across processors) and **speculative inference** (reducing the *number* of target forwards by using drafters). These were viewed as independent, additive optimizations: MP accelerates each forward, SI reduces how many forwards are needed. No prior work had recognized that the **verification step in SI constitutes a third, separable bottleneck** that admits its own parallelism — one that is qualitatively different from both MP and SI.

The field's default assumption was that the draft-then-verify loop was *inherently* sequential — that verification must complete before drafting can resume because drafts depend on the verified prefix. PEARL (Liu et al., 2025) made a partial challenge to this by overlapping the *next* iteration's drafting with the current verification, but it preserved the sequential structure in a weaker form: it could only advance one iteration ahead, and it offered no theoretical speedup guarantee. No prior work had asked: **can verification be made non-blocking entirely, such that drafting never waits for verification regardless of how many iterations ahead the speculation extends?**

DSI's conceptual contribution is to identify that the dependency between drafting and verification is not a logical necessity — it is an algorithmic choice. The dependency exists because standard SI *constructs* the next drafts from the verified prefix. DSI breaks this by allowing drafts to be built speculatively on *unverified* tokens, with a termination mechanism (cascading kill of descendants on rejection) that guarantees correctness even when speculation extends arbitrarily far ahead. This transforms verification from a *blocking synchronization point* into a *background cleanup process*. The paper names this new capability **speculation parallelism (SP)** and positions it as a third foundational parallelism type alongside tensor and pipeline parallelism: "DSI offers a new type of task parallelism we name speculation parallelism (SP) that orchestrates instances of the target and drafters to overlap in time" (Section 3.1).

The significance of this framing extends beyond the specific algorithm. By establishing SP as a first-class parallelism dimension, the paper creates a new **resource-latency tradeoff surface**. Before DSI, adding more GPUs to an inference setup offered rapidly diminishing returns for latency reduction because the autoregressive loop is sequential (Amdahl's law on the generation process itself). DSI changes this: with sufficient SP degree, verification latency can be completely hidden, and adding GPUs directly reduces end-to-end latency by increasing the pool of concurrent verifiers. This tradeoff is formalized through Equation 1, which relates target latency, drafter latency, lookahead, and SP degree — the first quantitative resource model for inference-time parallelism that goes beyond MP.

This is a **fundamental** shift, not an incremental refinement, because it identifies a bottleneck (sequential verification) that the field had not previously conceptualized as parallelizable, and it provides both an algorithm and a theoretical framework forparallelizing it. The proof that DSI is strictly faster than both SI and non-SI in expectation (Theorem 2, Proposition 1) — not just empirically but provably, under minimal assumptions — converts speculative speedup from a conditional empirical observation to an unconditional guarantee, which is a qualitative step beyond all prior SI work.

### Innovation 2: Proving That SI's Failure Mode (Slower Than Non-SI) Is Not Inherent — It Is an Artifact of Sequential Orchestration

The paper's diagnostic contribution is at least as important as its algorithmic contribution. Prior work on SI (Leviathan et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2023; Miao et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2025) consistently reported empirical speedups, but always in a narrow operational regime: drafters running at 1–5% of target latency, with moderate-to-high acceptance rates. The existence of a failure regime — where SI is *slower* than standard autoregressive decoding — was implicitly acknowledged (practitioners understood that SI isn't always beneficial) but was never systematically characterized, theorized, or addressed. PEARL (Liu et al., 2025) inherited this failure mode without comment: its heuristic parallelization still degrades to sequential SI when drafters are slow or inaccurate.

The field's implicit working assumption was that SI's applicability was inherently restricted to favorable drafter configurations, and that in unfavorable regimes, users should simply fall back to non-SI. DSI challenges this assumption by demonstrating that **the slowdown is not a property of speculation itself — it is a property of the sequential orchestration**. Given sufficient parallelism (multiple target servers), the verification work that causes the slowdown in SI can be overlapped with drafting, converting previously-blocking target forwards into background tasks whose latency never reaches the critical path.

This diagnostic move is captured in Figure 2, which is perhaps the paper's most conceptually important figure. It maps the entire space of `(drafter latency, acceptance rate)` configurations, showing that SI's speedup region (Figure 2a, non-pink area) is a narrow diagonal band — SI helps only when acceptance rate exceeds drafter latency as a fraction of target latency. Outside this band (the pink region, where SI is slower than non-SI), DSI consistently provides speedup (Figures 2b, 2c, 2d — no pink anywhere). This is not a marginal improvement; it is the **elimination of a failure mode**. Figure 2 demonstrates that DSI converts a conditional benefit (SI helps *if* the drafter is good enough) into an unconditional one (DSI always helps, for any non-zero acceptance rate).

The significance of this diagnostic contribution is that it reframes the problem of speculative inference. Before DSI, the research question was: "How do we build better drafters so that SI works?" After DSI, the question becomes: "Given that we can make SI work for any drafter (with enough hardware), how do we optimally allocate resources?" This shifts the bottleneck from drafter quality to hardware availability — a much more tractable constraint given the ongoing trend toward multi-GPU deployments.

The paper quantifies this through the Amdahl's law analysis (Section 3): for a perfectly accurate drafter at 1–5% latency with `lookahead ∈ [2, 5]`, DSI's theoretical speedup over SI is 4×–50×. This upper bound, while idealized, establishes the **scale of the gap** that sequential orchestration creates — a gap large enough to motivate the hardware investment DSI requires.

### Innovation 3: The SP Degree–Lookahead Tradeoff as a Principled Resource Allocation Framework

A common failure mode in parallel algorithm design is the "embarrassingly parallel" trap: the algorithm assumes unbounded parallelism (infinite processors), and the mapping to finite resources is left as an ad-hoc engineering detail. Algorithm 1 as initially presented falls into this trap — it spawns a new target verification thread for every draft token, requiring unbounded SP degree. The paper's third conceptual contribution is to **convert this idealized algorithm into a deployable one through a principled tradeoff between the lookahead hyperparameter and the available hardware**.

The key insight is that `lookahead` — originally introduced in SI as a batching optimization to reduce target forward overhead — serves a **completely different purpose** in DSI. In standard SI, larger `lookahead` means fewer, more expensive verification batches, trading off the overhead of individual target forwards against the risk of wasted drafting work on rejected long sequences. In DSI, `lookahead` becomes a **knob that controls the rate at which verification tasks are injected into the target server pool**. Equation 1 formalizes this: `lookahead` appears in the denominator of the required SP degree, meaning that doubling `lookahead` halves the number of target servers needed to avoid queuing.

This reframing of `lookahead` is subtle but important. In SI, `lookahead` is a heuristic parameter tuned empirically for speedup. In DSI, `lookahead` is **determined algebraically** from the available hardware and the latency ratio — it is not a free parameter but a constraint satisfaction variable. The optimal value is the minimum satisfying Equation 1, chosen to detect rejections as early as possible while respecting the hardware limit. This converts DSI from "an algorithm that needs a lot of GPUs" to "an algorithm that can be deployed on any number of GPUs ≥ 2, with the `lookahead` adapting automatically."

The practical significance of this framework is that it makes DSI's resource requirements **predictable and tunable**. Given a target model, a drafter, and a compute budget (number of GPUs), a practitioner can compute the minimal `lookahead` before running any experiments — and can predict that adding GPUs will reduce `lookahead` and thus improve speedup by catching rejections faster. This is a level of operational clarity that is rare in inference optimization, where parameters are typically set through grid search or rules of thumb.

The maximum useful SP degree — `⌈target latency / drafter latency⌉` — provides a natural saturation point: beyond this, additional target servers have no verification work to do. This gives DSI a **diminishing returns boundary** that is analytically computable, guiding hardware provisioning decisions. For example, with the Phi3-14B/Phi3-4B pair from Table 2 (drafter latency ~65% of target), the maximum useful SP degree is `⌈1/0.65⌉ = 2`, meaning DSI can productively use at most 3 GPUs (2 target servers + 1 drafter) — and Table 2's speedups (1.37–1.60×) were achieved with SP ≤ 7, suggesting the experiments had more hardware than needed and the `lookahead` could have been reduced. For the Vicuna-13B/Vicuna-68M pair (drafter at ~7% latency), the maximum useful SP degree is `⌈1/0.07⌉ = 15` — far more GPUs than the single-node 8-GPU setup used, meaning the experiments were hardware-limited and larger speedups would be possible with more GPUs or multi-node deployment.

This is an **incremental** contribution in the positive sense: it takes the standard SI `lookahead` parameter and gives it a new, mathematically grounded role in a resource-constrained setting, converting a heuristic into a constraint.

### Innovation 4: DSI as an Orchestration Layer — Provably Decoupled from the Underlying Computation, Enabling Universal Applicability

The paper's final conceptual contribution is architectural: DSI is designed as an **orchestration algorithm that is provably agnostic to the computation it orchestrates**. This is not an implementation detail — it is a structural property with far-reaching implications for composability and generality.

The proof of this decoupling is in the paper's modular design. Algorithm 1 specifies *when* threads are spawned and terminated, *which* thread is labeled as verifier, and *how* acceptance is determined — but it never specifies *how* a forward pass is computed, *how* the KV cache is managed, or *how* token sampling is implemented. These are black-box operations performed by threads. The theoretical guarantees (Theorems 1 and 2, Proposition 1) depend only on Assumptions 1–3 — bounded computation time, strict drafter speed advantage, and no scheduling delays — which are properties of the *relative timing* of forwards, not their internal implementation.

This decoupling means that DSI can be combined with **any existing inference optimization** without modification: model parallelism (TP, PP), kernel optimizations (FlashAttention), quantization, pruning, alternative architectures, and — critically — any existing SI verification method. The paper explicitly notes that relaxed rejection procedures (lossless in expectation) from prior SI work can replace the exact-match criterion in Lines 8 and 10 without changing the orchestration logic. The KV cache management from SpecInfer (Miao et al., 2024) can be plugged in as-is. The drafter models can be from any family, trained by any method, using any architecture.

This is a stronger claim than typical "our method is compatible with X" statements. The paper is asserting that DSI is a **provably correct orchestration layer** in the formal sense: given black-box models satisfying the timing assumptions, the orchestration guarantees losslessness and speedup regardless of what happens inside those black boxes. The significance is that DSI's value compounds with every improvement to individual forward pass speed: faster drafters → lower `lookahead` → faster rejection detection → larger speedup. Faster targets → lower SP degree required → fewer GPUs needed. The orchestration adapts automatically through Equation 1.

This positions DSI not as a competitor to existing inference optimizations but as a **universal accelerator** that can be deployed on top of them, converting their per-forward latency reductions into compounding end-to-end speedups. The paper's empirical demonstration of this — using off-the-shelf models from Hugging Face without any modification or fine-tuning, across diverse tasks (code generation, summarization, instruction following) — validates the claim of broad applicability. The 1.29–1.92× speedups in Table 2 are achieved without any model-specific tuning beyond selecting the `lookahead` to satisfy Equation 1.

This is a **fundamental** architectural contribution because it establishes a new abstraction boundary in the inference stack. Before DSI, the orchestration (draft-then-verify loop) was tightly coupled to the verification method and the model implementations. DSI separates "what to compute when" from "how to compute it," enabling independent innovation on both sides of the interface. This is the same architectural principle that made operating systems powerful — abstracting scheduling from computation — applied to the much narrower domain of speculative inference, but with the same generative consequences for composability.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The experiments use four established datasets spanning diverse tasks: CNN Daily Mail (CNN-DM; Hermann et al., 2015) for text summarization; Alpaca (Taori et al., 2023) for instruction-following; and HumanEval (Chen et al., 2021) and MBPP (Austin et al., 2021) for code generation. These are standard benchmarks in the LM evaluation literature, chosen to demonstrate DSI's effectiveness across different generation tasks. For each dataset, 50 prompts are sampled uniformly at random for latency estimation, and 256 tokens per prompt are generated for acceptance rate estimation.

- **Base model(s).** The experiments use off-the-shelf models from Hugging Face without modification or fine-tuning. Target models include StarCoder-15B (Li et al., 2023a), Phi3-14B (Phi-3-medium-128k-instruct), Vicuna-13B (lmsys/vicuna-13b-v1.3), and Vicuna-7B (lmsys/vicuna-7b-v1.3). Drafter models are StarCoder-168M (tiny_starcoder_py), Phi3-4B (Phi-3-mini-128k-instruct), and Vicuna-68M (double7/vicuna-68m). The model pairs are chosen from the same "families" (trained similarly, on similar data) because the paper's separate acceptance rate experiment (Appendix F.2) shows this yields higher acceptance rates — e.g., StarCoder-168M achieves 93% acceptance against StarCoder-15B. The paper argues that PaLM 2-S* (used in the reference example) is representative; here, the argument is practical: these are real, downloadable models that span different scales and tasks.

- **Metrics.** The primary metric is **end-to-end wall time latency** for generating a fixed number of output tokens (50 tokens in the main experiment). Latency is measured as the ratio of DSI's estimated wall time to SI's estimated wall time, reported as "Speedup DSI vs. SI" in Table 2. The speedup includes both prefilling (time-to-first-token, TTFT) and decoding (time-per-output-token, TPOT) latency but excludes tokenization time. The paper also reports speedups in the ablation as pairwise ratios (e.g., SI/non-SI, DSI/SI, DSI/non-SI) in Figure 2. The acceptance rate is estimated separately (see below) and reported in Table 2 as context for interpreting speedup results.

- **Baselines.** Three baselines are used:
  - **Non-SI (standard autoregressive inference):** The target model generates tokens one at a time sequentially, with no speculative drafting or verification. This is the "do nothing" baseline — the simplest possible generation procedure.
  - **Speculative Inference (SI; Leviathan et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2023):** The standard draft-then-verify algorithm using a single target instance and a single drafter instance. SI is run at lookahead ∈ {1, 5, 10}, and for each configuration, the optimal lookahead (yielding minimal latency) is selected. This ensures SI is presented in its best possible light for comparison.
  - **PEARL (Liu et al., 2025):** Discussed in Section 5 (Related Work) but not directly benchmarked in experiments. The paper provides a theoretical comparison rather than empirical, arguing that PEARL is a sequential algorithm limited to one target and one drafter instance, with no theoretical speedup guarantees.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The experiments do not use a generation budget in the sense of capping total forward passes. Instead, the "budget" is the **available hardware**: a single node with up to 8 GPUs. DSI is configured to use an SP degree ≤ 7 (one GPU for the drafter, up to 7 for target servers). The comparison between methods is at equal hardware, not equal FLOPs — both SI and DSI use the same target and drafter models, but DSI uses more GPUs for target instances. This is a critical difference from the reference example's FLOPs-matched comparison. The paper's fairness argument is implicit: given the same node with 8 GPUs, DSI uses the additional GPUs that SI would leave idle, converting available hardware into latency reduction.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** No cross-validation is reported. The main experiment (Table 2) runs each ⟨target, drafter, dataset⟩ configuration for 50 prompts, with 50 tokens generated per prompt. The ablation (Figure 2) runs each of millions of offline configurations for 5 repeated trials and averages results. The acceptance rate estimation uses 256 tokens per prompt to fit a geometric distribution, which the paper argues converges to the true expected acceptance rate as the number of tokens grows (Appendix F.2.1).

The paper implements DSI as a **multithreading system in Python**, using Python's built-in thread pool to manage OS-level threads. This means that real-world thread management latencies (context switching, thread creation, scheduling delays) are incurred in the experiments, not simulated away. However, due to budget constraints, the authors did not have access to an 8-GPU node. Instead, they used a single GPU and replaced actual forward pass computations with **`wait` commands** that block the thread for the expected forward latency of each model — while still running the full multithreading orchestration. The forward latencies (TTFT and TPOT) used in these wait commands were measured in a **separate calibration experiment** on a single NVIDIA A100 80GB GPU: for each model-dataset pair, 50 prompts were sampled, 20 tokens were generated per prompt, and the average TTFT and TPOT were recorded (Appendix F.1). The acceptance rates were similarly estimated in a **separate calibration experiment**: for each target-drafter-dataset triple, 256 tokens were generated from both models, and the average number of exact token matches between target and drafter outputs was fitted to a geometric distribution (Appendix F.2). These independently measured latencies and acceptance rates were then plugged into the main experiment.

This two-phase design — calibrate realistic timing parameters on real hardware, then run the full multithreading simulation with those parameters — is a pragmatic response to hardware constraints. The paper argues that the results are "realistic because DSI is implemented as a multithreading system, incurring all real-world latencies of such systems (e.g., context switching), and all the real-world GPU-related latencies are based on independent experiments accessing a GPU" (Section 6). A critical reader might note that the approach does not capture GPU-specific contention effects (multiple models competing for memory bandwidth, data transfer channels) that would arise in a real multi-GPU deployment.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### SI vs. Non-SI Failure Regime (Figure 2a in Ablation)

Before presenting DSI's speedups, the paper establishes the baseline failure mode it aims to fix. Figure 2a is a heatmap showing the speedup (or slowdown) of SI compared to non-SI as a function of drafter latency (as a fraction of target latency) and acceptance rate. The color scale ranges from 0 (dark, indicating slowdown) to >10 (bright, indicating speedup). The pink region — where the ratio exceeds 1 (SI is slower than non-SI) — appears in the upper-left portion of the plot: high drafter latency (above ~60–70% of target) combined with low-to-moderate acceptance rates (below ~0.5). This is the core diagnostic finding: **SI fails to provide speedup when drafters are either too slow or too inaccurate**.

The paper quantifies this boundary: "To achieve a speedup with SI compared to non-SI, the acceptance rate of the drafter must at least match the latency of the drafter model" — that is, the acceptance rate must exceed the drafter's relative latency for SI to beat non-SI. At 30% drafter latency, SI helps only if acceptance exceeds ~30%. At 70% drafter latency (roughly the Phi3-4B configuration in Table 2), SI helps only if acceptance exceeds ~70%. The non-pink region in Figure 2a is a narrow diagonal band, confirming that SI's operating window is restrictive.

This diagnostic is essential context for all the DSI speedup results that follow. The models in Table 2 span both regimes: StarCoder-168M at ~32% latency with 90–93% acceptance is comfortably in SI's favorable region; Vicuna-68M at ~7% latency with 58–67% acceptance is also favorable; but Phi3-4B at ~65% latency with 87–95% acceptance is near the boundary. The fact that DSI achieves speedups even in this marginal regime is part of the paper's argument for broader applicability.

#### DSI vs. SI Speedups (Table 2)

Table 2 reports the main empirical finding: across all 10 target-drafter-dataset configurations tested, DSI outperforms SI with speedups ranging from **1.29× to 1.92×**. The specific numbers, organized by model family:

**StarCoder family (fastest drafter relative to target):**
- StarCoder-15B / StarCoder-168M on HumanEval: **1.92×** (drafter at 32.3% latency, 93% acceptance rate)
- StarCoder-15B / StarCoder-168M on MBPP: **1.66×** (drafter at 32.9% latency, 90% acceptance rate)

These are the largest speedups, achieved with the drafter having the best combination of relative speed (~1/3 of target) and high acceptance (>90%). The HumanEval configuration (1.92×) represents the best case: fast, accurate drafter yields large DSI advantage.

**Phi3 family (slowest drafter relative to target):**
- Phi3-14B / Phi3-4B on Alpaca: **1.60×** (drafter at 67.4% latency, 87% acceptance rate)
- Phi3-14B / Phi3-4B on HumanEval: **1.41×** (drafter at 65.3% latency, 95% acceptance rate)
- Phi3-14B / Phi3-4B on CNN-DM: **1.39×** (drafter at 66.0% latency, 93% acceptance rate)
- Phi3-14B / Phi3-4B on MBPP: **1.37×** (drafter at 65.8% latency, 94% acceptance rate)

These are the most interesting configurations because the drafter is **very slow relative to the target** (65–67% of target latency). Standard SI would provide minimal or no speedup in this regime — Figure 2a shows that at ~65% drafter latency, the acceptance rate would need to be above ~65% for SI to beat non-SI, and at 87–95% acceptance, SI is barely in the green. DSI achieves 1.37–1.60× speedup *over SI* in this marginal regime, which is a direct demonstration of the paper's claim that DSI "unlocks the acceleration of LMs for which SI fails" (Abstract). The speedup is lower than for StarCoder (as expected — harder to hide latency of a slow drafter), but it is still substantial.

**Vicuna family (very fast drafter, moderate acceptance):**
- Vicuna-13B / Vicuna-68M on CNN-DM: **1.47×** (drafter at 6.5% latency, 63% acceptance rate)
- Vicuna-13B / Vicuna-68M on Alpaca: **1.41×** (drafter at 7.4% latency, 58% acceptance rate)
- Vicuna-7B / Vicuna-68M on CNN-DM: **1.29×** (drafter at 8.4% latency, 67% acceptance rate)
- Vicuna-7B / Vicuna-68M on Alpaca: **1.70×** (drafter at 9.5% latency, 59% acceptance rate)

These configurations have the fastest drafter relative to target (~7–10% latency) but the lowest acceptance rates (58–67%). The lower acceptance means DSI's speedup comes primarily from hiding latency of successful verifications, not from reducing total target forwards. The 1.70× speedup on Vicuna-7B/Alpaca despite only 59% acceptance is notable — it suggests DSI is effective even when the drafter is frequently wrong, because the fast drafter generates tokens quickly enough that the verification pipeline stays full.

**What "speedup" means in Table 2.** The reported speedup is the ratio of estimated end-to-end wall time latencies: DSI latency divided by SI latency. The estimation procedure works as follows (elaborated in Appendix F): for each configuration, the simulation runs the full multithreading orchestration (with real OS threads, thread pools, and wait commands replacing GPU computation) for 50 tokens per prompt across many prompts. The total wall time includes TTFT and TPOT for all forwards, plus real multithreading overhead. SI is run with its optimal `lookahead` (selected by grid search over {1, 5, 10}); DSI is run with `lookahead` values that satisfy Equation 1 for SP ≤ 7. The speedup is then computed as SI_total_time / DSI_total_time.

**Notable pattern across tasks.** Within each model family, speedup varies by dataset, indicating that the acceptance rate (which differs by task) matters. For Phi3-14B/Phi3-4B, speedup ranges from 1.37× (MBPP) to 1.60× (Alpaca) despite similar drafter latency (65–67%) and acceptance rates (87–95%). The paper does not analyze task-specific effects — acceptance rate is the only task-dependent variable reported — so the variation likely reflects interactions between task difficulty, model calibration, and the geometric distribution of accepted draft lengths that are not fully captured by the mean acceptance rate.

**Absolute latency values (raw numbers in Table 2).** Target latencies range from 20.6ms (StarCoder-15B on HumanEval) to 52.4ms (Phi3-14B on CNN-DM). Drafter latencies range from 2.5ms (Vicuna-68M) to 34.6ms (Phi3-4B on CNN-DM). These are TPOT (time-per-output-token) values, excluding the typically-larger TTFT. The paper reports TTFT/TPOT ratios separately in Table 3 (Appendix F.1), showing that TTFT ranges from roughly equal to TPOT (Vicuna-13B on Alpaca: 1.15×) to much larger (Vicuna-13B on CNN-DM: 5.36×). Since DSI parallelizes verification across the entire generation, the TTFT advantage (hiding prefilling latency) is captured in the end-to-end measurement, but the paper reports only TPOT in the main table for brevity.

#### DSI vs. SI and Non-SI: Complete Configuration Space (Figure 2 in Ablation)

The offline simulation (Section 4.1, Figure 2) provides a complementary view that is more comprehensive than Table 2. While Table 2 shows results for 10 specific real-world configurations, Figure 2 sweeps the entire 2D space of drafter latency (0.01 to 1.0 in 0.01 increments) and acceptance rate (0 to 1.0 in 0.01 increments) — millions of data points. The offline simulation differs from the online experiment by summing forward pass latencies without thread pool overhead, "intentionally ignoring additional real-world latencies of multithreading systems like context switching, allowing us to decouple the implementation details from the theoretical analysis" (Section 4.1). This makes it a cleaner test of the algorithmic properties.

The four subplots of Figure 2 show pairwise speedup ratios:

**Figure 2a: non-SI / SI.** This reproduces the diagnostic from the main results at higher resolution. The non-pink region (SI faster than non-SI) forms a diagonal band: acceptance rate must exceed drafter latency for SI to win. The boundary is sharp — at 50% drafter latency, the speedup crosses from <1 (pink, slowdown) to >1 (green) at around 50% acceptance rate. The maximum SI speedup (>10×) occurs in the upper-left corner: very fast drafters (<5% latency) with very high acceptance (>90%). The pink region (slowdown) covers a substantial fraction of the space — roughly the upper-left half — confirming that SI fails in many configurations.

**Figure 2b: SI / DSI.** This is the central result: DSI is faster than SI (ratio > 1, shown in green/yellow) across **all configurations with non-zero acceptance rate**. There is no pink region. The speedup is largest (>2×) in the upper-left region (fast drafters, high acceptance) and decreases toward the lower-right (slow drafters, low acceptance), but never drops below 1. The color scale saturates at >2, meaning the actual speedup in the fastest configurations exceeds 2× (the maximum plotted). The absence of any red/pink confirms Theorem 2: DSI is always at least as fast as SI.

**Figure 2c: non-SI / DSI.** DSI is faster than non-SI (ratio > 1) across all configurations with non-zero acceptance rate. The speedup exceeds 1.4× (yellow boundary) for most of the space, and reaches >1.6× in the upper-left corner. The important comparison is in the region where Figure 2a was pink (SI slower than non-SI): Figure 2c shows that DSI is faster than non-SI in this region — meaning DSI succeeds where SI fails. This is the empirical validation of the paper's claim that DSI "closes the gap where SI can be slower than non-SI" (Section 1).

**Figure 2d: min(SI, non-SI) / DSI.** This compares DSI against the faster of SI and non-SI for each configuration — the most relevant baseline for a practitioner who would choose whichever is faster. DSI is faster than this "best of both" baseline across all configurations with non-zero acceptance rate, with speedups reaching >1.6× in the upper-left corner. The figure answers the question: "Even if I can freely choose between SI and non-SI based on my drafter, does DSI still help?" The answer is yes — DSI provides additional speedup over the best available existing method.

**The 0% acceptance rate boundary.** At acceptance rate = 0 (bottom row of each heatmap), the speedup ratio is 1.0 (no improvement). This is expected: if every draft token is rejected, DSI's speculative threads are all wasted, and the algorithm effectively degrades to non-SI — but it never becomes *slower* than non-SI (Theorem 1). This is visible as the dark band at the bottom of all four subplots.

**Discretization artifacts.** The paper notes that "speedups are not smooth for drafter latencies < 20% due to the discretization of the lookahead hyperparameter" (Appendix F.3). The `lookahead` values are integers (1, 2, ..., 200), and small changes in drafter latency can cause `lookahead` to jump to the next integer satisfying Equation 1, creating step-like patterns in the heatmaps. The paper provides a smoothed version for `lookahead = 5` in Appendix F.7 (Figure 7), showing qualitatively identical results: DSI consistently outperforms both SI and non-SI across all configurations.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

The paper's experimental section is relatively sparse on ablations compared to the reference example. Most of the "ablation" analysis is in the offline simulation (Figure 2), which sweeps the full parameter space rather than holding any variables fixed. However, several aspects of the experimental design serve as implicit robustness checks:

- **Multi-dataset coverage (task diversity):** The paper uses four datasets spanning summarization (CNN-DM), instruction-following (Alpaca), and code generation (HumanEval, MBPP). Table 2 shows consistent DSI speedups across all tasks, with speedup varying by task due to differences in acceptance rate. The fact that DSI works across tasks with qualitatively different output characteristics (free-form text vs. structured code) suggests the algorithm's benefits are not task-specific.

- **Multi-model-family coverage (architectural diversity):** The paper tests three model families (StarCoder, Phi3, Vicuna) at different scales (7B–15B targets, 68M–4B drafters). Speedups are observed across all families, though they vary with drafter latency and acceptance rate. This addresses the concern that DSI might only work for specific model architectures or scale ratios.

- **Online vs. offline experiment consistency:** The online experiment (Table 2, with real multithreading overhead) and offline simulation (Figure 2, without multithreading overhead) show qualitatively consistent results: DSI outperforms SI and non-SI. The actual speedups in Table 2 (1.29–1.92×) are lower than what Figure 2 would predict for the corresponding (latency, acceptance) points, which is expected because the online experiment includes real-world overhead that the offline simulation ignores. The consistency in *direction* (DSI always wins) validates that multithreading overhead does not negate the algorithmic advantage.

- **Lookahead selection via Equation 1:** The main experiment runs DSI only for `lookahead` values that satisfy Equation 1 for SP ≤ 7. This is an implicit ablation: the paper is testing DSI in its *deployable* configuration, not in the idealized infinite-hardware version. The fact that speedups are achieved with this constraint confirms that the `lookahead`-SP tradeoff works as designed. However, the paper does not report results for configurations where Equation 1 is violated (e.g., running DSI with `lookahead = 1` at SP = 7 when the drafter is too fast), which would show the performance degradation from verification task queuing.

- **Acceptance rate estimation robustness:** The acceptance rates in Table 2 are estimated by fitting a geometric distribution to exact-match token sequences. The paper acknowledges (Appendix F.2.1) that this relies on an i.i.d. assumption and cites Mamou et al. (2024) for evidence that the assumption holds in practice. This is not a controlled ablation (no comparison with other acceptance rate estimators), but the consistency of speedup predictions across diverse acceptance rates (58–95%) suggests the estimation method is adequate.

**Notable missing ablations.** Several experiments that would strengthen the paper are absent:
- **SP degree scaling:** The paper does not report how speedup varies with SP degree for a fixed configuration. A plot of speedup vs. number of target GPUs (1 to 7) for a specific target-drafter pair would directly validate the claim that "DSI can orchestrate an arbitrary number of GPUs (≥ 2)" and would show the diminishing returns boundary predicted by the maximum useful SP degree formula.
- **Lookahead sensitivity:** Table 2 reports results for `lookahead ∈ {1, 5, 10}` but does not show how speedup varies with `lookahead` for a fixed configuration. The paper's theoretical claim — "selecting the minimum lookahead value that satisfies Equation 1 is the optimal choice" — is not empirically tested. A sweep across `lookahead` values for a fixed SP degree would validate or refine this claim.
- **KV cache overhead measurement:** The paper states that SpecInfer's KV cache management can be applied "as-is" with "negligible latency" overhead, but does not measure this overhead in any experiment. This is a gap between the theoretical algorithm and the practical deployment.
- **Relaxed rejection methods:** The paper notes that exact-match rejection can be replaced with lossless-in-expectation methods (Leviathan et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2023) to increase the effective acceptance rate, but does not experiment with these methods. This would likely increase all speedups (higher acceptance → more hidden verification), but the magnitude is unknown.
- **Comparison with PEARL:** Section 5 provides a theoretical critique of PEARL but no empirical comparison. A head-to-head benchmark on the same model pairs would strengthen the argument that DSI's generalization to arbitrary future iterations matters in practice.

### Critical Assessment

**Claim 1: DSI is provably faster than SI and non-SI in expectation — given any drafters.**

This is the paper's central theoretical claim (Theorems 1 and 2, Proposition 1). The experiments provide strong empirical support, but with important caveats about what was actually tested.

The offline simulation (Figure 2) is the most direct test: it sweeps the complete (drafter latency, acceptance rate) space and shows DSI is faster than both SI and non-SI for all configurations with non-zero acceptance rate. This matches the theoretical predictions exactly. The online experiment (Table 2) tests 10 real configurations and shows consistent DSI speedups (1.29–1.92× over SI), all in the direction predicted by theory.

However, both experiments have limitations that prevent them from being a complete validation:

**The online experiment simulates multi-GPU execution on a single GPU.** Due to budget constraints, the actual forward passes are replaced with `wait` commands, and the only real parallelism is in the OS-level multithreading orchestration. This means that GPU-specific contention effects — multiple models competing for memory bandwidth, data transfer over NVLink, L2 cache thrashing from concurrent kernel launches — are not captured. In a real 8-GPU deployment, these effects could reduce the effective parallelism below what the simulated timing predicts. The paper acknowledges this limitation (Section 6) but does not quantify the potential impact.

**The offline simulation intentionally ignores multithreading overhead.** The heatmaps in Figure 2 sum forward pass latencies without any thread management cost, which makes them an upper bound on achievable speedup. The paper is transparent about this ("intentionally ignoring additional real-world latencies of multithreading systems like context switching, allowing us to decouple the implementation details from the theoretical analysis"), but the gap between offline (upper bound) and online (realistic) speedups is not quantified. Comparing the StarCoder/HumanEval configuration: Figure 2 at ~32% drafter latency and ~93% acceptance would predict DSI speedup over SI of >2× (based on the color scale in Figure 2b); the online experiment achieves 1.92×. The ~8% gap is attributable to multithreading overhead, but the paper does not break this down.

**The theoretical proofs assume zero sampling time and no scheduling delays (Assumptions 1 and 3).** These are standard modeling assumptions that simplify analysis, but in real systems, sampling from a probability distribution and thread context switching both take measurable time. The online experiment *does* incur scheduling delays (since it uses real OS threads), so the speedups it reports already account for this — but the proofs do not bound how much these delays reduce theoretical speedup. For very fast drafters (Vicuna-68M at 2.5ms per token), scheduling overhead could become a significant fraction of per-token time, potentially changing the optimal `lookahead`.

**All tested configurations have non-zero acceptance rates.** Table 2 reports acceptance rates from 58% to 95%. The 0% acceptance rate case (where DSI theoretically degrades to non-SI speed) is only tested in the offline simulation (Figure 2, bottom row of each heatmap), where the speedup ratio is exactly 1.0. The paper does not construct a deliberate near-zero-acceptance configuration (e.g., a randomly initialized drafter) to stress-test this boundary. This is a minor concern since 0% acceptance is unrealistic in practice, but it means the "DSI is never slower" guarantee is empirically demonstrated only through extrapolation from the offline heatmaps.

**Assertion integrity:** The claim that DSI is faster than SI and non-SI is well-supported in the tested regimes. The "provably" qualifier is grounded in the proofs, and the experiments are consistent with the proofs. However, the gap between the idealized proof conditions (no scheduling overhead, zero sampling time) and the simulated experimental conditions (real multithreading, realistic forward latencies) is substantial enough that a practitioner should expect somewhat lower speedups in a real deployment than what Table 2 reports — and the paper does not quantify this degradation.

---

**Claim 2: DSI closes the gap where SI fails — i.e., accelerates inference for drafters too slow or inaccurate for SI to provide speedup.**

This is the paper's most practically significant claim. The evidence is strong for specific configurations but incomplete for the full range of "SI-failure" regimes.

**Strong evidence.** Figure 2a establishes the SI failure region (pink). Figures 2c and 2d show that DSI provides speedup (>1) throughout this region. The Phi3 configurations in Table 2 are particularly important: with the drafter at ~65% of target latency, SI operates at or near the boundary of its beneficial regime (for acceptance rates of 87–95%, SI is barely faster than non-SI at the optimal `lookahead`, per Figure 2a). DSI achieves 1.37–1.60× speedup over SI in these configurations, directly demonstrating that it succeeds where SI is marginal. Without DSI, a practitioner using Phi3-4B as a drafter for Phi3-14B would see little to no benefit from SI — and might even see a slowdown — so DSI genuinely "unlocks" acceleration for this model pair.

**Weaker evidence.** The experiments do not directly measure SI's slowdown relative to non-SI for the specific model pairs in Table 2. The paper states that "SI is slower than non-SI if drafters are too slow or inaccurate" (Section 1) and shows this in the offline simulation (Figure 2a), but does not report, for example, "SI is 0.8× the speed of non-SI for Phi3-14B/Phi3-4B on MBPP, while DSI is 1.37× faster than SI." This comparison would make the "closing the gap" narrative more concrete by quantifying the gap being closed. The reader must infer from Figure 2 that Phi3-4B at ~65% latency and 87–95% acceptance is in the marginal region where SI provides minimal benefit but is probably not actually slower than non-SI (the pink region in Figure 2a starts at lower acceptance rates for 65% latency). So DSI is demonstrably improving on SI in a marginal regime, but the experiments do not demonstrate DSI succeeding in configurations where SI is *strictly worse* than non-SI in a real deployment.

**Missing: explicit SI-slower-than-non-SI experiments.** To fully validate this claim, the paper would need to construct a target-drafter pair where SI is measurably slower than non-SI (e.g., by using a very slow or very inaccurate drafter) and show that DSI recovers speedup. The experiments do not do this. The offline simulation (Figure 2) provides theoretical coverage, but a real demonstration with actual model outputs (not just timing parameters) would be more convincing.

**Assertion integrity:** Supported with qualifications. The paper demonstrates DSI speedups in configurations where SI's benefit is marginal (Phi3 family), which is strong evidence that DSI expands the applicability of speculative inference. But the specific claim that DSI works "for drafters with which SI fails" — in the strict sense of SI being slower than non-SI — is demonstrated only through offline simulation, not through an end-to-end experiment with real model outputs where SI demonstrably underperforms non-SI.

---

**Claim 3: DSI is 1.29–1.92× faster than SI in single-node setups for various off-the-shelf LMs and tasks.**

This claim is directly supported by Table 2. The numbers are clearly reported, the configurations are specified, and the measurement methodology is described. However, several qualifications apply:

**The speedups are measured in a simulated environment, not on a real multi-GPU node.** As discussed above, the `wait` command replacement for GPU computation means that real hardware effects (GPU contention, PCIe bandwidth limits, NVLink saturation) are not captured. The paper's argument that "all the real-world GPU-related latencies are based on independent experiments accessing a GPU" (Section 6) only covers per-forward latency estimation, not multi-GPU interaction effects. In a real 8-GPU deployment, multiple target servers running concurrent forward passes would compete for shared resources (host-to-device bandwidth for KV cache transfers, cross-GPU synchronization for any model parallelism, etc.). The magnitude of this effect depends on the specific hardware configuration and is not bounded by the paper's experiments.

**The speedups are relative to SI with optimal `lookahead` selection.** This is a fair baseline — SI is given the best possible `lookahead` for each configuration — but it means the speedup numbers represent DSI's advantage over the *best possible* SI configuration, not over a naive SI implementation. If SI were run with a fixed `lookahead` (as is common in practice), DSI's advantage might be larger because SI would be suboptimal in many configurations.

**The 10 configurations span a limited range of the (latency, acceptance) space.** Table 2 covers drafter latencies from 6.5% to 67.4% and acceptance rates from 58% to 95%, which are realistic ranges but do not test the extremes. No configuration has drafter latency below 5% (where SI is extremely fast and DSI's advantage might be smaller or larger — the theory predicts larger speedup, Figure 2b) or above 70% (where SI is definitively in its failure region). No configuration has acceptance below 50%. The paper could have constructed such configurations by using deliberately poor drafters (e.g., a much smaller model from a different family, or a model trained on different data) to probe the boundaries of DSI's advantage.

**The absolute latency values are small (20–52ms for target, 2.5–35ms for drafter).** These are realistic for modern GPUs, but DSI's speedup depends on the ratio, not the absolute values. The paper does not test whether the speedup scales to larger models with longer per-forward latencies, where thread management overhead becomes proportionally smaller (favoring DSI) but communication overhead for distributing KV caches across GPUs might become larger.

**Assertion integrity:** The 1.29–1.92× numbers are credible for the tested configurations in a simulated environment. Whether these exact numbers would reproduce on a physical 8-GPU node depends on hardware-specific factors not captured by the simulation. The range is plausible, and the methodology is transparent about its limitations, so the claim is supported with appropriate caveats.

---

**Claim 4: DSI can orchestrate an arbitrary number of GPUs (≥ 2) by adjusting its lookahead hyperparameter.**

This claim is theoretical, not empirical. The paper provides Equation 1 as the mechanism for mapping available GPUs to `lookahead`, and the experiments run DSI with SP ≤ 7 and `lookahead` values satisfying this equation. However, no experiment systematically varies the number of GPUs (or SP degree) and measures the resulting speedup. A scaling plot (speedup vs. number of target GPUs for a fixed configuration) would directly validate this claim.

The paper does provide one illustrative example: "given a single drafter of 5% latency and SP = 4, having lookahead = 5 is sufficient... the maximum number of required processing units is 1 + ⌈1 / (5 · 0.05)⌉ = 5." This is a worked numerical example, not an experimental result. The theoretical framework is sound, but the claim that DSI "can orchestrate an arbitrary number of GPUs" is an assertion about the algorithm's parameterization, not a demonstrated empirical fact.

**Assertion integrity:** The claim is supported by the algorithmic design and the SP degree-`lookahead` inequality. The experiments are consistent with the claim (they use SP ≤ 7 and achieve speedups) but do not directly test it. A practitioner wanting to deploy DSI on 16 or 32 GPUs would need to extrapolate from the single-node results without direct empirical guidance.

---

**Missing experiments that would strengthen the paper:**

1. **Real multi-GPU deployment.** Running DSI on an actual 8-GPU node with real forward passes would validate that GPU contention effects do not negate the algorithmic speedup. This is the most important missing experiment.

2. **SP degree scaling curve.** A plot of speedup vs. number of target GPUs (1 through 8) for a fixed configuration would show whether DSI's benefit saturates as predicted by the maximum useful SP degree formula, and whether the saturation point matches theory.

3. **Breakdown of speedup sources.** How much of DSI's speedup comes from hiding verification latency (the SP contribution) vs. the standard SI benefit of reducing target forwards? A decomposition experiment — running DSI with SP = 1 (effectively sequential SI with DSI's orchestration but no parallelism) vs. SP > 1 — would isolate the parallelism benefit.

4. **SI-failure demonstration and recovery.** A configuration where SI is measurably slower than non-SI (verified with actual model outputs, not just timing parameters) and DSI recovers speedup would directly validate the core motivation. A slow, inaccurate drafter (e.g., a randomly initialized small model) would serve this purpose.

5. **Comparison with PEARL.** A head-to-head benchmark on the same models and tasks would validate the theoretical critique in Section 5.

6. **Relaxed rejection impact.** An experiment replacing exact-match verification with a lossless-in-expectation method (e.g., Leviathan et al., 2023) would show how much additional speedup DSI achieves when the effective acceptance rate is higher.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Empirical Results Are Based on Simulated Multi-GPU Execution, Not Real Multi-GPU Hardware

The paper's headline speedup numbers (1.29–1.92× in Table 2) were measured on a **single GPU** with forward pass computations replaced by `wait` commands that blocked threads for the expected forward latency of each model. The actual multi-GPU parallelism was limited to OS-level multithreading orchestration, not concurrent GPU kernel execution. The paper is transparent about this constraint (Section 4):

> "Due to budget constraints, instead of a node with eight GPUs, we only had access to one GPU. To evaluate DSI over a node with eight GPUs without access to such hardware, we adjusted the DSI implementation accordingly. While DSI incurs all the real-world latencies typical of multithreading systems (e.g., latencies of managing OS threads), each call to compute the forward pass of an LM was replaced by a wait command."

**Consequence.** In a real multi-GPU deployment, multiple target servers running concurrent forward passes would compete for shared hardware resources not captured by per-model latency calibration on an isolated GPU. These include: PCIe bandwidth contention when transferring KV cache entries between host and device memory across multiple GPUs simultaneously; NVLink or inter-GPU communication saturation if any model parallelism is combined with DSI; GPU memory bandwidth contention if multiple model instances share the same memory subsystem; and L2 cache thrashing from concurrent kernel launches on different GPUs sharing a common memory pool (relevant for multi-GPU setups with unified memory). The `wait` command approach also cannot capture **straggler effects** — in real multi-GPU systems, individual forward pass latencies have variance (due to thermal throttling, memory refresh cycles, operating system scheduling of GPU kernels), and DSI's synchronization at rejection points (Line 8 of Algorithm 1) means that a slow verification task on one GPU can delay the entire pipeline. The magnitude of these effects depends on the specific hardware configuration (GPU model, interconnect topology, memory architecture) and is not bounded by the paper's experiments.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides no measurement or modeling of multi-GPU contention effects. The latency calibration experiment (Appendix F.1) measures TTFT and TPOT for each model running alone on a single NVIDIA A100 80GB GPU — ideal conditions with no resource competition. The paper acknowledges the limitation explicitly (Section 6) but does not attempt to bound its impact or discuss how hardware choice might affect it. The offline simulation (Figure 2, Section 4.1) goes further in the other direction: it intentionally ignores all real-world overhead, including the OS-level threading overhead that the online experiment *does* capture. Neither experiment faces actual multi-GPU execution.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper frames this as a budget constraint ("Due to budget constraints") and future work, not as a methodological limitation to be mitigated. The code is open-sourced, which would enable a real multi-GPU deployment, but the paper provides no guidance on how to provision hardware to avoid contention (e.g., whether GPUs should be on separate PCIe root complexes, whether NVLink is required, etc.). The resource contention discussion in Section 3.1 addresses contention within DSI's verification task scheduling (avoiding queuing via Equation 1) but not GPU-level resource contention across concurrent forward passes. A practitioner deploying DSI on a real 8-GPU node should expect speedups somewhat lower than Table 2 reports, with the gap depending on hardware specifics that the paper does not characterize.

---

### The Theoretical Guarantees Assume Zero Sampling Time and No Scheduling Overhead, and the Proofs Only Cover Exact-Match (Lossless) Verification

The paper's three main theoretical results — Theorem 1 (DSI is never slower than non-SI), Theorem 2 (DSI is faster than SI in expectation), and Proposition 1 (expected latency bound) — all depend on Assumptions 1–3 (Section 3.2). Two of these assumptions are idealized in ways that affect the practical interpretation of the guarantees:

**Assumption 1** states that `T_wall[sampling x ~ f_j(x_{≤0})] = 0` — token sampling from the output distribution takes negligible time. In practice, sampling involves computing softmax over the full vocabulary (50k+ tokens for most models), generating a random number, and selecting the corresponding token index. For large vocabulary models, this can be tens to hundreds of microseconds — not zero. In DSI, sampling happens on both drafter and target threads at every token generation, and the cumulative sampling time across many threads can become measurable, especially for very fast drafters (Vicuna-68M at 2.5ms per forward pass leaves proportionally more room for sampling overhead).

**Assumption 3** states that computing a sequence of tokens takes exactly the sum of individual token times with no delays between them — essentially, zero scheduling and context-switching overhead. The online experiment (Table 2) **does** incur real OS-level multithreading overhead (thread creation, context switching, scheduling delays), meaning the measured speedups already reflect some violation of Assumption 3. However, the proofs provide no bound on how much these overheads reduce the theoretical speedup guarantee. For configurations where the per-token computation time is very small (fast drafters like Vicuna-68M), scheduling overhead could become a significant fraction of the total, potentially changing the optimal lookahead or reducing the speedup below what the proofs predict.

**Consequence for the "provably faster" claim.** The proofs establish that DSI is faster in an idealized timing model. In real systems, the claim should be interpreted as: DSI's algorithmic structure guarantees it never adds *unnecessary* sequential dependency that isn't already present in non-SI (Theorem 1), and any slowdown relative to this guarantee comes from implementation-level overheads (sampling, scheduling, GPU contention) that are not inherent to the algorithm. This is a meaningful but weaker guarantee than "DSI will always be faster than SI on your hardware." The paper does not make this distinction explicit.

**Additional gap: the proofs assume exact-match verification (lossless).** Theorem 1's correctness proof explicitly relies on verifier threads "returning the correct token" via exact matching (Section 3.2 and Appendix E). The paper states (Section 3.1) that relaxed rejection methods (lossless-in-expectation from Leviathan et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2023) can be applied "without changing the orchestration logic," but the theoretical guarantees in Theorems 1 and 2 and Proposition 1 are proven only for the exact-match case. Lossless-in-expectation methods introduce probabilistic acceptance that changes the dynamics of which threads survive and when verification is triggered — the proofs do not cover this case, and the expected latency bound would need to be re-derived with the modified acceptance probabilities.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. The paper provides no ablation measuring sampling overhead, no breakdown of where wall-clock time is spent in the online experiment (computation vs. sampling vs. scheduling), and no analysis of how tightly the theoretical bounds match the observed speedups. The gap between offline simulation speedups (Figure 2) and online experiment speedups (Table 2) is not quantified in terms of which overheads cause it.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The assumptions are stated (Section 3.2) but their practical validity is not evaluated. The extension to relaxed rejection is mentioned as possible future work but not carried out. A practitioner concerned with tight latency budgets would need to measure sampling and scheduling overheads in their specific deployment environment to determine how close they can get to the theoretical speedup.

---

### DSI Requires Multiple GPUs and Provides No Benefit Over SI on a Single GPU — the Speedup Comes from Additional Hardware, Not Algorithmic Efficiency Alone

DSI's core mechanism is running multiple concurrent target model instances to hide verification latency. This means DSI fundamentally requires **at least 2 GPUs** (one for the target, one for the drafter, with the drafter GPU also handling DSI's non-blocking property only if SP ≥ 1, which requires a separate target server) to provide any benefit over SI. On a single GPU, DSI cannot overlap verification with drafting — there is only one processor to run both — and the algorithm degenerates to sequential SI. The paper states this explicitly in the abstract and Section 1: DSI "leverages speculation parallelism (SP) ... to orchestrate target and drafter instances that overlap in time," and "DSI can orchestrate an arbitrary number of GPUs (≥ 2)."

This means the headline speedups (1.29–1.92× over SI) should be understood as the benefit of **adding hardware**, not as a pure algorithmic speedup at fixed hardware. If a practitioner currently runs SI on 2 GPUs and considers switching to DSI, DSI provides no benefit unless they add at least a third GPU (to create an SP degree ≥ 2, enabling concurrent verification). If they currently run SI on a single GPU (with target and drafter sharing the GPU through time-multiplexing or model swapping), DSI is inapplicable without a hardware upgrade.

**Consequence.** The paper's claim that DSI "unlocks the acceleration of LMs for which SI fails" (Abstract) is conditional on having sufficient hardware. A practitioner with a slow or inaccurate drafter who only has 2 GPUs cannot use DSI to recover speedup — they would need 3+ GPUs to get the SP degree necessary to hide verification latency. This hardware requirement is not a flaw in the algorithm — DSI is explicitly a *distributed* algorithm — but it changes the cost-benefit calculation: the speedup numbers in Table 2 should be weighed against the cost of the additional GPUs. The paper's comparison of SP vs. MP (Section 3.1) correctly argues that "given the same computing budget," SP can outperform MP, but this comparison assumes the budget is large enough to support SP degree > 1. For practitioners with limited GPU budgets, DSI offers no improvement over SI, while MP (which can provide speedup on 2 GPUs via tensor or pipeline parallelism) might still offer benefits.

The paper's positioning is somewhat ambiguous about this. The abstract says DSI is "provably faster than speculative inference (SI) ... and standard autoregressive inference (non-SI)" without qualification, but the "provably faster" result holds only when the SP degree is sufficient to overlap verification with drafting — which requires hardware beyond what SI needs. Theorem 1 (DSI runs at least as fast as non-SI) does not require multiple GPUs (it holds even on a single processor, since DSI in that case degenerates to non-SI), but Theorem 2 (DSI runs faster than SI in expectation) is vacuous on a single processor where DSI cannot parallelize.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None that quantifies this tradeoff. The paper does not report DSI speedup on 2 GPUs vs. 4 GPUs vs. 8 GPUs, does not discuss the minimum hardware needed for DSI to outperform SI, and does not discuss cost-effectiveness (speedup per GPU-dollar). The SP degree scaling curve that would directly show this tradeoff is a missing experiment (Section 5 flagged this). Table 2 reports results for SP ≤ 7, but since all configurations used `lookahead` satisfying Equation 1, it's impossible to tell from the reported data how much of the speedup comes from the SP degree vs. the `lookahead` choice vs. the acceptance rate.

**Mitigation status.** Partially addressed through the theoretical framework. Equation 1 provides an analytic relationship between hardware (SP degree), latency ratio, and `lookahead`, which allows a practitioner to compute whether their available hardware is sufficient. But the paper provides no guidance on the *minimum* hardware for DSI to beat SI in a specific configuration, and no experimental validation of DSI's behavior as SP degree increases from 1 to saturation. The claim that "DSI could be naturally combined with MP" (Section 3.1) offers a path to using limited GPUs more efficiently (e.g., 2 GPUs running MP on the target plus 1 for the drafter), but this combined configuration is not tested.

### The Acceptance Rate Is Estimated Offline Using Exact-Match Token Overlap, Which Does Not Capture the Dynamic Acceptance Behavior of DSI's Concurrent Speculation Tree

The paper estimates acceptance rates via a separate calibration experiment: for each `(target, drafter, dataset)` triple, 256 tokens are generated from both models autoregressively (without speculation), and the number of consecutive exact token matches is fitted to a geometric distribution (Appendix F.2). This yields a single scalar acceptance rate per configuration (Table 2, column "Acceptance Rate").

**Consequence.** This estimation procedure assumes acceptance events are i.i.d. with constant probability `p`, and that the acceptance rate measured in a sequential autoregressive setting (where the drafter always conditions on its own previously generated tokens) matches the acceptance rate in DSI's concurrent tree-based setting (where drafters may condition on tokens from different model instances along speculative paths). In DSI, a drafter thread `C_{(j_1,...,j_k)}` conditions on a sequence of tokens generated by models `f_{j_1}, f_{j_2}, ..., f_{j_k}` — which may be a mix of different drafter instances and the target, not a single model's autoregressive outputs. The marginal distribution of tokens along these mixed-model paths may differ from the pure drafter autoregressive distribution used in calibration, potentially changing the effective acceptance rate in ways the scalar estimate misses.

This matters particularly for configurations where the drafter and target have different token distributions (e.g., when they are from different model families or trained on different data). In the calibration, the drafter always conditions on its own previous tokens, so its outputs follow its own distribution. In DSI, after a verification step that accepts tokens from the target, the drafter conditions on a prefix containing target-generated tokens, which may be out-of-distribution for the drafter, potentially reducing its effective acceptance rate compared to the calibration estimate. The paper's model pairs (same families: StarCoder-168M/15B, Phi3-4B/14B, Vicuna-68M/7B/13B) might minimize this effect because same-family models have similar token distributions, but the issue would become significant if DSI were deployed with a drafter from a different family.

An additional concern: the acceptance rate calibration generates 256 tokens per prompt, but the main experiment generates only 50 tokens per prompt. The acceptance rate's dependence on sequence position — e.g., early tokens might be easier to predict than later tokens — is smoothed out by the 256-token average but might affect the 50-token regime differently.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper cites Mamou et al. (2024) for evidence that the number of accepted tokens per iteration follows a geometric distribution in practice (Appendix F.2.1), which justifies the i.i.d. assumption for standard SI. It does not provide evidence that the i.i.d. assumption holds for DSI's concurrent tree setting specifically. The paper does not compare the calibration-estimated acceptance rate against the empirically observed acceptance rate within a DSI run (which would require running actual DSI with real model outputs, not `wait` commands), so the accuracy of the calibration for DSI's specific token tree distribution is unknown.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper treats the acceptance rate as a scalar parameter that fully characterizes drafter accuracy, without discussing how DSI's concurrent tree structure might cause the effective acceptance rate to deviate from the calibration estimate. This is understandable as a simplifying assumption (the i.i.d. model is standard in the SI literature), but it means the speedup predictions in Table 2 assume a level of acceptance rate fidelity that may not hold precisely in a real DSI deployment, especially with heterogeneous model families.

### DSI's Scalability to Multi-Node Deployments Is Claimed but Not Tested, and the Communication Overhead for Distributed KV Cache Management Is Not Quantified

The paper states that "the theoretical guarantees in section 3.2 hold for both single- and multi-node setups" (Section 4) and that DSI can be implemented as "a multiprocessing system with a Message Passing Interface (MPI)" for multi-node deployment (Section 4). However, all experiments are confined to single-node scenarios with at most 8 GPUs, and the KV cache management discussion (Section 3.1) defers entirely to SpecInfer's approach with the claim that it "has been shown to add negligible latency."

**Consequence.** In a multi-node deployment, DSI's verification tasks would need to transfer KV cache entries across the network (e.g., via MPI or NCCL) when a target server on one node verifies draft tokens generated by a drafter on another node. The SpecInfer tree-based KV cache management (Miao et al., 2024) was designed and evaluated for single-node, multi-GPU setups where KV cache sharing happens over high-bandwidth NVLink or PCIe. The paper provides no evidence that this approach scales to multi-node latency and bandwidth constraints — where inter-node communication can be 10–100× slower than intra-node GPU-to-GPU transfers. DSI's speculative tree grows quickly (branching factor `m` at each token), and while pruning (Lines 8, 10 of Algorithm 1) limits the tree's width, the depth of speculative paths (how far ahead drafting proceeds before verification catches up) depends on the latency ratio and SP degree. For configurations with very fast drafters (Vicuna-68M at ~7% latency) and high SP degree, drafting could get many tokens ahead, creating large KV cache differentials that need to be synchronized at rejection points — potentially incurring substantial communication overhead that the single-node experiments do not capture.

This limitation is particularly relevant because the paper's theoretical maximum speedup (4×–50× over SI, Section 3) would likely require SP degrees far exceeding what a single 8-GPU node can provide, pushing deployment toward multi-node setups. Yet the paper's only empirical evidence comes from single-node simulations.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None for multi-node deployment. The paper does not measure KV cache transfer latency, does not model inter-node communication costs, and does not test DSI in any distributed setting beyond a single node. The statement that multi-node DSI "would be more appropriate" with MPI is a suggestion, not an evaluation. The claim that SpecInfer's KV cache management adds "negligible latency" is cited from Miao et al. (2024) without independent verification or adaptation analysis for DSI's specific tree structure.

**Mitigation status.** Acknowledged as future work. Section 6 states: "testing DSI in realistic multi-node environments, could unlock its potential despite communication latencies." This appropriately flags multi-node deployment as unvalidated, but it does not provide any analysis of what communication latencies would need to be for DSI to remain beneficial, nor does it suggest how to design the system to minimize communication overhead (e.g., locality-aware scheduling of verification tasks to keep KV cache transfers within a node when possible).

### DSI's Speedup Diminishes at Low Acceptance Rates, and the Algorithm Provides No Benefit for Hard Problems Where the Base Model's Capability Is Insufficient

DSI's speedup is fundamentally bounded by the acceptance rate of the drafter. When the acceptance rate is low, most draft tokens are rejected, and DSI's speculative tree is heavily pruned — the computation invested in incorrect branches is wasted, and the effective parallelism is reduced because the tree does not grow enough to keep target servers busy. In the limit of 0% acceptance (every draft token is wrong), DSI degenerates to non-SI speed (Theorem 1 guarantees it is never slower, but it provides no speedup).

The paper demonstrates DSI's effectiveness across acceptance rates from 58% to 95% (Table 2), but does not test configurations below 50% acceptance. The offline simulation (Figure 2b, 2c) shows that DSI's speedup over SI and non-SI approaches 1.0 as acceptance rate → 0 (bottom rows of heatmaps), confirming the theoretical limit. But the simulation does not reveal at what acceptance rate DSI's speedup becomes too small to justify the additional hardware — a question of practical importance for practitioners choosing whether to deploy DSI with a marginal drafter.

**Consequence.** DSI shares a fundamental limitation with all speculative inference methods: it can only accelerate tokens that the drafter correctly predicts. If a problem is too hard for the drafter — if the target model possesses capabilities the drafter lacks — then the drafter's acceptance rate on that problem will be low, DSI's speculative tree will be quickly pruned, and the algorithm will provide minimal speedup regardless of available hardware. This is analogous to the reference example's finding that test-time compute provides no benefit on the hardest problems because the base model cannot produce correct solutions. In DSI's case, the "hard problem" is any prompt where the drafter and target have systematically different output distributions — e.g., tasks requiring knowledge the drafter lacks, or reasoning patterns the drafter cannot approximate.

The paper's model pairs (same-family drafters) are designed to maximize acceptance rates, which means the experiments test DSI in its most favorable regime. The paper does not evaluate DSI with a weak drafter (low acceptance rate) on hard prompts, which would characterize the lower bound of DSI's usefulness.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The offline simulation heatmaps (Figure 2) show DSI speedup declining toward 1.0 at low acceptance rates. Table 2's lowest acceptance rate is 58% (Vicuna-13B/Vicuna-68M on Alpaca), which still yields 1.41× speedup. The paper does not provide a threshold analysis: at what acceptance rate and hardware budget does DSI cease to be worthwhile? This threshold would depend on the cost of additional GPUs and the value of latency reduction, but the paper offers no framework for making this decision.

**Mitigation status.** Not directly addressed. The paper's central claim — DSI is faster than SI "given any drafters" (Abstract) — is technically true (DSI is never slower), but the magnitude of the speedup can be arbitrarily small if the acceptance rate is low, to the point where the hardware cost may not be justified. The paper does not discuss this cost-effectiveness boundary or provide guidance on minimum acceptance rates for viable deployment.
