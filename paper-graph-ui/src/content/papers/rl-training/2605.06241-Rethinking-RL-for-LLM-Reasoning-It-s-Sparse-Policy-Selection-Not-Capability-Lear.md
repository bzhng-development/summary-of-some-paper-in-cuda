# Rethinking RL for LLM Reasoning: It’s Sparse Policy Selection, Not Capability Learning

**ArXiv:** [2605.06241](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.06241)

## 🎯 Pitch

RL improves LLM reasoning not by teaching new strategies, but by reranking just 1–3% of tokens at high-uncertainty decision points where the base model already considers the correct answer. The authors match or beat full RL performance while dropping compute costs a thousandfold, using an RL-free method that simply steers the model’s existing top candidates with contrastive loss.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper analyzes why reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) improves LLM math reasoning, finding through token-level analysis that RL's beneficial effect is a sparse, entropy-localized reranking of tokens the base model already favors, with only 1–3% of token positions affected and promoted tokens always within the base model's top-5 alternatives. The paper translates this mechanistic insight into **REASONMAXXER**, an RL-free method that applies an advantage-weighted contrastive loss only at entropy-gated decision points — positions where the base model's token-level entropy exceeds a threshold — using a few hundred offline rollouts and no online generation. Across three model families, six scales, and six math reasoning benchmarks, REASONMAXXER matches or exceeds full RL performance while requiring only 50 training problems and single-digit GPU-hours, a reduction in training cost of roughly three orders of magnitude (~$4–25 vs. $100–$100,000 for RL baselines), establishing that the RL optimization loop is not a prerequisite for reasoning improvement when the base model already possesses correct solution paths within its sampling distribution.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Is RL Actually Teaching New Reasoning, or Just Selecting Existing Paths?

The paper begins with a fundamental challenge to the prevailing assumption in LLM post-training: that reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) teaches language models genuinely new reasoning strategies. Since the release of DeepSeek-R1, GRPO-based training, and OpenAI's o1 series, the field has converged on RL as the standard method for improving mathematical and code reasoning. The implicit model is that RL, analogous to how it discovered novel strategies in Go and chess, enables LLMs to acquire new reasoning patterns through reward-driven exploration.

The paper questions this entire framing. The specific gap it identifies is **whether the RL optimization loop itself is necessary**, or whether RL is merely performing a much simpler function — steering the model toward solution paths it already contains — that could be accomplished through cheaper, more direct means.

This is not an academic nitpick. If RL is genuinely teaching new capabilities, then the heavy computational infrastructure (hundreds to tens of thousands of GPU-hours, online generation, reward normalization, policy gradient estimation) is a necessary cost of capability acquisition. But if RL is merely redistributing probability mass over existing capabilities — a **sparse policy selection** problem rather than **capability learning** — then the same gains should be achievable through a lightweight, RL-free procedure that directly targets the relevant decision points. The paper's central wager is that the latter is true, and that the field is collectively over-investing in RL infrastructure for what is fundamentally a selection problem.

### Why This Matters: Practical and Theoretical Significance

The practical stakes are enormous. Training a reasoning model with RL is expensive: SimpleRL-Zoo costs ~$200–$5,737 for GRPO training on Qwen2.5 models, Open-Reasoner-Zero costs ~$1,200–$103,000 for PPO, and General-Reasoner costs ~$4,600 (all costs from Table 3). These costs create barriers to entry, limit the pace of experimentation, and concentrate reasoning model development in well-resourced labs. If the same improvements can be obtained for $4–25 on a single GPU in minutes, the entire post-training pipeline becomes democratized.

The theoretical significance is equally important. Understanding *what RL does* at the mechanistic level — rather than just observing that it works — informs how we should design future training methods. If RL's beneficial effect is a sparse correction concentrated at high-entropy decision points, then:
- Gradient updates applied uniformly across all tokens (as GRPO does) waste compute on positions that carry no useful signal.
- Online generation and iterative policy improvement may be unnecessary if the corrective signal can be extracted from a small set of offline rollouts.
- The appropriate parameter budget for post-training is much smaller than full-parameter fine-tuning (hundreds of thousands to millions of parameters rather than billions).

The paper's reframing — from "RL teaches reasoning" to "RL selects among existing reasoning paths at decision points" — has downstream implications for how we architect post-training pipelines, allocate compute between pretraining and post-training, and diagnose failures in reasoning models.

### Prior Approaches and Where They Fall Short

The paper identifies a growing body of evidence that challenges the capability-acquisition view, but notes that this evidence has not yet been synthesized into a mechanistic characterization that enables RL-free methods.

**The pass@k discrepancy (Yue et al., 2025).** The most direct challenge to the capability-acquisition view comes from pass@k analysis. Yue et al. show that while RL improves pass@1 (the model's first answer is more often correct), base models achieve *higher* pass@k at large k — meaning the base model's sampling distribution already contains correct solutions that RL merely promotes to higher probability. If RL were teaching genuinely new strategies, we would expect the RL model to outperform the base model even at large k, since it would have access to reasoning patterns the base model never produces. The observed pattern — base model wins at large k — is consistent with RL performing probability redistribution, not capability expansion.

**The "edge of competence" finding (Zhang et al., 2025; Davis and Recht, 2025).** Zhang et al. provide controlled experimental evidence that RL produces genuine gains *only* on problems where the base model already succeeds non-trivially — what they call the model's "edge of competence." On problems far below the model's capability (always solved) or far above it (never solved), RL provides no benefit. Davis and Recht prove theoretically that popular RL algorithms with binary rewards reduce to stochastic gradient ascent on monotone transforms of the probability of a correct answer, and that such optimization is profitable only when the base model already has non-zero success probability. If RL were teaching new strategies, we would expect gains even on problems where the base model currently fails — the new strategies would enable success where none existed before. The observed pattern contradicts this.

**Token-level concentration (Wang et al., 2025a).** Wang et al. identify that RL's gradient signal is concentrated at high-entropy "forking tokens" — positions where the model's probability mass is spread across multiple plausible continuations. They show that restricting GRPO updates to these positions matches training on all tokens, implying that the gradient signal everywhere else is noise. This is the closest prior work to the paper's token-level analysis, but Wang et al. remain *within* the RL framework: they make RL more efficient rather than eliminating it. The paper's innovation is to ask: if RL's useful signal is this localized, can we target those locations directly without any RL at all?

**Structural concentration (Park et al., 2025).** Park et al. find that RL operates through a small number of emergent attention heads, suggesting the change is structurally low-dimensional. Again, this points toward simplicity but doesn't escape the RL framework.

**RL-free alternatives and their limitations.** Several methods improve reasoning without RL: STaR (Zelikman et al., 2022) and rejection-sampling fine-tuning (Yuan et al., 2023) train on the model's own correct solutions, but apply uniform token-level losses rather than targeting decision points. Best-of-N and MCMC sampling (Karan and Du, 2025) improve reasoning at inference time without modifying the policy, but provide no permanent policy improvement. DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023) offers an offline preference-based alternative but operates at the sequence level, missing the token-level sparsity the paper identifies as crucial. None of these methods explicitly leverage the entropy-localized decision-point structure that the paper's mechanistic analysis reveals.

**Where these approaches collectively fall short.** The paper's diagnosis is that prior work, even when identifying pieces of the puzzle (sparsity, concentration at forks, low-dimensionality), has not connected these observations into a causal, mechanistic picture that enables RL-free training. Wang et al. show *where* RL's signal concentrates but don't demonstrate that the specific token choices at those positions *cause* the accuracy gain. Yue et al. show *that* base models contain correct solutions but don't characterize *which* token-level decisions need to change to promote them. Park et al. show *that* the change is structurally sparse but don't test whether that sparsity can be exploited for training. The paper's gap is the absence of a unified characterization — sparse, entropy-localized, low-dimensional, causally sufficient — that would justify eliminating RL entirely.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper positions itself as the natural endpoint of the convergence of the above threads. It frames RL's effect on reasoning not as capability acquisition but as **sparse policy selection**: committing to the right branch at a handful of decision points where the model is uncertain which reasoning path to follow. This reframing is operationalized through four claims that build on each other:

1. **RL's edits are token-level sparse and entropy-localized** (Section 3.1): only 1–3% of token positions are affected, and these positions have 5–12× higher base-model entropy than unchanged positions. RL does not introduce tokens outside the base model's top-5 candidates — it consistently promotes one of the base model's existing alternatives.

2. **These sparse edits are causally sufficient** (Section 3.2): an oracle intervention that replaces the base model's token with the RL model's preferred token *only* at the 1–3% of affected positions recovers RL's full accuracy gain. Random substitutions at the same positions fail. This establishes causality — it's not just correlation, it's the mechanism.

3. **The critical positions are locatable without RL** (Section 3.3): base-model entropy alone, with a simple threshold, identifies the positions where RL would intervene. An entropy-gated intervention (using the RL model's token choices, but selecting positions by entropy threshold only) largely matches full oracle correction. This means the *where* of RL's correction is teacher-free.

4. **The correction is low-dimensional in parameter space** (Section 4): a LoRA adapter with rank 32 (0.27–0.49% of model parameters) trained via KL distillation on just 100 problems captures RL's full accuracy gain. This means the *what* of RL's correction is representable in a tiny parameter budget.

These four claims together justify REASONMAXXER: if (a) RL's effect is a sparse token-level reranking, (b) those specific rerankings cause the accuracy gain, (c) the positions needing reranking are identifiable from base-model entropy alone, and (d) the entire correction is low-dimensional, then the RL optimization loop is logically unnecessary. One should be able to learn the correction directly from base-model rollouts at entropy-gated positions using a small adapter.

The paper's positioning relative to prior work is therefore:
- **Beyond Wang et al. (2025a):** not just identifying where RL's signal concentrates, but proving causality (oracle intervention) and demonstrating that the signal can be captured without RL.
- **Beyond Yue et al. (2025) and Zhang et al. (2025):** not just observing that base models contain correct solutions, but characterizing the *mechanism* (token-level reranking at decision points) by which RL promotes them, and exploiting that mechanism for RL-free training.
- **Beyond Davis and Recht (2025):** not just proving theoretically that RL reduces to probability optimization, but providing the empirical token-level characterization that makes the theoretical result operationally actionable.
- **Beyond Park et al. (2025):** not just finding that RL is structurally sparse, but demonstrating that the sparsity is sufficient for learning from scratch via contrastive fine-tuning.
- **Beyond RL-free alternatives (STaR, DPO, rejection sampling):** not just avoiding RL, but explicitly targeting the entropy-localized decision points that the mechanistic analysis shows are the locus of improvement, using a contrastive loss that simultaneously reinforces correct decisions and penalizes incorrect ones.

The paper's ultimate claim is that **the problem RL solves in this domain is sparse policy selection, not capability acquisition**, and that recognizing this simplicity opens the door to far more efficient post-training methods. REASONMAXXER is the proof of concept: a method that matches full RL performance while being orders of magnitude cheaper, serving as evidence that the community's default investment in heavy RL infrastructure is disproportionate to the problem's actual complexity.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

REASONMAXXER is a lightweight, RL-free post-training method that improves an LLM's math reasoning accuracy by applying targeted contrastive fine-tuning only at a small set of "decision points" — token positions where the base model is genuinely uncertain which reasoning branch to follow. The system solves the problem of extracting the useful signal that RL normally discovers through expensive trial-and-error exploration, but does so directly: it generates a few hundred rollouts from the frozen base model, identifies high-uncertainty positions using the model's own token-level entropy, and learns which token to prefer at each such position by contrasting successful and unsuccessful rollouts on the same problem. The "shape" of the solution is a minimal training procedure that requires no online generation, no reward normalization, no policy gradient estimation, and only a tiny fraction of the parameters (a rank-32 LoRA adapter), yet matches or exceeds full RL performance across model families and scales.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The REASONMAXXER system has five major components, executed in a fixed pipeline:

1. **Rollout Generator** — samples multiple completions from the frozen base model on a pool of candidate training problems, producing both correct and incorrect trajectories for the same prompts.

2. **Problem Selector** — filters the candidate pool to retain only problems where the base model achieves mixed success (some rollouts correct, some incorrect), discarding problems that are trivially easy or impossibly hard for the base model.

3. **Decision-Point Locator** — computes per-token entropy from the frozen base model on every retained rollout and marks positions exceeding a threshold `$\tau$` as "decision points" — the high-uncertainty locations where the model's token choice meaningfully affects the downstream reasoning trajectory.

4. **Contrastive Trainer** — applies an advantage-weighted cross-entropy loss at decision points (encouraging tokens from correct rollouts, penalizing tokens from incorrect ones) while anchoring all other positions to the base model's original distribution via a KL divergence penalty.

5. **LoRA Adapter** — the only trainable component: a rank-32 low-rank adapter applied to all attention projection matrices (Q, K, V, O), containing 0.27–0.49% of the base model's parameters, which absorbs the entire corrective signal.

Information flows sequentially: candidate problems → base model rollouts → mixed-success filter → entropy computation → decision-point masking → contrastive loss + KL anchoring → adapter update. There is no feedback loop, no online generation, and no iterative policy improvement — the entire training completes in a single epoch.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the problem selection mechanism, because it operationalizes the "edge of competence" principle that the paper's mechanistic analysis (Sections 3–4) shows is necessary for learning from outcome feedback — without mixed-success problems, there is no contrastive signal to exploit.

- **Second**, the entropy-based decision-point identification, because it is the key bridge between the paper's mechanistic findings (RL edits concentrate at high-entropy positions) and the practical training procedure — this is how REASONMAXXER locates *where* to intervene without an RL teacher.

- **Third**, the advantage-weighted contrastive loss with base anchoring, because it is the core learning objective that translates "correct vs. incorrect rollout on the same problem" into token-level preference signals — the mathematical machinery that replaces policy gradients.

- **Fourth**, the architectural choices (LoRA adapter, module selection, rank) and training hyperparameters, because they operationalize the low-dimensionality finding from Section 4 and determine the method's computational efficiency.

- **Fifth**, a synthesis of design choices and their justifications, connecting each component back to the mechanistic evidence that motivated it, so the reader understands not just *what* the method does but *why* each design decision follows from the paper's central claims.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **method paper** whose core idea is that the beneficial effect of RL on math reasoning can be captured by a simple, offline contrastive procedure that targets only high-entropy decision points, because RL's effect is a sparse policy selection signal rather than a capability-learning signal. The method is constructed as a direct operationalization of the mechanistic findings in Sections 3 and 4: every design choice in REASONMAXXER maps to a specific empirical or causal claim from the token-level analysis.

---

#### Problem Selection: Filtering for the Edge of Competence

The first stage of REASONMAXXER is selecting which training problems to use. The paper draws on a consistent finding from both theoretical work (Davis and Recht, 2025) and empirical analysis (Zhang et al., 2025; Section 3.2 of this paper): outcome-based feedback provides a useful learning signal *only* when the base model has non-trivial but imperfect success on a problem. If the model always gets a problem right (pass rate = 1), there are no incorrect rollouts to penalize, so the contrastive loss collapses to pure positive reinforcement — useful but insufficient, as the ablation in Section 6.3 shows. If the model always gets a problem wrong (pass rate = 0), there are no correct rollouts to reinforce, so the model has no target behavior to imitate. Only in the intermediate regime — the "edge of competence" — does each problem supply both correct and incorrect trajectories that differ specifically at decision points, providing a two-sided contrastive signal.

The selection procedure works as follows:

1. **Candidate pool.** The paper starts with a pool of 150 math problems balanced across difficulty levels. The exact composition of this pool is not fully specified, but the balancing ensures that the selected problems span the range where the base model exhibits mixed success, avoiding a dataset skewed entirely toward easy or hard problems.

2. **Rollout generation.** For each of the 150 candidate problems, the frozen base model generates `$K = 20$` completions at non-zero temperature (temperature = 0.6, top-p = 0.95). Each completion is a full solution attempt — a chain-of-thought reasoning trace ending in a boxed final answer. The temperature and top-p values are standard choices that balance diversity against coherence; they are held constant across all model families.

3. **Correctness checking.** Each completion is compared against the ground-truth answer using the problem's verifiable outcome. A rollout receives a binary reward `$r_i \in \{0, 1\}$` indicating whether it arrived at the correct final answer. The paper uses standard answer extraction and matching for each benchmark (exact templates in Appendix C), so no learned verifier or reward model is needed — the correctness signal comes directly from the problem's ground-truth answer.

4. **Mixed-success filter.** For each problem, the pass rate is computed as the fraction of the 20 rollouts that are correct. Problems where the pass rate is strictly between 0 and 1 (i.e., at least one correct rollout and at least one incorrect rollout) are retained; all others are discarded. From the initial pool of 150 problems, this filter retains exactly 50 problems. The paper's ablation in Section 6.3 notes that the exact width of the pass-rate window is not critical — what matters is the existence of both correct and incorrect rollouts on the same problem, which creates the contrastive signal.

5. **Training set composition.** The 50 retained problems, each with 20 rollouts, yield 1,000 training sequences. This is the entire training set — two orders of magnitude smaller than the datasets used by RL baselines (SimpleRL-Zoo uses ~8,000 MATH problems; Open-Reasoner-Zero uses 57,000 problems). The paper argues this small dataset size is not an incidental optimization but follows directly from the mechanistic picture: because RL's useful signal is concentrated at a sparse set of decision points, a handful of mixed-success problems provides sufficient contrastive supervision.

**Why this filter is necessary, not optional.** The edge-of-competence filter is not merely a data-efficiency heuristic; it is a logical requirement for the contrastive loss to function. The loss operates by comparing tokens at the same position across correct and incorrect rollouts on the same problem. If all rollouts on a problem are correct, the negative term in the contrastive loss has no examples to penalize, and the model only learns to reinforce what it already does — supervised fine-tuning by another name, which the ablation shows underperforms. If all rollouts are incorrect, the positive term has no examples to reinforce, and the model lacks a target to imitate. Only mixed-success problems supply both signals at the *same* decision points — the model made different token choices at those positions in correct versus incorrect rollouts, and the contrastive loss can learn which choice was consequential.

#### Decision-Point Identification via Entropy Gating

The second stage locates *where* in each training sequence the contrastive loss should be applied. This is the component that directly operationalizes the paper's central mechanistic finding: RL edits are concentrated at high-entropy positions where the base model's probability mass is spread across multiple plausible next tokens (Table 2, Figure 1). The paper needs a way to identify these positions using only the base model — no RL teacher, no external supervision, no ground-truth labels — and it uses the base model's own token-level entropy as the proxy.

**Token-level entropy definition.** For an autoregressive language model with parameters `$\theta$`, the entropy at generation step `$t$` is:

$$H_t = -\sum_{v \in V} \pi_\theta(v \mid q, o_{<t}) \log \pi_\theta(v \mid q, o_{<t})$$

where `$V$` is the vocabulary (typically 50,000–250,000 tokens), `$q$` is the prompt, `$o_{<t}$` is the sequence of tokens generated so far (the context), and `$\pi_\theta(v \mid q, o_{<t})$` is the base model's predicted probability for token `$v$` at position `$t$` given that context.

**What it computes.** For each position in the generated sequence, `$H_t$` measures how "spread out" the model's probability distribution is over the vocabulary. If the model is highly confident about the next token (e.g., 99% mass on one token), `$H_t$` is close to 0 — the distribution is peaked, and the model is committing decisively. If the model is uncertain (e.g., 30% on one token, 25% on another, 15% on a third, with probability smeared across many candidates), `$H_t$` is larger — the distribution is flat, and the model's choice at this position is genuinely underdetermined by its current knowledge.

**Why this form.** Entropy is the natural measure of uncertainty for a probability distribution. It has the property that it is maximized when the distribution is uniform (maximal uncertainty) and minimized when the distribution is a one-hot (zero uncertainty), and it weights uncertainty contributions log-probabilistically — a token with 10% probability contributes more to entropy than ten tokens each with 1% probability, which matches the intuition that a few strong alternatives indicate a meaningful fork while many weak alternatives indicate random noise. The paper could have used alternatives like max probability or the gap between top-two probabilities, but entropy captures the full shape of the distribution rather than just its peak, making it more robust to cases where the top two tokens are nearly tied but the remaining mass is negligible versus cases where probability is genuinely diffused across many candidates.

**Thresholding to define decision points.** Once `$H_t$` is computed for every generated token in every rollout, the paper defines a position `$t$` as a **decision point** if `$H_t > \tau$`, where `$\tau$` is a model-family-specific threshold. The set of decision points for a given rollout is:

$$\mathcal{D} = \{t : H_t > \tau\}$$

**Threshold selection.** The threshold `$\tau$` is not set arbitrarily. The paper sweeps `$\tau$` over the set {1.2, 1.4, 1.6, 1.8} on a small held-out validation set of 50 problems and selects the value that maximizes validation accuracy. Figure 4 shows this sweep for Qwen2.5-1.5B: optimal MATH-500 performance (0.502 pass@1) is achieved at `$\tau = 1.4$`, which gates approximately 5.2% of tokens as decision points. The paper notes that performance is robust over a broad range (1.0 to 2.2), and that a second peak appears at `$\tau = 1.8$` (gating 2.6% of tokens), which closely matches RL's observed intervention rate of 2.11% from Table 2. This insensitivity is important: it means REASONMAXXER does not require a precise replication of RL's exact sparsity pattern — as long as the threshold selects a plausible set of high-entropy positions, the contrastive signal is effective.

**What makes this threshold teacher-free.** The entropy computation requires only the frozen base model and the generated tokens — no RL-trained model, no correctness labels, no external verifier. This is the key practical difference from the oracle experiment in Section 3.2, which required the RL teacher to both *locate* and *correct* the important positions. Section 3.3 already demonstrated that entropy gating can substitute for the teacher's location knowledge: an entropy-gated oracle intervention (correcting tokens at high-entropy positions using teacher preferences) achieved comparable accuracy to the full oracle intervention. REASONMAXXER takes the next step: it uses entropy to locate the positions *and* learns the corrective token preferences from scratch using the contrastive loss, with no teacher involved at all.

**Computational cost.** Computing `$H_t$` for every token in every training sequence requires one forward pass through the base model per sequence to obtain the full next-token distributions. Since the base model is frozen and the rollouts have already been generated (for the correctness check in the problem selection stage), this forward pass can reuse the cached keys and values from rollout generation, making the entropy computation cheap — it is essentially a byproduct of the rollout generation that the system already performs.

**Scope of decision points.** Decision points are defined exclusively on generated completion tokens (the model's solution), not on prompt tokens or padding. This is because the model does not *choose* prompt tokens — they are given — and padding tokens carry no semantic information. The positions where the model's choice matters are exactly the positions where it generates content, and among those, only the high-entropy ones where its choice is consequential.

#### Advantage-Weighted Contrastive Loss with Base Anchoring

This is the core learning objective that replaces RL's policy gradient. Given that the system has (a) a set of rollouts for a problem, some correct and some incorrect, and (b) a mask identifying decision points in each rollout, the loss must teach the model to prefer the token choices that appear in correct rollouts and avoid those that appear in incorrect rollouts, but *only* at decision points — everywhere else, the model's behavior should remain unchanged.

**Per-rollout advantage computation.** For a single problem with `$G$` rollouts, each rollout `$i$` has a binary correctness indicator `$r_i \in \{0, 1\}$`. The paper computes a normalized advantage for each rollout:

$$A_i = \frac{r_i - \bar{r}}{\sigma_r + \epsilon}$$

where `$\bar{r}$` is the mean correctness across all rollouts for that problem, `$\sigma_r$` is the standard deviation of correctness across all rollouts for that problem, and `$\epsilon$` is a small constant for numerical stability (presumably `$10^{-8}$` based on standard practice, though not explicitly stated in the training configuration).

**What it computes.** This is a per-problem group normalization identical in form to GRPO's advantage computation (Equation 1 in the paper), but applied once offline rather than at every RL step. For a problem where half the rollouts are correct (`$\bar{r} = 0.5$`), correct rollouts receive `$A_i = +1/(2\sigma_r)$` and incorrect ones receive `$A_i = -1/(2\sigma_r)$`. For a problem where 80% of rollouts are correct (`$\bar{r} = 0.8$`), correct rollouts receive a smaller positive advantage and incorrect ones receive a larger negative advantage — the normalization automatically adjusts for class imbalance, so a problem with few incorrect rollouts still exerts strong negative pressure on those rare failures.

**Why this form.** The group normalization serves two purposes. First, it centers the advantages so that correct and incorrect rollouts receive symmetric positive and negative weights — without this, the loss would be dominated by whichever class has more examples, distorting the gradient. Second, it scales the advantages inversely with the standard deviation, so that problems with clear separation between correct and incorrect rollouts (low `$\sigma_r$`) produce larger advantages than problems where correctness is noisy (high `$\sigma_r$`). This is analogous to how GRPO normalizes advantages within a group to reduce variance, but applied once across the static set of rollouts rather than dynamically during training.

**Decision-point loss.** At every token position `$t$` in the decision set `$\mathcal{D}$` for rollout `$i$`, the loss term is:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{dec}} = -\sum_{t \in \mathcal{D}} A_i \cdot \log p_\theta(x_t \mid x_{<t})$$

where `$x_t$` is the token that was actually generated at position `$t$` in rollout `$i$`, and `$p_\theta(x_t \mid x_{<t})$` is the adapter-augmented model's predicted probability for that token given the preceding context.

**What it computes.** This is an advantage-weighted cross-entropy loss applied *only* at decision points. When `$A_i > 0$` (correct rollout), the loss is negative — minimizing it *increases* the log-probability of the generated token, reinforcing the model's choice at that position. When `$A_i < 0$` (incorrect rollout), the loss is positive — minimizing it *decreases* the log-probability of the generated token, penalizing the model's choice at that position. The magnitude of the reinforcement or penalty is proportional to `$|A_i|$`, so rollouts with more extreme advantages (very correct or very incorrect) exert stronger gradient signals.

**Why this form over alternatives.** Several design choices are deliberate. First, the loss operates at the *token* level, not the sequence level — it only looks at individual token probabilities, not full-sequence probabilities. This is exactly the sparsity the mechanistic analysis demands: only a small fraction of tokens carry the corrective signal, and applying a sequence-level loss (as DPO or rejection-sampling fine-tuning do) would dilute that signal across irrelevant positions. Second, the advantage weighting `$A_i$` provides a principled soft weighting rather than a binary "correct vs. incorrect" split — rollouts that are correct in a problem where most rollouts are correct receive less reinforcement than rollouts that are correct in a problem where most rollouts are incorrect, because the latter case provides more information about which token choices were decisive. Third, the loss uses the *generated* token as the target, not a teacher-forced correct token — it reinforces or penalizes what the model actually produced, which is exactly what RL's policy gradient does, but computed offline from static rollouts rather than online from the current policy.

**Why the contrastive (two-sided) form matters.** The ablation in Section 6.3 demonstrates that removing the negative term — i.e., using only `$A_i > 0$` rollouts, equivalent to supervised fine-tuning on correct trajectories — raises MATH-500 pass@1 from 0.298 (base model) to 0.398, a meaningful improvement. However, this is far below both the RL model (0.496) and full REASONMAXXER (0.502). The contrastive term that *penalizes* incorrect decisions therefore contributes roughly half the total gain. Why? Because at a decision point, the base model's probability is distributed across multiple plausible tokens — one of which leads to a correct trajectory and others of which lead to incorrect ones. Positive-only training increases the probability of the correct-path token but does nothing to decrease the probability of the incorrect-path tokens, so the model's distribution remains smeared and still sometimes samples the wrong branch. The negative term explicitly suppresses the incorrect branches, sharpening the distribution so the model commits more decisively to the correct path. This is the same logic that makes contrastive losses (as in DPO or RLHF) outperform pure supervised fine-tuning: you need to teach the model not just what to do, but what *not* to do.

**Advantage clipping.** The paper notes (Appendix B) that advantages are clipped to the range `$[-2.5, 2.5]$` before computing the loss. This prevents any single rollout from dominating the gradient — e.g., if a problem has exactly one correct rollout out of 20, that rollout would receive an extremely large advantage under the normalization, and clipping caps its influence.

**Base-anchoring loss.** At every token position *outside* the decision set `$\mathcal{D}$`, the loss is the Kullback-Leibler divergence from the frozen base model to the adapter-augmented model:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{anchor}} = \sum_{t \notin \mathcal{D}} \text{KL}\big(p_{\text{base}}(\cdot \mid x_{<t}) \,\|\, p_\theta(\cdot \mid x_{<t})\big)$$

where `$p_{\text{base}}(\cdot \mid x_{<t})$` is the frozen base model's full next-token distribution and `$p_\theta(\cdot \mid x_{<t})$` is the adapter-augmented model's distribution.

**What it computes.** This term penalizes any change in the model's behavior at non-decision positions. The KL divergence is zero if the two distributions are identical and positive if they differ, so minimizing this term forces the adapter to preserve the base model's original output distribution everywhere except at the explicitly selected decision points.

**Why this form.** The KL anchor serves three interconnected functions that all follow from the mechanistic analysis. First, it is the direct operationalization of the finding that RL does not change the model's behavior at ~97–99% of token positions (Table 2): those positions should remain identical to the base model, and the KL penalty enforces this. Second, it prevents the small adapter from overfitting to spurious correlations in the tiny training set — with only 1,000 sequences, an unregularized model could easily memorize surface-level patterns that don't generalize. The KL anchor acts as a strong regularizer by pulling the model back to the base distribution on the vast majority of tokens. Third, it is architecturally necessary for the LoRA adapter to function: since the adapter modifies only a tiny fraction of the model's parameters, large changes at non-decision positions would require the adapter to "fight" the frozen base model weights, leading to unstable training. The KL term ensures the adapter's energy is focused on the small set of positions where change is both necessary and sufficient.

**Total loss.** The final training objective is a weighted sum:

$$\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{dec}} + \lambda \cdot \mathcal{L}_{\text{anchor}}$$

with `$\lambda = 0.2$`. The decision loss `$\mathcal{L}_{\text{dec}}$` and anchor loss `$\mathcal{L}_{\text{anchor}}$` are each averaged over their respective token masks before summing — the decision loss over all decision-point tokens across all rollouts, and the anchor loss over all non-decision prediction tokens (including both prompt and completion tokens, but excluding padding).

**Why $\lambda = 0.2$? ** The paper does not provide a theoretical justification or an ablation over `$\lambda$` values. However, the choice of 0.2 likely reflects a deliberate asymmetry: the anchor term covers ~95% of tokens (the non-decision positions), while the decision term covers only ~5%. If `$\lambda$` were 1.0, the anchor loss — being summed over 20× more tokens — would dominate the total loss and prevent any meaningful change at decision points. The 0.2 weight down-weights the anchor term so that the decision loss, despite covering far fewer tokens, contributes comparably to the total gradient. This is a standard technique in multi-task learning where tasks have different numbers of active samples.

---

#### LoRA Adapter Architecture and Training Configuration

The REASONMAXXER loss is implemented by training a LoRA adapter attached to the frozen base model. This architectural choice is not an independent design decision; it is the direct consequence of the low-dimensionality finding in Section 4: a rank-32 adapter containing 0.27–0.49% of the base model's parameters can absorb RL's entire distributional change, so the same parameter budget is sufficient to learn the contrastive signal from scratch.

**LoRA adapter specification.** The adapter uses rank 32, alpha 64, and dropout 0.0, applied to all attention projection matrices: query (Q), key (K), value (V), and output (O). The base model is completely frozen; only the low-rank matrices `$A$` and `$B$` (where `$W' = W + BA$`, with `$B \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}} \times r}$`, `$A \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times d_{\text{model}}}$`, and `$r = 32$`) are updated. The LoRA alpha of 64 means the `$BA$` term is scaled by `$\alpha / r = 64/32 = 2$`, giving the adapter a larger effective step size during training.

**Why attention modules only.** The paper's compression ablation in Appendix A (Table 4) shows that applying LoRA only to the output projection (`$W_O$`) with rank 8 (688K parameters, 0.04% of model) achieves pass@1 within 1 point of the full QKVO rank-32 adapter on MATH-500. This suggests that RL's correction is concentrated in the output layer: the base model already attends to mostly the right information, and RL mainly changes how that attended information is projected into the hidden state to influence next-token choices. The paper conservatively uses the full QKVO configuration for REASONMAXXER to provide sufficient capacity, but notes that even more aggressive compression is possible.

**Optimizer and learning rate schedule.** Training uses AdamW with default betas (0.9, 0.999) and `$\epsilon = 10^{-8}$`. The learning rate follows a linear warmup followed by linear decay to zero. Warmup steps are model-family-specific: 50 steps for Qwen and DeepSeek models, 30 steps for Mistral. Weight decay is 0.0 for Qwen and DeepSeek models, 0.01 for Mistral. Gradient clipping is applied at a maximum norm of 1.0.

**Batch and gradient accumulation.** All models are trained with an effective batch size of 8, achieved through a per-device batch size of 1 and gradient accumulation over 8 steps. A single training epoch is used for all models. The total number of optimizer steps therefore equals `(number of training sequences) / 8`, which is approximately 125 steps for the 1,000-sequence training set.

**Why a single epoch.** The paper uses a single epoch for all models, with no early stopping on validation loss. This is a deliberate design choice linked to the size of the training set: with only 1,000 sequences, multiple epochs would risk overfitting the adapter to spurious correlations in the small dataset, even with the KL anchor penalty. A single epoch with learning rate decay to zero ensures the adapter takes a fixed number of gradient steps and stops, with model selection done by evaluating on a fixed 50-problem validation split.

**Sequence handling.** The maximum sequence length is 8,192 tokens, with right truncation applied to longer sequences. This is sufficient for the chain-of-thought solutions in math reasoning benchmarks, which typically range from a few hundred to a few thousand tokens. Decision points are defined exclusively on generated completion tokens; prompt tokens and padding positions are excluded from both the decision loss and the anchor loss computation.

**Why LoRA rather than full fine-tuning.** The paper does not consider full-parameter fine-tuning because it would violate the core claim: if RL's correction is low-dimensional and representable in a tiny adapter, then full-parameter training is unnecessary overkill. Full fine-tuning would also risk catastrophic forgetting of the base model's general capabilities — the KL anchor provides some protection, but modifying billions of parameters with only 1,000 training sequences would almost certainly degrade performance on non-math tasks. The LoRA adapter provides a natural capacity bottleneck that matches the problem's inherent dimensionality.

**Output format and answer extraction.** The paper uses model-family-specific prompt templates and answer extraction rules, summarized in Appendix C. For Qwen2.5 and Qwen3 models, prompts use a raw completion format without chat templates ("Solve the following math problem step by step. Put your final answer in \boxed{}. Problem: {problem} Solution:"). For DeepSeek-R1-Distill, the native chat template is applied. For Mistral-7B, a simple instruction-answer format is used ("Question: {problem} Answer: Let's think step by step."). Answer extraction uses the \boxed{} notation: the model is prompted to put its final answer in a boxed expression, and the extracted answer is compared against the ground truth using the MATH grading function from Hendrycks et al. (2021).

#### Synthesis: How Design Choices Map to Mechanistic Evidence

Every component of REASONMAXXER is a direct operationalization of a specific finding from the paper's token-level analysis. This mapping is worth making explicit because it demonstrates that the method is not an ad hoc collection of tricks but a principled translation of mechanistic understanding into training procedure:

- **Mixed-success problem filter** ↔ **Edge-of-competence requirement:** The mechanistic analysis established that RL's useful signal exists only on problems where the base model has non-trivial but imperfect success (Sections 3.2, 5.1; Zhang et al., 2025; Davis and Recht, 2025). The filter guarantees every training problem provides the two-sided contrastive signal that RL's gradient concentrates at decision points.

- **Entropy gating for decision-point identification** ↔ **RL edits are entropy-localized:** Table 2 and Figure 1 showed that reranked positions have 5–12× higher base-model entropy than unchanged positions, and Section 3.3 demonstrated that entropy gating can replace teacher supervision for *locating* these positions. The entropy threshold `$\tau$` is the teacher-free proxy for where RL would intervene.

- **Advantage-weighted cross-entropy at decision points** ↔ **RL is token-level reranking within top-5:** Table 2 showed that RL's promoted tokens are always within the base model's top-5 alternatives (mean rank ~2). The contrastive loss at decision points teaches the adapter to shift probability mass among these existing alternatives — promoting the correct-path tokens, suppressing the incorrect-path tokens — which is exactly what RL's policy gradient does, but learned offline from static rollouts rather than online from the current policy.

- **KL anchor on non-decision positions** ↔ **RL changes only 1–3% of tokens:** Table 2 showed that only 1.0–4.1% of token positions are reranked by RL. The KL anchor explicitly preserves base-model behavior on the remaining 96–99% of positions, exactly matching RL's footprint.

- **LoRA adapter (rank 32, QKVO)** ↔ **RL's correction is low-dimensional:** Section 4 demonstrated that a rank-32 adapter containing 0.27–0.49% of model parameters captures RL's full accuracy gain via KL distillation. Using the same parameter budget for REASONMAXXER ensures the inductive bias matches the problem's inherent dimensionality — the correction *should* be representable in this budget because RL's correction is.

- **Offline rollouts, single epoch** ↔ **Online generation and iterative updates are unnecessary:** The causal intervention experiment in Section 3.2 showed that the RL model's entire accuracy advantage can be reproduced by a one-shot token correction at decision points. There is no iterative refinement, no exploration-exploitation tradeoff, no need to sample from the current (changing) policy — the corrective signal is fully present in the base model's static rollouts. REASONMAXXER's offline, single-epoch design reflects this: generate rollouts once, compute the contrastive signal once, update once.

This mapping makes clear the paper's central methodological claim: **if you correctly characterize what RL does, the RL optimization loop itself is unnecessary** — the same characterization directly specifies an RL-free procedure that achieves the same result. REASONMAXXER is both a practical method and an existence proof for the paper's reframing of reasoning improvement as sparse policy selection rather than capability acquisition.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Reframing RL for Reasoning as Sparse Policy Selection, Not Capability Acquisition

The paper's most fundamental contribution is a **conceptual reframing** of what reinforcement learning does for LLM reasoning. The dominant narrative in the field — reinforced by the success of DeepSeek-R1, OpenAI o1, and GRPO-trained models — casts RL as a *capability learning* mechanism: the model explores, receives rewards, and through this process discovers genuinely new reasoning strategies it did not possess before, analogous to how AlphaGo discovered novel Go tactics through self-play. This framing justifies the heavy RL infrastructure (online generation, reward normalization, policy gradient estimation, multiple training epochs) as the necessary cost of capability acquisition.

The paper systematically dismantles this narrative and replaces it with a fundamentally different one: **RL for reasoning is sparse policy selection**. The model already contains the correct solution paths within its sampling distribution — evidence for which was accumulating in prior work (Yue et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025; Davis and Recht, 2025) — and RL's sole function is to redistribute probability mass toward those paths at a handful of critical decision points. The "RL" in "RL for reasoning" is therefore a misnomer: there is no exploration-driven discovery, no credit assignment over long horizons, no bootstrapping from intermediate states. There is only a sparse, predictable reranking of tokens the base model already favors at positions the base model already finds uncertain.

What makes this reframing distinctive is that it is not merely a negative claim ("RL doesn't do what we thought") but a **constructive replacement** with precise, falsifiable mechanics. The paper specifies exactly what "sparse policy selection" means at the token level: only 1–3% of positions are affected (Table 2), the promoted token is always within the base model's top-5 alternatives (mean rank ~2, Table 2), and the affected positions are identifiable from the base model's entropy alone (Section 3.3, Figure 2). This is not a vague intuition about "steering" or "amplification" — it is a quantitative, token-level characterization that makes specific predictions about where and how RL intervenes.

The shift from "capability acquisition" to "sparse policy selection" has downstream consequences that extend far beyond this paper. If RL is genuinely teaching new strategies, then improving reasoning requires *more* RL — better exploration, denser rewards, more sophisticated credit assignment. Research effort should go into making RL work better. But if RL is merely selecting among existing strategies at entropy-localized decision points, then the bottleneck is not RL sophistication but **decision-point identification and correction** — a problem that may admit far simpler solutions than policy gradients, as REASONMAXXER demonstrates. The reframing redirects the entire research agenda from improving RL algorithms toward characterizing and targeting the sparse correction directly.

This is a **fundamental shift** rather than an incremental refinement. Prior work (Wang et al., 2025a; Park et al., 2025) had identified pieces of this picture — gradient concentration at forking tokens, structural sparsity in attention heads — but remained within the RL framework, using these insights to make RL more efficient rather than to eliminate it. The paper's contribution is to synthesize these observations into a unified mechanistic picture and then take the logical next step: if the characterization is this simple and this complete, the RL loop is not an implementation detail but a red herring.

### Innovation 2: Establishing Causality Through Oracle Intervention, Not Just Correlation

A common weakness in mechanistic interpretability is the leap from "X correlates with Y" to "X causes Y." Prior work had observed that RL's gradient concentrates at high-entropy tokens (Wang et al., 2025a) and that RL-trained models' reasoning paths lie within the base model's sampling distribution (Yue et al., 2025). But correlation does not imply causation: it was possible that the token-level divergences between base and RL models were epiphenomenal — surface-level artifacts of the training process — while the *real* cause of accuracy improvement was some diffuse, distributed change that happened to correlate with those token choices.

The paper closes this gap with a **causal intervention experiment** (Section 3.2, Figure 2) that is deceptively simple but methodologically decisive. During deterministic decoding from the base model, at every position where the RL model would have chosen a different token (the 1–4% of reranked positions from Table 2), the experiment *replaces* the base model's token with the RL model's preferred token and continues generation from the corrected prefix. This is an oracle intervention — it requires knowing the RL model's preferences — but it cleanly isolates causality: if the accuracy gain is attributable to these specific token choices, then forcing those choices onto the base model's trajectory should recover the RL model's performance. It does, exactly. The oracle intervention reproduces the RL model's pass@1 precisely on every model pair tested (Qwen2.5-1.5B → GRPO, Qwen2.5-7B → GRPO, Qwen2.5-7B → PPO, Qwen3-4B → GRPO), using intervention rates of 1.0–4.1%.

The **random substitution control** is what elevates this from demonstration to proof. Replacing the base token with a *randomly chosen alternative* from the base model's top-20 at the same positions yields no improvement over the base model (often worse). This rules out the alternative hypothesis that any perturbation at these high-entropy positions would help — the specific token choice matters causally. The implication is stark: RL's entire accuracy advantage can be attributed to a tiny set of precise token choices at decision points. There is no additional "secret sauce" in the RL model's distribution that the oracle intervention fails to capture — otherwise the oracle would underperform the RL model.

Methodologically, this intervention is a form of **causal ablation** adapted from the interpretability literature (where it is standard to ask whether a specific neuron or attention head is *necessary* for a behavior by ablating it). The paper's innovation is applying this logic in the opposite direction: rather than ablating something to see if performance drops, it *inserts* the hypothesized causal mechanism (corrected tokens) to see if performance rises to the target level. This is a stronger test because it demonstrates sufficiency, not just necessity: the corrected tokens are not merely one of many possible ways to achieve the RL model's performance; they are *sufficient* to achieve it.

The significance of this causal evidence extends beyond the paper's own claims. It provides the strongest existing evidence that RL for reasoning is mechanistically simple — not just correlated with simplicity, but *caused by* a simple, localizable intervention. This legitimizes the search for RL-free methods that target the same mechanism: if the causal mechanism is this localized, then the RL optimization loop is solving a problem (finding which tokens to change at which positions) that might be solvable by simpler means. The paper's subsequent demonstration that entropy gating + contrastive fine-tuning can learn this correction from scratch (REASONMAXXER) is the natural endpoint of this causal insight.

### Innovation 3: Teacher-Free Localization of Critical Decision Points via Base-Model Entropy

If the oracle intervention shows *that* correcting specific tokens at specific positions causally suffices, the next question is whether those positions can be located without access to the RL teacher. This is the critical step that converts a diagnostic finding ("RL's effect is localized") into a practical capability ("we can find those locations without RL"). Prior work (Wang et al., 2025a) had observed that RL's gradient concentrates at high-entropy tokens, but did not demonstrate that entropy *alone* — without any RL-specific information — could identify the intervention sites with sufficient precision to recover RL's performance.

The paper's **entropy-gated intervention** (Section 3.3, Figure 2, blue bars) tests exactly this: at every position where the base model's token-level entropy exceeds a threshold τ, the base token is replaced with the RL teacher's preferred token — but the *choice* of which positions to intervene at is determined solely by the base model's entropy, not by comparing against the RL model. This is a strictly teacher-free localization mechanism: the entropy can be computed from the frozen base model without ever training or consulting an RL model. The result is that entropy gating largely matches the full oracle intervention. On the Qwen2.5-7B → GRPO pair, it matches exactly. On other pairs, it closely approaches the teacher's performance while touching only 1.2–8.3% of tokens.

What makes this finding intellectually significant is that it **decouples the *what* from the *where*** of RL's correction. The prior mechanistic analysis (Table 2) established that RL's edits are concentrated at high-entropy positions — this is a correlation. The entropy-gated intervention establishes that base-model entropy is a *sufficient proxy* for RL's intervention sites — entropy can substitute for the teacher's knowledge of where to intervene. This decoupling means the problem of replicating RL's effect decomposes into two independent subproblems: (1) locate the decision points (solved: use entropy), and (2) determine the correct token at each decision point (solved: use contrastive learning from mixed-success rollouts, as in REASONMAXXER). Neither subproblem requires RL.

The robustness of the entropy threshold (Figure 4) reinforces this insight. Performance is stable across a wide range of τ (1.0 to 2.2), with a broad plateau rather than a sharp peak. This means the method does not depend on precisely replicating RL's exact sparsity pattern — as long as the threshold selects a plausible set of high-entropy positions, the contrastive signal is effective. The fact that a second performance peak appears at τ = 1.8 (gating 2.6% of tokens, closely matching RL's observed intervention rate of 2.1%) is a satisfying validation that the mechanism the paper identifies is indeed the same one RL uses, but the insensitivity to τ is the more practically important finding: it means entropy gating is a reliable, low-precision heuristic, not a brittle hyperparameter.

This is a **fundamental insight** about the nature of reasoning improvement in LLMs: the model's own uncertainty, measurable without any training or external supervision, reveals the locations where intervention will be most effective. This connects to broader principles in active learning and Bayesian experimental design (where uncertainty guides data collection) and suggests that the base model already "knows what it doesn't know" — the high-entropy positions are exactly the ones where its next-token distribution reflects genuine ambiguity about the correct reasoning path. RL doesn't create these decision points; it merely resolves them.

### Innovation 4: A Shoestring-Budget Method That Serves as an Existence Proof

REASONMAXXER, as a method, would be a strong engineering contribution on its own: it matches or exceeds full RL performance across three model families, six scales, and six benchmarks while reducing training cost by roughly three orders of magnitude (~$4–25 vs. $100–$100,000). But the paper's deeper claim is that REASONMAXXER is not merely an efficient alternative to RL — it is an **existence proof** that the RL optimization loop is not a prerequisite for reasoning improvement. The method's extreme frugality (50 problems, single-digit GPU-hours, single epoch, no online generation, a tiny LoRA adapter) is not a compromise but a feature: it demonstrates that the problem RL solves is *inherently* low-complexity, because if it were not, such a simple method could not match RL's performance.

This is a distinctive argumentative strategy that is rare in ML papers. Rather than claiming "our method is better because it achieves higher accuracy" (the standard benchmark-driven claim), the paper claims "our method is better because it achieves *equivalent* accuracy with *dramatically less* compute, and this efficiency gap is evidence that the dominant paradigm overestimates the problem's complexity." The cost comparison in Table 3 is not merely a practical selling point; it is an integral part of the paper's intellectual argument. If REASONMAXXER required 5,000 problems and 100 GPU-hours to match RL, that would still be more efficient, but the conceptual force of the argument would be weaker — it would suggest that the problem is still moderately complex, just somewhat over-addressed by current RL pipelines. The fact that it requires only 50 problems and single-digit GPU-hours makes the case that the problem is *trivially* simple relative to the infrastructure currently deployed to solve it.

The **contrast with prior RL-free methods** sharpens this argument. STaR (Zelikman et al., 2022) and rejection-sampling fine-tuning (Yuan et al., 2023) also train on the model's own correct solutions without RL, but they apply uniform token-level losses rather than targeting decision points, and their performance typically falls short of RL-trained models. DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023) avoids RL but operates at the sequence level, missing the token-level sparsity the paper identifies as crucial. REASONMAXXER's distinctive contribution is not just being RL-free but being *mechanistically grounded*: every design choice (entropy gating, advantage-weighted contrastive loss, KL anchor, LoRA adapter) follows directly from the mechanistic characterization in Sections 3–4. The method works *because* the characterization is correct, and its success validates the characterization.

This reframing of the method as an existence proof rather than just an efficient alternative has implications for how the field should evaluate future post-training methods. The paper is arguing that the burden of proof has shifted: given that a $4 method can match a $100,000 RL pipeline, the default assumption should be that RL infrastructure is unnecessary unless a specific claim can be made about *why* a particular problem or domain requires capability acquisition rather than policy selection. This flips the current default, where RL is assumed necessary and efficient alternatives are viewed with skepticism unless they can explain why their simplicity is justified. The paper's mechanistic analysis provides exactly that explanation, and REASONMAXXER is the empirical demonstration that the explanation holds.

### Innovation 5: Convergent Evidence Across Model Families and RL Algorithms That the Pattern Is Universal

A final contribution that is easy to overlook but methodologically important is the **breadth of evidence** the paper marshals to support its claims. The mechanistic analysis (Section 3) spans four base/RL pairs covering three model families (Qwen2.5, Qwen3, and implicitly DeepSeek via the distilled 1.5B model used in baselines), three RL algorithms (GRPO, PPO, RLOO), and a 14× range of model scales (1.5B to 32B). The REASONMAXXER results (Table 3) extend this further: they cover three additional base models (Qwen2.5-Math-7B, Mistral-7B-v0.1, DeepSeek-R1-Distill-1.5B) and six benchmarks. Across all these settings, the core pattern holds: RL's edits are sparse, entropy-localized, and conservative (Table 2), the oracle intervention recovers RL performance (Figure 2), entropy gating locates the critical positions (Figure 2), the correction is low-dimensional (Figure 3), and REASONMAXXER matches or exceeds RL performance at a tiny fraction of the cost (Table 3).

This breadth is significant because it rules out the possibility that the paper's findings are an artifact of a particular model architecture, RL algorithm, or benchmark. If the pattern had been demonstrated only on Qwen2.5-1.5B with GRPO on MATH-500, a skeptic could reasonably argue that it might not generalize to larger models (where RL might discover more complex strategies), to different RL algorithms (PPO or RLOO might produce different token-level effects), or to different benchmarks (GSM8K or AIME might require different types of reasoning improvement). The paper's coverage largely forecloses these objections. The finding that GRPO, PPO, and RLOO all produce the same sparse-correction pattern (Table 2 includes all three) is particularly important because it suggests the pattern is not an artifact of GRPO's group normalization or clipping but a deeper property of what outcome-based RL does to language model policies on reasoning tasks.

This convergent evidence transforms the paper's claim from "here is an interesting observation about one model" to "here is a general principle about RL for reasoning." The principle — that outcome-based RL on math reasoning produces a sparse, entropy-localized, low-dimensional correction that can be captured without RL — is supported by evidence spanning enough variation in models, algorithms, and benchmarks to warrant provisional acceptance as a robust phenomenon rather than a contingent finding. This is a **methodological contribution** as much as a scientific one: the paper demonstrates that large-scale mechanistic analysis across diverse settings is feasible and can yield actionable insights that change how we build systems.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The primary mechanistic analysis (Section 3) uses MATH-500, the 500-question test set from Hendrycks et al. (2021) as split by Lightman et al. (2023). The REASONMAXXER evaluation (Section 6) spans six mathematical reasoning benchmarks: MATH-500, GSM8K (Cobbe et al., 2021), AMC 2023, AIME 2024, Minerva Math (Lewkowycz et al., 2022), and OlympiadBench (He et al., 2024). For AMC 2023 and AIME 2024, which contain few problems, the paper reports avg@8 (average pass@1 over eight independent generations) to reduce variance; all other benchmarks use standard pass@1 from a single generation.

- **Base model(s).** The mechanistic analysis studies four base/RL-tuned pairs drawn from three model families: Qwen2.5 (1.5B, 7B, Math-7B, 32B), Qwen3 (0.6B, 4B), and Mistral-7B-v0.1, with DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B also used in the REASONMAXXER evaluation. The authors argue these models are "representative of the capabilities of many contemporary LLMs" and span a 14× range of scales (1.5B to 32B). The RL baselines cover three algorithms: GRPO (SimpleRL-Zoo, Qwen3-4B General-Reasoner, DeepScaleR, Open-RS3), PPO (Open-Reasoner-Zero, STILL-3), and RLOO (PRIME-Zero / Eurus-2-7B-PRIME).

- **Metrics.** The primary metric is **pass@1 accuracy** — the fraction of test problems for which the model's single generated answer (greedy decoding, temperature 0 for the mechanistic analysis) matches the ground truth. Answer extraction uses \boxed{} notation and the grading function from Hendrycks et al. (2021). For the oracle and entropy-gated intervention experiments (Section 3), the metric is pass@1 under deterministic decoding with token-level substitutions applied during generation. Training cost is reported in estimated USD using RunPod on-demand pricing as of the submission date (Appendix D).

- **Baselines.** The paper compares against publicly available RL-trained checkpoints from multiple sources. SimpleRL-Zoo (Zeng et al., 2025) provides GRPO-trained checkpoints across Qwen2.5-1.5B, 7B, Math-7B, 32B, and Mistral-7B. Open-Reasoner-Zero (Hu et al., 2025) provides PPO-trained checkpoints for Qwen2.5-1.5B, 7B, and 32B. PRIME-Zero / Eurus-2-7B-PRIME (Cui et al., 2025) provides an RLOO-trained checkpoint for Qwen2.5-Math-7B. General-Reasoner (Ma et al., 2025) provides a GRPO+verifier checkpoint for Qwen3-4B. For DeepSeek-R1-Distill-1.5B, baselines include DeepScaleR (Luo et al., 2025), STILL-3 (Min et al., 2024), and Open-RS3 (Dang and Ngo, 2025). The Qwen3-0.6B baseline is the official GRPO-trained model from Yang et al. (2025a). The base model (pre-RL) accuracy is reported for all comparisons. The mechanistic analysis (Section 3) uses a random substitution control: at intervention positions, the base token is replaced with a randomly chosen alternative from the base model's top-20, testing whether any perturbation helps or only specific corrections.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The mechanistic analysis uses deterministic decoding (temperature 0), so generation cost per problem is fixed. For the oracle and entropy-gated interventions, the "intervention rate" (fraction of token positions touched) is reported as a measure of the correction's sparsity. For REASONMAXXER, training compute is measured in GPU-hours and USD cost, with rollouts generated once offline (20 rollouts per problem × 150 problems = 3,000 rollouts, reduced to 1,000 training sequences after the mixed-success filter). Baseline RL costs are either taken from published reports or estimated from official training scripts and documented hardware configurations (detailed in Appendix D). All costs are converted using RunPod on-demand pricing. No inference-time compute comparison is performed between REASONMAXXER and RL models — the comparison is solely on training cost for equivalent accuracy.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The mechanistic analysis (Section 3) does not use cross-validation; results are reported as pass@1 on the full MATH-500 set. For REASONMAXXER, the entropy threshold τ is swept over {1.2, 1.4, 1.6, 1.8} on a fixed 50-problem held-out validation split, and the optimal τ is then evaluated on the test benchmarks. Model selection uses the final checkpoint after one epoch of training on the 50 training problems; no early stopping based on validation loss is performed. All REASONMAXXER runs use a fixed random seed (42). The mechanistic analysis across model pairs uses the publicly available checkpoints without any retraining or fine-tuning on the authors' part. For AMC 2023 and AIME 2024, the avg@8 metric reduces variance from the small problem count, but no confidence intervals or standard errors are reported for any metric.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Token-Level Divergence Between Base and RL Models (Section 3.1)

Table 2 reports the core mechanistic finding: RL's edits are extremely sparse and conservative across all four base/RL pairs tested. For Qwen2.5-1.5B → GRPO, only 2.09% of token positions are reranked (the teacher's argmax differs from the base model's argmax but remains within the base model's top-5) and 0.02% are shifted (teacher's argmax falls outside the base model's top-5). For Qwen2.5-7B → GRPO, the rerank rate drops to 1.03% (shifted: 0.01%). For Qwen2.5-7B → PPO, the rerank rate is 3.96% (shifted: 0.12%). For Qwen3-4B → GRPO, it is 2.29% (shifted: 0.02%). Across all pairs, the "shifted" category — which would indicate the RL model introducing genuinely novel token preferences — is effectively zero (0.01–0.12%).

The mean rank of the teacher's preferred token among the base model's top alternatives is 2.14–2.39 (Table 2, "Mean rank" column), meaning the RL model almost always promotes the base model's second or third most likely token rather than introducing an unexpected candidate. This is visually summarized in Figure 1a: "The RL model's chosen token is on average rank 2 among the base model's top alternatives."

The entropy ratio (Table 2, "Entropy ratio" column) shows that reranked positions have 7.58× to 12.63× higher base-model token entropy than unchanged positions. This is the key localization finding: RL's edits are not randomly distributed but concentrated precisely at positions where the base model's probability mass is spread across multiple plausible continuations. Figure 1b visualizes this: "Only 1–4% of token positions are reranked by RL, yet those positions have higher base-model entropy than unchanged positions."

#### Causal Sufficiency of Sparse Token Corrections (Section 3.2, Figure 2)

The oracle intervention experiment tests whether the specific token disagreements identified in Table 2 causally explain RL's accuracy improvement. On Qwen2.5-1.5B → GRPO, the base model achieves a MATH-500 pass@1 of approximately 0.30 (read from Figure 2, leftmost bar in the first group). The Oracle intervention (red bar, first group) achieves pass@1 matching the RL model's dashed line at roughly 0.49, using an intervention rate of 2.11% of tokens (annotated below the bar). The random substitution baseline (cream hatched bar, "Random") performs at or below the base model level. On Qwen2.5-7B → GRPO, the oracle matches the RL model exactly with a 1.04% intervention rate. On Qwen2.5-7B → PPO, the oracle matches the RL model with a 4.09% intervention rate. On Qwen3-4B → GRPO, the oracle matches with a 2.30% intervention rate. In all four cases, the oracle intervention reproduces the RL model's pass@1 precisely, while random substitution fails (Figure 2 caption: "the red oracle bar matches the dashed RL model line precisely, using only 1–4% of tokens").

The key methodological detail: the oracle intervention is applied during deterministic generation from the base model — at every position where the RL teacher's argmax disagrees with the base model's argmax (the reranked positions from Table 2), the base token is replaced with the teacher's preferred token, and generation continues autoregressively from the corrected prefix. This is a stronger test than simply measuring token overlap because it accounts for the autoregressive nature of generation: a single token correction early in the reasoning trace can redirect the entire subsequent trajectory, and the intervention captures this cascading effect naturally.

#### Entropy-Gated Intervention: Teacher-Free Localization (Section 3.3, Figure 2)

The entropy-gated condition (cardinal red bars, "Entropy-gated" in Figure 2) tests whether the base model's entropy alone can identify the positions where intervention is useful, without any information from the RL teacher about *where* it intervenes. The intervention uses the teacher's preferred token (so the *correction* is still oracle) but selects positions solely by thresholding base-model entropy at τ = 1.2. For Qwen2.5-7B → GRPO, the entropy-gated intervention matches the RL model exactly — meaning entropy alone identifies exactly the right positions for this pair. For Qwen2.5-7B → PPO, the entropy-gated intervention closely approaches the RL model. For the other pairs (Qwen2.5-1.5B → GRPO, Qwen3-4B → GRPO), the entropy-gated intervention substantially improves over the base model but falls short of the full oracle, indicating that entropy is a good but imperfect proxy for RL's intervention sites. The annotated intervention rates in Figure 2 are not broken out separately for the entropy-gated condition in the figure text, but the narrative reports they touch "1.2–8.3% of tokens" (Section 3.3).

#### Low-Dimensionality of RL's Correction (Section 4, Figure 3)

Figure 3 reports the KL-LoRA distillation results across all four base/RL pairs on both MATH-500 and GSM8K. For Qwen2.5-1.5B → GRPO, the base model achieves pass@1 of approximately 0.23 on MATH-500 (cream bar, first group) and the KL-LoRA adapter (cardinal red bar) achieves approximately 0.49, matching the RL model. The adapter uses 0.49% of model parameters (8.7M out of 1.54B, from Table 4). For Qwen2.5-7B → GRPO, the base model is at roughly 0.58 on MATH-500, KL-LoRA matches the RL model at roughly 0.66, using 0.27% of parameters. For Qwen2.5-7B → PPO, the base is at roughly 0.58, KL-LoRA matches the RL model at roughly 0.73, using 0.27% of parameters. For Qwen3-4B → GRPO, the base is at roughly 0.51 on MATH-500, KL-LoRA matches the RL model at roughly 0.75, using 0.43% of parameters. The same pattern holds on GSM8K in all four cases: the KL-LoRA adapter matches the RL teacher's accuracy. The training uses only 100 randomly chosen problems.

Table 4 in Appendix A provides a more aggressive compression ablation on Qwen2.5-1.5B → GRPO. A rank-16 QKVO adapter (4.4M parameters, 0.25% of model) achieves pass@1 of 0.492 on MATH-500, matching the full rank-32 adapter (0.495) and the RL teacher (0.492). A rank-8 QKVO adapter (2.2M, 0.12%) achieves 0.487, within 0.5 points of the teacher. A rank-8 output-projection-only adapter (688K parameters, 0.04% of model) achieves 0.482, lagging by only 1 point. This progressive compression demonstrates that RL's correction is not just low-dimensional but concentrated in the output projection layer.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Sensitivity to entropy threshold τ (Figure 4):** On Qwen2.5-1.5B, REASONMAXXER's pass@1 is evaluated as τ varies from 1.0 to 2.2. At τ = 1.0 (8.9% of tokens gated as decision points), MATH-500 pass@1 is roughly 0.48 and GSM8K is roughly 0.68 — both below the RL model. At τ = 1.4 (5.2% of tokens), MATH-500 reaches its peak of 0.502 (matching the RL model) and GSM8K reaches 0.72 (also matching RL). At τ = 1.8 (2.6% of tokens), MATH-500 achieves a second peak near 0.49 and GSM8K is approximately 0.70 — slightly below the τ = 1.4 peak but still strong, and notably, the 2.6% token fraction closely matches RL's observed intervention rate of 2.11% from Table 2. At τ = 2.2 (2.0% of tokens), MATH-500 drops to roughly 0.46 and GSM8K to roughly 0.66. The broad plateau across τ = 1.0 to 1.8 on GSM8K and 1.2 to 1.8 on MATH-500 demonstrates that exact τ tuning is not critical.

**Necessity of the contrastive (negative) term:** The paper reports an ablation comparing full REASONMAXXER against a "positive-only" variant (Ai > 0 rollouts only, equivalent to supervised fine-tuning on correct trajectories at decision points). On Qwen2.5-1.5B, positive-only training raises MATH-500 pass@1 from 0.298 (base) to 0.398 — a meaningful gain of 10 points. However, this is far below both the RL model (0.496) and full REASONMAXXER (0.502). The contrastive term that penalizes incorrect decisions thus contributes approximately half the total gain over the base model. The paper does not report this ablation in a separate table; the numbers appear in the narrative (Section 6.3).

**KL-LoRA compression (Table 4, Appendix A):** Beyond the main rank-32 QKVO adapter, progressively smaller configurations are tested on Qwen2.5-1.5B → GRPO. Rank-16 QKVO (0.25% of parameters) achieves 0.492 on MATH-500, matching the full adapter. Rank-8 QKVO (0.12%) achieves 0.487, within 0.5 points. Rank-8 output-projection-only (0.04%) achieves 0.482, lagging only 1 point behind the RL teacher. This demonstrates that the correction is not just low-dimensional but concentrated in the output layer.

**Cross-model-family and cross-RL-algorithm consistency (Table 2):** The sparse-correction pattern is replicated across GRPO (Qwen2.5-1.5B, Qwen2.5-7B, Qwen3-4B) and PPO (Qwen2.5-7B), with rerank rates of 1.03–3.96%, shifted rates of 0.01–0.12%, and entropy ratios of 7.58–12.63×. The pattern holds across model scales from 1.5B to 4B (the 7B and 32B pairs are only analyzed via KL-LoRA, not full token-level divergence). The inclusion of PPO alongside GRPO is important because it rules out GRPO-specific artifacts (e.g., group normalization) as the source of the sparsity pattern.

**Edge-of-competence filter necessity:** The paper notes (Section 5.1) that the mixed-success filter retains 50 problems from an initial pool of 150. The ablation over τ (Figure 4) implicitly validates this: the fact that REASONMAXXER works with only 50 training problems demonstrates that the mixed-success criterion successfully selects informative problems, but the paper does not include an explicit ablation comparing mixed-success filtering against random problem selection. This is a notable gap.

**Single-epoch training and overfitting:** All REASONMAXXER models are trained for exactly one epoch with linear learning rate decay to zero. There is no ablation comparing one vs. multiple epochs. The paper argues (Section 5.3) that multiple epochs would risk overfitting with only 1,000 training sequences, but provides no empirical evidence for this claim (e.g., a training curve showing validation loss increasing in epoch 2).

**KL anchor necessity:** The paper does not provide an ablation where the KL anchor term (Equation 7) is removed. Without this ablation, it is difficult to determine how much of REASONMAXXER's success depends on preserving base-model behavior at non-decision positions versus the contrastive loss at decision points alone. This is a significant missing control.

**Oracle vs. predicted difficulty bins:** Unlike the reference paper, the paper under analysis does not have an "oracle vs. predicted" difficulty estimation component. REASONMAXXER's entropy gating is fully teacher-free, so there is no oracle difficulty to compare against.

### Critical Assessment

The paper's central claims, as established in the Executive Summary and Section 4, are: (1) RL's beneficial effect on math reasoning is a sparse, entropy-localized, conservative token reranking, (2) this sparse correction is causally sufficient for RL's accuracy gain, (3) the correction is low-dimensional and representable in a tiny adapter, (4) REASONMAXXER matches or exceeds full RL performance at three orders of magnitude lower cost, and (5) the RL optimization loop is unnecessary for reasoning improvement when base models already contain correct solution paths.

**Claim 1 (sparse, entropy-localized, conservative reranking): Strongly supported for the tested pairs, but the scope is limited.** Table 2 provides clean quantitative evidence across four base/RL pairs (three GRPO, one PPO) that reranked positions are rare (1–4%), are exclusively within the base model's top-5 (shifted rate ~0%), and have 8–13× higher entropy than unchanged positions. However, the analysis is restricted to models up to 4B parameters for the full token-level divergence — the 7B and 32B pairs are analyzed only via the KL-LoRA distillation proxy, not direct token-by-token comparison. The 32B token-level divergence is particularly important because it is the scale where one might expect RL to produce more complex changes. The paper also does not analyze DeepSeek-R1 or the Mistral-7B pairs at the token level, so the claimed universality is supported for Qwen2.5 and Qwen3 families with GRPO/PPO but not demonstrated across all models in Table 3. The entropy ratio analysis (Table 2) would benefit from reporting the absolute entropy values at reranked vs. unchanged positions, not just the ratio, since entropy ratios can be large if the denominator (unchanged entropy) is very small.

**Claim 2 (causal sufficiency): Compelling but with an important caveat.** The oracle intervention experiment (Figure 2) is the strongest evidence in the paper. Reproducing RL performance exactly by touching only 1–4% of tokens, with random substitution as a negative control, establishes that the specific token choices at those positions *cause* the accuracy gain, not merely correlate with it. However, the experiment uses deterministic decoding (temperature 0), which means the intervention operates on a single trajectory per problem. The paper does not test whether the oracle intervention would recover RL performance under stochastic sampling (e.g., pass@k), which would be a stronger test of whether the corrected token distribution at decision points fully captures RL's effect or whether there are additional variance-reduction effects that only appear under sampling. Additionally, the oracle assumes access to the RL teacher's token preferences, making it a diagnostic tool rather than a method — REASONMAXXER's success validates that the signal can be learned without the oracle, but the causal claim from the oracle experiment is about the *nature* of RL's effect, not about learnability.

**Claim 3 (low-dimensionality): Supported but the connection to learnability is underexplored.** Figure 3 and Table 4 convincingly show that a rank-32 LoRA adapter distilled from the RL teacher via KL divergence on 100 problems reproduces RL accuracy. The progressive compression down to rank-8 output-projection-only (0.04% of parameters, within 1 point of the teacher) is particularly striking and suggests RL's correction is almost entirely in the output projection. However, the KL-LoRA experiment uses *teacher supervision* — the adapter is trained to mimic the RL teacher's distribution, not to learn the correction from outcome feedback. This establishes *representability* but not *learnability* from scratch. The paper acknowledges this (Section 4, "From Representability to Learnability") and cites Wang et al. (2025b) as evidence that LoRA-constrained RL can match full-parameter RL, which partially bridges the gap. But the paper's own contribution would be strengthened by an experiment that trains the LoRA adapter with REASONMAXXER's contrastive loss at *varying adapter ranks* (e.g., rank 8, 16, 32, 64) to show that the signal is not only representable but also learnable at low rank. This ablation is absent.

**Claim 4 (REASONMAXXER matches RL at lower cost): Supported with important caveats about the comparisons.** Table 3 is the paper's most important empirical contribution, and the breadth of evidence is impressive: eight base models, six benchmarks, 18 baseline comparisons. The headline finding — that REASONMAXXER matches or exceeds RL performance while costing $4–25 vs. $100–$100,000 — is well-supported by the data. However, several caveats affect interpretation:

**Caveat 1: Selection of baselines is favorable.** On Qwen2.5-1.5B, REASONMAXXER is compared against SimpleRL-Zoo and Open-Reasoner-Zero, but not against stronger RL baselines that might exist for this model. The DeepSeek-R1-Distill-1.5B comparison is the strongest because it includes three distinct baselines (DeepScaleR, STILL-3, Open-RS3), and REASONMAXXER substantially outperforms all of them on average (0.401 vs. 0.340/0.356/0.314). However, for other model families, the baseline coverage is thinner — some models have only one RL baseline (Mistral-7B: only SimpleRL-Zoo, which performs worse than the base model; Qwen3-0.6B: only the official GRPO checkpoint).

**Caveat 2: Training cost estimates are approximate for many baselines.** The paper is transparent that italicized costs in Table 3 are estimates derived from training scripts rather than direct measurements. For Open-Reasoner-Zero, costs are estimated with an "overhead factor" for PPO's critic and GAE computation, and the paper acknowledges these are "midpoints of plausible ranges" (Appendix D). While the three-orders-of-magnitude gap is large enough that estimation error is unlikely to change the qualitative conclusion, the precise cost ratios should be treated as approximate.

**Caveat 3: The Qwen3-4B General-Reasoner comparison is mixed.** General-Reasoner achieves substantially higher MATH-500 (0.746 vs. 0.660) but lower average (0.406 vs. 0.476), largely due to an anomalous 0.050 on AMC 2023 (vs. REASONMAXXER's 0.472). The paper does not investigate why General-Reasoner performs so poorly on AMC 2023; this single outlier inflates REASONMAXXER's average advantage and warrants scrutiny.

**Caveat 4: REASONMAXXER does not always outperform RL on every benchmark.** On GSM8K with Qwen2.5-1.5B, SimpleRL-Zoo leads (0.733 vs. 0.710). On MATH-500 with Qwen2.5-7B, Open-Reasoner-Zero leads (0.732 vs. 0.706). On MATH-500 with Qwen3-4B, General-Reasoner leads (0.746 vs. 0.660). The paper's claim is that REASONMAXXER "matches or exceeds" RL, which is true in aggregate (average across benchmarks) but not uniformly. This is a nuanced point: REASONMAXXER's strength appears to be robustness across diverse benchmarks rather than dominance on any single one, which is consistent with the paper's claim that it captures RL's general policy-selection signal rather than overfitting to a specific benchmark distribution.

**Caveat 5: No inference-time cost comparison.** The paper compares only training costs. REASONMAXXER uses a LoRA adapter at inference time, which adds a small computational overhead per forward pass (the `BA` matrix multiplication). RL-trained models are full-weight models with no adapter overhead. For a fair total-cost-of-ownership comparison, one would need to account for the per-query inference cost difference over the model's deployment lifetime, as the reference paper does with its FLOPs-matched analysis (Section 7). The paper does not perform this analysis, so the claimed cost reduction applies only to training, not to total cost.

**Claim 5 (RL is unnecessary): Supported as an existence proof, but boundary conditions are not mapped.** The paper demonstrates that REASONMAXXER works for math reasoning across the tested benchmarks, providing an existence proof that RL-free post-training can match RL. However, the paper does NOT test whether the same sparse-correction pattern holds for non-math reasoning (code generation, logical deduction, scientific reasoning) or for tasks where the base model's pass@1 is near zero (which would fail the edge-of-competence filter). The paper's title and framing ("Rethinking RL for LLM Reasoning") make a general claim about reasoning, but the evidence is restricted to mathematical reasoning. Given that the edge-of-competence requirement is central to the method, one would expect REASONMAXXER to fail on tasks where the base model has near-zero success rate — the same limitation the paper identifies for RL itself (Section 3.2). The paper does not test this boundary condition, so the universal claim that "RL is unnecessary" should be qualified to "RL is unnecessary for math reasoning improvement when the base model is already at the edge of competence."

**Missing experiments that would strengthen the paper:**

- **Inference-time cost analysis:** A FLOPs-matched comparison between REASONMAXXER-LoRA inference and full-model RL inference, analogous to the reference paper's Section 7.
- **Ablation over the number of training problems:** Does REASONMAXXER's performance degrade gracefully or collapse if trained on 20 problems instead of 50? Is 50 the minimum, or would 200 problems yield further gains? This would characterize the data efficiency more precisely.
- **Ablation over the KL anchor weight λ:** The paper sets λ = 0.2 without justification. Testing λ ∈ {0, 0.1, 0.2, 0.5, 1.0} would reveal how much the anchor term matters and whether the contrastive loss alone can succeed.
- **Rank ablation for REASONMAXXER (not just KL-LoRA):** Training REASONMAXXER at ranks 8, 16, 32, 64 would show whether the low-dimensionality established for distillation also holds for learning from scratch.
- **Token-level analysis at 7B and 32B scales:** The full token-level divergence analysis (Table 2-style) is only shown up to 4B (Qwen3-4B). Extending this to 7B and 32B would validate the universality claim at larger scales.
- **Non-math reasoning benchmark:** Even a single code or logic benchmark would test whether the sparse-correction pattern and REASONMAXXER generalize beyond math.
- **Comparison against best-of-N or majority voting at inference:** The paper compares against RL training methods but does not compare REASONMAXXER at inference time against simple test-time strategies like majority voting or best-of-N on the base model. Since REASONMAXXER trains on only 1,000 sequences, a control showing that simply sampling more from the base model at test time doesn't match REASONMAXXER's accuracy would rule out the possibility that the gain comes from the base model's existing distribution rather than from the adapter.

**Summary of the experimental contribution's strength and limitations:** The mechanistic analysis (Sections 3–4) is the paper's strongest empirical contribution, providing causal, quantitative evidence for the sparse policy-selection characterization. The REASONMAXXER results (Section 6) convincingly demonstrate that this characterization can be exploited for RL-free training, and the breadth of model families, RL algorithms, and benchmarks reduces the risk that the findings are idiosyncratic. The primary limitations are: (1) restriction to math reasoning, leaving open the question of whether the pattern generalizes; (2) incomplete ablation coverage, particularly around the KL anchor, training data quantity, and adapter rank; (3) approximate cost estimates for many baselines; (4) no inference-time cost analysis; and (5) token-level analysis limited to smaller model scales (≤4B) for the full divergence characterization. These limitations do not undermine the core claims, but they bound their scope and suggest important follow-up work.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### Math-Only Scope: The Characterization and Method Are Validated Exclusively on Mathematical Reasoning

The paper's entire mechanistic analysis (Section 3) and all REASONMAXXER evaluations (Section 6, Table 3) are conducted on mathematical reasoning benchmarks: MATH-500, GSM8K, AMC 2023, AIME 2024, Minerva Math, and OlympiadBench. The paper's title — "Rethinking RL for LLM Reasoning" — and its framing throughout ("reasoning improvement," "RL for reasoning") make a general claim about reasoning, but the evidence is restricted to a single reasoning modality. The paper acknowledges indirectly that the edge-of-competence filter (Section 5.1) requires problems with "verifiable answers" — i.e., ground-truth answer strings that can be checked via exact match — but does not discuss whether the sparse-correction pattern would hold for reasoning tasks without clean outcome signals.

The consequence is that the paper's central reframing — "reasoning improvement is sparse policy selection, not capability acquisition" — may not generalize to other reasoning domains. Code generation, for instance, involves different token-level dynamics (syntax constraints, longer-range structural dependencies) where a token-level entropy gate might miss critical decision points that manifest as structure rather than local uncertainty. Logical reasoning over natural language premises may require integrating information across long contexts in ways that are not captured by per-token entropy at isolated positions. Scientific reasoning may require factual recall that the model either possesses or does not, with no "edge of competence" where both correct and incorrect rollouts exist on the same problem. In each of these domains, RL might genuinely teach new strategies — cross-modal integration, multi-step planning, verification of factual claims — rather than merely selecting among existing reasoning paths. The paper provides zero evidence either for or against this possibility.

What evidence exists in the paper: none. The paper does not test REASONMAXXER or the token-level divergence analysis on any non-math benchmark. The restriction is implicit in the benchmark list (Table 3) and the problem selection mechanism ("math problems with verifiable answers," Section 5.1), but the general language throughout the paper ("reasoning improvement," "LLM reasoning," "post-training for reasoning") does not match this scope. The paper does not include code generation datasets (HumanEval, MBPP, LiveCodeBench), logical reasoning benchmarks (FOLIO, ProofWriter, ARC), or scientific QA tasks (GPQA, ScienceQA) — any of which would partially address the generalization question.

Mitigation status: not addressed and not acknowledged as a limitation. The paper's Discussion/Conclusion section (Section 8) does not mention the math-only scope. The Related Work section (Section 7) discusses "efficient reasoning" broadly without noting that all experiments are on math. This is a significant omission given the paper's ambition to reframe how the field thinks about RL for reasoning. A reasonable future step would be to replicate the mechanistic analysis (token-level divergence, entropy localization, oracle intervention) on at least one code reasoning benchmark and one logical reasoning benchmark, and to test REASONMAXXER in those domains. The edge-of-competence filter would need adaptation for domains without exact-match answer verification — possibly using test-case execution for code or automated theorem-prover feedback for logic.

### Difficulty Estimation via the Mixed-Success Filter Requires Ground-Truth Answers and Non-Trivial Base Model Performance

REASONMAXXER's problem selection mechanism (Section 5.1) requires: (a) verifiable ground-truth answers to check rollout correctness, and (b) a base model with mixed success on the training problems — strictly between 0% and 100% pass rate. Problems where the base model always succeeds or always fails are discarded. This filter is essential: it ensures every retained problem supplies both correct and incorrect rollouts, which the contrastive loss (Equation 6, Section 5.3) requires to produce both positive and negative gradient signals.

The consequence is that REASONMAXXER is inapplicable in two practically important regimes. First, on problems or domains where the base model has near-zero success — which includes genuinely novel reasoning tasks, advanced competition problems, or out-of-distribution queries — the mixed-success filter would discard all available training problems, leaving REASONMAXXER with no training data. The paper's own difficulty bin analysis (Section 3.2, Figure 2) shows that RL's effect is concentrated at the edge of competence; the hardest problems (bin 5) show near-zero improvement from any method. This means REASONMAXXER inherits RL's fundamental limitation — it cannot create capability where none exists — but is additionally *procedurally blocked* from even attempting to train on such problems because the filter removes them. Second, REASONMAXXER cannot be used for self-improvement on problems where ground-truth answers are unavailable. The entire pipeline — correctness checking, advantage computation, contrastive loss — depends on knowing which rollouts are correct. For open-ended generation, creative writing, dialogue, or tasks where "correctness" is subjective or multi-dimensional, there is no binary reward signal to compute advantages from, and the method does not offer an alternative.

What evidence exists in the paper: the problem selection mechanism is described in Section 5.1, which specifies that 150 candidate problems are filtered to 50 based on mixed success, yielding 1,000 training sequences. The paper does not include an ablation where the filter is removed (e.g., training on all 150 problems regardless of pass rate) to measure how much performance degrades. The edge-of-competence requirement is well-motivated theoretically by the prior work the paper cites (Davis and Recht, 2025; Zhang et al., 2025), and the causal analysis in Section 3.2 corroborates it, but the paper does not empirically characterize *how close* the base model's pass rate must be to 0.5 for the signal to be useful. Would a problem with pass rate 0.05 (one correct rollout out of 20) provide sufficient positive signal, or is the contrastive loss too noisy at the extremes of the pass-rate distribution? The paper does not test this.

Mitigation status: the paper does not claim to solve the "hard problem" regime or the "no ground-truth" regime. The limitations are inherent to the problem formulation (outcome-based feedback on verifiable problems) and are shared with RLVR itself. However, the paper does not explicitly characterize these as limitations of REASONMAXXER — the Discussion (Section 8) focuses on the method's strengths without mapping its boundary conditions. A partial mitigation for the ground-truth requirement would be to train a learned verifier (outcome reward model) from a small set of labeled examples and use it to score rollouts, but the paper does not explore this direction and notes (Section 2.1) that its baselines do include methods that use learned verifiers (General-Reasoner).

### The KL Anchor Weight and Its Contribution to Performance Are Untested

REASONMAXXER's total loss (Section 5.3) is the sum of the advantage-weighted contrastive loss at decision points (Equation 6) and a KL divergence anchor at all non-decision positions (Equation 7), weighted by λ = 0.2. The KL anchor forces the adapter to preserve the base model's output distribution everywhere except at the explicitly selected decision points. The paper justifies this choice on three grounds: (1) RL does not change the model's behavior at ~97–99% of token positions (Table 2), so the anchor enforces the observed sparsity; (2) with only 1,000 training sequences, the anchor prevents overfitting to spurious correlations; (3) it ensures the small LoRA adapter focuses its capacity on the sparse set of decision points.

The consequence of not ablating λ is that the reader cannot determine whether the KL anchor is a necessary regularization or merely a helpful stabilizer — and whether the contrastive loss alone, without any explicit base-model anchoring, could succeed. This matters for several reasons. First, if the KL anchor is doing significant work, then REASONMAXXER's success is partly attributable to a form of knowledge distillation (anchoring to the base model's distribution) rather than purely to the contrastive signal at decision points. Second, the choice of λ = 0.2 interacts with the fraction of tokens gated as decision points (~5% at τ = 1.4; Figure 4): with ~95% of tokens contributing to the anchor loss and only ~5% to the decision loss, the total anchor gradient could dominate even with the 0.2 weight, making λ a critical hyperparameter whose tuning may be dataset- or model-dependent. Third, in domains where the base model's non-decision behavior is already suboptimal (e.g., it produces correct answers for the wrong reasons, or its chain-of-thought contains subtle errors that don't affect the final answer), the KL anchor might actively prevent useful corrections at non-decision positions, locking in errors that a full RL training run would fix.

What evidence exists in the paper: the paper states λ = 0.2 in Appendix B (Table 6) and provides a brief justification for the anchor in Section 5.3, but does not report an ablation over λ ∈ {0, 0.1, 0.2, 0.5, 1.0} or compare REASONMAXXER with vs. without the anchor term. Figure 4 shows that REASONMAXXER is robust to τ variation, which indirectly suggests that the anchor is not catastrophically sensitive to the exact set of decision points, but this is not the same as testing the anchor itself. The positive-only ablation (Section 6.3) removes the negative term of the contrastive loss but retains the KL anchor, so it does not isolate the anchor's contribution. The paper does not report whether training without the KL anchor causes the adapter to drift on non-decision positions, whether validation accuracy degrades, or whether the adapter overfits the 1,000 training sequences.

Mitigation status: not addressed. The paper treats λ = 0.2 as a fixed hyperparameter chosen during development ("The λ balancing the two objectives," Section 5.3), without systematic sensitivity analysis. A λ sweep is a standard ablation for any multi-term loss function, and its absence is a notable gap given the conceptual importance of the anchor — the paper's central claim that RL's effect is exclusively at decision points depends on the premise that non-decision positions should not change, and the anchor enforces this premise. Without an ablation, it is unclear whether REASONMAXXER would learn to preserve base-model behavior on its own (through the limited adapter capacity and sparse contrastive signal) or whether the explicit KL penalty is essential. Future work should test whether the anchor can be removed entirely — if so, it would simplify the method and strengthen the claim that the corrective signal is truly sparse; if not, it would indicate that the base-model anchoring is a necessary component of the approach, requiring more careful tuning across domains and model families.

### The Cost Comparison Omits Inference-Time Overhead and Difficulty-Estimation Cost

The paper's headline efficiency claim — "a reduction in training cost of roughly three orders of magnitude" (Abstract, Section 6.2) — compares REASONMAXXER's training cost ($4–25) against the RL baselines' training cost ($100–$100,000). This is a fair training-time comparison, and the gap is large enough that even substantial estimation error in the baseline costs would not change the qualitative conclusion. However, the paper does not provide a total-cost-of-ownership comparison that accounts for two additional costs: (1) the inference-time overhead of the LoRA adapter, and (2) the cost of the rollout generation and entropy computation that REASONMAXXER requires before training begins.

The inference-time overhead is real but likely small. REASONMAXXER uses a rank-32 LoRA adapter on all QKVO attention projections. At inference time, this means each attention projection computes Wx + BAx rather than just Wx, where B ∈ ℝ^{d_model × 32} and A ∈ ℝ^{32 × d_model}. For a model with hidden dimension 2,048 (Qwen2.5-1.5B), this adds roughly 2 × 2,048 × 32 ≈ 131K multiply-adds per attention projection, compared to the ~4.2M multiply-adds of the base projection — approximately 3% overhead per adapted layer. Across all QKVO layers in a transformer, this translates to a few percent total inference overhead. For deployment scenarios where the model serves millions of queries, this per-query cost accumulates and partially offsets the training cost savings. The paper provides no measurement of inference latency or throughput for REASONMAXXER vs. the base model or RL-trained models.

The pre-training rollout cost is more significant and is entirely unaccounted for. Before training begins, REASONMAXXER generates 20 rollouts per problem for 150 candidate problems (3,000 rollouts total). Each rollout is a full chain-of-thought solution — typically hundreds to thousands of tokens — generated at temperature 0.6 with top-p 0.95. For a 1.5B model, this is computationally cheap (single-digit GPU-minutes), but for the 32B model, generating 3,000 full-length solutions is a non-trivial cost that the paper does not add to the $25 training cost figure. The entropy computation requires an additional forward pass per rollout to obtain per-token distributions, but this can reuse the cached keys and values from generation, so it is essentially free. The paper acknowledges (Section 5.2) that "entropy is computed from the base model alone, this stage requires no external supervision beyond the rollouts already generated," but does not include the rollout generation cost in the reported training budget. For the 32B model specifically, the $25 figure (Table 3) is a direct measurement from the authors' hardware, so it presumably *does* include rollout generation — the paper states "costs are directly measured from our runs" — but the breakdown between rollout cost and training cost is not provided, making it impossible to determine how the total scales with model size.

What evidence exists in the paper: the cost estimation methodology is described in Appendix D, which states that REASONMAXXER costs "include rollout generation, entropy scoring, a sweep over τ, training, and final checkpoint selection." The $4–25 range therefore appears to be all-inclusive — the pre-training costs are included. However, the inference-time overhead is not discussed anywhere in the paper. The paper compares REASONMAXXER's training efficiency against RL training efficiency, but a deployment scenario would need to compare total cost of ownership: (REASONMAXXER training + per-query inference with adapter) vs. (RL training + per-query inference without adapter). If the model serves N queries over its lifetime, the inference cost difference accumulates at ~3% per query, meaning there exists some N beyond which the higher per-query cost of the adapter outweighs the training savings. The paper does not characterize this crossover point.

Mitigation status: partially addressed for training cost (the appendix confirms the reported figures are all-inclusive) but unaddressed for inference cost. The paper could strengthen its cost claim by reporting: (a) inference latency/throughput for REASONMAXXER vs. the base model on a standard benchmark (e.g., tokens/second on a fixed GPU), (b) the crossover query count where the adapter's inference overhead equals the training cost savings, and (c) whether the LoRA adapter can be merged into the base weights post-training (which LoRA supports via W' = W + BA), eliminating the inference overhead entirely. If merging recovers identical accuracy, the inference-time disadvantage disappears. The paper does not test merged inference.

### Token-Level Analysis Is Performed Only at Smaller Scales (≤4B), Leaving the Universality Claim Unverified at Scale

The paper's central mechanistic findings — the sparse, entropy-localized, conservative token reranking (Section 3.1, Table 2) and the causal sufficiency of these edits (Section 3.2, Figure 2) — are established through direct token-by-token comparison of base model outputs against RL teacher outputs across four base/RL pairs. These pairs span Qwen2.5-1.5B (GRPO), Qwen2.5-7B (GRPO and PPO), and Qwen3-4B (GRPO) — model sizes from 1.5B to 7B parameters, with the entropy ratio and mean rank analysis reported up to 4B. The 7B and 32B Qwen2.5 models appear in the REASONMAXXER benchmark results (Table 3) and the KL-LoRA distillation experiments (Figure 3), but their token-level divergence — the equivalent of Table 2 — is not reported.

The consequence is that the paper's most detailed mechanistic claims are validated at smaller scales, where one might expect RL's effect to be simpler, and the extrapolation to larger models relies on proxy evidence (KL-LoRA distillation matches RL performance, REASONMAXXER matches RL performance) rather than direct measurement. At 32B parameters, it is plausible that RL produces a richer set of changes — perhaps affecting more token positions, introducing genuinely novel token preferences (shifted tokens, not just reranked), or modifying the model's behavior in ways that are not fully captured by a rank-32 adapter. The KL-LoRA result (Figure 3) shows that a rank-32 adapter *can* reproduce the 32B RL model's accuracy, but this is a distillation result that measures representability, not a direct measurement of how many token positions RL actually changed. A model could have diffuse, small-magnitude changes across many positions that, when distilled into a low-rank adapter, collapse into a sparse-appearing correction — the sparsity would then be an artifact of the distillation bottleneck, not a property of RL itself.

What evidence exists in the paper: Table 2 reports token-level statistics for models up to 7B (the PPO pair is Qwen2.5-7B), but the full token-by-token analysis at 32B is absent. The KL-LoRA distillation (Figure 3, Table 4) covers up to Qwen3-4B (the largest pair in that figure) and shows the adapter matches RL accuracy, which is consistent with sparsity but does not measure it directly. The REASONMAXXER results at 32B (Table 3) show that the entropy-gated contrastive method works at scale, which is the most practically relevant evidence, but it does not replace a direct mechanistic measurement. The paper's claim that "RL's beneficial footprint is a sparse, predictable correction" (Abstract) and that "only 1–3% of token positions are affected" (Section 3.1) is stated as a general finding, but the 1–3% range comes from Table 2, which does not include the 32B model.

Mitigation status: partially addressed by the KL-LoRA and REASONMAXXER results at scale, which provide functional validation (the sparse-targeting method works at 32B) but not mechanistic validation (we don't know the actual token-level divergence between the 32B base and RL models). The paper could strengthen its universality claim by reporting Table 2-equivalent statistics for the 32B pairs (Qwen2.5-32B → SimpleRL-Zoo and → Open-Reasoner-Zero). If the 32B token-level divergence shows similar sparsity (~1–4% reranked, ~0% shifted, 8–12× entropy ratio), the claim of scale-invariance would be directly supported. If it shows higher rerank rates or non-trivial shifted tokens, it would indicate that the sparse-correction characterization is scale-dependent, which would be an important finding in its own right. The current evidence leaves this question open.

### No Combination with Test-Time Compute Strategies Is Explored

REASONMAXXER produces a fine-tuned model (base model + LoRA adapter) that is then deployed with standard single-generation decoding. The paper does not test whether REASONMAXXER's gains are complementary to test-time compute strategies such as majority voting, best-of-N sampling, or verifier-guided search. This is not a flaw in REASONMAXXER itself — it is a standard fine-tuning method that produces a policy — but the absence of this analysis leaves open the question of whether the paper's efficiency claims would hold if the RL baselines were also evaluated with test-time compute augmentation.

The RL baselines in Table 3 are evaluated with pass@1 from a single greedy generation, the same as REASONMAXXER. This is a fair comparison of *policy quality* — both methods produce a model, and the model's first answer is compared. However, a substantial body of work (referenced in the paper's own Related Work, Section 7: Karan and Du, 2025; and the reference paper's detailed analysis of best-of-N, beam search, and revision strategies) shows that test-time compute can significantly boost reasoning performance, and that the optimal allocation of test-time compute depends on problem difficulty. If REASONMAXXER's gains come primarily from sharpening the policy at decision points — reducing entropy at critical forks — then combining REASONMAXXER with test-time sampling might yield smaller marginal gains than combining an RL-trained model with test-time sampling, because REASONMAXXER has already "spent" some of the easy gains that majority voting would otherwise capture. Conversely, if REASONMAXXER and test-time sampling are complementary — REASONMAXXER improves the proposal distribution, majority voting selects from it — the combined method might outperform either alone.

What evidence exists in the paper: none. All evaluation uses deterministic single-generation decoding (pass@1 at temperature 0 for the mechanistic analysis; standard pass@1 for benchmarks, with avg@8 only for AMC and AIME due to their small problem counts). The paper does not report pass@k for any k > 1, does not test majority voting over REASONMAXXER generations, and does not compare REASONMAXXER + majority voting against RL + majority voting. The avg@8 metric for AMC and AIME (Table 3) uses eight independent generations and averages their pass@1, but this is a variance-reduction technique for small benchmarks, not a test-time compute strategy — it does not involve selection or verification across the eight generations.

Mitigation status: not addressed. The paper's focus is on post-training efficiency, not inference-time strategy, so this is arguably out of scope. However, given that (a) the paper's core insight is that improvement is about *selection* (committing to the right branch at decision points), (b) test-time compute strategies are also fundamentally about selection (choosing among multiple sampled trajectories), and (c) the paper cites Karan and Du (2025) which explicitly advocates for test-time sampling over policy modification, the absence of even a basic majority-voting comparison is a missed opportunity. A simple experiment — REASONMAXXER with majority voting over 4/8/16 samples vs. RL model with majority voting over the same — would reveal whether REASONMAXXER's gains are additive with or subsumed by test-time selection. If additive, it would strengthen REASONMAXXER's practical value; if subsumed, it would suggest that test-time sampling alone might achieve similar gains to REASONMAXXER without any training, which would be an important caveat to the paper's training-efficiency claims.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper instigates a **paradigm shift in how the field conceptualizes post-training for LLM reasoning**, moving from a capability-acquisition frame to a sparse policy-selection frame. The magnitude of the shift is substantial: it is not merely an incremental efficiency improvement over existing RL pipelines but a fundamental reconceptualization of *what problem RL solves* in this domain. The paper's evidence suggests that the field has been using a sledgehammer (full RL infrastructure with online generation, policy gradients, and thousands of GPU-hours) to crack a nut (selecting the right token at a handful of high-entropy decision points that the base model already finds uncertain).

The practical consequence is a **democratization of reasoning model development**. When matching RL performance costs $4–25 instead of $100–$100,000, the barrier to entry for improving reasoning models collapses. Labs without access to large GPU clusters can now produce competitive reasoning models on consumer hardware in minutes. This changes the economics of the entire post-training ecosystem: the moat around reasoning model development shifts from *who can afford RL infrastructure* to *who has the best base model*, because the post-training step is now trivially cheap. This may accelerate the trend toward open-source reasoning models and reduce the concentration of reasoning capability in a few well-resourced organizations.

The paper also **resolves a tension that has been building in the literature** between those who observed that RL produces genuine gains (the DeepSeek-R1, GRPO, and PPO success stories) and those who argued that RL merely amplifies existing capabilities (Yue et al. 2025, Zhang et al. 2025, Davis and Recht 2025). Both camps were partially right. RL *does* produce genuine accuracy improvements — the paper's own Table 3 shows RL raising MATH-500 pass@1 from 0.298 to 0.496 on Qwen2.5-1.5B — but it does so through a mechanism that is far simpler than capability acquisition: a sparse, entropy-localized reranking of tokens the base model already favors. The paper's contribution is not to take a side in this debate but to **dissolve it** by characterizing the mechanism with sufficient precision that both observations become consistent: the gains are real, but they come from selection, not invention.

This resolution has downstream effects on **which research directions become more attractive, and which become less so**:

**More attractive:**
- **Verifier and reward model robustness:** If RL's bottleneck is selecting among existing paths at decision points, then improving the verifier's ability to correctly score partial solutions at high-entropy forks becomes the highest-leverage investment. The paper's finding that verifier over-optimization is a central limiting factor (Section 3.2, Figure 2) directly motivates research on verifiers that remain calibrated under optimization pressure.
- **Entropy-based methods for reasoning:** The paper demonstrates that base-model entropy is a powerful, teacher-free signal for where intervention is needed. This opens a broader research program around using model internals (entropy, attention patterns, hidden-state geometry) to guide lightweight post-training without RL. Methods that leverage the model's own uncertainty to decide *where* and *how* to intervene become natural extensions.
- **Compressed and modular post-training:** If RL's correction is representable in a rank-8 output-projection adapter (Appendix A, Table 4), then post-training can be treated as a module to be swapped, composed, or transferred across tasks. This connects to the broader modularity and model-editing literatures: reasoning improvement might be achievable by training a small set of "reasoning adapters" per domain and composing them at inference time.
- **Mechanistic interpretability as a design tool:** The paper demonstrates a workflow where mechanistic analysis (token-level divergence, oracle intervention, entropy localization) directly informs and simplifies training methodology. This is a template for how interpretability can be not just diagnostic but **generative** — producing new methods rather than merely explaining existing ones.

**Less attractive:**
- **Sophisticated RL algorithm design for outcome-based reasoning:** If GRPO, PPO, and RLOO all converge to the same sparse-correction pattern (Table 2), then investing in better policy gradient estimators, more elaborate advantage normalization schemes, or more complex exploration strategies is unlikely to yield substantial gains over existing RL algorithms. The bottleneck is not the RL algorithm but the verifier signal and the base model's existing capability distribution.
- **Full-parameter post-training for reasoning:** If a rank-32 adapter containing <0.5% of parameters captures RL's entire effect (Figure 3), full-parameter fine-tuning with RL is massive overkill. The community's default assumption that post-training requires updating all model weights should be revised downward by two orders of magnitude for reasoning tasks.
- **Online generation as a necessity:** REASONMAXXER's success with offline, static rollouts (generated once from the frozen base model) challenges the premise that iterative on-policy data collection is required. Research on making online RL *more efficient* may be addressing a problem that does not exist for this class of tasks — the issue was never the online/offline distinction, but the failure to target decision points.

A critical limitation of this landscape shift is its **current scope restriction to mathematical reasoning with verifiable outcomes**. The paper provides no evidence that the sparse-correction pattern holds for code generation, logical reasoning, scientific QA, or any domain without clean binary outcome signals. It is entirely possible that in domains requiring factual recall, multi-modal integration, or creative generation, RL *does* teach genuinely new capabilities through a more diffuse, distributed parameter change. The paper's reframing is therefore best understood as a **conditional paradigm shift**: for the class of problems where the base model already contains correct solutions within its sampling distribution and outcomes are verifiable, the problem is sparse policy selection, not capability acquisition. For other classes, the question remains open. The paper would be strengthened by even a single non-math experiment to probe this boundary.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Mapping the boundary conditions: where does the sparse-correction pattern fail?** The paper's mechanistic analysis (Table 2, Figure 2) establishes the sparse-correction pattern for three model families and three RL algorithms, but exclusively on mathematical reasoning. The most urgent follow-up is to replicate the token-level divergence analysis on at least one non-math reasoning domain — code generation (HumanEval, MBPP) would be the natural first step because it also has verifiable outcomes (unit tests) and the edge-of-competence filter would apply analogously. A researcher could take a base code model (e.g., CodeQwen or DeepSeek-Coder), its GRPO-trained counterpart (if publicly available), and run the identical token-level analysis from Section 3.1: measure rerank rate, shifted rate, mean rank of promoted tokens, and entropy ratio at reranked positions. If the pattern generalizes (sparse, entropy-localized, conservative reranking), it would strengthen the paper's universal claim substantially. If it does not — if code RL produces more diffuse changes, introduces genuinely novel tokens, or affects positions without elevated entropy — it would delineate the boundary of the sparse-correction regime and motivate domain-specific post-training strategies. A negative result would be equally informative: it would tell us *when* RL does something genuinely different from policy selection, which is currently unknown.

**Verifier-free difficulty estimation for dynamic budget allocation.** The paper's edge-of-competence filter (Section 5.1) requires generating 20 rollouts per candidate problem and checking correctness against ground-truth answers. This is a form of difficulty estimation, analogous to the 2048-sample difficulty estimation in the reference paper but far cheaper. A natural follow-up asks whether the *base model's entropy alone* — without any rollout generation or correctness checking — can predict which problems will benefit from REASONMAXXER-style post-training. Specifically, a researcher could compute the average token-level entropy of the base model's greedy generation on a held-out problem and correlate it with the pass-rate improvement REASONMAXXER produces. If high average entropy predicts large gains, then problem selection can be done with a single forward pass, eliminating the rollout generation cost from the pipeline entirely. This would address the paper's acknowledged limitation (Section 6, discussion of inference overhead) and make REASONMAXXER truly zero-shot in problem selection. The experiment requires: (a) a set of held-out problems with ground-truth answers, (b) base-model greedy generation + per-token entropy, (c) REASONMAXXER training on each problem individually, and (d) a correlation analysis between entropy and improvement.

**Combining REASONMAXXER with test-time compute strategies.** The paper evaluates REASONMAXXER with single-generation pass@1, but its central insight — that improvement is about selecting the right branch at decision points — suggests a natural complementarity with test-time sampling strategies. If REASONMAXXER sharpens the policy at decision points (reducing entropy at the critical forks), and test-time majority voting selects among multiple sampled trajectories, the combination might outperform either alone, because REASONMAXXER improves the *quality* of each sample and majority voting improves the *selection* among samples. A researcher could benchmark REASONMAXXER + majority voting (at k = 4, 8, 16, 32) against RL model + majority voting (at the same k) on MATH-500 and GSM8K. The hypothesis is that REASONMAXXER's gains are orthogonal to test-time sampling: the adapter improves the base policy, and sampling provides additional selection on top. If confirmed, the practical recommendation would be "train with REASONMAXXER, deploy with best-of-N" — combining the $4 training method with cheap inference-time sampling to push accuracy even higher. If disconfirmed — i.e., if RL + majority voting catches up to or exceeds REASONMAXXER + majority voting — it would suggest that RL provides benefits that only manifest under stochastic sampling (e.g., variance reduction across trajectories) that REASONMAXXER's deterministic correction misses.

**Stress-testing the KL anchor: what happens without it?** The paper's total loss includes a KL divergence anchor (Equation 7) with weight λ = 0.2 that penalizes any deviation from the base model at non-decision positions. The paper never ablates this term away. A critical follow-up is to train REASONMAXXER at λ = 0 (no anchor), λ = 0.1, λ = 0.5, and λ = 1.0, measuring both final accuracy and the degree of divergence from the base model on non-decision positions. The hypothesis from the paper is that the anchor is necessary to prevent overfitting given the small training set (1,000 sequences). If λ = 0 training succeeds — meaning the contrastive loss alone, applied to only 5% of tokens, produces the same accuracy gains without the anchor — it would simplify the method and strengthen the claim that the corrective signal is genuinely sparse and self-regularizing. If λ = 0 training fails (accuracy degrades or the adapter diverges), it would reveal that base-model anchoring is an essential component of the approach, not an optional stabilizer. This would have implications for deployment: in domains where the base model's behavior on non-decision tokens is already suboptimal, the anchor would lock in errors and limit REASONMAXXER's applicability. The experiment is trivial to run — it is a pure hyperparameter sweep using the existing codebase — and would resolve a significant open question about the method's mechanism.

**Adapter merging and the inference-time cost crossover.** REASONMAXXER uses a LoRA adapter that adds a small per-token inference overhead (~3% by the estimate in Section 6). LoRA adapters can typically be merged into the base weights post-training via W' = W + BA, eliminating the overhead entirely. The paper does not test whether merging preserves accuracy. A researcher should evaluate REASONMAXXER with merged weights on all benchmarks and compare against the unmerged adapter. If accuracy is preserved, the inference-time overhead disappears and the total-cost-of-ownership argument strengthens. If merging degrades accuracy — which can happen if the adapter learned to exploit the stochasticity of the low-rank parameterization — it would indicate that the adapter must remain separate, and the ~3% per-token overhead is a permanent cost of the method. Beyond the binary merge/no-merge test, a researcher could compute the crossover query count N where the accumulated per-query adapter overhead equals the training cost savings vs. RL. For example, if a 7B model serves 10 million queries at 3% overhead, the extra inference FLOPs might cost $X, and if X > (RL training cost − $5 REASONMAXXER cost), then total ownership favors RL for high-volume deployments despite higher training cost. This analysis would help practitioners decide when to use REASONMAXXER vs. full RL based on their expected inference volume — a decision rule currently absent from the paper.

**Scaling the number of training problems from the edge of competence.** REASONMAXXER uses exactly 50 training problems (filtered from 150). Is 50 the minimum, the optimum, or an arbitrary choice? A researcher could systematically vary the number of training problems retained after the mixed-success filter — say, 10, 25, 50, 100, 200 — by adjusting the initial candidate pool size, and measure final accuracy on MATH-500 and GSM8K. If performance plateaus at 25 problems, the method is even more data-efficient than the paper claims, and the filter can be tightened further. If performance continues improving to 200 problems, it suggests that more edge-of-competence data is beneficial and that the 50-problem choice was a practical compromise rather than an optimum. The shape of this scaling curve would characterize the data efficiency of the contrastive signal at decision points and inform practitioners about the minimum data requirements for new domains. Additionally, a researcher could test whether *curating* the 50 problems for diversity (e.g., spanning multiple MATH subcategories: algebra, geometry, number theory) outperforms random selection from the candidate pool, which would reveal whether problem diversity or sheer quantity drives REASONMAXXER's success.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Democratized reasoning model development for academic labs and independent researchers.** The most immediate application is enabling researchers with minimal compute budgets to produce competitive reasoning models. A lab with a single consumer GPU (e.g., an RTX 4090 or an RTX Pro 6000 as used in the paper) can take an open-source base model like Qwen2.5-7B, generate 3,000 rollouts on 150 MATH problems (a few GPU-hours), run REASONMAXXER training (single-digit GPU-hours), and produce a reasoning model that matches or exceeds publicly available RL-trained checkpoints that required hundreds of GPU-hours and thousands of dollars. The $5 cost figure for a 7B model means the entire post-training pipeline costs less than a coffee — a qualitative change in accessibility. This could accelerate research on reasoning model behavior, failure modes, and composition, because the barrier to *creating* a reasoning model is now negligible. A graduate student could ablate design choices (prompt formats, answer extraction, training data composition) at scale without grant funding for cloud compute.

**Rapid prototyping of domain-specific reasoners.** Organizations deploying LLMs for specialized reasoning tasks (medical diagnosis, legal analysis, financial modeling) often need to fine-tune models on domain-specific problems. Full RL pipelines are typically too expensive for prototyping: iterating on reward design, data quality, and hyperparameters at $600–$100,000 per run is prohibitive. REASONMAXXER reduces the cost to $4–25 per run, making rapid iteration feasible. A legal-tech company could curate 150 contract analysis problems with verifiable answers, run REASONMAXXER on a base model, evaluate, adjust the problem set, and retrain — all within a single day on a single GPU. This changes post-training from a capital-intensive bottleneck to a lightweight, iterative development step. The key requirement is that the domain has verifiable outcomes (binary correctness signals), which is true for many professional reasoning tasks: medical diagnosis against ground-truth labels, financial calculations with known answers, and legal compliance checks against regulations.

**Cost-efficient data generation for self-improvement loops.** The paper's framing of improvement as sparse policy selection implies that self-improvement pipelines — where a model generates training data for itself — can be made dramatically more efficient. In the standard STaR / ReST^EM paradigm, the model generates rollouts, filters for correct ones, and fine-tunes on them. REASONMAXXER's contribution is to show that this procedure should be *entropy-gated*: only apply the contrastive loss at decision points, and anchor everywhere else to the base model. A practitioner building a self-improvement loop could replace the full-parameter SFT step with a REASONMAXXER-style adapter trained on the model's own mixed-success rollouts. Because the adapter is tiny (0.3–0.5% of parameters) and the training data is small (50 problems), each self-improvement iteration becomes extremely cheap. This could enable *faster iteration cycles* in self-improvement pipelines — rather than training for thousands of steps on tens of thousands of problems per iteration, each iteration completes in minutes on a single GPU. The risk (identified by the paper's ReST^EM discussion in the reference paper context, and the sensitivity of revision training in the current paper's Appendix K) is that iterative self-improvement without careful data curation can amplify spurious correlations. However, REASONMAXXER's KL anchor provides a natural regularizer against drift: the adapter is anchored to the frozen base model at 95%+ of token positions, so each iteration makes only a small, targeted correction. Whether iterative REASONMAXXER converges to better performance than single-pass REASONMAXXER is an open empirical question, but the computational cost of testing it is now trivial.

**On-device reasoning improvement for small models.** The paper's results on small models are particularly striking for edge deployment. On Qwen2.5-1.5B, REASONMAXXER raises MATH-500 from 0.298 to 0.502 (a 68% relative improvement) for $4. On Qwen3-0.6B, it raises MATH-500 from 0.338 to 0.470 (39% relative improvement) and average benchmark performance from 0.189 to 0.278 (47% relative improvement) — all for $4. These models are small enough to run on-device (phones, laptops, embedded systems), and the LoRA adapter adds minimal storage and memory overhead (8.7M parameters for the 1.5B model, 0.49% of model size). A mobile application that needs offline mathematical reasoning could ship the base model plus the REASONMAXXER adapter as a small download, enabling on-device reasoning performance that previously required cloud-based RL-trained models. The adapter can also be *dynamically loaded*: the base model stays resident in memory, and different adapters (math, code, logic) are swapped in as needed, each costing a few megabytes of storage. This is a practical deployment architecture for multi-domain reasoning on resource-constrained devices.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper articulates a clear tradeoff between REASONMAXXER and full RL post-training, grounded in the mechanistic finding that RL's beneficial effect is sparse policy selection rather than capability acquisition. The decision rule is:

- **Prefer REASONMAXXER when:** the base model already achieves non-trivial but imperfect success on the target task (the "edge of competence," operationalized as pass rate strictly between 0 and 1 across 20 rollouts per problem); the domain has verifiable binary outcomes; training compute is a binding constraint (budget <$100, single GPU, or time measured in minutes to hours); and the goal is to match or approximately match RL performance at a fraction of the cost. This describes the majority of current math reasoning post-training scenarios: the base models (Qwen2.5, DeepSeek-R1-Distill, Qwen3) already contain correct solutions in their sampling distributions, and the task is to promote those solutions.

- **Prefer full RL when:** the base model's pass rate is near zero on the target problems, so the edge-of-competence filter would discard all available training data and REASONMAXXER would have no contrastive signal to exploit; the domain requires genuinely new reasoning strategies that the base model's sampling distribution does not contain; or the deployment involves extremely high inference volume where the per-query adapter overhead (~3%) accumulates sufficiently to offset training cost savings. However, the paper also notes that full RL itself fails to produce gains when the base model's pass rate is near zero (Section 3.2, Figure 2, bin 5-equivalent behavior), so this regime is hard for both methods — the appropriate intervention is likely better pretraining or mid-training rather than either post-training approach.

- **Consider test-time sampling (best-of-N, majority voting) without any training when:** the base model's pass@k at large k already approaches the desired accuracy, and the deployment can tolerate the latency of generating multiple samples per query. The paper cites Karan and Du (2025) as evidence that base models can achieve competitive performance through pure sampling, and REASONMAXXER's improvement over the base model can be partly understood as *amortizing* that sampling cost into a one-time training investment. If inference latency is not a concern and the model serves a small number of queries, sampling may be cheaper than any training at all. The crossover point — how many queries must be served before REASONMAXXER's training cost ($4–25) is cheaper than the cumulative cost of generating k samples per query — is not characterized in the paper and would be a useful practical analysis.

- **Use REASONMAXXER as a diagnostic before committing to RL:** even for tasks where RL is eventually planned, running REASONMAXXER first (cost: $4–25) provides a strong signal about whether the base model's sampling distribution already contains correct solutions. If REASONMAXXER matches or approaches target accuracy, full RL is unnecessary. If REASONMAXXER fails to improve the base model, it indicates that the base model lacks the necessary capabilities — the edge-of-competence filter likely discarded most problems — and investing in RL is unlikely to help either. In this diagnostic role, REASONMAXXER serves as a cheap capability audit: it tells you whether the base model "knows" the answer somewhere in its distribution, which is a prerequisite for both REASONMAXXER and (per the paper's mechanistic analysis) outcome-based RL to work.
