Here are the takeaways from this post on accelerated career growth (a friend reached Staff / IC6 at Google by 28 while working less than 40 hours a week):

**The core tradeoff**

The entire framework rests on one compressed claim: a friend got to Staff at Google by 28 while averaging under 40 hours a week, and the secret wasn’t grinding harder but being ruthless about *what* he worked on. "We all have the same amount of time. You can only get so much done if you grind with all your waking hours. So, the only way to have Staff+ impact is to be picky about what you work on."

**Skill one: understanding what results the org cares about most**

The first skill is learning to assess the impact of every task before committing. He turned down work he knew "wouldn’t be impactful enough," so each project he did take on kept the promotion door open even though his plate wasn’t overflowing. Ryan learned this lesson as a mid-level (IC4) engineer: he stopped blindly accepting work, and after enough conversations with his manager about senior (IC5) expectations, he knew that small features wouldn't be enough for promotion. The way to build this understanding, he says, is to discuss potential projects with a manager or tech leads.

**Skill two: saying “no” without causing thrash**

Simply dropping work isn't an option — "otherwise, you'll cause people problems." The **best way to say no is to overcommunicate**: explain that you have something more urgent or impactful on your plate. A good manager will be happy you're prioritising bigger impact over a small task. But he's careful to say you can't say no to everything that doesn't directly help your career: "you’d be a terrible team player, which will bite you long term."

**The extreme case — a Distinguished engineer (IC9) who negotiated his way out of oncall**

A mentor once told him about a Distinguished engineer (IC9) who was "so selfish with his time that he was the only person in the org to negotiate his way out of oncall and didn't answer most people who asked him questions." That left a bad taste in the mentor's mouth. Ryan frames the takeaway as a tradeoff, not a judgment: the IC9 wasn't necessarily wrong, but the story makes visible the cost extreme time-protection can have on relationships. His own stance is "evaluate case-by-case but still lean toward protecting your time."

**The closing thesis**

"If you don’t protect your time, you can’t get promoted no matter how much you work." Working as hard as you can isn't the strongest lever for impact by itself — selectivity is. Doing both — choosing high-impact work *and* protecting time to execute — is the multiplier that accelerates a career.
