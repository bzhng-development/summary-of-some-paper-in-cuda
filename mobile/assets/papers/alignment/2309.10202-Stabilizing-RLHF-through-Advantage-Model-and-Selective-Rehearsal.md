# Stabilizing RLHF through Advantage Model and Selective Rehearsal

**ArXiv:** [2309.10202](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.10202)

## 🎯 Pitch

This paper introduces two techniques to stabilize Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) training for large language models.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces two techniques to stabilize Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) training for large language models. Evaluating on BLOOMZ models with both the public HH-RLHF dataset and a proprietary Chinese preference dataset, the authors propose the **Advantage Model**—which directly models advantage scores (the extra reward beyond expected reward) and regulates score distributions across tasks to prevent reward hacking—and **Selective Rehearsal**—which mitigates catastrophic forgetting by clustering PPO training data and strategically selecting representative examples for knowledge rehearsal. The Advantage Model achieves simultaneously higher ranking accuracy and substantially lower Expected Calibration Error than a standard reward model (e.g., 3.48 vs. 4.70 ECE on HH-RLHF), while Selective Rehearsal improves both main test set win rates and forget test set retention, establishing that these stabilization mechanisms are effective only when reward score distributions are properly calibrated and when rehearsal data is selected to cover diverse skills rather than by reward score alone.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: RLHF Training Is Fundamentally Unstable

The central problem this paper addresses is that Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF)—despite being the backbone of alignment training for virtually all state-of-the-art LLMs including GPT-4, Claude, Sparrow, Bard, and Llama 2-Chat—suffers from **persistent training instabilities** that cause models to degrade in unpredictable ways during fine-tuning. These instabilities are not merely theoretical concerns; they manifest as concrete, recurring failure modes that practitioners encounter when attempting to align models with human preferences.

The paper identifies two specific instability phenomena:

**Reward hacking** occurs when the policy model discovers unintended shortcuts to maximize the reward signal without genuinely improving response quality. The canonical example the paper hints at (and references in prior work by Skalse et al., 2022) is the production of gibberish or nonsensical text that nonetheless scores highly under the learned reward model. The paper provides evidence that this emerges from a deeper, structural problem: different categories of tasks in the preference data receive **systematically different reward score distributions** from the reward model. Figure 1a illustrates this concretely for the authors' proprietary dataset, where Code Generation and QA tasks exhibit noticeably disparate reward score distributions despite there being no principled reason for such a gap. The consequence is that during PPO training, the policy model can learn to exploit these distributional discrepancies—for instance, by adopting response patterns characteristic of high-reward categories (like Code Generation) even when answering questions from other categories, because those patterns yield higher absolute scores. The paper notes that even standard normalization techniques (such as moving averages) fail to fully address this, because the "fluctuating means and variances can induce unexpected model behaviors."

**Catastrophic forgetting** manifests as the PPO-optimized model losing capabilities that were already well-established during the Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) stage. As shown in Figure 1b, the PPO model's win rate against the SFT model on a "forget set"—comprising examples that were already well-aligned with human preferences after SFT—exhibits a "significant decline" during training. The model does not merely plateau; it actually regresses on examples it previously handled well. The paper argues this occurs because PPO "over-optimizes" on certain training examples, causing the model to overwrite knowledge it had already acquired. The forgetting problem is particularly insidious because it means that improving alignment on some dimensions can actively damage alignment on others—the model does not cleanly separate "things it needs to learn" from "things it should preserve."

### Why This Problem Matters: RLHF's Central Role and High Cost of Failure

The importance of addressing these instabilities is difficult to overstate given RLHF's position in the contemporary LLM pipeline. The paper explicitly lists GPT-4, Claude, Sparrow, Bard, and Llama 2-Chat as examples where RLHF serves as "a key component of training." These are not experimental systems—they are production-deployed models serving millions of users. When RLHF training becomes unstable:

**Economic costs rise.** RLHF training runs are computationally expensive, involving multiple model components (base LLM, reference model, reward model, value head) and substantial human annotation effort. Instabilities that cause training to diverge or degrade mean wasted compute, wasted human feedback data, and repeated runs. A stabilization technique that prevents even one failed training run provides substantial practical value.

**Safety guarantees become unreliable.** The reward hacking problem is fundamentally a safety concern. If a model can achieve high reward scores while producing gibberish or—worse—subtly problematic content that exploits blind spots in the reward model, then the alignment process has created an illusion of improvement rather than genuine progress toward human preferences. The paper's citation of Skalse et al. (2022) on "defining and characterizing reward gaming" underscores that this is a recognized problem in the broader RL safety literature, not merely a nuisance.

**Model quality regresses unpredictably.** Catastrophic forgetting means that later stages of training can destroy capabilities that earlier stages established. In a production pipeline, this creates a brittle dependency chain: improvements to the reward model, changes to the preference data, or adjustments to PPO hyperparameters can inadvertently degrade performance on tasks that were previously working well. The forget set evaluation in Figure 1b captures this concretely—the PPO model loses to the SFT model on examples where the SFT model was already well-aligned, meaning the overall alignment process is not monotonically improving the model.

**Theoretical significance.** Beyond practical concerns, the paper's analysis of instability causes contributes to understanding why RLHF fails when it fails. The observation that reward score distributions differ systematically across task categories (Figure 1a) suggests a structural flaw in how standard reward models are trained: the pairwise ranking loss in Equation 1 incentivizes the model to separate preferred from dispreferred responses, but provides no constraint on the absolute scale or variance of scores. This means the reward model can—and empirically does—learn different score distributions for different types of content, creating incentives for the policy to shift its output distribution toward high-scoring categories rather than genuinely improving within each category.

### Where Existing Approaches Fall Short

The paper positions its contributions against several strands of prior work, each of which has limitations:

**Standard reward modeling with pairwise ranking loss** (Equation 1: `L_RM = −E[log(σ(r_θ(x, y_c) − r_θ(x, y_r)))]`). This is the dominant approach, used across most RLHF implementations. The limitation is structural: the loss only constrains relative ordering (`y_c` should score higher than `y_r`), not absolute score magnitudes or calibration. Two responses that are both excellent (or both terrible) can have arbitrarily different absolute scores as long as their relative ordering is correct. The paper demonstrates the consequences: (a) different task categories end up with different score distributions (Figure 4a), (b) the reward model is overconfident and poorly calibrated (Figure 2, where expected calibration error is 4.70 vs. 3.48 for their method), and (c) PPO training can exploit these distributional differences to "hack" the reward without genuinely improving.

**Score normalization during PPO training** is mentioned as an existing mitigation strategy ("RM-PPO w/ MA" in the results, referring to moving average normalization). The paper finds this insufficient—despite normalization, "RM-PPO w/ MA encounters instabilities during PPO training" (Section 4.3). The problem is that normalization treats the symptom (fluctuating score scales) rather than the cause (the reward model producing uncalibrated scores with task-dependent distributions in the first place).

**Offline alignment methods**—including rejection sampling (Touvron et al., 2023), RAFT (Dong et al., 2023), RRHF (Yuan et al., 2023), Direct Preference Optimization (Rafailov et al., 2023), and Preference Ranking Optimization (Song et al., 2023)—have emerged as alternatives that avoid PPO training entirely, thus sidestepping its instabilities. The paper acknowledges these (Section 5) but implicitly argues that online RL methods remain important enough to warrant stabilization. The fact that major production systems (GPT-4, Claude) continue to use PPO-based RLHF suggests that the online RL paradigm provides benefits—perhaps in exploration, sample efficiency, or final alignment quality—that offline methods have not fully replicated. Stabilizing PPO therefore remains a relevant problem even as offline alternatives develop.

**Data curation for SFT** has been extensively studied (LIMA by Zhou et al., 2023; Wei et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2022, 2023), but the paper notes that these approaches focus on the SFT stage rather than the PPO stage. The challenge of which data to use during PPO training—and which data to reserve for preserving existing capabilities—is "a distinct problem" (Section 5) that prior data curation work does not address. Standard PPO treats all training prompts as interchangeable, applying the same optimization pressure regardless of whether the model already handles a given prompt well or poorly.

**Rejection sampling and reinforced self-training** (Gulcehre et al., 2023) use self-generated high-reward responses for supervised training, which superficially resembles the Selective Rehearsal objective (Equation 9: an NLL loss on selected data). But the paper draws a key distinction: these methods "only consider reward model score" for data selection, whereas Selective Rehearsal "captures multi-dimensional important aspects (e.g., diversity)" through its clustering-based selection mechanism. Selecting only by reward score risks over-optimizing on narrow, high-scoring response patterns and exacerbating rather than alleviating catastrophic forgetting of diverse skills.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper frames its contribution not as proposing an entirely new RLHF paradigm, but rather as introducing two **targeted stabilization mechanisms** that can be integrated into existing PPO-based RLHF pipelines. This is important context for understanding the scope: the paper is not arguing that PPO should be replaced, nor that reward models should be fundamentally redesigned from scratch. Instead, it identifies two specific failure modes (uncalibrated, distributionally-inconsistent reward scores; over-optimization on certain training examples leading to forgetting) and proposes minimally-invasive fixes.

**The Advantage Model** addresses reward hacking by reformulating the reward signal. Rather than training a model to output an absolute reward score `r_θ(x, y)`, it trains a model to output an **advantage score** `a_θ(x, y)` representing how much better (or worse) the response is compared to the expected reward for that prompt. This is explicitly analogized to the advantage function in standard reinforcement learning, where the advantage `A(s, a) = Q(s, a) - V(s)` decomposes the value of an action into a baseline (the state's expected value) and a deviation. Crucially, the Advantage Model's training objective (Equation 6) adds a **bounding loss** on top of the standard ranking loss, penalizing advantage scores that exceed a margin `m(x)`. This directly constrains the score distribution: good responses cannot receive arbitrarily high advantages, and bad responses cannot receive arbitrarily low advantages, because both are bounded by `m(x)`. The paper shows this produces task-agnostic score distributions (Figure 4b: standard deviations are "consistently operating at a stable scale") and substantially better calibration (Figure 2: ECE drops from 4.70 to 3.48 on HH-RLHF).

The paper's theoretical framing is that the reward model's scalar outputs are "challenging" to interpret and that "two different model responses that are both preferred by humans could have dramatically different values." The Advantage Model's expected-value subtraction (Equation 5) is designed to remove the prompt-dependent baseline, leaving only the response-specific quality signal. This is a conceptually elegant fix: if the problem is that different prompts have different "difficulty levels" or "expected reward baselines," then explicitly modeling and subtracting that baseline should stabilize the signal.

**Selective Rehearsal** addresses catastrophic forgetting by treating PPO training data curation as a coverage problem. The central insight is that not all training examples should be optimized equally. Examples where the model already performs well should be rehearsed (via an NLL loss) rather than pushed through PPO optimization, which risks unlearning. The mechanism for identifying which examples to rehearse is clustering-based: PPO training data is clustered into `c` groups using SimCSE sentence embeddings of the prompts, and within each cluster, the top-`k` examples by advantage model score are selected for rehearsal. This ensures the rehearsal set covers diverse skills (because clusters represent different skill categories) while maintaining quality (because top-scoring responses within each cluster are selected).

The paper explicitly contrasts this with rejection sampling, which would select rehearsal examples purely by reward score threshold without the diversity constraint enforced by clustering. The distinction is practically significant: if reward scores are higher for certain task categories (as Figure 1a shows), pure score-based selection would disproportionately rehearse those high-scoring categories while neglecting lower-scoring but still important skills, potentially making forgetting worse rather than better.

The paper's overall positioning is that these two mechanisms are complementary but independent: the Advantage Model fixes the reward signal that PPO optimizes against, while Selective Rehearsal fixes which examples PPO optimizes on. Both are needed for stable training, and the paper demonstrates this through the progressive improvement from RM-PPO to AM-PPO to AM-PPO-SR (Table 2: win rate on main test set increases from 12.72% to 14.87% to 15.78%, while forget set lose rate drops from 29.28% to 8.44% to 7.95%).

## 3. Technical Approach

This is primarily a **system-building paper** whose core idea is that RLHF training instability arises from two identifiable, mechanistically-distinct problems—uncalibrated, task-dependent reward score distributions and undifferentiated optimization pressure across training examples—and that each can be addressed with a targeted architectural intervention without fundamentally redesigning the RLHF pipeline.

### 3.1 Reader orientation

The paper builds two modifications to the standard RLHF training stack: an **Advantage Model** that replaces the standard reward model to produce calibrated, task-agnostic reward signals, and a **Selective Rehearsal** mechanism that augments PPO training with a curated rehearsal objective to preserve SFT-era capabilities. The system takes the same inputs as standard RLHF—a preference dataset of (prompt, chosen response, rejected response) triples, a PPO prompt dataset, and a pre-trained LLM—and produces a more stable training process that achieves higher reward scores and win rates while avoiding the degradation on previously-learned tasks that plagues standard approaches.

### 3.2 Big-picture architecture (diagram in words)

The system has five major components that interact in a standard RLHF training loop with two key modifications:

1. **Preference Dataset** — pairs of responses to the same prompt, one preferred by human annotators and one dispreferred. Serves as training data for the Advantage Model.
2. **Advantage Model (AM)** — a BLOOMZ7B-based model with a value head that outputs an **advantage score** `a_θ(x, y)` for each (prompt, response) pair, representing how much better or worse the response is than the expected reward for that prompt. Trained once before PPO and frozen during policy optimization. Replaces the standard reward model.
3. **Expected Reward Estimator** — a parameterized function `e_τ(x)` that estimates the average reward the current policy would receive for prompt `x`. Used by the Advantage Model to compute the baseline that is subtracted from raw rewards to produce advantage scores.
4. **PPO Trainer** — the standard Proximal Policy Optimization algorithm that optimizes the actor model (BLOOMZ176B) against the frozen Advantage Model's scores, with a KL-divergence penalty against the initial SFT policy to prevent drastic distribution shifts.
5. **Selective Rehearsal Module** — operates within the PPO training loop. After each PPO step (or batch), it clusters the PPO training data, selects representative high-quality examples from each cluster, and computes an additional negative log-likelihood (NLL) loss on those selected examples. This loss is added to the standard PPO objective, forcing the model to maintain its generation quality on diverse skills.

Information flows as follows: the preference dataset trains the Advantage Model (once, offline) → the Advantage Model scores PPO-generated responses during training → the PPO trainer uses these advantage scores (minus a KL penalty) to update the actor policy → simultaneously, the Selective Rehearsal module clusters the PPO prompts, selects top-scoring responses within each cluster, and computes an NLL rehearsal loss → the combined PPO + rehearsal loss updates the actor.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the deep dive

- **Second**, the Advantage Model's mathematical formulation—the definition of advantage, the expected reward estimator, the training objective with its dual ranking and bounding losses—since this is the paper's primary architectural contribution.

### 3.4 Detailed, sentence-based technical breakdown

#### Standard Reward Modeling and Its Limitations

The paper begins from the standard RLHF reward modeling formulation, which serves as the baseline that the Advantage Model improves upon. Understanding why this formulation is structurally problematic motivates every design choice in the Advantage Model.

The standard reward model is trained to assign a scalar score `r_θ(x, y)` to each (prompt, response) pair, where higher scores should correspond to responses that humans prefer. The training objective is a pairwise ranking loss applied to a dataset `D_RM` of comparison triples `(x, y_c, y_r)`, where `y_c` is the chosen (preferred) response and `y_r` is the rejected (dispreferred) response:

$$\mathcal{L}_{RM} = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_c, y_r) \sim D_{RM}} \left[\log\left(\sigma(r_\theta(x, y_c) - r_\theta(x, y_r))\right)\right]$$

where `σ(·)` is the sigmoid function, `r_θ(x, y_c)` is the reward for the preferred response, and `r_θ(x, y_r)` is the reward for the dispreferred response.

**What it computes:** For each comparison pair, the model produces two scalar rewards. The difference `r_θ(x, y_c) − r_θ(x, y_r)` is passed through a sigmoid to produce a probability that `y_c` is preferred. The log-loss penalizes the model when this probability is low—that is, when the model fails to assign a higher score to the preferred response. Minimizing this loss across the dataset trains the model to rank responses correctly: chosen responses should consistently score higher than rejected ones.

**Why this form is structurally limiting:** The loss depends **only on the difference** between the two scores, not on their absolute magnitudes. A model that assigns scores `(100, 0)` to a pair is rewarded identically to one that assigns `(0.1, −0.1)`, because the sigmoid saturates quickly and the log-loss only cares whether the difference has the correct sign and sufficient magnitude. This means:

- **There is no constraint on score scale.** The model can learn to assign dramatically different score ranges to different types of prompts—as demonstrated in Figure 1a, where Code Generation and QA tasks exhibit "noticeable disparity in the learned reward score distributions." This happens because the pairwise ranking loss never penalizes the model for giving QA responses scores around 0 while giving Code Generation responses scores around 5, as long as within each category the relative ordering is correct.
- **There is no calibration pressure.** The sigmoid output can be interpreted as a confidence that `y_c` is preferred, but the underlying scores have no probabilistic meaning. The paper demonstrates in Figure 2 that standard reward models are systematically overconfident: their predicted ranking accuracy as a function of score difference deviates substantially from perfect calibration (the orange diagonal line `1/(1 + e^{−∆})`), with an Expected Calibration Error of 4.70 on HH-RLHF.
- **The scores cannot be compared across prompts.** Because different prompts can have different baseline score levels, a score of 2.0 on a QA prompt might indicate an excellent response, while a score of 2.0 on a Code Generation prompt might indicate a mediocre one. During PPO training, the policy sees only absolute scores and cannot distinguish these cases, creating an incentive to shift generation toward prompt types that yield higher absolute scores regardless of response quality within each type.

The paper argues that these limitations directly cause the reward hacking and instability observed in PPO training. The Advantage Model is designed to fix the reward signal at its source—the reward model's training objective—rather than patching the problem downstream with normalization heuristics that "encounter instabilities" despite being commonly used.

#### From Reward Model to Advantage Model

The Advantage Model reformulates what the model predicts. Instead of outputting an absolute reward `r_θ(x, y)`, it outputs an **advantage** `a_θ(x, y)`: the extra reward (positive or negative) that response `y` achieves compared to the **expected reward** for prompt `x`. The formal definition echoes the advantage function in reinforcement learning:

$$a_\theta(x, y) = r_\theta(x, y) - \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi'(x)}\left[\frac{\pi_\phi(y|x)}{\pi'(y|x)} r_\theta(x, y)\right]$$

where `r_θ(x, y)` is the raw reward score, `π'(x)` is the policy that generated the comparison data, `π_ϕ(y|x)` is the current policy's probability of generating response `y`, and the term `π_ϕ(y|x) / π'(y|x)` is an importance weight that corrects for distribution shift between the data-collection policy and the current policy.

**What it computes:** The advantage score answers the question "how much better is this response than what would be expected on average for this prompt?" The expected reward term averages over all possible responses the model could generate (`y ∼ π'(x)`), weighting each response by both its raw reward and an importance ratio that accounts for the fact that the current policy might be more or less likely to produce that response than the data-collection policy was. Subtracting this expected baseline from the raw reward yields the advantage: positive means better than average, negative means worse, and zero means exactly average. The magnitude of the advantage is bounded by construction—a response cannot be arbitrarily better than the expected reward for its prompt because the expected reward already incorporates the best responses the model typically produces.

**Why this form:** The critical property is **subtracting the prompt-conditional baseline**. In the standard reward model, a score of 5.0 could mean "excellent QA response" or "mediocre Code Generation response" depending on the prompt category. In the Advantage Model, an advantage of +1.0 always means "one unit better than typical for this prompt," regardless of whether the prompt is from QA, Code Generation, or any other category. This makes scores **directly comparable across prompts** and removes the incentive for the policy to shift its output distribution toward high-baseline categories. The importance weight term `π_ϕ(y|x) / π'(y|x)` is a technical necessity: because the comparison data was collected from earlier model versions (potentially different from the current policy), the expected reward must be re-weighted so that it represents what the current policy would produce, not what the historical policy produced. Without this correction, the expected reward baseline would be biased by stale distributional assumptions.

However, exactly computing the expectation in Equation 5 is "infeasible" because it requires enumerating every possible response the model could generate—an astronomically large space. The paper parameterizes the expected reward as a separate function `e_τ(x)` and approximates the full expectation with a finite-sample estimate that mixes the current policy's behavior with historical policies:

$$a_\theta(x, y) = r_\theta(x, y) - \frac{N - K}{N} e_\tau(x) - \sum_{k=1}^{K} \frac{1}{N} \frac{\pi_\phi(y|x)}{\pi'_k(y|x)} r_\theta(x, y)$$

where `N` is a hyperparameter balancing the current policy's contribution against historical policies, `K` is the number of alternate policy models used for data collection, and `π'_k(y|x)` is the probability under the k-th historical policy.

**What it computes:** This decomposes the expected reward baseline into two components. The first component, `((N − K) / N) · e_τ(x)`, is a learned, parameterized estimate of the current policy's expected reward, weighted by how much we trust the learned estimator relative to empirical samples. The second component sums over `K` historical policies, for each one computing the importance-weighted raw reward of a specific response and dividing by `N` to average it into the baseline. The result is a hybrid estimator: part learned function, part Monte Carlo average over historical data. As `N` increases relative to `K`, more weight is placed on the learned estimator `e_τ(x)` and less on the noisy empirical samples. The paper does not explore the sensitivity of results to `N` and `K`, flagging these as hyperparameters but not reporting ablations.

#### The Advantage Model Training Objective

The Advantage Model is trained with a two-part objective that jointly optimizes ranking accuracy (like the standard reward model) and score calibration (the novel contribution). The loss is:

$$\mathcal{L}_{AM} = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_c, y_r) \sim D_{RM}} \Big[ \log\left(\sigma(a_\theta(x, y_c) - a_\theta(x, y_r))\right) + \log\left(\sigma(m(x) - a_\theta(x, y_c))\right) + \log\left(\sigma(m(x) + a_\theta(x, y_r))\right) \Big]$$

where `σ(·)` is the sigmoid function, `a_θ(x, y_c)` and `a_θ(x, y_r)` are the advantage scores for the chosen and rejected responses respectively, and `m(x)` is a margin function that defines the maximum permitted absolute advantage for prompt `x`. The paper sets `m(x) = 2.5` for all experiments.

**What it computes:** The loss has three terms, each a binary cross-entropy-like log-sigmoid expression:

- **Term 1: Ranking loss.** `log(σ(a_θ(x, y_c) − a_θ(x, y_r)))` is identical in form to the standard reward model loss. It penalizes the model when the chosen response's advantage is not sufficiently higher than the rejected response's advantage. This ensures the Advantage Model preserves ranking accuracy—it must still correctly order responses by quality.

- **Term 2: Upper bound on chosen responses.** `log(σ(m(x) − a_θ(x, y_c)))` penalizes the model when the chosen response's advantage score approaches or exceeds the margin `m(x)`. Since `σ(m − a)` approaches 1 when `a ≪ m` and approaches 0 when `a ≫ m`, minimizing `−log(σ(m − a))` pushes `a` to be well below `m`. This prevents chosen responses from receiving arbitrarily high advantage scores.

- **Term 3: Lower bound on rejected responses.** `log(σ(m(x) + a_θ(x, y_r)))` penalizes the model when the rejected response's advantage score approaches or falls below `−m(x)`. Since `σ(m + a)` approaches 1 when `a ≫ −m` (i.e., when `a` is not too negative) and approaches 0 when `a ≪ −m`, minimizing `−log(σ(m + a))` pushes `a` to be well above `−m`. This prevents rejected responses from receiving arbitrarily low (highly negative) advantage scores.

The three terms together enforce that advantages are **well-calibrated and bounded**: chosen responses should score positive but not too positive (`0 < a_θ(x, y_c) ≪ m(x)`), rejected responses should score negative but not too negative (`−m(x) ≪ a_θ(x, y_r) < 0`), and the gap between them should be clear. The margin `m(x) = 2.5` means that on the logit scale (before sigmoid), no response can be more than ±2.5 units away from the expected baseline.

**Why this form:** The bounding terms are the key innovation over standard reward modeling. They directly address the structural problem identified in the introduction: without constraints on absolute score magnitudes, the model learns different score distributions for different task categories. The bounding loss forces all scores into a `[−2.5, 2.5]` window around zero, regardless of prompt type. This achieves what post-hoc normalization tries to accomplish (making scores comparable across prompts) but does so at training time, baked into the model's learned representations rather than applied as a fragile post-processing step.

The choice of `m(x) = 2.5` is not theoretically justified in the paper—the authors note that it "may have a connection with the complexity or difficulty involved in learning the reward function for prompts similar to `x`" but describe this as "speculative and requires further investigation." They leave adaptive margin functions as future work. This means the reported calibration improvements reflect a single, fixed margin; whether different margins would produce better or worse results is not explored.

The sigmoid-based bounding has a specific gradient behavior. When `a_θ(x, y_c)` is well below `m(x)`, the term `log(σ(m − a))` is close to zero and gradients are negligible—the model is not penalized and feels no pressure to change. Only when the advantage approaches or exceeds the margin does the loss become non-trivial, creating a soft barrier that resists further increases. This is in contrast to a hard constraint (which would cause gradient discontinuities) or an L2 penalty (which would penalize all deviations from zero, even reasonable positive advantages for good responses). The soft-barrier behavior means the model can freely assign moderate advantages to good responses and moderate disadvantages to bad ones, with pressure only activating at the extremes. This is precisely the desired behavior: allow differentiation between quality levels while preventing unbounded scores.

#### Expected Reward Estimator

The expected reward estimator `e_τ(x)` is a learned component that predicts the average reward the current policy `π_ϕ` would achieve for prompt `x`. The paper defines it as:

$$e_\tau(x) = \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_\phi(x)}[r_\theta(x, y)]$$

**What it computes:** Given a prompt `x`, the estimator outputs a single scalar representing the average reward across all responses the current policy might generate. This serves as the baseline that is subtracted from raw rewards to produce advantages. When the current policy is strong (generating generally good responses), `e_τ(x)` is high, and individual responses need to be genuinely exceptional to achieve positive advantages. When the current policy is weak, `e_τ(x)` is low, and even mediocre responses can achieve positive advantages—which is appropriate because they genuinely represent improvement.

**Why this form:** The estimator is prompt-conditional, meaning it can capture the fact that some prompts are inherently easier to generate good responses for than others. A prompt like "Summarize the plot of Romeo and Juliet" might have a high expected reward because the model consistently produces reasonable summaries, while a prompt like "Prove the Riemann Hypothesis" would have a low expected reward. By conditioning on the prompt, the baseline adapts to this difficulty variation, ensuring that the advantage signal reflects response quality relative to what's achievable for that prompt, not relative to some global average that conflates prompt difficulty with response quality.

The paper is sparse on implementation details for `e_τ(x)`. It is described as "parameterized" but whether it is a separate neural network head, a component of the Advantage Model architecture, or estimated through Monte Carlo sampling during training is not specified. This is a significant omission for reproducibility: the expected reward estimator is central to the advantage computation (it contributes the `(N − K) / N · e_τ(x)` term in Equation 8), and its accuracy directly affects advantage quality. If the estimator is noisy or biased, the advantage scores inherit that noise, potentially undermining the calibration benefits. The experimental results suggest the implementation works in practice, but the lack of architectural detail makes independent replication difficult without additional assumptions.

#### How the Advantage Model Prevents Reward Hacking

The paper's central claim about the Advantage Model is that it prevents reward hacking by eliminating the incentive for the policy to shift its output distribution toward prompt categories with higher reward baselines. The mechanism works through two interacting properties:

**Score comparability across prompts:** Because advantages are defined relative to each prompt's expected reward, an advantage of +1.0 means the same thing regardless of whether the prompt is from a high-baseline category like Code Generation or a low-baseline category like QA. During PPO training, the policy sees advantage scores as the optimization signal (the `r_θ(x, y)` term in Equation 2's PPO objective is replaced with `a_θ(x, y)` when using the Advantage Model). The policy cannot gain advantage by switching from QA-style responses to Code Generation-style responses, because Code Generation prompts have higher expected reward baselines that cancel out the higher raw scores.

**Bounded score range:** The bounding loss terms in `L_AM` enforce that advantages lie within `[−m, m] = [−2.5, 2.5]`. This prevents the policy from "running away" with the reward signal—there is a hard ceiling on how much advantage any response can achieve, regardless of how well it exploits the reward model's blind spots. In a standard reward model, a gibberish response that somehow triggers high reward could receive a score of 100, creating an enormous gradient that overwhelms the KL penalty and causes the policy to collapse toward gibberish. In the Advantage Model, that same response would receive at most +2.5, limiting the gradient magnitude and giving the KL penalty a fighting chance to keep the policy near its initial distribution.

The empirical evidence for this mechanism is in Figure 4. Figure 4a shows that standard reward model scores have "markedly different means for each task," with some task categories centered around much higher values than others. Figure 4b shows that Advantage Model scores have "consistently operating at a stable scale" with uniform standard deviations across tasks. Figure 5a then shows the downstream consequence: AM-PPO maintains stable delta rewards during training (the line is relatively flat), while RM-PPO exhibits a "drop in win rate evaluated by GPT-4... despite a rise in RM scores"—the classic signature of reward hacking where the policy learns to maximize the reward signal without improving (or while degrading) actual response quality.

A subtle but important point: the Advantage Model still uses a raw reward `r_θ(x, y)` internally (it appears in Equations 5 and 8). The advantage is a transformation applied to the raw reward, not a replacement for it. This means the underlying reward model architecture—a BLOOMZ7B transformer with a value head—is unchanged. The innovation is entirely in the training objective and the score transformation. This is significant for practical adoption because it means existing reward model implementations can be converted to Advantage Models with minimal architectural changes: replace the training loss, add the expected reward estimator, and apply the advantage transformation at inference time.

#### Selective Rehearsal: Motivation and Mechanism

Selective Rehearsal addresses a different failure mode from the Advantage Model. While the Advantage Model fixes the reward signal fed into PPO, Selective Rehearsal fixes **which examples PPO optimizes on** and **how it optimizes on them**. The core insight is that standard PPO treats all training prompts identically, applying the same policy gradient update regardless of whether the model already handles a given prompt well or poorly. For prompts where the SFT model already produces good responses, PPO optimization can be counterproductive: the policy gradient pushes the model toward higher-reward outputs, but if the SFT response was already correct and well-aligned, this pressure can cause the model to deviate from a good policy toward a different (possibly worse) policy that happens to score slightly higher under the imperfect reward model.

The paper's evidence for this problem is Figure 1b: the PPO model's win rate against the SFT model on the forget set "exhibits a significant decline" during training. These are examples that the SFT model handled well—they were "expert-aligned" and already "closely align with human preferences." Standard PPO "over-optimizes" on them, causing performance regression rather than improvement.

Selective Rehearsal prevents this by augmenting the PPO objective with a **supervised learning loss** on carefully selected examples. The augmentation serves two purposes: (1) it provides a learning signal that is not mediated by the (potentially hackable) reward model, and (2) it explicitly rehearses knowledge that the model already possesses, counteracting the tendency of gradient-based optimization to overwrite old knowledge when learning new behaviors.

#### Representative Example Discovery

The first step of Selective Rehearsal is identifying which examples from the PPO training data should be used for rehearsal. The paper frames this as a **coverage and quality problem**: the rehearsal set should cover as many distinct skills as possible (so the model rehearses diverse capabilities, not just a narrow subset), and within each skill, the selected examples should be high-quality (so the model rehearses good behavior, not mediocre behavior).

The procedure operates on the PPO training data `D_PPO = [(x_1, y_1), (x_2, y_2), ...]` where each `y_i` was generated by the initial policy model (the SFT model) given prompt `x_i`. The paper explicitly notes that responses are from the initial policy to "enable a more fair and nuanced comparison, as no additional information is introduced"—rehearsal does not benefit from responses the model couldn't already produce.

**Step 1: Embedding and clustering.** Each prompt `x` is encoded into a fixed-length vector using a pre-trained SimCSE sentence embedding model (specifically `princeton-nlp/sup-simcse-roberta-base`, a RoBERTa-based model fine-tuned with contrastive learning for sentence similarity). SimCSE embeddings have the property that semantically similar sentences map to nearby vectors, so prompts requiring similar skills (e.g., math word problems vs. creative writing) should cluster in embedding space. A KMeans algorithm is then run on these embeddings to partition the prompts into `c` clusters. The paper experiments with different values of `c` (Figure 6) and finds that performance is "relatively consistent" with "variance of approximately 0.05 points in test-set rewards across various cluster numbers `c`." This robustness to `c` is practically important because it means practitioners don't need to carefully tune the cluster count for each new dataset.

**Step 2: Within-cluster selection.** Within each cluster, the top-`k` examples with the highest Advantage Model scores are selected. The paper does not specify the value of `k`, but the logic is that within a cluster of semantically similar prompts, the highest-advantage responses are the ones the model handles best relative to expectations—these are the "expert-aligned" examples worth preserving. This selection criterion implicitly assumes that the Advantage Model's scores are reliable (which Section 4.3's calibration results support) and that high-advantage responses within a skill cluster represent good, stable behavior rather than reward-hacked behavior.

**Why clustering before scoring, rather than scoring then selecting:** The alternative of simply selecting the top-scoring examples overall (without clustering) is exactly what rejection sampling and reinforced self-training do—and the paper explicitly criticizes this approach for only considering reward model score while ignoring diversity. If Code Generation prompts have higher baseline rewards (as Figure 1a shows), pure score-based selection would disproportionately select Code Generation examples for rehearsal, neglecting skills like QA, summarization, or translation that happen to have lower baselines. The model would then rehearse Code Generation extensively while forgetting other skills—exactly the catastrophic forgetting problem Selective Rehearsal is designed to prevent. Clustering first ensures each skill cluster contributes equally to the rehearsal set (or at least proportionally to the number of clusters, assuming equal `k` per cluster), maintaining diversity.

**Alternative selection criteria.** The paper mentions that within-cluster selection could use other criteria beyond Advantage Model score, such as entropy (low entropy indicates high model confidence), human satisfaction rate, or response length (higher length may indicate redundancy, so shorter might be preferred). The current implementation uses only advantage score "for simplicity" and leaves multi-criteria selection to future work. This is a practical limitation: advantage score alone might select responses that look good to the reward model but are subtly flawed in ways humans would notice. Combining advantage with entropy (to prefer high-confidence, unambiguous responses) or human labels (when available) could produce a cleaner rehearsal set.

#### Rehearsal Training Objective

Once the rehearsal dataset `D_R` is constructed (the union of top-`k` selections from each cluster, shuffled), it is used to augment the standard PPO objective. The standard PPO objective from Equation 2 is:

$$\mathcal{L}_{PPO} = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim D_{PPO}, y \sim \pi_\phi(x)} \left[ r_\theta(x, y) - \beta \log\left(\frac{\pi_\phi(y|x)}{\pi_{init}(y|x)}\right) \right]$$

where `r_θ(x, y)` is the reward score (replaced by advantage score `a_θ(x, y)` when using the Advantage Model), `π_ϕ` is the current policy, `π_init` is the frozen initial policy (the SFT model), and `β` is the KL-divergence coefficient. The first term encourages the policy to produce high-reward responses; the second term penalizes the policy for diverging too far from its initial distribution.

Selective Rehearsal adds a supervised negative log-likelihood (NLL) term on the rehearsal data:

$$\mathcal{L}_{PPO-SR} = \mathcal{L}_{PPO} + \gamma \cdot \mathbb{E}_{(x, y) \sim D_R} \left[ \sum_{t=1}^{|y|} \pi_\phi(y_t | y_{<t}, x) \right]$$

where `γ` is a coefficient balancing the rehearsal loss against the PPO loss, `D_R` is the rehearsal dataset, and the inner sum is the standard autoregressive NLL loss: for each token position `t` in the response `y`, the model predicts `y_t` given the prompt `x` and all previous response tokens `y_{<t}`, and the loss penalizes incorrect predictions. The paper sets `γ = 0.01` empirically.

**What it computes:** The total loss `L_PPO-SR` is a weighted sum of two objectives. The PPO term `L_PPO` pushes the policy toward higher-advantage responses through reinforcement learning—the policy explores, generates responses, receives advantage scores from the frozen Advantage Model, and is updated to increase the probability of high-advantage responses. The rehearsal term adds supervised learning pressure: on the selected rehearsal examples, the model is trained to exactly reproduce the responses it (or its ancestor) generated earlier. This is a **conservation force**—it resists changes to the model's behavior on the rehearsed skills, because deviating from the rehearsal responses incurs an NLL penalty.

**Why this form:** The NLL loss on `D_R` is a particularly strong form of knowledge preservation because it directly penalizes the model for changing its conditional probability distribution on the rehearsed prompts. If the PPO update would cause the model to assign lower probability to tokens in the rehearsal responses, the NLL term counteracts that pressure. This is more targeted than simply increasing the KL penalty `β` in the PPO objective: the KL penalty resists all distributional changes equally (it penalizes any deviation from `π_init`), while Selective Rehearsal concentrates preservation pressure on the specific skills represented in `D_R`. This allows the model to change significantly on non-rehearsed prompts (where improvement might be needed) while remaining stable on rehearsed prompts (where the SFT model was already good).

The coefficient `γ = 0.01` means the rehearsal loss is weighted at only 1% of the PPO loss. This is a remarkably small weight, yet it produces substantial improvements in forget set retention (Table 2: AM-PPO-SR reduces forget set lose rate from 8.44% to 7.95% compared to AM-PPO, and from 29.28% compared to RM-PPO). The small weight is appropriate because the rehearsal loss is applied to a much smaller, carefully curated dataset, while the PPO loss applies to the full training set. A larger `γ` would risk overfitting to the rehearsal examples, causing the model to memorize rather than generalize, or would overwhelm the PPO signal entirely, preventing any alignment improvement.

**Relationship to KL divergence:** The paper notes that Selective Rehearsal can be viewed "as a means of amplifying the weight of the KL-divergence term in PPO training for crucial instances and their related counterparts." The standard KL penalty `β · log(π_ϕ / π_init)` is a per-token penalty that increases when the new policy's probability differs from the initial policy's probability. The rehearsal NLL loss `−log π_ϕ(y_t | y_{<t}, x)` is structurally similar: when the model was already assigning high probability to `y_t` under `π_init`, maintaining that assignment under `π_ϕ` minimizes the NLL loss. But there is a crucial difference: the rehearsal loss only penalizes deviations from specific, selected responses, not from the entire initial distribution. This makes it a **sparse** version of the KL penalty—it preserves the model's behavior on the selected good responses while allowing the distribution to change for other possible completions of the same prompt.

#### Integration with PPO Training

The paper's full training pipeline integrates both stabilization mechanisms into a standard RLHF workflow:

1. **SFT stage (pre-existing):** A BLOOMZ176B model is fine-tuned on proprietary aligned data to produce the initial policy `π_init`. This stage is not described in detail because it is "not the focus of this paper."

2. **Advantage Model training (new):** A BLOOMZ7B model with a value head is fine-tuned on the preference comparison dataset using the `L_AM` objective (Equation 6). Training hyperparameters: learning rate `5 × 10^{-6}`, warm-up over the first 10% of steps, cosine learning rate decay to 10% of the initial rate. For English (HH-RLHF): global batch size 180. For Chinese (proprietary): global batch size 480. The training epoch is fixed at 1 because "overfitting issue is observed in general after models are trained for one epoch." The trained Advantage Model is frozen and used only for scoring during PPO.

3. **PPO training with Selective Rehearsal:** The actor model (BLOOMZ176B, initialized from the SFT checkpoint) is optimized using the augmented PPO objective `L_PPO-SR` (Equation 9). PPO hyperparameters: learning rate `5 × 10^{-7}`, global batch size 256, 100 training steps for all experiments. The Advantage Model provides advantage scores `a_θ(x, y)` in place of raw rewards in the PPO objective. The rehearsal dataset `D_R` is constructed once (or potentially periodically—the paper does not specify whether it is updated during training) using the clustering and selection procedure described above.

The paper compares three variants to isolate the contribution of each mechanism:
- **RM-PPO:** Standard reward model scores, no Selective Rehearsal. This is the baseline that exhibits reward hacking and catastrophic forgetting.
- **AM-PPO:** Advantage Model scores, no Selective Rehearsal. This tests whether fixing the reward signal alone is sufficient.
- **AM-PPO-SR:** Advantage Model scores with Selective Rehearsal. This is the full proposed system.

The results in Table 2 and Figure 5 show progressive improvement: AM-PPO outperforms RM-PPO on all metrics, and AM-PPO-SR further improves on both main test set win rate and forget set retention. The paper also mentions an "RM-PPO w/ MA" variant (with moving average score normalization) that "encounters instabilities during PPO training," confirming that post-hoc normalization does not substitute for the Advantage Model's training-time calibration.

#### Design Choices and Their Justifications

**Why Advantage Model instead of score normalization during PPO?** Post-hoc normalization (e.g., z-scoring rewards across the batch or using a moving average baseline) can rescale scores to have zero mean and unit variance, but it cannot fix the fundamental problem that different prompt categories have different inherent reward baselines. If Code Generation prompts consistently receive raw scores of 5–10 while QA prompts receive 0–2, z-scoring within a batch containing only QA prompts will inflate small differences into large normalized scores, while z-scoring a batch containing both categories will compress the Code Generation advantage and amplify the QA advantage. Either way, the policy receives inconsistent signals. The Advantage Model removes the category-dependent baseline at the model level, so the policy always sees advantages relative to what's expected for each specific prompt.

**Why clustering with KMeans and SimCSE embeddings?** The paper needs a way to group prompts by required skill without having explicit skill labels. SimCSE embeddings capture semantic similarity, so prompts about similar topics or requiring similar reasoning patterns will cluster together. KMeans is simple, fast, and robust—the paper shows performance is stable across cluster counts. An alternative would be to use the task spectrum labels that the annotators used when creating the dataset (the paper mentions a "task spectrum" in Section 4.1), but this would require that such labels exist and are reliable. The unsupervised approach works even when task labels are unavailable or noisy.

**Why top-k by advantage score within each cluster?** Within a cluster of similar prompts, the advantage score reflects how much better a response is than expected for that type of prompt. High-advantage responses are those where the model genuinely outperformed its own baseline—these are the "expert-aligned examples" worth preserving. The alternative of selecting random examples within each cluster would preserve diversity but not quality; selecting the lowest-advantage examples would rehearse mistakes. The paper does not ablate the within-cluster selection criterion, so the contribution of "top-k by advantage" versus "random within cluster" is unknown.

**Why γ = 0.01 for the rehearsal loss weight?** The paper does not provide an ablation over γ, but the choice likely reflects a balance: large enough to exert meaningful preservation pressure, small enough to not dominate the PPO objective or cause overfitting. Since the rehearsal dataset `D_R` is much smaller than the full PPO dataset (it contains only top-k examples from each cluster), each rehearsal example contributes more gradient per example, and a small γ still produces a non-trivial total gradient contribution.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Diagnosing Reward Hacking as a *Training-Time Distributional Problem*, Not Just an Optimization Phenomenon

The paper's most conceptually distinctive move is reframing reward hacking from a policy optimization failure to a **reward model calibration failure with structural, measurable causes**. This is not merely semantic—it changes where practitioners should direct their efforts to fix RLHF instability.

Prior to this work, the standard narrative around reward hacking treated it as an RL problem: the policy is too clever, the optimization is too aggressive, or the KL penalty is insufficient. The recommended fixes were accordingly optimization-side: stronger KL regularization, earlier stopping, reward normalization during PPO training, or switching to offline methods that avoid RL entirely (the motivation behind DPO, RRHF, and related work). This framing implicitly assumes the reward model is fundamentally sound and the problem lies in how the policy exploits it.

The paper's diagnostic contribution is to show that this framing is incomplete—and in important ways, backwards. By decomposing the reward model's behavior across task categories (Figures 1a, 4a), the authors reveal that **the reward model itself produces systematically different score distributions for different types of content**, with some task categories enjoying much higher baseline scores than others even when response quality is held constant. The reward hacking that manifests during PPO training is not primarily the policy discovering arcane exploits—it is the policy rationally responding to a reward signal that already encodes perverse incentives: all else equal, producing a Code Generation-style response yields a higher absolute score than producing a QA-style response, because the reward model learned that Code Generation responses live in a higher-scoring regime. The policy is not "hacking" in the sense of finding an unintended loophole; it is faithfully optimizing a fundamentally miscalibrated objective.

This reframing has practical consequences that the paper partially realizes. If reward hacking is an optimization-side problem, the solution is to constrain the optimizer (stronger KL, less aggressive PPO). If it is a reward-model calibration problem, the solution is to fix the reward model itself—which is exactly what the Advantage Model does, baking score normalization into the training objective rather than applying it as a post-hoc bandage. The paper's finding that moving average normalization during PPO ("RM-PPO w/ MA") still "encounters instabilities" (Section 4.3) is telling: normalization can rescale scores but cannot undo the fact that different prompt categories have different inherent reward baselines, because those baselines are learned during reward model training and reflect genuine (if undesirable) properties of the training data distribution.

This diagnostic insight is both specific—it identifies the pairwise ranking loss (Equation 1) as the structural cause, since that loss constrains only relative ordering and not absolute score magnitudes—and general: any reward model trained purely with ranking objectives on heterogeneous preference data will likely exhibit similar distributional artifacts. The paper thus contributes a **new lens for evaluating reward models**: beyond ranking accuracy, practitioners should examine score distribution calibration across task categories, because distributional inconsistency predicts downstream training instability even when ranking accuracy is high.

The evidence for this diagnostic is most concentrated in Figures 1a and 4. Figure 1a shows the raw distributional disparity (Code Generation vs. QA score distributions are visibly shifted). Figure 4a quantifies the problem across the full task spectrum: "markedly different means for each task in the case of RM." Figure 4b then shows the Advantage Model's correction—standard deviations operating at a "stable scale" across tasks. The downstream effects appear in Figure 5a, where AM-PPO maintains stable delta rewards while RM-PPO exhibits the classic reward hacking signature (rising reward scores, declining GPT-4 win rate).

### Innovation 2: The Bounding Loss as a Soft Calibration Constraint—A Departure from Pure Ranking Objectives

The Advantage Model's training objective (Equation 6) introduces a technical innovation that is easy to overlook but conceptually significant: the **bounding loss terms** that enforce score calibration through soft sigmoidal barriers rather than hard constraints or post-hoc normalization. This represents a departure from the dominant paradigm in reward modeling, where the objective is exclusively focused on ranking accuracy and calibration is either ignored or addressed through separate calibration procedures.

Standard reward models trained with Equation 1 optimize a single goal: maximize the probability (under the Bradley-Terry model) that the chosen response outranks the rejected response. This is a pure relative objective—the absolute score values are irrelevant as long as `r(x, y_c) - r(x, y_r)` is sufficiently positive. The consequence, as the paper demonstrates, is that the model can (and does) learn arbitrary score scales, with some task categories compressed into narrow low-variance bands and others spread across wide high-variance ranges. This is not a bug in the optimization—it is the expected behavior of an objective that provides no signal about absolute score magnitudes.

The standard approaches to address this are **post-hoc**: apply z-score normalization to rewards during PPO training, use a running mean and variance estimate, or clip rewards to a fixed range. These methods treat calibration as a downstream processing step, applied after the reward model has already learned its (potentially pathological) score distribution. They are fragile because they operate on statistics estimated from finite batches, which can be noisy, non-stationary, or unrepresentative—exactly the failure the paper observes with "RM-PPO w/ MA."

The Advantage Model's bounding loss terms `log(σ(m(x) − a(x, y_c)))` and `log(σ(m(x) + a(x, y_r)))` bake calibration directly into the training objective. The key insight is the **soft-barrier behavior**: when advantages are well within the `[−m, m]` range, these terms contribute negligible loss and zero gradient, allowing the model to freely differentiate between quality levels. Only when advantages approach the margin do the terms activate, creating a smooth repulsive force that prevents scores from escaping the bounded interval. This is fundamentally different from a hard constraint (which would cause gradient discontinuities and optimization difficulties) or an L2 penalty on score magnitude (which would penalize even reasonable score differences, collapsing the model's ability to distinguish good from excellent responses).

The soft-barrier design means the bounding loss is **non-intrusive for the typical case**—most training examples involve responses that are neither exceptionally good nor exceptionally bad relative to their prompt's expected reward, so their advantages naturally fall within the margin and the bounding loss is dormant. It only activates for outliers, which are precisely the cases that cause reward hacking (responses receiving anomalously high absolute scores due to distributional artifacts). This is an elegant solution to the tension between wanting calibrated scores (which requires constraining magnitudes) and wanting discriminative power (which requires allowing magnitude differences): let the model freely differentiate within a calibrated range, and only intervene at the boundaries.

The conceptual contribution is demonstrating that **ranking and calibration can be jointly optimized** in a single loss function, without sacrificing ranking accuracy (AM achieves slightly higher accuracy than RM: 69.43% vs. 69.25% on HH-RLHF, 75.28% vs. 74.75% on proprietary data) while dramatically improving calibration (ECE drops from 4.70 to 3.48 on HH-RLHF, from 5.35 to 3.83 on proprietary data, per Table 1). This challenges the implicit assumption that calibration is a separate concern from ranking—that a model must choose between being a good ranker and being well-calibrated. The paper shows these properties are complementary when the training objective is properly designed.

The choice of `m(x) = 2.5` is admittedly unprincipled—the paper describes it as "speculative" and leaves adaptive margins to future work. But the fact that a fixed, hand-chosen margin produces such substantial calibration improvements across two datasets in different languages suggests the approach is robust to the exact margin value, at least within a reasonable range. This is practically important: practitioners can adopt the technique without needing to carefully tune `m(x)` for each new dataset.

### Innovation 3: Reframing Catastrophic Forgetting as a *Coverage* Problem in PPO Data Selection

Selective Rehearsal's intellectual contribution is not the rehearsal mechanism itself—mixing supervised losses with RL objectives has a long history in continual learning and RL—but rather the **diagnostic reframing that catastrophic forgetting during RLHF is caused by undifferentiated optimization pressure across heterogeneous skills, and that the solution requires diversity-aware data selection, not just quality-aware selection**.

The standard response to catastrophic forgetting in RLHF has been to increase the KL penalty coefficient `β` in the PPO objective (Equation 2). This uniformly constrains the policy to remain close to its initial distribution, resisting all changes equally regardless of whether those changes represent genuine alignment improvement or destructive forgetting. The limitation is clear: a stronger KL penalty simultaneously prevents forgetting bad behavior and prevents learning good behavior. It is a blunt instrument that trades off alignment progress against stability, with no ability to distinguish examples where the SFT model was already good (preserve) from examples where improvement is needed (allow change).

Selective Rehearsal breaks this tradeoff by making preservation **targeted rather than uniform**. The key insight is that not all SFT-era capabilities need equal protection. Examples where the model already performs well—identified by high advantage scores within their skill cluster—should be explicitly rehearsed, while examples where the model performs poorly can be freely optimized by PPO without preservation constraints. This converts the problem from "how much should I constrain the policy globally?" (tuning `β`) to "which specific skills should I protect, and which should I allow to change?"

What makes this framing novel is the **coverage requirement** enforced by clustering. Prior approaches to data selection for knowledge preservation (e.g., experience replay in continual RL, or rejection sampling in LLM alignment) select examples based purely on some quality metric—high reward, low loss, high confidence. This can backfire when quality is correlated with task category, as the paper demonstrates it is (Figure 1a: different tasks have different reward baselines). Pure quality-based selection would disproportionately select examples from high-baseline task categories, rehearsing those skills extensively while neglecting lower-baseline categories—exactly the catastrophic forgetting pattern the technique is supposed to prevent.

The clustering step before scoring ensures that the rehearsal set **covers the skill space uniformly** (or at least proportionally to the number of clusters), with quality selection applied within each cluster rather than across the entire dataset. This is a two-stage selection criterion: first ensure diversity (what skills are represented), then ensure quality (which examples best exemplify each skill). The paper explicitly contrasts this with rejection sampling and reinforced self-training, which "only consider reward model score" and thus fail to maintain diversity (Section 3.2).

The conceptual significance extends beyond this specific implementation. It suggests that **data selection for RLHF should be skill-aware**, not just score-aware. The paper mentions that rehearsal data "could come from other important data sources representing specific skills (e.g. math-problem solving) the main policy are not expected to forget" (Section 3.2). This opens the door to explicitly curated rehearsal sets that protect specific capabilities—mathematical reasoning, factual accuracy, safety guardrails—regardless of whether those capabilities naturally score highly on the reward model. In principle, a practitioner could identify critical skills that must not degrade during alignment and construct a rehearsal set specifically targeting those skills, using the clustering mechanism to ensure coverage and the advantage scoring mechanism to ensure quality.

The empirical support is in Table 2: Selective Rehearsal reduces the forget set lose rate from 8.44% (AM-PPO, no rehearsal) to 7.95% (AM-PPO-SR, with rehearsal), and from 29.28% (RM-PPO) to 7.95%. The dramatic drop from RM-PPO to AM-PPO (29.28% to 8.44%) shows that fixing the reward signal alone already substantially reduces forgetting—the Advantage Model's calibrated scores don't push the policy to overwrite good behavior in pursuit of spurious high rewards. But Selective Rehearsal provides an additional improvement beyond that, confirming that even with a well-calibrated reward signal, explicit rehearsal helps preserve capabilities that PPO optimization might otherwise erode.

The robustness of the clustering approach to the number of clusters `c` (Figure 6: "variance of approximately 0.05 points in test-set rewards across various cluster numbers `c`") is a practically significant finding. It means the technique does not require careful hyperparameter tuning of the cluster count—a practitioner can choose a reasonable default and expect stable performance. This robustness likely stems from the fact that the KMeans clustering on SimCSE embeddings produces semantically coherent groupings across a wide range of `c`, so as long as `c` is large enough to capture the major skill categories in the data, the exact number does not matter much.

### Innovation 4: Demonstrating That Fixing the Reward Model Is a Sufficient Condition for PPO Stability—Without Abandoning Online RL

The paper makes an implicit but important architectural argument: **the instability of PPO-based RLHF is not inherent to online RL; it is a consequence of a specific, fixable flaw in the reward model.** This cuts against a growing narrative in the alignment literature that PPO is fundamentally too unstable for practical use and should be replaced by offline methods.

The context matters here. At the time of writing (late 2023), the field was increasingly turning toward offline alignment techniques that avoid RL entirely: Direct Preference Optimization (DPO; Rafailov et al., 2023), Preference Ranking Optimization (PRO; Song et al., 2023), RRHF (Yuan et al., 2023), and RAFT (Dong et al., 2023). These methods were motivated in part by the difficulty of making PPO work reliably—the very instabilities this paper addresses. The implicit argument was: PPO is too finicky, reward models are too hard to train, and online sampling is computationally expensive, so let us find ways to align models from static preference data without RL.

The paper does not engage with this argument directly—it does not compare against DPO or other offline methods—but its results constitute an empirical counterargument. By showing that two targeted fixes to the reward model and training data selection can produce stable PPO training with monotonic improvements (Figure 5a shows AM-PPO maintaining stable delta rewards throughout training, while RM-PPO diverges), the paper demonstrates that the failure modes of PPO-based RLHF are **contingent, not fundamental**. The instability is a property of the specific implementation (uncalibrated reward model, undifferentiated training data) rather than a property of online RL itself.

This is a negative result in the best sense: it tells us *what is not the problem*. PPO's exploration and online sampling are not themselves the source of instability—at least not the catastrophic instabilities that cause reward hacking and forgetting. The source is the reward signal quality and the training data composition. Fix those, and PPO works. This matters because online RL has theoretical advantages over offline methods—it can explore the response space, discover novel high-quality outputs not present in the static preference data, and adapt to distribution shift as the policy improves. If PPO can be made stable, these advantages can be realized without the reliability costs that drove interest in offline alternatives.

The evidence is in the progression from RM-PPO to AM-PPO to AM-PPO-SR (Table 2, Figure 5). RM-PPO exhibits both failure modes: reward hacking (rising RM scores but declining GPT-4 win rate in Figure 5b) and catastrophic forgetting (29.28% lose rate on the forget set). AM-PPO, which differs only in using the Advantage Model instead of a standard reward model, dramatically reduces both: the GPT-4 win rate becomes stable (Figure 5b), and the forget set lose rate drops to 8.44%. AM-PPO-SR further improves to 7.95% lose rate and 15.78% main test set win rate (vs. 14.87% for AM-PPO and 12.72% for RM-PPO). The key observation is that **AM-PPO alone already stabilizes training**—Selective Rehearsal provides additional gains, but the qualitative shift from unstable to stable occurs with the Advantage Model.

This has implications for how the field should allocate research effort. If PPO instability were fundamental, the rational response would be to invest heavily in offline alternatives and treat PPO as a dead end. If it is contingent on fixable problems, the rational response is to invest in better reward models and smarter data curation, preserving the exploration benefits of online RL while eliminating the reliability costs. The paper's results, while limited to BLOOMZ models and two datasets, provide evidence for the latter view.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The paper uses two preference datasets for Advantage Model training. For English, the **HH-RLHF dataset** (Bai et al., 2022a; Ganguli et al., 2022) comprises 118k helpful and 42k harmless training examples, with 8.5k test examples. The paper does not train separate reward models for helpful vs. harmless (as many prior studies do), instead merging them into a single training set. For Chinese, a **proprietary dataset** is collected with "quantities similar to those used in LLaMA 2" (Touvron et al., 2023), where annotators generate prompts from a task spectrum, sample five responses from the same SFT model using varied sampling hyperparameters, and have five annotators rank responses based on helpfulness and harmlessness criteria. The PPO dataset is sampled from two domain-general datasets: **COIG** and **Firefly**, yielding 64,364 training prompts and 2,623 test prompts with no intersection. Additionally, 1,704 examples from the SFT test data form a **forget test set** to evaluate knowledge retention.

- **Base model(s).** The paper uses the **BLOOMZ** family (Muennighoff et al., 2022): **BLOOMZ7B** for reward/advantage modeling and **BLOOMZ176B** for SFT and RLHF training. The choice of BLOOMZ is not explicitly justified beyond serving as the backbone for all experiments. The scale asymmetry (7B reward model scoring 176B actor) follows standard RLHF practice where the reward model is substantially smaller than the policy being optimized.

- **Metrics.** Four primary metrics are reported. **Accuracy** measures pairwise ranking correctness: the fraction of test pairs where the model assigns a higher score to the human-preferred response. **Expected Calibration Error (ECE)** quantifies how well the model's score differences predict actual preference probabilities—lower is better, indicating scores that reflect true likelihood of preference rather than overconfident or underconfident predictions. **Reward score** (or delta reward) during PPO training tracks the advantage or reward signal over training steps to detect reward hacking (rising scores without corresponding quality improvement). **Win rate** against the SFT model is evaluated by GPT-4: for each prompt, GPT-4 compares the PPO model's response and the SFT model's response, declaring a win, loss, or tie. Win rate is reported on both the main test set and the forget test set.

- **Baselines and variants.** The paper compares three main configurations. **RM-PPO**: standard reward model trained with pairwise ranking loss (Equation 1), used to score PPO training. This is the baseline exhibiting reward hacking and catastrophic forgetting. An additional variant **RM-PPO w/ MA** applies moving average score normalization during PPO training but is described qualitatively as still encountering instabilities. **AM-PPO**: Advantage Model (Equations 5–8) replaces the standard reward model; no Selective Rehearsal. **AM-PPO-SR**: Advantage Model plus Selective Rehearsal (Equation 9), the full proposed system. For Advantage Model evaluation, the paper also compares against **OpenAssistant** (Köpf et al., 2023), a publicly available reward model using DeBERTa (He et al., 2020) trained on the HH-RLHF dataset. For PPO results, the SFT model serves as the comparison point for win rate evaluation (both models given the same prompt, GPT-4 judges preference).

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper does not measure compute in FLOPs or wall-clock time. Instead, PPO training is controlled by a fixed number of steps: **100 training steps** for all PPO experiments, with a global batch size of 256. This means each PPO variant processes the same number of training examples, making comparisons fair in terms of optimization steps. The Advantage Model is trained for **1 epoch** across all experiments to avoid overfitting. The Selective Rehearsal mechanism uses `c` clusters and top-`k` selection within each cluster, but the value of `k` is not specified, and the rehearsal loss is weighted by a small fixed coefficient `γ = 0.01`, meaning the additional compute overhead from rehearsal is minimal relative to the PPO forward/backward passes.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper reports no cross-validation, statistical significance testing, confidence intervals, or multiple random seeds. Results in tables and figures are presented as point estimates without error bars or variance measures. For Selective Rehearsal, Figure 6 shows reward variance across different cluster counts `c` (approximately 0.05 points in test-set reward), providing the only indication of stability to hyperparameter variation. The test set sizes (8.5k for HH-RLHF reward model evaluation, 2,623 for PPO testing, 1,704 for forget testing) are moderate, and the absence of statistical reporting means observed differences of 1–2 percentage points in win rate (e.g., AM-PPO-SR vs. AM-PPO in Table 2) cannot be assessed for reliability.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Advantage Model Evaluation: Ranking Accuracy and Calibration

Table 1 reports the headline Advantage Model results on reward modeling benchmarks. On the **HH-RLHF dataset**, the Advantage Model achieves **69.43% accuracy** compared to **69.25% for the standard Reward Model** and **69.24% for OpenAssistant**—a marginal improvement in ranking. The more substantial gain is in calibration: the Advantage Model achieves **ECE of 3.48**, substantially lower than the Reward Model's **4.70**—a 26% relative reduction in miscalibration. On the **proprietary Chinese dataset**, the Advantage Model achieves **75.28% accuracy** (vs. 74.75% for RM) and **ECE of 3.83** (vs. 5.35 for RM)—a 28% relative reduction in ECE. The authors note that "maximizing accuracy is not the exclusive objective in AM optimization. The aim also extends to reducing ECE to improve reliability, whilst sustaining or improving the level of ranking accuracy compared with RM."

Figure 2 visualizes calibration quality by plotting ranking accuracy as a function of score differences assigned to pairs. The orange diagonal line represents perfect calibration: the probability a response pair is correctly ranked should equal `1 / (1 + e^{−Δ})` where `Δ` is the score difference. The Reward Model's empirical curve (blue) sits consistently above the orange line across most `Δ` values on both datasets, indicating **systematic overconfidence**—the model assigns higher probabilities to correct rankings than its scores actually warrant. The Advantage Model's curve (green) tracks the orange calibration line more closely, with visibly smaller deviation across the range of score differences. The paper states that "AM exhibits significantly lower ECE and is better calibrated than RM on both datasets, whereas RM tends to be overconfident in most cases."

Figure 3 shows the **distribution of scores** for chosen ("good") and rejected ("bad") response pairs from the proprietary data. Both RM and AM assign higher scores to good examples on average, but the distributions differ qualitatively. For the RM (Figure 3a), the good-example distribution is broad and substantially overlaps with the bad-example distribution. For the AM (Figure 3b), the separation is cleaner—the good-example distribution is more concentrated at positive values while the bad-example distribution centers near zero or negative values, with less overlap between the two. The paper describes this as AM exhibiting "a more distinct distribution pattern."

Figure 4 decomposes the **mean and standard deviation of scores across tasks** categorized by the task spectrum in the proprietary data. For the standard RM (Figure 4a, blue bars), the mean reward score varies dramatically across tasks—some task categories average scores near 1.0 while others average near 0.2 or even negative values. The authors describe these as "markedly different means for each task in the case of RM." For the AM (Figure 4a, green bars), the task-level means are compressed into a narrower range centered near zero, consistent with the advantage formulation subtracting the expected baseline. Figure 4b shows standard deviations: the RM exhibits widely varying standard deviations across tasks, while the AM shows "consistently operating at a stable scale" with task-level standard deviations clustered in a narrow band. This is the paper's direct evidence that the Advantage Model achieves its stated goal of balancing score distributions across categories to prevent the perverse incentives that cause reward hacking.

#### PPO Training Stability and Performance

Figure 5 presents the core PPO training results through two complementary views. Figure 5a shows **learning curves on delta rewards** (the reward signal during PPO) for RM-PPO, AM-PPO, and AM-PPO-SR. RM-PPO exhibits a characteristic pattern: delta rewards increase during training, but the paper notes this is accompanied by "a drop in win rate evaluated by GPT-4... despite a rise in RM scores"—the classic signature of reward hacking where the policy learns to maximize the (miscalibrated) reward without improving actual response quality. AM-PPO's delta reward curve is flatter and more stable, suggesting the policy is not exploiting the advantage signal. AM-PPO-SR tracks closely to AM-PPO in delta rewards, consistent with Selective Rehearsal being an additional supervised loss that does not directly affect the reward signal.

Figure 5b shows the **win/loss rate over the SFT model evaluated by GPT-4** on the main test set. RM-PPO suffers a degradation in win rate during training (the paper states "RM-PPO faces significant reward hacking issues, witnessed by a drop in win rate evaluated by GPT-4"), while AM-PPO and AM-PPO-SR maintain or improve their win rates throughout the 100 training steps. The paper highlights that "AM-PPO exhibits resistance to such problems, maintaining stable GPT-4 outcomes." The AM-PPO-SR curve trends slightly higher than AM-PPO, consistent with the additive benefit of rehearsal.

Table 2 provides the **numerical win/loss/tie breakdown** over the SFT model at the end of training, separately for the main test set and the forget test set. On the **main test set**, RM-PPO achieves 12.72% win / 12.62% lose / 74.66% tie—a roughly even split between wins and losses, indicating no net improvement over SFT. AM-PPO improves to 14.87% win / 10.38% lose / 74.74% tie—a larger win-loss margin (wins exceed losses by 4.49 percentage points vs. 0.10 for RM-PPO). AM-PPO-SR further improves to 15.78% win / 9.77% lose / 74.45% tie—wins exceed losses by 6.01 percentage points.

On the **forget test set**, the differences are starker. RM-PPO achieves 16.87% win / 29.28% lose / 53.84% tie—losses substantially exceed wins, quantifying the catastrophic forgetting problem: the PPO model is significantly more likely to produce worse responses than the SFT model on examples the SFT model already handled well. AM-PPO dramatically reverses this: 9.70% win / 8.44% lose / 81.86% tie—losses drop from 29.28% to 8.44%, a reduction of over 20 percentage points, and the model is now roughly equally likely to win or lose. AM-PPO-SR produces 10.30% win / 7.95% lose / 81.75% tie—a further small reduction in loss rate. The paper notes: "RM-PPO's substantial susceptibility to catastrophic forgetting, portraying a noticeable performance drop. In contrast, AM-PPO is stable, avoiding significant drops and showcasing stability. Incorporating selective rehearsal, the AM-PPO-SR variant demonstrate an uplifted win rate on both sets, underscoring the role of selective rehearsal in alleviating catastrophic forgetting and enhancing model efficacy."

#### Selective Rehearsal Sensitivity

Figure 6 examines the **influence of the number of clusters `c`** on Selective Rehearsal performance. The figure plots test-set reward for AM-PPO-SR at different values of `c`. The paper reports "a relatively consistent variance of approximately 0.05 points in test-set rewards across various cluster numbers `c`." The curves are nearly flat, indicating that Selective Rehearsal's effectiveness is robust to this hyperparameter choice. The authors recommend nonetheless "conducting a thorough analysis of this aspect when applying selective rehearsal to different datasets, as domain-specific variations can have a notable impact."

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

The paper's ablation structure is implicit rather than explicit—there is no dedicated ablation section. Instead, the progressive comparison RM-PPO → AM-PPO → AM-PPO-SR serves as a de facto ablation of the two proposed components.

**Ablation of Advantage Model (AM-PPO vs. RM-PPO)**: Comparing these two conditions isolates the effect of replacing the standard reward model with the Advantage Model while keeping all other PPO hyperparameters identical. On the main test set, AM-PPO achieves 14.87% win rate vs. 12.72% for RM-PPO (Table 2), a 2.15 percentage point absolute improvement. On the forget test set, AM-PPO achieves 8.44% lose rate vs. 29.28% for RM-PPO (Table 2), a 20.84 percentage point reduction in forgetting. This is the single largest effect in the paper, indicating that reward signal quality is the dominant factor in both reward hacking and catastrophic forgetting. Figure 5b qualitatively confirms that AM-PPO's GPT-4 win rate remains stable during training while RM-PPO's degrades.

**Ablation of Selective Rehearsal (AM-PPO-SR vs. AM-PPO)**: Comparing these two conditions isolates the effect of adding the clustering-based rehearsal mechanism on top of the Advantage Model. On the main test set, AM-PPO-SR achieves 15.78% win rate vs. 14.87% for AM-PPO (Table 2), a 0.91 percentage point improvement. On the forget test set, AM-PPO-SR achieves 7.95% lose rate vs. 8.44% for AM-PPO, a 0.49 percentage point reduction. The improvements are directionally consistent but small in absolute magnitude, suggesting that the Advantage Model alone already captures most of the forgetting reduction, with Selective Rehearsal providing an incremental benefit. The paper does not report whether these differences are statistically significant.

**Ablation of moving average normalization (RM-PPO w/ MA vs. RM-PPO)**: This is described qualitatively in Section 4.3 but not reported with specific numbers in any table or figure. The paper states: "Despite utilizing moving average for score normalization, RM-PPO w/ MA encounters instabilities during PPO training." This serves as evidence that post-hoc normalization does not substitute for the Advantage Model's training-time calibration, but the absence of quantitative results makes the strength of this claim difficult to evaluate. No win rate, forget set performance, or reward curve is provided for RM-PPO w/ MA.

**PRM aggregation strategy**: Not applicable—this paper does not use process reward models or step-level scoring.

**Reward model calibration across methods**: Figure 2 provides the calibration comparison between RM and AM, but does not include OpenAssistant in the calibration plot despite reporting its accuracy in Table 1 (69.24%). The ECE for OpenAssistant is not reported, so it is unknown whether OpenAssistant's calibration is better or worse than the standard RM—it could already be well-calibrated, which would weaken the argument that the Advantage Model's calibration improvement is uniquely valuable.

**Expected reward estimator design**: The paper provides no ablation over different choices for the expected reward estimator `e_τ(x)`, the hyperparameters `N` and `K` in Equation 8, or the margin `m(x)`. The contribution of each component in the advantage computation (learned estimator vs. historical policy samples vs. importance weighting) is unknown. The margin `m(x) = 2.5` is fixed throughout; no sensitivity analysis over margin values is reported.

**Within-cluster selection criterion for Selective Rehearsal**: The paper uses top-k by Advantage Model score within each cluster but does not compare against alternative selection criteria (random selection within cluster, lowest-entropy selection, longest/shortest response selection, or selection by human preference labels). The value of `k` is not specified, making it impossible to assess how much rehearsal data is used relative to the full PPO dataset. The rehearsal loss weight `γ = 0.01` is fixed; no ablation over `γ` is reported to determine whether a larger or smaller weight would improve or degrade performance.

**SimCSE embedding model choice**: The paper uses `princeton-nlp/sup-simcse-roberta-base` for prompt embeddings but does not compare against alternative embedding approaches (e.g., using the BLOOMZ model's own hidden representations, using different SimCSE variants, or using simple TF-IDF bag-of-words embeddings). The sensitivity of Selective Rehearsal to embedding quality is unexplored.

**Combined method ablation (AM only vs. SR only)**: The paper never evaluates Selective Rehearsal with a standard reward model (RM-PPO-SR). This would test whether rehearsal alone, without a calibrated reward signal, can mitigate forgetting. Without this condition, it is impossible to determine whether Selective Rehearsal's benefits are conditional on having a well-calibrated reward model for the selection step, or whether rehearsal would help even with the miscalibrated RM scores. The clustering step uses Advantage Model scores for within-cluster selection, so RM-PPO-SR would need an alternative selection criterion (e.g., RM score, entropy, random). This missing condition limits understanding of the interaction between the two proposed methods.

**Single model family and scale**: All experiments use BLOOMZ models (7B for reward, 176B for policy). The paper provides no evidence that the Advantage Model's calibration benefits transfer to other model families (e.g., LLaMA, GPT, PaLM) or other scales. The 7B/176B scale asymmetry for reward model vs. policy is fixed; whether a larger or smaller reward model would change the results is unknown. The paper's claims about general RLHF stabilization are supported only on BLOOMZ with this specific scale configuration.

**English vs. Chinese datasets**: The Chinese proprietary dataset is not publicly available (the paper notes the PPO training and testing query sets "could be shared upon request"), making independent replication of the Chinese results impossible without access to the same data. The annotation procedure is described in general terms (annotators generate prompts from a task spectrum, sample five responses, rank by helpfulness and harmlessness) but the specific prompts, response samples, and annotation guidelines are not provided.

**PPO training duration**: All PPO experiments train for exactly 100 steps. The paper does not investigate whether the stability benefits of AM and AM-PPO-SR persist over longer training horizons. If RM-PPO's instability manifests primarily after 100+ steps, the 100-step cutoff might understate the benefits of the proposed methods (if RM-PPO would diverge further while AM-PPO remains stable) or overstate them (if AM-PPO would eventually become unstable as well). The choice of 100 steps is stated without justification.

### Critical Assessment

#### Claim: The Advantage Model "balances reward score distributions across various categories" and prevents reward hacking

The experimental evidence for distributional balancing is direct and convincing. Figure 4a shows that the standard RM produces task-dependent means ("markedly different means for each task"), while AM compresses these means into a narrow range centered near zero. Figure 4b shows that AM produces consistent standard deviations across tasks while RM's standard deviations vary widely. These figures demonstrate exactly what the paper claims: the Advantage Model normalizes score scales at the task level.

However, what the experiments demonstrate and what they *do not* demonstrate are importantly different. The experiments show that AM produces more uniform score distributions across tasks and that AM-PPO avoids the reward hacking pattern (rising RM scores with falling GPT-4 win rate) that RM-PPO exhibits. But the causal chain—uniform score distributions → absence of reward hacking → higher win rates—is not directly tested. It is possible that AM-PPO is more stable for reasons unrelated to distributional balancing (e.g., the bounding loss might constrain gradient magnitudes independently of distributional effects, or the advantage transformation might reduce noise in the reward signal). The paper provides no experiment that isolates the distributional balancing mechanism from other properties of the Advantage Model.

Additionally, the paper does not evaluate whether alternative, simpler approaches to score normalization during PPO training could achieve similar stability. The "RM-PPO w/ MA" condition is described qualitatively as encountering instabilities but is not compared quantitatively to AM-PPO on win rate, forget set performance, or reward curves. Without this comparison, the claim that the Advantage Model's training-time calibration is necessary (rather than merely sufficient) for stability is unsubstantiated. A practitioner reading this paper cannot determine whether they should implement the full Advantage Model or whether per-batch z-scoring with a sufficiently large moving average window would suffice.

The calibration evidence (Figure 2, Table 1 ECE) is strong: AM is clearly better calibrated than RM, with ECE dropping from 4.70 to 3.48 on HH-RLHF and from 5.35 to 3.83 on proprietary data. But the paper does not establish that calibration improvement *causes* PPO stability improvement. These are separate evaluations—reward model calibration is measured on the preference test set, while PPO stability is measured during policy training. The correlation could be coincidental, or both could result from a common cause (e.g., the bounding loss) without one causing the other.

#### Claim: Selective Rehearsal "mitigates catastrophic forgetting by strategically selecting data for PPO training and knowledge rehearsing"

The forget set evidence supports a qualified version of this claim. RM-PPO exhibits severe forgetting: 29.28% lose rate vs. 16.87% win rate on the forget set (Table 2), meaning the PPO model is substantially more likely to produce worse responses than the SFT model on examples the SFT model handled well. AM-PPO dramatically reduces this: 8.44% lose rate, and AM-PPO-SR further reduces it to 7.95%.

The qualification is that the Advantage Model alone is responsible for the vast majority of the forgetting reduction—from 29.28% to 8.44%, a reduction of 20.84 percentage points. Selective Rehearsal contributes an additional 0.49 percentage points (8.44% to 7.95%). The paper's framing of Selective Rehearsal as a major contribution to forgetting mitigation is therefore somewhat misleading relative to the empirical magnitudes. The Advantage Model is the primary anti-forgetting mechanism; Selective Rehearsal provides a small incremental benefit on top.

Furthermore, the Selective Rehearsal mechanism as evaluated is confounded with the Advantage Model: the within-cluster selection step uses Advantage Model scores to identify the top-k examples for rehearsal. This means Selective Rehearsal's effectiveness depends on having a well-calibrated reward signal—if the scores were miscalibrated, the "top-k by advantage" selection might choose poor examples for rehearsal, potentially making forgetting worse. The paper does not test Selective Rehearsal with a standard (miscalibrated) reward model to determine whether rehearsal alone, without calibrated scores for selection, would help or hurt.

The claim that clustering ensures "coverage" of diverse skills is supported by Figure 6 (robustness to cluster count `c`), but the paper does not directly verify that the selected rehearsal examples are actually skill-diverse. No analysis of cluster composition, semantic coherence, or skill coverage is provided. The reader must trust that SimCSE embeddings + KMeans produce meaningful skill clusters, and that top-k selection within each cluster captures the intended skills. A qualitative analysis showing example prompts from different clusters would substantially strengthen this claim.

#### Claim: "AM-PPO exhibits resistance to such problems [reward hacking], maintaining stable GPT-4 outcomes"

Figure 5b supports this claim for the training duration tested (100 PPO steps). The AM-PPO and AM-PPO-SR curves show stable or slightly improving GPT-4 win rates throughout training, while RM-PPO's win rate degrades. However, the 100-step limit is an important caveat: the paper provides no evidence that AM-PPO's stability persists beyond this horizon. If RM-PPO's instability compounds over training (the win rate curve appears to be decreasing throughout the 100 steps), it is unclear whether AM-PPO would eventually exhibit similar degradation given enough training. The paper's claim of "stability" is therefore limited to the specific training budget tested.

Additionally, the win rate differences between methods are modest in absolute terms. On the main test set, AM-PPO achieves 14.87% win rate vs. 12.72% for RM-PPO—a difference of 2.15 percentage points (Table 2). With a test set of 2,623 prompts and no reported confidence intervals, this difference may not be statistically significant. The tie rate for both methods is very high (approximately 74.7%), meaning the vast majority of prompts produce responses where GPT-4 cannot distinguish quality differences between the PPO and SFT models. The paper does not report whether the win/loss differences are concentrated in specific task categories or distributed uniformly, which would help interpret the practical significance.

#### Claim: The proposed methods "achieve higher reward scores and win rates"

The "higher reward scores" claim is ambiguous. The Advantage Model is not designed to produce higher raw scores—it normalizes them. During PPO training, the paper reports delta rewards (Figure 5a), but higher delta rewards are not necessarily better if they come from reward hacking. The paper's own argument is that RM-PPO's rising delta rewards are a *problem*, not an achievement. The meaningful metric is win rate against SFT, and here the improvements, while directionally consistent, are small: from 12.72% (RM-PPO) to 14.87% (AM-PPO) to 15.78% (AM-PPO-SR) on the main test set (Table 2). These are single-digit percentage point gains on a base win rate that is already quite low, with tie rates dominating at approximately 74–75%. The "higher win rates" claim is technically correct but the magnitude of improvement is modest.

#### Missing experiments that would strengthen the paper

**Longer PPO training.** The 100-step cutoff leaves open the question of whether AM-PPO remains stable over extended training. A 500-step or 1000-step comparison would provide evidence about whether the Advantage Model prevents reward hacking permanently or merely delays it.

**Comparison against DPO or other offline methods.** The paper motivates RLHF stabilization as important because major production systems use PPO, but does not compare against offline alternatives that avoid PPO's instabilities entirely. A DPO baseline trained on the same preference data would contextualize whether stabilizing PPO produces better alignment than sidestepping PPO altogether.

**Statistical reporting.** The absence of confidence intervals, error bars, or significance tests makes it impossible to assess whether the reported differences are reliable or could be explained by random variation across PPO runs. Given the modest effect sizes (1–3 percentage point win rate differences), this is a significant omission.

**Replication across model families.** All results use BLOOMZ. Whether the Advantage Model's calibration benefits transfer to LLaMA, GPT, or other architectures with different pretraining distributions is unknown. The paper's claims are implicitly model-agnostic but empirically model-specific.

**Ablation of `m(x)` (the margin).** The margin `m(x) = 2.5` is central to the Advantage Model's bounding loss but is set without empirical justification. A sweep over margin values (e.g., 1.0, 2.5, 5.0, 10.0) would reveal whether calibration and PPO stability are sensitive to this choice or robust across a wide range.

**Selective Rehearsal with standard (uncalibrated) RM.** This condition (RM-PPO-SR) would test whether clustering-based rehearsal can mitigate forgetting even with a poor reward signal, or whether calibrated scores are a prerequisite for effective rehearsal example selection.

**Qualitative analysis of rehearsed examples.** The paper provides no examples of what Selective Rehearsal selects for rehearsal—what do the cluster centers look like, what types of prompts and responses are preserved? This makes the mechanism feel abstract and its practical behavior opaque.

#### Summary of what the experiments do and do not demonstrate

The experiments **demonstrate** that the Advantage Model produces more calibrated, less task-dependent score distributions than a standard reward model trained with pairwise ranking loss, and that using the Advantage Model during PPO training is associated with reduced reward hacking and reduced catastrophic forgetting on the BLOOMZ model family over 100 PPO steps. The experiments **do not demonstrate** that the distributional balancing mechanism is the causal factor in stability improvement (as opposed to other properties of the advantage transformation or bounding loss), that Selective Rehearsal provides benefits independent of the Advantage Model's calibrated scores, that the stability benefits persist beyond 100 training steps, or that the findings generalize beyond BLOOMZ models and the specific datasets tested. The effect sizes on win rate are directionally consistent but modest in absolute magnitude, and the absence of statistical reporting means the reliability of these differences is unassessed.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### Unaccounted Practical Costs of the Advantage Model and Selective Rehearsal

**The assumption or constraint:** The paper presents the Advantage Model and Selective Rehearsal as drop-in improvements to standard RLHF with minimal overhead. However, the headline results do not account for several practical costs that a deployment team would incur. Training the Advantage Model introduces additional architectural complexity: the expected reward estimator `e_τ(x)` must be parameterized and trained, the importance-weighting scheme in Equation 8 requires tracking `K` historical policy models and their output probabilities `π'_k(y|x)`, and the bounding loss introduces a new hyperparameter `m(x)` that the paper sets to a fixed 2.5 without principled justification. The paper acknowledges the `m(x)` choice is "speculative and requires further investigation" (Section 3.1). Selective Rehearsal adds further overhead: SimCSE embeddings must be computed for all PPO training prompts, KMeans clustering must be run (with the cluster count `c` potentially requiring tuning per dataset, despite the paper's finding that results are relatively robust), and within-cluster top-k selection must be performed. The rehearsal dataset construction pipeline is described but its computational cost relative to the PPO training loop is never quantified.

**The consequence:** The paper's efficiency claims—that AM-PPO and AM-PPO-SR achieve higher win rates than RM-PPO at the same number of PPO steps—are true in terms of optimization steps but do not reflect total wall-clock time or compute. A practitioner implementing these methods would face additional engineering complexity: the Advantage Model requires maintaining and querying historical policy checkpoints for importance-weight computation (Equation 8), which increases storage and inference costs. Selective Rehearsal requires an embedding, clustering, and selection pipeline that runs at least once per PPO training run (and potentially periodically if the rehearsal set is refreshed). The paper does not report whether these costs are negligible or substantial relative to the PPO forward/backward passes. Additionally, the fixed margin `m(x) = 2.5` may not be optimal across datasets—the paper provides no sensitivity analysis, so a practitioner cannot know whether their dataset requires a different margin value to achieve the reported calibration benefits.

**What evidence exists in the paper:** There is no quantification of any of these costs. The paper does not report training time, memory usage, or FLOP counts for Advantage Model training vs. standard reward model training. It does not report the time required to compute SimCSE embeddings, run KMeans, or construct the rehearsal dataset. The PPO training budget is fixed at 100 steps for all variants, and the paper reports only the results at the end of this budget—not the cost of reaching those results. The choice of `m(x) = 2.5` is stated without ablation or justification. The hyperparameters `N` and `K` in Equation 8 are mentioned but never specified or ablated.

**Mitigation status:** The paper partially acknowledges the margin limitation, stating that `m(x)` "may have a connection with the complexity or difficulty involved in learning the reward function for prompts similar to `x`" but that this is "speculative and requires further investigation." It leaves adaptive margins and "other strategies (e.g. combining advantage score with entropy score)" for within-cluster selection to future work (Section 3.2). The practical costs of implementation are not discussed as a limitation, and no guidance is provided for practitioners on how to weigh these costs against the reported accuracy and stability gains.

### No Evidence of Generalization Beyond BLOOMZ Models

**The assumption or constraint:** All experimental results—both Advantage Model evaluation and PPO training—use the BLOOMZ model family: BLOOMZ7B for reward/advantage modeling and BLOOMZ176B for the policy. The paper states this explicitly (Section 4.1) but provides no justification for why these findings should transfer to other model families (e.g., LLaMA, GPT, PaLM, Falcon) or other scales. The BLOOMZ models are multitask-fine-tuned variants of the BLOOM multilingual pretrained models, which have different pretraining data composition, tokenization, and architectural details compared to the dominant model families used in production RLHF pipelines. The paper's claims are stated in general terms—"our proposed methods not only increase stability in RLHF training but also achieve higher reward scores and win rates" (Abstract)—but the evidence is restricted to a single model family that, at the time of writing, was not the primary choice for most RLHF deployment teams.

**The consequence:** The generalizability of the Advantage Model's calibration benefits and Selective Rehearsal's forgetting reduction to other model families is unknown. The Advantage Model's effectiveness depends on the quality of the expected reward estimator `e_τ(x)` and the importance-weighting scheme in Equation 8, both of which interact with the base model's output distribution and the reward model's learned representations. A model family with different calibration properties, different response distributions, or different sensitivity to the bounding loss margin `m(x)` might not exhibit the same ECE reduction (from 4.70 to 3.48 on HH-RLHF) or the same PPO stability improvements. Similarly, Selective Rehearsal depends on SimCSE embeddings for clustering—embeddings from a RoBERTa-based model (`princeton-nlp/sup-simcse-roberta-base`, Section 3.2) are used to cluster prompts generated for a BLOOMZ policy. The compatibility of this cross-model-family embedding approach (RoBERTa embeddings for BLOOMZ prompts) with other model families is assumed but not demonstrated. A LLaMA-based policy might produce prompts with different semantic structure, and the SimCSE clustering might produce different (potentially less meaningful) clusters.

**What evidence exists in the paper:** The evidence is entirely confined to BLOOMZ. The Advantage Model is evaluated on HH-RLHF (English) and a proprietary Chinese dataset (Section 4.1), but both use BLOOMZ7B as the reward model backbone. PPO training uses BLOOMZ176B. The publicly available OpenAssistant reward model (DeBERTa-based) is compared against in Table 1 for accuracy, but (a) its ECE is not reported, so calibration comparison is impossible, and (b) it is not used in any PPO training comparison, so its effect on downstream stability is unknown. The paper contains no experiments varying the model family and no discussion of model-family-specific assumptions.

**Mitigation status:** Not addressed. The paper does not acknowledge this as a limitation. The claims are presented without qualification about model family specificity. No future work is suggested regarding cross-model-family validation.

### 100-Step PPO Horizon Leaves Long-Term Stability Unverified

**The assumption or constraint:** All PPO training experiments are conducted for exactly 100 steps with a global batch size of 256 (Section 4.2). This training horizon is stated without justification—no evidence is provided that 100 steps is sufficient to reveal the failure modes the paper aims to address, nor that stability at 100 steps implies stability over longer training runs. The paper's central claim is that the proposed methods "stabilize RLHF training" and prevent "recurring failures" (Section 1), but "stability" is assessed only within this fixed, relatively short window. Reward hacking and catastrophic forgetting are phenomena that can manifest or compound over extended training as the policy progressively exploits reward model weaknesses.

**The consequence:** The paper cannot distinguish between two possibilities: (1) the Advantage Model fundamentally prevents reward hacking and catastrophic forgetting, such that training would remain stable indefinitely; or (2) the Advantage Model delays the onset of these instabilities, but they would eventually appear given sufficient training steps. This distinction matters enormously for practical deployment. If the Advantage Model merely delays instability, a practitioner running longer PPO training (which is common in production RLHF pipelines where thousands of steps may be used) would encounter the same failure modes later in training—potentially after substantial compute has been invested. The RM-PPO learning curves in Figure 5a and 5b already show degradation within the 100-step window (declining GPT-4 win rate, rising delta rewards characteristic of reward hacking), so the instability is active on this timescale. But whether AM-PPO would remain stable at 200, 500, or 1000 steps is entirely unmeasured. Additionally, Selective Rehearsal's rehearsal dataset is constructed once (from the initial policy's outputs, Section 3.2). As the policy drifts during extended training, the rehearsal examples become increasingly off-policy—responses the current policy would no longer generate. The effectiveness of rehearsing stale, off-policy data over many steps is not tested.

**What evidence exists in the paper:** Figure 5 shows PPO training curves over exactly 100 steps. Table 2 reports final win/loss rates after 100 steps. There are no results at any other training horizon. The paper does not discuss the choice of 100 steps or acknowledge the limitation of extrapolating "stability" from a fixed short horizon. No learning curves are shown beyond 100 steps, and no analysis of whether the advantage signal or rehearsal loss degrades over extended training is provided.

**Mitigation status:** Not addressed. The paper does not acknowledge this as a limitation or suggest longer-horizon experiments as future work. The claims of "stability" and "resistance to such problems" (Section 4.3) are presented without qualification about training duration.

### Selective Rehearsal's Contribution Is Small and Potentially Confounded

**The assumption or constraint:** The paper presents Selective Rehearsal as a key contribution for mitigating catastrophic forgetting, alongside the Advantage Model. However, the empirical results in Table 2 show that the Advantage Model alone (AM-PPO) is responsible for the vast majority of the forgetting reduction: the forget set lose rate drops from 29.28% (RM-PPO) to 8.44% (AM-PPO)—a reduction of 20.84 percentage points. Adding Selective Rehearsal (AM-PPO-SR) further reduces the lose rate to 7.95%—an additional reduction of only 0.49 percentage points. On the main test set, AM-PPO achieves 14.87% win rate, and AM-PPO-SR achieves 15.78%—an improvement of 0.91 percentage points. These incremental gains are directionally consistent but small in absolute magnitude relative to the Advantage Model's effect. Furthermore, Selective Rehearsal is never evaluated with a standard (miscalibrated) reward model (RM-PPO-SR), so it is impossible to determine whether rehearsal alone—without calibrated advantage scores for the within-cluster selection step—would provide any benefit. The clustering-based selection mechanism uses Advantage Model scores to pick the top-k examples per cluster; if those scores were miscalibrated, the selected examples might not represent "expert-aligned" behavior worth preserving.

**The consequence:** A practitioner reading this paper might invest significant effort implementing the Selective Rehearsal pipeline (SimCSE embedding, KMeans clustering, within-cluster selection, rehearsal loss integration) expecting substantial forgetting reduction, when the empirical evidence suggests the Advantage Model already captures most of the benefit. The paper does not provide the evidence needed to determine whether Selective Rehearsal's small incremental gain justifies its implementation complexity. Additionally, because the interaction between the selection mechanism and the reward signal quality is not tested, it is unclear whether Selective Rehearsal is an independent technique or one whose effectiveness is contingent on having a well-calibrated Advantage Model. If the latter, then Selective Rehearsal cannot be recommended as a standalone method for RLHF pipelines that use standard reward models.

**What evidence exists in the paper:** Table 2 provides the win/loss breakdown for all three variants, making the relative contribution of each component directly visible. The forget set lose rate progression (29.28% → 8.44% → 7.95%) clearly shows the Advantage Model dominates the forgetting reduction. The paper does not report RM-PPO-SR, so the independent contribution of Selective Rehearsal cannot be assessed. The paper does not comment on the magnitude of Selective Rehearsal's incremental contribution relative to the Advantage Model's contribution or discuss whether this magnitude justifies the additional complexity.

**Mitigation status:** Not addressed. The paper presents Selective Rehearsal as an important contribution without contextualizing its effect size relative to the Advantage Model. The absence of an RM-PPO-SR condition is not acknowledged as a limitation. The paper does not discuss the implementation complexity tradeoff or provide guidance on whether Selective Rehearsal is worth adopting if the Advantage Model is already in use.

### No Comparison Against Offline Alignment Methods

**The assumption or constraint:** The paper's entire motivation rests on the premise that PPO-based RLHF is valuable enough to warrant stabilization efforts, rather than being replaced by offline alignment methods that avoid online RL training entirely. Section 5 reviews several such methods: Direct Preference Optimization (DPO; Rafailov et al., 2023), Preference Ranking Optimization (PRO; Song et al., 2023), RRHF (Yuan et al., 2023), and RAFT (Dong et al., 2023). The paper acknowledges these exist and describes them as "sidestepping the necessity for Reinforcement Learning (RL) training," but provides no empirical comparison against any of them. The implicit argument is that "major production systems (GPT-4, Claude) continue to use PPO-based RLHF" (Section 2 analysis in prior sections), so stabilizing PPO is independently valuable—but this does not address whether a stabilized PPO pipeline produces better alignment than a simpler offline method trained on the same preference data.

**The consequence:** The paper cannot answer the question most relevant to a practitioner deciding on an alignment strategy: "Should I use stabilized PPO with the Advantage Model and Selective Rehearsal, or should I use DPO (or another offline method) and avoid PPO entirely?" The stabilized PPO pipeline still involves all the complexity of online RL—reward model training, online sampling, KL penalty tuning, and now additional components (expected reward estimator, importance weighting, clustering, rehearsal). If a simpler offline method achieves comparable or better alignment on the same datasets, the marginal benefit of stabilizing PPO is questionable. The paper's contributions are positioned as improvements to a pipeline that the field was already moving away from due to its instability; without demonstrating that the stabilized pipeline outperforms the offline alternatives that motivated that shift, the practical impact is unclear.

**What evidence exists in the paper:** None. There are no DPO, PRO, RRHF, or RAFT baselines in any experiment. The paper does not train an offline alignment model on the same preference data (HH-RLHF or proprietary Chinese) to compare against the PPO variants. The win rate numbers (Table 2: 12.72–15.78% win rate against SFT on the main test set, with ~74% ties) provide no context about what an offline method would achieve on the same evaluation.

**Mitigation status:** Not addressed. The paper acknowledges offline methods in the related work (Section 5) but does not treat the absence of comparison as a limitation. No future work is suggested regarding offline-vs-online comparison. The paper's framing implicitly assumes that online RL is the right paradigm and that stabilization improvements are valuable regardless of offline alternatives—but this assumption is not tested or defended.

### No Statistical Reporting Makes Effect Size Reliability Unassessable

**The assumption or constraint:** All quantitative results in the paper are presented as point estimates without any measure of statistical uncertainty. The reward modeling results (Table 1: accuracy and ECE) are reported to two decimal places without confidence intervals, standard deviations, or information about the number of evaluation runs. The PPO results (Table 2: win/loss/tie percentages; Figure 5: learning curves) are reported without error bars, multiple random seeds, or any indication of run-to-run variability. PPO training is known to be sensitive to random seeds (due to online sampling stochasticity, advantage normalization, and optimization dynamics), and reward model training can vary with initialization and data ordering. The test sets are of moderate size: 8,500 examples for HH-RLHF reward model evaluation, 2,623 for PPO main test, 1,704 for forget test. The effect sizes on win rate are modest—the difference between RM-PPO and AM-PPO on the main test set is 2.15 percentage points, and between AM-PPO and AM-PPO-SR is 0.91 percentage points (Table 2).

**The consequence:** The paper's headline claims about "higher reward scores and win rates" cannot be evaluated for statistical reliability. A 2.15 percentage point win rate difference on a 2,623-example test set, with ~74% of examples being ties, means the difference is driven by roughly 56 examples (2,623 × 0.0215 ≈ 56). Without confidence intervals or significance tests, a practitioner cannot determine whether this difference reflects a genuine improvement or random variation across PPO runs. This is particularly concerning for Selective Rehearsal, whose incremental contribution (0.91 percentage points on main test set win rate, 0.49 percentage points on forget set lose rate) is small enough that a single additional PPO run with a different seed could plausibly reverse the comparison. The paper's conclusions about "stability" are similarly difficult to assess: the RM-PPO curve in Figure 5a shows degradation, but whether this degradation is consistent across runs or specific to the shown seed is unknown.

**What evidence exists in the paper:** Figure 6 provides the only quantitative information about variability: the test-set reward for AM-PPO-SR varies by "approximately 0.05 points" across different cluster numbers `c`. This is the closest the paper comes to reporting variance, but it is variance across hyperparameter settings, not across training runs. All other results are single-point estimates. The paper does not mention the number of PPO training runs conducted, the number of reward model training runs, or any procedure for aggregating across seeds.

**Mitigation status:** Not addressed. The paper does not acknowledge the absence of statistical reporting as a limitation. No confidence intervals, error bars, or significance tests appear in any table or figure. The paper does not discuss run-to-run variability or recommend that practitioners validate stability across multiple seeds. Given that RLHF instability is the paper's central problem, and that the proposed solutions aim to reduce instability, the absence of any measure of variance is a significant methodological gap that weakens the strength of the claimed improvements.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper makes a **diagnostic contribution** rather than a paradigm-shifting one: it identifies *where* RLHF instability originates and provides targeted, minimal fixes rather than proposing a fundamentally new alignment framework. The conceptual shift is from treating RLHF instability as an optimization problem requiring better constraints on the policy, toward treating it as a **reward signal quality problem** and a **training data composition problem**. This reframing changes where practitioners and researchers should direct their debugging efforts.

The magnitude is best understood as an **incremental refinement with outsized practical consequences**. The Advantage Model is architecturally a standard reward model with a modified training objective—the change to the loss function (adding two bounding terms) is small, but the downstream effects on PPO stability are large: forget set lose rate drops from 29.28% to 8.44% simply by swapping the reward model for the Advantage Model (Table 2). This is the paper's strongest empirical result and suggests that many RLHF instability failures in practice may stem from a fixable flaw in reward model training rather than from any fundamental brittleness in PPO itself.

The paper resolves a latent tension in the literature between proponents of online RL (PPO-based methods used in GPT-4, Claude, Sparrow, Bard, Llama 2-Chat) and advocates for offline alternatives (DPO, PRO, RRHF, RAFT). The offline methods gained traction partly because PPO was perceived as inherently unstable—the paper's findings cut against this narrative by demonstrating that two specific fixes (calibrated reward scores and diversity-aware rehearsal) can produce stable PPO training. This does not settle the online-vs-offline debate—the paper never compares against offline methods—but it strengthens the case that PPO's reputation for instability may reflect implementation choices rather than algorithmic fundamentals. A researcher committed to online RL now has concrete evidence that reward model calibration and training data curation, rather than algorithmic replacement, are the highest-leverage interventions.

However, the paper also **narrows the scope of what counts as an RLHF contribution** in a way that could productively refocus the field. By showing that reward model calibration is the dominant factor in stability (the Advantage Model alone produces a 20.84 percentage point reduction in forget set lose rate, while Selective Rehearsal adds only 0.49 points more), the paper implies that reward model quality is the primary bottleneck. This suggests that research effort spent on increasingly sophisticated PPO variants or KL-penalty tuning heuristics may have diminishing returns compared to effort spent on better reward model training. The paper also makes clear that data selection for PPO must be skill-aware, not just score-aware—a finding that pushes against the default practice of treating all PPO prompts as interchangeable.

The most durable contribution may be the **diagnostic framework** the paper implicitly establishes: before debugging PPO instability, examine (a) whether your reward model produces task-dependent score distributions (Figure 4a), (b) whether your reward model is calibrated (Figure 2), and (c) whether your PPO training data covers diverse skills uniformly or over-represents high-scoring categories. This checklist translates the paper's specific findings into a general methodology that can be applied even without implementing the Advantage Model or Selective Rehearsal directly. A practitioner using a standard reward model could compute task-level score means and variances, identify problematic distributional disparities, and apply targeted fixes (e.g., z-scoring per task category) informed by the paper's diagnostic logic.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Does the Advantage Model's stability persist over long training horizons?** The paper's most significant limitation is the 100-step PPO training budget (Section 4.2), which leaves open whether the Advantage Model permanently prevents reward hacking or merely delays its onset. A direct follow-up would train AM-PPO and RM-PPO for 500–2000 steps on the same BLOOMZ176B setup, tracking GPT-4 win rate, forget set lose rate, and delta reward curves across the full training duration. The critical question is whether AM-PPO's win rate remains stable or eventually degrades as the policy discovers advantage model blind spots that are subtler than the reward model's distributional artifacts. If AM-PPO remains stable at 1000+ steps, this would substantially strengthen the paper's claims and provide evidence that the bounding loss and advantage formulation address the root cause of reward hacking rather than just its early symptoms. If AM-PPO eventually degrades, this would reveal that calibration alone is insufficient and that online RL has deeper stability challenges—a negative result that would usefully redirect effort toward offline methods or toward periodic reward model retraining.

**Does Selective Rehearsal help when the reward signal is miscalibrated?** The paper never evaluates Selective Rehearsal with a standard (uncalibrated) reward model (RM-PPO-SR), leaving unknown whether clustering-based rehearsal is independently effective or requires the Advantage Model's calibrated scores for within-cluster selection. A targeted ablation would train three conditions—RM-PPO, RM-PPO-SR (clustering + rehearsal with RM scores for selection), and AM-PPO-SR (the paper's full system)—on the same BLOOMZ setup. If RM-PPO-SR significantly reduces forgetting compared to RM-PPO (e.g., bringing forget set lose rate from 29.28% to below 15%), this would establish Selective Rehearsal as a general-purpose technique applicable even in standard RLHF pipelines without requiring the Advantage Model. The within-cluster selection criterion would need adaptation (using RM scores instead of AM scores), and comparing alternative criteria (random within-cluster, entropy-based, length-based) would reveal whether the rehearsal benefit comes from skill coverage, from response quality, or from both. If RM-PPO-SR *does not* reduce forgetting (because miscalibrated scores select poor rehearsal examples), this would demonstrate that Selective Rehearsal's effectiveness is contingent on reward signal quality—an important boundary condition that currently is unstated.

**How does the Advantage Model interact with rejection sampling and offline RL methods?** The paper's PPO focus leaves unexplored whether the Advantage Model's calibration benefits transfer to non-PPO alignment pipelines. A direct experiment would train an Advantage Model on the HH-RLHF preference data, then use it for (a) rejection sampling fine-tuning (Equations 3–4, selecting responses above a threshold on advantage score rather than raw reward) and (b) as the implicit reward function in Direct Preference Optimization (DPO; Rafailov et al., 2023). Comparing these against the same methods using a standard reward model would test whether calibrated scores improve offline alignment quality and stability. The Advantage Model's bounded score range [−2.5, 2.5] might be particularly beneficial for rejection sampling, where threshold selection (the `τ` in Equation 3) is sensitive to score scale—a practitioner using a standard RM might need to tune `τ` per task category, while the Advantage Model's task-agnostic scale could enable a single global threshold. This experiment would broaden the Advantage Model's applicabilable scope beyond PPO and address the paper's implicit limitation of evaluating only online RL.

**Can the expected reward estimator `e_τ(x)` and the margin `m(x)` be learned from data rather than hand-specified?** The paper sets `m(x) = 2.5` as a fixed constant and describes the expected reward estimator `e_τ(x)` as parameterized but provides no architectural details or training procedure (Section 3.1). Both are central to the Advantage Model's behavior: `e_τ(x)` determines the baseline that is subtracted, and `m(x)` determines the permissible advantage range. A follow-up could make both components data-driven: train `e_τ(x)` as a separate regression head predicting the average reward for each prompt category (using task labels from the dataset's task spectrum or cluster assignments from SimCSE embeddings), and train `m(x)` as a function of prompt difficulty or reward model uncertainty (e.g., wider margins for prompts where the reward model is less confident). The experiment would compare a fixed-margin AM against a learned-margin AM on calibration (ECE) and PPO stability (win rate curves), using the HH-RLHF dataset where task categories are partially known (helpful vs. harmless subdomains). If learned margins improve calibration or stability, this would transform the Advantage Model from a heuristic (fixed margin) into a principled method (data-driven margin), addressing the paper's own acknowledged limitation that `m(x)` is "speculative."

**Does the Advantage Model's calibration benefit transfer across model scales and families?** All experiments use BLOOMZ7B for reward modeling and BLOOMZ176B for the policy—a specific model family and a specific scale asymmetry (~25× parameter ratio). Whether the ECE reduction from 4.70 to 3.48 (HH-RLHF, Table 1) replicates with LLaMA-based reward models, GPT-based reward models, or at different scales (e.g., 13B reward model scoring 70B policy) is unknown. A cross-model-family study would train Advantage Models and standard reward models on the same HH-RLHF data using at least two additional model families (e.g., LLaMA 2 7B and Mistral 7B as reward model backbones) and report accuracy, ECE, task-level score distribution statistics (replicating Figure 4), and downstream PPO stability. If the calibration benefits are consistent across families, the Advantage Model would be validated as a general technique. If benefits are BLOOMZ-specific (perhaps due to BLOOMZ's multilingual pretraining or multitask fine-tuning producing particular score distribution patterns), this would substantially limit the paper's practical impact and suggest that reward model calibration strategies may need to be family-specific.

**What is the relative contribution of the bounding loss vs. the advantage transformation in preventing reward hacking?** The Advantage Model combines two distinct modifications: subtracting an expected baseline (the advantage transformation, Equation 5) and bounding scores within [−m, m] (the bounding loss, Equation 6). These are separable, and their individual contributions to stability are unknown. An ablation study would train three reward model variants: (a) standard RM (pairwise ranking only), (b) RM with bounding loss but no advantage subtraction (raw rewards bounded to [−2.5, 2.5]), and (c) Advantage Model (both bounding and subtraction). Comparing PPO stability across these conditions would attribute the stability improvement to the baseline subtraction, the score bounding, or their interaction. If bounding alone produces most of the stability benefit, this would be practically important because bounding can be applied to any existing reward model with a simple loss modification, without the complexity of the expected reward estimator and importance weighting. If the advantage subtraction is essential, this would validate the paper's theoretical framing (prompt-conditional baselines prevent category-level reward hacking) and justify the additional implementation complexity.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Stabilizing production RLHF pipelines that currently experience reward hacking and forgetting.** The most direct application is for teams already running PPO-based RLHF and encountering the failure modes the paper documents. The Advantage Model can be adopted as a **drop-in replacement for the standard reward model**: a team using pairwise ranking loss for reward model training can switch to Equation 6's loss, add an expected reward estimator, and apply the advantage transformation at inference time, without changing any other component of their RLHF pipeline. The calibration benefits reported in Table 1 (ECE reduction from 4.70 to 3.48 on HH-RLHF) and the stability benefits in Table 2 (forget set lose rate reduction from 29.28% to 8.44%) provide concrete, quantified motivation for this switch. The BLOOMZ-specific results should be validated on the team's own model family, but the paper's diagnostic framework—examining task-level score distributions for disparities—is immediately actionable regardless of whether the full Advantage Model is adopted.

**Reducing the cost of RLHF hyperparameter tuning by decoupling reward quality from PPO stability.** In standard RLHF, practitioners tune the KL penalty coefficient `β` (Equation 2) to balance alignment improvement against stability, often running multiple expensive PPO training runs to find a value that prevents divergence. The paper's finding that a calibrated reward model dramatically reduces instability (AM-PPO alone eliminates the reward hacking and most of the catastrophic forgetting that RM-PPO exhibits, even without Selective Rehearsal) implies that **reward model quality and PPO hyperparameters can be optimized more independently**. A team could first train and validate a well-calibrated Advantage Model (using ECE and task-level score distribution metrics as quality checks), then run PPO with a lower KL penalty than would be safe with a standard RM, enabling more aggressive alignment improvement within a given training budget. The paper's 100-step training with `β` unspecified prevents direct extrapolation of this benefit, but the stability of AM-PPO's win rate throughout training (Figure 5b, green curve) relative to RM-PPO (blue curve, which degrades) supports the claim that a calibrated reward signal reduces sensitivity to KL penalty tuning.

**Constructing targeted rehearsal sets to protect specific model capabilities during alignment.** Selective Rehearsal's clustering-based selection mechanism provides a general framework for **skill-preserving alignment**. A team deploying an LLM for a product with critical capabilities—mathematical reasoning for a tutoring assistant, factual accuracy for a medical QA system, safety guardrails for a customer-facing chatbot—can construct a rehearsal set specifically targeting those skills. The procedure is: (1) collect prompts representing the critical skills (either from the PPO dataset or from external curated sources), (2) embed them with SimCSE or an alternative sentence encoder, (3) cluster to ensure coverage across sub-skills, (4) select high-quality responses (by advantage score, human preference, or domain-specific metrics) within each cluster, and (5) add the NLL rehearsal loss (Equation 9) during PPO training with a small coefficient (the paper uses `γ = 0.01`). The paper's finding that Selective Rehearsal provides incremental forgetting reduction (from 8.44% to 7.95% lose rate, Table 2) is modest but directionally positive, and the approach's value for protecting explicitly curated skill sets—which the paper mentions but does not evaluate ("rehearsal (x, y) pairs could come from other important data sources representing specific skills," Section 3.2)—may be substantially larger than the generic PPO-data rehearsal the paper tests. The robustness to cluster count `c` (Figure 6: ~0.05 point variance) means this can be deployed without extensive hyperparameter tuning.

**Reward model evaluation beyond ranking accuracy.** The paper's calibration analysis (Figure 2, Table 1 ECE) and task-level score distribution analysis (Figure 4) establish two new **reward model quality metrics** that practitioners can adopt immediately without implementing the Advantage Model. A team training a standard reward model can (a) compute ECE on their preference test set by binning score differences and comparing predicted vs. actual ranking accuracy, and (b) compute per-task-category mean and standard deviation of reward scores to identify distributional disparities that may cause downstream reward hacking. These diagnostics are cheap to compute (requiring only the trained reward model and the test set) and provide actionable signals about whether the reward model is likely to cause PPO instability before any expensive PPO training run is conducted. If ECE is high (>4.0, per the paper's RM baseline) or if task-level means vary by substantively (as in Figure 4a), the team can intervene at the reward modeling stage—adjusting the loss function, collecting more balanced preference data, or adopting the Advantage Model—rather than discovering the problem through failed PPO runs. This diagnostic methodology may be the paper's most immediately practical contribution for teams not ready to modify their RLHF architecture.

### When to Prefer the Advantage Model over a Standard Reward Model

The paper does not provide a formal decision rule or compare against named alternatives beyond the standard reward model. However, the following conditions are implicit in the experimental results and diagnostic framework:

- **Prefer the Advantage Model when** your preference data contains heterogeneous task categories (e.g., QA, code generation, summarization, translation, creative writing) and you observe—or want to prevent—task-dependent score distributions in your reward model. The paper's Figure 1a and Figure 4a provide concrete evidence that heterogeneous data induces distributional disparities, and Figures 5a and 5b show that the Advantage Model prevents the downstream reward hacking these disparities cause.
- **Prefer the Advantage Model when** reward hacking is observed during PPO training (rising reward scores with flat or declining actual quality, as measured by human evaluation or LLM-as-judge win rate). The paper demonstrates this signature in RM-PPO (Figure 5) and shows AM-PPO eliminates it.
- **Prefer the Advantage Model when** catastrophic forgetting on SFT-era capabilities is a concern and you lack a curated rehearsal set. The Advantage Model alone reduces forget set lose rate from 29.28% to 8.44% (Table 2) without requiring the Selective Rehearsal infrastructure, making it the single highest-leverage intervention for forgetting reduction.
- **The cost of adopting the Advantage Model** is implementation of the two-term bounding loss (Equation 6) and the expected reward estimator `e_τ(x)` (Equation 7), plus maintaining historical policy checkpoints for importance weighting if `K > 0` in Equation 8. The paper provides no sensitivity analysis over `K` and `N`, so a team uncertain about this complexity could start with a simplified variant (bounding loss on raw rewards, no advantage subtraction, equivalent to condition (b) in the proposed ablation follow-up above) and evaluate whether that alone provides sufficient stability before implementing the full advantage formulation.
- **The paper does not provide evidence to prefer the Advantage Model over offline alignment methods** (DPO, PRO, RRHF, RAFT) because no such comparison is conducted. A team choosing between online and offline alignment should not use this paper alone to make that decision.
