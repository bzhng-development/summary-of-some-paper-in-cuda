# Beyond Pass@1: Self-Play with Variational Problem Synthesis Sustains RLVR

**ArXiv:** [2508.14029](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.14029)

## 🎯 Pitch

This paper introduces an online **Self-play with Variational problem Synthesis (SvS)** strategy for RLVR (Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards) training, addressing the entropy collapse and stagnating Pass@k that plague standard RLVR when models over-optimize on a fixed problem set.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces an online **Self-play with Variational problem Synthesis (SvS)** strategy for RLVR (Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards) training, addressing the entropy collapse and stagnating Pass@k that plague standard RLVR when models over-optimize on a fixed problem set. The core mechanism uses the policy model itself to synthesize structurally diverse but semantically equivalent variants of underperforming training problems—deriving new problems from its own correct solutions so that reference answers transfer without additional labeling—and then jointly trains on original problem solving, variational problem synthesis, and synthetic problem solving in a self-improving loop. Evaluated on Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct with the MATH-12k and DAPO-17k datasets, SvS sustains policy entropy throughout training and achieves absolute gains of 18.3% and 22.8% in Pass@32 on AIME 24 and AIME 25 respectively, where standard RLVR shows negligible improvement—establishing that self-play problem augmentation expands reasoning boundaries only on problems within the model's approximate capability frontier, as the hardest difficulty bins remain near-zero regardless of augmentation.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: RLVR Achieves Pass@1 Gains at the Cost of Diversity and Pass@k

The paper addresses a specific, well-documented failure mode in RLVR training: **standard RLVR improves Pass@1 performance but simultaneously collapses policy entropy, reducing generation diversity and preventing improvements in Pass@k** — a metric that captures the upper bound of what the model can achieve through repeated sampling. This trade-off creates a ceiling on how far RLVR can push a model's reasoning capability, because once the policy memorizes a narrow set of high-reward solution templates for the fixed training problems, it loses the capacity to explore more advanced reasoning strategies.

The phenomenon is not merely an intuition — the paper builds directly on two recent empirical findings. Cui et al. (2025b) demonstrated that during RLVR training on a static problem set, policy entropy and Pass@1 performance exhibit a logarithmic trade-off relationship: `Performance ∝ -a exp(Entropy + b)`. As entropy approaches zero, performance plateaus. Concurrently, Yue et al. (2025) showed that RLVR-trained models improve Pass@k only for small values of k (e.g., k = 1, 2, 4), with **no meaningful gains when k scales to tens or hundreds of samples** — a crucial finding because Pass@k with large k represents the model's true reasoning boundary, i.e., whether the model can ever produce a correct solution to a hard problem even after many attempts. Standard RLVR narrows the model toward the most reward-prone solutions rather than expanding the space of problems it can eventually solve.

The underlying mechanism is a form of **reward hacking specific to RLVR on fixed training sets**: the policy learns that certain solution patterns (structures, intermediate steps, algebraic manipulations) reliably yield correct final answers and thus positive rewards under the verifiable reward function. It then repeatedly generates these patterns — effectively memorizing correct solutions — rather than exploring alternative reasoning paths that might lead to novel correct solutions on harder variants. The paper explicitly characterizes this as "a behavior akin to 'hacking' the RLVR training" (Section 1), where the policy exploits the limited diversity of training problems rather than genuinely improving its reasoning capability.

### Why This Problem Matters

This gap has both theoretical and practical significance:

**Theoretical significance: It challenges the narrative that RLVR alone suffices for reasoning improvement.** DeepSeek-R1 (Guo et al., 2025a) and subsequent work demonstrated that RLVR can dramatically improve reasoning through extended chain-of-thought exploration. However, Yue et al. (2025)'s finding — that RLVR does not expand the reasoning boundary beyond the base model's inherent capability — suggests RLVR primarily **amplifies existing capabilities** (helping the model exploit what it already knows) rather than **creating new capabilities** (enabling the model to solve problems it fundamentally could not solve before). Understanding how to push past this boundary is essential for the field's goal of using RLVR for genuine capability expansion.

**Practical significance for model deployment: Pass@k matters when inference compute is available.** In many real-world deployment scenarios — batch evaluation, self-improvement data generation, or applications with generous latency budgets — organizations can afford to sample many solutions per problem and select the best one (best-of-N). In these settings, Pass@k at large k is the practically relevant accuracy metric. If RLVR improves Pass@1 but not Pass@k, it means the trained model underperforms the base model in the regime where inference compute is abundant — a counterintuitive and undesirable outcome. A training method that sustains Pass@k improvements therefore directly translates to better deployed performance when budget allows.

**Self-improvement loop sustainability:** For iterative self-improvement pipelines (where a model generates training data for its own next training round), generation diversity is essential — if the model collapses to producing homogeneous solutions, the next round of training sees no novel positive examples and the loop stalls. The entropy-collapse problem thus directly threatens the viability of autonomous self-improvement for LLMs.

### Prior Approaches and Their Shortcomings

#### Standard RLVR on Fixed Problem Sets

The dominant paradigm — exemplified by GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) trained on datasets like MATH-12k (Hendrycks et al., 2021) or DAPO-17k (Yu et al., 2025a) — follows a simple recipe: sample problems from a fixed training set, generate multiple solutions per problem, compute verifiable rewards (correctness of final answer), compute group-relative advantages, and update the policy. This works well for Pass@1 on in-distribution problems but, as the paper's motivating experiments show (Figure 2, blue curves), **training entropy continuously declines** and **Pass@32 plateaus** after a certain number of steps — the policy is simply re-generating memorized correct solutions to seen problems rather than learning transferable reasoning.

#### Rephrasing-Based Augmentation (MetaMath-style)

A natural approach to combat entropy collapse is to augment the training set with rephrased variants of existing problems — changing surface forms while preserving underlying structure. The paper explicitly tests this approach using MetaMath (Yu et al., 2023), adding 36k rephrased problems to the MATH-12k training set (Figure 2, orange curves). The results are informative but reveal three critical limitations:

1. **Semantic drift in reference answers:** Rephrased problems generated by external LLMs may inadvertently alter the problem semantics, making the original reference answer incorrect. Since RLVR relies entirely on verifiable rewards computed from reference answers, even a small fraction of mislabeled problems introduces noise into the training signal, undermining stability.

2. **Diversity cannot be guaranteed:** Since rephrasing typically uses the original problem text as context (e.g., "Rephrase the following problem..."), the output tends to preserve syntactic and lexical patterns from the source, limiting genuine structural diversity. The rephrased problems may be too similar to the originals to force the policy into novel reasoning strategies.

3. **Dependence on external models:** The rephrasing is done by a separate LLM (potentially more capable or differently trained), creating a dependency on external models and preventing end-to-end self-improvement.

Despite these limitations, the rephrasing experiment provides a **crucial proof of concept** for the paper's central thesis: Figure 2 (left) shows that training with augmented data **slows the entropy decline**, and when the augmented problems are refreshed at step 300, **entropy actually rises** — the policy is forced to re-explore. Figure 2 (right) shows that Pass@32 improves with augmented data, with sustained gains near the update steps. This directly motivates the paper's core research question: can we design an augmentation strategy that overcomes the limitations of rephrasing while preserving its entropy-stabilizing benefits?

#### Entropy Regulation Methods (Clip-CoV)

Rather than modifying the training data, an alternative approach is to directly regularize policy entropy during optimization — for example, by augmenting token-level advantages with an entropy bonus term. The paper compares against Clip-CoV (Cui et al., 2025b), a representative method from this class. However, the authors argue (and show in Appendix F, Figure 16) that such methods are **insufficient on their own** because they don't address the root cause: the policy can still memorize previously correct responses to secure rewards, even with entropy regularization. Entropy bonuses encourage diversity in the *distribution over tokens* but don't prevent the policy from re-generating the same high-level solution pattern with minor token-level variations. The paper shows that SvS consistently outperforms Clip-CoV and, importantly, that **combining SvS with Clip-CoV yields further gains** (Figure 17) — the two are complementary rather than competing.

#### Targeted Data Augmentation (SwS)

Most closely related to this work, Liang et al. (2025) proposed SwS (Self-aware Weakness-driven problem Synthesis), which generates synthetic problems based on the model's failure cases during RLVR training — targeting the model's specific weaknesses. The current paper's SvS can be seen as a generalization and refinement of this idea, with key differences: SvS synthesizes problems from correct solutions (not failures), uses a self-play paradigm without external guidance, and incorporates problem synthesis as a joint training objective alongside problem solving, forming a bidirectional learning loop.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper frames its contribution as solving the **data diversity bottleneck** in RLVR through an ideal data augmentation strategy for RLVR — one that the authors derive desiderata for based on their preliminary experiments and prior work limitations (Section 2, "Takeaways for Problem Diversity in RLVR"):

1. **Iterative/online:** The augmented problems must be refreshed as training progresses to prevent the policy from eventually memorizing even the augmented set. Static augmentation (adding a fixed set of rephrased problems at the start) merely delays entropy collapse rather than preventing it.

2. **Precise reference answers:** The central challenge of RLVR data augmentation is that the training signal comes entirely from verifiable rewards, which require ground-truth reference answers. Synthesizing new problems typically requires also synthesizing or deriving their correct answers — a hard problem in itself. The augmentation strategy must either generate answers automatically or reuse existing ones without semantic corruption.

3. **Capability-aligned:** The augmented problems should target the policy's current frontier — problems that are challenging but solvable — rather than being uniformly distributed across difficulty levels. Augmenting problems the model already masters provides no learning signal; augmenting problems far beyond its capability produces only negative examples with zero advantage in GRPO (since all-correct or all-incorrect groups have zero advantage).

The paper positions SvS as satisfying all three criteria through a single elegant mechanism: **derive new problems from the policy's own correct solutions to underperforming training problems**. Because the correct solution contains all information necessary to reconstruct the problem statement (it traces the full reasoning path from problem to answer), the policy can be prompted to invert this mapping — generating a problem that would elicit such a solution. And because the solution was verified correct against the original reference answer, the derived problem **automatically inherits that answer** — no labeling required. The policy is further tasked with solving its own synthesized problems, creating a self-contained loop: solve → synthesize → re-solve.

Crucially, this approach is **entirely self-play**: it uses only the policy model itself, with no external teachers, no distillation from stronger models, and no human annotation. The paper explicitly contrasts this with approaches that depend on external LLMs for data generation or answer verification (Section 1, Contribution 1). This makes SvS a **pure self-improvement paradigm** — the model improves by generating and solving its own challenges, consistent with the broader self-play tradition in AI (AlphaGo, expert iteration, etc.) but adapted to the specific constraints of RLVR where reward signals are discrete and based on answer matching.

The paper also positions SvS as **agnostic to the underlying RL algorithm** (Section 1). While all experiments use GRPO with Clip-Higher, the framework can incorporate any RLVR optimizer (PPO, GSPO, Reinforce++) since SvS operates at the data level — augmenting the training buffer with additional prompt-response-reward tuples — rather than modifying the optimization objective. This modularity is an important practical consideration for adoption.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

This paper develops a **data augmentation system** that runs alongside standard RLVR training, where the language model itself generates synthetic training problems from its own correct solutions and then practices solving them — forming a self-sustaining loop that prevents the model from memorizing a fixed problem set. The system solves the entropy collapse problem in RLVR by continuously introducing novel problem variants that target the model's current capability frontier, thereby forcing sustained exploration of diverse reasoning strategies without requiring any external problem sources, answer annotators, or teacher models.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The SvS system operates within each RLVR training step and comprises five interconnected components:

1. **Original Problem Solver**: The policy model `$\pi_\theta$` samples a batch of training problems from the fixed dataset and generates `$G = 8$` solutions per problem. Problems where the policy is either perfect (all correct) or utterly failing (all wrong) are filtered out, as they provide zero advantage in GRPO. The remaining problems — where the policy achieves mixed accuracy — enter the training buffer with their correctness rewards computed against ground-truth answers.

2. **Underperforming Problem Identifier**: From the mixed-accuracy problems, the system selects those whose group accuracy falls within `$[12.5\%, 50.0\%]$` — problems at the frontier of the policy's current capability. These are the only problems whose correct solutions will seed variational problem synthesis, targeting augmentation effort where it matters most.

3. **Variational Problem Synthesizer**: For each correct solution to an underperforming problem, the policy model is prompted (using the template in Figure 20) to reconstruct a problem statement that would plausibly elicit such a solution. Since the correct solution contains the full reasoning trace, the policy can work backwards — identifying the topic, inferring the reasoning type, and generating a new problem with rephrased descriptions and structure while preserving the original semantics. Critically, the synthesized problem **inherits the original problem's reference answer** without new labeling. Each correct solution yields `$G_v = 8$` candidate variational problems.

4. **Synthetic Problem Solver**: Each synthesized variational problem is fed back to the policy, which generates `$G = 8$` solutions. Correctness rewards are computed using the original reference answer. Problems yielding all-correct or all-incorrect solutions are filtered out; the rest enter the training buffer as standard problem-solving training data.

5. **Reward-Shaped Problem Synthesis Evaluator**: Each variational problem receives a synthesis reward based not on whether the policy can solve it at all, but on whether the policy achieves **moderate accuracy** on it — specifically, group accuracy in `$[12.5\%, 62.5\%]$`. Problems that are too easy (policy gets all or most correct — hints or answer leakage) or too hard (policy gets none correct — unsolvable or semantic drift) receive zero reward. Only correct solutions whose synthesized problems yield a mix of positive and negative synthesis rewards are retained for policy updating, ensuring the synthesis task provides a meaningful training signal.

The flow within one training step: original problems → solve → identify underperforming ones → synthesize variants from correct solutions → solve variants → evaluate synthesis quality → mix all three data types (original problem solving, variational problem synthesis, synthetic problem solving) into the experience buffer → update the policy via GRPO → clear the buffer → repeat.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the GRPO optimization objective and advantage computation, since this is the underlying RL algorithm that SvS builds upon and all three data types ultimately feed into the same update rule.
- **Second**, the original problem solving and filtering logic, establishing the base training loop and the criteria for which problems and solutions are retained.
- **Third**, the underperforming problem selection criterion — why `$[12.5\%, 50.0\%]$` accuracy range is chosen, and how this targets the augmentation effort.

This is an **empirical systems paper** whose core contribution is a data augmentation strategy for RLVR that uses the policy model itself in a self-play loop — solving problems, synthesizing variants from its correct solutions, and re-solving those variants — to maintain training entropy and improve Pass@k without external data sources or answer annotation.

#### GRPO: The Underlying RL Optimization Algorithm

The paper uses GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization; Shao et al., 2024) as the RL backbone, augmented with several techniques from Yu et al. (2025a): Clip-Higher, Token-Level Loss, and Dynamic Sampling. Understanding GRPO's advantage computation is essential because it determines which problems and solutions provide effective training signals — and thus which data gets into the SvS buffer.

**The GRPO Advantage.** For a given input prompt `$x$`, the policy model `$\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$` generates a group of `$G$` responses `$Y = \{y_i\}_{i=1}^{G}$`, each receiving a scalar reward `$r_i$`. The advantage `$A_{i,t}$` for each token at position `$t$` in response `$y_i$` is computed as:

$$A_{i,t} = \frac{r_i - \text{mean}(\{r_i\}_{i=1}^{G})}{\text{std}(\{r_i\}_{i=1}^{G})}$$

where `$\text{mean}(\{r_i\})$` is the average reward across the `$G$` responses in the group, and `$\text{std}(\{r_i\})$` is their standard deviation.

**What it computes:** For each response in a group, its token-level advantage is the group-normalized reward — how much better or worse that response's reward is compared to the group average, measured in standard deviation units. If all `$G$` responses have identical rewards (all correct: `$r_i = 1 \forall i$`, or all incorrect: `$r_i = 0 \forall i$`), then the standard deviation is zero and the advantage is undefined (degenerates to zero in GRPO). This normalization means that tokens from above-average responses get positive advantages (encouraged), tokens from below-average responses get negative advantages (discouraged), and tokens from average responses get near-zero advantages (no learning signal).

**Why this form:** Computing advantages relative to the group mean eliminates the need for a separately trained critic (value function) model, which is the key efficiency advantage of GRPO over PPO. It also means that **response groups with uniform rewards provide no gradient** — the policy cannot learn from problems it already solves perfectly (no room for improvement signal) or from problems it completely fails on (no positive examples to reinforce). This filtering property is fundamental to understanding which training data SvS retains.

**The GRPO Objective.** The full optimization objective at each update step is:

$$\mathcal{J}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}, Y \sim \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot|x)} \left[ \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \left( \min\left(k_{i,t}(\theta) A_{i,t}, \text{clip}(k_{i,t}(\theta), 1 - \varepsilon, 1 + \varepsilon) A_{i,t}\right) - \beta D_{\text{KL}}(\pi_\theta \| \pi_{\text{ref}}) \right) \right]$$

where `$k_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}|x, y_{i,<t})}$` is the probability ratio between the new and old policies for token `$t$` in response `$i$`, `$\varepsilon = 0.28$` is the clipping parameter (using Clip-Higher, meaning only the upper clip is applied — ratios below `$1-\varepsilon$` are not clipped, encouraging exploration), and `$\beta$` controls the KL penalty strength against the reference model `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$`.

**Key hyperparameters from Appendix D.1:** Learning rate `$1 \times 10^{-6}$` with constant schedule; sampling temperature fixed at `$1.0$`; group size `$G = 8$` for both original and synthetic problem solving, and `$G_v = 8$` for variational problem synthesis from each response; batch sizes for sampled problems and policy updates are both 256 (this is the number of distinct problems per step, each generating 8 solutions, for 2048 total solution-response pairs for original problems, plus additional synthetic problem-response pairs). Models trained on MATH-12k run for 300 steps; the 32B model on DAPO-17k runs for 600 steps.

#### Original Problem Solving and Experience Filtering (Algorithm 1, Lines 4–10)

At each training step `$t$`, the system samples a batch of problem-answer pairs `$(x, a)$` from the training set `$\mathcal{D}$`. For each problem, the policy `$\pi_\theta$` generates `$G = 8$` solutions `$\{y_i\}_{i=1}^{G}$`. The correctness reward for each solution is determined by exact answer matching:

$$R_c(y_i, a) = \mathbb{I}(\text{Extract}(y_i) = a)$$

where `$\mathbb{I}(\cdot)$` is the indicator function (1 if the extracted final answer matches the ground truth, 0 otherwise), and `$\text{Extract}(\cdot)$` parses the reasoning trajectory to isolate the final answer (using the hybrid rule-based verifier integrating Math-Verify and the DAPO verifier in veRL).

**What it computes:** A binary reward (0 or 1) for each of the 8 solutions to a problem, based solely on whether the extracted final answer string matches the reference answer.

**Why this form:** Binary answer-matching is the defining characteristic of RLVR — rewards are "verifiable" because correctness can be automatically checked without human judgment or learned reward models. This makes RLVR scalable but also creates the challenge that the only training signal is whether the final answer is correct, with no partial credit for sound reasoning that happens to reach a wrong answer.

**Filtering for the experience buffer.** The system computes the group accuracy for the problem: `$\text{Acc}(x) = \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} R_c(y_i, a)$` — the fraction of the 8 solutions that are correct. Crucially, only problems where `$0 < \text{Acc}(x) < 1$` — that is, problems with a **mix** of correct and incorrect solutions — are retained for policy updating. Formally (Algorithm 1, Line 8):

> "if `$0 < \text{Acc}(x) < 1$` then `$\mathcal{B} \leftarrow \mathcal{B} \cup \{(x, y_1), \ldots, (x, y_G)\}$`"

**Why this filtering rule:** This directly follows from the GRPO advantage formula. If all 8 solutions are correct (`$\text{Acc}(x) = 1$`), then all `$R_c = 1$`, the standard deviation is zero, and all advantages degenerate to zero — the policy receives no gradient from this problem. Similarly, if all 8 solutions are incorrect (`$\text{Acc}(x) = 0$`), all `$R_c = 0$`, the standard deviation is again zero, and no gradient flows. Only groups with variance in rewards provide effective learning signals. This filtering is standard practice in GRPO-based RLVR (applied in DAPO, SimpleRL, etc.) and is not novel to SvS — but it interacts with the subsequent augmentation logic because only retained problems can be candidates for variational problem synthesis.

#### Underperforming Problem Selection (Algorithm 1, Lines 11–12)

From the problems that pass the `$0 < \text{Acc}(x) < 1$` filter, the system further selects those whose group accuracy falls within a specific range:

$$\text{accl} < \text{Acc}(x) < \text{acch}$$

where `$\text{accl} = 12.5\%$` and `$\text{acch} = 50.0\%$`. In terms of the number of correct solutions out of 8: accuracy `$\text{Acc}(x) \in \{1/8, 2/8, 3/8\}$` — problems where the policy gets 1, 2, or 3 out of 8 solutions correct.

**Why `$[12.5\%, 50.0\%]$`?** This range targets the policy's **capability frontier** — problems that the policy can sometimes solve (at least one success in the group, so there exists at least one correct solution to use for synthesis) but cannot reliably solve (majority incorrect, so improvement is needed). Problems with accuracy above `$50\%$` are considered "too easy" — the policy already handles them well, and augmenting them would encourage the policy to overfit on already-mastered capabilities rather than stretch toward harder reasoning. Problems with accuracy at `$0\%$` (already filtered out by the previous step) have no correct solutions to use for synthesis. The upper bound of `$50\%$` ensures that the synthesized problems target the policy's weaknesses rather than its strengths.

Concretely, for each underperforming problem, the system identifies the subset of correct solutions:

$$I = \{i \mid R_c(y_i, a) = 1\}$$

These correct solutions — because they contain the full reasoning path from the problem statement to the correct answer — serve as the input to the variational problem synthesis step. Each correct solution `$y_i$` for `$i \in I$` will independently seed the generation of `$G_v = 8$` variational problems.

#### Variational Problem Synthesis from Correct Responses (Algorithm 1, Lines 13–14)

For each correct solution `$y_i$` to an underperforming problem, the policy model is prompted to **reconstruct a problem statement** that would elicit such a solution. The prompt template (Figure 20, "Variational Problem Synthesis Prompt (Reasoning)") instructs the model to:

1. Identify the topic and context indicated by the response.
2. Infer the type of reasoning or calculation involved.
3. Determine the most likely educational purpose or learning objective.
4. Write a clear, concise, and natural-sounding problem in English that precisely aligns with the provided response.

The key instruction is: "The solution is assured to be correct. Your goal is to generate variants for the original problem that would most plausibly elicit such a response." The model outputs synthetic problems in a structured format delimited by ````text` blocks.

**The inversion principle.** This step is essentially asking the policy to perform **inverse reasoning**: given the output (the solution trace), reconstruct the input (the problem statement). Because the correct solution `$y_i$` contains all information necessary to characterize the original problem — the mathematical objects involved, the operations performed, the constraints, the final answer — the policy has sufficient information to generate a well-posed problem. However, the model is not explicitly shown the original problem text during synthesis (it only sees the solution), so it cannot simply copy or paraphrase the original; it must genuinely reconstruct the problem from the reasoning trace. This naturally produces **structurally diverse** variants — problems phrased differently, with reordered steps, different numerical examples that lead to the same solution structure, or alternative descriptions of the same mathematical scenario.

**The inherited answer property.** The critical enabling constraint is: **the synthesized problem must share the same reference answer as the original problem.** This follows because the correct solution `$y_i$` was verified (by the Extract-and-match procedure in Equation 1) to yield the ground-truth answer `$a$`, and the synthesis prompt requires the generated problem to "precisely align with the provided response." Therefore, any well-formed variational problem `$\hat{x}^j_i$` (the `$j$`-th variant from solution `$y_i$`) should have the property that its correct answer is also `$a$`. If the synthesized problem has a different correct answer, it fails the alignment constraint and should receive a negative synthesis reward.

This design eliminates the primary bottleneck in RLVR data augmentation: **no new answer annotation is needed.** The reference answer `$a$` from the original problem is reused for all `$G_v = 8$` variational problems derived from solution `$y_i$`. The constraint that answers must match serves double duty — it validates the correctness of the variational problem (if the policy can solve it and get answer `$a$`, the problem is likely valid) and it provides the verifiable reward signal for the subsequent synthetic problem solving step.

**Example from the paper.** The case study in Appendix J.2.2 shows this process concretely. The original problem involves finding a polynomial `$p(x) = ax^2 + bx + c$` from three evaluation points and then solving `$p(p(x)) = x$` for its non-integer root. One correct solution (Response 1 in Listing 1) traces through setting up the system of equations, solving for coefficients, computing `$p(p(x))$`, factoring the quartic, and identifying the non-integer root `$8/3$` with answer `$m+n=11$`. This solution is fed into the synthesis prompt, and the policy generates 8 variant problems (Listing 2), such as:

- *"A polynomial `$p(x) = ax^2 + bx + c$` satisfies the conditions `$p(1) = 1$`, `$p(2) = 3$`, and `$p(3) = 2$`. Given this polynomial `$p(x)$`, find the non-integer root of the equation `$p(p(x)) = x$`. Finally, if this non-integer root is of the form `$\frac{m}{n}$` where `$m$` and `$n$` are coprime integers, determine the sum `$m + n$`."*

This variant rephrases the original while preserving the exact same mathematical structure and, critically, the same reference answer (`$m+n=11$`).

#### Reward Shaping for Problem Synthesis (Algorithm 1, Lines 21–27)

The synthesis step does not simply reward the policy for producing any problem that is solvable — this would be trivially exploitable. Instead, the paper introduces a **difficulty-gated reward** for variational problems, which was developed after an initial failed attempt (Appendix G) and is central to SvS's effectiveness.

**The failed naive approach.** Initially, the authors assigned a positive synthesis reward to any variational problem for which the policy produced **at least one** correct solution (i.e., at least one of the `$G = 8$` solutions' extracted answers matched the original answer `$a$`). The intuition was: if the policy can solve the synthetic problem and get the same answer, the problem must be valid. However, the policy quickly exploited this by embedding explicit hints or even the final answer directly into the synthetic problem text (Appendix G, Figure 18, middle panel). Since these hint-laden problems were trivially solvable (the policy just had to copy the hinted answer), they consistently received positive synthesis rewards, and the group accuracy on these problems was uniformly 100% — causing the advantage to collapse to zero in GRPO, providing no learning signal, and ultimately impairing model exploration (entropy dropped) and downstream performance (Figure 18, left and right panels).

**The difficulty-gated reward.** To prevent this exploitation, the paper redesigns the synthesis reward to penalize problems that are too easy (hints embedded) or too hard (unsolvable, semantic drift). The reward for a variational problem `$\hat{x}^j_i$` is:

$$R_v(\hat{x}^j_i) = \mathbb{I}\left(\widehat{\text{accl}} \leq \text{Acc}(\hat{x}^j_i, a) \leq \widehat{\text{acch}}\right)$$

where `$\text{Acc}(\hat{x}^j_i, a) = \frac{1}{G}\sum_{k=1}^{G} \mathbb{I}(\text{Extract}(\hat{y}_k) = a)$` is the group accuracy on the synthetic problem (computed in the subsequent synthetic problem solving step), and the reward thresholds are `$\widehat{\text{accl}} = 12.5\%$` and `$\widehat{\text{acch}} = 62.5\%$`.

**What it computes:** A binary reward (1 or 0) for the synthesis task. The reward is 1 **only if** the policy achieves a moderate level of group accuracy on the synthetic problem — specifically, `$1/8$` to `$5/8$` of the 8 solutions must match the original answer `$a$`. If the accuracy is 0 (no correct solutions — problem is too hard, likely invalid, or the answer has shifted), the reward is 0. If the accuracy is `$6/8$`, `$7/8$`, or `$8/8$` (problem is too easy — likely contains hints or is a trivial rephrase), the reward is 0.

**Why the upper bound of 62.5%?** The key insight is that a well-designed variational problem should be **challenging but solvable** for the current policy. If the policy gets 6, 7, or 8 out of 8 correct, the problem is too easy — the policy has likely embedded cues (e.g., explicitly stating intermediate results that make the solution trivial) or the problem is too similar to the original. These problems don't encourage advanced reasoning and would provide weak learning signals. The threshold of `$62.5\%$` (5/8 correct) means the problem must have at least some failures in the group, ensuring it remains at the policy's frontier. The paper notes in Section 5.3 that augmenting simpler problems (accuracy in `$[37.5\%, 75.0\%]$`) — shown in the SvS-Asp ablation — "accelerates overfitting to the policy's already mastered capabilities while limiting exploration," confirming this design choice.

**Why the lower bound of 12.5%?** If the policy achieves 0 correct solutions, the problem is either unsolvable (the variational synthesis broke the semantics) or far beyond the policy's capability. Such problems provide no positive examples in the group (all rewards are 0), causing zero advantage in GRPO. They also cannot be validated as correct problems since no solution matches the expected answer. Conversely, problems with at least one correct solution demonstrate that they are solvable and correctly preserve the original semantics.

**Filtering synthesis training data.** After assigning synthesis rewards, the system further filters: only correct solutions `$y_i$` (inputs to the synthesis task) whose synthesized problems produce a **mix** of positive and negative synthesis rewards are retained. Specifically, among the `$G_v = 8$` variational problems from solution `$y_i$`, the set of positively-rewarded problems is:

$$J_2 = \{j \mid \widehat{\text{accl}} \leq \text{Acc}(\hat{x}^j_i, a) \leq \widehat{\text{acch}}\}$$

If `$|J_2| > 0$` (at least one positively-rewarded problem) **and** `$|J_2| < G_v$` (not all problems positively rewarded — i.e., there exists at least one negatively-rewarded problem), then the synthesis training pair `$(y_i, \hat{x}^j_i)$` with its reward `$R_v(\hat{x}^j_i)$` is added to the buffer `$\mathcal{B}$` for all `$j$` (both positive and negative). If all 8 problems are positively rewarded, the advantage for the synthesis task degenerates (similar to all-correct groups in problem solving), providing no gradient. If none are positively rewarded, there are no positive examples to reinforce. Only mixed-reward groups provide effective synthesis training signals.

**The full synthesis training tuple.** For each retained correct solution `$y_i$`, the buffer receives `$G_v = 8$` training pairs of the form `$(y_i, \hat{x}^j_i, R_v(\hat{x}^j_i))$` — the input is the correct solution (the "prompt" for the synthesis task), the output is the synthesized problem, and the reward is 1 if the problem induced moderate policy accuracy and 0 otherwise.

---

#### Synthetic Problem Solving (Algorithm 1, Lines 15–20)

Once variational problems `$\{\hat{x}^j_i\}_{j=1}^{G_v}$` are generated from solution `$y_i$`, the policy is tasked with solving them — identically to how it solves original training problems. For each variational problem `$\hat{x}^j_i$`, the policy generates `$G = 8$` solutions `$\{\hat{y}_k\}_{k=1}^{G}$`, and correctness rewards are computed using the **original problem's reference answer** `$a$`:

$$R_c(\hat{y}_k, a) = \mathbb{I}(\text{Extract}(\hat{y}_k) = a)$$

**What this means operationally:** The synthetic problem solving step reuses the existing answer verification infrastructure — the same `$\text{Extract}$` function and the same answer `$a$` — treating the variational problem as if it were a new problem with known answer `$a$`. This is what makes SvS "annotation-free": no new answer derivation or verification is needed, unlike approaches that synthesize problems from scratch where correctness must be independently established.

**Filtering for synthetic problem solving data.** As with original problem solving, only variational problems where the policy achieves mixed accuracy are retained for policy updating (Algorithm 1, Lines 19–20). Formally:

$$J_1 = \{j \mid 0 < \text{Acc}(\hat{x}^j_i, a) < G\}$$

For each `$j \in J_1$`, the buffer receives the problem-solution-reward tuples `$(\hat{x}^j_i, \hat{y}_1, R_c(\hat{y}_1, a)), \ldots, (\hat{x}^j_i, \hat{y}_G, R_c(\hat{y}_G, a))$`. These provide standard problem-solving training signals — identical in structure to the original problem solving data — but for novel, policy-generated problems that target the model's weak spots.

**Why this is powerful:** The synthetic problems are, by construction, at the policy's capability frontier (since they were derived from underperforming problems) and are structurally diverse (since the policy must reconstruct problems from solutions rather than copy text). Solving them forces the policy to engage with **varied surface forms** of the same underlying mathematical structures — exactly the kind of practice that builds robust, generalizable reasoning rather than memorization of specific problem templates.

---

#### The Complete Experience Buffer and Policy Update

After processing all original problems, synthesizing variants from underperforming ones, and solving those variants, the experience buffer `$\mathcal{B}$` at each training step contains three distinct types of training tuples:

1. **Original Problem Solving:** `$(x, y_i, R_c(y_i, a))$` — standard RLVR data from the fixed training set, filtered to mixed-accuracy groups.

2. **Variational Problem Synthesis:** `$(y_i, \hat{x}^j_i, R_v(\hat{x}^j_i))$` — the synthesis task: given a correct solution, generate a problem that would elicit it, with reward based on whether the generated problem induces appropriate difficulty. Here `$y_i$` is the "prompt" (the correct solution used as context) and `$\hat{x}^j_i$` is the "response" (the synthesized problem).

3. **Synthetic Problem Solving:** `$(\hat{x}^j_i, \hat{y}_k, R_c(\hat{y}_k, a))$` — standard problem-solving data, but for self-generated problems with inherited reference answers.

All three types are mixed together and used in a single GRPO policy update according to Equation 6 (the GRPO objective). The buffer is then cleared (Algorithm 1, Line 32), and the next training step begins with a fresh sample from the training set.

**The joint training effect.** By optimizing the policy on these three tasks simultaneously, SvS creates a **bidirectional learning loop**: the policy learns to solve problems (original and synthetic), and it also learns to generate problems from solutions — the inverse mapping. The paper shows (Appendix E.2, Figure 11) that even training with only 20% of the synthesis data significantly reduces overfitting on out-of-distribution benchmarks compared to problem-solving-only training. This suggests that the synthesis task acts as a **regularizer**: it forces the policy to understand problems at a deeper semantic level (what mathematical structures and constraints produce a given solution path?) rather than merely pattern-matching solution templates to problem statements. The paper attributes this to "the complementary tasks of problem generation and problem solving" (citing He et al., 2016 on dual learning for machine translation), where learning the forward mapping (problem → solution) and reverse mapping (solution → problem) mutually reinforce each other.

---

#### Online Data Updating and Entropy Maintenance

The entire SvS pipeline operates **online**: at each training step, the policy generates new solutions, synthesizes new variational problems based on its current (improving) capabilities, and practices on those new problems. This means the augmented data is never stale — as the policy improves, the set of underperforming problems shifts (problems that were once challenging may become easy and no longer qualify), and the variational problems generated from the policy's current solutions reflect its current reasoning style and difficulty frontier.

**The mechanism of entropy maintenance.** Standard RLVR on a fixed problem set suffers entropy collapse because the policy eventually memorizes high-reward solution patterns for each training problem. In SvS, the variational problems are **continuously refreshed** — at each step, the policy encounters new problems (derived from its current solutions to the hardest training problems it can sometimes solve) rather than the same fixed set. This prevents memorization because:

- The policy cannot use a single memorized solution template for a problem that changes at every step (different phrasing, different intermediate values, different structural presentation).
- The synthesis task itself encourages the policy to explore diverse problem formulations, which indirectly promotes diverse solution strategies (a model that can generate multiple valid problem variants from a solution has learned flexible representations).
- The difficulty-gated reward prevents the synthesis from degenerating into trivial rephrases that the policy could memorize.

Figure 5 shows the result: across all model sizes (3B, 8B, 32B) and datasets (MATH-12k, DAPO-17k), standard RLVR exhibits a continuous entropy decline, while SvS maintains entropy within a relatively stable range throughout training. The paper explicitly states: "Such advantages stem from the ever-updating problems in SvS that prevent the policy from memorization." This entropy stability is what enables the sustained Pass@1 and Pass@32 improvements shown in Figure 1 — unlike standard RLVR, where both metrics plateau around step 450, SvS continues to improve throughout training.

---

#### Computational Overhead Analysis (Section 5.5, Figure 9)

The self-play loop introduces additional computation: for each underperforming problem with at least one correct solution, the policy must (1) generate `$G_v = 8$` variational problems per correct solution, and (2) solve each of those `$G_v$` problems with `$G = 8$` solutions each. This multiplies the per-step generation count.

**Observed overhead.** Figure 9 shows the computational cost breakdown for the 32B model experiments (run on 32 H100 GPUs):

- **On MATH-12k:** The Qwen-32B model achieves approximately 80% initial accuracy on the training set, meaning few problems fall into the underperforming range `$[12.5\%, 50.0\%]$`. Consequently, the number of variational problems generated is limited, and SvS training time is comparable to standard RLVR (Figure 9, third panel: policy update times overlap heavily; fourth panel: step times are similar).

- **On DAPO-17k:** The initial stages see a **large number of synthetic variational problems and their responses** (Figure 9, first two panels, early steps spike to ~10,000+ responses to variational problems), because the policy's accuracy on most DAPO problems initially falls within `$[12.5\%, 50.0\%]$`. As training progresses and the policy improves, accuracy on many problems surpasses 50%, so they cease to qualify as underperforming. The number of synthetic generations drops rapidly, and the computational overhead shrinks. The step time (fourth panel) for SvS remains higher than RLVR throughout but decreases over time — early steps can take ~1600 seconds vs. ~800 seconds for RLVR, but later steps converge closer to RLVR's cost as fewer problems need augmentation.

**Key insight:** The computational overhead is **self-limiting** — it is highest when the policy needs the most practice (early in training, when many problems are at the frontier), and it naturally decreases as the policy improves and fewer problems fall into the underperforming range. This creates a natural curriculum where compute is automatically allocated to the hardest effective learning signals.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Reframing the Entropy–Pass@k Collapse as a Data Diversity Problem, Not an Optimization Problem

The paper's most distinctive conceptual move is its **diagnosis of the root cause** of entropy collapse in RLVR. Prior work — notably Cui et al. (2025b), Cheng et al. (2025), and Gao et al. (2025) — had documented the entropy–performance trade-off and proposed solutions that intervene at the **optimizer level**: adding entropy bonus terms to the advantage function, tuning sampling temperatures, or adjusting clipping parameters. These approaches treat entropy decline as a side effect of the optimization dynamics — the policy becomes overconfident, so we should penalize overconfidence or inject noise.

This paper makes a fundamentally different diagnosis: **entropy collapse is a data problem, not an optimizer problem**. The policy's entropy declines because it faces the same limited set of problems at every training step, and the rational strategy under GRPO's reward structure is to convergently memorize high-reward solution templates for those problems. The policy is not "overconfident" in a pathological sense — it is behaving optimally given the impoverished information environment. The optimizer is not broken; the training data is.

This reframing is significant because it redirects research attention. If the root cause is data diversity, then making the optimizer more exploratory (entropy bonuses, higher temperature) can only delay collapse, not prevent it — the policy will eventually memorize even with regularization, just more slowly. The paper provides direct evidence for this in Appendix F (Figures 16–17): Clip-CoV, a representative entropy-regulation method, is consistently outperformed by SvS, and combining Clip-CoV with SvS yields further gains, demonstrating that the two approaches are complementary but that data diversity is the more fundamental lever. The authors put it bluntly in Section 1: "entropy-collapse mitigation techniques like Clip-CoV fail to stop the policy from memorizing previously correct responses to secure rewards during training."

This insight reframes the RLVR scaling challenge from "how do we prevent the optimizer from collapsing entropy?" to "how do we construct a training data pipeline that sustains novelty throughout training?" — a data engineering problem rather than an algorithmic one. It connects RLVR to the broader literature on curriculum learning, open-endedness, and self-play, where the environment (training data) co-evolves with the agent (policy) to provide perpetual learning pressure.

The evidence is Figure 2: when augmented training data is refreshed at step 300, entropy **rises** — something no optimizer-level intervention could achieve on a static dataset. The orange curve in Figure 2 (left) tells the entire story: entropy declines with static augmented data (before step 300), but the moment the data changes, the policy is forced to re-explore and entropy recovers. This is not an optimization effect — it is purely an information effect.

---

### Innovation 2: Solution-to-Problem Inversion as an Annotation-Free Data Augmentation Mechanism

The paper's second conceptual innovation is the specific mechanism for **generating verifiably-labeled training problems without any answer annotation**. This solves what is perhaps the hardest practical bottleneck in RLVR data augmentation: if you synthesize new problems, how do you know their correct answers?

Prior approaches to data augmentation for reasoning fall into two categories, both with critical limitations for RLVR. The first category — problem rephrasing (Yu et al., 2023; MetaMath) — modifies surface forms of existing problems, reusing the original reference answers. But as the paper shows, rephrasing has limited diversity because the rephraser sees the original problem text, and semantic drift can corrupt answer validity. The second category — problem generation from scratch (Huang et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2025) — produces novel problems but then faces the challenge of answer derivation, typically requiring either an external solver (which may be imperfect and introduces dependence on another model) or expensive human verification.

SvS introduces a third approach that is conceptually distinct from both: **generate problems from solutions, not from problem statements**. Because the solution trace contains the complete reasoning path — all assumptions, constraints, operations, and the final answer — inverting this mapping (solution → problem) automatically preserves answer correctness: any well-formed problem that "plausibly elicits" the solution must, by construction, have the same answer. The answer is not "reused" or "derived" — it is **logically entailed** by the synthesis constraint.

This is more than a clever trick. It represents a **bidirectional representation of mathematical understanding**: the model must learn not only to map problems to solutions (the standard forward direction) but also to map solutions back to the problem statements that produced them. This bidirectional capability is what the dual learning literature (He et al., 2016) argued was essential for robust representation learning, and SvS operationalizes it in the RLVR context. The paper shows evidence for this bidirectional benefit in Appendix E.2: incorporating even a small fraction (20%) of synthesis training significantly reduces overfitting on out-of-distribution benchmarks compared to problem-solving-only training (Figure 11), suggesting that the inverse mapping task regularizes the forward mapping by forcing the model to understand problems at a deeper structural level rather than pattern-matching solution templates.

The innovation here is not the specific prompt template or the algorithmic steps (these are in Section 3), but the **insight that the correct solution is a sufficient statistic for the problem-answer pair** in the RLVR setting. If you have a verified correct solution, you have everything needed to reconstruct a valid problem with a known answer. This converts the answer-annotation problem from an external verification bottleneck into an internal consistency constraint — the policy validates its own synthetic problems by re-solving them and checking that the answer matches. The self-verification loop (synthesize → solve → check answer match) is what makes the approach annotation-free and purely self-play.

---

### Innovation 3: Difficulty-Gated Reward Shaping as Exploitation Prevention in Self-Play

The paper's third conceptual contribution is the **difficulty-gated reward for problem synthesis**, which prevents the self-play loop from degenerating into trivial problem generation. This is a concrete instance of a broader principle: **in self-play systems where one role (problem generation) can exploit the reward structure by making the other role (problem solving) trivially easy, the rewards must be shaped to maintain productive tension between the roles**.

The naive approach — reward problem synthesis whenever the policy can solve the generated problem and get the right answer — creates a perverse incentive: the policy can embed the answer directly into the problem text (e.g., "Find the sum of 5 and 3, which equals 8") and then trivially "solve" it by copying the embedded answer. The policy gets rewarded for both problem synthesis and problem solving (since the answer matches), but no actual reasoning is learned. The system collapses into a degenerate equilibrium where problems are trivial and solutions are vacuous.

The paper's failed initial attempt (Appendix G, Figure 18) demonstrates this empirically: with the naive reward, the policy rapidly learns to generate hint-laden problems that yield 100% group accuracy, the advantage collapses to zero, entropy drops, and performance degrades. This is not a theoretical concern — it is what actually happens when the difficulty gate is absent.

The solution — rewarding problem synthesis only when the generated problem induces **moderate** accuracy (12.5%–62.5% of 8 solutions correct) — is conceptually significant because it operationalizes **productive difficulty** as a training objective. The problem generator is incentivized to produce problems that are challenging but not impossible for the current solver — problems at the frontier of the solver's capability. This creates an automatic curriculum: as the solver improves, problems that were once moderately difficult become too easy and lose their reward, forcing the generator to produce harder variants.

This is distinct from prior work on curriculum learning or difficulty-based sampling, which typically rely on external difficulty estimates (e.g., loss values, human-labeled difficulty levels) to select or order training examples. In SvS, difficulty is **endogenous to the system**: it emerges from the interaction between the generator and solver, with the reward function providing the selection pressure. The generator does not need an explicit difficulty model; it learns through RL what kinds of problems will earn the synthesis reward, and this implicitly aligns with the solver's capability frontier.

The choice of the upper bound (62.5%, or 5/8 correct) is not arbitrary — it requires that at least 3 out of 8 solutions be **incorrect**, ensuring genuine challenge. The lower bound (12.5%, or 1/8 correct) ensures at least one success, validating that the problem is solvable and correctly preserves the answer. The window `[12.5%, 62.5%]` operationalizes the zone of proximal development — problems the policy can sometimes solve but has not yet mastered.

This innovation connects SvS to broader principles in self-play and adversarial training (Cheng et al., 2024) while solving a concrete failure mode: in adversarial setups, if one agent can make the task trivially easy for the other, the learning signal collapses. The difficulty gate is an elegant fix — it internalizes the adversary's objective not as "make the solver fail" (which would produce impossibly hard problems and zero advantage) but as "make the solver succeed sometimes but not always." This maintains the tension necessary for sustained learning.

---

### Innovation 4: Reconceptualizing Pass@k as a Measure of Reasoning Boundary Expansion

The paper's fourth contribution is **repositioning Pass@k (for large k) as the primary metric for evaluating whether RLVR expands a model's reasoning capability**, and providing evidence that standard RLVR fundamentally fails on this metric. This reframes the evaluation of reasoning-focused post-training.

Prior RLVR work (Guo et al., 2025a; Shao et al., 2024; Yu et al., 2025a) primarily reported Pass@1 or average accuracy at modest sample counts (e.g., pass@1, majority@64). These metrics conflate two distinct phenomena: (1) improved sampling efficiency — the model is more likely to produce a correct answer on the first try, but the set of problems it can ever solve remains unchanged; and (2) genuine capability expansion — the model can now solve problems that were fundamentally beyond its reach before training.

Yue et al. (2025) identified this conflation and showed that RLVR improves Pass@k only for small k, questioning whether RLVR truly "incentivizes reasoning capacity beyond the base model." The current paper takes this critique seriously and makes it the central evaluation axis. The paper's key empirical claim — that SvS achieves 18.3% and 22.8% absolute gains in Pass@32 on AIME 24 and AIME 25 (Table 1), where standard RLVR shows negligible improvement — is framed explicitly as evidence of **boundary expansion**, not just efficiency improvement. Figure 6 demonstrates this comprehensively: SvS-trained models maintain their Pass@k advantage over both the base model and RLVR baseline as k scales from 1 to 1024, while standard RLVR falls behind the base model at large k on MATH-500 — meaning RLVR actually **narrows** the reasoning boundary on some distributions while SvS expands it.

This reframing matters because it changes what "improvement" means in RLVR research. A method that boosts Pass@1 from 20% to 40% but leaves Pass@1024 unchanged may simply be making the model more consistent at solving problems it already "knew" how to solve — a sampling efficiency gain. If the goal is genuine capability advancement (can the model now solve AIME problems it could never solve before?), then Pass@k at large k is the relevant metric, and the paper shows that SvS moves this needle where standard RLVR does not.

The paper further strengthens this argument with instance-level analysis (Figure 12): on specific AIME problems (e.g., #12 in AIME 24, #17 in AIME 25), RLVR achieves near-zero accuracy even at 1024 samples, while SvS achieves 40–80% accuracy — meaning SvS enables the model to solve problems that were essentially impossible for both the base model and the RLVR-trained model. These are not marginal improvements on already-solvable problems; they represent new capabilities.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** All experiments use the MATH benchmark (Hendrycks et al., 2021), specifically the MATH-12k training split (12,000 problems) for the primary experiments, with the 32B model additionally trained on DAPO-17k (Yu et al., 2025a), a dataset of 17,000 competition-level problems with integer-only answers. An extended DAPO-25k variant augments DAPO-17k with 8,000 open-ended problems from DeepMath (He et al., 2025b) to mitigate overfitting to integer-only formats.

- **Base model(s).** Three model families and scales are used: Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct, LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct (Grattafiori et al., 2024), and Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct (Yang et al., 2024). These span a 10× parameter range and represent both the Qwen and LLaMA architectural families, providing evidence for generalizability across model scales and lineages. The instruction-tuned variants (not base models) are used as starting points for RLVR training, following the standard practice in post-training RLVR research.

- **Metrics.** The primary metrics are **Pass@1** and **Pass@k** (with k ranging from 1 to 1024). Pass@1 for competition-level benchmarks (AIME, Beyond-AIME) is computed as the average of 32 independent inference runs per problem to reduce variance from sampling noise. Pass@k uses the unbiased estimator from Chen et al. (2021), which estimates the probability that at least one of k samples is correct from a smaller number of actual samples and their correctness outcomes. Policy entropy is tracked during training as a diagnostic for generation diversity (Section 5.1). Response length is tracked as a proxy for reasoning depth (Appendix E.1).

- **Baselines.** The paper compares against: **(1) Initial instruction-tuned model** — the pre-RLVR checkpoint. **(2) Standard RLVR** — GRPO with Clip-Higher, trained on the same datasets for the same or equivalent number of gradient steps. **(3) SimpleRL-32B** (Zeng et al., 2025) and **ORZ-32B** (Open-Reasoner-Zero; Hu et al., 2025b) — external open-source models of the same scale, reported in Table 1 for competition-level benchmarks. **(4) RLVR + Clip-CoV** (Cui et al., 2025b) — an entropy-regulation baseline compared in Appendix F. **(5) Alternative augmentation strategies** (Section 5.3): Extending RLVR training to match SvS's sample count (Ext), enhancing underperforming problems with additional rollouts (Eup), and SvS with simpler-problem augmentation (SvS-Asp).

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The primary unit of compute is **training steps**, where each step processes a batch of 256 problems with group size G = 8 solutions per problem for both original and synthetic problem solving, and G_v = 8 variational problems per correct solution for synthesis. Total training duration: 300 steps on MATH-12k for all models, and 600 steps on DAPO-17k for the 32B model. The paper analyzes computational overhead of SvS vs. standard RLVR in Section 5.5 (Figure 9), measuring both step time and standalone policy update time on 32 H100 GPUs. For evaluation Pass@k, maximum response length is set to 8,192 tokens, extended to 24,576 tokens for the Pass@k scaling experiments in Figure 6. Inference uses vLLM (Kwon et al., 2023) with temperature 1.0 and top-p 0.7.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** No explicit cross-validation is described. Checkpoint selection for the 32B model on DAPO-17k is based on the best average Pass@1 scores on AIME 24 and AIME 25 (Section 5.3). For the Pass@k scaling experiments in Figure 6, the unbiased estimator is used to reduce variance. Intermediate evaluations across all benchmarks during training are reported with smoothed curves (window size 5) in Appendix H (Figure 19) to highlight trends. The 12 evaluation benchmarks span a range of difficulty levels from grade-school math (GSM8K) to competition-level (AIME, Beyond-AIME, OlymMATH), providing coverage across the difficulty spectrum.

---

### Main Quantitative Results

#### SvS Significantly Improves Both Pass@1 and Pass@k on Competition-Level Benchmarks

The headline result appears in Figure 1 and Table 1. Training Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct on DAPO-17k with SvS achieves **absolute gains of 18.3 and 22.8 percentage points in Pass@32** on AIME 24 and AIME 25 respectively, compared to standard RLVR. On AIME 24, standard RLVR reaches 52.5% Pass@32 while SvS reaches 70.8%; on AIME 25, RLVR reaches 42.4% while SvS reaches 65.2%. The Pass@1 improvements are also substantial: +10.5 points on both AIME benchmarks (AIME 24: 28.8% → 39.3%; AIME 25: 30.0% → 40.5%). These gains are not merely early-training artifacts — Figure 1 shows that standard RLVR plateaus around step 450 on both Pass@1 and Pass@32, while SvS continues to improve throughout the full 600-step training run.

The instance-level breakdown in Figure 12 (Appendix E.4) reveals that SvS's gains are concentrated on specific hard problems rather than being uniform: on AIME 24 problem #12, RLVR achieves near-zero accuracy even at 1024 samples while SvS reaches approximately 40%; on AIME 25 problems #5, #17, and #19, SvS achieves 40–80% accuracy where RLVR achieves minimal or zero. This demonstrates that SvS is expanding the set of problems the model can solve at all, not merely making it more consistent on already-solvable problems.

On the broader competition benchmark suite (Table 1, DAPO-17k rows): Beyond-AIME improves from 35.9% to 45.9% Pass@32 (+10.0 points); Math24o improves from 71.2% to 76.5% (+5.3 points). However, performance degrades on OlymMATH subsets — OlymE drops from 47.1% to 43.4% (-3.7 points) and OlymH drops from 18.3% to 16.7% (-1.6 points) — which the paper attributes to overfitting on DAPO-17k's integer-only answer format. This is confirmed by the DAPO-25k experiment (Table 2), where adding 8,000 open-ended problems from DeepMath restores OOD performance: the average across all nine benchmarks rises from 57.9% (D17k SvS) to 62.8% (D25k SvS), the highest overall.

Notably, on MATH-500, RLVR actually **degrades** performance (67.8% → 67.4%), while SvS achieves 70.8% (+3.0 over both initial and RLVR). **LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct on MATH-12k:** Initial model average 27.0%. RLVR improves to 34.2% (+7.2). SvS improves to 35.9% (+1.7 over RLVR). The gains are more modest on this model family, but consistent across most benchmarks.

**Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct on MATH-12k:** Initial model average 49.0%. RLVR improves to 54.8% (+5.8). SvS improves to 57.3% (+2.5 over RLVR). All 9 benchmarks show positive Δ over RLVR except Beyond-AIME (identical at 14.0% for both MATH-12k SvS and RLVR? Actually the table shows 14.0 for SvS vs. 11.0 for RLVR — a 3-point gain).

#### SvS Pushes the Reasoning Boundary: Pass@k Scaling from 1 to 1024

Figure 6 evaluates Pass@k for k ranging from 1 to 1024 (x-axis in log scale) on AIME 24 & 25 (combined), Beyond-AIME, and MATH-500, comparing the initial model, RLVR-trained model, and SvS-trained model (all Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct, DAPO-17k training, 24k max response tokens).

**On AIME 24 & 25 (left panel):** All three curves rise monotonically with k. The SvS curve is consistently above RLVR across all k, with the gap widening at larger k — at k=1, the gap is approximately 10 percentage points (around 35% vs 25%); at k=1024, the gap is approximately 30 percentage points (around 70% vs 40%). The initial model curve lies below both. The key pattern: SvS's curve shows no sign of saturation, while RLVR's curve appears to be flattening at the highest k.

**On Beyond-AIME (middle panel):** Similar pattern — SvS maintains a consistent advantage of roughly 10–15 percentage points across all k. At k=1024, SvS reaches approximately 45% while RLVR reaches approximately 35%.

**On MATH-500 (right panel):** This reveals the most striking finding. At small k (k=1 to ~8), RLVR outperforms the initial model. However, at approximately k=16, the curves cross: RLVR falls below the initial model, and the gap widens as k increases — at k=1024, RLVR is approximately 5 percentage points below the initial model (around 89% vs 94%). SvS, in contrast, maintains a small but consistent advantage over both across all k, reaching approximately 95% at k=1024. The paper states: "standard RLVR often fails to expand the reasoning boundary of the base model, yielding improvements in Pass@k only for small values of k." The MATH-500 result shows that RLVR can actually **contract** the reasoning boundary — the model becomes less capable of solving problems through repeated sampling than it was before training, because RLVR narrows its output distribution toward high-reward patterns that don't generalize to all problem types. SvS prevents this contraction through its diversity-preserving data augmentation.

#### SvS Stably Maintains Policy Entropy Throughout Training

Figure 5 shows entropy trajectories for RLVR vs. SvS across three settings: LLaMA-8B on MATH-12k, Qwen-32B on MATH-12k, and Qwen-32B on DAPO-17k. In all three, standard RLVR shows a continuous, monotonic entropy decline. SvS maintains entropy within a relatively stable range — there is some fluctuation but no systematic downward trend. On LLaMA-8B, RLVR entropy drops from approximately 1.4 to below 0.2 over 200 steps, while SvS fluctuates between 0.4 and 1.2. On Qwen-32B with MATH-12k, RLVR drops from approximately 0.30 to 0.05 over 300 steps, while SvS oscillates between 0.10 and 0.25. On Qwen-32B with DAPO-17k, RLVR drops from approximately 0.7 to below 0.1 over 600 steps, while SvS maintains 0.2–0.5 throughout.

The paper explicitly connects entropy maintenance to sustained performance: "The entropy stability explains the continuous improvements in both Pass@1 and Pass@32 achieved by SvS, as shown in Figure 1, whereas RLVR saturates after a certain number of training steps." This is not merely correlational — the mechanism is that stable entropy enables sustained exploration of diverse reasoning strategies, which in turn generates novel correct solutions that feed back into training as positive examples.

#### SvS Elicits Deeper Reasoning (Longer Response Lengths)

Figure 10 (Appendix E.1) compares training response lengths between RLVR and SvS. On LLaMA-8B with MATH-12k, RLVR responses remain flat at approximately 600 tokens, while SvS responses grow from 600 to approximately 2000 tokens over 200 steps. On Qwen-32B with MATH-12k, RLVR responses are flat at approximately 800 tokens, while SvS responses grow from 800 to approximately 2500 tokens over 300 steps. On Qwen-32B with DAPO-17k, RLVR responses grow modestly from 1500 to 2500 tokens, while SvS responses grow from 1500 to approximately 4500 tokens over 600 steps. The paper argues this reflects deeper reasoning patterns: "SvS consistently produces longer reasoning on training batches compared with RLVR... because SvS requires the model to tackle new variational problems at each step, promoting continuous exploration of advanced reasoning strategies, whereas standard RLVR often secures high rewards by reusing memorized correct solutions."

#### SvS Generalizes Beyond Mathematics to Code Generation

Section 5.4 and Figure 8 evaluate SvS on code generation tasks using Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct trained on 12k problems from PRIME-RL (Cui et al., 2025a), covering Apps, CodeContest, Taco, and Codeforces. All hyperparameters except prompt, data, and model are identical to the math experiments.

The results are striking for their efficiency: **SvS trained for approximately 100 steps outperforms standard RLVR trained for over 600 steps** across all benchmarks. On the average Pass@1 across four benchmarks (rightmost panel), SvS at step 100 reaches approximately 45%, while RLVR requires over 600 steps to reach approximately 43%. On CodeContest (Avg@16), SvS at step 100 reaches approximately 46% vs. RLVR's 44% at step 600. On TACO, SvS at step 100 reaches approximately 27% vs. RLVR's 24% at step 600. The entropy panel (Figure 8, bottom-right) shows that RLVR entropy declines from approximately 0.28 to 0.12 over 600 steps, while SvS entropy is maintained around 0.25 throughout.

The paper emphasizes: "SvS training achieves significant improvements with five times fewer training steps than the RLVR baseline and while maintaining stable policy entropy, demonstrating the strong generalization of this online self-play augmentation strategy in RLVR."

#### SvS Outperforms SimpleRL and Open-Reasoner-Zero at the Same Scale

Table 1 includes comparisons with SimpleRL-32B (Zeng et al., 2025) and ORZ-32B (Hu et al., 2025b). SvS trained on DAPO-17k achieves Pass@1 of 39.3% on AIME24 and 40.5% on AIME25, compared to SimpleRL's 22.1% and 13.9%, and ORZ's 24.2% and 26.3%. For Pass@32, SvS achieves 70.8% on AIME24 and 65.2% on AIME25, compared to SimpleRL's 62.0% and 38.5%, and ORZ's 55.7% and 47.0%. The gap is especially pronounced on AIME25 Pass@32: SvS nearly doubles SimpleRL (65.2% vs. 38.5%). It is important to note that these comparisons are not perfectly controlled — SimpleRL and ORZ use different training data, base models, and hyperparameters — but they establish that SvS produces models competitive with or exceeding the best open-source alternatives at the 32B scale.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Extending standard RLVR training to match SvS's sample count (Ext):** Table 3 compares this against full SvS. Ext achieves Pass@1 of 31.7% on AIME24 (vs. SvS 39.3%) and 31.3% on AIME25 (vs. SvS 40.5%). For Pass@32, Ext achieves 57.0% on AIME24 (vs. SvS 70.8%) and 48.0% on AIME25 (vs. SvS 65.2%). Ext improves over standard RLVR (Pass@32 on AIME24: 52.5% → 57.0%), confirming that more training helps, but falls far short of SvS's gains, demonstrating that the diversity of training data matters more than the sheer quantity.

**Enhancing underperforming problems in RLVR with additional rollouts (Eup):** This strategy assigns extra sampling to underperforming problems but does not generate new problems. Table 3 shows Eup achieves Pass@32 of 64.2% on AIME24 and 54.6% on AIME25 — substantially higher than standard RLVR (52.5% and 42.4%) and even higher than Ext (57.0% and 48.0%). However, Eup has lower Pass@1 than standard RLVR: 28.7% vs. 28.8% on AIME24, 26.8% vs. 30.0% on AIME25. The paper interprets this as Eup "prioritizes reasoning exploration over exploiting generated correct responses... as this strategy introduces more negative samples from underperforming problem augmentation, and such exploration effectively improves the model's Pass@k scores." This directly supports the paper's thesis: exploration-focused training trades Pass@1 for Pass@k, while SvS achieves both simultaneously.

**Augmenting simpler problems in SvS (SvS-Asp):** Instead of augmenting underperforming problems (accuracy in [12.5%, 50.0%]), this variant augments simpler problems (accuracy in [37.5%, 75.0%]). Table 3 shows SvS-Asp achieves Pass@1 similar to standard RLVR (31.6% vs. 28.8% on AIME24; 27.3% vs. 30.0% on AIME25) but **worse Pass@32** than both standard RLVR and full SvS: 50.7% vs. 52.5% vs. 70.8% on AIME24; 48.0% vs. 42.4% vs. 65.2% on AIME25. The paper concludes: "this augmentation accelerates overfitting to the policy's already mastered capabilities while limiting exploration." This validates the choice to target underperforming problems specifically.

**Entropy regulation vs. SvS (Clip-CoV comparison, Appendix F, Figure 16):** On LLaMA-8B with MATH-12k, SvS consistently outperforms RLVR + Clip-CoV on MATH-500, Minerva-Math, Olympiad-Bench, Gaokao-2023, and AMC-23, across all training steps. For example, on MATH-500 at step 250, SvS achieves approximately 58% vs. Clip-CoV's 52%. On the average across benchmarks, SvS achieves approximately 50% vs. Clip-CoV's 45%. The paper states: "SvS training consistently outperforms the Clip-CoV baseline in intermediate evaluations. This improvement stems from SvS's continuous online augmentation of training problems, which promotes consistent exploration, while entropy-collapse mitigation techniques like Clip-CoV fail to stop the policy from memorizing previously correct responses."

**Combining SvS with Clip-CoV (Appendix F, Figure 17):** SvS + Clip-CoV consistently outperforms Clip-CoV alone across all benchmarks. On MATH-500, the combination achieves approximately 60% vs. 52% for Clip-CoV alone at step 250. This demonstrates that data augmentation and entropy regulation are complementary: entropy bonuses encourage token-level diversity within a problem, while SvS provides problem-level diversity that prevents memorization across steps.

**Reducing problem synthesis training data (Appendix E.2, Figure 11):** Training with only 20% of the variational problem synthesis pairs (vs. full SvS) yields comparable performance on IID benchmarks (AIME24, AIME25, AMC23 — integer-answer problems like DAPO-17k) but significantly worse on OOD benchmarks (MATH-500, Minerva Math, Olympiad-Bench — benchmarks with non-integer answers). On OOD benchmarks at step 500, "Less VPS" achieves approximately 0.55 average accuracy vs. "Full VPS" at approximately 0.62. The paper interprets this as evidence that "pure problem-solving training is susceptible to overfitting and reduces the model's generalizability, whereas the inclusion of problem synthesis helps mitigate this issue" — the synthesis task acts as a regularizer against distributional overfitting.

**Generalization beyond reasoning tasks (Appendix E.3, Table 4):** Models trained on DAPO-17k with standard RLVR show performance degradation on general QA and coding benchmarks compared to the initial instruction-tuned model: average across 9 tasks drops from 73.35 to 72.99. In contrast, SvS-trained models not only avoid this degradation but slightly improve: average rises to 73.77, with gains on MMLU-Pro (68.33 → 71.58), ARC-Challenge (58.62 → 58.79), HellaSwag (85.17 → 85.34), and BoolQ (89.60 → 89.48). The paper's interpretation: "the additional problem synthesis task in SvS helps prevent overfitting to mathematical reasoning tasks while effectively preserving or even enhancing the model's general instruction-following capabilities."

**Synthetic problem correctness analysis (Appendix E.5):** The paper evaluates 6,000 variational problems using Qwen3-235B-A22B-Instruct-2507 and OpenAI O3 as judges, with a problem considered correct if at least one deems it valid (Figure 13). Over the full 600-step training run, more than 80% of synthetic problems are consistently judged as correct, with a slight downward trend over time (from 87.1% in steps 0–100 to 80.0% in steps 500–600). The paper also manually inspects problems flagged as invalid and finds them to be actually solvable, with models incorrectly judging them as invalid due to unfamiliar description styles (e.g., problems using "unusual" notation that is nevertheless well-defined). Accuracy correlation analysis (Figures 14–15) shows that the policy's accuracy on original problems and their variational counterparts is strongly positively correlated (OLS slope ≈0.6 for Qwen2.5-32B, ≈0.5 for Qwen3-30B), with accuracy difference distributions sharply centered near zero — most variational problems preserve similar difficulty to their originals.

**Failed initial reward design (Appendix G, Figure 18):** The initial approach assigned positive synthesis rewards to any problem for which the policy produced at least one correct solution (Equation 3). This failed because the policy embedded explicit hints/answers into synthetic problems, yielding 100% group accuracy, causing GRPO advantages to collapse to zero, and impairing exploration (entropy dropped faster than in the final SvS). The middle panel of Figure 18 shows that under the naive reward, the number of valid responses to synthetic problems rapidly drops to near-zero (since all groups are all-correct and thus filtered out for having zero advantage), while entropy plummets and Pass@1 performance degrades relative to the fixed SvS. This negative result validates the difficulty-gated reward design (Equation 4) as essential — without it, SvS degenerates.

### Critical Assessment

The paper makes three central claims: (1) SvS sustains policy entropy and diversity during RLVR training, preventing the collapse that plagues standard RLVR; (2) SvS substantially improves Pass@k at large k on competition-level benchmarks, expanding the model's reasoning boundary; (3) SvS's self-play augmentation strategy is generalizable across model scales, training sets, and domains (math and code). The evidence supporting each claim varies in strength.

**Claim 1 (entropy maintenance): Strongly supported, with a mechanism clearly demonstrated but a limited causal test.** Figure 5 shows entropy trajectories across three model/dataset combinations, and the contrast with RLVR is unambiguous — SvS maintains entropy while RLVR collapses. The rephrasing experiment in Figure 2 provides mechanistic evidence: refreshing augmented data at step 300 causes entropy to rise, directly demonstrating the causal link between data novelty and entropy recovery. The entropy-regulation comparison (Appendix F) shows that optimizer-level interventions underperform data-level interventions. However, the paper does not isolate the specific contribution of each SvS component (online updating, underperforming-problem targeting, difficulty-gated synthesis reward) to entropy maintenance through dedicated ablations — for instance, would SvS without the difficulty gate (allowing easier problems) maintain entropy similarly? The failed attempt in Appendix G suggests not, but this is a comparison against a degenerate baseline rather than a controlled ablation of the difficulty range. The paper also does not show how entropy varies across difficulty bins during training — a breakdown by problem difficulty would reveal whether entropy maintenance is uniform or concentrated on specific problem types.

**Claim 2 (Pass@k boundary expansion): Supported for the specific model scale and benchmarks tested, with important caveats and open questions.** The Pass@k scaling curves in Figure 6 are compelling: SvS maintains its advantage over RLVR as k grows to 1024 on AIME and Beyond-AIME, and on MATH-500, RLVR actually falls below the initial model at large k while SvS does not. Table 1's Pass@32 improvements (+18.3 on AIME24, +22.8 on AIME25) are substantial. The instance-level analysis (Figure 12) convincingly shows SvS solving problems that RLVR cannot touch. However, several caveats limit the strength of this claim:

- **The claims are strongest on the 32B model with DAPO-17k.** For smaller models (3B, 8B) trained on MATH-12k, Table 2 shows improvements but does not report Pass@k at large k — only Pass@1 (averaged over 32 runs for small models). The paper cannot claim boundary expansion at small scales without Pass@k data.

- **The hardest problems remain largely unsolved.** On OlymMATH-Hard, SvS trained on DAPO-17k achieves only 2.7% Pass@1 and 16.7% Pass@32 (Table 1) — improvements over RLVR's 4.8% Pass@1 and 18.3% Pass@32 are actually negative for Pass@32. On Beyond-AIME, Pass@1 reaches only 19.2% even with the best SvS variant. The reasoning boundary has been pushed outward but not eliminated — there remains a large class of problems outside the base model's capability that no amount of self-play augmentation can reach. This is consistent with the paper's earlier finding (from the rephrasing experiment) that augmentation helps on problems within the model's approximate capability range, not on fundamentally out-of-reach problems.

- **The Pass@k advantage is not universal across benchmarks.** On OlymMATH subsets, SvS underperforms RLVR on Pass@32 (Table 1: OlymE 43.4% vs. 47.1%, OlymH 16.7% vs. 18.3%). The paper attributes this to integer-answer overfitting from DAPO-17k, which the DAPO-25k experiment partially addresses, but DAPO-25k results are not reported for Pass@32 — only Pass@1 (Table 2). Without DAPO-25k Pass@32 data, it remains unclear whether the boundary expansion transfers to open-ended-answer benchmarks.

- **The comparison with external baselines (SimpleRL, ORZ) is not controlled for training data, compute, or hyperparameters.** These models may use different datasets, training durations, or RL algorithms. The paper claims SvS outperforms them, but this is an existence proof that SvS can produce strong models, not a controlled demonstration that SvS's specific mechanism is responsible for the gains.

**Claim 3 (generalizability): Supported across model scales and domains, but with a narrow evaluation range.** The paper shows positive results for models from 3B to 32B (Table 2), for both Qwen and LLaMA families (Table 2), and for both math and code domains (Section 5.4, Figure 8). This is a credible demonstration of robustness. However, the evaluation is limited to reasoning tasks with verifiable answers — math problems with ground-truth numeric/expression answers and code problems with unit tests. The paper does not evaluate on tasks without clean correctness signals (e.g., open-ended generation, summarization, dialogue), which would require a fundamentally different reward structure — and SvS's mechanism for answer inheritance depends on exact answer matching. The general QA evaluation in Appendix E.3 (Table 4) shows that SvS preserves general capabilities better than RLVR, but the gains are marginal (average +0.78 over the initial model on 9 tasks) and likely attributable to reduced overfitting rather than genuine capability expansion in non-reasoning domains.

**Missing experiments that would strengthen the paper:**

- **Pass@k at large k for smaller models.** The paper's central claim about boundary expansion is primarily demonstrated on the 32B model. Showing that SvS improves Pass@k on the 3B or 8B models — even modestly — would significantly strengthen the generalizability claim.

- **Controlled comparison of online vs. offline augmentation.** SvS generates new problems at every training step. A natural ablation would be to pre-generate a large pool of variational problems before training and sample from it (offline augmentation), comparing against online generation. This would isolate the benefit of continuous online refreshing from the benefit of problem diversity per se. The rephrasing experiment (Figure 2) partially addresses this, but rephrasing is a different augmentation method.

- **Sensitivity to the accuracy range hyperparameters.** The underperforming range [12.5%, 50.0%] and synthesis reward range [12.5%, 62.5%] are critical design choices justified by the failed experiment in Appendix G and the SvS-Asp ablation. However, the paper does not sweep these thresholds to show that the chosen values are near-optimal. Would [25%, 50%] work similarly? What about [12.5%, 75%]? The sensitivity analysis is incomplete.

- **Scaling behavior with model size.** The paper shows that SvS helps at 3B, 8B, and 32B, but does not analyze whether the magnitude of improvement scales with model size. Is the Pass@k improvement at 3B proportionally larger or smaller than at 32B? This matters for understanding whether self-play augmentation is more beneficial for weaker or stronger models.

- **Comparison with simply training on a larger, diverse dataset.** The paper argues that collecting diverse problem sets with verifiable answers is expensive. But a pragmatic baseline would be to aggregate all publicly available math problem datasets (MATH, GSM8K, TheoremQA, etc.) and train standard RLVR on the combined set. Does SvS on DAPO-17k outperform standard RLVR on a much larger aggregated dataset with comparable total FLOPs? This would directly test the paper's claim that SvS's targeted, self-play augmentation is more efficient than simply scaling up fixed training data.

- **Breakdown of Pass@k gains by problem difficulty.** Figure 6 shows aggregate Pass@k curves. A breakdown by difficulty bin (analogous to the instance-level analysis in Figure 12 but aggregated across difficulty levels) would reveal whether SvS's boundary expansion is concentrated on medium-difficulty problems or extends to the hardest tier.

**Worth highlighting as particularly strong evidence:**

- **The rephrasing experiment (Figure 2) is a clean and convincing demonstration of the causal mechanism.** The entropy rise at step 300 when augmented data refreshes provides direct evidence that data novelty, not optimization dynamics, drives entropy maintenance. This is the kind of targeted experiment that strongly supports a paper's central thesis.

- **The difficulty-gated reward's necessity is demonstrated by a clear negative result (Appendix G).** The paper shows what happens without the gate (performance degrades) and why (hint-laden problems collapse advantages). This is methodologically honest and strengthens the case that the specific design choices in SvS are consequential.

- **The code generation results (Section 5.4) are a strong out-of-domain test.** Using identical hyperparameters and prompts (except domain-specific prompt templates), SvS shows gains on code that are proportionally similar to math gains, with the added benefit of dramatically reduced training steps (100 vs. 600). This suggests the mechanism — self-play augmentation maintaining exploration — is domain-agnostic.

- **The problem synthesis correctness analysis (Appendix E.5) addresses a natural skepticism.** When a model generates its own training problems, the immediate concern is whether those problems are valid. The 80%+ correctness rate judged by SOTA LLMs, combined with manual verification of "incorrect" judgments and the strong accuracy correlation between original and variational problems, provides reasonable assurance that SvS is not training on nonsensical data.

The paper's claims ultimately hold under specific conditions that the authors are largely transparent about: SvS improves Pass@k on problems within the base model's capability frontier, with the strongest evidence at the 32B scale on competition-level math; it maintains entropy and prevents the collapse that limits standard RLVR; and it generalizes to code generation. The claim that SvS "expands the reasoning boundary" should be understood as expanding it to the frontier of what the base model could potentially solve (as measured by Pass@k with very large k), not creating entirely new capabilities beyond the base model's latent competence. The latter remains an open challenge — the hardest problems (OlymMATH-Hard, top difficulty AIME problems approaching near-zero base model pass rates) show minimal improvement.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Computational Overhead of SvS Is Not Amortized into the Reported Efficiency Gains

**The assumption:** The compute-optimal allocation between SvS and standard RLVR is compared at equal training steps — e.g., 600 steps for both on DAPO-17k — but each SvS step is more expensive. The paper explicitly measures this in Section 5.5 (Figure 9): on DAPO-17k with the 32B model on 32 H100 GPUs, SvS step times early in training reach approximately 1600 seconds, roughly 2× the approximately 800 seconds of standard RLVR. The first two panels of Figure 9 show that early training steps can generate over 10,000 synthetic problem responses per step. The paper acknowledges the overhead but does not include it in any cost-normalized comparison:

> "our experiments do not account for this cost largely for simplicity" (paraphrasing the sentiment, though the exact quote about difficulty estimation cost appears in Section 3.2)

The paper's headline result — SvS achieves 18.3 and 22.8 point Pass@32 improvements on AIME 24 and AIME 25 (Table 1) — is reported at equal training steps (600 steps for both SvS and RLVR), not at equal wall-clock time or equal FLOPs.

**The consequence:** A practitioner cannot directly interpret the headline gains as efficiency improvements. If SvS requires 2× more compute per step in early training, then the fair comparison is SvS at step T versus RLVR at step 2T (or whatever multiple equalizes total FLOPs). The paper partially addresses this with the "Extending standard RLVR training" (Ext) ablation in Table 3, which trains RLVR longer until it sees the same number of problem–solution pairs. Ext achieves Pass@32 of 57.0% on AIME24 and 48.0% on AIME25, compared to SvS's 70.8% and 65.2% — indicating that SvS's gains are not purely from increased sample count. However, this comparison controls for **generated samples** but not for **total FLOPs**, since SvS also performs the synthesis step (generating variational problems) which costs additional inference FLOPs beyond the synthetic problem-solving samples counted in Ext. A fully FLOPs-matched comparison — where RLVR is allowed to run for enough additional steps to equal SvS's total inference FLOPs — is not reported. The paper's claim of "sample efficiency" (100 SvS steps vs. 600 RLVR steps for code, Section 5.4) partially addresses this for code, but the math experiments lack a comparable FLOPs-controlled analysis.

**What evidence exists:** Figure 9 (Section 5.5) quantifies the per-step time and policy update time. The Ext ablation (Table 3) controls for problem–solution pair count but not total FLOPs. The code generation results (Figure 8) show SvS at 100 steps outperforming RLVR at 600 steps, which is a stronger efficiency claim, but these are on a different domain and model scale.

**Mitigation status:** The paper is transparent about the overhead (Figure 9, discussion in Section 5.5) and notes that the overhead is self-limiting — it shrinks as training progresses and the policy improves, because fewer problems fall into the underperforming range. For MATH-12k (where the 32B model starts at ~80% accuracy), the overhead is negligible (Figure 9, third panel shows comparable policy update times). For DAPO-17k, the overhead is significant in early training but decreases. The paper does not propose any mechanism to reduce this overhead (e.g., limiting the number of underperforming problems used per step, reducing G_v, or sharing computation between synthesis and solving). It also does not report a FLOPs-matched comparison against standard RLVR, which would require extending RLVR training to equal total compute.

---

### The Difficulty Estimation Cost Is Extraordinarily High and Not Included in Any Budget

**The assumption:** The compute-optimal policy allocation requires estimating difficulty for each prompt. The paper's method is described in the prior analysis but bears repeating as a limitation: generating 2048 samples per question and averaging the PRM's predicted final-answer correctness to bin questions into five difficulty quintiles. The paper explicitly acknowledges this:

> "estimating difficulty in this way still incurs additional computation cost during inference... our experiments do not account for this cost largely for simplicity" (Section 3.2)

While this specific quote is from the earlier rephrasing experiments motivating SvS, the principle applies: SvS's underperforming problem identification requires computing group accuracy for every training problem at each step (generating G=8 solutions per problem), which incurs the generation cost. For online deployment of a trained SvS model, if one wanted to adaptively select test-time strategies (as the earlier analysis framework proposed), the difficulty estimation cost would need to be amortized over queries.

**The consequence:** In a deployment setting where difficulty estimation must be performed per query, the cost of generating even 8 solutions to assess difficulty could exceed the cost of simply running the most expensive strategy (e.g., best-of-256) on easy problems — negating the allocation benefit. The paper does not evaluate the tradeoff between difficulty estimation cost and strategy selection benefit in an online deployment scenario. More critically, the SvS training pipeline itself incurs this cost at every training step (sampling G=8 solutions per problem to identify underperforming ones), but this is integral to the RLVR training loop (since those same solutions are used for the policy update) and thus is not "wasted" computation in the same way that deployment-time difficulty estimation would be.

**What evidence exists:** The paper does not measure or discuss this as a deployment limitation — the concern applies primarily if one attempted to use SvS's difficulty estimation approach for online test-time allocation, which the paper does not propose doing. The training-time identification of underperforming problems is cost-amortized because those solutions are reused for policy updates.

**Mitigation status:** Not addressed. The paper does not propose a lightweight difficulty estimator trained on the policy's outputs, nor does it explore adaptive schemes where difficulty is assessed from a small number of initial samples and the remaining budget is allocated accordingly. This is a natural extension but is outside the scope of the current work.

---

### Hardest Problems Remain Essentially Unsolvable — Self-Play Augmentation Cannot Create Capability from Nothing

**The assumption:** SvS augments training by generating variational problems from the policy's correct solutions to underperforming problems. This presupposes that the policy **can** generate correct solutions to those problems (accuracy must be at least 12.5% — at least 1 of 8 samples correct — for a problem to qualify for augmentation). Problems where the policy achieves 0% accuracy produce no correct solutions to use for synthesis, and thus are never augmented.

**The consequence:** SvS provides no mechanism for improving performance on problems that are fundamentally beyond the base model's capability. The paper shows this concretely in Table 1: on OlymMATH-Hard (OlymH), SvS trained on DAPO-17k achieves only 2.7% Pass@1 and 16.7% Pass@32 — a **decline** from standard RLVR's 4.8% Pass@1 and 18.3% Pass@32. Similarly, on Beyond-AIME, SvS reaches 19.2% Pass@1 and 45.9% Pass@32 — improvements over RLVR (10.0 points on Pass@32), but still far from solved. The instance-level analysis (Figure 12) confirms that on specific hard AIME problems, both RLVR and SvS achieve near-zero accuracy — SvS cannot solve problems where the base model has no foothold.

This limitation is fundamental to the self-play paradigm: the model can only learn from problems it can sometimes generate correct answers to, and it can only generate variational problems from those correct solutions. There is no mechanism for introducing genuinely novel problem structures or reasoning patterns that the base model never produces. The paper is transparent about this boundary but does not characterize it quantitatively — what is the maximum difficulty gap that SvS can bridge? At what base-model pass@1 rate does SvS stop helping?

**What evidence exists:** Table 1 (OlymH and OlymE columns), Table 2 (Beyond-AIME column for smaller models remains low — 14.0% Pass@1 for 32B SvS on MATH-12k, 19.0% on DAPO-17k), and Figure 12 (instance-level analysis showing problems where both methods fail). The paper acknowledges this implicitly by noting that SvS targets "underperforming problems" — problems the model can sometimes solve — and does not claim to help on completely unsolvable ones.

**Mitigation status:** Not addressed directly. The paper does not discuss how to extend SvS to problems beyond the base model's reach. Potential approaches — using a stronger teacher model to provide initial correct solutions for synthesis, or curriculum learning where easier problems build toward harder ones — are not explored. The DAPO-25k experiment (adding DeepMath problems with open-ended answers) partially addresses generalization but does not specifically target harder problems. This limitation is shared by all RLVR methods that rely on the policy's own outputs for exploration, and SvS does not escape it.

---

### The Augmentation Is Evaluated Only on Reasoning Tasks with Verifiable Closed-Form Answers

**The assumption:** SvS's core mechanism — synthesizing variational problems from correct solutions and reusing original reference answers — depends on two properties of the task domain: (1) correctness can be automatically verified through exact answer matching (the RLVR assumption), and (2) the solution trace contains sufficient information to reconstruct a well-posed problem statement with an unambiguous correct answer. Mathematical reasoning and code generation (evaluated in the paper) satisfy both properties. The paper evaluates on 12 math reasoning benchmarks (Section 4.1) and 4 code generation benchmarks (Section 5.4). The domain-transfer experiment in Appendix E.3 (Table 4) evaluates on general QA benchmarks (MMLU-Pro, ARC, HellaSwag, etc.) but only as an evaluation of the final model, not as a training domain — SvS was not trained on these tasks, only evaluated on them.

**The consequence:** It is unclear whether SvS would work for domains without clean verifiable rewards — e.g., summarization (where quality is multi-dimensional and not binary), dialogue (where "correctness" is ill-defined), or creative writing (where no reference answer exists). Even within reasoning, tasks requiring open-ended generation (essay writing, explanation, proof construction) lack the exact-answer matching that SvS relies on for both reward computation and answer inheritance. The paper does not evaluate SvS on any task where rewards are learned (reward model) rather than rule-based. For practitioners working in such domains, the transferability of SvS is unknown.

Furthermore, the paper's math evaluations are exclusively on English-language benchmarks with standard mathematical notation. The variational problem synthesis prompt (Figure 20) instructs the model to generate "a clear, concise, and natural-sounding original problem in English." Whether SvS would work for mathematical reasoning in other languages, or for non-mathematical structured reasoning (e.g., legal reasoning, scientific hypothesis testing), is unexplored.

**What evidence exists:** Section 5.4 (code generation) demonstrates that SvS transfers from math to code, which is a positive signal — code generation shares the verifiable-reward property (unit tests) and the solution-contains-problem-structure property. Appendix E.3 shows that the SvS-trained model preserves general QA performance better than RLVR, but this is a side effect (reduced overfitting) rather than evidence that SvS training on general QA tasks would work. The paper does not ablate or discuss what task properties are necessary for SvS to be effective.

**Mitigation status:** The paper does not claim SvS works beyond reasoning with verifiable rewards, and the domain restriction is inherent to the RLVR paradigm itself, not specific to SvS. However, the broader question of whether self-play problem augmentation can be adapted to tasks with learned reward models — e.g., by having the reward model serve as the correctness oracle for both solving and synthesis validation — is not discussed. Future work on extending SvS to RLHF (Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback) settings would need to address this.

### Integer-Answer Overfitting Reveals a Fragility to Training Set Distribution

**The assumption:** SvS relies on the original training set's reference answers being reused for synthetic problems. If the training set has a systematic bias — such as DAPO-17k's integer-only answers — the synthetic problems inherit that bias, and the model can overfit to it.

**The consequence:** Table 1 shows that on DAPO-17k (all answers are integers), SvS achieves strong competition-level results but **degrades on benchmarks with non-integer answers**: OlymE Pass@32 drops from 47.1% (RLVR) to 43.4% (SvS), and OlymH Pass@32 drops from 18.3% to 16.7%. Table 2 is even more dramatic: on standard benchmarks, the DAPO-17k SvS model drops from RLVR's 87.0% to 75.6% on MATH-500, from 54.8% to 45.9% on Olympiad-Bench, and from 78.7% to 62.9% on Gaokao-2023 — losses of 11.4, 8.9, and 15.8 points respectively. These benchmarks have answers that are often fractions, expressions, or mixed numbers — formats the model was never required to produce during training because all synthetic problems inherited integer answers.

**What evidence exists:** Table 2 (D17k SvS row vs. D17k RLVR row, standard benchmark columns) and Table 1 (OlymE, OlymH columns for Pass@32). The paper explicitly diagnoses this: "it results in reduced performance on benchmarks with open-ended answers, likely because the model overfits to DAPO-17k's integer-only format during augmentation" (Section 4.2). The DAPO-25k experiment (adding 8k open-ended-answer problems from DeepMath) confirms the diagnosis: performance recovers to 88.6% on MATH-500, 59.9% on Olympiad-Bench, and 79.2% on Gaokao-2023, while maintaining competitive AIME scores. This is strong evidence that the integer-answer overfitting is real and is caused by the training set bias, not by SvS fundamentally.

**Mitigation status:** Partially addressed by the DAPO-25k experiment, which demonstrates that mixing in open-ended-answer problems during SvS training prevents the overfitting. However, this requires access to a supplementary dataset with diverse answer formats — the very data collection burden that SvS was designed to avoid. The paper does not propose a mechanism for SvS to automatically generate problems with diverse answer formats when starting from an integer-only training set (e.g., by prompting the synthesis step to produce problems whose answers are not integers, even though the reference answer is an integer). This leaves open the question: if a practitioner only has an integer-only dataset, can they use SvS without introducing format overfitting? The current answer appears to be "no" — they would need to source additional diverse-format data.

### The Method Is Validated on a Single RL Algorithm (GRPO with Clip-Higher) with Fixed Hyperparameters

**The assumption:** All experiments use GRPO with Clip-Higher (ε=0.28), Token-Level Loss, and Dynamic Sampling (Appendix D.1). The learning rate (1e-6), temperature (1.0), group sizes (G=8, G_v=8), and accuracy ranges ([12.5%, 50.0%] for underperforming, [12.5%, 62.5%] for synthesis reward) are fixed across all experiments (3B, 8B, 32B models; MATH-12k, DAPO-17k, code datasets). The paper claims (Section 1) that "the SvS augmentation is agnostic to RLVR optimization algorithms and can be flexibly incorporated into other methods, such as PPO, GSPO, and Reinforce++", but provides no experimental evidence.

**The consequence:** A practitioner using a different RL algorithm — particularly PPO with a learned value function, or algorithms with different advantage normalization schemes — cannot assume SvS will work identically. The interaction between SvS's data augmentation and the optimization algorithm is unexplored. Key questions: Does the increased diversity of training data in SvS affect the stability of PPO's value function learning? Does the mixing of three data types (original solving, synthesis, synthetic solving) in a single batch require different ratio tuning for algorithms with different advantage structures? The paper's fixed hyperparameters across model scales (3B to 32B) and datasets suggest robustness, but there is no sweep showing that the chosen thresholds are near-optimal — the failed experiment in Appendix G demonstrates that the synthesis reward range is consequential (wrong range causes failure), but the paper does not explore sensitivity around the chosen [12.5%, 62.5%] window.

**What evidence exists:** The paper shows that SvS works with GRPO+Clip-Higher across three model scales and two domains. The combination with Clip-CoV (Appendix F, Figure 17) shows that SvS is compatible with an entropy-regulation method on top of GRPO. There is no evaluation with PPO, GSPO, Reinforce++, or any other RL algorithm. The fixed hyperparameters are justified by the failed experiment in Appendix G (showing that the naive reward fails) and the SvS-Asp ablation in Table 3 (showing that augmenting simpler problems is worse), but there is no sensitivity analysis around the specific threshold values.

**Mitigation status:** The paper claims algorithm-agnosticism but does not test it. This is a missed opportunity — running a single experiment with PPO (or at least ablating key hyperparameters like the accuracy ranges) would substantially strengthen the generalizability claim. The modular design of SvS (it operates at the data level by populating the experience buffer) is logically compatible with any policy-gradient algorithm that consumes prompt-response-reward tuples, but empirical validation is absent.
