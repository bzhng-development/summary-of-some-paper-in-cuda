Here are the takeaways from this Simon Kindström interview (went from new grad IC3 to Staff IC6 in 3 years, earned two "Redefines Expectations" ratings, and received secret discretionary equity bonuses):

**Joining the right team for outsized impact**

Simon's trajectory was set in motion by a manager who explicitly sold him on the connection between business impact and career growth. During the recruiting process, the manager laid out how the team's work directly drove revenue, and how that visibility would accelerate Simon's career. He joined because of that pitch—a decision that proved foundational. On that team, he landed multiple unexpected revenue wins that became the raw material for high ratings and fast promotions. The framing was deliberate: don't pick a team purely for technical interest; pick one where your work will be legible as business impact to the people who decide your ratings.

**The "Redefines Expectations" engine: following failing experiments to the root**

The centerpiece of Simon's rapid rise was earning the "Redefines Expectations" rating twice—the highest possible performance rating, above "Greatly Exceeds." The story behind it reveals a specific pattern: experiments that flopped unexpectedly. Most engineers, when an A/B test comes back flat or negative, will shrug, document the null result, and move on to the next idea. Simon did the opposite: he refused to give up on a failed experiment until he understood *why* it failed. He would make adjustments, re-run it, analyze again, and iterate—sometimes multiple cycles—until he either had a clear explanation for the failure or, in some cases, actually turned the results around into a significant win. Some of his biggest career-defining wins came from experiments that initially looked like dead ends. The "Redefines" rating wasn't about flawless execution; it was about the stubbornness to extract signal from noise that everyone else had abandoned.

**Writing as leverage: putting the punchline first**

Simon identified writing as one of the highest-leverage skills in his career. His core tactical advice: put the impactful, interesting part at the *top*. He observed that engineers habitually bury the lede—writing long documents that walk through methodology, data, and edge cases before finally arriving at the conclusion. Most readers (especially senior leaders making resource decisions) don't care about the journey; they need the destination immediately. By front-loading the revenue impact, the surprising finding, or the key recommendation, you earn the reader's attention for the details that follow. This wasn't just about communication hygiene; it directly amplified the perceived impact of his work because decision-makers could actually see and act on his results without wading through paragraphs of context.

**Scaling himself: "Delegate, don't abdicate"**

As he grew toward Staff, Simon hit a predictable ceiling: delegation. He was handing off work to others but disengaging from it—a pattern that led to end-of-half scrambles when delegated work came back incomplete, off-target, or simply not done. The fix was a mindset shift he described as **Delegate, don't abdicate**. Taking ownership of the *results* of delegated work, not just the assignment of tasks. Practically, this meant staying close enough to course-correct early, treating a junior engineer's output as his own responsibility, and never letting a half-cycle pass with a surprise gap. The phrase captures a specific failure mode: delegation as dumping, followed by discovery at deadline. His correction was to maintain a light but persistent thread of accountability—checking in, unblocking, and ultimately feeling personally on the hook for what shipped.

**The manager multiplier**

A through-line in Simon's story is his manager, who had himself been promoted quickly at the company before transitioning into management. This person coached Simon closely throughout, serving as both a career strategist and a translator of the company's unwritten rules. The manager knew what calibrated at each level, what kinds of impact would register in calibration rooms, and how to position Simon's work so that it was legible to leadership. Simon attributes much of his acceleration to having someone who could say "that project won't count for promotion" or "this is the dimension you're weak on; go fix it"—unglamorous, tactical guidance that compounds.

**The management detour and transparent return to IC**

At some point Simon tried transitioning to management—partly to learn a new skill set, partly to help the org. It didn't go well. He found himself pulled further from the technical work he enjoyed, and the role introduced a level of thrash (reorgs, people issues, shifting priorities) that he hadn't anticipated. Crucially, he was transparent about the whole journey: he communicated openly with his leadership about why he was trying management, what wasn't working, and his decision to switch back to IC. The episode didn't derail his career; the transparency turned what could have been a silent struggle into a legible, professionally handled pivot. His takeaway wasn't anti-management—he frames it as a legitimate learning experience—but a recognition that management operates on a different axis of satisfaction, and the distance from hands-on technical work wasn't one he wanted.

**Secret discretionary equity bonuses**

Beyond ratings and promotions, Simon also received what he describes as "secret" bonuses—discretionary equity grants that aren't part of any published compensation framework. These existed outside the normal review cycle and weren't something he knew to expect early on. They were tied directly to the outsized revenue impact he'd generated and the visibility his writing had created. The mechanism isn't documented in any internal wiki; it's something managers and directors can tap into when they need to retain someone whose contributions are disproportionate. The insight: the formal comp and promo system is a floor, not a ceiling. Truly exceptional business impact can unlock entirely off-cycle rewards, but only if your work is visible and legible to the people with discretionary budget authority.
