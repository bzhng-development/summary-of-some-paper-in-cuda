Here are the takeaways from this Ryan Peterman post on advice for software engineers to improve their writing:

**Background: business leverage**

In 2018, Naval Ravikant famously wrote about **business leverage**, which is the idea that you can have more impact with less effort. For software engineers, the two most actionable forms are code and media. Since most engineers already code a lot, writing—the most relevant media—is the highest-leverage skill to develop. Ryan started caring more about writing partly because of this insight, and it's why he writes his newsletter.

**Spend extra time on the outline**

He's thrown away many rough drafts that went off-track, and each time the root cause was unclear thinking in the outline. The outline is "the most simple and malleable form of our writing." It's worth spending extra time here, when you can make efficient changes—"measure twice, cut once."

**Don't sweat the rough draft**

It's common to get blocked because the writing seems bad during the rough draft. That's expected, because it's *rough*. The best way to fight writer's block is to "get all the words down without thinking about quality yet."

**Polish until perfect**

Your writing will be read orders of magnitude more times than it's written. All those future readers benefit from your effort to polish. Ryan spends about 80% of his writing time on the final draft, reading it repeatedly to "reduce clutter and make it easier to read." He uses three tools: **Grammarly** for typos and grammar, **Hemingway** for readability (he aims for ~6th-grade level), and **ChatGPT** for rewordings.

**The ultimate goal: be understood by the reader**

The process itself is just an implementation detail. What matters is making yourself understood, which has two components. First, *make the reader want to read*—write in a way that's interesting to your audience (knowing your audience is a critical skill). Second, *make your writing easy to read*: be concise ("don't waste your reader's mental capacity on needless words") and write simply ("don't pick words that are more complicated than they need to be"), an idea Paul Graham's famous essay on writing explores well.

**Leading advice**

The best way to improve is to write more and ask for feedback from someone who writes well. Over time, "this feedback loop will make you an excellent writer."
