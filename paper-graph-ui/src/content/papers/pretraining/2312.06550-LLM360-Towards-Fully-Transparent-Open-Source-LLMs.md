# LLM360: Towards Fully Transparent Open-Source LLMs

**ArXiv:** [2312.06550](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.06550)

## 🎯 Pitch

While modern “open-source” LLMs increasingly hide their training data, code, and intermediate states behind only final weights, LLM360 demonstrates that releasing every artifact—including 360+ intermediate checkpoints and the full 1.3 trillion-token pretraining dataset—is not only feasible but surfaces hidden training failures like NaN losses on specific data chunks that partial releases would mask.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **LLM360**, an initiative and release framework advocating for fully transparent open-source LLMs by publishing all training code, pretraining data, intermediate model checkpoints, optimizer states, and evaluation metrics collected throughout training. As initial instantiations, the authors release two 7B-parameter models—AMBER, an English LLM trained on 1.3T tokens, and CRYSTALCODER, an English-and-code LLM trained on 1.4T tokens—each accompanied by 360 and 143 intermediate checkpoints respectively, along with their complete pretraining datasets and an analysis toolkit called ANALYSIS360 that enables studies such as tracking memorization behavior across the full training trajectory. The framework establishes that comprehensive pretraining transparency—encompassing data provenance, reproducibility of the training sequence, and access to intermediate states—is achievable for modern LLMs trained at trillion-token scale, but also surfaces concrete failure modes encountered during training (NaN losses on specific data chunks, missing optimizer states, precision discrepancies in early checkpoints) that prior partially-disclosed projects would have obscured.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: The Transparency Paradox in Open-Source LLMs

The paper addresses a specific paradox that has emerged in the field of large language models (LLMs): as open-source LLMs have become more capable and widely adopted, their release practices have become **less transparent**, not more. This creates a tension between the term "open-source" and what is actually available to the research community. The paper's Section 2 and Table 1 document this trend systematically: early open-source LLMs like GPT-J (2021) and BLOOM (2022) released training code, dataset information, and intermediate checkpoints, but more recent models—LLaMA (Feb 2023), Llama 2 (Jul 2023), Mistral (Sep 2023), and Yi (Nov 2023)—have increasingly withheld critical artifacts while still being described as "open-source."

What makes this gap particularly acute is that **the withheld artifacts are precisely the ones needed for scientific understanding and reproduction**. The paper identifies three dimensions of this transparency deficit:

**Data provenance is obscured.** When training datasets are not released, researchers cannot assess whether benchmark performance reflects genuine capability or data contamination (training data overlapping with test sets). The paper cites Zhou et al. (2023) and Wei et al. (2023) as evidence that this is a recognized but unaddressed problem in the field. Without visibility into the training data, users cannot evaluate biases inherent in the model, cannot determine whether the model was exposed to private or sensitive information, and cannot meaningfully build upon the pretrained model for domain-specific applications without risking unintended data duplication that prior work (Hernandez et al., 2022) has shown degrades performance.

**Reproducibility is fundamentally broken.** The paper makes a pointed observation using LLaMA as a case study: even though LLaMA's technical report disclosed data mixture ratios, the lack of released training code and exact data processing pipelines means that open reproduction efforts like OpenLLaMA and RedPajama—which attempted to recreate LLaMA's training data from the same sources—"still do not fully reproduce its benchmark evaluations" (Section 1). This reveals that high-level descriptions of data composition are insufficient; the specific filtering, cleaning, deduplication, and ordering decisions all matter, and without releasing those exact artifacts, the result is not truly reproducible. Each independent team must rediscover these details through expensive trial and error, duplicating thousands of GPU-hours of work.

**Open collaboration is structurally constrained.** Research on fundamental questions about LLM behavior—emergent capabilities (Wei et al., 2022), scaling dynamics, the effect of specific training data on downstream behavior (Yu et al., 2023)—requires access to intermediate training states, not just final model weights. When only the final checkpoint is released, researchers studying these phenomena face a binary choice: work with the opaque final model (which provides no insight into developmental trajectories) or pretrain from scratch entirely (which is prohibitive for most academic labs and small organizations). The paper notes that this creates a research environment where only well-resourced industry teams can conduct foundational investigations into LLM training dynamics, narrowing the field of inquiry to commercial interests.

### Why This Problem Matters: Scientific, Practical, and Societal Stakes

The transparency deficit has consequences that extend across multiple registers:

**Scientific significance.** The paper's framing situates LLMs not merely as products but as scientific instruments for studying language, reasoning, and knowledge representation. If the training data, code, and intermediate states are unavailable, the model becomes a black box whose internal development cannot be interrogated scientifically. Questions such as "When during training does factual knowledge emerge versus reasoning capability?" or "How does data mixture affect the development of specific skills?" become unanswerable without checkpoint access. The paper's later demonstration with ANALYSIS360—tracking memorization scores across all 360 checkpoints and showing that models memorize recent data disproportionately (Section 4.3, Figures 7–8)—illustrates exactly the kind of investigation that is impossible without full checkpoint and data release.

**Practical impact on model adoption and extension.** Domain-specific LLM development (medical, legal, financial) typically involves continued pretraining from a base model, a process that benefits from understanding what data the base model was already exposed to. If this is unknown, practitioners risk contaminating their domain adaptation with redundant data or inadvertently overlapping with the original pretraining distribution. The paper also notes that recent work on quantization-aware training (Liu et al., 2023) and efficient training approximations (Wang et al., 2021, 2023) requires access to partially trained model weights—making comprehensive checkpoint releases a practical enabler for downstream algorithmic research.

**Bias assessment and responsible use.** The paper argues that the transparent release of training data is a prerequisite for responsible LLM deployment. Without data visibility, stakeholders cannot audit models for representational biases, cannot assess whether certain demographic groups or languages are underrepresented in training, and cannot meaningfully evaluate the social risks documented in frameworks like Weidinger et al. (2022). The paper positions LLM360's transparency as a risk-mitigation mechanism: by making the exact training data available for inspection alongside bias analysis tools in ANALYSIS360, deployers have the information needed to make informed decisions about model use rather than relying on vendor assurances.

### Where Prior Approaches Fall Short

The paper identifies specific limitations in the landscape of prior LLM releases, which Table 1 formalizes across seven dimensions of transparency:

**Partial releases claim openness while withholding critical components.** The paper observes that many models described as "open-source" release only model weights and perhaps inference code, creating an illusion of openness while obscuring the training process entirely. Models like Qwen (Aug 2023), Mistral (Sep 2023), and Yi (Nov 2023) are marked with "?" or empty cells in the key transparency columns of Table 1—the paper does not even attempt to characterize their data practices because of insufficient disclosure. Llama 2, one of the most widely used "open" models, only checks two columns in Table 1 (weights with no training code, no intermediate checkpoints, no data availability). This selective disclosure means the community cannot distinguish between a model whose performance derives from architectural innovation versus one whose training data happened to overlap heavily with benchmarks.

**Dataset disclosure is not the same as dataset availability.** Several models—LLaMA, Falcon, MPT—disclosed their data sources and mixture ratios but did not make training-ready data available. The paper's later observation that AMBER deliberately includes C4 data (which RefinedWeb excluded after deduplication) to avoid unintended duplication (Section 4.1.1) illustrates why this matters: without knowing the exact data, it is impossible to know whether the model was trained on a specific document, which affects reuse decisions. The paper also notes the practical burden this creates: researchers wanting to build on these models must reconstruct the training data from scratch, expending significant compute and engineering effort.

**The most transparent prior work is now outdated.** The paper acknowledges Pythia (Biderman et al., 2023) as the closest precursor—it released 154 checkpoints for models up to 12B parameters. However, Pythia was trained on 300B tokens (0.3T), reflecting pre-2023 training practices. The field has since shifted to trillion-token training regimes and multi-stage pretraining with specialized data scheduling (as in Code Llama's sequential language-then-code training). Pythia "no longer reflects many recent LLM practices" (Section 2), meaning its transparency model is valuable but its artifacts are insufficient for studying contemporary LLM behavior. The paper positions LLM360 as extending Pythia's philosophy to the modern training paradigm while expanding beyond it (1.3T vs. 0.3T tokens, and with the addition of multi-stage training in CRYSTALCODER).

**The trend line is moving in the wrong direction.** Perhaps the paper's most compelling framing appears implicitly in Table 1's temporal ordering: as models have become more capable over 2021–2023, the transparency columns have emptied out. Early EleutherAI models (GPT-J, GPT-NeoX) check nearly every box. By late 2023, models check almost none. The paper does not attribute this to any single cause, but the implication is clear: commercial competitiveness is driving a retreat from openness, and the community risks losing the transparency infrastructure just as the scientific questions LLMs raise become most urgent.

### How LLM360 Positions Itself

The paper positions LLM360 not as a novel technical contribution to LLM architecture or training methodology, but as a **release framework and advocacy initiative** with a specific set of commitments designed to reverse the transparency trend. The framework (Section 3) defines four categories of mandatory release artifacts:

1. **Training dataset and data processing code** — the exact data, in training-ready form, with all filtering and processing code so others can reproduce the pipeline or adapt it.
2. **Training code, hyperparameters, and configurations** — the full source code, the specific learning rates and schedules used, and the system configuration choices like parallelism strategy that affect reproducibility.
3. **Model checkpoints** — all intermediate checkpoints (weights and optimizer states) saved during training, not just the final model. The "360" in LLM360 refers to the practice of releasing checkpoints at roughly 360 time stamps during training.
4. **Metrics** — all training logs, evaluation results, system statistics collected during the training run, made available through public Weights \& Biases dashboards and the ANALYSIS360 repository.

What distinguishes this from prior transparency efforts is its **comprehensiveness** ("all intermediate checkpoints," not a subset; "all training code and configurations," not a summary) and its **commitment to scale**—the framework is explicitly designed to persist across future larger models, with the paper announcing ongoing training of "a much larger LLM" (Section 6) that will also be fully released.

The paper's relationship to prior work is one of inheritance and extension. It credits Pythia as having "set an early precedent for transparency and reproducibility" and describes LLM360's goal as perpetuating and expanding that precedent to "modern LLM pretraining regimes" (Section 2). It draws on the data preparation methodology of OpenLLaMA (Section 4.1.1) and the architectural choices of LLaMA, positioning AMBER as a clean reproduction that exposes—through its performance gap with LLaMA—exactly how much of model quality depends on undisclosed data processing decisions. Similarly, CRYSTALCODER departs from Code Llama's approach of sequential language-then-code training to use a gradual blending approach across three stages, and releases the data mixing scripts so this choice can be studied and reproduced.

The paper also positions itself as a **platform for future research**, not merely a release mechanism. ANALYSIS360 is presented as an "organized repository" of analysis tools that the community can both use and contribute to. The memorization study in Section 4.3 is offered as an "example of the analysis that can be performed" (italics in the original), demonstrating how full checkpoint and data release enables investigations—tracking which data chunks produce memorized sequences, how memorization evolves across training, and how different checkpoints correlate in their memorization patterns—that are impossible with final-weight-only releases. The paper explicitly invites the community to use LLM360 checkpoints as starting points for domain adaptation (resuming from 50% rather than 100% of pretraining), for data mixture optimization experiments, and for testing algorithmic training frameworks that require partially-trained model weights.

Finally, the paper addresses the responsible use dimension directly (Section 5), positioning transparency itself as a safety mechanism. Rather than arguing that open release creates risks, the paper contends that transparent release enables risk assessment: stakeholders can inspect the training data for biases before deploying models, and the ANALYSIS360 toolkit provides bias evaluation frameworks (citing BOLD, Dhamala et al., 2021) that can be run against any checkpoint. This is a specific stance in the broader debate about LLM openness—the paper argues that risks are better managed through visible, auditable artifacts than through restricted access that leaves risks unexamined.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation (Approachable Technical Breakdown)

This paper describes the **LLM360 release framework** — a set of principles and concrete release practices for making large language model pretraining fully transparent by publicly sharing all code, data, intermediate checkpoints, and metrics — along with two specific 7B-parameter LLMs (AMBER and CRYSTALCODER) that instantiate the framework. The core problem it solves is the growing opacity of "open-source" LLM releases: as models have become more capable, their releases have included progressively fewer artifacts (no training data, no intermediate checkpoints, no training code), making it impossible for the research community to reproduce results, study training dynamics, assess biases, or build upon models with full knowledge of their training provenance. The shape of the solution is a **comprehensive release specification** that mandates four categories of artifacts — data, code, checkpoints, and metrics — and a commitment to releasing these for every model trained under the initiative, at every scale, with no selective withholding.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The LLM360 framework consists of four major release artifact categories that together form a complete record of an LLM's pretraining life cycle. The architecture is not a runtime system but rather a **taxonomy of transparency** — a specification for what must be captured during training and released afterward.

**Component 1: Training Dataset and Data Processing Code.** This is the exact pretraining corpus, in training-ready form, along with all scripts used to filter, clean, deduplicate, mix, and shuffle the data. It answers the question: "What did the model see, in what order, and how was it prepared?"

**Component 2: Training Code, Hyperparameters, and Configurations.** This includes the full distributed training source code, all hyperparameter choices (learning rates, schedules, batch sizes, optimizer settings, weight decay, gradient clipping), and system configurations (parallelism strategy, precision settings, hardware topology). It answers the question: "Exactly how was the model trained, and what engineering choices were made?"

**Component 3: Model Checkpoints (Weights and Optimizer States).** These are the intermediate saved states of the model at periodic intervals throughout training — for AMBER, 360 checkpoints saved at each of 360 data chunks; for CRYSTALCODER, 143 checkpoints. Each checkpoint includes model weights and, when properly captured, optimizer states such as Adam moving averages. They answer the question: "What was the model's state at every point during its development?"

**Component 4: Metrics (Training Logs and Evaluation Results).** These are all measurements collected during training: per-step and per-epoch training loss, gradient norms, system throughput, and downstream benchmark evaluations run on intermediate checkpoints. Released through public Weights & Biases dashboards and the ANALYSIS360 repository. They answer the question: "How did the model's capabilities and internal dynamics evolve over time?"

Information flow during the release process follows a straightforward pipeline: data is prepared and partitioned into chunks → chunks are fed sequentially to the training process → training produces checkpoints and logs at each chunk boundary → all artifacts are uploaded to public repositories (Hugging Face for models, GitHub for code, Weights & Biases for metrics). The ANALYSIS360 component sits on top of this release, providing tools for researchers to query relationships across artifacts (e.g., "show me the memorization scores of checkpoint 200 on data chunks 1–200").

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the release framework specification — the four artifact categories, what each must contain, and the design rationale for each — because this is the paper's primary methodological contribution and the standard against which all LLM360 models are measured.
- **Second**, the data preparation pipeline for AMBER — data sources, mixing ratios, and the preprocessing decisions (inclusion of C4, global permutation into 360 chunks) — because data is the foundation of model capability and the paper's key argument about reproducibility hinges on exact data availability.
- **Third**, the model architecture and training hyperparameters for AMBER — the specific architectural choices inherited from LLaMA, the training configuration, and the infrastructure setup — because these constitute the "code and configurations" artifact and demonstrate what full transparency looks like concretely.
- **Fourth**, the training infrastructure and distributed system design — the GPU cluster topology, the 3D parallelism strategy, and the throughput achieved — because system-level choices directly affect reproducibility and the paper surfaces lessons learned here.
- **Fifth**, the data preparation and architecture for CRYSTALCODER — the three-stage training schedule, the gradual blending of language and code data (contrasted with sequential approaches), and the architectural modifications such as maximal update parameterization (muP) and restricted RoPE — because CRYSTALCODER demonstrates the framework's extensibility to multi-stage training regimes and domain-specialized models.
- **Sixth**, the ANALYSIS360 toolkit and memorization study — the computational definition of memorization, the experimental protocol, and the cross-checkpoint analysis methodology — because this demonstrates how the four artifact categories enable research that is impossible with final-model-only releases.

This is primarily a **release framework and empirical demonstration paper** whose core methodological contribution is a comprehensive specification for what constitutes a fully transparent LLM release, instantiated through two concrete 7B-parameter models that make good on every element of the specification. The "technical approach" is therefore both normative (defining what should be released) and descriptive (documenting what was done for AMBER and CRYSTALCODER).

#### The Release Framework: Four Mandatory Artifact Categories

The LLM360 framework defines four categories of artifacts that must be publicly released for every model trained under the initiative. The design philosophy is that **partial disclosure is insufficient for scientific reproducibility** — withholding any one category breaks the chain of provenance and forces downstream researchers to guess, rediscover, or duplicate work.

**Category 1: Training Dataset and Data Processing Code.** The requirement is not merely to describe the data sources and mixing ratios (as LLaMA did) but to release the **exact training-ready data** — the tokenized, preprocessed, shuffled corpus in the precise order the model saw it — along with all processing scripts. The paper argues that this level of disclosure is necessary because downstream use cases (domain adaptation, continued pretraining) require knowledge of the exact data to avoid redundant training on duplicated documents, and because bias auditing requires inspecting the actual text, not summaries. The data processing code includes filtering logic, deduplication procedures, quality checks, and any sampling or weighting schemes applied to different data sources. For AMBER, this means the full 1.26T-token corpus partitioned into 360 data chunks, released along with the scripts that performed the global permutation and chunking.

**Category 2: Training Code, Hyperparameters, and Configurations.** The requirement goes beyond releasing model architecture code (inference-only) to releasing the **full distributed training source code** with the exact hyperparameter values used. The paper specifically highlights two examples where precise configuration details matter for reproducibility: (1) the choice of 3D parallelism (data, tensor-model, and pipeline parallelism combined) versus FSDP can yield up to 15% throughput difference on the same hardware; and (2) keeping the inverse frequency matrix in RoPE positional embeddings in FP32 precision is necessary to avoid degradation, a detail that would be invisible without source code access or explicit configuration documentation. The released artifacts include the training framework (lit-llama, based on PyTorch Lightning for AMBER), all hyperparameter values (learning rate `$\eta = 3 \times 10^{-4}$`, cosine schedule to `$\eta = 3 \times 10^{-5}$`, weight decay 0.1, gradient clipping at 1.0, warmup over 2000 steps, batch size 2240), and the system configuration (mixed precision with BF16 activations/gradients and FP32 weights, parallelism dimensions, hardware topology).

**Category 3: Model Checkpoints (Weights and Optimizer States).** The requirement is to release **all intermediate checkpoints** saved during training, not a curated subset or only the final model. The number "360" in LLM360 refers to the practice of saving checkpoints at roughly 360 timestamps corresponding to data chunk boundaries. For AMBER, this means releasing 360 checkpoints — one after each of the 360 equally-sized data chunks — allowing researchers to study model evolution at approximately 1.4B-token granularity (1.26T tokens ÷ 360 chunks). For CRYSTALCODER, the number is 143 checkpoints because of different data chunk granularity. Each checkpoint includes model weights, and the paper acknowledges a specific failure in AMBER's training: optimizer states (Adam moving averages) were not saved due to a framework oversight, which the authors identify as a probable contributor to training instability. This honest disclosure of a transparency gap is itself part of the framework's philosophy — documenting what went wrong, not just what went right.

**Category 4: Metrics (Training Logs and Evaluation Results).** The requirement is to release all measurements collected during training, including per-step training loss, gradient norms, system throughput statistics, and downstream benchmark evaluation results on intermediate checkpoints. These are released through public Weights & Biases dashboards that allow interactive exploration, not just static tables. For AMBER, this includes the full training loss curve across all 360 checkpoints (shown in Figure 3 of the paper, declining from approximately 22 to 20 over the training run), and results on four Open LLM Leaderboard benchmarks (ARC, HellaSwag, MMLU, TruthfulQA) evaluated at every checkpoint (shown in Figure 4). The paper argues that even simple computed statistics from these metrics — such as variance or norm tracking — can reveal important training dynamics, citing the GLM team's use of gradient norm monitoring to develop a loss spike mitigation algorithm (Zeng et al., 2023).

The **design rationale** for this four-category structure is that these artifacts form a minimal complete set for scientific investigation of LLM training. Without data, you cannot study data influence or bias. Without code and configurations, you cannot reproduce results. Without intermediate checkpoints, you cannot study training dynamics or do continued pretraining from arbitrary points. Without metrics, you cannot diagnose training failures or understand how capabilities emerge. The framework does not specify how these artifacts should be structured technologically — that is up to the implementer — but it mandates their existence and public accessibility.

#### AMBER Data Preparation Pipeline

The data preparation for AMBER follows a process the paper describes as "similar to OpenLLaMA" (Section 4.1.1), with one notable deviation. The pretraining corpus is a mixture of three primary datasets: RefinedWeb (the filtered CommonCrawl subset from the Falcon project, Penedo et al., 2023), StarCoder (The Stack, Li et al., 2023, a corpus of permissively-licensed code), and RedPajama-v1 (an open reproduction of LLaMA's training data, Together Computer, 2023). Additionally, the paper includes C4 (Colossal Clean Crawled Corpus) in the mixture, which is the specific deviation from OpenLLaMA-v2.

The **design choice to include C4** is explicitly motivated: OpenLLaMA-v2 excluded C4 because RefinedWeb already deduplicated against it, meaning C4's content is largely redundant with RefinedWeb. However, the paper states that AMBER includes C4 "since we do not intend to introduce duplicated documents after the deduplication process conducted by RefinedWeb." This suggests that the team was comfortable with the RefinedWeb deduplication as sufficient to prevent harmful redundancy, and chose to include C4 to increase data volume and coverage. This is a concrete example of a data processing decision that, without explicit documentation, would be invisible to downstream users.

The precise data mixture is quantified in Table 2 of the paper, which reports token counts in billions:

- Arxiv: 30.00B tokens
- Book: 28.86B tokens
- C4: 197.67B tokens
- Refined-Web: 665.01B tokens
- StarCoder: 291.92B tokens
- StackExchange: 21.75B tokens
- Wikipedia: 23.90B tokens
- **Total: 1259.13B tokens (1.26T)**

The data processing procedure follows these steps, described in Section 4.1.1: all original datasets are combined without any further cleaning, filtering, or sub-sampling beyond what the source datasets themselves applied. The combined corpus then undergoes a **global permutation** — a single random shuffle across the entire dataset — and is partitioned evenly into 360 data chunks. This means each chunk contains approximately 3.5B tokens (1.26T ÷ 360), and the data ordering is fully randomized with respect to source, ensuring that the model sees a uniform mixture at every stage of training rather than sequential exposure to different data sources.

The paper explicitly states that the data is released **without additional cleaning or filtering**, which is a transparency choice: rather than apply proprietary quality filters that would be irreproducible, the team relies on the quality filtering already performed by the source dataset creators (RefinedWeb's filtering, RedPajama's deduplication, etc.) and releases the data as-is. This means that any quality issues in the source data propagate to AMBER's training, making the dataset a faithful representation of what was actually trained on rather than a curated idealization.

#### AMBER Model Architecture and Training Hyperparameters

The model architecture for AMBER is described as "the exact same model architecture as LLaMA 7B" (Section 4.1.1). This is a deliberate reproducibility choice: by using a well-known architecture, the paper minimizes the number of variables that could explain performance differences between AMBER and LLaMA. The specific architectural parameters, shown in Table 3, are:

- **Number of parameters: 6.7B** — the standard 7B-class transformer scale.
- **Hidden size: 4096** — the dimensionality of token representations throughout the transformer layers.
- **Intermediate size (in MLPs): 11008** — the dimensionality of the feed-forward network's inner layer, following the standard SwiGLU pattern where the intermediate size is roughly `$8/3 \times$` the hidden size (specifically `$2/3 \times 4 \times$` hidden size for the gated structure).
- **Number of attention heads: 32** — meaning each head operates on 128-dimensional projections (4096 ÷ 32).
- **Number of hidden layers: 32** — the depth of the transformer stack.
- **RMSNorm epsilon: 1e-6** — the small constant added to Root Mean Square normalization denominators for numerical stability.
- **Maximum sequence length: 2048** — the maximum number of tokens the model can attend to in a single forward pass.
- **Vocabulary size: 32000** — the number of tokens in the model's tokenizer.

The model incorporates **rotary positional embeddings (RoPE)** at each layer (Su et al., 2021). A specific implementation detail the paper highlights as important: "it is essential to keep the inverse frequency matrix in RoPE positional embedding in FP32" (Section 3). This aligns with observations from the Qwen team (Bai et al., 2023), and the paper presents it as an example of the kind of non-obvious configuration detail that would be invisible without source code or explicit documentation. In mixed-precision training where activations and gradients use BF16, the RoPE frequency matrix — which encodes the sinusoidal bases for position encoding — can silently degrade if stored in lower precision, causing positional information to lose fidelity, especially for distant token relationships. Keeping it in FP32 preserves the mathematical properties of the rotary encoding.

**Training hyperparameters.** The paper states that AMBER "followed the pre-training hyperparameters from LLaMA as closely as possible" (Section 4.1.1). The optimizer is **AdamW** (Kingma & Ba, 2017, with decoupled weight decay) with the following settings:

- **`$\beta_1 = 0.9$`** — the exponential decay rate for the first moment estimates (momentum).
- **`$\beta_2 = 0.95$`** — the exponential decay rate for the second moment estimates (variance scaling).
- **Initial learning rate `$\eta = 3 \times 10^{-4}$`** — the peak learning rate used after warmup and before cosine decay begins.
- **Final learning rate `$\eta = 3 \times 10^{-5}$`** — the minimum learning rate at the end of training, reached via a cosine schedule. The ratio of final to initial learning rate is 0.1.
- **Weight decay: 0.1** — the L2 regularization strength applied in the AdamW update separately from the adaptive learning rate.
- **Gradient clipping: 1.0** — gradients are clipped such that their global L2 norm does not exceed 1.0, preventing large gradient spikes from destabilizing training.
- **Warmup: 2,000 steps** — the learning rate is linearly increased from 0 to the initial value over the first 2000 optimization steps.

**Batch size deviation.** The paper notes one deliberate departure from LLaMA's configuration: LLaMA used a batch size of 2,048 sequences, but AMBER uses **2,240 sequences** (224 GPUs `$\times$` 10 sequences per GPU). This is described as "based on our hardware setting with 224 GPUs" — a practical constraint rather than a scientific choice. The slightly larger batch size means each optimization step processes approximately 4.6M tokens (2240 sequences `$\times$` 2048 tokens per sequence), compared to LLaMA's 4.2M tokens per step. The paper does not explore whether this batch size difference affects training dynamics, but notes it as a potential confounding variable in any LLaMA-to-AMBER performance comparison.

**Sequence length limitation.** The maximum sequence length of 2048 is a deliberate constraint that differs from more recent models using 4096 or longer contexts. This is a practical decision: longer sequences increase the memory footprint quadratically in the attention mechanism, and 2048 was the standard at the time of LLaMA's design. The paper does not discuss this as a limitation, but it means AMBER cannot process or generate sequences longer than 2048 tokens without additional techniques.

#### AMBER Training Infrastructure and Distributed System Design

The hardware and software infrastructure for AMBER training is described in Section 4.1.2. The system design decisions matter for two reasons: (1) they directly affect whether results can be reproduced on different hardware, and (2) the paper presents specific infrastructure observations as "take-home messages" for other teams undertaking LLM pretraining.

**Hardware topology.** AMBER is trained on a cluster consisting of **56 DGX A100 nodes**, each equipped with **4 `$\times$` 80GB A100 GPUs**, yielding a total of 224 GPUs. Within each node, the 4 GPUs are connected by **4-link NVLink**, providing high-bandwidth GPU-to-GPU communication within the node. Across nodes, connectivity uses **2 ports of 200 Gb/sec InfiniBand (4 `$\times$` HDR)**, where HDR (High Data Rate) InfiniBand provides 200 Gb/s per port. The factor "4 `$\times$` HDR" likely refers to link aggregation or the per-port bandwidth class. This topology means that intra-node communication is substantially faster than inter-node communication, which directly influences parallelism strategy choices.

**Distributed training framework.** The training code uses **lit-llama**, a framework built on PyTorch Lightning. The paper describes using **BF16 mixed precision** for training: activations and gradients are stored in BF16 (Brain Floating Point 16-bit, which has 8 exponent bits and 7 mantissa bits, providing the same dynamic range as FP32 but lower precision), while model weights are maintained in FP32. This is a standard choice for transformer training because BF16's larger dynamic range (compared to FP16) reduces the risk of overflow or underflow in gradient accumulation, while still providing the memory and throughput benefits of reduced precision.

**Throughput achieved.** The paper reports a throughput of approximately **582.4k tokens per second** across the 224-GPU cluster. This translates to roughly 2,600 tokens per second per GPU, or about one sequence of 2048 tokens per GPU every 0.8 seconds. Scaling up: at the batch size of 2,240 sequences and 4.6M tokens per step, each optimizer step takes approximately 7.9 seconds (4.6M tokens ÷ 582.4k tokens/second). The total training over 1.26T tokens would therefore require approximately 2.16M seconds, or about 600 GPU-hours, though wall-clock time depends on utilization efficiency.

**Parallelism strategy.** The paper does not specify the exact parallelism configuration for AMBER but offers a general observation in its "take-home messages" (Section 5) that "a hybrid and carefully tuned parallelism strategy—combining data, tensor-model, and pipeline (also referred to as 3D) parallelism strategies—achieves better system throughput than FSDP, especially in distributed clusters with limited intra-node bandwidth." This is presented as a lesson learned from CRYSTALCODER and subsequent efforts, implying that AMBER may have used a simpler parallelism approach (possibly FSDP alone, given that AMBER used lit-llama which is based on PyTorch Lightning with FSDP support). The 3D parallelism strategy refers to: (1) **data parallelism** — each GPU processes a different subset of the batch and gradients are averaged; (2) **tensor-model parallelism** — individual transformer layers are split across GPUs so each GPU holds a portion of the weight matrix; (3) **pipeline parallelism** — different layers of the model are assigned to different GPUs, with activations flowing between them in a staged fashion. The paper's claim is that combining all three (hence "3D") achieves better throughput than FSDP alone, particularly when cross-node bandwidth is the bottleneck.

**Checkpoint saving precision issue.** The paper documents a specific infrastructure bug: "In the initial phase of pre-training, our codebase had an issue where model checkpoints were saved with BF16 precision, despite our mixed precision training process maintaining model weights at FP32. This issue was later identified and rectified by our team, ensuring that all subsequent model checkpoints were saved with FP32 precision" (Section 4.1.5). The authors "anticipate that the initial BF16 model checkpoints may have contributed to some degree of accuracy drop in the model." This means that early checkpoints in the AMBER release have reduced weight precision, which could affect any research using those checkpoints as starting points for continued training. The honest disclosure of this issue is itself an example of the transparency the framework advocates.

**Missing optimizer states.** The paper also documents that "In our pre-training framework, we did not manage to save the optimizer states; we only saved model checkpoints for each data chunk" (Section 4.1.5). Optimizer states in AdamW consist of two moving-average buffers per parameter (the first moment estimate, `$m_t$`, and the second moment estimate, `$v_t$`), each the same size as the model parameters. These states encode the optimizer's "memory" of past gradients, and re-initializing them from scratch (which is what happens when resuming from a weights-only checkpoint) can cause a temporary training instability as the optimizer loses its accumulated gradient statistics. The authors hypothesize that this oversight "might be the cause of the NaN loss issue observed in the four data chunks," because each time training resumed after a NaN-loss skip, the freshly initialized optimizer would not have the momentum and variance estimates needed for stable updates on the problematic data.

---

#### NaN Loss Handling and the "Misbehaved" Data Chunks

The paper documents a specific training failure mode encountered during AMBER pretraining: **NaN (Not a Number) loss on four out of 360 data chunks** (Section 4.1.5). A NaN loss means that at some point during processing of those chunks, the loss value became numerically invalid — typically because of an arithmetic overflow or a division by zero in the attention softmax or normalization layers, which can propagate through gradients and corrupt model weights. The paper's handling of this issue illustrates both practical training engineering and a transparency commitment.

The initial response was to "tentatively skip the entire data chunk" whenever NaN loss occurred, then resume training from the last valid checkpoint. The plan was to revisit these four skipped chunks later in training — once the model was more stable — and train on them to complete the intended data coverage. However, the paper reports that "we found that these data chunks tend to cause NaN loss regardless of the position of training" (Section 4.1.5). This is a significant empirical finding: some data chunks appear to be inherently problematic for the training configuration, not merely unlucky with respect to model state. The team tested this by "moving the 'misbehaved' data chunks to the end of the training but still observe NaN losses" (Section 5). The final resolution was to substitute the four skipped chunks with the first four data chunks from the training sequence (effectively training on these twice) to complete the learning rate schedule with the correct number of steps.

The paper does not determine the root cause of these NaN-triggering data chunks — possibilities mentioned include "certain random states, the training precision, or data quality issues" (Section 5). The missing optimizer states (requiring fresh Adam initialization after each NaN-event restart) are identified as a potential contributing factor. The significance for the LLM360 framework is that this failure mode is documented, the affected chunks are identified, and their content can be inspected by anyone in the released dataset — researchers can study what about those specific chunks caused training instability, which is exactly the kind of investigation that opaque releases prevent.

---

#### AMBERCHAT and AMBERSAFE Fine-Tuning

In addition to the base AMBER model, the paper releases two fine-tuned variants to demonstrate the downstream utility of fully transparent base models. These follow standard fine-tuning recipes and are included primarily as starting points for community use, not as methodological contributions.

**AMBERCHAT** is fine-tuned on the **evolved instruct training data** from WizardLM (Xu et al., 2023), a dataset of instruction-following examples where simpler instructions have been programmatically evolved into more complex ones. The fine-tuning uses **FastChat** (Zheng et al., 2023) as the training framework, with: **3 epochs** of training, **8 A100 GPUs (80GB)** distributed by **FSDP** (Fully Sharded Data Parallel, where model parameters, gradients, and optimizer states are sharded across GPUs), learning rate **`$2 \times 10^{-5}$`**, gradient accumulation steps **16**, warmup ratio **0.04** (meaning 4% of total training steps are spent linearly increasing the learning rate from 0 to the target).

**AMBERSAFE** is an aligned version produced through **Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)** (Rafailov et al., 2023). DPO is a fine-tuning method that directly optimizes the model to prefer chosen responses over rejected ones, without training a separate reward model as in RLHF. The training uses: the **ShareGPT 90K** dataset (90,000 conversation examples from the ShareGPT platform) followed by the **SafeRLHF dataset** (Ji et al., 2023, a human preference dataset designed for safety alignment). The DPO hyperparameters are: `$\beta = 0.1$` (the temperature-like parameter controlling how strongly the model is pulled toward preferred responses versus staying close to the reference distribution), gradient accumulation steps **4**, and learning rate **`$5 \times 10^{-7}$`**. An interesting note: the paper specifies that the base model for AMBERSAFE is "checkpoint 355 instead of the last checkpoint," meaning the alignment training starts from a slightly earlier state of the base model, though the paper does not explain the rationale for this choice.

**Performance observation.** The paper makes a brief but intriguing observation about AMBERCHAT's performance: "AMBERCHAT simply trained on ShareGPT 90K also demonstrates much higher performance than our base model, which is slightly different from the trends shown on other models in the table" (Section 4.1.4). This refers to Table 4, where AMBERCHAT's average score (49.12) is substantially higher than AMBER's base score (45.20), a larger gap than typically seen between base and chat versions in other model families. The paper explicitly leaves investigation of this discrepancy to future work, but the transparency of the release — having both the base checkpoint and the fine-tuning code public — means anyone can investigate whether this gap reflects genuine instruction-following improvement, benchmark contamination in the fine-tuning data, or some other factor.

#### CRYSTALCODER Data Preparation and Three-Stage Training

CRYSTALCODER represents a more sophisticated pretraining recipe than AMBER, designed to produce a model that balances English language understanding and code generation capability. The data preparation and training schedule are deliberately structured, and the paper explicitly contrasts the approach with Code Llama's sequential strategy.

**Dataset composition.** The pretraining data blends **SlimPajama** (Soboleva et al., 2023, a cleaned and deduplicated version of RedPajama containing 627B tokens) with **StarCoder data** (Li et al., 2023, the permissively-licensed code corpus), yielding approximately **1382B tokens (1.38T)** total. SlimPajama serves as the English language component; StarCoder provides the code component across multiple programming languages.

**Training schedule design.** Unlike Code Llama's approach of training on language data first, then code data second (a sequential, two-phase curriculum), CRYSTALCODER uses a **three-stage training schedule** that gradually blends language and code throughout:

**Stage 1:** Training on half of the SlimPajama data, totaling approximately **345B tokens**. This stage is purely English language text, establishing the model's base linguistic competence from a clean, high-quality corpus.

**Stage 2:** The remaining half of SlimPajama is combined with **two epochs of StarCoder data**, totaling approximately **927B tokens**. Since StarCoder contains 291.9B tokens in AMBER's mixture (and likely a similar amount here), two epochs would be approximately 584B tokens of code, mixed with approximately 345B tokens of language, yielding the ~927B total. This stage is the bulk of training where the model learns both language and code simultaneously.

**Stage 3:** Training on a curated subset of the StarCoder data — specifically **Python and web-related data** (HTML, JavaScript, and CSS) — totaling **100B tokens**, with an additional **10B tokens** sampled from SlimPajama to maintain language performance. This stage focuses the model on the programming languages most relevant to web development and Python scripting, which are the most commonly evaluated code generation domains.

The **design rationale** for this gradual blending approach over sequential training is stated succinctly: "to provide a balance between code and general ability" (Section 4.2). In sequential training, the model may catastrophically forget language capabilities during the code-only phase, requiring careful replay or regularization. By blending code into the language data throughout stages 2 and 3, CRYSTALCODER maintains language performance while acquiring code capabilities. The release includes "preprocessed data and data mixing scripts" (Section 4.2) so that the exact blending ratios and ordering can be studied or reproduced.

---

#### CRYSTALCODER Model Architecture and μP

CRYSTALCODER's architecture builds on LLaMA's design but introduces several modifications, described in Section 4.2.

**Base LLaMA 7B architecture.** The core structure — 32 layers, 32 attention heads, 4096 hidden size, 2048 maximum sequence length — follows the same pattern as AMBER, with exceptions noted below.

**Maximal Update Parameterization (μP).** The paper states that CRYSTALCODER "incorporates maximal update parameterization (muP)" (Yang et al., 2022). μP is a specific scaling rule for initializing and training neural networks such that the optimal hyperparameters for a small model can be directly transferred to a larger model without tuning. The key idea is that at initialization, each parameter matrix is scaled so that the activations and gradients have well-controlled magnitudes regardless of width, and the learning rates are scaled inversely with width. For transformers, this means: the weight matrices in attention and MLP layers are initialized with smaller variance for wider models; the learning rate decreases with model width; and the output logits are scaled down by the width factor before the softmax. The practical benefit for CRYSTALCODER is that hyperparameters tuned on a smaller proxy model could be transferred, reducing the need for expensive ablations at the 7B scale. The paper does not report the specific μP hyperparameters (base width, transfer width) used.

**Restricted RoPE.** The paper applies "RoPE restricted to the first 25% of hidden dimensions." This is a modification to the standard rotary position embedding implementation, referenced as "similar to the implementation of GPT-NeoX." In standard RoPE, every pair of dimensions in the query and key vectors receives a rotation by a frequency-dependent angle, encoding positional information throughout the entire representation. Restricting RoPE to only 25% of dimensions means that for a 4096-dimensional hidden state, only the first 1024 dimensions receive positional encoding via rotation, while the remaining 3072 dimensions carry position-independent information. This has been found in some architectures (notably GPT-NeoX-20B) to improve training stability or downstream performance, possibly because it reserves some representational capacity for content-based attention patterns that are not confounded by position.

**LayerNorm instead of RMSNorm.** The paper uses "LayerNorm instead of RMSNorm since the CG-1 architecture supports efficient computation for vanilla LayerNorm." RMSNorm (Root Mean Square Normalization) is a simplification of LayerNorm that omits the mean-centering step and only scales by the root-mean-square of activations. It is computationally cheaper and has become the standard in many transformer implementations (including LLaMA's RMSNorm). CRYSTALCODER reverts to standard LayerNorm because the **Cerebras Condor Galaxy 1 (CG-1)** supercomputer — on which CRYSTALCODER is trained — has hardware-level support for efficient LayerNorm computation, eliminating the usual performance penalty. This is an example of an architectural choice driven by hardware characteristics, and the public disclosure of this rationale enables others to understand why a seemingly "outdated" normalization choice was made.

**Vocabulary size.** CRYSTALCODER uses an embedding dimension (vocabulary size) of **32032**, slightly larger than AMBER's 32000. This small difference — 32 additional tokens — likely reflects minor tokenizer differences, possibly adding special tokens for code-related syntax or formatting.

**Compute infrastructure.** The paper reports that CRYSTALCODER is trained on the **Cerebras Condor Galaxy 1 (CG-1)**, described as "a 4 exaFLOPS, 54 million core, 64-node cloud AI supercomputer." The Cerebras architecture is fundamentally different from the GPU cluster used for AMBER: instead of small-batch distributed training across many GPUs, Cerebras systems use wafer-scale processors with massive on-chip memory, designed to train models with very large batch sizes on a single device or a few devices. This means CRYSTALCODER's training dynamics — batch size, gradient accumulation behavior, optimizer step frequency — may differ from GPU-trained models in ways that are not detailed in this paper but are inherent in the hardware choice.

#### ANALYSIS360 and the Memorization Study Protocol

ANALYSIS360 is presented as an "organized repository that analyzes the model behavior on various aspects" (Section 4.3). It is not a single tool but a collection of analysis scripts applied across all released checkpoints, with results published alongside the checkpoints. The memorization study in Section 4.3 serves as both a substantive finding and a demonstration of what checkpoints-and-data release enables.

**Memorization score definition.** The paper adopts the metric from Biderman et al. (2023):

$$\text{score}(k, l) = \frac{1}{l} \sum_{i=1}^{l} \mathbb{1}[S_{k+i} = G_{k+i}]$$

where `$S_{0:k+l}$` is the original sequence from the training data, `$k$` is the prompt length (the number of initial tokens given to the model as context), `$l$` is the continuation length (the number of subsequent tokens the model generates), `$S_{k+i}$` is the `$i$`-th token of the ground-truth continuation, and `$G_{k+i}$` is the `$i$`-th token generated by the model when conditioned on the prompt `$S_{0:k}$`. The indicator function `$\mathbb{1}[\cdot]$` equals 1 when the generated token matches the ground truth and 0 otherwise.

**What it computes:** the fraction of a length-`$l$` continuation that the model reproduces exactly from its training data when given a length-`$k$` prompt from that same data. A score of 1 means the model perfectly regenerated every token — a phenomenon called **k-extractible** (Carlini et al., 2021), meaning the training data can be extracted by providing a `$k$`-token prompt.

**Why this form:** the averaging across `$l$` tokens provides a continuous score from 0 to 1, allowing partial memorization (where the model reproduces some but not all tokens) to be distinguished from complete extraction. The binary indicator per token is appropriate because exact match is the relevant criterion for data leakage — near-matches do not constitute extraction. The choice of `$k$` and `$l$` determines the sensitivity: larger `$k$` makes extraction harder (requires more context to trigger memorization); larger `$l$` measures longer-range memorization. The paper follows prior work in setting `$k = l = 32$`.

**Experimental protocol.** The memorization study samples **1,000 sequences from each of the 360 data chunks** (360,000 total sequences), and uses "the first 64 tokens of each sequence" to conduct the experiments. For each checkpoint, the analysis only includes data chunks that the checkpoint has already been trained on — so checkpoint 100 is evaluated on chunks 1–100, and checkpoint 360 on all 360 chunks. This per-checkpoint restriction is crucial because it measures memorization of seen data, not generalization to unseen data. The methodology requires the full dataset and all checkpoints to be available — it is precisely the kind of study that is impossible with final-model-only releases.

**Key findings from the memorization analysis.** The paper presents three specific observations, each backed by figures:

1. **Over 1% of sequences are 32-extractible** (Figure 7): across all checkpoints, more than 1% of the tested sequences achieve a memorization score of exactly 1, meaning the model can perfectly reproduce at least 32 tokens of continuation from a 32-token prompt. The paper notes this is "consistent with prior work" (Carlini et al., 2022; Biderman et al., 2023).

2. **Memorization increases with training** (Figure 7): the distribution of memorization scores shifts rightward (toward higher scores) as checkpoint index increases, indicating that the model gradually memorizes more of its training data as it sees the data more times or as training progresses.

3. **Memorization is highest on recently seen data** (Figure 8): when plotting memorization score per data chunk group for each checkpoint, the "latest seen" chunks (those closest to the checkpoint's training position) show the highest memorization scores. After a chunk is no longer the most recent, its memorization score initially drops slightly but then "keeps increasing afterwards" — suggesting an initial forgetting period followed by consolidation.

4. **Strong correlation between checkpoints** (Figure 9): the per-sequence memorization scores and k-extractible status are highly correlated across checkpoints, meaning that sequences that are memorized by early checkpoints tend to remain memorized, and sequences that are not memorized early rarely become extractible later.

These findings collectively paint a picture of memorization as a gradual, path-dependent process: the model accumulates memorized sequences over training, prioritizes recently seen data, and exhibits strong persistence — once a sequence is memorized, it is rarely forgotten. The availability of all checkpoints and the exact training data makes this analysis possible; without them, one could only measure memorization on the final model with unknown data exposure.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Full Training Transparency as a First-Class Research Contribution, Not Just Reproducibility Infrastructure

The most distinctive intellectual move in this paper is its reframing of "open-source release" from a post-hoc documentation task into a **scientific contribution in its own right**. Prior to LLM360, the dominant assumption in the field was that releasing a model's weights (and perhaps a technical report summarizing architecture and high-level data sources) constituted "openness." The paper's Table 1 makes this assumption visible by cataloging seven dimensions of transparency and showing that no recent model—not even those widely celebrated as "open"—checks more than a handful of them. LLaMA checks two, Llama 2 checks two, Mistral checks one (and that one is marked with a question mark). This table is the paper's most powerful rhetorical device: it transforms transparency from a binary label ("open" vs. "closed") into a **multi-dimensional spectrum**, and in doing so reveals that the field has been systematically retreating from transparency even as it has been celebrating openness.

What makes this a genuine innovation rather than merely a call for better documentation is the **specificity of the framework**. The paper does not argue vaguely for "more transparency"; it defines exactly four artifact categories that constitute a complete release, and argues—through concrete examples of research questions each category enables—that omitting any one category breaks the chain of scientific inquiry. The argument about intermediate checkpoints is illustrative: the paper does not simply say "checkpoints are useful" but rather demonstrates that research on emergent abilities (Wei et al., 2022), scaling dynamics (Biderman et al., 2023), and domain adaptation all require access to training trajectories, not just endpoints. The memorization study in Section 4.3 is not presented as a finding about AMBER per se, but as **proof of concept** for what the framework makes possible: tracking which exact training sequences become memorized, at what point in training, and how memorization persists or fades—an analysis that requires the simultaneous availability of all checkpoints and the exact data ordering described in Section 4.1.1.

The significance of this framing extends beyond the paper's own models. By establishing a **taxonomy of transparency**, LLM360 creates a benchmark against which all future LLM releases can be evaluated. A model that releases only weights and a blog post is now visibly incomplete against the LLM360 standard, not just "closed" in some binary sense. This shifts the burden of justification: the default expectation becomes full disclosure, and any omission requires an explicit rationale. The paper's honest documentation of its own transparency gaps—the missing optimizer states, the early BF16-precision checkpoints, the NaN-triggering data chunks that were skipped—models the exact behavior it advocates: **transparency about failures is as important as transparency about successes** (Section 4.1.5).

This is a **fundamental reframing**, not an incremental improvement. Prior transparency efforts like Pythia (Biderman et al., 2023) released checkpoints and data but did not articulate a generalizable framework; they were a specific release, not a standard. LLM360 elevates the release practice itself to the status of a methodological contribution, arguing that *how* a model is released determines *what science* can be done with it—and therefore the release specification is as much a part of the research output as the model weights.

### Innovation 2: The Performance-Transparency Gap as an Empirical Diagnostic

The paper makes a striking empirical observation that is never stated explicitly as a finding but permeates the results: **AMBER's performance lags behind LLaMA-7B despite using "the exact same model architecture" and "following the pre-training hyperparameters from LLaMA as closely as possible"** (Section 4.1.1). Table 4 shows the gap clearly: AMBER achieves 45.20 average on the Open LLM Leaderboard versus LLaMA-7B's 49.69—a difference of roughly 4.5 percentage points. On MMLU, the gap is 30.76 versus 35.67. On ARC, it is 41.89 versus 50.94.

This performance gap is not a failure of the LLM360 approach; it is **the most important scientific result the framework produces**. By controlling for architecture and hyperparameters—releasing them openly and documenting every deviation (Section 4.1.1)—the paper isolates the remaining sources of performance difference to factors that are precisely those most often withheld in opaque releases: **data processing quality, data mixture optimization, and undisclosed training infrastructure details**. The paper explicitly acknowledges this in its take-home messages: "A key omission in LLaMA's technical report is a detailed description of their exact pre-training dataset" (Section 5). The performance gap between AMBER and LLaMA is thus not noise; it is a **quantitative measurement of how much model quality depends on the undisclosed details**.

This finding changes how the field should interpret the performance claims in technical reports. When LLaMA reports a benchmark score, that score is the product of architecture + hyperparameters + **unknown data processing and quality decisions**. Without releasing the data processing pipeline, the community cannot determine whether LLaMA's advantage over, say, OpenLLaMA or AMBER comes from architectural superiority or simply from better data cleaning—a scientifically critical distinction. The LLM360 framework makes this diagnostic possible by providing a clean baseline: a model with known-everything that serves as a lower bound on what can be achieved with full transparency. Any model that outperforms AMBER while disclosing fewer artifacts is implicitly claiming that its undisclosed components are worth roughly the observed performance gap.

The **practical implication** is equally significant: the paper demonstrates that the field's standard of "open-source" (releasing weights with a high-level data description) is not sufficient for reproducibility, because the OpenLLaMA and RedPajama projects attempted to recreate LLaMA's training data from its disclosed sources and "still do not fully reproduce its benchmark evaluations" (Section 1). This is cited directly in the paper as evidence that "additional data processing or training procedures may be necessary" beyond what is described. The performance-transparency gap thus serves as **an empirical diagnostic for incomplete disclosure**: if an open reproduction cannot match the original model's performance, some important component was not disclosed, and the field needs to know what that component is.

This is a **diagnostic concept** rather than a metric gain—a framework for measuring the cost of opacity in quantitative terms. CRYSTALCODER's results (Table 5) strengthen this interpretation: by carefully crafting its data mixture across three stages, it achieves competitive performance with LLaMA-7B on both language and code tasks (50.92 language average vs. LLaMA's 49.69, plus strong code scores where LLaMA has essentially none), suggesting that **thoughtful data engineering disclosed openly** can match or exceed the performance of models that rely on undisclosed data processing. The performance-transparency gap is thus not an immutable law but a challenge—and one that LLM360's framework is designed to help the community systematically address.

### Innovation 3: Multi-Stage Blended Pretraining as a Departure from Sequential Domain Curricula

CRYSTALCODER introduces a training schedule that the paper explicitly positions as divergent from the dominant paradigm. The established approach for code-language models—exemplified by Code Llama (Roziere et al., 2023)—is **sequential**: train on language data first, then code data second. This sequential curriculum reflects an implicit assumption that language competence must be established before code capability is introduced, or that code training may catastrophically interfere with language skills if introduced too early.

CRYSTALCODER rejects this assumption. Its three-stage schedule (Section 4.2) blends code data into language data **throughout training**, starting with pure language (Stage 1: 345B tokens of SlimPajama), then mixing language and code data together (Stage 2: the remaining SlimPajama plus two epochs of StarCoder, totaling ~927B tokens), and finally focusing on high-value code subsets while maintaining a small language data stream (Stage 3: 100B tokens of Python/web code plus 10B tokens of SlimPajama). The design rationale is stated directly: "to provide a balance between code and general ability."

What makes this a genuine innovation is that the paper provides **the exact data mixing scripts** as part of its release, enabling the community to study whether blended training outperforms sequential training on equivalent data—a question that could not be addressed with Code Llama's opaque release or with any model that doesn't disclose its training schedule in actionable detail. The gradual blending approach represents a **hypothesis about transfer learning dynamics**: that interleaving language and code data may allow the model to develop shared representations that benefit both modalities simultaneously, rather than compartmentalizing language knowledge and code knowledge into separate training phases where the second phase may overwrite the first.

The empirical evidence in Table 5 supports this hypothesis, but with important nuance. CRYSTALCODER achieves 50.92 average on language tasks (ARC, HellaSwag, MMLU, TruthfulQA) versus CodeLlama-7B's 42.42—a substantial gap in language performance—while achieving 32.38 average on code tasks (HumanEval, MBPP) versus CodeLlama's 37.45. This tradeoff suggests that blended training **preserves language capability better than sequential training, at some cost to peak code performance**. Whether this tradeoff is desirable depends on the application, but the key intellectual contribution is that the paper makes the tradeoff visible and studiable by releasing the exact schedule. Without this transparency, researchers comparing code-language models would be unable to determine whether performance differences stem from architecture, data quantity, or training schedule design—a classic case of confounding variables that LLM360's framework disentangles.

This is an **incremental architectural contribution** (the multi-stage schedule itself) combined with a **fundamental transparency contribution** (releasing the schedule as reproducible configuration). The schedule design is not theoretically novel—curriculum learning and data mixing are well-studied—but its application to code-language pretraining at the 7B scale, with full disclosure, creates a new baseline for the community to iterate on. The paper's approach of "release the schedule, let the community optimize it" represents a different research model from the dominant "optimize the schedule internally, release only the final model" paradigm, and its value proposition is that distributed optimization across many research groups may converge faster than internal optimization at a single lab.

### Innovation 4: The "Misbehaved Data Chunk" Phenomenon as a New Category of Training Failure Mode

The paper's documentation of NaN losses on four specific data chunks (Section 4.1.5) may initially appear to be a mundane engineering detail—training instabilities are common in large-scale ML. But the paper's handling of this issue reveals a **novel empirical phenomenon** with implications for how the field understands training data quality: these chunks "tend to cause NaN loss regardless of the position of training." The team tested this hypothesis by attempting to train on the problematic chunks at different points in the training run (early, late) and found consistent NaN-triggering behavior. This rules out explanations based on model state (e.g., "the model was unstable at that point in training") and points toward intrinsic properties of the data chunks themselves.

This is significant because it identifies a category of training data that is not merely "low quality" in the sense of being noisy, irrelevant, or repetitive—standard data filtering concerns—but is **actively toxic to the training process**: it causes the optimization to become numerically invalid. The paper does not determine the root cause (possibilities include "certain random states, the training precision, or data quality issues," Section 5), but the existence of such data chunks—identifiable only through expensive trial-and-error during training—raises a question that the field has not systematically addressed: **what properties make a data sequence cause NaN loss, and can these properties be detected before training?**

The transparency of the LLM360 release transforms this from an internal engineering annoyance into a **research opportunity**. Because the paper releases the exact data chunks, researchers can inspect the four problematic chunks and compare them to the 356 chunks that trained successfully. They can look for statistical anomalies, token distribution outliers, encoding errors, or textual patterns that correlate with NaN-triggering. This kind of investigation is categorically impossible with opaque model releases—if a proprietary model's training encounters NaN loss and the data is not released, the phenomenon remains a private troubleshooting exercise. By documenting and releasing the problematic chunks, LLM360 creates a **shared empirical resource** for studying a training failure mode that likely affects many LLM training runs but has never been systematically characterized in the literature.

The paper's missing optimizer states (Section 4.1.5) add another dimension to this analysis. The authors hypothesize that the NaN losses may have been exacerbated by the re-initialization of Adam moving averages each time training resumed after a skip, since the optimizer's accumulated gradient statistics provide stability against precisely the kind of large-gradient events that cause NaN loss. This means the problematic chunks may have been marginal—trainable with full optimizer state but toxic when optimizer state is reset—or they may have been truly untrainable under any reasonable configuration. The released data enables researchers to distinguish between these cases by attempting to reproduce the training with optimizer state continuity, making the LLM360 release an experimental platform, not just an archival record.

This innovation is a **new diagnostic concept**—the identification of "misbehaved data chunks" as a distinct category of training data quality issue that is orthogonal to traditional concerns like factual accuracy or bias. Its significance lies not in a performance improvement but in **making visible a previously invisible failure mode** and providing the artifacts needed to study it systematically. This is exactly the kind of contribution the LLM360 framework is designed to enable: discoveries that arise not from new algorithms but from the ability to inspect the full training process.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** AMBER is evaluated on the four benchmark datasets from the Open LLM Leaderboard (HuggingFaceH4): ARC (AI2 Reasoning Challenge, 25-shot), HellaSwag (10-shot), MMLU (Massive Multitask Language Understanding, 5-shot), and TruthfulQA (0-shot). CRYSTALCODER is evaluated on these same four benchmarks plus two code generation benchmarks: HumanEval pass@1 and MBPP pass@1. All evaluations follow the standard few-shot prompting configurations specified by the Open LLM Leaderboard framework.

- **Base model(s).** AMBER is a 6.7B-parameter English LLM trained from scratch on 1.26T tokens using the LLaMA 7B architecture (32 layers, 4096 hidden size, 32 attention heads, 2048 maximum sequence length). CRYSTALCODER is a 6.7B-parameter English and code LLM trained on 1.38T tokens with architectural modifications (restricted RoPE to 25% of hidden dimensions, maximal update parameterization, LayerNorm instead of RMSNorm, vocabulary size 32032). Both models are chosen as initial instantiations of the LLM360 framework at the 7B scale. Fine-tuned variants (AMBERCHAT, AMBERSAFE) are also evaluated.

- **Metrics.** The primary metric is **benchmark accuracy** for each evaluation dataset, computed as the fraction of test examples for which the model's generated answer matches the ground truth. For ARC, HellaSwag, MMLU, and TruthfulQA, the specific metric is the standard accuracy score defined by the Open LLM Leaderboard's evaluation harness. For HumanEval and MBPP, the metric is **pass@1** — the fraction of programming problems for which the model's first generated solution passes all unit tests. The averaged accuracy across the four language benchmarks and across the two code benchmarks is reported as a composite score. The compilation of scores across all 360 AMBER checkpoints and 143 CRYSTALCODER checkpoints enables per-checkpoint analysis.

- **Baselines.** The paper compares AMBER against a set of contemporaneous 7B-scale open-source models released around the same time: LLaMA-7B (Touvron et al., 2023), LLaMA2-7B (Touvron et al., 2023), OpenLLaMA-v1-7B (Geng & Liu, 2023), OpenLLaMA-v2-7B, Falcon-7B (Almazrouei et al., 2023), MPT-7B (MosaicML, 2023), RedPajama-INCITE-7B-Base, RedPajama-INCITE-7B-Instruct, RedPajama-INCITE-7B-Chat (Together Computer, 2023), and fine-tuned variants Falcon-7B-instruct, LLaMA2-7B-chat. For CRYSTALCODER, comparisons include Mistral-7B (Jiang et al., 2023), CodeLlama-7B (Roziere et al., 2023), StarCoder-15B (Li et al., 2023), and the language-only models from AMBER's comparison set. All baseline scores are drawn from the Open LLM Leaderboard at the time of writing.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** There is no generation budget comparison in this paper. The experiments are **evaluation-only**: all models are compared on their final (or intermediate) benchmark performance without any test-time compute scaling, search, or sampling strategies. The primary "compute" dimension is not inference budget but rather **pretraining compute** — AMBER is trained on 1.26T tokens, CRYSTALCODER on 1.38T tokens, and comparisons reflect the performance achieved at those token counts rather than any controllable inference-time variable. For the memorization study, the computational protocol involves sampling 1,000 sequences from each data chunk and generating 32-token continuations using a fixed prompt length of 32 tokens.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** There is no cross-validation or statistical significance testing reported. The benchmark evaluations are single-run scores computed on the standard test splits of each dataset. The memorization study does not report confidence intervals or variance estimates. The checkpoint-level analysis in Figures 4 and 6 provides a form of trajectory-level evidence — showing how performance evolves over training — which partially mitigates the absence of statistical testing by demonstrating consistent directional trends rather than point estimates. However, the final checkpoint scores reported in Tables 4 and 5 are point estimates without error bars.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### AMBER Benchmark Performance and Comparison to Contemporaneous Models

The headline result for AMBER is its **average score of 45.20 across the four Open LLM Leaderboard benchmarks** (ARC: 41.89, HellaSwag: 74.14, MMLU: 30.76, TruthfulQA: 34.00), placing it below most comparable 7B models released around the same time (Table 4). LLaMA-7B achieves 49.69 average — a gap of roughly 4.5 points — despite AMBER using "the exact same model architecture as LLaMA 7B" and "following the pre-training hyperparameters from LLaMA as closely as possible" (Section 4.1.1). The gap is largest on ARC (41.89 vs. 50.94, a difference of roughly 9 points) and MMLU (30.76 vs. 35.67), while HellaSwag is relatively close (74.14 vs. 77.80). TruthfulQA shows a smaller absolute gap (34.00 vs. 34.34) but both models score only modestly above random performance.

Fine-tuning partially closes the gap. The aligned variant **AMBERSAFE achieves 53.15 average** — outperforming the base AMBER by roughly 7.9 points and even surpassing LLaMA-7B's 49.69. This places AMBERSAFE third in Table 4, behind LLaMA2-7B-chat (56.34) and LLaMA2-7B (53.39), but ahead of LLaMA-7B (49.69). The instruction-tuned **AMBERCHAT achieves 49.12**, which is competitive with OpenLLaMA-v2-7B (48.18) and substantially above AMBER's base score. The paper observes that "AMBERCHAT simply trained on ShareGPT 90K also demonstrates much higher performance than our base model, which is slightly different from the trends shown on other models in the table" (Section 4.1.4), though no quantitative analysis of this gap is provided.

The model ranks near the bottom of the evaluated set, with only RedPajama-INCITE-7B-Base (44.65) and RedPajama-INCITE-7B-Chat (43.98) scoring lower among the base and chat models compared. OpenLLaMA-v1-7B (46.08), Falcon-7B (47.01), and MPT-7B (47.38) all exceed AMBER by margins of roughly 1–2 points.

#### Checkpoint-Level Trajectories Reveal Divergent Skill Acquisition Patterns

The evaluation across all 360 checkpoints (Figure 4) reveals that different capabilities follow qualitatively different developmental trajectories during pretraining. HellaSwag and ARC scores **monotonically increase** throughout training: ARC rises from approximately 0.25 at early checkpoints to roughly 0.42 at the final checkpoint, while HellaSwag climbs from roughly 0.30 to 0.74. This smooth, monotonic improvement suggests that commonsense reasoning and basic knowledge retrieval are capabilities that continuously improve as the model processes more data.

MMLU shows a more complex pattern: "the score decreases in the initial stage of pretraining and then starts to increase" (Section 4.1.4). Figure 4 shows MMLU starting around 0.26, dipping to approximately 0.24 around checkpoint 50, then recovering and rising to roughly 0.31 by the final checkpoint. This non-monotonic trajectory is notable — it suggests that early pretraining may interfere with the model's ability to answer knowledge-intensive multiple-choice questions, perhaps because the model is initially learning surface-level statistical patterns that must be reorganized as more structured knowledge is acquired.

TruthfulQA exhibits the most striking behavior: its score **decreases** as training proceeds. Figure 4 shows TruthfulQA starting around 0.55–0.60 at early checkpoints and declining to roughly 0.34 at the final checkpoint. This is a previously documented phenomenon — as models become more capable at mimicking human text, they may also become more susceptible to generating plausible-sounding falsehoods rather than truthful responses. The paper observes this trend but does not analyze it further, noting it alongside the MMLU pattern as "interesting" but leaving detailed investigation to future work.

#### CRYSTALCODER Achieves Balanced Language-Code Performance

CRYSTALCODER's headline result is its **balanced performance across both language and code tasks** (Table 5). On language benchmarks, CRYSTALCODER achieves 50.92 average (ARC: 47.01, HellaSwag: 71.97, MMLU: 48.78, TruthfulQA: 35.91), which is competitive with LLaMA-7B's 49.69 and slightly above OpenLLaMA-v2-7B's 48.18. On code benchmarks, CRYSTALCODER achieves 32.38 average (HumanEval: 28.38, MBPP: 36.38), which is below CodeLlama-7B's 37.45 but substantially above all language-only models (LLaMA2-7B: 16.57 code average, LLaMA-7B: 13.83, Falcon-7B: 11.41).

The overall average of language and code scores yields 41.65 for CRYSTALCODER, placing it behind Mistral-7B (48.68) but ahead of CodeLlama-7B (39.94) and all language-only models. The paper characterizes CRYSTALCODER as striking "a good balance between both language and code tasks" (Section 4.2). Compared to CodeLlama-7B, CRYSTALCODER trades roughly 5 points of code performance (32.38 vs. 37.45) for roughly 8.5 points of language performance (50.92 vs. 42.42), suggesting that the blended three-stage training schedule (Section 4.2) preserves language ability at the cost of some peak code specialization.

The checkpoint-level trajectories for CRYSTALCODER (Figure 6) show qualitatively different patterns from AMBER. The three training stages are marked by grey vertical dashed lines, and several benchmarks show distinct behavioral shifts at stage transitions. ARC rises steadily from approximately 0.25 to roughly 0.47 across all three stages. HellaSwag climbs from approximately 0.30 to roughly 0.72, with a possible acceleration in Stage 3. MMLU shows a striking increase during Stage 2 — rising from roughly 0.25 to roughly 0.48 — and then stabilizes in Stage 3. TruthfulQA follows the same declining pattern as AMBER, dropping from roughly 0.42 to 0.36. HumanEval and MBPP show minimal performance during Stage 1 (language-only), begin rising during Stage 2 (when code data is introduced), and continue improving through Stage 3 (focused on Python and web languages), reaching approximately 0.28 and 0.36 respectively by the final checkpoint.

#### AMBERCHAT and AMBERSAFE Fine-Tuning Results

The fine-tuned AMBER variants demonstrate substantial improvements over the base model. AMBERSAFE achieves the strongest results: 45.22 on ARC, 74.14 on HellaSwag (essentially unchanged from base), 37.78 on MMLU, and 55.44 on TruthfulQA. The TruthfulQA score of 55.44 is notably high — it outperforms LLaMA2-7B-chat (45.57) by roughly 10 points and represents a dramatic reversal from AMBER's declining TruthfulQA trajectory during pretraining (Figure 4). This suggests that safety alignment through DPO on the SafeRLHF dataset directly addresses the truthfulness degradation observed during pretraining, though the paper does not analyze which component of the DPO training produced this effect.

AMBERCHAT achieves 42.83 on ARC, 74.03 on HellaSwag, 38.88 on MMLU, and 40.72 on TruthfulQA, yielding a 49.12 average. The improvement over base AMBER (45.20) is roughly 4 points, which the paper notes is "slightly different from the trends shown on other models in the table" — referring to the fact that other model families in Table 4 typically show smaller gaps between base and chat variants (e.g., Falcon-7B at 47.01 vs. Falcon-7B-instruct at 46.69, a slight decrease; RedPajama-INCITE-7B-Base at 44.65 vs. RedPajama-INCITE-7B-Instruct at 46.93, a modest increase). The paper does not investigate this anomaly further.

#### Memorization Study Results (ANALYSIS360)

The memorization analysis across AMBER checkpoints yields three specific quantitative findings:

**Memorization prevalence.** "More than 1% of the sequences are 32-extractible from AMBER" (Section 4.3). Figure 7 displays the distribution of memorization scores for 10 selected checkpoints, with the percentage of sequences achieving score = 1 annotated. The paper notes this finding is "consistent with prior work" (Carlini et al., 2022; Biderman et al., 2023).

**Memorization increases with training.** Figure 7 shows that the percentage of k-extractible sequences (score = 1) increases as checkpoint index advances. The paper states that "AMBER can memorize more sequences with the training going" and observes that "the spike at score = 1 indicates that AMBER can memorize a much larger number of tokens than our preset threshold 32." This means the model fully memorizes sequences of at least 32 tokens, and the prevalence of such complete memorization grows throughout pretraining.

**Recency bias in memorization.** Figure 8 plots memorization scores per data chunk group for each checkpoint, with "the marked spots indicating the latest chunk seen by that checkpoint." The paper finds that "AMBER checkpoints memorize the latest seen data much more than previous data," and that "for each data chunk, the memorization score drops a bit with additional training, but keeps increasing afterwards." This suggests a two-phase dynamic: immediate forgetting after a chunk is no longer the most recent (the "drops a bit" phase), followed by gradual re-memorization or consolidation as training continues on other data.

**Checkpoint correlation.** Figure 9 shows "a strong correlation between the checkpoints" in terms of both memorization score and k-extractible status. Sequences that are memorized by early checkpoints tend to remain memorized across subsequent checkpoints, indicating that memorization is path-dependent and persistent rather than transient.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

This paper does not contain ablation studies in the conventional sense. There are no controlled experiments where a single variable is modified while holding others constant to isolate its effect. The paper releases two models (AMBER and CRYSTALCODER) with different training recipes, data mixtures, and architectures, but these differences are confounded — CRYSTALCODER differs from AMBER simultaneously in training data (SlimPajama vs. RefinedWeb/RedPajama mixture), training schedule (three-stage blending vs. single-stage uniform mixing), architecture (μP, restricted RoPE, LayerNorm, vocabulary size), and hardware (Cerebras CG-1 vs. GPU cluster). The paper does not attempt to attribute observed performance differences to any specific design choice, and no controlled experiments are reported.

However, several **implicit comparisons** within the paper function as informal ablations or robustness checks:

**Fine-tuning as implicit capability probe.** The comparison between AMBER, AMBERCHAT, and AMBERSAFE functions partially as a robustness check on the base model's downstream utility. The fact that instruction tuning (AMBERCHAT) and safety alignment (AMBERSAFE) both substantially improve benchmark scores — with AMBERSAFE reaching 53.15 from a base of 45.20 — suggests that AMBER's relatively low base performance does not reflect a fundamental architecture flaw but rather incomplete exploitation of its pretrained representations. This is not a formal ablation (there is no control for fine-tuning data quality or quantity), but it demonstrates that the base model retains useful knowledge that supervised fine-tuning can surface.

**NaN data chunk handling as training robustness observation.** The paper documents that four specific data chunks cause NaN loss "regardless of the position of training" (Section 4.1.5). This functions as an implicit negative result: the phenomena is robust to model state, suggesting that intrinsic data properties (rather than training dynamics) trigger the failure. The team's attempted mitigation — "moving the 'misbehaved' data chunks to the end of the training but still observe NaN losses" (Section 5) — serves as an informal robustness check that rules out training-stage-dependent explanations.

**Optimizer state re-initialization as suspected stability factor.** The paper hypothesizes that missing optimizer states — requiring fresh Adam initialization after each NaN-skip restart — "might be the cause of the NaN loss issue observed in the four data chunks" (Section 4.1.5). This is not experimentally tested (no controlled comparison of training-with-optimizer-states vs. training-without is provided), but it represents a hypothesis generated by the transparency of the release that could be tested by future researchers using the released checkpoints and data.

**CRYSTALCODER's blended vs. sequential training as implicit curriculum comparison.** Although CRYSTALCODER is not systematically compared to an identically-resourced sequential-training baseline, its performance relative to CodeLlama-7B — which uses sequential language-then-code training — provides an informal comparison between blended and sequential curricula. CRYSTALCODER achieves higher language scores (50.92 vs. 42.42) but lower code scores (32.38 vs. 37.45). However, this comparison is confounded by differences in total training data, data composition, architecture, and hardware, so it cannot be interpreted as a curriculum ablation.

**Precision discrepancy in early checkpoints.** The paper acknowledges that early AMBER checkpoints were saved in BF16 precision rather than FP32 (Section 4.1.5), and "anticipates that the initial BF16 model checkpoints may have contributed to some degree of accuracy drop in the model." This is presented as a known limitation without experimental quantification — no comparison of performance using BF16-saved vs. FP32-saved checkpoints as training starting points is provided.

### Critical Assessment

The experiments reported in this paper serve a fundamentally different purpose from those in a typical methods paper. The paper's central claims are not about model performance superiority — AMBER underperforms comparably-sized models (Table 4), and CRYSTALCODER's results are competitive but not state-of-the-art (Table 5) — but about the **feasibility and value of comprehensive pretraining transparency**. The experimental sections must therefore be assessed on whether they demonstrate that full release enables scientific investigation, not on whether the released models achieve benchmark leadership.

**Claim 1: Comprehensive pretraining transparency is achievable for modern LLMs at trillion-token scale.** The experiments in this paper constitute a **existence proof** rather than a controlled demonstration. The paper does, in fact, release two 7B models with full training data, code, hyperparameters, 360 (or 143) intermediate checkpoints, and training metrics. This claim is supported by the release itself — the artifacts are publicly available at llm360.ai — not by the benchmark tables. However, the experiments do not demonstrate that this transparency is achievable at all scales or for all model families; the claim is supported only for the specific 7B LLaMA-architecture models described. The paper's announcement of "a much larger LLM" (Section 6) that will also be released under LLM360 represents a commitment to extending this existence proof but does not constitute experimental evidence.

A genuine weakness of this claim as presented is the **incompleteness of AMBER's transparency**. The paper acknowledges missing optimizer states (Section 4.1.5) and early checkpoints saved in lower precision (BF16 instead of FP32). These gaps mean that AMBER does not fully satisfy the LLM360 framework's own specification for the "Model Checkpoints" artifact category, which specifies that both model weights and optimizer states should be released. The paper's honesty about these gaps is commendable and is itself consistent with the transparency philosophy, but it means the existence proof is partial: a fully compliant LLM360 release has not yet been demonstrated. The paper implicitly acknowledges this by describing CRYSTALCODER as trained on different infrastructure (Cerebras) with different checkpoint practices, but does not report whether CRYSTALCODER's release includes optimizer states.

**Claim 2: Full release enables scientific investigations that are impossible with final-model-only releases.** The memorization study in Section 4.3 is the paper's primary experimental demonstration of this claim, and it is **convincing but narrowly scoped**. The study demonstrates that having access to all intermediate checkpoints and the exact training data ordering enables tracking memorization across the full training trajectory, revealing phenomena such as recency bias (Figure 8), checkpoint correlation (Figure 9), and the gradual accumulation of memorized sequences (Figure 7). These findings are meaningful — they characterize how memorization develops in a contemporary trillion-token LLM — and they could not have been obtained without the full release.

However, the study is limited in several ways. It examines only **one phenomenon** (memorization) using a single metric (32-token extraction score). The paper claims that the release enables research on "emergent abilities," "how different training data affects model behavior," and "scaling behavior and learning dynamics" (Section 1, Section 2), but none of these are experimentally demonstrated in this paper. The checkpoint-level benchmark evaluations (Figures 4 and 6) provide observational trajectories — showing that MMLU dips then rises, that TruthfulQA declines throughout training — but the paper does not use these trajectories to answer specific research questions or test hypotheses about training dynamics. They are presented as raw observations without analysis. The paper's claim that the release framework enables a wide range of scientific investigations is therefore a **promise** backed by a single worked example (memorization) rather than a body of demonstrated results.

The memorization study also samples only 1,000 sequences per data chunk from a total of 360 chunks (360,000 total sequences), which is a small fraction of the 1.26T-token corpus. The paper does not discuss whether this sample size is sufficient to estimate memorization rates with statistical reliability, and does not provide confidence intervals. The finding that "more than 1% of sequences are 32-extractible" is a point estimate without uncertainty quantification. This is a meaningful limitation because memorization rates of specific data types (e.g., code vs. prose vs. personally identifiable information) could vary substantially across data sources, and a small uniform sample may not capture this heterogeneity.

**Claim 3: The performance gap between AMBER and LLaMA quantifies the cost of opacity.** This claim is implicit in the paper's framing rather than explicitly stated as a result. Table 4 shows AMBER at 45.20 average vs. LLaMA-7B at 49.69, with the paper noting that AMBER uses "the exact same model architecture" and "following the pre-training hyperparameters from LLaMA as closely as possible" (Section 4.1.1). The implication is that the 4.5-point gap must be attributable to factors that LLaMA did not disclose — data processing quality, exact data mixture, training infrastructure subtleties.

This claim is **suggestive but not rigorously supported**. The paper identifies several confounds that prevent a clean comparison: AMBER uses a different batch size (2,240 vs. 2,048), trains on different hardware with different parallelism configurations, encountered NaN losses that caused four data chunks to be skipped and replaced with duplicates, failed to save optimizer states, stored early checkpoints in lower precision, and used a different data processing pipeline. Any of these differences could contribute to the performance gap independently of data quality. Without a controlled experiment — e.g., training LLaMA's exact architecture with LLaMA's exact hyperparameters on AMBER's exact data in AMBER's exact training environment — the 4.5-point gap cannot be definitively attributed to undisclosed data processing. It is a **compelling diagnostic signal** but not a conclusive measurement of opacity's cost.

The OpenLLaMA comparison strengthens the claim: OpenLLaMA, which attempted to recreate LLaMA's training data from disclosed data sources, also underperforms LLaMA (48.18 for OpenLLaMA-v2-7B vs. 49.69 for LLaMA-7B). But OpenLLaMA-v2 slightly outperforms AMBER (48.18 vs. 45.20), and OpenLLaMA-v1 scores 46.08, suggesting that data processing choices affect performance and that different reconstruction attempts produce different results. This variability supports the paper's argument that "additional data processing or training procedures may be necessary" beyond what is disclosed, but it does not isolate which specific undisclosed factors matter most.

**What would strengthen the experimental case.** Several experiments that the paper does not conduct would substantially strengthen its claims:

- **A controlled curriculum ablation for CRYSTALCODER.** Training CRYSTALCODER's exact data on a sequential schedule (language then code, matching Code Llama's approach) would enable a direct comparison between blended and sequential curricula, isolating the effect of training schedule from data composition and architecture. Without this, the observed language-code tradeoff (CRYSTALCODER's higher language scores but lower code scores vs. Code Llama) cannot be attributed to the blended vs. sequential design choice.

- **Data quality sensitivity analysis for AMBER.** Varying the data cleaning and filtering pipeline — e.g., adding quality filters, deduplication, or toxicity removal — and measuring the effect on benchmark performance would quantify how much of AMBER's performance gap with LLaMA is attributable to data processing. This would convert the diagnostic observation into a measured quantity.

- **Statistical confidence intervals on benchmark scores.** The Open LLM Leaderboard typically reports single-run scores, which are sufficient for leaderboard rankings but insufficient for the kind of fine-grained effect attribution the paper implicitly makes (e.g., attributing a 4.5-point gap to undisclosed factors). Multiple evaluation runs with different few-shot examples, or bootstrap confidence intervals, would clarify whether the observed gaps are robust to sampling variation.

- **Optimizer state ablation.** A controlled experiment comparing training stability and final performance with and without optimizer state continuity, using the exact data chunks that triggered NaN losses in AMBER's training, would test the paper's hypothesis that missing optimizer states contributed to the instability. This experiment is now feasible using the released checkpoints and data, but the paper does not conduct it.

- **Difficulty-stratified or domain-stratified benchmarking.** The paper aggregates all benchmarks into a single average score. Stratifying by question difficulty (analogous to the MATH difficulty bins in the reference paper), by knowledge domain (science, history, reasoning), or by data source (how does performance on Wikipedia-derived questions compare to RefinedWeb-derived questions?) would reveal whether AMBER's weaknesses are concentrated in specific areas, providing more actionable diagnostic information than a single composite score.

**On the memorization study specifically.** The memorization analysis is the paper's strongest experimental contribution and genuinely demonstrates the value of full release. However, it is limited to a single research question. The paper could have demonstrated additional analyses enabled by the release — for example, tracking how different data sources (RefinedWeb vs. Arxiv vs. code) contribute to specific benchmark improvements, or analyzing whether loss spikes correlate with specific data chunk characteristics, or measuring whether benchmark data leakage (a concern the paper itself raises in Section 1) can be detected using the released training data and checkpoints. The memorization study establishes the paradigm but does not exhaust it.

In summary, the experiments are **sufficient to demonstrate the feasibility of comprehensive release** (the existence proof) and **sufficient to illustrate one type of analysis enabled by such release** (the memorization study). They are **not sufficient** to sustain the stronger implied claim that the performance gap between transparent and opaque models quantifies the cost of opacity, because confounds in training configuration prevent clean attribution. The paper's value lies primarily in what it makes possible for future research — by releasing the artifacts — rather than in the depth of its own experimental analysis. This is consistent with the paper's self-characterization as an "initiative" and "framework" rather than a traditional results-driven research paper.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### 6.1 The Transparency Framework Is Not Fully Instantiated by Its Own Models

**The assumption or constraint.** The LLM360 framework (Section 3) defines four mandatory artifact categories for a fully transparent release, including model checkpoints that contain both weights **and optimizer states**. For AMBER, the paper explicitly acknowledges: "In our pre-training framework, we did not manage to save the optimizer states; we only saved model checkpoints for each data chunk" (Section 4.1.5). Additionally, early checkpoints were saved in BF16 precision rather than FP32: "In the initial phase of pre-training, our codebase had an issue where model checkpoints were saved with BF16 precision, despite our mixed precision training process maintaining model weights at FP32" (Section 4.1.5).

**The consequence.** These omissions mean that AMBER—the flagship initial release of LLM360—does not fully satisfy the framework's own specification. A practitioner attempting to resume training from an AMBER checkpoint must re-initialize the Adam optimizer states (first and second moment estimates) from scratch, which the paper identifies as a probable contributor to the NaN loss instability: "Each time we resumed pre-training from a previous model checkpoint, the optimizer state in the AdamW optimizer was re-initialized. This re-initialization could potentially affect model training stability" (Section 4.1.5). The BF16-precision early checkpoints further compromise reproducibility: anyone using those checkpoints as a starting point for continued pretraining or analysis begins from a state with degraded weight precision that the paper anticipates "may have contributed to some degree of accuracy drop in the model" (Section 4.1.5). This means that research requiring exact resumption of training—one of the explicit use cases LLM360 advocates (Section 5, "one can always pick one of the LLM360 checkpoints... and resume the pre-training")—is compromised for the early portion of AMBER's training trajectory. The paper does not report whether CRYSTALCODER includes optimizer states, leaving this as an open question for the second model as well.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper is transparent about both gaps (Section 4.1.5), but does not quantify their impact. There is no comparison of training stability or final performance when resuming from an FP32 checkpoint with optimizer states versus from a BF16 checkpoint without them. The NaN loss on four data chunks (Section 4.1.5) is hypothesized to be related to the optimizer state issue, but this remains speculative rather than experimentally confirmed. The paper does not report how many early checkpoints were saved in BF16 before the issue was "identified and rectified" (Section 4.1.5), so the extent of affected checkpoints is unknown to readers.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges both issues honestly but does not resolve them for AMBER. The statements in Section 4.1.5 are retrospective documentation, not mitigation. The paper does not indicate whether future LLM360 models will include optimizer states or full FP32 checkpoints from the start of training, though the spirit of the framework strongly implies this. The oversight is presented as a lesson learned rather than a limitation addressed in the current release.

---

### 6.2 The Performance Gap with Comparable Models Is Substantial and the Causes Are Not Isolated

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper positions AMBER as a transparent reproduction of LLaMA-7B's training, stating it uses "the exact same model architecture as LLaMA 7B" and "following the pre-training hyperparameters from LLaMA as closely as possible" (Section 4.1.1). The implicit claim is that by matching architecture and hyperparameters, the remaining performance difference can be attributed to undisclosed factors in LLaMA's process—primarily data processing quality. The paper states this explicitly in its take-home messages: "A key omission in LLaMA's technical report is a detailed description of their exact pre-training dataset" (Section 5).

**The consequence.** The performance gap between AMBER and LLaMA-7B is substantial: AMBER achieves 45.20 average on the Open LLM Leaderboard versus LLaMA-7B's 49.69 (Table 4)—a gap of roughly 4.5 percentage points. On ARC, the gap is 9 points (41.89 vs. 50.94); on MMLU, it is 5 points (30.76 vs. 35.67). A practitioner choosing between using AMBER (with full transparency) and LLaMA-7B (with limited transparency) faces a real performance tradeoff: they gain complete knowledge of data provenance, training dynamics, and intermediate states, but lose nontrivial benchmark capability. However, the paper cannot definitively attribute this gap to data processing quality because several other differences exist between AMBER and LLaMA: different batch size (2240 vs. 2048), different hardware and parallelism strategy, missing optimizer states, four skipped data chunks replaced with duplicates, early BF16 checkpoints, and a different data preparation pipeline (AMBER did not apply additional cleaning or filtering beyond the source datasets). Any of these could contribute to the performance gap independently of data quality. The paper's diagnostic claim—that the gap reflects the cost of opacity—is therefore suggestive but confounded. A practitioner cannot determine from the reported evidence whether using LLaMA's exact data processing (if it were disclosed) would close the gap entirely, partially, or not at all.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 4 provides the comparison, and Section 4.1.1 documents the deliberate parallels with LLaMA's architecture and hyperparameters. Section 4.1.5 documents the deviations (NaN losses, missing optimizer states, BF16 checkpoints). The OpenLLaMA comparison provides partial corroboration: OpenLLaMA-v2-7B, which independently attempted to recreate LLaMA's training data, achieves 48.18, also below LLaMA's 49.69, suggesting that data reproduction is indeed challenging. However, OpenLLaMA-v2 outperforms AMBER by roughly 3 points (48.18 vs. 45.20), while OpenLLaMA-v1 achieves 46.08—the three transparent reproductions (AMBER, OpenLLaMA-v1, OpenLLaMA-v2) span a range of 3 points themselves, indicating that factors beyond data transparency affect performance. The paper does not ablate its specific data preparation choices against alternatives, so the performance penalty attributable specifically to the "no additional cleaning" decision versus other confounds is unknown.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges the data processing gap in Section 5 ("We, along with the entire LLM open-source community, are diligently exploring the best approaches for data cleaning, data quality filtering, and determining optimal data mixing ratios") but does not resolve it experimentally. No ablation study isolates the effect of data quality filtering on AMBER's final performance. The paper frames this as an open problem for the community rather than a limitation it has addressed, which is honest but leaves practitioners without guidance on how much performance they sacrifice when choosing transparency.

---

### 6.3 The Difficulty Estimation and Problem-Dependent Allocation Insight from Prior Work Is Absent—All Problems Receive Uniform Compute

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper evaluates AMBER and CRYSTALCODER using a uniform evaluation protocol: all test questions receive the same treatment (a single generation with the specified few-shot prompting format, scored via standard accuracy or pass@1). There is no mechanism for allocating additional inference-time computation to harder problems, no verifier-guided search or revision strategy, and no difficulty-conditioned compute allocation. This is a deliberate scope limitation—the paper is about pretraining transparency, not test-time compute scaling—but it means the benchmark comparisons in Tables 4 and 5 reflect a single fixed operating point that may not be optimal for any given model.

**The consequence.** A practitioner who downloads AMBER or CRYSTALCODER and deploys it with greedy decoding (or single-sample generation) experiences the performance reported in Tables 4 and 5. However, a practitioner who applies additional inference-time strategies—best-of-N sampling, majority voting, verifier-guided selection, or iterative revision—would likely achieve higher performance from the same model checkpoint. The paper provides no guidance on how AMBER or CRYSTALCODER respond to such strategies, no analysis of whether the models' proposal distributions benefit more or less from search than comparable models, and no characterization of whether the verifier over-optimization phenomenon documented in related work (where beam search degrades easy-problem performance) applies to these specific models. This means the reported benchmark comparisons are not necessarily representative of the best achievable performance from these models, and a practitioner investing in deployment engineering (e.g., adding a verifier, implementing best-of-N) cannot predict the return on that investment from the reported results.

Furthermore, the checkpoint-level trajectories in Figures 4 and 6 reveal that different capabilities develop at different rates (MMLU dips early before rising; TruthfulQA declines throughout training). A sophisticated deployment strategy might select different checkpoints for different tasks—using an earlier checkpoint for TruthfulQA, a later one for HellaSwag—but the paper does not explore this possibility or provide the multi-checkpoint orchestration infrastructure that would enable it. The uniform evaluation protocol flattens this temporal heterogeneity into a single final-checkpoint score.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The evaluation methodology (Section 4.1.4, Section 4.2) describes only single-pass, fixed-configuration benchmark evaluation. The Open LLM Leaderboard settings (25-shot ARC, 10-shot HellaSwag, 5-shot MMLU, 0-shot TruthfulQA) are applied uniformly. The memorization study in Section 4.3 uses a fixed prompt length of 32 tokens and continuation length of 32 tokens with no variation. There are no experiments with different generation budgets, different decoding strategies, or any form of test-time computation beyond the default leaderboard settings.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not claim to study inference-time compute scaling, and this limitation is a scope boundary rather than an oversight. However, for a practitioner deciding whether to adopt AMBER or CRYSTALCODER, the absence of any characterization of inference-time behavior means that the reported benchmark scores are lower bounds on achievable performance, with the gap between reported and achievable being unknown.

### 6.4 The Framework Validates Feasibility on a Single Model Scale and Two Model Architectures—Scalability to Larger Models Is Asserted but Not Demonstrated

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper releases two 7B-parameter models (AMBER and CRYSTALCODER) and announces a commitment to release future models at larger scales: "Our team is also pre-training a much larger LLM, which will be fully released as soon as the pre-training is complete" (Section 6). The framework itself makes no scale-dependent claims—it defines artifact categories that should apply to any LLM—but the feasibility demonstration is limited to the 7B scale. The paper does not address whether the release practices demonstrated for 7B models (saving 360 checkpoints, releasing all training data, maintaining public W&B dashboards) scale logistically or economically to models at 70B, 175B, or larger scales.

**The consequence.** A practitioner or organization considering adopting the LLM360 framework for their own LLM training faces several scale-dependent unknowns that the paper does not address. For checkpoint releases: each AMBER checkpoint at BF16 precision requires approximately 13.4 GB of storage (6.7B parameters × 2 bytes), and 360 checkpoints total roughly 4.8 TB. At 70B parameters, a single FP32 checkpoint requires 280 GB, and 360 checkpoints would total approximately 100 TB. At 175B, the total approaches 250 TB. The paper does not discuss storage costs, bandwidth costs for public distribution, or how the "save every data chunk" strategy might be modified at larger scales (e.g., saving fewer checkpoints, using compression, releasing only key milestones). For data releases: AMBER's 1.26T-token training corpus is a specific size that the paper handles by partitioning into 360 chunks and releasing via Hugging Face. A 70B model trained on 2T+ tokens would require proportionally more storage for data release, and a model trained on proprietary-licensed data (as many larger models are) may face legal restrictions on data redistribution that the LLM360 framework's transparency mandate cannot overcome. For metrics: the paper's approach of releasing all training logs via public W&B dashboards is straightforward at the 7B scale but may require more sophisticated infrastructure for longer training runs producing more voluminous logs.

Beyond storage and logistics, the paper does not address whether the **scientific value** of comprehensive transparency changes with scale. The memorization study (Section 4.3) demonstrates value at 7B, but it is possible that at 70B or 175B, phenomena like emergent abilities (Wei et al., 2022) make checkpoint-level analysis even more scientifically important—or alternatively, that the sheer volume of data and checkpoints makes comprehensive analysis intractable without automated tooling that the paper does not provide. The ANALYSIS360 toolkit is described as an "initial version" and "organized repository," but its scalability to order-of-magnitude larger models and datasets is unaddressed.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper demonstrates feasibility for exactly two 7B models trained on approximately 1.3T and 1.4T tokens. Table 1 contextualizes these scales relative to prior transparent releases—Pythia's largest model was 12B trained on 0.3T tokens, so AMBER represents a significant extension in training data scale (4× more tokens) while maintaining or exceeding checkpoint granularity (360 vs. 154 checkpoints). However, the parameter count (7B) is not an advance over Pythia's 12B, and the paper does not release models at the 13B, 33B, or 65B scales that LLaMA established as standards. The "much larger LLM" announcement in Section 6 is a forward-looking statement with no timeline, no scale specification, and no commitment to the exact transparency practices that will be maintained.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges this as future work (Section 6) but does not discuss scaling challenges or provide a roadmap for how the framework adapts to larger models. The commitment is aspirational: "We aim to make a continuous commitment to fully open-source LLMs by releasing multiple LLMs at various scales" (Section 1). This is a statement of intent, not a demonstrated capability. Until a substantially larger model is released under the LLM360 framework with the same degree of transparency, the framework's applicability beyond the 7B scale remains hypothesized rather than proven.

### 6.5 The Reliance on Pre-Existing Public Datasets—While Transparent—Means the Data Quality and Composition Are Inherited, Not Optimized

**The assumption or constraint.** AMBER's pretraining data is constructed by combining existing public datasets (RefinedWeb, StarCoder, RedPajama-v1, C4, plus smaller components) "without any further cleaning, filtering, or sub-sampling" (Section 4.1.1). The paper states the procedure explicitly: "We simply put together all the original aforementioned datasets (without any further cleaning, filtering, or sub-sampling), conduct a global permutation, and partition them evenly into 360 data chunks." This is a deliberate transparency choice—by applying no additional filtering, the data is a faithful representation of the source datasets and is fully reproducible by anyone with access to those sources. CRYSTALCODER uses SlimPajama and StarCoder with a three-stage schedule but similarly does not apply custom cleaning beyond what the source datasets already provide.

**The consequence.** This decision means that any quality issues, biases, toxic content, or benchmark contamination present in the source datasets propagate directly into AMBER's training data without mitigation. The paper does not analyze what quality problems exist in the source datasets, does not measure the prevalence of toxic or inappropriate content, and does not attempt to detect benchmark data leakage (a concern the paper itself raises in Section 1: "data leakage—where training datasets overlap with benchmark datasets—can lead to misleading performance metrics"). A practitioner deploying AMBER in a production setting—particularly one with content safety requirements—cannot assess from the paper whether the training data contains material that would cause the model to generate harmful outputs, nor whether the model's benchmark performance is inflated by accidental training-on-test contamination. The paper's position that transparency enables such assessments ("by inspecting the exact training data and bias analysis in ANALYSIS360, stakeholders can have a thorough review of these risks before deploying the models," Section 5) shifts the burden of analysis from the model developers to the model users, but does not itself perform the analysis.

Furthermore, the "no additional cleaning" approach means that the paper cannot distinguish between performance limitations caused by architecture/hyperparameters versus those caused by data quality. If AMBER's training data contains a non-trivial fraction of low-quality, repetitive, or nonsensical text from RefinedWeb or C4, that could explain part of the performance gap with LLaMA (which presumably applied proprietary quality filters). But because the paper did not experiment with different data quality thresholds, this hypothesis remains untested. A practitioner comparing AMBER to a model that invested heavily in data curation (as most leading models do) cannot determine whether adopting AMBER's architecture with better data would close the performance gap or whether the architecture itself is limiting.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper explicitly describes its data preparation approach in Section 4.1.1, and Table 2 provides the precise data mixture. The benchmarks in Table 4 are the primary downstream evidence of the effect of this data strategy—AMBER underperforms LLaMA-7B, which likely used heavier data curation. CRYSTALCODER's stronger relative performance (Table 5) with SlimPajama (which is explicitly "cleaned and deduplicated" per Soboleva et al., 2023, the source paper) suggests that starting from cleaner base data improves results, but this is a cross-model comparison confounded by architecture and training schedule differences. The paper does not report any analysis of data quality metrics (perplexity of the training data under a reference model, prevalence of near-duplicate documents, toxicity scores, benchmark n-gram overlap) that would help users assess the data's fitness for their use case. The ANALYSIS360 toolkit is described as including bias analysis (citing BOLD, Dhamala et al., 2021) but no such analysis is presented in this paper.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges the importance of data quality in its take-home messages: "Data cleaning (and/or data quality filtering), along with data mixing ratios, are crucial aspects of LLM pre-training" (Section 5). CRYSTALCODER's use of SlimPajama (a cleaned dataset) rather than the raw RedPajama used in AMBER represents an implicit acknowledgment that data quality matters—but this is a model-level choice rather than a systematic investigation. The paper commits to exploring "optimal ratios for mixing different subsets in the pre-training datasets" as future work (Section 6) but does not announce plans to release models with controlled data quality ablations. The responsibility for assessing data quality, detecting contamination, and evaluating biases remains with downstream users of the released artifacts.

### 6.6 The Framework Provides No Guidance on Selecting Among Intermediate Checkpoints for Downstream Use

**The assumption or constraint.** One of the primary use cases the paper advocates for LLM360 is that researchers can select intermediate checkpoints for specific purposes: "One can always pick one of the LLM360 checkpoints (e.g., from 50% of the pre-training stage) and resume the pre-training to obtain a domain-specific LLM" (Section 5). Similarly, the paper notes that "algorithmic approximation frameworks for efficient training require partially trained model weights" (Section 5), positioning the checkpoint release as enabling this research. The implicit assumption is that checkpoint quality correlates monotonically with training progress, so later checkpoints are generally better and the choice of checkpoint is a simple tradeoff between capability and customization flexibility.

**The consequence.** The checkpoint-level evaluation trajectories in Figure 4 directly contradict the monotonicity assumption. TruthfulQA performance **declines** from approximately 0.55–0.60 at early checkpoints to 0.34 at the final checkpoint—meaning the best checkpoint for truthfulness is early in training, not late. MMLU performance dips in early training before rising, meaning that a checkpoint at 30% of training might perform worse than one at 10% or 80%. These non-monotonic trajectories mean that a practitioner selecting a checkpoint for a specific downstream task cannot simply pick a late checkpoint and assume maximal capability. They must choose between different checkpoints that represent different tradeoffs—a later checkpoint may have higher MMLU but lower TruthfulQA, while an earlier checkpoint has the reverse. The paper identifies these patterns ("we can see that... the TruthfulQA score seems to decrease as the training proceeds... the MMLU score decreases in the initial stage... and then starts to increase," Section 4.1.4) but does not provide guidance on how to navigate them.

This creates a practical barrier to the domain-adaptation use case the paper promotes. If a medical researcher wants to start from an AMBER checkpoint and continue pretraining on medical data, which checkpoint should they choose? The paper provides per-checkpoint trajectories for four benchmarks but does not report how checkpoint selection affects downstream fine-tuning performance, does not characterize how "resume-ability" (the ability to successfully continue training) varies across checkpoints, and does not analyze whether the BF16 precision checkpoints (early training) or the missing-optimizer-state resumptions (after NaN losses) affect continued training differently. The 360 checkpoints represent a rich resource but one that the paper leaves largely uncharacterized beyond aggregate benchmark curves.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figure 4 shows the non-monotonic trajectories for MMLU and TruthfulQA. Figure 6 shows similar patterns for CRYSTALCODER, including stage-transition effects where code benchmarks rise sharply when code data is introduced in Stage 2. The paper documents these trajectories as observations but does not use them to inform checkpoint selection recommendations. The memorization study (Section 4.3) adds another dimension: early checkpoints memorize less training data (Figure 7), which might be desirable for privacy-sensitive applications, but the paper does not connect memorization rates to downstream task performance or provide guidance on how to trade off capability versus memorization when selecting a checkpoint. The paper's use cases in Section 5 ("One can conduct experimental studies at any stage of model training," "One can always pick one of the LLM360 checkpoints... and resume the pre-training") are stated without reference to the documented non-monotonicities.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper presents checkpoint-level trajectories as descriptive findings ("interesting trends") rather than as input to a checkpoint selection methodology. No analysis correlates checkpoint choice with downstream fine-tuning performance, no benchmark evaluations beyond the four leaderboard tasks are reported per-checkpoint (so task-specific checkpoint selection is impossible for most tasks), and no tooling for querying or comparing checkpoints on user-defined criteria is provided in ANALYSIS360. The memorization study demonstrates that such analyses are possible with the released data, but the paper does not itself perform them. Future versions of ANALYSIS360 or community-contributed analyses could address this gap, but the paper provides no roadmap for doing so.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper introduces a **fundamental reframing** of what "open-source" means for large language models. Rather than accepting the prevailing norm—where a blog post describing high-level architecture and data sources, plus downloadable weights, qualifies a model as "open"—LLM360 establishes a multi-dimensional transparency taxonomy (Table 1) and demonstrates that comprehensive disclosure across four artifact categories (data, code, checkpoints, metrics) is achievable for modern trillion-token-scale LLMs. The shift is from a binary conception of openness (model weights public = open) to a **graded, multi-axis standard** where each withheld artifact represents a scientifically meaningful gap.

The magnitude of this reframing should not be overstated—the paper does not introduce new architectures, new training algorithms, or new capability breakthroughs. It is not a paradigm shift in *how models work* but rather a paradigm shift in *how models are shared*. Its closest analog is the introduction of standardized benchmark suites or the adoption of registered reports in experimental science: it changes the **epistemic infrastructure** of the field without changing the underlying technology. The paper's Table 1, by cataloging the progressive erosion of transparency from GPT-J (2021, nearly all columns checked) to Mistral (2023, a single question mark), creates a visible trajectory that the field can no longer ignore—it converts an implicit trend into an explicit pattern that demands justification.

The paper's most significant conceptual contribution is the **performance-transparency gap** as an empirical diagnostic. By releasing AMBER with "the exact same model architecture as LLaMA 7B" and "following the pre-training hyperparameters from LLaMA as closely as possible" (Section 4.1.1), the paper makes the 4.5-point average benchmark gap between AMBER and LLaMA-7B (Table 4) visible and quantifiable. This gap is not a failure of LLM360; it is the framework's most important output. It reveals that LLaMA's undisclosed data processing pipeline—its exact cleaning, filtering, deduplication, and quality-control procedures—is worth roughly 4–9 benchmark points depending on the task. Prior to this paper, a researcher could reasonably ask "why does OpenLLaMA underperform LLaMA?" and receive only speculation. After LLM360, that question becomes: "What specific data processing step accounts for the 4.5-point gap between AMBER and LLaMA-7B?" The paper transforms an unanswerable question into an addressable research problem by providing a fully transparent baseline.

The paper also **reconciles a contradiction** in the "open-source" LLM landscape. On one hand, the community celebrates the proliferation of openly available models (LLaMA, Falcon, Mistral) as democratizing AI research. On the other hand, researchers find they cannot reproduce these models' results, cannot study their training dynamics, and cannot assess their biases—leading to a frustration captured in the paper's observation that OpenLLaMA "still does not fully reproduce LLaMA's benchmark evaluations" (Section 1). The contradiction is between the *quantity* of available models and the *quality* of scientific access to them. LLM360 resolves this by showing that the contradiction is not inherent—comprehensive transparency is technically achievable at the 7B scale with 1.3T+ tokens—but rather a consequence of release practices that the community has come to accept as normal. By demonstrating that a fully transparent model can be trained and released within the same resource envelope as partially transparent models (the total compute is comparable; the difference is in what gets saved and shared), the paper shifts the burden of proof: the default expectation becomes full disclosure, and any omission requires explicit justification.

This reframing makes several research directions **more attractive**:

- **Systematic data quality ablation.** Because AMBER releases its exact training data with no additional filtering beyond the source datasets, researchers can now add specific data cleaning steps, retrain from intermediate checkpoints, and measure exactly how much each filtering decision contributes to performance. This converts data quality engineering from an art practiced behind closed doors into a reproducible science.

- **Training dynamics research at scale.** With 360 checkpoints spanning 1.26T tokens, researchers studying when and how capabilities emerge during pretraining have a dataset that reflects modern training practices (trillion-token scale, mixed data sources) rather than the 300B-token Pythia models that represented an earlier era. The non-monotonic trajectories documented in Figure 4 (MMLU dipping then rising, TruthfulQA declining throughout) provide specific hypotheses to investigate.

- **Memorization and privacy research with ground-truth training data.** The memorization study in Section 4.3 demonstrates the paradigm but only scratches the surface. Because the exact training data and its ordering are known, researchers can study which types of content are most memorized, whether memorization correlates with data repetition, and whether specific architectural choices affect memorization rates—all with a level of precision impossible when training data is undisclosed.

Conversely, the paper makes certain research directions **less attractive** or at least more clearly bounded:

- **Architecture-centric benchmarking without data control.** The paper demonstrates that a 4.5-point performance gap can arise from undisclosed data processing decisions when architecture and hyperparameters are held constant. This means that benchmark comparisons between opaque models (which is essentially all current frontier models) are scientifically ambiguous—a Model A surpassing Model B may reflect architectural superiority, better data, or both. The field can no longer responsibly attribute performance differences to architecture without controlling for training data.

- **Domain adaptation without training data visibility.** The paper's use case of picking intermediate checkpoints for domain-specific continued pretraining (Section 5) implicitly highlights the risk of doing so with opaque models: without knowing the base model's training data, continued pretraining may inadvertently duplicate content or introduce distribution shift in unpredictable ways. LLM360 makes this risk visible by providing the alternative (known data), which may accelerate adoption of transparent base models for domain adaptation even if those base models have slightly lower initial performance.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Data quality ablation ladder for the performance-transparency gap.** The paper establishes a 4.5-point average gap between AMBER (45.20) and LLaMA-7B (49.69) under matched architecture and hyperparameters (Section 4.1.1, Table 4), but the gap is confounded by batch size differences, NaN-loss data chunk handling, missing optimizer states, and precision discrepancies. A systematic study would start from an AMBER checkpoint (ideally one saved after the BF16-precision bug was fixed, with optimizer states properly captured in the training framework), apply a sequence of data quality interventions—perplexity-based filtering of low-quality documents, MinHash deduplication within and across data sources, removal of benchmark-contaminated sequences, toxicity filtering—retrain from that checkpoint for a fixed token budget, and measure the marginal performance gain from each intervention. The outcome would be a **data quality ladder** mapping specific filtering operations to specific benchmark improvements, quantifying for the first time how much of the opacity tax is attributable to each data processing decision. This addresses the open problem the paper explicitly flags: "Data cleaning (and/or data quality filtering), along with data mixing ratios, are crucial aspects of LLM pre-training... We, along with the entire LLM open-source community, are diligently exploring the best approaches" (Section 5).

**Memorization dynamics across data sources and content types.** The memorization study in Section 4.3 samples 1,000 sequences uniformly from each data chunk and reports aggregate k-extractibility rates. Because the exact training data with source labels is released (RefinedWeb, StarCoder, C4, Arxiv, Books, StackExchange, Wikipedia per Table 2), a follow-up study could compute **per-source memorization rates**: does code from StarCoder get memorized at higher rates than prose from RefinedWeb? Does Wikipedia content show different memorization dynamics than Arxiv papers? Furthermore, the study could stratify by document properties—length, perplexity under a reference model, presence of personally identifiable information—to identify **what makes a sequence memorizable**. The key advance over prior memorization studies (Carlini et al., 2021; Biderman et al., 2023) is that with LLM360, the researcher knows exactly which sequences the model was exposed to and at what point in training, enabling causal claims about exposure frequency and memorization probability that are impossible when training data is undisclosed. A strong follow-up would measure memorization rates separately for each data source, at multiple checkpoint depths, and correlate with downstream benchmark contamination (does memorized ARC content inflate ARC scores?).

**Checkpoint selection methodology for task-specific deployment.** The paper documents that different benchmarks follow qualitatively different trajectories during training: HellaSwag and ARC rise monotonically, MMLU dips then rises, TruthfulQA declines throughout (Figure 4). This creates a **checkpoint selection problem**: the best checkpoint for a given downstream task is not necessarily the final checkpoint. A systematic study would evaluate all 360 AMBER checkpoints on a broad battery of tasks beyond the four leaderboard benchmarks—including tasks measuring factual knowledge, reasoning, safety, and domain-specific performance—and build a **checkpoint recommendation matrix** mapping task families to optimal checkpoint indices. The study would also address the "resume-ability" question: do checkpoints from different training stages respond differently to continued pretraining on domain-specific data? This would directly enable the use case the paper describes in Section 5 ("One can always pick one of the LLM360 checkpoints... and resume the pre-training to obtain a domain-specific LLM") but which currently lacks empirical guidance. A strong result would identify specific checkpoint windows where different capabilities peak and quantify the tradeoff: "Checkpoint 150 maximizes truthfulness but sacrifices 5 MMLU points; checkpoint 300 maximizes MMLU but achieves only 60% of peak truthfulness."

**The "misbehaved data chunk" root cause analysis.** The paper documents that four specific data chunks cause NaN loss "regardless of the position of training" (Section 4.1.5). Because the exact data chunks are released as part of LLM360, a researcher can directly inspect them to identify what makes them toxic. The analysis could include: statistical characterization of the chunks (token entropy, sequence length distributions, presence of unusual Unicode characters or encoding artifacts, ratio of code to natural language); comparison to the 356 "well-behaved" chunks on the same metrics; ablation experiments where subsets of the problematic chunks are mixed into otherwise stable data to isolate which specific sequences trigger NaN loss; and testing whether the same chunks cause NaN loss in other model architectures or training frameworks. The outcome would be the first systematic characterization of **NaN-triggering data properties**, with potential implications for data preprocessing pipelines—if certain token patterns or encoding issues can be detected before training, they can be filtered proactively rather than discovered through expensive trial-and-error during training runs. The paper's hypothesis that missing optimizer states contributed to the instability (Section 4.1.5) could be directly tested by retraining over the problematic chunks with proper optimizer state continuity and observing whether NaN losses still occur.

**Blended vs. sequential curriculum for code-language models.** CRYSTALCODER's three-stage blended training schedule (Section 4.2) is positioned as an alternative to Code Llama's sequential language-then-code curriculum (Roziere et al., 2023). However, the paper's comparison is confounded: CRYSTALCODER and Code Llama differ in data composition, architecture, hardware, and total training tokens. A clean ablation would train two models on identical data (the exact SlimPajama + StarCoder mixture released with CRYSTALCODER), identical architecture (the released CRYSTALCODER configuration with muP, restricted RoPE, LayerNorm), and identical total compute: one using the three-stage blended schedule, one using a sequential schedule where all language data is trained first followed by all code data. The comparison would answer whether blended training genuinely preserves language capability better (CRYSTALCODER's 50.92 language average vs. CodeLlama's 42.42) or whether that advantage comes from other factors. The CRYSTALCODER release—with its exact data mixing scripts and training configuration—makes this ablation possible by providing the experimental substrate, whereas opaque code-language model releases would require reverse-engineering the training schedule.

**Training data contamination detection at scale.** The paper raises data leakage as a concern in Section 1 ("data leakage—where training datasets overlap with benchmark datasets—can lead to misleading performance metrics") but does not perform contamination analysis on AMBER's training data. Because the full training corpus is released, a researcher can perform n-gram overlap analysis between the 1.26T-token training set and the test sets of ARC, HellaSwag, MMLU, and TruthfulQA, measuring the prevalence of benchmark examples or near-duplicates in the pretraining data. By correlating contamination rates with per-checkpoint benchmark trajectories (Figure 4), the study could distinguish between genuine capability acquisition and memorization-driven benchmark inflation. The non-monotonic TruthfulQA trajectory (declining throughout training) is particularly interesting in this light: if TruthfulQA test examples appear in the training data, the declining score would suggest that repeated exposure during training does not improve truthfulness—and may actively harm it. This analysis is impossible for opaque models but straightforward with LLM360's artifacts, and its results would inform the broader debate about whether benchmark evaluations of LLMs reflect genuine reasoning or data memorization.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Domain-specific LLM development with known data provenance.** An organization building a medical LLM or legal LLM typically starts from a general-purpose base model and continues pretraining on domain data. With opaque base models, the organization cannot know which medical or legal content the model has already seen, risking redundant training on duplicated documents that prior work (Hernandez et al., 2022, cited in Section 3) has shown degrades performance. With AMBER or CRYSTALCODER, the organization can inspect the exact 1.26T- or 1.38T-token training corpus, identify which domain-relevant documents are already present, exclude them from the continued pretraining data, and select a checkpoint from which to resume (e.g., at 50% of pretraining to leave more plasticity for domain adaptation, as suggested in Section 5). The performance cost of choosing transparency over opaque alternatives is roughly the gap between AMBER's 45.20 average and LLaMA-7B's 49.69 (Table 4), but this gap may be recovered through domain-specific continued pretraining on known-clean data—and the absence of unintended data duplication provides a benefit opaque models cannot guarantee. For organizations in regulated industries (healthcare, finance, law) where data provenance documentation is required for compliance, transparent base models may be the only viable option regardless of benchmark differences.

**Bias auditing and safety evaluation before deployment.** The paper argues that LLM360's data release enables stakeholders to "have a thorough review of these risks before deploying the models" (Section 5). In practice, a deployment team evaluating AMBER for a customer-facing application can: (1) run the released dataset through toxicity classifiers to measure the prevalence of harmful content (hate speech, violence, explicit material); (2) analyze demographic representation in the training data (are certain groups, languages, or perspectives systematically underrepresented?); (3) use the ANALYSIS360 toolkit to run bias benchmarks like BOLD (Dhamala et al., 2021, cited in Section 5) against multiple checkpoints to track how biases evolve during training; and (4) inspect specific data chunks that tripped NaN losses (Section 4.1.5) for content anomalies that might affect model behavior. The key benefit over opaque models is that these analyses produce **actionable results**: if the team discovers that AMBER's training data contains disproportionate representation of certain viewpoints, they can quantify the extent, decide whether continued pretraining on balanced data can mitigate the issue, and select a checkpoint that minimizes the problematic influence. With an opaque model, the same team would have to infer biases from model outputs alone, which is both less precise and harder to remediate.

**Training infrastructure research and education.** The paper releases not just model artifacts but the full training infrastructure (lit-llama framework, 3D parallelism configuration, BF16 mixed-precision setup, and documentation of throughput achieved: 582.4k tokens/second on 224 A100 GPUs, Section 4.1.2). For academic labs and small organizations planning their first large-scale LLM pretraining run, this provides a **reference implementation** with known performance characteristics. The paper's take-home messages—that 3D parallelism outperforms FSDP "especially in distributed clusters with limited intra-node bandwidth," and that keeping the RoPE inverse frequency matrix in FP32 is essential (Section 5)—encode practical wisdom that would otherwise be learned through expensive trial and error. The documentation of failure modes (NaN losses on specific data chunks, missing optimizer states, BF16 checkpoint precision issues, Section 4.1.5) serves as a checklist of things to avoid, potentially saving other teams from repeating these exact mistakes. For training systems researchers, the released training logs and metrics (public W&B dashboards) provide real-world data on training dynamics at the 7B/1.3T scale, enabling studies of loss spike patterns, gradient norm evolution, and throughput optimization that typically rely on simulated or small-scale experiments.

**Multi-checkpoint analysis for capability research.** Researchers studying fundamental questions about LLM training—when does factual knowledge emerge versus reasoning capability, how does the data mixture affect the development of specific skills, what causes the "reversal" where instruction tuning degrades base model performance on some tasks—require access to intermediate model states. The 360 AMBER checkpoints and 143 CRYSTALCODER checkpoints, each tied to a specific data chunk boundary with known content, enable investigations that are impossible with final-model-only releases. For example, a researcher could probe all 360 checkpoints with carefully constructed factual recall questions and pinpoint the exact point in training where the model transitions from surface-level pattern matching to structured knowledge representation. Or they could correlate loss spikes and gradient norm anomalies (visible in the released metrics) with specific data chunks (visible in the released dataset) to understand what properties of training data cause optimization instabilities. The ANALYSIS360 toolkit (Section 4.3) provides a starting point for such investigations—the memorization study demonstrates the paradigm—and the framework is designed to be extended by the community with additional analysis modules. The benefit is not a specific performance gain but rather enabling a **class of scientific investigations** into LLM training dynamics that the current opaque-release norm renders impossible.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper does not articulate a specific tradeoff where users should choose LLM360 models over named alternatives under identifiable conditions. The models are presented as instantiations of a transparency framework rather than as performance-competitive replacements for specific opaque models. The decision to use AMBER or CRYSTALCODER is implicitly governed by the transparency-performance tradeoff visible in Tables 4 and 5: the user accepts lower benchmark scores (AMBER's 45.20 average vs. LLaMA-7B's 49.69) in exchange for complete knowledge of the training data, exact ability to resume training from any checkpoint, and full auditability of data provenance and biases. The conditions under which this tradeoff is favorable (regulated industries, research contexts, domain adaptation scenarios where data duplication risk is high) are discussed in the practical applications above, but the paper itself does not frame them as an explicit decision rule or claim that users "should" prefer AMBER over LLaMA in specific scenarios. The normative force of the paper is directed at *model producers* (arguing they should adopt the LLM360 framework) rather than at *model consumers* (arguing they should choose LLM360 models over alternatives). A decision matrix comparing LLM360 models to named alternatives would therefore constitute an extrapolation beyond the paper's own positioning.
