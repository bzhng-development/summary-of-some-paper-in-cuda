Here are the takeaways from this Ryan Peterman interview (behind the scenes of The Peterman Post podcast so far, from inception to 25,000 subscribers):

**Story behind the podcast**

The Peterman Post was born from a simple, self-interested premise: Ryan knows “speaking well is a super high leverage skill for your career, for your relationships, pretty much anything in life,” and he wanted a better practice mechanism. He’d gone to Toastmasters, which he calls “a great way to practice public speaking,” but the feedback loop was too slow—he’d record his speeches and never actually review them because other priorities always crowded them out. The insight was to use public content as a **powerful commitment mechanism**. “If I know that I have to post it, then I'm really gonna pour over the thing,” he explains, making the editing process an intense, enforced reflection on his own speaking.

Content format was another deliberate choice. He figured video content made sense, but podcasting felt like the best fit because he naturally gravitates toward professional conversations. He set an intentionally “really lax goal” of one episode per month, knowing he was “already drowning in responsibility and a bunch of other things.” What he didn’t anticipate was how fun the process would be: “podcasting, if we do it right, is just having a conversation that I want to have,” with guests he considered “dream guests that I wanted to talk to.” The show’s entire intellectual premise traces back to his early days at Facebook, where he’d devour 50-minute career retrospectives written by very senior ICs—“super impressive people that I looked up to, and they had a ton of interesting stuff that I was learning from.” The podcast became a way to bring those same stories into audio form, hosting “super senior people and have them tell their career stories,” which he describes as something he’s “already so interested in hearing about, which makes for a very natural and interesting conversation.”

**Behind the scenes of the top episodes**

The first episode that genuinely broke out was with Philip Sue, a super senior engineer whose internal writing was legendary within Meta but who was otherwise “not super well known” externally. The recording itself felt different. “I felt so enamored while he was speaking. I was fully zoned in and almost addicted to the conversation while it was happening,” Ryan recalls. After he poured extra editing effort into it, the graphs went “vertical” within a day or two—a moment of validation that proved “it's not just me that likes these stories,” and that the show might actually have product-market fit.

A similar electric energy returned during the more recent Ethan Evans interview, which he describes as a conversation he simply “couldn't stop listening to.” Ethan, he says, is “one of the best speakers I've seen in tech,” and the raw recording energy matched Philip’s. But the key behind-the-scenes difference with Ethan’s episode was the trailer strategy. Without the brand recognition of an Acquired or Lex Fridman—whose names alone are the hook—Ryan realized a cold-start show needs to manufacture that hook. His video editor Tino assembled a trailer cut that Ryan calls “ridiculous. It was so good.” He describes it as “super aggressive,” opening with a “crazy attention-grabbing energy”: “The video starts out saying, ‘This is the most brutally honest,’ and it has crazy attention-grabbing energy.” This sparked a balancing act he’s still navigating. He’s heard feedback from some listeners that the trailers are too over-the-top, and he’s experimenting with a platform-specific fix. On Spotify and Apple Podcasts, where recurring listeners dominate, he’s considering “doing no trailers or maybe a very easy-to-listen-to quick one that just has one or two lines.” On YouTube, the aggressive trailer stays to win new viewers, though he’s trying to “keep them a little more tasteful and not go overboard,” and he’s explicitly asking his audience to “keep me honest” via comments, “and we can figure out a good balance as we go.”

**Dream guest list**

Ryan’s dream guest list is explicitly counter-programmed against the typical tech podcast. “I want to find these hidden super senior engineers who no one knows at all,” he says—the architects of incredible careers inside Google, Facebook, Apple, or Microsoft who are “super well known” within their orgs, have “incredible stories,” and are “engineers that we can all look up to,” but who you’ve “probably never heard of.” The sourcing challenge is steep: “LinkedIn search is pretty bad,” and these people are rarely active on social media, so “you can't just Google for this list of people.” Early episodes leaned entirely on his own network—“the people that I really look up to and think are incredible engineers” from his time in big tech. Expanding beyond that circle has relied on a crowdsourcing strategy: “asking the audience, who is that senior IC in your org that you're super impressed by that you want me to interview?” He reports this “has worked super well,” and it’s how he’s now lining up guests from companies like Apple, Google, and Microsoft who would have been unreachable through his personal network alone—made possible “because someone in the comments on YouTube said this guy is a super strong engineer, please interview him.”

**Learnings from podcasting**

The single biggest unanticipated challenge is a conversational tic that audio production punishes ruthlessly: verbal backchanneling. In normal conversation, saying “uh-huh, right, yeah, mm-hmm” is natural and signals engagement. But “if you do that while the guest is speaking, you can just imagine someone in your AirPods, you're trying to listen to someone and all you can hear is the uh-huh, right, yeah, mm-hmm.” He’s had to retrain himself, and says he’s “still getting better at this.” His current technique is to replace every instinct to vocalize with vigorous physical nodding: “I'm just nodding vigorously and trying to hold myself back from saying anything.” He calls it “very different from normal conversation” and names it as “probably the biggest thing that's different” from what he expected going in.

**Balancing content with a full-time job**

The balance is, bluntly, brutal. With big tech expectations intensifying—“to meet expectations, you already have to work a lot”—any side project “is going to push out something in my life.” For roughly two years, his Thursday-night routine was “staying up till 4:00 AM writing a newsletter” because he was “struggling to stay afloat” and pushing everything to the last minute. He recounts a lunch with Gergely Orosz, who writes the largest engineering newsletter, where Gergely told him he was “surprised that I was working at the same time as doing the newsletter” and warned him to “be careful about burning the candle at both ends.” Ryan’s retrospective take, with two or three years of experience since: “he's totally right.” He adds a sobering qualifier: “If I had a family or other time commitments, I would literally be drowning.”

---

His entire show is an expression of the same **commitment mechanism** insight that drove him to start it—public stakes force the deep work, whether on speaking cadence or a brutal trailer edit. The **“hidden super senior engineers” sourcing playbook**—crowdsourcing names from an audience to find people who’d never surface on LinkedIn—is itself a product of the podcast’s premise: the stories worth telling are buried inside orgs, not algorithm-fed feeds, and you only reach them by building trust with an audience that wants the same thing. Even the balancing act with trailers (aggressive on YouTube, stripped-back for recurring listeners) mirrors his early goal-setting: find the minimum viable commitment that still creates a feedback loop, then tune it relentlessly.
