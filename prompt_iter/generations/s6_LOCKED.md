## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### Difficulty estimation cost makes the reported 4× efficiency an upper bound, not a realised saving

The entire compute‑optimal policy hinges on knowing a prompt’s difficulty *before* the main inference budget is spent. The paper’s method for predicting difficulty is to generate 2,048 samples per question, score them with the PRM, average the scores, and then bin the result into five quintiles. The authors are transparent about the overhead:

> “estimating difficulty in this way still incurs additional computation cost during inference … our experiments do not account for this cost largely for simplicity” (Section 3.2).

This cost is enormous — for a 500‑question test set it requires 1,024,000 sample generations just for difficulty estimation, far exceeding the largest test‑time compute budgets studied (256–512 generations per question). The headline “4× efficiency over best‑of‑N” is computed *after* difficulty is known, without amortising the estimation expense. A real deployment would either have to pay this full estimation tax per prompt or find a cheaper proxy, neither of which is demonstrated in the paper. In devices or applications with strict latency and cost limits, the pre‑binning overhead could dominate the total inference spend, nullifying or even reversing the reported advantage. The paper suggests future work on predicting difficulty directly from the question text but provides no implementation or analysis of how such a model would affect the tradeoff.

### The entire analysis rests on a single benchmark and a single model family, leaving generalisation uncertain

All experiments use the MATH benchmark (500 questions) and all models are from the PaLM 2 family — PaLM 2‑S* as the base model and a ∼14× larger PaLM 2 variant for the FLOPs‑matched comparison. The paper argues that PaLM 2‑S* “is representative of the capabilities of many contemporary LLMs” (Section 4), but provides no corroborating evidence on other architectures (GPT‑class, open‑source models) or on other reasoning domains (code generation, formal logic, scientific QA). MATH is a narrowly scoped task: high‑school competition‑level mathematics with a clean ground‑truth answer that can be checked with exact string matching. The difficulty‑dependent patterns — beam search over‑optimising on easy problems, revisions helping only on easy prompts, and no method helping on hard prompts — may not transfer to tasks where the verifier signal is less clean, where “difficulty” has a different structure, or where models are differently calibrated. The reliance on a single 500‑question test set also raises statistical questions: with five difficulty bins of ~100 questions each and two‑fold cross‑validation, the compute‑optimal strategy is selected from roughly 50 samples per bin per fold, and no confidence intervals are reported for the scaling curves (Figures 4, 8). While the paper’s qualitative findings are compelling, their quantitative reliability and out‑of‑domain transferability remain unproven.

### The hardest problems are essentially unsolvable with test‑time compute — the method amplifies existing capability but cannot create it

Across every axis — PRM search, revisions, compute‑optimal allocation — the most difficult quintile (bin 5, pass@1 near zero) shows **near‑zero improvement** regardless of how much test‑time compute is applied. Figure 3 (right) shows bin 5 accuracy at 1–3% for all search methods at all budgets. Figure 7 (right) shows bin 5 revision accuracy at 2–3% across all sequential‑to‑parallel ratios. In the FLOPs‑matched comparison (Figure 9), bin 5 scaling lines are essentially flat near 0–5% for both revisions and PRM search, always below the ∼14× larger model’s performance. The paper is candid: “on the hardest questions … no method makes much meaningful progress” (Section 5.3). This is not a weakness of the compute‑optimal policy per se, but a fundamental boundary: test‑time compute exploits solutions already present in the model’s output distribution. If the base model’s pass@1 is near zero, there are no correct straws to grasp. For problem classes that genuinely require new reasoning capabilities not present in the pretrained model, additional inference‑time computation — with current methods — cannot substitute for larger‑scale pretraining, a fact the paper itself reinforces by showing pretraining wins on hard problems at higher `$R$` values.

### The FLOPs‑matched comparison against pretraining uses a sub‑optimal larger model, weakening the “test‑time > pretraining” claim

In the exchange‑rate analysis (Section 7), the “larger model” scales parameters by ∼14× while keeping training data fixed, matching the LLaMA paradigm rather than a Chinchilla‑optimal scaling that grows data and model size jointly. The paper acknowledges this:

> “we choose this setting as it is representative of a canonical approach to scaling pretraining compute and leave the analysis of compute-optimal scaling of pretraining compute … to future work.”

A Chinchilla‑optimal larger model would almost certainly be stronger, narrowing the advantage of test‑time compute. Additionally, the larger model is evaluated with **greedy decoding only** — no best‑of‑N, no majority voting, no verifier. In a real deployment, even a modest test‑time compute allowance (e.g., best‑of‑8) would boost the larger model’s performance, making the comparison asymmetric. The FLOPs equations in Section 7 provide a framework that could incorporate such enhancements, but they are not explored. Consequently, the headline finding that “test‑time compute can outperform a 14× larger model” should be interpreted as an existence proof under the specific, intentionally conservative baseline, not as a guarantee that inference‑time computation generally beats scaling pretraining — especially on hard problems or when inference volume is high.

### The revision model’s 38% correct‑to‑incorrect reversion rate and sensitivity to training procedure indicate unresolved fragility

During sequential revision, around **38% of correct answers are inadvertently revised back into incorrect answers** in the next step (Section 6.1). This occurs because the model is trained exclusively on trajectories where all in‑context answers are incorrect followed by a correct one; it never learns to “stay correct.” The current mitigation — scanning the whole chain with a verifier or majority vote — is a patch, not a solution: it does not prevent the model from wasting budget on destructive revisions. Moreover, Appendix K shows that attempting to optimise the revision model with ReST$^{\text{EM}}$ backfires dramatically, causing performance to degrade with more sequential steps (Figure 16). The authors hypothesise that on‑policy data collection “exacerbates spurious correlations in revision data, causing the optimized model to fail to learn the revision task.” This demonstrates that the revision approach is sensitive to the data‑generation strategy in ways that are not fully understood, and that the positive results depend on specific, carefully constructed offline data and edit‑distance‑based incorrect‑correct pairing. A practitioner replicating this work with a different base model or task would need to invest significant effort in data engineering to avoid similar pitfalls, and the 38% reversion rate is a persistent drain on the effective batch size of sequential sampling.

### The analysis ignores latency and serialisation costs, making the sequential‑heavy strategies favoured by the policy unrealistic for interactive settings

The paper measures compute solely in “generations” (approximately total FLOPs), which equates one sample from a parallel batch with one step in a sequential revision chain. In wall‑clock time, however, generating a chain of 64 sequential revisions costs roughly 64× the latency of generating 64 independent samples in parallel (assuming sufficient hardware). The compute‑optimal policy often prescribes purely sequential revisions on easy problems (Figure 7, right) and a sequential‑to‑parallel ratio favouring sequential steps at lower budgets (Figure 7, left). For real‑time applications — such as interactive assistants, code completion, or any system with a human in the loop — a strategy that requires tens of serial steps is simply infeasible, regardless of its FLOPs efficiency. The paper does not discuss this tradeoff at all. A more complete cost model would incorporate both throughput (total FLOPs) and latency (wall‑clock time), but no such model is provided, leaving practitioners to guess whether the accuracy gains from sequential revisions are worth the added delay. This omission is especially acute given that the paper’s primary use‑case motivation — on‑device deployment (Section 1) — is exactly the setting where latency is most critical.
