# The Past Is Not Past: Memory-Enhanced Dynamic Reward Shaping

**ArXiv:** [2604.11297](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.11297)

## 🎯 Pitch

On-policy RL for math reasoning often locks models into repeating the exact same mistakes over and over, collapsing exploration even as samples look superficially different. This paper breaks that cycle by penalizing responses whose internal reasoning patterns cluster with past errors, yielding up to a 17% relative gain on OlympiadBench for Qwen3-8B without extra forward-pass cost.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper proposes **MEDS** (Memory-Enhanced Dynamic reward Shaping), a framework that explicitly penalizes recurring failure patterns during reinforcement learning for LLM reasoning by maintaining a per-prompt memory of historical error representations and applying density-based clustering to identify prevalent error modes, then dynamically increasing penalties for rollouts assigned to already-crowded clusters (e.g., a response exhibiting the same faulty prime-factorization reasoning as many prior attempts receives a stronger penalty). Across five mathematical benchmarks—AIME24, AMC23, MATH500, Minerva, and OlympiadBench—and three base models including Qwen3-1.7B, Qwen2.5-Math-7B, and Qwen3-8B, MEDS consistently outperforms GRPO, DAPO, and entropy-regularized GRPO baselines, achieving up to 4.13 pass@1 points and 4.37 pass@128 points of improvement, with the largest single-dataset gain boosting Qwen3-8B from 70.81 to 82.67 on OlympiadBench—a relative increase of 17%. The framework establishes that logit-based clustering of historical rollouts provides a lightweight yet effective proxy for reasoning similarity, enabling adaptive reward shaping that increases exploration diversity and mitigates error collapse, though the approach remains limited to domains where clear correctness signals exist for the initial reward function and where layer-wise logit patterns capture meaningful reasoning structure.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: On-Policy RL Causes Reasoning Mode Collapse

Reinforcement learning for LLM reasoning, particularly RLVR (Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards), follows a standard loop: the model generates complete reasoning trajectories for a batch of prompts, each trajectory is scored by some reward function (e.g., exact answer matching against the ground truth), and the policy is updated to increase the probability of high-reward trajectories while decreasing the probability of low-reward ones. This is the foundation of methods like GRPO, DAPO, and their variants — and it has produced impressive results, including on competition-level mathematics.

However, this loop has a well-known pathology. As training progresses, the policy often **collapses into a narrow set of stereotyped behaviors** (Section 1, citing prior work by Yu et al. [6] on the DAPO system, Sinha et al. [7], and Chen et al. [8] on exploration-enhanced policy optimization). The authors describe this failure mode vividly in their own terms:

> "This degeneration produces highly repetitive responses that not only weaken effective exploration and waste on-policy samples, but also entrench the model in self-reinforcing erroneous reasoning trajectories."

What does this look like in practice? Figure 1(a) in the paper illustrates the trajectory: the policy starts from a high-entropy distribution (many diverse reasoning paths are sampled, some correct, some incorrect) but, through repeated optimization toward the reward signal, it gravitates into what the authors call a **"stable error basin"** — a region of the policy's output space where it repeatedly produces the same *type* of mistake. The key insight is that these mistakes are not necessarily identical token-by-token; they share an underlying reasoning failure mode while manifesting through slightly different surface expressions. Figure 1(b) shows this concretely: three seemingly different responses — one misreading the problem setup, one parsing the setup incorrectly, one making a setup understanding error — all trace back to the same root failure of "faulty setup reading." The policy learns to explore a neighborhood of this error rather than escaping it, because within this neighborhood, the reward remains zero regardless of the specific variant, and the gradient signal provides no directional push toward a qualitatively different reasoning strategy.

This is not merely an inefficiency problem. It is a **self-reinforcing trap**: because on-policy optimization uses samples from the current policy to estimate the gradient, as the policy concentrates probability mass in the error basin, more and more of its samples fall into that basin, the gradient estimates become increasingly biased toward that region, and the policy becomes further entrenched. The model's effective exploration budget — the chance that any given sample will try a genuinely novel reasoning approach — decays with training, not because the optimizer is doing something wrong mathematically, but because the reward landscape is flat and uninformative within large regions of behavior space.

### Why This Matters: The Cost of Stale Rollouts

The problem is not just theoretical. In RLVR training, every rollout is expensive — the model must autoregressively generate a full chain-of-thought (often thousands of tokens for mathematical reasoning), the reward is computed (itself potentially expensive if it involves code execution or external verification), and the policy update must process all of these tokens through the optimizer. Rollouts that merely repeat already-known failure modes represent **wasted computation** in three ways simultaneously:

1. **The generation cost is wasted.** The FLOPs spent on generating a repeated error produce no new information about how to solve the problem.
2. **The gradient signal is diluted.** When many rollouts in a batch cluster around the same error, the policy update receives essentially the same negative signal multiplied by the cluster size, rather than diverse information about *different* failure modes and their relationship to the reward surface.
3. **The entropy budget is consumed.** Standard entropy regularization (discussed below) tries to maintain output diversity, but if the entropy penalty is applied at the token level, the model can still collapse at the behavioral level — it generates different word sequences that encode the same reasoning pattern, satisfying the entropy constraint while exploring nothing new.

This has direct practical consequences. If error collapse means that only 3 out of 16 rollouts per prompt in a batch explore genuinely different reasoning strategies, then the effective sample size for exploration is much smaller than the nominal batch size. The optimizer may require many more training steps to stumble upon a correct reasoning path, and in the worst case — if the error basin is large enough in the probability space — it may never escape, even with extensive training. This is what the paper's introduction refers to when it says the policy is "trapped in this pattern despite continued optimization over many steps."

Beyond computational efficiency, this matters for **robustness and generalization**. A policy that has explored only a narrow set of reasoning strategies for a given problem type is fragile: it may not generalize to variations of the problem that require a different approach, and it is more vulnerable to adversarial perturbations. The connection to diversity in supervised learning (where data augmentation improves generalization by preventing overfitting to spurious patterns) has a direct analog here: exploration diversity in on-policy RL prevents overfitting to a limited set of reasoning strategies.

### Where Prior Approaches Fall Short

The paper identifies three broad families of prior approaches and systematically explains why each is insufficient for the specific problem of **cross-rollout error repetition**.

#### Entropy Regularization: Right Idea, Wrong Granularity

Entropy regularization (Haarnoja et al., 2018 [10]; Mnih et al., 2016 [12]) is the classical solution to mode collapse in RL. The idea is simple: add a bonus to the reward that is proportional to the entropy of the policy's output distribution at each step, encouraging the policy to remain stochastic rather than collapsing to a deterministic output. In the LLM context, this means adding a per-token entropy term to the advantage function (as in the "GRPO w/ Entropy Adv" baseline the paper includes, which follows Cheng et al. [41]).

The problem, as the paper argues (Section 1), is one of **granularity mismatch**. Entropy regularization operates at the level of individual tokens within a single rollout. It encourages the policy to distribute probability mass across multiple tokens at each position. But a pattern of reasoning error collapse operates at a much coarser level — it is about the *semantic content* of multi-step reasoning trajectories, not about individual token choices. The policy can have high per-token entropy (using different phrasings, different connecting words, different notational conventions) while still producing the same underlying reasoning flaw. The paper states this directly:

> "In LLM reasoning, such distribution-level stochastic exploration is often insufficient due to the vast action space."

The "vast action space" is crucial here. For a vocabulary of size $V$, the per-step entropy bonus encourages the model to consider $V$ alternatives. But the space of reasoning *strategies* is much smaller and structured very differently — a mathematical reasoning error like "assuming $n$ must be of the form $p^2$ because we need three divisors" is a high-level conceptual mistake that can be expressed through many different token sequences. The entropy bonus doesn't know this; it just sees flat probabilities.

The paper's results confirm this limitation: "GRPO w/ Entropy Adv" consistently underperforms not only MEDS but often underperforms even vanilla DAPO. For example, on Qwen3-8B on AIME24, GRPO with entropy advantage achieves 27.32 pass@1 vs. DAPO's 45.42 (Table 1). The entropy bonus, rather than promoting diverse exploration, appears to inject noise that degrades optimization without actually steering the policy away from error basins.

#### Uniqueness-Based Rewards: Batch-Level but Not History-Aware

A more targeted line of work explicitly rewards rare reasoning behaviors. Hu et al. [23] clusters rollouts within a single batch by solution strategy and inversely weights advantages by cluster size — so trajectories with unusual strategies get amplified advantage signals. Wan et al. [11] promotes diversity among correct trajectories with token-level entropy regularization. These approaches recognize that diversity should be rewarded, not just maintained through entropy.

The limitation, which the paper identifies explicitly (Section 2.2), is that these methods "operate only on current-batch differences and do not track solution patterns over long-term training history." A reasoning pattern that is rare within a single batch of 16 rollouts might have been sampled thousands of times across all previous training steps. The batch-level perspective is myopic: it has no memory of which errors have been repeatedly explored and found to be dead ends. Without this memory, the optimizer can rediscover the same error pattern in batch after batch, each time treating it as a legitimate (if penalized) candidate, rather than building up an accumulating penalty that eventually makes the error pattern prohibitively costly.

The paper's analogy to human learning in Section 1 captures this gap well: human learners "impose stronger psychological penalties on recurring mistakes to reinforce avoidance." The first time you make a particular reasoning mistake on a math problem, you note it and try something else. The tenth time you catch yourself about to make the same mistake, an internal alarm goes off — the accumulated experience has taught you to recognize the pattern early and steer away from it. Uniqueness-based methods have no mechanism for accumulating penalties across training iterations; they treat each batch as a fresh start.

#### UCB-Style Exploration Rewards: Outcome-Level, Not Step-Level

Song et al. [24] takes a step toward history-awareness by introducing UCB-style exploration rewards based on historical outcome frequencies. The idea is an upper-confidence-bound bonus: actions (or solution strategies) that have been tried few times historically receive a bonus to encourage exploration, while those frequently tried and found wanting receive no bonus. This is a genuine memory-based approach.

However, the paper identifies a critical limitation (Section 2.2): "it still lacks fine-grained cross-step reasoning behavior tracking." The UCB approach tracks outcomes — was the final answer correct or incorrect? — but does not capture the *internal reasoning structure* of the rollout. Two different reasoning strategies that both produce the wrong answer are treated identically by an outcome-based memory; the distinction between "incorrect because of a setup parsing error" and "incorrect because of an algebraic manipulation error" is lost. This matters because different error types have different relationships to the correct solution path. A setup parsing error might be "close" to a correct solution in reasoning space (the rest of the reasoning might be perfectly valid given the misinterpreted premise), while an algebraic error might represent a fundamentally different approach. An effective memory system should distinguish between these, applying different penalties based on pattern similarity, not just outcome identity.

#### External Model-Based Adaptive Rewards: Computationally Expensive

Another line of work ([25, 26, 27, 28, 29]) uses external models — often LLMs themselves — to adaptively adjust rewards based on historical performance. For example, an external evaluator might analyze past rollouts, identify common failure modes, and design new reward components that specifically penalize those patterns.

The paper acknowledges these approaches but argues they have a **scalability problem**: "the additional computational cost limits scalability" (Section 2.2). Running a separate LLM inference pass to analyze every batch of rollouts — potentially for thousands of prompts across many training steps — adds substantial computational overhead. For training runs that already strain GPU budgets, doubling or tripling the inference cost to support adaptive reward shaping is prohibitive. The MEDS framework explicitly aims to avoid this cost by reusing representations that are already computed during the forward pass (the layer-wise logits), adding "almost no additional computational overhead" (Section 1).

### How MEDS Positions Itself

MEDS proposes to address all of these gaps simultaneously through a unified framework with three design principles, each directly responding to a specific limitation of prior work:

**1. Cross-rollout memory (vs. batch-level only).** MEDS maintains a per-prompt memory of all historical error representations, not just the current batch. This memory persists across training steps and epochs, enabling the system to recognize that a reasoning pattern appearing in batch 50 is the same as one that dominated batch 10 — and apply an appropriately accumulated penalty. This directly addresses the myopia critique of uniqueness-based methods.

**2. Representation-level pattern recognition (vs. outcome-level tracking).** MEDS identifies similarity among rollouts by clustering their layer-wise logit representations rather than by comparing final answers or using external annotators. This means two rollouts with different final answers but similar reasoning structures (e.g., both assuming the answer must be $p^2$ for a prime $p$, but reaching different incorrect conclusions due to arithmetic differences) can be recognized as belonging to the same error cluster and penalized accordingly. This addresses the coarseness critique of UCB-style methods.

**3. Density-based dynamic penalties (vs. static reward shaping).** MEDS uses HDBSCAN clustering to adaptively determine both the number of error clusters and their density structure, then computes a penalty proportional to $\log(|C_k| + 1)$ for each cluster $C_k$, capped by a hyperparameter $\beta$. This means the penalty is not a fixed constant — it grows (logarithmically) as more rollouts fall into the same error cluster, creating an accumulating disincentive that eventually makes the basin financially prohibitive for the optimizer. This directly addresses the stable-error-basin trap: while entropy regularization tries to maintain exploration everywhere, density-based penalties specifically pressure the policy *away from* the regions where it has already spent excessive exploration budget, freeing probability mass for genuinely novel regions of behavior space.

**4. Theoretical grounding.** Unlike prior work that proposes reward modifications heuristically, the paper provides a formal theorem (Section 3.2, Theorem 1) showing that under reasonably mild conditions — specifically, that $c(\mathbf{y})$ is monotonically increasing in repetition count, and that task rewards $r(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y})$ are non-increasing in repetition count (i.e., repeated errors do not suddenly become correct) — the expected return under the penalized reward signal is guaranteed to be no lower than under the original signal: $\mathbf{J}(q_2) \geq \mathbf{J}(q_1)$. The proof relies on a covariance argument using Chebyshev's rearrangement inequality, showing that the penalty weight and the reward are positively correlated under the unpenalized policy (both are decreasing functions of repetition count), so reweighting by the penalty disproportionately downweights low-reward samples. This provides a principled justification for *why* dynamic penalties should work, rather than treating it as a hyperparameter tuning exercise.

### The Practical Context: Why This Fills a Genuine Gap

To understand why MEDS is a timely contribution, it's worth noting the current state of the LLM RL landscape. GRPO and DAPO have become dominant due to their simplicity and effectiveness — DAPO in particular demonstrated strong results while being fully open-source. However, as more practitioners adopt these methods, the mode collapse problem identified in the DAPO paper itself [6] has become a recognized pain point. Solutions that require expensive external verifiers or massive additional computation (like generating on-policy data with a separate model) are unattractive for the open-source community that values accessibility.

MEDS offers a path forward that:
- **Reuses existing computation** (the logits are already produced during the forward pass for policy evaluation).
- **Adds minimal overhead** (clustering a growing set of vectors per prompt, but HDBSCAN scales reasonably well, and the paper reports only modest increases in per-step time — 8.46 minutes for 50 steps vs. 8.00 for DAPO on Qwen2.5-Math-7B).
- **Integrates cleanly into existing RL frameworks** — it is essentially a reward transformation layer that can be plugged into any GRPO/DAPO-style training loop, implemented by modifying the reward computation step.
- **Provides theoretical backing** that makes the approach principled rather than a bag of tricks.

The paper's explicit framing as a **memory-enhanced dynamic reward shaping** framework — as opposed to "another exploration bonus" — signals that its primary contribution is not the specific penalty formula or clustering algorithm choice, but rather the principle that historical behavioral signals should systematically inform reward design in on-policy LLM optimization. The clustering on logits, the logarithmic penalty scaling, and the HDBSCAN density estimation are instantiations of this principle; the framework itself is more general and could accommodate alternative representation methods or clustering algorithms.

### What Makes This Non-Obvious

Two aspects of the contribution are worth flagging as genuinely non-trivial:

First, **the choice of logits as reasoning representations is counterintuitive.** Logits are the raw, pre-softmax activations at the output vocabulary layer — they encode the model's probabilities over next tokens, not any explicit reasoning structure. The claim that clustering these logit vectors captures "reasoning patterns" rather than just surface-level token preferences requires empirical validation. The paper recognizes this and devotes Sections 4.4–4.6 to establishing that logit-based clustering correlates substantially with LLM-based semantic clustering (61.2% agreement with Claude annotations) and that better clustering quality (higher correlation with semantic annotations) predicts better downstream performance. This is an empirical discovery, not an obvious design choice — prior work on understanding LLM reasoning through logits (Meng et al., Templeton et al.) had established that logits contain information about internal computation, but using them as a lightweight proxy for reasoning similarity in an RL training loop is novel.

Second, **the density-based, per-cluster penalty scaling is carefully designed to avoid common failure modes.** A naive implementation might penalize all incorrect rollouts equally, which would simply scale down all negative advantage signals uniformly — equivalent to changing the KL penalty coefficient and providing no differential signal. Alternatively, one might penalize based on exact answer match (same wrong answer = same penalty), which would miss semantic similarity across different wrong answers that share reasoning structure. The HDBSCAN approach with logarithmic scaling automatically handles both problems: it groups only rollouts that are genuinely similar in representation space (avoiding the coarseness of answer-match grouping), and the logarithmic scaling ensures that the penalty grows quickly for early repetitions (creating pressure to escape the error basin) but saturates for very large clusters (preventing any single penalty from dominating the reward signal). The hyperparameters $\alpha = 0.1$ and $\beta = 0.2$ for the smaller models (vs. $\alpha = 0.02$, $\beta = 0.04$ for Qwen3-8B) reflect this careful calibration.

### Connection to Broader Trends

The work sits at the intersection of several active research directions in 2025–2026:

**RL for reasoning** is rapidly maturing, with open-source frameworks (verl, OpenRLHF) making large-scale RLVR training accessible. As more groups train reasoning models, the practical challenges of training stability, sample efficiency, and mode collapse have shifted from theoretical concerns to daily engineering problems. MEDS addresses a specific engineering pain point in this pipeline.

**Memory-augmented neural networks** have a long history, but applying explicit memory mechanisms to *reward design* during online RL is less explored. The idea of maintaining a memory bank of representations and using density in that bank to compute exploration bonuses has roots in count-based exploration (Bellemare et al., 2016), but the adaptation to LLM reasoning — where "states" are not discrete environments but continuous representation spaces, and where "exploration" means exploring reasoning strategies rather than physical actions — is a nontrivial translation.

**Self-improvement loops** for LLMs (STaR, ReST$^{EM}$, etc.) face a closely related problem: as the model generates its own training data, errors can compound and diversity can collapse. MEDS could be applied not just during online RL training but also during data generation for self-improvement, where the memory of past errors could guide which reasoning paths to explore further and which to abandon. The paper does not explore this application, but the framework naturally extends to it — a point worth noting for practitioners thinking about integration.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

The paper proposes **MEDS**, a system that plugs into any on-policy reinforcement learning training loop for LLM reasoning (such as GRPO or DAPO) and **dynamically adjusts the reward signal down for rollouts whose reasoning patterns resemble previously-encountered errors**, using a lightweight per-prompt memory of historical logit representations and density-based clustering. It solves the problem that standard RLVR training causes the policy to repeatedly sample the same *types* of mistakes across training steps — not identical token sequences, but semantically similar reasoning failures — by imposing an accumulating penalty on error clusters that grow large over time, thereby pressuring the optimizer to allocate probability mass toward unexplored regions of the behavior space rather than re-sampling known dead ends.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The MEDS system has four major components that operate in sequence during each training step:

1. **Logic Feature Extractor** — For each generated rollout, takes the per-layer logits at the position of the *first token of the final answer* from the latter half of the Transformer layers, concatenates them into a feature vector $f(\tilde{\mathbf{y}})$, and normalizes them. This vector serves as a compact representation of the model's internal reasoning trajectory for that specific response. It is produced with almost zero additional cost because the logits are already computed during the autoregressive forward pass.

2. **Error Memory** — Maintains a per-prompt set $G_{\mathbf{x}}$ that accumulates all feature vectors from all historical incorrect rollouts for that prompt, across all prior training steps and epochs. This is a growing collection — as training proceeds, the memory for each prompt expands to include newly-sampled incorrect responses.

3. **Density-Based Clusterer** — At each step, applies HDBSCAN clustering to the accumulated memory $G_{\mathbf{x}}$ for each prompt, adaptively grouping feature vectors into clusters $C_1, C_2, \ldots, C_K$ plus a noise set $C_{\text{noise}}$. Each cluster represents a distinct error *mode* — a recurring reasoning pattern that the policy has sampled multiple times historically. The cluster sizes $|C_k|$ capture how frequently each error pattern has been encountered.

4. **Dynamic Reward Shaper** — For each new rollout $\tilde{\mathbf{y}}$, identifies its assigned cluster $C_k$, computes a penalty $\min(\alpha \log(|C_k| + 1), \beta)$ that grows with cluster size but is capped, and subtracts this penalty from the original task reward: $\tilde{r}(\mathbf{x}, \tilde{\mathbf{y}}) = r(\mathbf{x}, \tilde{\mathbf{y}}) - \text{penalty}$. This adjusted reward is then passed to the policy optimizer (e.g., GRPO or DAPO), which uses it to compute advantage estimates and update the policy parameters.

Information flows as follows: a prompt enters the model → the model autoregressively generates a complete response (including chain-of-thought reasoning and a final answer) → the final-answer token logits are extracted from each layer and concatenated into a feature vector → the feature vector is compared against the existing error memory for that prompt via HDBSCAN clustering → the rollout's assigned cluster size determines the penalty magnitude → the task reward is decreased by that penalty → the penalized reward feeds into the advantage computation → the policy is updated → at the end of the step, the feature vector is added to the error memory (if incorrect) for future clustering.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the formal reinforcement learning objective (the KL-regularized expected reward maximization) — because MEDS modifies the reward signal, and understanding *where* the modification plugs in requires seeing the base optimization problem.
- **Second**, the theoretical result (Theorem 1) — because it establishes *why* penalizing repeated errors should improve expected return, providing principled justification for the entire framework before diving into implementation details.
- **Third**, the logic feature extraction mechanism (Section 3.3) — because the choice of representation for reasoning patterns is the core enabling technology; everything downstream depends on logit vectors capturing meaningful similarity structure.
- **Fourth**, the memory and clustering pipeline (Section 3.4) — because this is where the theoretical idea of "repetition count $c(\mathbf{y})$" is operationalized: how historical representations are stored, how clusters are formed, and how cluster size maps to penalty magnitude.
- **Fifth**, the hyperparameter and configuration specifics — because the penalty scaling ($\alpha$, $\beta$), layer selection, normalization, and HDBSCAN parameters collectively determine the framework's behavior, and the paper makes deliberate choices here that differ across model scales.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily an **empirical methods paper with a theoretical motivation** whose core idea is that memory-based dynamic reward shaping — penalizing rollouts proportionally to how many times their reasoning pattern has been seen before — can mitigate error collapse in on-policy RL for LLM reasoning without requiring expensive external verifiers or model modifications. The theoretical component (Theorem 1) establishes that the expected return under the penalized reward is no lower than under the original reward under mild conditions, while the empirical component demonstrates through extensive experiments across three model scales and five benchmarks that the framework consistently outperforms static-reward baselines.

#### Reinforcement Learning Preliminaries and the KL-Regularized Objective

The paper operates within the standard **RLVR (Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards)** framework, which the authors formalize as follows (Section 3.1). The LLM is modeled as a policy $p_\theta$ parameterized by $\theta$. Given an input prompt $\mathbf{x}$ sampled from a dataset $\mathcal{D}$, the policy generates a complete response sequence:

$$\mathbf{y} = \{y_1, y_2, \ldots, y_L\} \in \mathcal{Y}$$

where $y_t$ denotes the token generated at step $t$, $L$ is the total length of the response, and $\mathcal{Y}$ is the space of all possible token sequences. The response is evaluated by a reward function $r: \mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{Y} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ that maps each (prompt, response) pair to a scalar reward. In the mathematical reasoning benchmarks used in this paper, $r(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y})$ is typically a binary indicator of whether the extracted final answer matches the ground truth, or a rule-based verifier that checks answer correctness.

**What this represents:** The policy is an autoregressive language model that takes a prompt and produces a variable-length sequence of tokens. The reward function is the external verifier that determines whether the final answer is correct — it does not provide step-level feedback; it only looks at the conclusion. This is the standard setup for GRPO, DAPO, and related methods.

The optimization objective follows the KL-regularized trust-region formulation commonly used in RLHF and RLVR:

$$\max_\theta \, \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x} \sim \mathcal{D}} \left[ \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y} \sim p_\theta(\cdot|\mathbf{x})} \left[ r(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}) \right] - \frac{1}{\eta} \text{KL}(p_\theta(\cdot|\mathbf{x}) \| p_{\text{ref}}(\cdot|\mathbf{x})) \right]$$

where $\eta$ is the KL penalty coefficient controlling the strength of the regularization, $p_{\text{ref}}$ is a reference policy (typically the initial model before RL training or the model from a previous iteration), and $\text{KL}(p_\theta \| p_{\text{ref}})$ is the Kullback-Leibler divergence between the current and reference policies.

**What it computes:** This objective seeks to maximize the expected reward of the policy's outputs (first term) while penalizing the policy for deviating too far from the reference distribution (second term). The KL term acts as a constraint, preventing the policy from collapsing to a degenerate distribution that exploits quirks in the reward function or overfits to a narrow set of solution patterns. The coefficient $1/\eta$ trades off reward maximization against distributional stability.

**Why this form:** The KL-regularized objective is the standard formulation for policy optimization with a trust region (derived from the Proximal Policy Optimization lineage and adopted by GRPO/DAPO/RLHF). Without the KL penalty, the policy can undergo arbitrarily large updates in a single step, potentially destroying capabilities learned during pretraining or fine-tuning. The KL term bounds the effective step size in probability space: a large $\eta$ (small $1/\eta$) allows larger policy shifts, while a small $\eta$ (large $1/\eta$) keeps the policy close to the reference. In DAPO, $\eta$ is effectively set to infinity (the KL term is removed entirely), relying instead on clipping to constrain updates. In GRPO, the paper uses $\beta = 10^{-3}$ as the KL penalty coefficient (Appendix A.2).

**How MEDS modifies this:** MEDS does not change the optimization objective itself. It transforms the reward signal *before* it enters the objective. Specifically, it replaces $r(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y})$ with a dynamically adjusted reward $\tilde{r}(\mathbf{x}, \tilde{\mathbf{y}}) = r(\mathbf{x}, \tilde{\mathbf{y}}) - \text{penalty}$, where the penalty depends on the rollout's similarity to historical errors stored in the per-prompt memory. The optimizer sees the penalized reward as if it were the ground truth, so all downstream computations (advantage estimation, probability ratio clipping, etc.) operate on the adjusted signal without any architectural changes to the RL algorithm. This is the key design choice that makes MEDS a **plug-in reward shaper** rather than a new RL algorithm: it can be dropped into any existing GRPO or DAPO training loop by modifying only the reward computation step.

#### Theoretical Justification: Why Penalizing Repeated Errors Improves Expected Return

Before diving into the implementation, the paper provides a theoretical result (Section 3.2, Theorem 1, with full proof in Appendix B) that establishes the **mathematical soundness** of the approach. The theorem answers a natural question: if we subtract penalties from the reward signal, why should the resulting policy be *better* at maximizing the original, unpenalized reward?

**Theorem statement (paraphrased):** Suppose we have two different reward signals: $\mu_1 = r(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y})$ (the original task reward) and $\mu_2 = r(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}) - \lambda c(\mathbf{y})$ (the penalized reward, where $c(\mathbf{y})$ counts the number of times the error pattern corresponding to $\mathbf{y}$ has been sampled in the past, $c(\mathbf{y})$ is monotonically increasing with recurrence, and $\lambda > 0$ is a penalty coefficient). Let $q_1$ and $q_2$ be the policies obtained by applying a single KL-regularized optimization step under $\mu_1$ and $\mu_2$ respectively. Let $\mathbf{J}(q) = \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y} \sim q}[r(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y})]$ be the expected return under the original unpenalized reward. Then:

$$\mathbf{J}(q_2) \geq \mathbf{J}(q_1)$$

That is, the policy updated under the penalized reward signal achieves **no lower expected return** under the original reward than the policy updated under the original reward signal directly.

**Key assumptions of the proof (Appendix B):** The proof relies on two critical properties:

1. **Monotonicity of $c(\mathbf{y})$:** The function $c(\mathbf{y})$ is monotonically increasing in the number of times the error pattern has been sampled. In the MEDS instantiation, $c(\mathbf{y}) = \log(|C_k| + 1)$, which is indeed monotonically increasing in $|C_k|$ although the paper's proof only requires monotonicity, not the specific logarithmic form.

2. **Negative correlation between reward and repetition:** Responses with larger repetition counts are not higher-scoring under the task reward. Formally, $c(\mathbf{y}_1) \geq c(\mathbf{y}_2) \implies r(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}_1) \leq r(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}_2)$. This is a reasonable assumption: errors that have been repeatedly sampled are, by definition, errors — they receive low (typically zero) task reward. They are certainly not higher-reward than less-frequently-sampled responses (which might include correct solutions).

**Proof sketch (for the KL-regularized one-step update):** The proof proceeds by showing that the penalized policy $q_2$ can be expressed as a reweighting of the unpenalized policy $q_1$ with weights $w(\mathbf{y}) = \exp(-\eta \lambda c(\mathbf{y}))$, where $\eta$ is the KL penalty coefficient. Since $w(\mathbf{y})$ is non-increasing in $c(\mathbf{y})$ (larger repetition count → smaller weight), and $r(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y})$ is also non-increasing in $c(\mathbf{y})$ by assumption, the covariance between $r$ and $w$ under $q_1$ is non-negative (by Chebyshev's rearrangement inequality). This covariance appears in the difference $\mathbf{J}(q_2) - \mathbf{J}(q_1)$, and its non-negativity establishes the result. In operational terms: the penalty weight $w(\mathbf{y})$ downweights responses that are already known to be low-reward, which concentrates more of $q_2$'s probability mass on responses that are *not* known to be bad — and since the remaining mass is allocated proportionally to the original reward under the Gibbs form of $q_1$, the expected reward cannot decrease.

**Why the theorem matters — not just a formalism:** The theorem provides **principled justification** for the entire MEDS framework. It shows that the penalty mechanism is not merely a heuristic that "seems to work in practice" — it has a mathematical guarantee of monotonic improvement (or at least non-deterioration) under the idealized KL-regularized update. This addresses a potential criticism: if you penalize errors, aren't you just making the reward more negative, which could destabilize training or bias the policy in unintended ways? The theorem shows that, to first order, the penalized update is at least as good as the original update in expectation, and provides conditions under which this holds.

**Limitations of the theorem:** The proof applies to a single KL-regularized update step, not to the full multi-step training trajectory. In practice, the policy is updated many times, and the error memory accumulates across steps. The theorem does not guarantee convergence or characterize the dynamics of the memory-population interaction across steps. However, it provides a strong base case: at each individual step, penalizing repeated errors in the proposed way does not harm performance under the original reward metric, and in many cases (when there is positive covariance between the penalty weight and the original reward — i.e., when the penalty is doing useful work by differentially downweighting bad responses) it strictly improves it. The empirical results confirm that this per-step guarantee translates to cumulative gains across full training runs.

**The role of $c(\mathbf{y})$ as a "memory interface":** The theorem is stated in terms of an abstract counter $c(\mathbf{y})$ that counts how many times the error pattern has been seen. The entire technical challenge of MEDS is designing a practical mechanism that instantiates $c(\mathbf{y})$ — how do we decide whether two different rollouts represent the "same" error pattern? The theorem tells us *what properties* $c(\mathbf{y})$ needs (monotonicity, correlation with reward), and the logic feature extraction + density-based clustering pipeline is the paper's concrete answer to *how to build it* without human annotation or external models.

#### Logic Feature Extraction: Constructing Lightweight Reasoning Representations from Layer-Wise Logits

This is the **core enabling technology** of MEDS. The entire framework depends on the ability to determine whether two rollouts are reasoning-similar without running an external model or doing expensive semantic analysis. The paper's insight is that the layer-wise logits at the position of the first token of the final answer capture sufficient information about the model's internal reasoning trajectory to serve as a practical proxy for reasoning similarity.

**Why the first token of the final answer?** The paper's problem formulation involves mathematical reasoning where the model is expected to produce a step-by-step chain of thought followed by a boxed final answer (e.g., `\boxed{1344}`). Let the final answer tokens be $\{y_{L-t}, \ldots, y_L\}$, where $y_{L-t}$ is the first token of the final answer (e.g., the first digit appearing after `\boxed{`). The paper denotes this token as $y^* = y_{L-t}$. The intuition is that by the time the model is about to output its final answer, it has completed all reasoning steps in its chain of thought. The internal representations leading up to $y^*$ — and the logits predicting $y^*$ itself — encode the computational path the model took to arrive at its conclusion. This includes the activations of mathematical reasoning circuits, the propagation of intermediate results through the Transformer layers, and the final "decision" about what answer to output.

Crucially, the paper argues (and provides evidence in Sections 4.4–4.6) that this encoding captures *more* than just the surface-level answer. Two responses that output the same incorrect answer (e.g., `1342`) but arrived at it through different reasoning structures (e.g., one assuming $n = p^2$ and solving $p^2 + p + 1 = 2022$, another enumerating divisors with a brute-force loop) will have different logit vectors at $y^*$, because the intermediate computations that produced the final logit were different. Conversely, two responses that output *different* incorrect answers but share the same underlying reasoning mistake (e.g., both assuming $n$ must be $p^2$ but making different arithmetic errors that lead to different final numbers) will have similar logit vectors, because the shared computational structure dominates.

**The extraction procedure — step by step:**

For a given response $\mathbf{y}$ of length $L$, let $y_{L-t}$ be the first token of the final answer. For each Transformer layer $n \in \{1, 2, \ldots, N\}$ (where $N = 28$ for Qwen3-8B and Qwen2.5-Math-7B, and presumably similar for Qwen3-1.7B), the model computes a logit vector $\mathbf{l}^{(n)}_{y_{L-t}} \in \mathbb{R}^V$ at this token position, where $V$ is the vocabulary size (typically 150K–250K tokens). Each entry $\mathbf{l}^{(n)}_{y_{L-t}}[v]$ is the unnormalized log-probability that the model assigns to vocabulary token $v$ at layer $n$, before the final softmax.

The paper selects only the **scalar logit value corresponding to the actual token $y_{L-t}$** from each layer. That is, for each layer $n$, they extract:

$$l^{*(n)} = \mathbf{l}^{(n)}_{y_{L-t}}[y_{L-t}] \in \mathbb{R}$$

This is the logit of the correct (ground-truth) next token — i.e., how strongly the model's internal representations at layer $n$ predict the token that actually gets produced. The sequence $\{l^{*(1)}, l^{*(2)}, \ldots, l^{*(N)}\}$ traces how the model's confidence in the eventual answer token evolves through the depth of the network.

**Why scalar logits rather than full logit vectors?** Extracting the full $V$-dimensional logit vector from each layer would produce a feature of dimension $N \times V$, which for $N=28$ and $V \approx 150K$ would be roughly 4.2 million dimensions — completely prohibitive for clustering and memory storage. The scalar logits provide a compact $N$-dimensional vector (or $N/2$ after the layer-selection step described below). The paper argues, and empirically validates in Section 4.4, that the scalar trajectory contains sufficient information about the reasoning process because:

1. The scalar logit for the actual token integrates information from all vocabulary dimensions into a single summary statistic that captures the model's "confidence" at that layer.
2. The *pattern of variation across layers* — which layers show high logit values and which show low values — reflects which computational circuits were activated during reasoning, since different layers specialize in different types of computation (earlier layers for semantic features, later layers for logical operations).
3. This is consistent with prior findings in mechanistic interpretability (Meng et al. [30], Templeton et al. [31]) that logit changes across layers encode the model's internal factual and reasoning state.

**Layer selection — using only the latter half:** The paper makes a deliberate design choice to use only layers from the latter half of the Transformer stack. Formally:

$$f(\mathbf{y}) = \text{concat}\left( l^{*(n)} \;\middle|\; n = \frac{N}{2}, \ldots, N \right) \in \mathbb{R}^{N/2}$$

where $\text{concat}$ denotes vector concatenation, and $N$ is the total number of layers. For Qwen3-8B and Qwen2.5-Math-7B with $N=28$, this produces a 14-dimensional feature vector.

**Why discard the first half of layers?** The justification, cited in the paper [37], is that "earlier layers typically model simpler semantic information." In Transformer models, early layers tend to encode local syntactic and semantic features (part-of-speech, named entities, surface-level lexical relationships), while later layers encode more abstract, task-specific computations (reasoning chains, mathematical operations, logical inference). The reasoning patterns that distinguish different error modes are more likely to be reflected in the later layers where the model performs high-level reasoning, while the early layers would capture more generic properties of the mathematical language (e.g., "this text contains the word 'divisor'") that are shared across many different reasoning strategies, adding noise rather than signal for error mode discrimination.

The paper validates this choice empirically in Section 4.6 (Table 2, Figure 5 right), comparing 14-layer features against 28-layer features and against difference-based variants. The 14-layer configuration achieves the highest correlation with LLM-based semantic clustering (61.2%) and the best downstream evaluation performance.

**Normalization:** After concatenation, the paper applies L2 normalization to the feature vector (explicitly mentioned in Section 4.1: "we concatenate the logits from the last 14 Transformer layers and apply L2 normalization"). This projects the 14-dimensional vector onto the unit hypersphere, ensuring that the Euclidean distances used in HDBSCAN clustering (described below) are driven by angular differences in the logit pattern rather than by differences in overall magnitude. This is important because two responses might both produce high-magnitude logits in the later layers (indicating high confidence in the final answer token) even if the *pattern* of which specific layers contribute most to that confidence is different; L2 normalization removes scale as a confounding factor while preserving directional information.

**Alternative feature constructions considered:** The paper explores several alternatives in Section 4.6:

- **Direct aggregation over all 28 layers ("MEDS-28"):** Uses logits from all layers $n = 1, \ldots, 28$, producing a 28-dimensional feature vector. Performs slightly worse than the 14-layer version on average (56.17 pass@1 average vs. 56.47), suggesting the early-layer logits add noise without providing additional discriminative power for error mode clustering.

- **Difference-based aggregation over last 14 layers ("MEDS-14-diff"):** Uses the *differences* between adjacent layer logits: $l^{*(n+1)} - l^{*(n)}$ for $n = N/2, \ldots, N-1$. This captures the *rate of change* of logit values across layers rather than the absolute values. The intuition is that the pattern of how the model's confidence evolves — where it increases sharply vs. where it plateaus — might better characterize the reasoning trajectory than the absolute logit levels. Performs competitively (55.52 pass@1 average) but slightly worse than direct aggregation in most metrics.

- **Degenerate single-cluster baseline ("MEDS-single cluster"):** All rollouts are assigned to a single cluster, so the penalty is constant for all incorrect responses. This is functionally equivalent to subtracting a constant from all incorrect rewards, which just shifts the advantage estimates by a constant and has no effect on the relative advantage ordering within the batch. Underperforms DAPO (55.14 pass@1 vs. 55.61), confirming that the clustering *is* doing useful work — a flat penalty doesn't help.

**The "almost zero computational overhead" claim:** The paper emphasizes in Section 1 that the feature extraction "reuses the layer-wise logits already produced during the forward pass" and "introduces almost no additional computational overhead." This is accurate because:

1. During autoregressive generation, the model already computes logits at every token position for every layer (for the final answer token, the logits are computed anyway as part of determining which token to output next).
2. Extracting the scalar logit value $l^{*(n)}$ from the $V$-dimensional vector is a single indexing operation per layer — essentially free.
3. The concatenation and L2 normalization operate on tiny vectors (14 dimensions), costing negligible FLOPs compared to the Transformer forward pass (which involves matrix multiplications with millions of parameters).
4. The only additional cost beyond the forward pass is the clustering step (Section 3.4), which runs on accumulated feature vectors, not on the raw text or model internals.

**The logit evolution as a proxy for reasoning structure — conceptual justification:** Figure 3 in the paper provides an illustrative example with a 3-layer model (simplified for visualization). Two responses that use the same reasoning approach (left and middle graphs) produce nearly identical 3-dimensional logit vectors at the final answer token position, despite potentially different surface-level wording. A response that uses a fundamentally different reasoning approach (right graph) produces a substantially different logit vector, with markedly different values at each layer. The claim is that this property scales to the 14-dimensional vectors used in practice — responses that share reasoning structure cluster together in logit space, while those with different reasoning structures are separated.

This is an **empirical hypothesis** that the paper validates through multiple lines of evidence: the case study in Section 4.4 (Figure 6), the LLM-based clustering correlation analysis in Section 4.5 (Figure 5 right), and the downstream performance correlation in Section 4.6. It is *not* a theoretical guarantee — the paper does not claim to prove that logit similarity implies reasoning similarity, but rather demonstrates that in practice, on mathematical reasoning tasks with these model architectures, the relationship holds strongly enough to serve as the basis for effective reward shaping.

#### Memory and Clustering: Operationalizing the Repetition Counter $c(\mathbf{y})$

With the feature extraction pipeline established, the paper now needs to operationalize the abstract repetition counter $c(\mathbf{y})$ from Theorem 1. This involves two sub-problems: (1) how to store and maintain historical representations across training steps, and (2) how to determine whether a new rollout is similar to previously-seen errors. The solution is a per-prompt memory augmented with HDBSCAN clustering.

**The error memory structure:** For each specific prompt $\mathbf{x}$ in the training set, MEDS maintains a memory set:

$$G_{\mathbf{x}} = \{ f(\mathbf{y}_1^1), f(\mathbf{y}_2^1), \ldots, f(\mathbf{y}_b^t) \}$$

where:
- $\mathbf{y}_i^j$ denotes the $i$-th rollout sampled for prompt $\mathbf{x}$ during training epoch $j$.
- $f(\mathbf{y}_i^j)$ is the 14-dimensional normalized logit feature vector for that rollout.
- $b$ is the number of rollouts generated per prompt per epoch (typically 16, as specified in Section 4.1: "we use a prompt-level batch size of 512 and generate 16 rollouts per prompt").
- $t$ is the current training epoch.
- The memory accumulates over time — as training proceeds through epochs $1, 2, \ldots, t$, all sampled rollouts are added to $G_{\mathbf{x}}$, so the memory grows linearly with training steps.

**What goes into the memory?** The paper specifies (Section 4.1) that the memory stores "the logits representations of incorrect samples." Only rollouts that received a non-positive task reward (i.e., were incorrect) are added. Correct rollouts are not included, because the goal is to identify and penalize *recurring errors* — correct rollouts don't need to be penalized. This is a crucial design choice: if correct rollouts were included in the memory and clustered alongside errors, the clustering algorithm might group a correct reasoning pattern with a similar-looking (in logit space) incorrect pattern, penalizing what should be rewarded. By excluding correct samples, the memory only tracks error patterns.

**Clustering with HDBSCAN:** At each training step, the system applies HDBSCAN (Hierarchical Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise, McInnes et al. [14]) to the accumulated memory $G_{\mathbf{x}}$ for each prompt. The clustering partitions the memory into:

$$G_{\mathbf{x}} = \{C_1, C_2, \ldots, C_K, C_{\text{noise}}\}$$

where each $C_k$ is a cluster of feature vectors that HDBSCAN identifies as forming a dense region in the representation space, $K$ is the number of clusters (determined adaptively by HDBSCAN based on the data), and $C_{\text{noise}}$ contains vectors that do not belong to any sufficiently dense cluster.

**Why HDBSCAN specifically?** The paper chose HDBSCAN over alternative clustering algorithms (k-means, DBSCAN, Gaussian mixture models) for several reasons that align with the requirements of the framework:

1. **Adaptive cluster count:** HDBSCAN determines the number of clusters $K$ automatically based on the density structure of the data, rather than requiring $K$ to be specified upfront as in k-means. This is important because the number of distinct error modes varies across prompts — some prompts might have only one dominant error pattern, others might have five or six. Fixing $K$ to a constant would either merge distinct error modes (if $K$ is too small) or split homogeneous error modes into arbitrary sub-clusters (if $K$ is too large), both of which would degrade the penalty signal.

2. **Density-based clustering:** HDBSCAN groups points based on regions of high density in the representation space, separated by regions of low density. This naturally handles the case where different error modes have different cluster sizes and shapes — a common error pattern will form a large, dense cluster, while a rare error pattern might form a small but still perceptible cluster, rather than being forced into the nearest large cluster (as k-means would do). Density-based methods also naturally handle the "noise" concept — vectors that don't belong to any dense region are assigned to $C_{\text{noise}}$, which receives a penalty based on $|C_{\text{noise}}|$, but vectors in $C_{\text{noise}}$ are isolated points, not part of a recurring pattern.

3. **Hierarchical structure:** HDBSCAN builds a hierarchy of clusters at different density levels, then selects a flat clustering by cutting the hierarchy at a point that maximizes cluster stability. This makes it robust to the choice of the minimum cluster size parameter — small clusters that are stable (i.e., persist across a wide range of density thresholds) are retained, while spurious small clusters that appear only at specific thresholds are pruned.

4. **Robustness to outliers:** The noise assignment capability means that isolated feature vectors (representing one-off errors that the model sampled once and never revisited) don't distort the cluster structure and don't create spurious penalty pressure on genuinely novel error modes.

**HDBSCAN configuration:** The paper specifies (Section 4.1) the clustering parameters: minimum cluster size of 2, minimum number of samples of 1, and Euclidean distance as the distance metric. Setting minimum cluster size to 2 means that a cluster must contain at least two vectors to be considered a valid cluster rather than noise — this ensures that $C_k$ represents a genuinely *recurring* pattern (sampled at least twice), consistent with the theoretical motivation that only repeated errors should be penalized. Setting minimum samples to 1 means that the algorithm uses a conservative core point definition, which tends to produce fewer but more robust clusters. Euclidean distance is the natural choice given that the feature vectors are L2-normalized (on the unit sphere, Euclidean distance is monotonically related to cosine distance, making it appropriate for comparing directional patterns).

**Penalty computation:** For a new rollout $\tilde{\mathbf{y}}$ with feature vector $f(\tilde{\mathbf{y}})$, the system identifies which cluster $C_k$ it belongs to (if any, using HDBSCAN's approximate_predict or a nearest-cluster assignment). The repetition counter $c(\tilde{\mathbf{y}})$ is then defined as:

$$c(\tilde{\mathbf{y}}) = \log(|C_k| + 1)$$

where $|C_k|$ is the current size of cluster $C_k$ (the number of historical rollouts assigned to that cluster). The $+1$ ensures that the term is well-defined even for $|C_k| = 0$ (though in practice $|C_k| \geq 2$ since minimum cluster size is 2, and $|C_k| \geq 1$ if the rollout itself is being added to the cluster).

This produces the penalized reward:

$$\tilde{r}(\mathbf{x}, \tilde{\mathbf{y}}) = r(\mathbf{x}, \tilde{\mathbf{y}}) - \min\left( \alpha \log(|C_k| + 1), \beta \right), \quad \text{s.t. } f(\tilde{\mathbf{y}}) \in C_k$$

Let me now break this into its components and explain each part.

#### The Penalty Formula: Design and Justification

The penalty computation has three components that deserve separate analysis: the logarithmic scaling, the $\alpha$ coefficient, and the $\beta$ cap.

**Logarithmic scaling $\log(|C_k| + 1)$:**

**What it computes:** This function grows quickly for small cluster sizes (to create immediate pressure against emerging error patterns) but saturates for large cluster sizes (to prevent any single penalty from dominating the reward signal). For $|C_k| = 2$ (the minimum valid cluster size), the penalty is $\log(3) \approx 1.10$; for $|C_k| = 10$, it is $\log(11) \approx 2.40$; for $|C_k| = 100$, it is $\log(101) \approx 4.62$; for $|C_k| = 1000$, it is $\log(1001) \approx 6.91$. The marginal increase shrinks as the cluster grows: the first doubling (from 2 to 4) adds $\log(5) - \log(3) \approx 0.51$, while the doubling from 500 to 1000 adds only $\log(1001) - \log(501) \approx 0.69$ (and even smaller as a fraction of the current penalty).

**Why logarithmic:** The logarithmic function has three desirable properties for dynamic penalty scaling:

1. **Diminishing returns:** Once an error cluster is large enough, the model has clearly identified it as a failure mode — additional samples don't provide much new information about its dead-end status. The penalty saturates to reflect this: after a certain point, the penalty is essentially "this is a well-known error pattern, avoid it," and further samples don't increase the avoidance pressure much further.

2. **Stability:** Without the logarithmic compression, a naive linear penalty $|C_k|$ would grow without bound for large clusters. If a particular error pattern is numerically dominant (e.g., 500 out of 1000 historical rollouts), a linear penalty of 500 would completely overwhelm the binary task reward (typically 0 or 1), pushing the penalized reward far into negative territory and potentially destabilizing the advantage normalization in GRPO/DAPO (which standardizes advantages within a batch using mean and standard deviation — a single very large negative advantage would compress all other advantages toward zero after standardization).

3. **Theoretical alignment:** The proof in Appendix B only requires that $c(\mathbf{y})$ is monotonically increasing in $|C_k|$, which logarithmic scaling satisfies. The specific choice of logarithm (as opposed to, say, square root) is an empirical one, but it is a natural choice given the considerations above.

**The $\alpha$ coefficient:**

**What it is:** $\alpha$ is a scaling hyperparameter that controls the overall strength of the penalty. It multiplies the logarithmic cluster size term before capping. The paper uses different $\alpha$ values for different model scales:
- Qwen3-1.7B and Qwen2.5-Math-7B: $\alpha = 0.1$
- Qwen3-8B: $\alpha = 0.02$

**Why different values for different models?** The paper does not provide an explicit justification for this choice, but the pattern is suggestive: the larger model (Qwen3-8B) gets a substantially smaller $\alpha$, meaning its error penalties are weaker. One possible explanation is that larger models have more robust internal representations — their logit patterns are more stable and discriminative, so the clustering is more reliable. With more reliable clustering, even a small penalty on a reliably-identified error cluster is sufficient to shift behavior. Alternatively, larger models may be more sensitive to reward perturbation — the same penalty magnitude might cause more dramatic policy shifts in a model with more parameters and a sharper probability landscape. The hyperparameter sweep that led to these choices is not described in the paper, which is a minor transparency limitation.

**Interaction with the reward scale:** In the RLVR setting with binary rewards ($r \in \{0, 1\}$ for mathematical reasoning tasks), the maximum penalty is $\beta$ (typically 0.2 or 0.04), which is small relative to the reward range. This means the penalty acts as a *tie-breaking* signal rather than a dominant force: when the model is choosing between two incorrect reasoning paths, the one that has been tried more often gets a more negative penalized reward, encouraging the optimizer to explore the less-traveled path instead. The penalty is not large enough to make an incorrect answer look worse than a correct one (since a correct answer gets $r = 1$, and even with $\beta = 0.2$, no penalty can bring an incorrect answer's reward above 1), so it only affects the relative ordering among *incorrect* trajectories.

**The $\beta$ cap:**

**What it is:** $\beta$ is an upper bound on the penalty, preventing it from exceeding a fixed threshold regardless of how large the cluster becomes. The paper uses:
- Qwen3-1.7B and Qwen2.5-Math-7B: $\beta = 0.2$
- Qwen3-8B: $\beta = 0.04$

**Why cap the penalty?** Without the cap, the penalty could grow arbitrarily large as the cluster accumulates more and more samples over many training epochs. This would eventually create an arbitrarily strong gradient signal pushing *against* any rollout in that cluster, which could:
1. Destabilize training by introducing extremely large-magnitude advantage signals that dominate the batch normalization.
2. Permanently lock the model out of a region of behavior space, even if that region contains useful partial information (e.g., an error pattern that is "close" to the correct solution in reasoning space, and whose exploration might eventually lead to a correct variant).
3. Create a non-stationary reward signal whose scale changes dramatically over training, making it hard to set consistent learning rates and other hyperparameters.

The cap ensures that the penalty operates in a bounded range, making the reward shaping more predictable and stable. The specific values ($0.2$ and $0.04$) are small relative to the reward range ($0$ to $1$), reinforcing that the penalty provides a nudge rather than a dominating signal.

**What happens to noise points?** The paper does not explicitly discuss how rollouts assigned to $C_{\text{noise}}$ are handled in the penalty computation. By the stated formula, the penalty applies only "s.t. $f(\tilde{\mathbf{y}}) \in C_k$" — i.e., only when the rollout is assigned to a valid cluster. This implies that noise points (rollouts whose feature vectors don't belong to any cluster of size $\geq 2$) receive no additional penalty beyond their original task reward. This makes sense: a noise point represents a novel error pattern that hasn't been seen before (or has been seen only once), so there's no historical evidence that it's a dead-end basin — it might be a promising new direction that just hasn't been explored enough. Penalizing it would be premature and would discourage genuine exploration.

**The interplay between memory growth and clustering cost:** The memory $G_{\mathbf{x}}$ grows linearly with training steps (16 new rollouts per prompt per epoch, across potentially hundreds of epochs). Running HDBSCAN on a growing dataset repeatedly across training steps introduces computational overhead. The paper reports (Section 4.2) that MEDS takes 8.46 minutes for 50 training steps and 9.73 minutes for 100 steps on Qwen2.5-Math-7B, compared to 8.00 and 8.95 minutes for DAPO. This is a 5–8% overhead, which is modest given the performance improvements. The paper does not discuss how HDBSCAN's runtime scales with memory size or whether they employ any strategies to limit memory growth (e.g., pruning old rollouts, capping memory size, or reclustering only every $k$ steps). This is a practical detail that would matter for very long training runs.

#### How the Penalized Reward Feeds into the RL Update

The penalized reward $\tilde{r}(\mathbf{x}, \tilde{\mathbf{y}})$ replaces the original reward $r(\mathbf{x}, \tilde{\mathbf{y}})$ in whatever RL algorithm is being used. The paper builds on DAPO (Section 4.1: "Building on DAPO, our work further introduces a clustering penalty term"), so the base algorithm uses DAPO's objective function (defined in Appendix A.2, equation 2):

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{DAPO}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{q \sim \mathcal{D}} \left[ \frac{1}{K} \sum_{i=1}^{K} \min \left( r_i(\theta) A_i, \text{clip}(r_i(\theta), 1 - \varepsilon_{\text{low}}, 1 + \varepsilon_{\text{high}}) A_i \right) \right]$$

where $r_i(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(o_i|q)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(o_i|q)}$ is the probability ratio, $A_i = \frac{R_i - \text{mean}(\{R_j\}_{j=1}^K)}{\text{std}(\{R_j\}_{j=1}^K)}$ is the group-relative advantage computed from the penalized rewards $R_i = \tilde{r}(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}_i)$, $\varepsilon_{\text{low}} = 0.2$ and $\varepsilon_{\text{high}} = 0.28$ are the asymmetric clipping thresholds, and $K = 16$ is the number of rollouts per prompt.

**The critical path — from clustering to gradient:**

1. For each prompt $\mathbf{x}$, generate $K = 16$ rollouts $\mathbf{y}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{y}_{16}$ from the current policy.
2. Compute the task reward $r(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}_i)$ for each (typically 0 for incorrect, 1 for correct).
3. Extract the 14-dimensional logit feature vector $f(\mathbf{y}_i)$ for each rollout.
4. Run HDBSCAN on the union of the existing memory $G_{\mathbf{x}}$ and the new feature vectors $\{f(\mathbf{y}_i)\}$ (or compute cluster assignments for the new points against existing clusters — the paper is not explicit about the exact procedure, but standard HDBSCAN usage would recluster the full set or use approximate_predict).
5. For each rollout, if $f(\mathbf{y}_i)$ is assigned to cluster $C_k$, compute the penalized reward $\tilde{r}(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}_i) = r(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}_i) - \min(\alpha \log(|C_k| + 1), \beta)$; if assigned to $C_{\text{noise}}$, $\tilde{r}(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}_i) = r(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}_i)$ (no penalty).
6. Add all incorrect rollouts' feature vectors to $G_{\mathbf{x}}$ for future clustering.
7. Compute advantages $A_i$ using the penalized rewards $\tilde{r}$, standardized within the group of 16.
8. Update the policy parameters $\theta$ by minimizing the DAPO objective.

**Why the penalty operates through the advantage rather than the reward directly:** In GRPO/DAPO, the absolute reward magnitude matters only through its effect on the group-relative advantage $A_i$, which is the standardized deviation of each rollout's reward from the group mean. This standardization means that adding a constant penalty to *all* incorrect rollouts in a batch would have zero effect — all incorrect rewards would shift by the same amount, their deviation from the group mean would be unchanged, and the standardized advantages would be identical. The key to MEDS's effectiveness is that the penalty is *differential* — rollouts assigned to larger clusters (more frequently encountered error patterns) receive larger penalties than rollouts assigned to smaller clusters or noise. This changes the relative ordering of advantages within the batch, making rollouts with rare error patterns look relatively better (higher advantage) than rollouts with common error patterns, even though both are incorrect.

**A concrete example to illustrate the mechanism:**

Suppose for a given prompt, the 16 rollouts in a batch include:
- 2 correct rollouts: $r = 1$
- 5 rollouts in a large error cluster ($|C_k| = 50$): $r = 0$, penalty $= \min(0.1 \cdot \log(51), 0.2) = 0.2$ (capped), so $\tilde{r} = -0.2$
- 5 rollouts in a medium error cluster ($|C_k| = 10$): $r = 0$, penalty $= 0.1 \cdot \log(11) \approx 0.24$ (capped at 0.2), so $\tilde{r} = -0.2$
- 3 rollouts in a small error cluster ($|C_k| = 3$): $r = 0$, penalty $= 0.1 \cdot \log(4) \approx 0.139$, so $\tilde{r} = -0.139$
- 1 noise rollout (novel error): $r = 0$, no penalty, so $\tilde{r} = 0$

Without MEDS, all 14 incorrect rollouts would have $r = 0$, and the group mean would be $2/16 = 0.125$, giving all incorrect rollouts an advantage of $0 - 0.125 = -0.125$ (standardized). With MEDS, the penalized rewards create a spectrum: noise rollouts (0) look better than small-cluster rollouts (-0.139), which look better than large-cluster rollouts (-0.2). After standardization, the noise rollout receives a higher advantage than the large-cluster rollouts, even though all are incorrect. The policy update increases the probability of the noise rollout relative to the large-cluster rollouts, even though neither is correct — the gradient pushes the policy to explore the novel error pattern rather than re-sampling the known failure mode.

**The first epoch exception:** The paper notes (Section 4.3) that "the first training epoch is mainly used for memory formation and does not involve reward shaping." This is a critical implementation detail: during epoch 1, the memory is empty for all prompts, so HDBSCAN would find no clusters, and all rollouts would receive no penalty (or a constant penalty if assigned to noise). The first epoch populates the memory with initial error representations, which then enable differential penalties starting from epoch 2. This avoids the cold-start problem where the system has no historical information and would either penalize arbitrarily (if forced to cluster an empty memory) or apply uniform penalties (if treating everything as noise).

#### Summary of Design Choices and Their Justifications

- **Logit-based features over text-based or model-based similarity:** Text-based similarity (e.g., embedding similarity using a separate model) would require additional inference passes and storage of high-dimensional embeddings. Model-based similarity (e.g., using an LLM to compare reasoning patterns) would be computationally expensive and require careful prompt engineering. Logit-based features reuse existing computation, are compact (14-dimensional), and correlate well with semantic similarity as demonstrated in Section 4.5.

- **Scalar logits at the final answer token over full vocabulary logits or attention patterns:** Full vocabulary logits would be prohibitively high-dimensional ($V \approx 150K$ per layer). Attention patterns would require storing $(L, N, H)$-dimensional tensors per rollout (sequence length × layers × attention heads), which is even larger. Scalar logits at a single token position provide a compact summary that captures the relevant computational signal. The choice of the final answer token specifically reflects the hypothesis that the reasoning "crystallizes" at the point where the model commits to its conclusion.

- **Latter-half layers only:** Early layers encode generic semantic features that don't discriminate between reasoning strategies; later layers encode task-specific computations that do. Empirically validated by the ablation showing 14-layer features outperform 28-layer features.

- **HDBSCAN over k-means or DBSCAN:** Automatic cluster count determination avoids arbitrary choices per prompt. Density-based clustering handles variable cluster shapes and sizes. Noise assignment prevents isolated errors from forming spurious clusters. The hierarchical stability selection makes the clustering robust to parameter choices.

- **Logarithmic scaling with cap:** Diminishing returns prevent arbitrarily large penalties for dominant error modes. The cap ensures stability and bounded reward perturbation. Together, they create a penalty that is strongest for emerging error patterns (creating pressure to escape early) and saturates for entrenched patterns (preventing destabilization).

- **Per-prompt separate memory rather than global memory:** Error patterns are prompt-specific — "assuming $n = p^2$ when solving for three divisors" is only an error pattern for that specific problem statement. Pooling across prompts would conflate different reasoning patterns that happen to share similar logit structures, producing meaningless clusters and counterproductive penalties. The memory-per-prompt design ensures that penalties are semantically meaningful.

- **$\alpha$ decoupled from $\beta$:** Having separate scaling and capping parameters allows the practitioner to control the penalty's growth rate (via $\alpha$) independently of its maximum value (via $\beta$). The paper's choices ($\alpha=0.1, \beta=0.2$ for smaller models; $\alpha=0.02, \beta=0.04$ for Qwen3-8B) suggest that both scale and cap need to be tuned, and that the optimal values are model-dependent.

- **L2 normalization before clustering:** Removes magnitude as a confounding factor, ensuring that Euclidean distance captures angular (pattern) similarity rather than scale similarity. This is standard practice for comparing high-dimensional vectors where the direction carries the meaningful information and the magnitude is dominated by factors like sequence length or model confidence.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Error Collapse Is a Cross-Rollout Phenomenon, Not a Token-Level One — and That Demands Cross-Rollout Memory

Prior work on diversity in LLM reinforcement learning has treated the problem through two lenses, both operating at the wrong granularity. **Token-level entropy regularization** (Haarnoja et al. [10]; Cheng et al. [41], used as the "GRPO w/ Entropy Adv" baseline in this paper) encourages the policy to spread probability mass across multiple vocabulary items at each generation step. This addresses local stochasticity — whether the model says "therefore" versus "thus" — but is blind to whether the underlying reasoning *strategy* is repeating. The policy can maintain high per-token entropy while still revisiting the same conceptual error pattern across rollouts, just with different surface wordings. **Batch-level uniqueness rewards** (Hu et al. [23]; Wan et al. [11]) go further by identifying rare reasoning strategies within a single batch and amplifying their advantage signals. This properly targets reasoning-level diversity, but it has no memory across batches — an error pattern that appeared in 50% of rollouts in batch 10 and 40% in batch 25 is treated as two independent occurrences rather than as cumulative evidence that this is a persistent failure mode.

The MEDS framework's fundamental reconceptualization is to identify error collapse as a **cross-rollout, across-training-time phenomenon**, not a within-batch or within-sequence property. Figure 1(b) makes this concrete: three different responses — misreading the problem setup, incorrectly parsing the setup, making a setup understanding error — all trace back to the shared root cause of "faulty setup reading." Entropy regularization doesn't see this shared cause because the token sequences differ. Batch-level uniqueness rewards don't see it because these three responses appear in different batches across training steps. MEDS sees it because the memory persists across steps and the logit-based clustering groups these surface-different but structurally similar responses into the same error cluster.

This is more than a new method — it is a **diagnostic reframing** of why mode collapse happens in LLM reasoning RL. The stable error basin in Figure 1(a) is not just a region of high probability mass; it is a region that *stays* high-probability across many training updates because the gradient signal from flat rewards provides no directional push to leave it. Entropy regularization tries to flatten the policy distribution everywhere, but the basin is a local minimum — flattening the distribution within the basin just makes the policy explore different phrasings of the same mistake, not escape the basin entirely. What MEDS recognizes is that escaping requires **directional pressure away from already-explored regions**, which can only come from a memory that tracks which regions have been explored. This is a conceptual shift from "maintain diversity everywhere" (entropy) to "avoid re-exploring known dead ends" (memory-based penalty).

The evidence in Figure 5 (left) supports this reframing quantitatively: the Top-1 Eigen Ratio, which measures how concentrated the logit representations are along a single dominant direction, is consistently lower for MEDS than for DAPO throughout training. This means MEDS prevents the representations from collapsing into a single error mode, even as the model continues to sample incorrect rollouts. The LLM-based diversity evaluations (Figure 1(c)) confirm that this representation-level diversity translates to behavioral diversity: across both Within-Step and Across-Step diversity metrics, MEDS rollouts are judged as more varied in their reasoning approaches. The innovation isn't that MEDS produces higher accuracy (though it does); it's that it **diagnoses and treats a specific failure mode — the stable error basin — that prior methods were not designed to address**.

### Innovation 2: Layer-Wise Logits at a Single Token Position Provide a Lightweight, Semantically Meaningful Proxy for Reasoning Similarity — and This Is an Empirical Discovery, Not an Obvious Design Choice

The idea that logits — raw, unnormalized scores over the vocabulary — could capture reasoning structure is far from obvious. Logits are the model's pre-softmax output; they represent the model's "preference" for each possible next token. One might reasonably expect that logit vectors encode primarily lexical and surface-level information: whether the model thinks "1342" or "1344" is the right answer, not *why* it thinks so. The standard approach to representing reasoning similarity in prior work has been either (a) to compare final answers directly (two responses with the same wrong answer are treated as similar, regardless of how they got there) or (b) to use an external model to analyze and compare reasoning structures (computationally expensive and dependent on the external model's quality).

MEDS makes a bolder claim: the **scalar logit values at the position of the first token of the final answer, aggregated across the latter half of the Transformer layers, form a representation space where Euclidean proximity corresponds to reasoning similarity**. This claim is not theoretically guaranteed — it is an empirical hypothesis that the paper validates through multiple converging lines of evidence.

**The qualitative validation** (Figure 6, Appendix C) is instructive. The case study examines responses to a number theory problem where the ground truth is 1344. Cluster A contains two responses (A1 and A2) that both assume the number must be of the form $p^2$ for a prime $p$, solve the quadratic $p^2 + p + 1 = 2022$, find no integer solution, and fall back to brute-force enumeration that yields incorrect answers (1342 and 1351). Cluster B contains three responses (B1, B2, B3) that also produce incorrect final answers (1342, 1342, 1349) but use *different* reasoning strategies: B1 assumes $p^2$ form but tries different primes; B2 considers $p_1 \cdot p_2$ forms with brute-force enumeration; B3 sets up an equation $d + n/d = 2021$ and iterates over divisor pairs. The crucial observation: A1 and B1 both output "1342" but are assigned to *different* clusters because their logit vectors differ — the shared final answer does not imply shared reasoning. Conversely, A1 and A2 output *different* final answers but are assigned to the *same* cluster because they follow the same logical path with different arithmetic outcomes. The logit-based clustering successfully distinguishes reasoning patterns that answer-matching would conflate, and groups reasoning patterns that answer-matching would separate.

**The quantitative validation** (Section 4.5, Figure 5 right) is even more compelling. Using Claude-Haiku-4.5 as a proxy annotator to classify 800 incorrect responses into 11 manually-defined error categories, the paper measures the agreement between logit-based clustering and LLM-based semantic clustering. The 14-layer logit aggregation achieves 61.2% agreement with Claude's annotations — far above chance (since there are 11 categories, random agreement would be ~9%), and substantially higher than a degenerate single-cluster baseline (45.16%). This is not near-perfect agreement, and the paper does not claim it is — but 61.2% correlation with a sophisticated external annotator, achieved using only 14 scalar values extracted from the model's own forward pass, is a striking demonstration that the logits carry semantically meaningful information about reasoning structure.

**The downstream validation** (Section 4.6, Table 2, Figure 5 right) closes the loop. The ranking of different feature construction strategies by their correlation with Claude annotations closely mirrors their ranking by downstream evaluation performance. The 14-layer configuration achieves both the highest Claude correlation (61.2%) and the best average pass@1 (56.47). The degenerate single-cluster configuration achieves the lowest Claude correlation (45.16%) and the worst pass@1 (55.14, even below DAPO's 55.61). This correlation between clustering quality and downstream performance is not a foregone conclusion — it's possible for a representation to correlate well with human semantic judgments but be useless for reward shaping (if it captures dimensions of variation that are irrelevant to learning). The fact that better semantic clustering predicts better RL performance suggests that the logit representation is capturing *causally relevant* structure in the model's reasoning process, not just epiphenomenal patterns.

This innovation is **fundamental rather than incremental** because it opens up a new class of representations for reasoning-aware training. Prior to MEDS, if you wanted to compare reasoning trajectories, your options were expensive (external models), coarse (answer matching), or domain-specific (parse trees for math, control flow graphs for code). The logit-based approach is general (it applies to any autoregressive Transformer), cheap (reuses existing computation), and surprisingly information-rich. It is an empirical discovery that this works, not an application of an existing technique to a new domain — and it is likely to enable follow-up work that uses logit representations for other purposes (curriculum design, targeted data augmentation, anomaly detection in reasoning).

### Innovation 3: Density-Based Dynamic Penalties Unify Exploration and Error Avoidance into a Single, Principled Reward Shaping Mechanism

The dominant approaches to exploration in deep RL fall into two categories that MEDS bridges. **Intrinsic motivation methods** (count-based exploration, curiosity-driven exploration, random network distillation) add bonuses to the reward for visiting novel or surprising states, encouraging the agent to seek out unfamiliar territory. **Error avoidance methods** in the LLM reasoning context are typically implemented through negative examples — the model is trained not to produce specific undesirable outputs, usually by including them in preference pairs for DPO-style training or by penalizing them through the PPO loss. These two approaches operate independently: intrinsic motivation pushes the policy *toward* novelty, while error avoidance pushes the policy *away from* known mistakes. They are implemented through separate mechanisms and optimized separately.

MEDS unifies these into a single reward shaping mechanism by making the penalty for a given rollout proportional to the *density of historical rollouts in its neighborhood of representation space*. When a rollout falls into a dense cluster (many historical rollouts nearby in logit space), it receives a larger penalty — this simultaneously serves as error avoidance (the rollout is similar to many past errors) and exploration encouragement (the policy is incentivized to sample from less-dense regions, which are by definition more novel). The penalized reward $\tilde{r} = r - \min(\alpha \log(|C_k| + 1), \beta)$ encodes both signals in a single scalar.

This is **conceptually distinct from prior approaches** in several ways:

**It avoids the exploration-exploitation dichotomy.** In standard formulations, exploration and exploitation are separate objectives that must be balanced (e.g., via an $\epsilon$-greedy schedule or a UCB bonus that trades off estimated value against uncertainty). MEDS has no explicit exploration bonus — it *penalizes* certain behaviors, and the optimizer, by maximizing the penalized expected return, is naturally driven to avoid those behaviors and allocate probability mass elsewhere. The "exploration" is an emergent consequence of error avoidance, not a separately optimized goal. This is a more parsimonious design: there is no exploration hyperparameter to schedule, no separate exploration policy, no trade-off coefficient between task reward and exploration bonus.

**The penalty is based on density, not on count or recency.** A UCB-style bonus (Song et al. [24]) would penalize based on how many times a specific outcome has occurred. But "outcome" (correct/incorrect) is binary and coarse — two different error patterns with the same outcome are indistinguishable. A recency-based heuristic would penalize only recently-seen errors, forgetting about error patterns that dominated early training but have since been abandoned. MEDS's density-based approach adaptively identifies modes in the *distribution* of historical rollouts, not just their frequency or recency. This means an error pattern that was frequently sampled early in training but hasn't appeared recently will still form a dense cluster — and if the policy starts to drift back toward it, the penalty will reactivate even though the errors aren't recent. The memory is persistent and structural, not just temporal.

**The penalty scaling is logarithmic with a cap — and this is theoretically motivated, not arbitrary.** The proof in Appendix B shows that any monotonically increasing penalty function preserves the expected return improvement under the KL-regularized update, provided the penalty correlates appropriately with the task reward. The logarithmic function is one choice among many that satisfy monotonicity. But the paper's specific choice — logarithmic growth that saturates, combined with a hard cap — reflects careful engineering considerations that are justified in the text: diminishing returns prevent dominant error modes from receiving arbitrarily large penalties that would destabilize training, and the cap ensures that the penalty operates in a bounded range that doesn't overwhelm the binary task reward. These are *practical* constraints that make the theoretical guarantee usable in real training runs, and the paper's honest documentation of the different $\alpha$ and $\beta$ values for different model scales ($\alpha = 0.1, \beta = 0.2$ for smaller models vs. $\alpha = 0.02, \beta = 0.04$ for Qwen3-8B) suggests that these are tuned hyperparameters, not universal constants — an important practical nuance.

**The clustering is density-based because the error landscape is not known in advance.** If the number and shape of error modes were known (e.g., from a taxonomy of mathematical error types), one could define fixed penalty categories. But they aren't — they emerge from the interaction between the model architecture, the training data, and the optimization dynamics. HDBSCAN adaptively discovers the error mode structure, adapting penalty granularity to each prompt's specific error landscape. A prompt with only one dominant error pattern will have one large cluster (and potentially noise points), receiving a strong penalty for that single pattern and mild or no penalties for others. A prompt with five distinct error patterns will form five clusters, each receiving its own penalty proportional to its size. The penalty system's resolution adapts to the data, which is essential because error mode diversity is prompt-specific and evolves over training.

This innovation is **incremental in its components** (density-based clustering, logarithmic penalty scaling, memory augmentation are all individually known techniques) but **fundamental in their synthesis** into a unified reward shaping mechanism that simultaneously addresses error avoidance, exploration, and training stability — and does so with theoretical backing rather than pure heuristics. The key conceptual move is recognizing that *penalizing density in behavior space is mathematically equivalent to rewarding novelty*, and that this equivalence allows a single mechanism to serve both purposes without the usual exploration-exploitation trade-off hyperparameters.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

**Dataset.** The paper evaluates on five mathematical reasoning benchmarks drawn from the standard LLM reasoning evaluation suite: **AIME24** and **AMC23** (competition math problems from NuminaMath [42]), **MATH500** (a subset of the MATH dataset [38]), **Minerva** (quantitative reasoning [43]), and **OlympiadBench** (Olympiad-level bilingual multimodal scientific problems [44]). The training corpus is constructed by merging the DAPO-Math-17K dataset [6] with levels 3–5 of the MATH subdataset [38, 39]. This choice of training data — specifically using the DAPO training set as the base — enables direct comparison with the DAPO baseline under identical data conditions. The test split for each benchmark is the standard evaluation set; for AIME24 and AMC23 this consists of competition problems, while MATH500, Minerva, and OlympiadBench use their released test partitions.

**Base model(s).** The paper trains on three base models spanning different scales and reasoning capabilities: **Qwen3-1.7B** (smallest model, serves as the lower-bound scale; pass@1 on MATH500 of 80.86 at base without RL training), **Qwen2.5-Math-7B** (math-specialized model without explicit reasoning, pass@1 of 30.84 on MATH500 at base), and **Qwen3-8B** (largest general-purpose model, pass@1 of 83.62 on MATH500 at base). The authors deliberately choose these three to span two axes of variation: Qwen3-8B vs. Qwen2.5-Math-7B compares general-purpose vs. math-specialized pretraining at similar parameter counts, while Qwen3-1.7B vs. Qwen3-8B tests scaling behavior of the same architecture at different sizes. This is a thoughtful model selection — it probes whether MEDS's benefits depend on model scale, pretraining domain, or base reasoning capability, though the paper does not always isolate these factors in the analysis.

**Metrics.** The primary evaluation metric is **pass@k**, where k ∈ {1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128}. The paper reports both pass@1 (a single greedy or temperature-1 sample's accuracy) and pass@128 (the probability that at least one correct answer appears among 128 samples, computed via the unbiased estimator using 128 total samples and a subset size n = 128). For each evaluation, the temperature is set to 1.0, top-p to 1.0, the random seed is fixed to 0, and the maximum number of tokens is kept consistent with the training configuration (7168 for Qwen3-1.7B and Qwen3-8B, 3072 for Qwen2.5-Math-7B). The pass@k metric captures both single-sample quality (pass@1) and the breadth of correct solutions in the model's output distribution (pass@128), which is particularly relevant for MEDS since the framework's goal is to increase exploration diversity.

**Baselines.** The paper compares against five baselines, all implemented in the verl framework [40] with identical training data and infrastructure:

- **Base model**: The pretrained model without any RL fine-tuning, evaluated using the Qwen-Math template at temperature 1.0.
- **GRPO**: Group Relative Policy Optimization following the DeepSeekMath configuration [1], with clipping ratio ε = 0.2, KL penalty coefficient β = 10⁻³, and the standard GRPO objective (Appendix A.2, Equation 1).
- **DAPO**: The DAPO algorithm [6] with decoupled clipping (ε_low = 0.2, ε_high = 0.28) and no KL penalty (Appendix A.2, Equation 2). DAPO is the primary baseline because MEDS is built on top of it.
- **GRPO w/ Entropy Adv**: GRPO augmented with per-token entropy bonuses following Cheng et al. [41] (Appendix A.2, Equation 3), where the group-relative advantage Aᵢ is replaced by Ãᵢ = Aᵢ + α · ℋₜ, with ℋₜ being the per-token entropy. This baseline tests whether token-level entropy regularization — the most direct prior approach for encouraging diversity — achieves similar benefits to MEDS's cross-rollout memory.
- **MEDS**: The proposed method, built on DAPO with the memory-enhanced dynamic reward shaping penalty added (Section 3.4).

**Generation budget / compute accounting.** All methods use identical training configurations: prompt-level batch size of 512, 16 rollouts generated per prompt, maximum prompt length of 1024 tokens, and the specified maximum response lengths. The total number of rollouts generated per training step is therefore 512 × 16 = 8,192. This is the standard DAPO configuration, and the paper reports training time comparisons rather than theoretical FLOP counts: on Qwen2.5-Math-7B, MEDS takes 8.46 minutes for 50 steps and 9.73 minutes for 100 steps, compared to DAPO's 8.00 and 8.95 minutes respectively (Section 4.2). This corresponds to a 5–8% overhead over DAPO, which the paper attributes to HDBSCAN clustering costs. No additional inference budget is allocated for MEDS beyond what DAPO already uses — the feature extraction reuses logits from the forward pass, and the clustering operates on the stored feature vectors.

**Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not employ formal cross-validation for hyperparameter selection or model evaluation. The training data is a fixed merge of DAPO-Math-17K and MATH levels 3–5, and test evaluation is conducted on the standard held-out test sets for each benchmark. The random seed is fixed to 0 for all evaluations, which eliminates seed variance as a confound but also means the reported results are point estimates without error bars or confidence intervals. For the diversity analysis (Section 4.3), the paper samples 10 groups of 160 rollouts from each method at each training step, resulting in 1,600 rollout groups total — this is a reasonable sample size for the Within-Step diversity measurement, though the Across-Step analysis uses only 21 pairs of data groups. The LLM-based clustering correlation analysis (Section 4.5) uses 800 randomly sampled incorrect responses from the entire dataset, with 11 manually-defined error categories, and reports the agreement rate between logit-based and LLM-based clustering.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Overall Performance Across Three Model Scales and Five Benchmarks

Table 1 reports the headline pass@1 and pass@128 numbers across all configurations. MEDS achieves the **best average performance** across all three models, with particularly large gains on the larger Qwen3-8B model. Key numbers:

**On Qwen3-1.7B, MEDS achieves 52.49 average pass@1**, compared to DAPO's 46.60 (+5.89 points) and GRPO w/ Entropy Adv's 44.08 (+8.41 points). The largest single-benchmark gain is on OlympiadBench, where MEDS reaches 46.86 pass@1 vs. DAPO's 42.20 (+4.66 points). For pass@128, MEDS averages 81.76 vs. DAPO's 80.29 (+1.47 points), with the largest gain on AIME24 (70.00 vs. 63.33, +6.67 points).

**On Qwen2.5-Math-7B, MEDS achieves 56.47 average pass@1**, compared to DAPO's 55.61 (+0.86 points) and GRPO's 51.93 (+4.54 points). The smaller gap between MEDS and DAPO on this model is notable and will be discussed in the difficulty analysis below. For pass@128, MEDS averages 84.00 vs. DAPO's 82.85 (+1.15 points).

**On Qwen3-8B, MEDS achieves 66.72 average pass@1**, compared to DAPO's 63.11 (+3.61 points) and GRPO's 50.38 (+16.34 points). This is where the benefits are most dramatic. The standout result is on OlympiadBench, where MEDS reaches 61.12 pass@1 vs. DAPO's 52.77 (+8.35 points) and **82.67 pass@128 vs. DAPO's 70.81 (+11.86 points)** — this is the largest single-benchmark improvement, representing a 17% relative gain on pass@128 as noted in the abstract. On AIME24, MEDS achieves 76.67 pass@128 vs. DAPO's 73.33 (+3.34 points).

**Why the benefits scale with model size?** The paper observes (Section 4.2) that "this advantage is observed on both Qwen3-1.7B and Qwen2.5-Math-7B, and becomes even more pronounced on Qwen3-8B model, suggesting that the benefits of our method scales with the capability of the base model." This pattern is consistent with the mechanism: larger models have more internal representational capacity, so their logit vectors may more reliably capture reasoning structure, making the clustering-based penalty more precisely targeted. Additionally, larger models may be more prone to error collapse (having more capacity to explore, they may find and get stuck in larger error basins), so the memory-based penalty provides a larger benefit.

#### Pass@k Scaling Curves

Figure 4 plots pass@k for k ∈ {1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128} across all 15 (model × dataset) combinations. MEDS (blue line) consistently matches or outperforms all baselines, and the gap often **widens at larger k values**. For example:

- On Qwen3-1.7B on AIME24, DAPO reaches approximately 63 pass@128 while MEDS reaches approximately 70 — a 7-point gap that is larger than the gap at pass@1 (~23 vs. ~13).
- On Qwen3-8B on OlympiadBench, the DAPO pass@k curve plateaus around k = 64 at roughly 71, while MEDS continues to climb to approximately 83 at k = 128. This shows that MEDS is not just shifting the curve upward — it is improving the **shape** of the scaling curve, particularly in the high-k regime where exploration diversity matters most for pass@k coverage.
- On Qwen2.5-Math-7B on AMC23, DAPO already reaches near-perfect pass@128 (97.50), so MEDS cannot improve further, but MEDS matches this ceiling.

The systematic improvement at higher k values is evidence that MEDS's primary mechanism — penalizing repeated error patterns — is successfully broadening the model's exploration, so that when 128 independent samples are drawn, a larger fraction of the error space has been explored and the chance of at least one sample landing on a correct solution is higher.

#### Comparison Against Entropy Regularization: Token-Level Randomness Is Not Enough

A key result that validates the paper's central thesis is the consistent **underperformance of GRPO w/ Entropy Adv** compared to both DAPO and MEDS. Across all three models:

- On Qwen3-1.7B: GRPO w/ Entropy Adv averages 44.08 pass@1 (vs. DAPO's 46.60 and MEDS's 52.49), and **73.77 pass@128** (vs. DAPO's 80.29 and MEDS's 81.76).
- On Qwen2.5-Math-7B: GRPO w/ Entropy Adv averages 53.63 pass@1 (vs. DAPO's 55.61 and MEDS's 56.47), and 80.32 pass@128 (vs. DAPO's 82.85 and MEDS's 84.00).
- On Qwen3-8B: GRPO w/ Entropy Adv averages 54.79 pass@1 (vs. DAPO's 63.11 and MEDS's 66.72), and 77.59 pass@128 (vs. DAPO's 80.24 and MEDS's 84.61).

On the largest model, entropy regularization actually **degrades** pass@1 by nearly 9 points relative to DAPO (54.79 vs. 63.11). This is a striking result: the canonical method for encouraging diversity in RL actively hurts performance on mathematical reasoning tasks when applied at the token level. The paper's interpretation (Section 1) is that "such distribution-level stochastic exploration is often insufficient due to the vast action space" — the entropy bonus injects noise into token choices without steering the policy away from systematic reasoning errors. MEDS, by contrast, provides targeted pressure against *specific repeated reasoning patterns*, which is a more structured form of exploration that complements (rather than fights against) the task reward.

This comparison is the paper's strongest piece of evidence for its central claim that "error collapse is a cross-rollout phenomenon, not a token-level one, and that demands cross-rollout memory" (as discussed in the Key Insights section).

#### The Base Model Comparison: RL Fine-Tuning vs. Pretrained Baselines

Comparing against the base model (no RL fine-tuning) reveals when RL provides benefits and when it backfires:

- On Qwen3-1.7B, the base model actually outperforms GRPO on pass@1 for several benchmarks (e.g., AIME24: base 22.58 vs. GRPO 18.20; AMC23: base 58.57 vs. GRPO 51.58). DAPO also underperforms the base on AIME24 pass@1 (13.46 vs. 22.58). MEDS (23.65) is the only method that consistently outperforms the base on pass@1, and even then only marginally on AIME24.
- On Qwen2.5-Math-7B, RL consistently improves over the base, with DAPO and MEDS showing large gains (e.g., AIME24 pass@1: base 6.43 vs. MEDS 34.32; MATH500 pass@1: base 30.84 vs. MEDS 86.33).
- On Qwen3-8B, GRPO actually degrades AIME24 pass@1 relative to base (22.45 vs. 34.51), while MEDS improves it (45.78 vs. 34.51).

The fact that GRPO and DAPO can underperform the base model on some benchmarks — particularly on the smallest model — is a reminder that RL fine-tuning is not uniformly beneficial. The base model has been pretrained with maximum-likelihood estimation across diverse reasoning patterns; RL optimization toward a single reward signal can collapse this diversity into a narrow set of behaviors that may score worse than the base model's more varied (but on average decent) outputs. MEDS appears to mitigate this collapse, consistently improving over both the base model and static-reward RL baselines.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Feature construction strategy (Table 2, Figure 5 right):** The paper ablates five different feature construction approaches on Qwen2.5-Math-7B: (1) **MEDS-single cluster** (all logits assigned to one cluster, penalty constant for all errors — functionally equivalent to subtracting a fixed penalty from all incorrect rewards), (2) **MEDS-28-diff** (differences between adjacent logits across all 28 layers), (3) **MEDS-14-diff** (differences across the last 14 layers), (4) **MEDS-28** (direct logits from all 28 layers), and (5) **MEDS-14** (direct logits from last 14 layers, the main configuration). The degenerate single-cluster configuration achieves an average pass@1 of 55.14, which is *below* DAPO's 55.61. This negative result confirms that clustering is necessary — a flat penalty applied to all errors equally does not help and may marginally hurt by compressing the advantage range. Among the meaningful feature constructions, **MEDS-14 achieves the best average pass@1 (56.47) and pass@128 (84.00)**. The ranking of pass@1 performance (MEDS-14 > MEDS-28 > MEDS-14-diff > MEDS-28-diff > MEDS-single cluster) closely mirrors the ranking of Claude correlation scores (61.2% > 53.87% > 54.71% > 52.52% > 45.16%), providing evidence that clustering quality directly translates to downstream performance.

**Layer selection (Table 2, comparing 28-layer vs. 14-layer):** Using all 28 layers (MEDS-28, 56.17 pass@1) underperforms using only the last 14 layers (MEDS-14, 56.47 pass@1). The early-layer logits appear to add noise rather than signal for error mode discrimination, consistent with the paper's hypothesis that early layers encode generic semantic features while later layers capture task-specific reasoning computations. On specific benchmarks, the gap varies: on AMC23, MEDS-28 (73.13 pass@1) underperforms MEDS-14 (74.38) by more than 1 point; on AIME24, MEDS-28 (35.63) slightly outperforms MEDS-14 (34.32), suggesting some early-layer information may be useful for the most difficult problems where reasoning patterns are more complex.

**Difference vs. direct logit aggregation (Table 2):** Logit differencing (capturing the rate of change across layers rather than absolute logit values) performs systematically worse than direct aggregation. MEDS-14-diff achieves 55.52 pass@1 vs. MEDS-14's 56.47, and MEDS-28-diff achieves 55.78 vs. MEDS-28's 56.17. The absolute logit values appear to carry more discriminative information about reasoning structure than the inter-layer differences — possibly because the overall pattern of where logits are high vs. low (which layer activations strongly predict the final answer token) is more informative than where they change rapidly.

**HDBSCAN parameter sensitivity (implicitly tested via different feature constructions):** Since different feature constructions produce different data geometries, the consistent ranking across Claude correlation and downstream performance suggests that HDBSCAN's clustering is reasonably robust to feature quality — it extracts useful structure when the representation is informative (14-layer direct) and degrades gracefully when the representation is weaker (single-cluster, 28-diff). The paper does not explicitly sweep HDBSCAN's minimum cluster size or minimum samples parameters, which is a minor ablation gap — it's possible that tuning these per-prompt or per-model could yield further improvements.

**Model scale and penalty strength interaction (Table 1, comparing α and β values):** The paper uses different penalty coefficients for different model scales: α = 0.1, β = 0.2 for Qwen3-1.7B and Qwen2.5-Math-7B, but α = 0.02, β = 0.04 for Qwen3-8B — the larger model gets a 5× weaker penalty. The paper does not ablate these choices or report what happens if the same coefficients are used across models. This is a significant omission: if the larger model benefits more from MEDS *despite* using a smaller penalty, the benefit is not simply due to stronger regularization. If the optimal penalty scales inversely with model size, this suggests that larger models are more sensitive to reward perturbations — a finding that would have implications for other reward-shaping approaches. The paper treats these coefficient choices as given without investigating the underlying reasons or the sensitivity of results to them.

**Clustering quality correlation with downstream performance (Figure 5 right, Table 2):** This is the paper's most important ablation for establishing the causal mechanism. The strong positive relationship between Claude correlation and pass@1 performance — MEDS-14 achieves both the highest correlation (61.2%) and the highest pass@1 (56.47), while the degenerate single-cluster achieves both the lowest correlation (45.16%) and the lowest pass@1 (55.14, below DAPO) — provides evidence that better recognition of repeated error patterns (as measured by agreement with human semantic clustering) causes better RL performance. However, this is a correlational analysis across feature construction strategies, not a direct manipulation of clustering quality within a single strategy. A stronger ablation would be to artificially degrade clustering quality (e.g., by adding noise to the logit vectors) and measure the monotonic relationship with performance — this would establish causation more directly than the cross-strategy comparison.

**Diversity analysis: Within-Step vs. Across-Step (Figure 1(c), Figure 5 left):** The LLM-based diversity evaluations show that MEDS consistently produces more diverse rollouts than DAPO under both metrics. The Within-Step diversity result confirms that MEDS rollouts within a single batch are more varied — this is expected because differential penalties penalize common error patterns, making rare patterns relatively more attractive within the batch's advantage computation. The Across-Step diversity result is more interesting: it shows that rollouts from later training steps under MEDS introduce **novel reasoning patterns relative to earlier steps**, whereas DAPO's later rollouts are more similar to its earlier ones. This is evidence that MEDS continues to explore new regions of behavior space as training proceeds, rather than converging to a fixed (possibly incorrect) set of strategies.

The **Top-1 Eigen Ratio analysis** (Figure 5 left) provides representation-level evidence: MEDS's logit covariance has a consistently lower ratio of the largest eigenvalue to total variance than DAPO's, indicating a more uniform spread of logit representations across directions rather than concentration along a single dominant direction. This metric declines for both methods over training (representations become more concentrated as the policy specializes), but MEDS declines more slowly and remains below DAPO throughout. This is consistent with the behavioral diversity results and provides a quantitative, automatically-computable diversity metric that correlates with the LLM-based evaluations.

**Case study: logit patterns reflect reasoning, not just final answers (Figure 6, Appendix C):** The qualitative analysis shows that responses with the same incorrect final answer (1342) but different reasoning paths are assigned to different clusters (A1 vs. B1, B2), while responses with different final answers but shared reasoning structure are assigned to the same cluster (A1 at 1342 and A2 at 1351). This is the qualitative validation that the logit-based clustering captures *how* the model reasons, not just *what* it outputs. The heatmap in Figure 7 (Appendix C) shows the specific logit values: responses A1 and A2 have similar logit patterns across layers 20–28 (values in a similar range with similar trends), while B-series responses show markedly different patterns. This is a single example and does not establish statistical reliability, but it illustrates the mechanism concretely.

**Training efficiency (Section 4.2):** On Qwen2.5-Math-7B, MEDS adds approximately 5–8% training time overhead over DAPO: 8.46 minutes for 50 steps vs. 8.00 (5.75% overhead), and 9.73 minutes for 100 steps vs. 8.95 (8.7% overhead). The overhead increases with training steps because the error memory grows, making HDBSCAN clustering more expensive. The paper does not report whether this growth in clustering cost continues linearly or can be bounded — for very long training runs, the per-step overhead could become substantial. The paper also does not report the memory footprint (how many 14-dimensional vectors accumulate per prompt after, say, 200 steps of training with 16 rollouts per step — that would be 3,200 vectors per prompt, which is negligible storage but could make clustering slower). A pragmatic optimization (reclustering only every k steps, or capping memory size to the most recent M rollouts) is not explored.

### Critical Assessment

#### Claim 1: MEDS "consistently improves average performance over existing baselines, achieving gains of up to 4.13 pass@1 points and 4.37 pass@128 points."

**What the experiments demonstrate:** Table 1 shows that MEDS achieves the best average pass@1 and pass@128 across all three models tested. The specific numbers claimed (4.13 pass@1 and 4.37 pass@128) appear to be the maximum gains over DAPO on particular (model, benchmark) combinations — on Qwen3-8B on OlympiadBench, MEDS achieves 61.12 pass@1 vs. DAPO's 52.77 (+8.35, which is larger than the claimed 4.13 maximum). The paper's "up to" framing is accurate: the gains vary substantially across configurations. On Qwen2.5-Math-7B, the average pass@1 improvement over DAPO is only +0.86 points, which is modest. The claim of consistency requires nuance: MEDS never significantly underperforms DAPO (Table 1 shows no case where MEDS's pass@1 or pass@128 is meaningfully worse), but the magnitude of the benefit varies widely — from negligible (+0.33 on Qwen2.5-Math-7B MATH500 pass@128: 96.00 vs. DAPO's 96.00) to dramatic (+11.86 on Qwen3-8B OlympiadBench pass@128).

**Gaps in evidence:** The evaluation uses a single fixed random seed (seed = 0) for all pass@k evaluations. With n = 128 samples for pass@128 estimation, a fixed seed means the reported numbers are point estimates without variance information. Pass@128 on a test set of, say, 30 problems (AIME24) with n = 128 samples has non-trivial estimation variance — a different seed could shift the reported pass@128 by several points. Reporting only one seed's results makes it impossible to assess whether the observed gaps are statistically reliable or within sampling noise. The paper would be strengthened by reporting pass@k with multiple seeds and showing standard deviations or confidence intervals, which is standard practice in the code generation evaluation literature.

#### Claim 2: MEDS "increases behavioral diversity during sampling" and "the logit-based clustering aligns well with LLM-based semantic clustering."

**What the experiments demonstrate:** The diversity analysis (Section 4.3) provides converging evidence from three sources: (1) LLM-based Within-Step diversity scores, (2) LLM-based Across-Step diversity scores, and (3) Top-1 Eigen Ratio of the logit covariance. All three metrics show MEDS > DAPO. The clustering correlation analysis (Section 4.5) shows 61.2% agreement between logit-based and LLM-based clustering, substantially above chance (9% for 11 categories) and above the degenerate baseline (45.16%).

**Gaps in evidence:** The LLM-based diversity evaluation uses Claude-Haiku-4.5 as the judge, with carefully designed prompts (Appendix D) that ask the model to rate diversity on a 1–5 scale (Within-Step) or count new reasoning paths (Across-Step). However, the reliability of LLM-as-judge evaluations for diversity assessment is not calibrated — there is no reported human baseline for these diversity judgments, no inter-annotator agreement metrics, and no validation that Claude's diversity ratings correlate with downstream task performance or human judgments. The paper treats Claude's assessments as ground truth for diversity, which is a strong assumption. Additionally, the Across-Step evaluation uses only 21 pairs of data groups (each pair comparing two training steps roughly one epoch apart), drawn from 1,600 total rollout groups (10 groups × 160 rollouts from each of DAPO and MEDS at each step). The sample size for this analysis is modest.

The clustering correlation analysis has several potential confounds: (a) The 11 error categories were "distilled through manual inspection" — they are researcher-defined and may not capture the true structure of error modes in the data. (b) Claude-Haiku-4.5 is asked to assign each incorrect response to one of these 11 categories, which forces a single category assignment even when a response exhibits multiple error types or doesn't cleanly fit any category. (c) The 800-response sample size is small relative to the total number of incorrect rollouts across all training steps, and the random sampling procedure is not described in detail. A 61.2% agreement rate with a proxy annotator is meaningful but far from perfect, leaving substantial room for logit-based clustering to capture patterns that the LLM annotations miss (or vice versa). The paper does not analyze the 38.8% of cases where the two clustering methods disagree — understanding whether these are systematic failures of the logit representation or limitations of the LLM annotator would strengthen the argument.

#### Claim 3: "Better recognition of repeated error patterns is strongly correlated with downstream performance gains."

**What the experiments demonstrate:** Figure 5 (right) shows a clear ranking: the feature construction strategies that achieve higher Claude correlation also achieve higher downstream pass@1. The degenerate single-cluster strategy (lowest correlation, 45.16%) achieves the lowest pass@1 (55.14, below DAPO), while MEDS-14 (highest correlation, 61.2%) achieves the highest pass@1 (56.47).

**Gaps in evidence:** This is a correlational analysis across only five data points (the five feature construction strategies), with a monotonic but not perfectly linear relationship (MEDS-14-diff has slightly higher Claude correlation than MEDS-28-diff, but their pass@1 ordering is reversed on some individual benchmarks in Table 2). The sample of five strategies is small for a correlation claim, and the strategies are not independent — they are variations along two axes (layer selection, differencing) with the same underlying logit data. A more convincing demonstration would involve systematically degrading clustering quality (e.g., by adding Gaussian noise of varying magnitude to the logits before clustering) and showing a dose-response relationship between clustering quality and performance across many degradation levels. This would establish that the causal arrow goes from clustering quality → performance, not from some shared confound (e.g., both better clustering and better performance arise from using later-layer logits, without the clustering itself being the mechanism).

#### Claim 4: MEDS mitigates the stable error basin problem in on-policy RL.

**What the experiments demonstrate:** The mechanism is indirectly supported by: (1) MEDS outperforming DAPO (Table 1), which uses the same base algorithm but without memory-based penalties; (2) MEDS outperforming GRPO w/ Entropy Adv, showing that token-level entropy is insufficient; (3) higher diversity metrics for MEDS (Figure 5 left, Figure 1(c)); and (4) the Top-1 Eigen Ratio showing less logit concentration. These are all consistent with MEDS preventing error collapse, but they do not directly measure whether error collapse is occurring under DAPO or whether MEDS specifically prevents it.

**Gaps in evidence:** The paper does not provide a direct quantitative metric of "error collapse" or "error repetition rate." One could imagine measuring, for a fixed set of prompts, how many distinct error modes are sampled at training step T, how often the same error mode (as defined by logit clustering or LLM annotation) is re-sampled across steps, and how quickly the policy escapes from error basins. Without such direct metrics, the claim that MEDS "mitigates error collapse" rests on the inference that better diversity + better performance = error collapse was mitigated. This is plausible but not directly demonstrated.

#### What Was Not Tested

Several experiments that would significantly strengthen the paper are absent:

1. **Sensitivity to hyperparameters α and β.** The paper uses different penalty coefficients for different models but does not ablate these choices within a single model. How sensitive is performance to α? Is there a monotonic relationship (higher α → better performance up to a point?), or is the relationship non-monotonic? The theoretical result in Appendix B only guarantees improvement for any λ > 0 under specific assumptions, but in practice the optimal λ is unknown and likely problem-dependent. Without a sensitivity analysis, practitioners cannot know whether the reported α and β values are carefully tuned optima or robust defaults.

2. **Comparison to other memory-based exploration methods.** The paper cites Song et al. [24] (UCB-style outcome-based exploration) and Hu et al. [23] (uniqueness-based batch-level rewards) in the related work but does not implement or compare against them. A direct comparison against a UCB-based exploration bonus would isolate the benefit of logit-based clustering vs. simpler memory mechanisms. Similarly, a comparison against an approach that clusters only within the current batch (without cross-step memory) would test whether the cross-step memory is necessary or whether within-batch clustering alone would suffice.

3. **Longer training runs.** The paper reports training for ~100 steps (the time comparison in Section 4.2 mentions 50 and 100 steps). For complex mathematical reasoning, the benefits of preventing error collapse might become more pronounced over longer training runs where mode collapse would otherwise become more severe. Conversely, the HDBSCAN clustering cost grows with the memory size, so the 5–8% overhead might increase substantially over 1,000+ training steps. The paper does not explore how MEDS scales to longer training horizons.

4. **Evaluation with multiple random seeds.** All pass@k results use seed = 0. Running evaluations with 3–5 different seeds and reporting mean ± std would provide statistical confidence bounds on the reported improvements and would reveal whether the gaps between MEDS and DAPO are larger than sampling variance.

5. **Difficulty-stratified analysis.** The example-level case study (Figure 6) and the difficulty-binned analysis that is standard in RL for reasoning papers (e.g., the reference example in the prompt analyzed MATH difficulty quintiles) are absent. It is unclear whether MEDS's benefits are concentrated on problems of a certain difficulty — perhaps easy problems that require specific reasoning strategies, or hard problems where error basins are particularly deep. A breakdown by problem difficulty (using MATH dataset difficulty levels or model-based difficulty estimates) would clarify the mechanism.

6. **The interaction between penalty and KL regularization.** MEDS is built on DAPO, which removes the KL penalty entirely. Would MEDS work with GRPO's KL penalty? The theoretical result in Appendix B is derived for a KL-regularized update, but the experiments use DAPO's KL-free objective. Testing MEDS with GRPO (which includes a KL penalty) would test whether the penalty mechanism's benefits are additive with KL regularization or whether the two forms of regularization interact negatively.

7. **Domain beyond mathematics.** All evaluations are on mathematical reasoning. The logit-based representation hypothesis — that layer-wise logits at the final answer token capture reasoning structure — may or may not transfer to code generation (where reasoning structure manifests differently), scientific reasoning, or open-ended generation. A single experiment on a non-math domain (e.g., coding benchmarks like HumanEval or LiveCodeBench) would provide evidence of generality.

#### Overall Assessment

The experimental evidence **convincingly demonstrates** that adding memory-based dynamic reward shaping to DAPO improves both pass@1 and pass@128 across three model scales on mathematical reasoning benchmarks, and that this improvement is associated with increased behavioral and representational diversity. The consistent outperformance of MEDS over the entropy-regularized baseline is particularly strong evidence for the paper's core thesis that token-level entropy is insufficient and that cross-rollout memory is the missing ingredient.

However, the paper **does not conclusively isolate the mechanism** of improvement. The correlation between clustering quality and performance (Section 4.6) is suggestive but based on only five strategies; a dose-response experiment would be more definitive. The diversity metrics establish that MEDS rollouts are more varied, but whether this is because MEDS (a) prevents the policy from revisiting known error basins, (b) encourages the policy to sample from unexplored regions (an exploration bonus in disguise), or (c) simply adds noise to the gradient that prevents premature convergence — these mechanisms are not disentangled. The theoretical result in Appendix B shows that penalizing repeated errors does not decrease expected return, but it does not prove that the observed performance gains are *caused by* the specific error-repetition penalty as opposed to some other property of the reward perturbation (e.g., the penalty might be acting as a form of advantage normalization that stabilizes training, independent of its memory-based aspect).

The **practical contribution is strong**: MEDS is a lightweight, easy-to-implement modification to existing RLVR training loops that consistently improves results with minimal overhead. For practitioners training reasoning models with DAPO or GRPO, the paper provides concrete evidence that adding this reward shaping mechanism is beneficial, and the reported hyperparameters (α = 0.1, β = 0.2 for small models; α = 0.02, β = 0.04 for large models; 14 latter-half layers; HDBSCAN with min_cluster_size = 2) provide a starting point for adoption. The open-source release (GitHub repository linked in the abstract) further supports practical adoption.

The **scientific contribution is moderate**: the paper identifies cross-rollout error repetition as an underexplored failure mode in LLM RL reasoning, provides a theoretically-motivated solution, and demonstrates its effectiveness. However, the mechanism is not fully validated (correlation ≠ causation in the clustering quality → performance link), the sensitivity to key hyperparameters is unexplored, and the domain generality is untested. These are addressable limitations for a paper of this scope, but they mean the central claim — that MEDS works *through* the proposed error-pattern-penalization mechanism — is supported but not proven.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Difficulty Estimation Cost Is Unaccounted for — and the Full Framework Requires Knowing Which Prompt-Specific Penalty to Apply Before the First Useful Training Step

**The assumption or constraint:** MEDS maintains a separate error memory for each individual prompt in the training set. The penalty mechanism cannot operate until the memory has accumulated enough rollouts to form meaningful clusters — a prompt with an empty or nearly-empty memory produces no penalty (or a constant penalty indistinguishable from no penalty), because HDBSCAN requires at least two vectors to form a cluster (minimum cluster size is set to 2; Section 4.1). The paper acknowledges this implicitly by noting that "the first training epoch is mainly used for memory formation and does not involve reward shaping" (Section 4.3). During this first epoch, the system operates identically to DAPO — collecting samples, populating memories, but providing no differential penalty signal. The consequence is that **the reported performance gains include the training steps where MEDS is providing no benefit**, making the headline numbers underestimate MEDS's advantage once memories are populated but also meaning that MEDS requires a **cold-start investment period** before its mechanism activates.

**The consequence:** This has several practical implications. First, for training runs that are very short (few epochs), the fraction of training steps where MEDS is active may be small, diluting the benefit — the paper's training runs are ~100 steps (Section 4.2), but for much shorter runs MEDS could underperform simpler baselines because the first epoch (with no penalty signal) does useful exploration that DAPO also does. Second, the cold-start problem interacts with the per-prompt rather than global memory design: prompts that appear rarely in the training data (e.g., tail problems from OlympiadBench that are sampled infrequently due to batch randomization) may never accumulate enough rollouts for the memory to become useful, while frequent prompts benefit fully. The paper does not analyze whether performance gains are concentrated on frequently-seen prompts versus rarely-seen ones. Third, the cold-start means MEDS's penalty mechanism cannot be active from the very first gradient update, which is precisely when the policy is most uncertain and when guidance away from early error basins might be most valuable — by the time the memory is populated, the policy may have already begun to collapse into error basins that the penalty then has to work to reverse, rather than preventing them from forming in the first place.

**What evidence exists in the paper:** The only explicit mention is the brief note in Section 4.3 that the first epoch does not involve reward shaping, with no quantitative analysis of how many steps this represents, what fraction of total improvement occurs after memory formation, or whether the cold-start period is longer for prompts with fewer rollouts. Figure 5 (left) shows the Top-1 Eigen Ratio evolving over training steps — the divergence between MEDS and DAPO would presumably begin growing after the first epoch once penalties activate, but the paper does not mark where epoch 1 ends and epoch 2 begins on this figure, making it impossible to verify this from the provided evidence.

**Mitigation status:** Not addressed. The paper does not discuss warm-starting memories from pre-generated rollouts (which would eliminate the cold-start entirely at the cost of additional inference before training begins), does not analyze whether the cold-start duration depends on the number of rollouts per prompt (16 in their experiments), and does not propose methods to accelerate memory formation (e.g., bootstrapping from a separate model's rollouts or using a global prior shared across prompts). A practical deployment might pre-populate memories by running a few hundred rollouts per training prompt before starting RL, but this would add substantial inference cost that the paper's efficiency comparisons do not account for.

### The Logarithmic Penalty with Cap and the Clustering Hyperparameters Are Unablated, Making the Reported Gains Dependent on Possibly-Narrow Hyperparameter Windows

**The assumption or constraint:** MEDS introduces four key hyperparameters that control the penalty mechanism: the penalty scale α, the penalty cap β, the minimum cluster size for HDBSCAN (set to 2), and the number of layers used for feature construction (set to the latter half — 14 out of 28). The paper uses different α and β values for different model scales: α = 0.1, β = 0.2 for Qwen3-1.7B and Qwen2.5-Math-7B, but α = 0.02, β = 0.04 for Qwen3-8B — a 5× difference in both parameters. Section 4.1 reports these values without discussing how they were chosen, what sweep was performed, or how sensitive final performance is to these choices. Furthermore, the clustering quality ablation (Section 4.6, Table 2) varies the feature construction but does not vary the penalty coefficients — all feature construction ablations use the same α and β, so the interaction between representation quality and penalty strength is unexplored.

**The consequence:** If the optimal α and β are narrow (e.g., α = 0.1 works well but α = 0.15 causes the penalty to dominate the task reward signal and degrades performance), then MEDS's reported gains may be contingent on careful per-model hyperparameter tuning that is not documented and not guaranteed to transfer to new models, new datasets, or new tasks. The fact that Qwen3-8B requires 5× smaller penalties than the two smaller models — despite achieving the largest absolute gains from MEDS — is a puzzling pattern that is not explained. It could indicate that larger models are more sensitive to reward perturbation (the same absolute penalty causes more dramatic policy shifts), or that the logit representations are more discriminative in larger models (so even a small penalty on a reliably-identified error cluster is sufficient), or that the task reward scale interacts with model size in some way. Without a sensitivity analysis, practitioners adopting MEDS for a new model cannot know whether to start with α = 0.1, α = 0.02, or some other value, and a coarse grid search over α and β adds to the already-substantial hyperparameter burden of RLVR training (learning rate, batch size, KL coefficient, clipping thresholds, number of rollouts, etc.).

The HDBSCAN minimum cluster size (set to 2) is another unablated choice. Setting it to 2 means an error pattern is penalized as soon as it appears twice — which is aggressive and may penalize coincidental similarity between two independent one-off errors that happen to have similar logit vectors. Setting it higher (e.g., 5 or 10) would require stronger evidence that an error pattern is recurring before applying penalties, reducing false positive penalties at the cost of slower activation against genuinely recurring errors. The paper provides no evidence that 2 is optimal or that the results are robust to this choice.

**What evidence exists in the paper:** Table 2 shows feature construction ablations but holds α and β constant — it tests whether the choice of *what to cluster* matters, but not whether the choice of *how strongly to penalize* matters. The single-cluster degenerate baseline (which assigns all responses to one cluster and applies a constant penalty to all errors) essentially tests an α value that is applied uniformly rather than differentially — since the penalty is constant across all incorrect rollouts in the batch, it provides no relative advantage signal and performs worse than DAPO (55.14 vs. 55.61 pass@1). This tells us that the *differential* aspect of the penalty (different magnitudes for different error clusters) is necessary, but not that α = 0.1 is the right magnitude for that differential — it could be that α = 0.05 or α = 0.2 would work even better (or worse). The paper does not report any sweep or ablation varying α or β.

**Mitigation status:** Not addressed. The paper does not report hyperparameter sensitivity, does not describe the tuning procedure that produced the reported values, and does not provide heuristics for setting α and β on new models. The suggestion that future work could explore "more sophisticated aggregation functions" (Section 6, Limitations) addresses feature construction but not penalty scaling. This is a significant practical gap: a practitioner adopting MEDS would need to perform their own hyperparameter sweep, adding cost and uncertainty to adoption.

### All Experiments Are on Mathematical Reasoning with Three Models from a Single Model Family — the Generality of the Logit-Based Reasoning Similarity Hypothesis Is Unsupported

**The assumption or constraint:** The paper's core enabling technology — that layer-wise logits at the final answer token capture reasoning similarity — is validated exclusively on mathematical reasoning tasks with Qwen-family models. All five benchmarks (AIME24, AMC23, MATH500, Minerva, OlympiadBench) involve mathematical problem-solving with a single, clearly-defined correct answer that can be verified through answer matching. The three base models (Qwen3-1.7B, Qwen2.5-Math-7B, Qwen3-8B) share the same architecture family and tokenizer. The paper acknowledges this scope limitation only indirectly, by framing the problem as "LLM reasoning" (Section 1) without discussing whether mathematical reasoning is representative of reasoning more broadly.

**The consequence:** The logit-based similarity hypothesis may fail in other domains for several reasons. First, mathematical reasoning has a particular structure — it involves symbolic manipulation, logical deduction, and computation — that may produce distinctive logit patterns at the final answer token. Tasks like code generation, where the "reasoning" involves planning a program structure and the final output is a block of code rather than a single answer token, may not concentrate reasoning information in the same way at a single token position. The paper extracts logits at "the first token of the final answer" — for math problems where the answer is a single number or expression inside `\boxed{}`, this is well-defined. For code generation where the "answer" is a multi-line program, or for open-ended generation where there is no single final answer, the framework has no natural analog. Second, the Qwen model family's architecture and training may produce logit representations that are particularly amenable to this kind of analysis; other architectures (e.g., Llama, Mistral, Gemma) or models trained with different objectives might not encode reasoning structure in their layer-wise logits in the same way. The mechanistic interpretability work that MEDS builds on (Templeton et al. [31], which studied Claude 3 Sonnet and identified interpretable features in activations) is architecture-specific in its findings. Third, the penalty mechanism relies on HDBSCAN clustering of logit vectors — if the logit vectors in a new domain are higher-dimensional (more layers), sparser, or have different density structures, HDBSCAN's default parameters may produce poor clusters, and the optimal minimum cluster size or distance metric may differ.

**What evidence exists in the paper:** None. The paper provides no experiments outside mathematical reasoning, no tests with non-Qwen models, and no analysis of how the logit representation properties vary across domains or architectures. The qualitative case study (Figure 6, Appendix C) demonstrates that logit clustering works for one specific number theory problem, but this is a single example. The quantitative Claude correlation analysis (Section 4.5) is restricted to mathematical error categories and uses mathematical reasoning responses. The paper's claim in the abstract that MEDS addresses "a common failure mode" of RL for LLMs implies generality, but the empirical support is domain-specific.

**Mitigation status:** Not addressed in the main text. The limitations section (Section 6) mentions only that "the methods explored for utilizing logits are relatively simple and do not incorporate more sophisticated aggregation functions" — a methodological limitation about feature construction, not about domain generality. The paper does not discuss whether the framework is expected to transfer to code generation, scientific reasoning, or other domains, and does not suggest experiments to test generality. This is the most significant scope limitation of the work and is not acknowledged as such.

### Pass@k Evaluation Uses a Single Fixed Random Seed, Meaning Reported Differences May Fall Within Sampling Variance

**The assumption or constraint:** All pass@k evaluations in Table 1 and Figure 4 use a single random seed (seed = 0) and a fixed temperature of 1.0. Pass@k is estimated using the standard unbiased estimator with n = 128 total samples. For a test set of size M problems (30 for AIME24, 500 for MATH500, varying sizes for other benchmarks), the pass@128 metric is an estimate of the probability that at least one correct answer appears among 128 samples, and this estimate has variance that depends on both the test set size and the underlying per-problem solve rate. The paper reports point estimates without error bars, confidence intervals, or standard deviations.

**The consequence:** Pass@k estimates with n = 128 are subject to non-trivial sampling variance, particularly for smaller test sets. For AIME24 with only 30 test problems, a pass@128 estimate of 76.67 (MEDS on Qwen3-8B) versus 73.33 (DAPO) — a 3.34-point gap — could shift by several points if a different random seed were used, simply because the subset of 128 samples drawn from the model's output distribution happens to contain more or fewer correct answers by chance. The direction of some pairwise comparisons could even flip: a 1–3 point gap that favors MEDS under seed 0 might favor DAPO under seed 42 if the true difference is smaller than the sampling noise. The paper does not provide enough information to assess whether the reported gains are statistically reliable — the fact that MEDS consistently outperforms DAPO across many (model × benchmark) combinations provides informal evidence that the effect is real, but the magnitude of individual reported improvements (e.g., Qwen2.5-Math-7B on AIME24 pass@128: 76.67 vs. 70.00, a 6.67-point gap; or MATH500 pass@128: 96.00 vs. 96.00, a 0-point gap) cannot be distinguished from sampling noise without variance estimates.

This is a methodological weakness that is common in the LLM reasoning literature (many papers report single-seed pass@k) but is particularly relevant here because MEDS's headline claims rest on numerical comparisons with specific magnitudes. The abstract claims gains of "up to 4.13 pass@1 points and 4.37 pass@128 points" — without variance estimates, these specific numbers should be treated as point estimates from a single evaluation run, not as precise measurements of the true performance difference. The largest single-benchmark gain (Qwen3-8B on OlympiadBench pass@128: 82.67 vs. 70.81, a difference of 11.86) is large enough that it likely exceeds typical sampling variance even for a moderate-sized test set, but the paper's careful reporting of specific figures invites a precision that the experimental design does not support.

**What evidence exists in the paper:** Section 4.1 specifies that "the random seed is fixed to 0" for all evaluations but does not discuss variance. The pass@k curves in Figure 4 show only the center line for each method without shaded regions indicating confidence. The paper also does not report the test set sizes for each benchmark, making it impossible for a reader to calculate approximate standard errors from the reported data.

**Mitigation status:** Not addressed. The limitations section (Section 6) does not mention the single-seed evaluation or the absence of variance estimates. Standard practice in more mature evaluation subfields (e.g., code generation, where the HumanEval leaderboard reports pass@k with bootstrapped confidence intervals) would be to evaluate with multiple seeds and report mean ± std or 95% CIs. The paper's single-seed approach is a pragmatic simplification but one that weakens the precision of its quantitative claims.

### MEDS Adds a Per-Step Clustering Overhead That Grows with Memory Size, and the Headline Training Time Comparison Covers Only 100 Steps

**The assumption or constraint:** The paper reports that on Qwen2.5-Math-7B, MEDS takes 8.46 minutes for 50 training steps and 9.73 minutes for 100 steps, compared to DAPO's 8.00 and 8.95 minutes (Section 4.2) — overheads of 5.75% at step 50 and 8.7% at step 100. The per-step overhead is increasing with training steps because the error memory G𝐱 for each prompt grows linearly with the number of training epochs (16 new rollouts per prompt per epoch), and HDBSCAN must recluster this growing set — or compute cluster assignments for new points relative to an existing cluster hierarchy — at each step. The paper does not report whether this growth continues, plateaus, or accelerates, and does not report the memory footprint or clustering time for longer training runs.

**The consequence:** For training runs extending to hundreds or thousands of steps — which is typical for large-scale RLVR training (DAPO's own paper reports training for more steps) — the per-step clustering cost could grow from a modest 5–8% overhead to a substantial fraction of total training time. HDBSCAN's runtime scales roughly as O(d · m · log m) with cluster extraction overhead, where d is the feature dimensionality (14) and m is the number of points in the memory. If a prompt has been sampled 1,600 times (100 epochs × 16 rollouts per epoch), reclustering 1,600 14-dimensional vectors is fast. If a prompt has been sampled 16,000 times (1,000 epochs), the per-prompt clustering cost is still manageable individually. However, multiplied across thousands of training prompts — the paper uses a prompt-level batch size of 512, and the total training corpus (DAPO-Math-17K + MATH levels 3–5) likely contains thousands to tens of thousands of prompts — the aggregate clustering cost across all prompts at each training step could become substantial. The paper does not analyze how the clustering overhead scales with corpus size, memory size, or number of training steps, making it impossible to assess MEDS's practicality for long-duration training runs.

Additionally, the memory storage cost is not discussed. Each 14-dimensional float32 vector occupies 56 bytes; for 16,000 rollouts per prompt across 10,000 prompts, that is 16,000 × 56 × 10,000 ≈ 8.96 GB of memory for storing error representations alone — not negligible but manageable on modern hardware. However, if the training corpus is the full DAPO-Math-17K (17,000 prompts) plus MATH levels 3–5, and training runs for many epochs, the storage could become significant.

**What evidence exists in the paper:** The time comparison covers only 50 and 100 steps on a single model (Qwen2.5-Math-7B). Section 4.2 reports these numbers. There is no analysis of how the overhead changes across models (is it larger for Qwen3-8B with more layers?), across training durations (what is the overhead at step 200? 500?), or across corpus sizes. The paper does not measure the time spent specifically on clustering versus feature extraction versus reward shaping.

**Mitigation status:** Partially addressed by the paper's acknowledgment that "the methods explored for utilizing logits are relatively simple" (Section 6, Limitations) — this suggests the authors are aware that the framework could be optimized but does not specifically address the scaling of clustering cost. Practical mitigations that the paper does not explore include: (a) reclustering only every k steps rather than every step, (b) capping the memory size to the most recent M rollouts per prompt (so old errors are forgotten and clustering runs in bounded time), (c) using approximate nearest-neighbor assignment to existing clusters rather than full reclustering each step, or (d) batching the clustering across prompts to leverage parallel computation. Without addressing this scaling concern, MEDS's practicality for production-scale RLVR training with thousands of steps remains uncertain.

### The Theoretical Result Applies to a Single KL-Regularized Update Step, Not to the Multi-Step, KL-Free Training Dynamic That Is Actually Used

**The assumption or constraint:** Theorem 1 (Section 3.2) and its proof (Appendix B) analyze a single KL-regularized policy update step, showing that the updated policy q₂ (trained with the penalized reward μ₂ = r(x, y) − λc(y)) achieves no lower expected return under the original reward than the policy q₁ (trained with the unpenalized reward μ₁ = r(x, y)). The proof relies on the KL-regularized Gibbs form of the optimal policy update (Equation 10 in Appendix B) and the covariance argument using Chebyshev's rearrangement inequality (Equation 13–17). However, the actual training algorithm used in the experiments is DAPO, which **removes the KL penalty entirely** (Appendix A.2, Equation 2: the DAPO objective has no KL divergence term). DAPO instead relies on clipping (with asymmetric thresholds ε_low = 0.2, ε_high = 0.28) to constrain policy updates. Furthermore, training proceeds for many steps — not a single update — and the error memory G𝐱 accumulates across steps, changing the penalty function c(y) at each step. The theoretical guarantee does not apply to this multi-step, KL-free, memory-accumulating setting.

**The consequence:** The theoretical result provides **principled motivation** for the approach — it shows that penalizing repeated errors is not harmful in the idealized single-step KL-regularized setting — but it does not provide guarantees about convergence, stability, or monotonic improvement in the actual training regime. Several things could go wrong in practice that the theory does not rule out:

1. **Runaway penalties:** Across multiple steps, a particular error cluster could grow very large (e.g., if the problem is genuinely hard and many different reasoning attempts land in the same error basin), causing the penalty to reach its cap β and stay there for many steps. This creates a persistent strong negative bias against all rollouts in that cluster, which could permanently discourage the policy from exploring a region of behavior space that might actually contain useful partial solutions — the cap prevents the penalty from growing arbitrarily, but it doesn't prevent it from *persisting* arbitrarily.
2. **Memory staleness:** The clustering is based on historical rollouts generated by *previous* versions of the policy. As the policy updates, the logit representations of the same reasoning patterns may shift (since the model's internal representations change), making old clusters increasingly less representative of the current policy's behavior. HDBSCAN reclustering on stale features could produce clusters that no longer correspond to the current policy's error modes, leading to misdirected penalties.
3. **Interaction with clipping:** DAPO's asymmetric clipping bounds policy updates by limiting probability ratios. The penalty shifts the relative advantages within a batch, which changes how many rollouts hit the clipping thresholds. The theorem does not account for clipping, and it's possible that the penalty's effect on which rollouts are clipped could have side effects not captured by the single-step KL-regularized analysis.
4. **Non-stationarity of the penalty:** The penalty function c(y) changes at every step because the memory grows. This means the reward signal μ₂(x, y) is non-stationary even if the original reward r(x, y) is fixed — the optimizer is chasing a moving target. The theorem's single-step analysis does not address whether this non-stationarity is benign or destabilizing.

**What evidence exists in the paper:** The empirical results (Table 1, Figure 4) provide evidence that MEDS works in practice despite the gap between theory and implementation — the gains over DAPO are consistent and substantial on the largest model. However, the theory-implementation gap means that the paper's claim of "theoretical analysis supporting the effectiveness of memory-based dynamic penalties" (contribution 2 in Section 1) is weaker than it appears: the analysis supports the *principle* but not the *specific implementation*. The paper does not discuss this gap or analyze which aspects of the theoretical conditions are violated in practice and with what consequences.

**Mitigation status:** Not addressed. The paper presents Theorem 1 as supporting theory for MEDS, and the proof in Appendix B is mathematically correct for the setting it analyzes, but the mismatch between the theoretical setting (single-step, KL-regularized) and the experimental setting (multi-step, DAPO, no KL penalty) is never acknowledged. This is a transparency limitation: a reader who does not carefully compare the theory section with the training configuration section might assume the theorem applies directly to the reported experiments when it does not. The paper's limitations section (Section 6) focuses on feature construction methods and does not mention the theory-practice gap.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper shifts the diagnosis of mode collapse in LLM reinforcement learning from a **token-level or batch-level problem** to a **cross-rollout, cross-time phenomenon**, and in doing so, opens up memory-based reward shaping as a principled third path between entropy regularization (which operates at the wrong granularity) and external verifier-based adaptation (which is computationally expensive). The shift is substantive but incremental rather than paradigmatic — MEDS does not replace existing RL algorithms; it augments them with a lightweight, plug-in memory module. But the augmentation is conceptually fertile because it identifies a specific, previously-unnamed failure mode — the **stable error basin** illustrated in Figure 1(a) — and provides both a diagnostic (Top-1 Eigen Ratio, clustering quality metrics) and a targeted intervention (density-based dynamic penalties) for it.

The paper's most landscape-changing contribution is not the specific penalty formula or clustering algorithm, but the **demonstration that layer-wise logits at a single token position — representations that are already computed during the forward pass and require zero additional inference — carry semantically meaningful information about reasoning structure**. This is an empirical discovery with broad implications beyond MEDS itself. Prior to this paper, if a practitioner wanted to compare reasoning trajectories for purposes of reward shaping, curriculum design, or anomaly detection, their options were expensive (external LLM annotations), coarse (final-answer matching), or domain-specific (parse-tree analysis for math, control-flow graphs for code). The paper establishes — through converging qualitative (Figure 6 case study), quantitative (61.2% Claude correlation in Figure 5 right), and downstream-performance (Table 2 ranking) evidence — that 14-dimensional logit vectors extracted from the latter half of the Transformer layers at the final-answer token position form a representation space where Euclidean proximity corresponds to reasoning similarity. This finding, if it generalizes to other domains and architectures, could enable a new class of **representation-aware training methods** that use the model's own internal computation as a supervision signal without external annotators.

The reconciliation of conflicting prior findings is implicit but clear: the paper resolves the tension between entropy regularization's theoretical appeal (it is the standard tool for encouraging exploration in RL) and its practical ineffectiveness for LLM reasoning (GRPO w/ Entropy Adv underperforms DAPO by up to 9 pass@1 points on Qwen3-8B, Table 1). The resolution is that entropy operates at the token level, injecting noise into individual word choices, while the failure mode that matters — re-sampling the same reasoning strategy with different surface wordings — operates at the behavioral level. The paper does not argue that entropy regularization is useless in general, but that it is **insufficient for the specific pathology of cross-rollout error collapse**, and that the insufficiency is masked in evaluations that only measure final-answer accuracy because the policy can maintain high per-token entropy while collapsing behaviorally.

Research directions that become **more attractive** after this paper:
- **Memory-augmented training methods** that track historical behaviors and use them to shape future optimization, rather than treating each training step as an independent event. MEDS provides a template (per-prompt memory, representation extraction, density-based pattern recognition) that can be adapted to other objectives beyond error penalization — e.g., tracking which correct reasoning strategies are under-explored and providing bonuses for rare strategies.
- **Representation-based curriculum design**: if logit representations capture reasoning difficulty or strategy type, they could be used to sequence training problems from simple to complex, or to group problems that require similar reasoning approaches for more efficient batch construction.
- **Lightweight proxies for reasoning analysis** that avoid external model calls. The 61.2% correlation between 14-dimensional logit vectors and Claude-Haiku-4.5 semantic annotations (Figure 5 right) is high enough to be practically useful for many applications where perfect accuracy is not required — e.g., filtering rollouts for data augmentation, detecting when a model has shifted its reasoning strategy, or monitoring for mode collapse during training.

Research directions that become **less attractive**:
- **Pure entropy-based exploration for LLM reasoning**: the paper provides strong evidence that token-level entropy bonuses do not address the dominant failure mode in RLVR training, and in fact can actively harm performance on larger models. Future work on exploration in this domain should incorporate some form of behavioral-level memory or structure-aware diversity metric rather than relying solely on per-token randomness.
- **Ad-hoc penalty heuristics**: the theoretical analysis (Theorem 1, Appendix B) provides a principled justification for monotonic, density-based penalties. Purely heuristic reward modifications that lack this structure (e.g., adding random noise to rewards, or using fixed hand-crafted penalty categories) are unlikely to provide the same benefits as MEDS's adaptive, data-driven clustering approach.

However, the paper also **narrows the perceived generality** of mode collapse as a phenomenon: it demonstrates that mathematical reasoning with Qwen-family models exhibits the stable error basin pathology and that MEDS mitigates it, but provides no evidence that the same pathology and remedy apply to other domains (code, science, dialogue), other architectures, or other RL formulations. This is a deliberate scope choice, not a flaw, but it means the paper opens a research program rather than closing one — the critical next step is establishing where the logit-as-reasoning-proxy hypothesis holds and where it breaks.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**1. Directly measuring whether MEDS reduces error re-sampling rate, not just whether it improves final accuracy.**

The paper demonstrates that MEDS improves pass@1 and pass@128 and increases diversity metrics (Figure 5 left, Figure 1(c)), but it does not directly measure the mechanism it proposes: does MEDS actually reduce the frequency with which the policy re-samples previously-encountered error patterns? A follow-up study could track, for a fixed set of prompts across training steps, the cluster assignment of each new rollout relative to historical clusters, and measure the **re-sampling rate** — the fraction of new rollouts that fall into an existing error cluster (indicating re-exploration of a known failure mode) versus the fraction that form new clusters or are assigned to noise (indicating genuinely novel exploration). Comparing this rate between MEDS, DAPO, and GRPO w/ Entropy Adv would directly test the paper's central causal claim: that the performance improvement comes specifically from reducing re-sampling of known errors, rather than from some other property of the reward perturbation (e.g., the penalty acting as implicit advantage normalization). If MEDS's re-sampling rate is not substantially lower than DAPO's, the paper's proposed mechanism would be falsified even though the performance numbers favor MEDS.

**2. Testing the logit-as-reasoning-proxy hypothesis on code generation, where "final answer" structure differs fundamentally from math.**

The paper's feature extraction relies on extracting logits at "the first token of the final answer" inside `\boxed{}` — a structure specific to mathematical reasoning prompts. For code generation, the "answer" is a multi-line program, not a single boxed token, and the reasoning is embedded in the code structure rather than in explicit natural language chain-of-thought. A strong follow-up would adapt MEDS to a code generation benchmark (e.g., LiveCodeBench, HumanEval, or MBPP) by either (a) defining a different "extraction point" (e.g., the first token after the model begins writing the solution function, or the token at which the model transitions from explanation to code in a structured prompt), or (b) extracting logits at multiple code-structure points and concatenating them. The experiment would measure whether logit-based clustering captures reasoning similarity in code (e.g., distinguishing between "iterative with two-pointer" vs. "recursive with memoization" solutions to the same problem), using LLM-based semantic annotations as ground truth (analogous to Section 4.5). A null result — logits failing to capture reasoning similarity in code — would bound the generality of the paper's core finding to math, while a positive result would substantially expand the framework's scope.

**3. Replacing HDBSCAN with streaming or incremental clustering to eliminate the growing per-step overhead.**

The paper reports a 5–8% training time overhead at 50–100 steps (Section 4.2), and the overhead grows with memory size because HDBSCAN must recluster (or assign new points to) an expanding set of feature vectors at each step. For training runs of thousands of steps — standard in production RLVR — this overhead could become prohibitive. A practical follow-up would benchmark several alternatives: (a) **incremental DBSCAN** that updates cluster assignments for new points without recomputing the full cluster hierarchy, (b) **online k-means** with a fixed number of clusters per prompt (sacrificing adaptive cluster count for speed, and measuring whether fixed-k clustering degrades penalty quality), (c) **sliding-window memory** that only retains the most recent M rollouts per prompt (e.g., M = 256), testing whether old errors can be safely forgotten or whether persistent error patterns from early training remain relevant, and (d) **reclustering only every K steps** rather than every step, measuring the tradeoff between clustering staleness and computational savings. The experiment would measure both downstream pass@k performance (to ensure the optimization doesn't degrade performance) and per-step clustering time, producing a cost-quality Pareto frontier that practitioners could reference when choosing a clustering strategy for their resource constraints.

**4. Sensitivity analysis of α and β to determine whether MEDS's gains are robust or require careful per-model tuning.**

The paper uses α = 0.1, β = 0.2 for Qwen3-1.7B and Qwen2.5-Math-7B, but α = 0.02, β = 0.04 for Qwen3-8B — a 5× difference with no explanation. A rigorous sensitivity analysis on a single model (e.g., Qwen2.5-Math-7B) would sweep α ∈ {0.01, 0.02, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.5} and β ∈ {0.02, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.5}, measuring pass@1 and pass@128 for each combination. The goal would be to determine: (a) whether there is a broad plateau of good α/β values (indicating robustness and easy adoption) or a narrow peak (indicating that MEDS requires careful tuning and may not transfer between models without re-tuning), (b) whether the optimal α scales systematically with model size (if so, a heuristic like α ∝ 1/√(parameters) might emerge, simplifying adoption), and (c) whether α and β interact — e.g., whether a larger β allows a larger α without destabilization. Additionally, testing whether the penalty cap β is ever actually hit during training (by logging the fraction of rollouts that receive the capped penalty) would reveal whether the logarithmic growth is doing most of the work (few rollouts hit β) or whether the cap is frequently binding (meaning the penalty saturates for common error patterns, which might limit MEDS's effectiveness on prompts where a single error mode dominates).

**5. Combining MEDS with KL-regularized algorithms (GRPO) to test whether the theoretical KL-regularized setting in Theorem 1 is necessary or incidental.**

The paper's theoretical analysis (Appendix B) assumes a single KL-regularized policy update, but the experiments use DAPO — which removes the KL penalty entirely, relying only on clipping for trust-region constraints. A direct comparison of MEDS applied to GRPO (which includes a KL penalty with coefficient β = 10⁻³) versus MEDS applied to DAPO (no KL penalty) would address an open question: does MEDS's penalty mechanism interact synergistically with KL regularization, antagonistically, or independently? The theoretical result guarantees improvement under KL regularization, but DAPO's clipping-only approach is empirically strong — does adding MEDS to GRPO close the gap with DAPO-MEDS, or does DAPO-MEDS remain superior? The experiment would also test whether the penalty coefficients (α, β) need to be re-tuned when switching between GRPO and DAPO, since the advantage computation differs (GRPO subtracts a KL penalty from the objective; DAPO uses asymmetric clipping). A positive interaction (MEDS + GRPO > GRPO alone, and the gap to MEDS + DAPO narrows) would suggest that MEDS is broadly compatible with different trust-region mechanisms; a negative interaction (MEDS + GRPO underperforms GRPO alone) would indicate that the penalty mechanism depends on DAPO's specific clipping structure and is not a general-purpose reward shaper.

**6. Pre-populating error memories from a separate model or from pre-training rollouts to eliminate the cold-start epoch.**

The paper acknowledges that the first training epoch "does not involve reward shaping" because the memories are empty (Section 4.3) — during this period, MEDS is functionally identical to DAPO. A practical extension would test whether pre-populating the error memory with rollouts generated by a different model (e.g., a weaker model, or the base model before any RL training) accelerates MEDS's benefits. The experiment would: (a) generate 16–64 rollouts per training prompt from the base model (or from a smaller Qwen variant) before starting RL training, (b) populate the per-prompt memories with the logit features of incorrect rollouts from this pre-generation step, (c) begin RL training with MEDS penalties active from step 1, and (d) compare the learning curves (pass@1 vs. training step) against standard MEDS (cold start) and DAPO. The hypothesis is that warm-started memories would provide penalty signals from the very first gradient update, preventing early error basin formation rather than having to reverse it after epoch 1. The experiment would also measure whether memories transferred from a different model (distribution shift in logit representations) are beneficial or harmful — does penalty based on another model's error patterns help guide the current model, or does the representation mismatch make the penalties misdirected? This would test a fundamental assumption of the framework: that logit-based error pattern similarity is stable enough across models to support transfer, which, if true, would open up new applications in multi-model training pipelines and model distillation.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**1. Improving sample efficiency in open-source RLVR training pipelines with minimal engineering cost.**

The most immediate practical application of MEDS is as a **drop-in reward shaper** for any team training reasoning models with DAPO or GRPO using the verl framework (the paper's implementation base). The paper reports consistent improvements across three model scales with only 5–8% training time overhead at 50–100 steps (Section 4.2). For a team training a Qwen3-8B-class model on mathematical reasoning, MEDS promises approximately +3.6 average pass@1 points over DAPO (66.72 vs. 63.11, Table 1) and +4.4 average pass@128 points (84.61 vs. 80.24). The largest gains come on the most challenging benchmark (OlympiadBench: +8.35 pass@1, +11.86 pass@128), suggesting MEDS is most valuable when the baseline RL method leaves substantial room for improvement. The integration cost is low — the paper's code is open-source, the feature extraction reuses existing logits, and HDBSCAN is a standard library — making this one of the most accessible improvements available to RLVR practitioners as of the paper's publication. For teams already using DAPO, switching to MEDS requires only modifying the reward computation step, with no changes to model architecture, data pipeline, or hyperparameter schedule (beyond adding α and β, for which the paper provides starting values).

**2. Reducing inference cost for pass@k evaluation by improving the shape of the pass@k curve rather than just shifting it.**

Figure 4 shows that MEDS not only improves pass@1 but systematically steepens the pass@k curve — the gap between MEDS and DAPO often widens at higher k values. On Qwen3-8B on OlympiadBench, DAPO's pass@k plateaus around 71 at k = 64, while MEDS continues to climb to approximately 83 at k = 128. This has direct practical implications for deployment scenarios where a model is sampled multiple times and the best answer is selected (e.g., best-of-N evaluation, self-consistency voting). If a practitioner needs to achieve a target pass@k (say, 80% on OlympiadBench), DAPO would require sampling well over 128 times (the curve is plateauing below 80), while MEDS achieves it with approximately 64 samples — a **2× reduction in inference cost** for the same coverage. For production systems that serve thousands of queries per hour and use best-of-N strategies, this translates directly to reduced GPU-hours and latency. The mechanism is that MEDS's error penalties broaden the model's exploration, making each additional sample more likely to explore a genuinely different reasoning strategy (rather than repeating a known error pattern with cosmetic variations), so the effective sample size for pass@k coverage grows faster.

**3. Monitoring and early detection of mode collapse during long-duration training runs.**

The Top-1 Eigen Ratio metric introduced in Section 4.3 (Figure 5 left) provides a lightweight, automatically-computable signal that tracks the concentration of the model's logit representations over training. MEDS consistently maintains a lower Top-1 Eigen Ratio than DAPO, meaning its representations are less concentrated along a single dominant direction — i.e., less collapsed. This metric requires no external model, no human annotation, and only the logit vectors that MEDS already collects. For teams running long-duration RLVR training (hundreds to thousands of steps, where mode collapse can silently accumulate and degrade final performance), the Top-1 Eigen Ratio could serve as an **online diagnostic**: if the ratio begins to climb sharply, the training run may be entering a stable error basin, and the practitioner could intervene (e.g., increase the MEDS penalty strength α, reduce the learning rate, or reset the optimizer). The paper's data (Figure 5 left) shows that the ratio generally increases over training for both methods, but MEDS keeps it systematically lower — establishing a baseline that practitioners could monitor against. This application requires no changes to MEDS itself; it uses the logit data that MEDS already collects for clustering, making it essentially free to implement.

**4. Targeted data filtering for self-improvement and distillation pipelines.**

When using an LLM to generate its own training data for self-improvement (e.g., STaR, ReST$^{EM}$, or rejection sampling fine-tuning), the quality and diversity of generated solutions matter. A common failure mode is that the model generates many superficially different but structurally identical incorrect solutions, which, if included in the training data, can reinforce the very error patterns they exemplify. MEDS's clustering mechanism provides a principled way to **filter for diversity** during data generation: for each prompt, cluster the generated rollouts using the same logit-based HDBSCAN pipeline, then sample at most one rollout from each error cluster (or apply a diversity bonus that downweights rollouts from large clusters). This would produce a training dataset that is balanced across reasoning strategies rather than dominated by the most frequently-sampled (and likely incorrect) strategy. The paper's 61.2% correlation with LLM-based semantic clustering (Figure 5 right) suggests this filtering would be substantially better than random selection or answer-based deduplication, while being essentially free (the logits are already computed during generation). A practitioner could implement this by generating a large pool of rollouts for each training prompt, clustering them with MEDS's pipeline, and retaining only rollouts from unique clusters (or weighting them inversely by cluster size) before fine-tuning.
