Here are the takeaways from this Ryan Peterman essay synthesizing the Ethan Evans interview (a meditation on corporate incentives, "the game," and what high performers actually do about it):

**The core of the Ethan Evans interview: understanding what *is***

The piece opens by framing the internet's reaction—"a mixture of disgust and admiration"—to a clip from Ryan's interview with ex-Amazon VP Ethan Evans. In it, Ethan articulates a truth many find uncomfortable: when you identify a weakness in your direct manager and escalate to your skip, the skip's incentive structure often doesn't align with fixing your problem or reprimanding the manager. As Ethan bluntly put it, "One of the hardest things for people to understand is I've identified a legitimate weakness in my boss. I go to my skip. Why doesn't he do something?" The outrage came from people hearing this as moral endorsement. Ryan reframes it not as an endorsement but as an observation of reality. Ethan "shared the incentive structure with unusual transparency. He's not commenting on what is right or wrong. He's saying what is."

**High performers and the game**

Ryan's central thesis is that across his career at Meta, all high performers shared one common trait: they understand **"the game"** and its incentives. Some play it selfishly; others play it while also orienting toward what's right. But the awareness itself is non-negotiable. His own early-career mistake, he admits, was misallocating effort. "When I was a junior engineer, I spent so much time working on random code cleanups and miscellaneous projects that were not impactful. Later, I still worked hard but I focused on projects that mattered." Understanding incentives lets you channel hard work into rewarded work. In the skip-level escalation scenario, Ethan is simply naming the gravitational pull of those incentives: his role's priorities don't naturally bend toward a junior engineer's grievance about their manager, even if the grievance is legitimate. Ryan's gloss is blunt: "You can hate him, but he's just playing the game. You probably just hate that game."

**What to do if you hate the game**

Ryan offers a practical, almost fatalistic prescription. Don't try to change the game—"the effort is usually not worth it." The optimal move is to find a different team, role, or company where the incentives match the work you care about. He cites his own early luck: "I loved coding and optimizing infrastructure, which Meta rewarded well." Those alignment zones exist in tech, but they require deliberate searching. He quotes Ethan's summary of the options when trying to avoid office politics, and issues a stark warning about playing the game naively on your own terms: "if you hate the game but try to play it based on how you think the incentives should be, you will get burned." The spectrum of outcomes ranges from mediocre results to getting fired "for being difficult or doing work that doesn't matter." Ethan's closing advice: "do yourself a favor and get good at this [politics]." The implication is that fluency in the system's actual reward structure isn't a sellout move—it's table stakes for surviving long enough to do work you care about.
