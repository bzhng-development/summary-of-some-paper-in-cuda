# Bootstrapping Language Models with DPO Implicit Rewards

**ArXiv:** [2406.09760](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.09760)

## 🎯 Pitch

This paper proposes **self-alignment with DPO ImpliCit rEwards (DICE)**, a method that bootstraps language model alignment by using the implicit reward model already present in a DPO-tuned LLM to iteratively label its own generated responses—constructing new preference datasets for subsequent DPO rounds without any external feedback.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper proposes **self-alignment with DPO ImpliCit rEwards (DICE)**, a method that bootstraps language model alignment by using the implicit reward model already present in a DPO-tuned LLM to iteratively label its own generated responses—constructing new preference datasets for subsequent DPO rounds without any external feedback. Tested on both Zephyr-7B-beta and Llama-3-8B-DPO base models evaluated on AlpacaEval 2, DICE incorporates two refinements: length-regularized reward shaping (optimizing a penalty coefficient α so that the constructed preference dataset becomes length-unbiased, rather than favoring verbose responses) and experience replay (mixing the model's self-generated preference data with the original high-quality offline dataset to prevent catastrophic forgetting). The approach yields an increase of more than 8% in length-controlled win rate on AlpacaEval 2 across all tested base models—for instance, the Llama-3-8B-DPO variant reaches 27.55% LC, surpassing Gemini Pro—establishing that a DPO-tuned model can substantially self-improve through iterative bootstrapping with its own implicit rewards, provided that length exploitation and knowledge degradation are explicitly addressed.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Improving Alignment Beyond the Initial DPO Round

The central problem this paper addresses is deceptively simple: **once you have a DPO-tuned model, can you continue improving it without any additional external feedback?** DPO (Direct Preference Optimization) has become a dominant method for aligning LLMs with human preferences because it simplifies the RLHF pipeline—replacing the separate reward model training and PPO optimization stages with a single classification-style loss. However, a DPO-trained model represents a single point in the optimization trajectory, and the natural question is whether further rounds of improvement are possible using only resources already available at that point: the model itself and the preference data it was originally trained on.

This problem matters for several interconnected reasons that the paper raises implicitly through its experimental design (Section 1, Section 4.1):

**Practical resource constraints.** Collecting high-quality human preference data is expensive and time-consuming. The UltraFeedback dataset used in this work contains 60k preference pairs—a substantial resource that required significant annotation effort. If a model can continue improving itself by generating and self-labeling its own preference data from that same initial dataset, it dramatically extends the return on investment from each round of human annotation. This is especially relevant for organizations that lack the resources of major AI labs to continuously collect fresh human feedback.

**The on-policy/off-policy gap.** There is a fundamental mismatch in standard DPO training: the preference data comes from some behavior policy (the distribution over responses that existed when the data was collected), but the model being trained is itself changing. As the model's own response distribution diverges from the original data collection distribution, the preference labels become increasingly "stale"—they reflect what was preferred among responses from the *old* policy, not the *current* one. The paper's theoretical analysis in Appendix A formalizes this: if a suboptimal response exists in the model's high-likelihood region but was never sampled in the original dataset, it can never be suppressed by training on that fixed dataset alone. The result is that the model may converge to a policy far from optimal, limited by what the original behavior policy happened to capture.

**Empirical evidence of DPO plateauing.** The paper explicitly notes (Section 3, opening paragraph) that "continuing DPO training on a fixed offline dataset results in inferior policy." The experimental results in Table 1 starkly confirm this: continuing offline DPO for a second iteration on Zephyr causes the LC win rate to *drop* from 13.40% to 4.96%—worse than the base model's 12.69%. The model is not merely plateauing; it is actively degrading. This is the concrete failure mode that motivates the search for an alternative way to continue training.

### Why Iterative Self-Improvement Is Theoretically Compelling

Beyond the practical motivation, the paper's approach is grounded in a theoretical insight about the DPO objective itself. The DPO loss (Equation 3) reparameterizes the reward function in terms of the policy and reference model:

$$r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}$$

This implicit reward is not a byproduct—it is mathematically equivalent to the reward that would have been learned by an explicit reward model in the RLHF framework, as established by Theorem 1 in Rafailov et al. (2024b). This means that after DPO training, every DPO-tuned model comes with a built-in reward function *for free*. The paper's key insight is that this implicit reward can serve as a preference labeler for the model's own generated responses, enabling an iterative self-improvement loop:

1. Current policy $\pi_{\theta^{(t-1)}}$ generates responses to prompts
2. The implicit reward $r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta^{(t-1)}}(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}$ scores those responses
3. The highest- and lowest-scoring responses form a new preference pair $(y_w, y_l)$
4. DPO trains $\pi_{\theta^{(t)}}$ on this self-generated preference data

This is fundamentally different from methods that require an external reward model or an external LLM judge, because it uses a reward signal that is **intrinsic to the DPO training process itself**. The policy and the reward model are two views of the same trained parameters.

### Where Prior Approaches Fall Short

**Offline DPO continuation degrades performance.** As noted above, simply running more DPO rounds on the same offline dataset causes performance collapse (Table 1). The paper attributes this to both data staleness (the fixed dataset cannot capture on-policy responses from the evolving model) and overfitting. This is a well-documented phenomenon: Guo et al. (2024) showed that offline DPO quickly overfits the preference dataset, while online feedback on on-policy samples yields much more stable and effective training.

**Iterative DPO requires external reward signals.** Previous iterative DPO frameworks (Tran et al., 2023; Dong et al., 2024; Xiong et al., 2024) address the staleness issue by having the updated policy generate fresh responses each round, but they require an external reward model to label these new responses with preference signals. The external reward model itself must be trained on human preference data—so these methods don't reduce the dependency on external feedback; they just use it to train a reward model rather than directly for DPO. In the paper's setting, where the goal is self-improvement *without* external feedback beyond what was used in the initial DPO training, these approaches don't apply.

**LLM-as-a-Judge provides only coarse, potentially biased signals.** An alternative is to use the model itself to judge its own responses—the self-rewarding LM approach of Yuan et al. (2024). The paper implements this as a baseline (Table 1), and the results are revealing. For Zephyr, LLM-as-a-Judge achieves a respectable 15.36% LC win rate at iteration 1, but this is substantially below DICE's 19.03%. For Llama-3, the improvement is even smaller (20.30% vs. DICE's 25.08%). The paper hypothesizes (Section 4.2) that this is because LLM-as-a-Judge requires the model to output discrete scores (0-5) through a prompt template, producing "coarse rewards which are not able to provide effective preference signals when responses are of high quality." The implicit reward, in contrast, produces a continuous, fine-grained signal based on the model's own probability ratios.

**Length exploitation is pervasive and unaddressed.** A known pathology in preference-tuned LLMs is that they learn to produce increasingly verbose responses, because human annotators tend to prefer longer outputs even when the additional length doesn't add information (Park et al., 2024). This problem compounds in iterative self-training: if the implicit reward assigns higher scores to longer responses, and those longer responses are labeled as preferred in the constructed dataset, then each round of DPO amplifies the bias. The paper's Figure 2 (top) quantifies this for the vanilla implicit reward: the average length difference between winning and losing responses is **1031 characters**, with a heavily right-skewed distribution. In contrast, a high-quality human preference dataset (UltraFeedback, bottom panel) shows an approximately normal distribution centered near zero. Without explicit correction, iterative DPO with implicit rewards would produce models that "win" on raw win rate by being verbose while actually being *worse* after length correction—a degenerate outcome that doesn't represent genuine alignment improvement.

**Catastrophic forgetting during iterative fine-tuning.** When a model is fine-tuned exclusively on its own self-labeled data, it risks drifting away from the knowledge and preferences encoded in the original training data. This is the classic catastrophic forgetting problem from continual learning (Rolnick et al., 2019), but with an LLM alignment twist: the implicit reward model is only an approximation of true human preferences, and over-reliance on it can cause the model to optimize for what the proxy reward values rather than what humans actually prefer. The paper's experience replay ablation (Figure 3) demonstrates this concretely: with $\gamma = 0.0$ (using only self-generated data), performance saturates at 18.88% LC; with $\gamma = 1.0$ (using only offline data), it drops to 13.40%. The optimal mixture ($\gamma = 0.5$, achieving 20.71%) sits between these extremes, showing that both data sources are necessary.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper does not claim to invent iterative DPO or self-play fine-tuning—both are established concepts. Rather, it identifies a **specific, previously unexploited resource** (the DPO implicit reward) and shows that it can serve as an effective preference signal for bootstrapping, provided that two specific failure modes are addressed:

1. **Length bias in the implicit reward** → resolved by length-regularized reward shaping with an automatically optimized penalty coefficient $\alpha^\star$ (Equation 6), which debiases the dataset construction phase rather than modifying the training objective (Section 3.1). This is explicitly contrasted with Park et al. (2024), who incorporate length regularization into the DPO loss itself—requiring expensive hyperparameter tuning—whereas DICE's approach finds $\alpha^\star$ via a simple black-box optimization that minimizes the average absolute length difference in the resulting dataset.

2. **Knowledge degradation from self-training** → resolved by experience replay (Section 3.2), which mixes the self-generated preference data with the original offline dataset at a tunable ratio $\gamma$. This is framed as a balance between on-policy learning (the generated data is closer to the current policy's distribution, making training more effective per the theoretical analysis in Appendix A) and knowledge preservation (the offline data provides high-quality, human-validated preference signals that prevent the model from drifting into degenerate regions).

The paper explicitly scopes the method's applicability (Section 1, contributions): the approach works for a model that has already undergone at least one round of DPO training with an available reference model. This is not a method for alignment from scratch—it assumes a foundation of human preference data (the initial DPO training) and extends the alignment process beyond that initial investment. The "self-alignment" in the title refers to this *bootstrapping* phase, not to fully unsupervised alignment.

A key aspect of the paper's positioning is its relationship to external feedback. The abstract and contributions emphasize that DICE operates "without any external feedback" and "does not require any in-house data or external reward model" beyond what was used in the initial DPO training. This is a deliberately narrow and well-defined constraint: given only a DPO-tuned model and the preference dataset it was trained on, how much further can you push alignment? This constraint makes the method applicable in settings where the original training data exists but no additional annotation budget is available—a realistic scenario for many practitioners.

The paper also positions itself relative to scalar reward models (Section 4.4, Table 5). One might ask: if you have the offline preference data, why not just train an explicit reward model and use that for iterative DPO, as in Tran et al. (2023)? The paper's response is an empirical comparison showing that the DPO implicit reward achieves a higher alignment rate with GPT-4o preference labels (69.8%) than either an internal reward model trained on the same data (62.4%) or an external reward model trained on 555k examples (65.6%), when evaluating the model's own generated responses. The implicit reward has an advantage because it is naturally calibrated to the policy's output distribution—it is, after all, derived from the same parameters that generated the responses being evaluated. This finding justifies the choice to bootstrap from the implicit reward rather than train a separate reward model.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

The system is a self-improving alignment pipeline that takes a DPO-tuned language model and iteratively refines its alignment with human preferences by using the model's own internal reward signal—the implicit reward that DPO training naturally produces—to label its own generated responses as better or worse, construct new preference datasets from those labels, and retrain itself via another round of DPO, with two corrective mechanisms (length regularization and experience replay) to prevent degenerate behavior. The problem it solves is that a single round of DPO on a fixed offline dataset leaves alignment gains on the table—the model can improve further but continuing standard DPO on the same data degrades performance, and obtaining fresh human labels is expensive—so the solution is a bootstrapping architecture where each DPO round produces both a stronger policy and a reward function (the implicit reward) that can supervise the next round, enabling iterative self-improvement without any external feedback beyond the original preference data.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The DICE system has four major components connected in a loop:

1. **Current policy model** `$\pi_{\theta^{(t-1)}}$`: A DPO-tuned LLM that generates candidate responses. At iteration `$t = 0$`, this is the initial DPO-tuned base model (e.g., Zephyr-7B-beta or Llama-3-8B-DPO). At subsequent iterations, it is the policy produced by the previous round of DICE training.

2. **Implicit reward evaluator**: Uses the current policy `$\pi_{\theta^{(t-1)}}$` and a reference model `$\pi_{\text{ref}} = \pi_{\theta^{(t-2)}}$` (the policy from the previous iteration) to compute a scalar reward `$r_{\text{LR}}(x, y; \alpha)$` for each generated response `$y$` given prompt `$x$`. This reward combines the DPO log-ratio reward with a length penalty controlled by coefficient `$\alpha$`. An automatic search over `$\alpha$` finds the value that makes the constructed preference dataset length-unbiased.

3. **Preference dataset constructor**: Takes `$K$` responses per prompt, scores them with the length-regularized implicit reward, selects the highest-scoring response as the winner `$y_w$` and the lowest-scoring as the loser `$y_l$`, producing a new preference dataset `$D(\alpha^\star)$`. This self-labeled dataset is then mixed with the original offline human-preference dataset `$D_{\text{offline}}$` at a ratio `$\gamma$` to create the final training dataset `$D_t$`.

4. **DPO trainer**: Applies the standard DPO loss (Equation 3) using `$D_t$` as training data, with `$\pi_{\theta^{(t-1)}}$` as the reference model, to produce the updated policy `$\pi_{\theta^{(t)}}$`. The process then loops: the updated policy becomes the new current policy for the next iteration, and the old policy becomes the new reference model.

Information flows: prompts from the offline dataset's prompt set → current policy generates `$K$` responses per prompt → implicit reward evaluator scores all responses → dataset constructor selects winners and losers, optimizes `$\alpha^\star$` for length balance, and mixes with offline data → DPO trainer updates the policy → the cycle repeats.

#### The Iterative DPO Framework

DICE is built on the iterative DPO framework introduced by Tran et al. (2023), but with the crucial difference that the preference signals come from the model's own implicit reward rather than from an external reward model. Understanding this framework is essential because it defines the round-by-round mechanics that DICE inherits.

**The core insight of iterative DPO.** Standard DPO trains on a fixed offline preference dataset. This means the policy is optimized against responses generated by some *behavior policy*—the model (or mixture of models, or humans) that produced the data. As DPO training progresses and the policy `$\pi_\theta$` changes, the responses in the training data become increasingly unrepresentative of what the current policy would generate. The training data is *stale*. This mismatch between the training distribution and the current policy's output distribution is what the paper calls the off-policy/on-policy gap (Section 3, opening paragraph; Appendix A).

Iterative DPO addresses this gap by periodically refreshing the training data: at each round `$t$`, the current policy `$\pi_{\theta^{(t-1)}}$` generates fresh responses to a set of prompts, those responses are labeled with preferences (by whatever reward signal is available—traditionally an external reward model), and DPO trains the next policy `$\pi_{\theta^{(t)}}$` on this fresh, more on-policy data. The paper's theoretical analysis in Appendix A formalizes why this helps: a suboptimal response `$y^-$` that sits in the high-likelihood region of `$\pi_\theta$` but never appeared in the original offline dataset can never be suppressed by training on that fixed dataset alone (because it is never selected as a losing response to be downweighted). On-policy sampling makes it likely that such a response *will* be sampled from the current policy, labeled as a loser, and optimized away in the next round.

**The role of the reference model in each round.** In standard DPO (Equation 3), the reference model `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$` is typically the SFT model that was fine-tuned before alignment. In iterative DPO, the reference model for round `$t$` is set to `$\pi_{\theta^{(t-1)}}$`—the policy from the *previous* round. This means each round's DPO training optimizes the new policy relative to the immediately preceding policy, rather than relative to the original SFT model. This is important because it means the KL penalty in the DPO objective (implicit in the loss formulation) regularizes toward the most recent policy rather than toward the distant SFT starting point, allowing the policy to move further from the SFT model over multiple rounds while maintaining a controlled step size per round.

**The sampling procedure.** At the start of each round `$t$`, for each prompt `$x$` in the prompt set (extracted from the offline dataset `$D_{\text{offline}}$`), the current policy `$\pi_{\theta^{(t-1)}}$` generates `$K$` responses `$y_1, y_2, \ldots, y_K$`. The paper uses `$K = 16$` ("we sample with different random seeds to get K = 16 diverse responses for each prompt," Section 4.1). The sampling uses temperature `$T = 0.9$` with `$p = 1.0$` for the Llama3 setting, and `$T = 0.7$` with `$p = 0.9$` for the Zephyr setting (Section 4.1, "Response Generation and Dataset Construction"). These are relatively high-temperature settings designed to produce diverse responses—diversity is critical because the implicit reward needs to discriminate among candidates with meaningful differences.

**From responses to preference pairs.** Once the `$K$` responses are generated, they need to be ordered by quality so that a winner and loser can be selected per prompt. In standard iterative DPO with an external reward model, this is done by scoring all responses with the reward model and picking the highest- and lowest-scoring. DICE replaces the external reward model with the implicit reward derived from `$\pi_{\theta^{(t-1)}}$` itself. The specific form of this implicit reward—and the length regularization applied to it—is detailed in the following subsections.

#### The DPO Implicit Reward Model

The implicit reward model is the mathematical object that makes DICE possible without external feedback. It is not a separately trained model—it is a direct consequence of the DPO training objective and exists for free in any DPO-tuned model.

**Derivation from the DPO objective.** DPO (Rafailov et al., 2024b) starts from the same KL-regularized reward maximization objective as RLHF:

$$\max_{\pi_\theta} \mathbb{E}_{x \sim D, y \sim \pi_\theta(y|x)} [r_\phi(x, y)] - \beta \cdot D_{KL} [\pi_\theta(y|x) \| \pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)]$$

where `$\pi_\theta$` is the policy being optimized, `$r_\phi$` is a (hypothetical) ground-truth reward function, `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$` is a reference distribution (typically the SFT model), and `$\beta$` is a hyperparameter controlling the strength of the KL penalty. This objective says: maximize expected reward while not drifting too far from the reference distribution.

The key mathematical insight of DPO is that this constrained optimization problem has a closed-form solution. The optimal policy `$\pi^*$` satisfies:

$$r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)} + \beta \log Z(x)$$

where `$Z(x) = \sum_y \pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x) \exp(r(x, y) / \beta)$` is the partition function (normalizing constant) that ensures the distribution sums to 1. This equation can be rearranged to express the reward in terms of the optimal policy and reference policy.

**What the implicit reward computes.** The DPO implicit reward is defined by dropping the partition function term (which depends only on `$x$`, not on `$y$`, and therefore cancels out when comparing two responses to the same prompt):

$$r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}$$

where:

- `$\beta$` is the same KL penalty coefficient used in the DPO training objective (Equation 3). It is a temperature-like parameter that controls the scale of the reward. The paper hypertuned `$\beta \in \{0.01, 0.1\}$` based on AlpacaEval 2 performance (Section 4.1, "Training Details").

- `$\pi_\theta(y|x)$` is the probability that the current policy assigns to response `$y$` given prompt `$x$`. This is computed as the product of token-level probabilities `$\prod_{i=1}^{|y|} \pi_\theta(y_i | x, y_{<i})$`, summed in log space.

- `$\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)$` is the probability that the reference policy assigns to the same response. In DICE iteration `$t$`, the reference policy is `$\pi_{\theta^{(t-2)}}$`—the policy from *two* iterations ago. For the first DICE iteration (`$t = 1$`), the reference policy is denoted `$\pi_{\theta^{(-1)}}$`, which is the reference model used in the initial DPO training (typically the SFT model).

- `$|y|$` is the length of the response (used in the length-regularized variant, discussed below).

**What the computation means operationally.** The log-ratio `$\log \pi_\theta(y|x) - \log \pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)$` measures how much the current policy favors response `$y$` compared to the reference policy. If `$\pi_\theta$` assigns higher probability to `$y$` than `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$` does, the log-ratio is positive, indicating that the current policy "likes" this response more than the reference policy does. If the ratio is negative, the current policy assigns lower probability—it has moved away from this response relative to the reference. Multiplying by `$\beta$` scales this preference strength. The result is a single scalar reward per response.

**Why this form is valid as a reward.** Theorem 1 in Rafailov et al. (2024b) proves that this parameterization of a reward model is without loss of generality under the Bradley-Terry preference model—any reward function that is consistent with the preference data can be expressed in this form with the appropriate optimal policy. The DPO loss (Equation 3) can be viewed as the reward modeling loss (Equation 1) with the reward parameterized as `$r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}$`. At convergence of DPO training, the trained policy `$\pi_\theta$` is the optimal policy for some reward function, and that reward function is exactly the implicit reward defined above.

**Why the partition function can be ignored.** The full relationship between reward and policy includes the partition function `$Z(x) = \sum_y \pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x) \exp(r(x, y)/\beta)$`. Since `$Z(x)$` depends only on the prompt `$x$` (it sums over all possible responses), not on the specific response `$y$`, it is constant across all responses to the same prompt. When comparing two responses `$y_w$` and `$y_l$` to construct a preference pair, the difference in rewards is:

$$r(x, y_w) - r(x, y_l) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)}$$

The `$Z(x)$` terms from the full reward expressions cancel exactly. Therefore, the implicit reward without the partition function is *ranking-equivalent* to the full reward for any fixed prompt. Since DICE only needs to rank responses to the same prompt (to pick a winner and loser), dropping `$Z(x)$` is correct.

**Relationship to the DPO training loss.** The DPO loss (Equation 3) is:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\pi_\theta; \pi_{\text{ref}}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim D} \left[ \log \sigma \left( \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)} \right) \right]$$

where `$\sigma$` is the logistic (sigmoid) function: `$\sigma(z) = 1 / (1 + e^{-z})$`. The argument to `$\sigma$` is exactly the difference between the implicit rewards of the winning and losing responses. This makes clear why the implicit reward is directly relevant: during DPO training, the model is literally being optimized to make `$r(x, y_w) - r(x, y_l)$` large (so that `$\sigma(\cdot)$` is close to 1 and the loss is low). The implicit reward is not an afterthought—it is the quantity that DPO directly manipulates.

**Practical computation.** In practice, computing `$\log \pi_\theta(y|x)$` requires a forward pass of the policy model on the response `$y$` to obtain token-level log-probabilities, then summing them. For `$K = 16$` responses per prompt across a prompt set of approximately 10k prompts, this means the model must process `$16 \times 10,\!000 = 160,\!000$` responses per iteration. This is computationally intensive but parallelizable, and the paper uses 8 Nvidia A100 GPUs for all experiments (Section 4.1, "Training Details").

#### Length-Regularized Reward Shaping

The vanilla implicit reward `$r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}$` has a known pathology: it tends to assign higher scores to longer responses, even when the additional length does not reflect higher quality. This section explains why this bias occurs, how DICE detects and measures it, and how length-regularized reward shaping corrects it at the dataset construction stage.

**Why the implicit reward exhibits length bias.** The DPO training objective itself can introduce length bias because human preference data tends to favor longer responses (Park et al., 2024)—if annotators prefer verbose answers, DPO learns to assign higher implicit rewards to longer text. Additionally, the log-probability of a response `$\log \pi_\theta(y|x)$` is the sum of per-token log-probabilities, which naturally tends to be more negative for longer sequences (since each additional token contributes a negative log-probability). This effect is partially cancelled by subtracting `$\log \pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)$`, but imperfectly. The net result is that the implicit reward can systematically prefer longer responses, regardless of content quality.

**Quantifying the bias.** The paper measures length bias by looking at the distribution of `$|y_w| - |y_l|$`—the difference in string length between the winning and losing responses in the constructed preference dataset. Using the vanilla implicit reward (Figure 2, top, green curve), this distribution is heavily right-skewed with a mean of **+1031 characters**. This means that, on average, the response selected as the winner is over a thousand characters longer than the response selected as the loser. Since genuine quality differences are unlikely to consistently produce this magnitude of length difference, this is strong evidence that length, not quality, is driving the preference labeling.

In contrast, the original human-annotated UltraFeedback dataset (Figure 2, bottom) shows an approximately normal distribution of length differences centered near zero—human annotators, overall, do not systematically prefer longer responses when the dataset is well-constructed. The goal of length regularization is to transform the implicit-reward-constructed dataset to match this unbiased distribution.

**Length-regularized reward shaping formula.** The correction is applied directly to the reward used for ranking responses:

$$r_{\text{LR}}(x, y; \alpha) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)} - \alpha |y|$$

where:

- `$\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}$` is the vanilla DPO implicit reward (unchanged).
- `$\alpha \geq 0$` is a scalar penalty coefficient controlling the strength of length penalization. Larger `$\alpha$` more strongly discourages long responses from being selected as winners.
- `$|y|$` is the string length of response `$y$` (number of characters; the paper uses string length, not token length, as the penalty target, as evidenced by the units in Figure 2 and the optimization objective in Equation 6 which refers to string length).

**What the length penalty does operationally.** For each generated response, the system computes the vanilla implicit reward and then subtracts `$\alpha$` times the response's character count. A response that would otherwise score highly but is very long gets penalized proportionally to its length, making it potentially lose to a shorter response with a slightly lower vanilla reward. The penalty is linear in length, meaning that each additional character reduces the score by `$\alpha$`. The strength `$\alpha$` is not a hyperparameter to be tuned by trial and error—it is automatically optimized.

**Automatic optimization of `$\alpha$`.** The key innovation in DICE's length regularization is that the penalty coefficient `$\alpha$` is found by solving an optimization problem over the dataset itself, rather than being manually tuned or incorporated into the training loss. The objective is:

$$\alpha^\star = \arg\min_\alpha \left| \mathbb{E}_{(y_w, y_l) \sim D(\alpha)} \left( |y_w| - |y_l| \right) \right|$$

where `$D(\alpha)$` is the preference dataset constructed using the length-regularized reward with coefficient `$\alpha$`: for each prompt, the response with the highest `$r_{\text{LR}}(x, y; \alpha)$` is labeled the winner, and the response with the lowest `$r_{\text{LR}}(x, y; \alpha)$` is labeled the loser.

**What the optimization objective means.** The objective computes the average length difference between winners and losers in the dataset constructed with penalty `$\alpha$`, takes the absolute value, and minimizes it. When the absolute value is zero, the dataset is perfectly length-balanced—winners are not systematically longer or shorter than losers. This is a non-differentiable objective (because the winner/loser selection involves a discrete argmax/argmin over the `$K$` responses, and changing `$\alpha$` can change which responses are selected), so gradient-based optimization is not applicable.

**The optimization procedure.** The paper "find[s] a simple random search suffices" (Section 3.1). Specifically, they use Bayesian optimization with a Gaussian process surrogate model, implemented via the scikit-optimize library (Appendix C). The optimizer proposes candidate values of `$\alpha$`, the dataset `$D(\alpha)$` is constructed for each candidate, the average absolute length difference is computed, and the surrogate model is updated. The optimization converges quickly because the objective landscape (shown in Appendix C, Figure 4, left panel) is relatively well-behaved: it has a clear minimum near `$\alpha = 0.023$` (for the Zephyr setting; this value depends on the model and data), with the average absolute length difference rising as `$\alpha$` moves away from this optimum.

**Contrast with Park et al. (2024).** Park et al. (2024) also address length exploitation in DPO, but they incorporate a length penalty directly into the DPO training loss:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{R-DPO}} = -\mathbb{E} \left[ \log \sigma \left( \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)} - \lambda (|y_w| - |y_l|) \right) \right]$$

where `$\lambda$` is the length regularization coefficient. This approach has two drawbacks: first, the optimal `$\lambda$` must be found through expensive hyperparameter sweeps (training full DPO runs for each candidate `$\lambda$`); second, there is a quality-length tradeoff—larger `$\lambda$` reduces length but can also reduce response quality. DICE's approach avoids both issues: the length correction happens during dataset construction (not during training), so `$\alpha^\star$` can be optimized purely by evaluating the dataset statistics without any model training, and the resulting dataset is used with standard (unmodified) DPO training.

**The effectiveness of `$\alpha^\star$`.** With the optimal penalty `$\alpha^\star = 0.023$`, the length difference distribution transforms dramatically (Figure 2, top, orange curve): the mean shifts from +1031 to **-21 characters**—essentially unbiased. The distribution becomes more symmetric and centered near zero, though not perfectly normal. This dataset-level debiasing is what enables subsequent DPO training to improve alignment quality (as measured by length-controlled win rate) rather than merely generating longer responses.

**What happens with incorrect `$\alpha$`.** Table 4 shows the consequences of suboptimal penalty coefficients. With no penalty (`$\alpha = 0$`), the average response length balloons (2570 characters with `$\gamma = 0$`, 2600 with `$\gamma = 0.5$`), and the LC win rate is low because length inflation is penalized by the length-controlled evaluator. With double the optimal penalty (`$\alpha = 2\alpha^\star = 0.046$`), the length is aggressively driven down (876-1185 characters), but the LC win rate also drops substantially (14.40% vs. the optimal 18.88-19.03%), indicating that over-penalizing length harms response quality. The `$\alpha^\star$` found by minimizing the absolute length difference strikes the best balance.

#### Experience Replay

Even with length-unbiased preference labels, training exclusively on self-generated data risks catastrophic forgetting: the model can drift away from the knowledge and human preferences encoded in the original training data, instead reinforcing whatever biases or errors exist in the implicit reward. Experience replay addresses this by mixing self-generated data with the original offline human-preference data.

**The mechanism of experience replay.** At each iteration `$t$`, after constructing the debiased self-generated dataset `$D(\alpha^\star)$`, the system creates a mixed dataset `$D_t$` by sampling from a weighted combination:

$$p_{D_t} = (1 - \gamma) \cdot p_{D(\alpha^\star)} + \gamma \cdot p_{D_{\text{offline}}}$$

where:

- `$\gamma \in [0, 1]$` is the experience replay ratio—the fraction of training data that comes from the original offline preference dataset.
- `$p_{D(\alpha^\star)}$` is the distribution over the self-generated, length-debiased preference pairs.
- `$p_{D_{\text{offline}}}$` is the distribution over the original human-annotated preference pairs (the same data used in the initial DPO training of the base model).
- `$D_t$` is the final dataset containing `$N$` preference pairs (9,600 in the paper's experiments; Section 4.1, "Training Details").

**What the mixing achieves.** The self-generated data (from `$D(\alpha^\star)$`) provides on-policy training samples—responses generated by the current policy, labeled with preferences by the implicit reward. These samples allow the model to correct for the distribution shift between the offline data and its own output distribution (the staleness problem). The offline data (from `$D_{\text{offline}}$`) provides high-quality, human-validated preference labels that anchor the model to ground-truth human preferences. Without offline data (`$\gamma = 0$`), the model may overfit to imperfections in the implicit reward. Without self-generated data (`$\gamma = 1$`), the model trains on stale off-policy samples and degrades (this is equivalent to the "Offline DPO w/ new ref" baseline, which Table 1 shows drops to LC win rates of 4.58-4.96% at iteration 2).

**The conceptual connection to continual learning.** Experience replay is a classic technique in continual learning (Rolnick et al., 2019) for preventing catastrophic forgetting: when training a model on a sequence of tasks, mixing in data from previous tasks helps preserve previously learned capabilities. In DICE, the "previous task" is the initial DPO alignment on human preferences, and the "new task" is the self-improvement on the model's own reward signal. The offline data serves as a memory buffer that prevents the model from forgetting what humans actually prefer.

**The conceptual connection to reinforcement learning.** The paper also relates experience replay to "deep Q-learning with demonstrations" (Hester et al., 2018), where supervised learning from an offline dataset of expert demonstrations accelerates online reinforcement learning. In DICE, the offline preference data serves a similar role: it provides high-quality supervision (the "demonstrations") that guides the model's self-improvement (the "online learning"), preventing the model from wandering into degenerate regions of policy space.

**How `$\gamma$` is selected.** The mixing ratio `$\gamma$` is a hyperparameter that the paper "hypertuned ... using cross-validation to ensure fair assessment" (Section 4.1). In the Zephyr setting, `$\gamma$` was swept over `$\{0.0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1.0\}$`, and `$\gamma = 0.5$` gave the best AlpacaEval 2 LC win rate (Figure 3). This means half the training data comes from self-generated preferences and half from the original human data. In the Llama3 setting, a coarser sweep over `$\{0.0, 0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1.0\}$` identified `$\gamma = 0.1$` as optimal (Appendix D, Figure 5)—the Llama3 base model produces higher-quality self-generated data, requiring less replay of offline data.

**The shape of the `$\gamma$`-performance curve.** Figure 3 and Appendix D Figure 5 show an inverted-U relationship: performance is poor at `$\gamma = 0$` (only self-generated data, at risk of reward hacking and catastrophic forgetting), rises to a peak at an intermediate `$\gamma$` (balancing on-policy learning and knowledge preservation), and then drops again at `$\gamma = 1$` (only offline data, suffering from staleness). This inverted-U shape is consistent across both base models, though the optimal `$\gamma$` differs, confirming the general principle that both data sources are necessary.

#### The Complete DICE Algorithm

Algorithm 1 in the paper (Section 3.2) provides the pseudocode for one complete round of DICE. Here we walk through it in detail, connecting each line to the components described above.

**Inputs (Line 1).** The algorithm requires:

- A prompt set `$\mathcal{X}$` extracted from the offline preference dataset `$D_{\text{offline}}$`. The paper samples "a subset of around 10k preference pairs from UltraFeedback as the offline dataset" (Section 4.1, "Base Models and Datasets"). The prompts for generation are extracted from these preference pairs—each preference pair contains a prompt `$x$`, a winner `$y_w$`, and a loser `$y_l$`; the `$x$` values form the prompt set.
- An initial DPO-tuned policy `$\pi_{\theta^{(0)}}$`, which is the starting model (Zephyr-7B-beta or Llama-3-8B-DPO).
- An initial reference policy `$\pi_{\text{ref}} = \pi_{\theta^{(-1)}}$`, which is the reference model from the initial DPO training (typically the SFT model). This is used only in the implicit reward computation for the first DICE iteration.
- The number of generated samples per prompt `$K = 16$`.
- The DPO regularization weight `$\beta$`, hypertuned from `$\{0.01, 0.1\}$`.
- The experience replay ratio `$\gamma \in (0, 1)$`, hypertuned for each base model.

**Iteration loop (Lines 2-7).** The algorithm runs for `$t = 1, 2, \ldots$` iterations. The paper conducts two DICE iterations in total (Table 1 shows "Iter 1" and "Iter 2" results). Going beyond two iterations did not yield further improvements (Appendix B, Table 6, shows a drop at iteration 3).

**Response generation (Line 3).** For each prompt `$x$` sampled from `$\mathcal{X}$` (the paper processes the entire prompt set in each iteration), the current policy `$\pi_{\theta^{(t-1)}}$` generates `$K = 16$` responses `$y_1, \ldots, y_K$` with the temperature and top-p settings described earlier. Each response is a full completion to the prompt.

**Preference dataset construction with length regularization (Line 4).** This is the most complex step. For each candidate penalty coefficient `$\alpha$` explored during optimization:

1. Compute `$r_{\text{LR}}(x, y_k; \alpha)$` for all `$k \in [K]$` using Equation 5: `$r_{\text{LR}} = \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta^{(t-1)}}(y_k|x)}{\pi_{\theta^{(t-2)}}(y_k|x)} - \alpha |y_k|$`. For iteration `$t = 1$`, the reference model is `$\pi_{\theta^{(-1)}}$`; for `$t \geq 2$`, it is `$\pi_{\theta^{(t-2)}}$` (the policy from two iterations ago).

2. For each prompt `$x$`, identify the response with the maximum `$r_{\text{LR}}$` (label it `$y_w$`) and the response with the minimum `$r_{\text{LR}}$` (label it `$y_l$`). This constructs the dataset `$D(\alpha) = \{(x, y_w, y_l)\}$` for this `$\alpha$`.

3. Compute the objective value: `$|\mathbb{E}_{(y_w, y_l) \sim D(\alpha)} (|y_w| - |y_l|)|$`—the absolute average length difference.

4. The black-box optimizer (Bayesian optimization with Gaussian process) proposes new `$\alpha$` values based on the objective values observed so far, iterating until convergence to `$\alpha^\star$`.

5. The final dataset `$D(\alpha^\star)$` is the preference dataset constructed with this optimal penalty.

**Experience replay mixing (Line 5).** The mixed dataset `$D_t$` is created by sampling `$(1 - \gamma)$` fraction of its `$N = 9,\!600$` preference pairs from `$D(\alpha^\star)$` and `$\gamma$` fraction from `$D_{\text{offline}}$`. Sampling is done independently for each preference pair in the final dataset. The total dataset size `$N$` remains constant across iterations and conditions.

**DPO training (Line 6).** The policy is updated by minimizing the DPO loss on the mixed dataset:

$$\theta^{(t)} \leftarrow \arg\min_\theta \; -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim D_t} \left[ \log \sigma \left( \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\theta^{(t-1)}}(y_w|x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\theta^{(t-1)}}(y_l|x)} \right) \right]$$

Key details of this training step:

- **Reference model:** The reference model is `$\pi_{\theta^{(t-1)}}$`—the policy from the previous round. This is the "new ref" approach mentioned in the baselines (the paper refers to this as "we assign the trained policy after each round as the new reference model for the next round," Section 4.1, "Offline DPO w/ new ref"). Using the most recent policy as the reference means each round of DPO optimizes relative to the immediately preceding policy.

- **Training hyperparameters:** The model is trained for 300 steps on the 9,600 preference pairs, with a global training batch size of 32 (meaning 32 preference pairs per gradient update), a constant learning rate of `$5 \times 10^{-7}$`, and a warm-up of 50 steps (Section 4.1, "Training Details"). The learning rate is notably low—two orders of magnitude smaller than typical fine-tuning learning rates—to prevent the model from deviating too far from its starting point in each round.

- **`$\beta$` selection:** The `$\beta$` parameter is "hypertuned based on the model performance on AlpacaEval 2 for each method and model separately" (Section 4.1, "Training Details"). The candidate values are `$\beta \in \{0.01, 0.1\}$`. `$\beta$` controls the strength of the KL penalty implicitly embedded in the DPO loss—smaller `$\beta$` allows the policy to move further from the reference, while larger `$\beta$` keeps it closer.

- **No separate SFT stage:** Unlike the initial Zephyr training pipeline (Tunstall et al., 2023), which includes an SFT stage before DPO, DICE applies DPO directly without any intermediate supervised fine-tuning. The self-generated dataset is used exclusively for preference learning through the DPO objective.

**What the training step accomplishes.** The DPO loss pushes the new policy `$\pi_{\theta^{(t)}}$` to assign higher probability to responses labeled as winners and lower probability to responses labeled as losers, relative to the reference policy `$\pi_{\theta^{(t-1)}}$`. Since the winners and losers were selected by the implicit reward (with length regularization) operating on the *previous* policy `$\pi_{\theta^{(t-1)}}$`, the new policy is effectively being trained to internalize the preferences that the implicit reward expressed. The implicit reward itself is derived from `$\pi_{\theta^{(t-1)}}$`'s own probability ratios, so this process can be seen as the model distilling its own relative confidence judgments into policy improvements.

**Iteration continuation (Line 7).** After training, `$\pi_{\theta^{(t)}}$` becomes the current policy for the next iteration, and `$\pi_{\theta^{(t-1)}}$` becomes the reference model for computing implicit rewards in the next round. The cycle repeats.

**Why the algorithm is self-contained.** At no point does DICE require any component that was not available after the initial DPO training: the policy model, the SFT model (or previous policy) for the reference, and the offline preference data. The implicit reward is computed from the policy and reference models—no external reward model or LLM-as-judge is needed. The length regularization coefficient `$\alpha^\star$` is optimized purely from the generated data statistics. The experience replay uses the same offline data that was used in the initial DPO training. This is what makes the approach "self-alignment"—the model improves itself using only its own outputs and the original training data.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Reframing the DPO Implicit Reward as a General-Purpose, Intrinsic Preference Signal

The single most distinctive intellectual move in this paper is the realization that the implicit reward `r(x, y) = β log π_θ(y|x) / π_ref(y|x)` — which was previously understood as a mathematical artifact of the DPO derivation or a diagnostic tool — can be repurposed as a **general-purpose preference labeler** for the model's own generated responses in a bootstrapping loop. This is a reframing, not merely a new application, because it changes how one views what DPO produces: not just a tuned policy, but a policy-reward pair where the reward is a first-class functional component available for downstream use.

Prior to this work, the DPO implicit reward was understood primarily in three capacities: (a) as an analytical device in Theorem 1 of Rafailov et al. (2024b) that proves DPO's equivalence to RLHF under the Bradley-Terry model; (b) as a reward function that DPO-trained models could *in principle* use for scoring, with Rafailov et al. (2024a) and Zhong et al. (2024) exploring token-level dense rewards for PPO training; and (c) as a way to select training data for annotation efficiency (Yang et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024a). The implicit reward was treated as something you use *externally* — to benefit a different training process or to filter external data — not as the engine of the same model's self-improvement.

DICE's reframing is: the implicit reward and the policy are two views of the same learned parameters, so they are naturally calibrated to each other. This makes the implicit reward uniquely suited to evaluate the policy's own generations, because both derive from identical training. An external reward model trained on the same data would face a distribution shift when evaluating the policy's on-policy outputs (it was trained on the behavior policy's distribution, not the current policy's). The implicit reward, being computed directly from the policy's own probability ratios, is inherently on-policy. Table 5 provides the empirical anchor for this insight: the DPO implicit reward achieves a 69.8% alignment rate with GPT-4o preference labels when evaluating the model's own generated responses, compared to 62.4% for an internal scalar reward model trained on the same data (UltraFeedback) and 65.6% for a reward model trained on 555k examples — nearly 2× the data. The implicit reward is not just *available*; it is *more accurate* than alternatives explicitly trained for the same evaluation task on the policy's own outputs.

This reframing also explains why DICE is architecturally self-contained in a way that prior iterative DPO frameworks are not. Tran et al. (2023) and subsequent work (Dong et al., 2024; Xiong et al., 2024) require a separately trained reward model to label on-policy responses — a dependency that means each round of improvement relies on a component external to the policy being improved. DICE collapses this dependency: the policy being optimized *is* the reward model being used to supervise that optimization (from its previous training round). This is a conceptual shift from "train a policy, use an external reward to label new data, retrain" to "train a policy that comes with a reward, use that reward to label new data, retrain." The former treats the reward as a separate module; the latter treats alignment as an intrinsic property of the policy parameters that can be self-distilled.

### Innovation 2: Diagnosing and Correcting Length Bias at the Dataset Construction Stage, Not in the Loss Function

The paper makes a deliberate methodological choice that, while appearing minor, represents a genuine conceptual shift in how to handle the length exploitation problem in preference tuning: it corrects length bias **in the dataset** rather than **in the training objective**. This is not an incremental engineering tweak — it is a different way of thinking about where the bias lives and how to address it.

The baseline approach, exemplified by Park et al. (2024), embeds length regularization directly into the DPO loss: `L_R-DPO` includes a term `−λ(|y_w| − |y_l|)` inside the sigmoid, penalizing the model during training when the winner is longer than the loser. This means the length penalty is entangled with the optimization dynamics — the model learns to trade off quality and length simultaneously, and the tradeoff is controlled by a hyperparameter λ that must be tuned by running full training runs at each candidate value. The cost of this tuning is high, and the resulting policy reflects a compromise between two objectives optimized jointly.

DICE's approach is to frame the problem as one of dataset curation rather than loss engineering. The implicit reward is modified by subtracting `α|y|` before any training happens, and `α⋆` is found by directly optimizing a dataset-level statistic (average absolute length difference between winners and losers) via black-box search over the constructed dataset. This decouples the length-correction problem from the training problem entirely: the bias is corrected in the preference labels, and standard unmodified DPO is then applied to the corrected labels. The DPO training is completely unaware that length correction occurred; it simply sees a length-balanced preference dataset.

This framing matters because it reveals that length exploitation in iterative self-training is fundamentally a *data generation* problem, not a *training* problem. The bias originates when the implicit reward (or any reward function) systematically prefers longer responses during dataset construction. If you fix the dataset to be length-unbiased, the training dynamics need no special handling. The conceptual advantage is that the bias correction is transparent and auditable: you can look at the length difference distribution of the constructed dataset (Figure 2, top) and directly verify whether it matches the desired unbiased distribution (Figure 2, bottom). With loss-based regularization, the only way to assess whether length bias is being controlled is through downstream evaluation of the trained model, which conflates the effects of regularization with all other training dynamics.

The empirical validation of this insight is in Table 7: DICE's dataset-level correction achieves 18.88% LC win rate with an average response length of 2109 characters, while Park et al.'s loss-level regularization at `λ = 0.05` achieves 16.03% LC with 2030 characters. The dataset-level approach produces better quality (higher win rate) with comparable length control, and does so without the expensive hyperparameter sweeps that loss-level regularization requires (`α⋆` is found by a Bayesian optimizer evaluating dataset statistics, which takes minutes rather than the hours required for multiple training runs at different λ values).

There is also a negative result embedded in this comparison: Table 4 shows that over-penalizing length (`α = 2α⋆ = 0.046`) drops the LC win rate to 14.40–14.91%, substantially below the optimum. This demonstrates that length and quality are not purely separable — there exists a penalty strength beyond which length control comes at the expense of response quality, likely because the penalty suppresses genuinely useful elaboration. The dataset-level approach makes this boundary visible as a discrete optimization problem: finding `α⋆` that balances the length difference distribution without destroying quality. The loss-level approach blurs this boundary into the training dynamics, making it harder to diagnose when length regularization is suppressing quality rather than merely correcting bias.

### Innovation 3: Establishing Experience Replay as a Mechanism for Balancing On-Policy Learning and Knowledge Preservation in Alignment Bootstrapping

The paper imports experience replay from continual learning (Rolnick et al., 2019) and demonstrates that it serves a functionally distinct purpose in alignment bootstrapping compared to its original context. In continual learning, experience replay prevents catastrophic forgetting of *previously trained tasks* by interleaving old-task data with new-task data during training. In DICE, experience replay prevents the model from forgetting *human preferences* while it learns from its own (potentially imperfect) self-labeled data. This is a subtle but important distinction: the "old task" being preserved is not a previously trained capability but rather the alignment target itself — the ground-truth human preferences against which the implicit reward is only an approximation.

This distinction matters because it clarifies why both extremes fail. With `γ = 0.0` (only self-generated data), the model is training exclusively on its own implicit reward's preferences. Since the implicit reward is not a perfect proxy for human preferences — it inherits whatever biases and errors exist in the policy's learned parameters — the model can drift toward optimizing for what the implicit reward values rather than what humans value. This is essentially a subtle form of reward hacking at the meta-level: the reward model (implicit reward) and the policy are entangled, so the policy can learn to produce responses that score highly under the implicit reward without genuinely improving. With `γ = 1.0` (only offline data), the model suffers from the staleness problem formalized in Appendix A: the training data was generated by a different model distribution, and suboptimal responses in the current policy's high-likelihood region are never labeled as losers and thus never suppressed.

The inverted-U shape of the `γ`-performance curve (Figure 3, and Appendix D Figure 5 for Llama3) is the empirical signature of this tradeoff. For Zephyr, the peak is at `γ = 0.5` (LC 20.71%), with performance dropping to 18.88% at `γ = 0.0` and 13.40% at `γ = 1.0`. For Llama3, the peak is at `γ = 0.1` (LC 27.55%), dropping to 26.93% at `γ = 0.0` and 22.50% at `γ = 1.0`. The fact that Llama3's optimal `γ` is so much lower (0.1 vs. 0.5) is itself informative: it suggests that Llama3's implicit reward is a better proxy for human preferences (so less offline data is needed to anchor it), or that Llama3's generated data is higher quality (so more of it can be used without degradation). This model-dependent optimal ratio is a diagnostic: the better the implicit reward aligns with true human preferences, the less experience replay is needed.

This also connects to the paper's comparison with scalar reward models (Section 4.4, Table 5). The DPO implicit reward's higher alignment rate (69.8%) compared to the internal reward model (62.4%) suggests it is a better proxy for true preferences on its own outputs. But it is still not perfect — 30.2% of its preference labels disagree with GPT-4o — which is why even with `γ = 0.5` (Zephyr) or `γ = 0.1` (Llama3), some offline data is retained. The experience replay mechanism can be understood as a Bayesian prior: the offline data represents high-certainty preference information (human-validated), while the self-generated data represents lower-certainty information (implicit-reward-labeled). The mixing ratio `γ` is the relative weight given to the prior versus the new evidence.

---

### Innovation 4: Demonstrating That a DPO-Tuned Model Can Self-Improve Without Additional Annotation Budget, With Practical Magnitude

While the mechanisms above are individually novel, the paper's combined result — that a DPO-tuned model can improve by more than 8% LC win rate on AlpacaEval 2 across different base models using only the resources from its initial training — constitutes an empirical finding with direct implications for how alignment resources are allocated. This is not a theoretical contribution but a practical demonstration that changes the expected return on investment from preference data: one round of human annotation can yield not just one round of alignment improvement, but potentially multiple rounds through bootstrapping.

The scale of improvement is what makes this finding significant beyond incremental gains. For Zephyr-7B-beta, DICE improves LC win rate from 12.69% to 20.71% (a 63% relative improvement). For Llama-3-8B-DPO, it improves from 18.20% to 27.55% (a 51% relative improvement). Both models reach performance levels comparable to or exceeding much larger or more expensively trained models: DICE-Llama3 at 27.55% LC surpasses Gemini Pro (24.38%), Mixtral 8×7B (23.69%), the official Llama 3 8B Instruct (22.92%), and GPT-3.5 Turbo 0613 (22.35%) — all without any data beyond what was used in the initial DPO training. The fact that this holds across two different base models (Mistral-based Zephyr and Llama-3-based) and two different model families suggests the approach is not specific to a particular architecture or training regime.

The comparison against baselines in Table 1 reveals which components of the improvement are attributable to bootstrapping versus simply continuing training. Offline DPO with new reference — which is essentially DICE with `γ = 1.0` and no length regularization — degrades to LC 4.58–4.96% at iteration 2, far below the base model. This confirms that the mere act of iterative training, without fresh on-policy data or length correction, is harmful. LLM-as-a-Judge — which uses the same self-labeled data approach but with prompted discrete scoring rather than implicit rewards — achieves only 14.14–21.80% LC at iteration 2, substantially below DICE's 20.71–27.55%. This gap (roughly 6 percentage points for both base models) isolates the advantage of the continuous implicit reward signal over discrete prompted judgments, consistent with the paper's hypothesis that coarse rewards fail when discriminating high-quality responses.

The iteration-3 results in Appendix B (Table 6) introduce a sobering boundary condition: after two successful rounds of improvement (iterations 1 and 2 of DICE), performance drops at iteration 3 for both base models. This is consistent with prior work — Yuan et al. (2024) and Wu et al. (2024) show improvement only up to three iterations of their respective self-improvement pipelines — but it reveals a fundamental open question: why does self-bootstrapping saturate and then degrade? The paper hypothesizes that using a fixed prompt set across iterations may contribute (other works vary prompts across rounds), but this is speculative. The saturation point suggests that the implicit reward's utility as a preference signal is finite: after a few rounds, the policy has extracted all the useful information from the initial preference data that can be surfaced through self-labeling, and further rounds amplify residual errors rather than genuine quality improvements.

The practical implication is that DICE should be understood as a method for extracting additional alignment value from an existing preference dataset (roughly one to two rounds' worth), not as a method for unbounded self-improvement. This is a more constrained but more realistic framing than the "self-improving AI" narrative: DICE doesn't create new alignment information from nothing; it surfaces alignment information that is latent in the DPO-trained model's parameters and makes it usable for further training.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The primary evaluation benchmark is **AlpacaEval 2** (Li et al., 2023b), which uses the AlpacaFarm prompt set (Dubois et al., 2023) composed of general human instructions. Model responses are judged by a GPT-4-Turbo-based annotator against reference responses generated by GPT-4-Turbo. The paper reports both **raw win rate (WR)** and **length-controlled win rate (LC)** (Dubois et al., 2024), where LC corrects for the annotator's known bias toward longer responses. A secondary benchmark, **Arena-Hard** (Li et al., 2024), incorporates 500 well-defined technical problem-solving queries and uses Mistral-Large-Instruct-2407 (123B) as the judge model, with results reported as length-controlled scores with 95% confidence intervals (Section 4.1, "Evaluation").

- **Base model(s).** Two DPO-tuned models are used: **zephyr-7B-beta** (based on mistralai/Mistral-7B-v0.1) and **Llama-3-8B-DPO** (based on meta-llama/Meta-Llama-3-8B). Both are trained following the Zephyr pipeline (Tunstall et al., 2023) on the UltraFeedback dataset. The initial DPO training of these base models constitutes round 0; DICE adds up to two additional bootstrapping rounds. The choice of two different base model families (Mistral and Llama) tests whether DICE's benefits generalize beyond a single architecture. A third model, mistral-7b-sft-beta, is used in the reward model alignment rate comparison (Section 4.4) as the SFT base from which both the implicit reward (via zephyr-7B-beta) and the internal scalar reward model are derived.

- **Metrics.** The primary metric is **AlpacaEval 2 length-controlled win rate (LC)** expressed as a percentage, which measures how often the model's response is preferred over the GPT-4-Turbo reference after correcting for length bias. Raw win rate (WR) is reported alongside LC to illustrate the effect of length exploitation. On Arena-Hard, the metric is the **length-controlled score** with 95% confidence intervals. In the reward model comparison (Section 4.4), the metric is **alignment rate**: the fraction of preference labels assigned by the reward model that match GPT-4o's preference labels on 500 sampled `(x, y_1, y_2)` tuples from the first DICE iteration's generated data.

- **Baselines.** Four baseline methods are evaluated alongside DICE (Section 4.1, "Baselines"):
  - **Offline DPO**: Continue DPO training using the same offline UltraFeedback preference data in each round, with the original SFT model as the fixed reference model. This tests whether simply training longer on the existing data provides improvement.
  - **Offline DPO w/ new ref**: Same as Offline DPO but updates the reference model to the trained policy from the previous round after each iteration (equivalent to DICE with γ = 1.0). This isolates the effect of using stale off-policy data with an updated reference.
  - **LLM-as-a-Judge** (inspired by Yuan et al., 2024): The current policy is prompted to judge its own generated responses using a discrete 0–5 scoring rubric (prompt template in Appendix E, Figure 6), and the highest- and lowest-scoring responses form preference pairs for the next DPO round. No external judge model is used. This tests whether the model can self-improve through explicit prompted evaluation rather than implicit rewards.
  - **PPO with Implicit Reward** (Appendix D, Table 8): Uses the implicit reward model from zephyr-7B-beta as the reward function for PPO training, testing whether implicit rewards can be used in reward-model-based methods. This is a secondary baseline since DICE's primary comparison is against DPO-based alternatives.
  In the reward model comparison (Section 4.4), two additional reward model baselines are introduced: an **Internal Reward Model (IntlRM)** trained exclusively on the UltraFeedback dataset (60k preference pairs) using the OpenRLHF framework, and **ERM-555k**, an external reward model trained by OpenRLHF maintainers on 555k preference pairs. The scalar reward model for the internal comparison is evaluated alongside the DPO implicit reward and a PPO-based variant (in the appendix).

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper measures compute implicitly through the **number of iterations** and the **dataset size per round** rather than through FLOPs or generation counts. In each DICE iteration, the current policy generates K = 16 responses for each of approximately 10k prompts (from the UltraFeedback subset), producing roughly 160k responses that must be scored by the implicit reward model. DPO training then proceeds for 300 steps on a dataset of 9,600 preference pairs with batch size 32. All experiments use 8 Nvidia A100 GPUs. The fixed budget of 300 training steps per round and 9,600 preference pairs ensures fair comparison across methods: every baseline (Offline DPO, LLM-as-a-Judge, DICE) receives the same number of gradient updates on the same-sized dataset, with differences arising only from dataset composition. The hyperparameter β (DPO KL penalty coefficient) is hypertuned from `{0.01, 0.1}` for each method and model separately. For DICE specifically, the experience replay ratio γ is additionally hypertuned.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not use traditional k-fold cross-validation on evaluation benchmarks. Instead, hyperparameter selection (β for all methods, γ for DICE) is performed using performance on AlpacaEval 2 as the selection criterion, with the selected hyperparameters then applied to Arena-Hard without further tuning. To address concerns about overfitting hyperparameters to AlpacaEval 2, the paper treats the two benchmarks as mutual validation-test pairs: it validates on AlpacaEval 2 and tests on Arena-Hard, then reverses roles (Appendix F). The optimal γ identified on one benchmark consistently achieves the best performance on the other across all four validation-test instances (Tables 9 and 10), demonstrating robustness to the choice of validation benchmark. For the length regularization coefficient α⋆, the optimization is performed via Bayesian optimization with a Gaussian process (Appendix C) directly on the constructed dataset's length difference statistic, with no model training involved in the search procedure—the objective landscape (Appendix C, Figure 4, left) shows a clear minimum that the optimizer finds quickly.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Bootstrapping with Implicit Rewards Substantially Outperforms All Baselines Using Only Initial Training Resources

Table 1 presents the central comparison across both base models and both evaluation benchmarks. For the Zephyr setting, DICE achieves an **LC win rate of 20.71% at Iteration 2** on AlpacaEval 2, compared to the base model's 12.69%—an absolute improvement of **8.02 percentage points** (63% relative). For the Llama3 setting, DICE reaches **27.55% LC at Iteration 2**, compared to 18.20% for the base model—an improvement of **9.35 percentage points** (51% relative). The raw win rate improvements are even larger: from 10.71% to 20.16% for Zephyr, and from 15.50% to 30.99% for Llama3.

Comparing DICE to baselines at Iteration 2 on AlpacaEval 2 (Zephyr setting): Offline DPO degrades to 4.96% LC (worse than the base model's 12.69%); Offline DPO with new reference drops to 4.58% LC; LLM-as-a-Judge achieves only 14.14% LC—a gap of **6.57 percentage points** below DICE. For the Llama3 setting, the gap is similar: Offline DPO reaches 21.04% LC at Iteration 2 (below DICE's 27.55%), and LLM-as-a-Judge reaches only 21.80% LC (a **5.75 percentage point gap**). PPO with implicit reward (Appendix D, Table 8, Zephyr only) achieves only 13.32% LC, barely above the base model's 12.69%, suggesting that the implicit reward's effectiveness in DICE is tightly coupled to the DPO training objective rather than being a general-purpose reward function for any RL algorithm.

On Arena-Hard (Table 1), DICE similarly dominates. For Zephyr: DICE Iteration 2 achieves 18.45% LC versus LLM-as-a-Judge Iteration 2 at 13.47% and Offline DPO Iteration 2 at 1.89%. For Llama3: DICE Iteration 2 reaches 39.13% LC versus LLM-as-a-Judge at 27.99% and Offline DPO at 24.04%. The Arena-Hard results are notable because, per Appendix F, the hyperparameters were selected on AlpacaEval 2 and applied directly to Arena-Hard without further tuning—the strong performance on this separate benchmark supports the claim that DICE's gains are not an artifact of overfitting hyperparameters to a single evaluation set.

#### The Best DICE Variant Competes Favorably with Much Larger Proprietary Models

Table 2 positions DICE-Llama3 Iteration 2 (27.55% LC, 30.99% WR) on the AlpacaEval 2 leaderboard relative to published models, all evaluated under the same protocol. DICE-Llama3 outperforms GPT-3.5 Turbo 0613 (22.35% LC), GPT-3.5 Turbo 1106 (19.30% LC), Llama 3 8B Instruct (22.92% LC), Mixtral 8×7B v0.1 (23.69% LC), and Gemini Pro (24.38% LC). It also exceeds Snorkel's Mistral-PairRM-DPO (26.39% LC) which uses an external reward model. The model trails only GPT-4 0613 (30.18% LC), Mistral Medium (28.61% LC), and Claude 2 (28.15% LC)—all substantially larger or more expensively trained models. For Zephyr, DICE Iteration 2 (20.71% LC) surpasses GPT-3.5 Turbo 1106 (19.30% LC) and approaches GPT-3.5 Turbo 0613 (22.35% LC). These comparisons use the base model sizes (7B/8B parameters) against models with up to 70B+ parameters (Tulu 2+DPO 70B achieves only 21.24% LC) or proprietary training budgets.

Critically, all DICE variants achieve these results using only the data from the initial DPO training of the base model—no additional human annotations, no external reward models, no larger teacher models for distillation. The paper emphasizes (Table 2 caption, Section 4.2) that DICE-Llama3 "performs better than the official instruct version of Llama3 by a non-trivial margin, 4.63%," which is notable because the official instruct version presumably received substantially more alignment engineering than a single round of DPO on UltraFeedback followed by DICE bootstrapping.

#### Performance Saturates and Then Degrades After Two Bootstrapping Rounds

Appendix B (Table 6) reports results for three DICE iterations on both base models. Zephyr-DICE reaches 20.71% LC at Iteration 2 but drops to **14.02% LC at Iteration 3**—worse than both Iteration 2 and Iteration 1 (19.03%). Llama3-DICE similarly peaks at 27.55% LC at Iteration 2 and drops to **20.61% LC at Iteration 3**, below the Iteration 1 performance (25.08%). The LLM-as-a-Judge baseline shows the same pattern: Zephyr peaks at 15.36% LC at Iteration 1 and declines to 13.95% at Iteration 3; Llama3 peaks at 21.80% at Iteration 2 and drops to 16.86% at Iteration 3.

This saturation-and-collapse pattern is a negative result that bounds the method's practical utility. The paper notes (Appendix B) that "this is a known challenge in the literature; both self-rewarding language model (Yuan et al., 2024) and SPPO (Wu et al., 2024) show successful improvement only up to three iterations, which corresponds to two iterations in our approach, as our method begins after one initial iteration of DPO training." The paper hypothesizes that using a fixed prompt set across iterations may contribute to the degradation, since other works vary prompts across rounds, but this remains speculative. The practical implication is that DICE provides approximately one to two rounds of meaningful improvement beyond the initial DPO training, after which additional bootstrapping becomes counterproductive.

#### DICE-Generated Data Improves Other Direct Alignment Algorithms, Though DPO Benefits Most

Table 3 investigates whether the preference dataset generated by DICE (at Iteration 1, Zephyr setting) can improve the base model when used with DAP algorithms other than DPO: IPO (Azar et al., 2024), KTO (Ethayarajh et al., 2024), and Hinge loss (Zhao et al., 2023). All algorithms are trained on the identical DICE-generated dataset and compared against the same algorithm trained on the offline UltraFeedback dataset. For DPO, the DICE dataset improves LC from 13.40% (offline) to 19.03%—a gain of 5.63 percentage points. For IPO: 14.83% (offline) → 18.51% (DICE), gain of 3.68 points. For KTO: 13.92% → 14.88%, gain of 0.96 points. For Hinge: 13.51% → 15.92%, gain of 2.41 points. All four algorithms show higher LC win rates with the DICE dataset than with the offline dataset, demonstrating that DICE's data generation process produces preference pairs that are useful beyond the specific DPO training objective. However, DPO shows the largest absolute improvement, consistent with the fact that the implicit reward used to label the dataset is directly derived from the DPO objective's parameterization.

#### The DPO Implicit Reward Is More Accurate Than Comparable Scalar Reward Models on Self-Generated Data

Table 5 (Section 4.4) compares how well different reward models align with GPT-4o preference labels when evaluating the model's own generated responses. On 500 sampled `(x, y_1, y_2)` tuples from the first DICE iteration under the Zephyr setting, the DPO implicit reward achieves an **alignment rate of 0.698**, meaning 69.8% of its preference judgments agree with GPT-4o. The Internal Reward Model (IntlRM), trained on the same UltraFeedback dataset (60k pairs) using the OpenRLHF framework with the same base SFT model (mistral-7b-sft-beta), achieves only **0.624**—a gap of 7.4 percentage points. The External Reward Model (ERM-555k), trained on 555k preference pairs (over 9× more data), achieves **0.656**—still 4.2 points below the implicit reward.

This finding has two implications. First, it provides the empirical justification for DICE's core design choice: the implicit reward genuinely discriminates quality on the model's own outputs better than an explicitly trained reward model on the same data, so bootstrapping from the implicit reward is not merely a convenience but a performance advantage. Second, it explains why external reward model baselines are not stronger competitors in this specific self-alignment setting: even with substantially more training data, the scalar reward model faces a distribution shift when evaluating on-policy responses that the implicit reward, being derived from the same parameters generating those responses, does not face. The paper notes (Section 4.4) that "scalar reward models in general excel on a wider range of tasks when the preference data is abundant," citing ArmoRM-Llama3-8B-v0.1 trained on 1M examples, so the implicit reward's advantage is specifically in the self-alignment context where the evaluation distribution matches the reward model's implicit training distribution.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Length-regularized reward shaping (α coefficient)**: Table 4 ablates the length penalty coefficient α across two experience replay ratios (γ = 0.0 and γ = 0.5, Zephyr setting, Iteration 1). With no length regularization (α = 0.0), the model achieves low LC win rates despite high raw win rates: 13.32% LC / 15.37% WR (γ = 0.0) and 15.92% LC / 19.08% WR (γ = 0.5). The average response lengths are 2570 and 2600 characters respectively, indicating severe length exploitation—the model is "winning" on raw win rate by being verbose but losing on length-controlled metrics. With the optimal penalty α⋆ = 0.023 (found by minimizing the absolute length difference in the constructed dataset per Equation 6), the LC win rate jumps to 18.88% (γ = 0.0) and 19.03% (γ = 0.5)—gains of 5.56 and 3.11 percentage points over the unregularized versions, respectively. Average response length drops to 2109 (γ = 0.0) and 1848 (γ = 0.5). With double the optimal penalty (α = 2α⋆ = 0.046), performance degrades substantially: 14.40% LC / 9.08% WR for γ = 0.0, and 14.91% LC / 10.80% WR for γ = 0.5. Average response length drops further to 876 and 1185 characters, but the quality collapse shows that over-penalizing length suppresses genuinely useful content. The key finding is that α⋆ identified by the automated search procedure (minimizing average absolute length difference) consistently yields the best LC win rate, validating the search criterion as a reliable proxy for downstream performance without requiring multiple training runs.

**Comparison of length-regularization approaches (dataset-level vs. loss-level)**: Table 7 compares DICE's dataset-level reward shaping against Park et al. (2024)'s loss-level regularization (R-DPO), both at γ = 0.0 in the Zephyr setting. R-DPO with the author-recommended hyperparameter range (λ ∈ {0.02, 0.05}) achieves LC win rates of 16.30% (λ = 0.02, length 2629 chars) and 16.03% (λ = 0.05, length 2030 chars). DICE achieves 18.88% LC with length 2109 chars—comparable length to R-DPO at λ = 0.05 but 2.85 percentage points higher LC. This demonstrates that correcting length bias at the dataset level produces better quality-length tradeoffs than embedding the correction in the loss function, while also avoiding the expensive hyperparameter sweeps that loss-level methods require (DICE's α⋆ is found by a Bayesian optimizer evaluating dataset statistics, which "takes minutes rather than the hours required for multiple training runs at different λ values," per the paper's discussion in Section 3.1).

**Experience replay ratio (γ)**: Figure 3 (Zephyr setting) sweeps γ ∈ {0.0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1.0} across two DICE iterations, reporting AlpacaEval 2 LC win rate. The curve shows an inverted-U shape peaking at γ = 0.5 with LC win rates of 19.03% (Iteration 1) and 20.71% (Iteration 2). The extremes perform worse: γ = 0.0 (only self-generated data) achieves 18.88% at Iteration 2; γ = 0.25 achieves 17.87%; γ = 0.75 reaches 16.90%; γ = 1.0 (only offline data, equivalent to Offline DPO w/ new ref) drops to 13.40%. The performance gap between the peak (20.71%) and γ = 1.0 (13.40%) is 7.31 percentage points, demonstrating that the self-generated data provides critical on-policy supervision that offline data alone cannot replicate. The gap between the peak and γ = 0.0 (18.88%) is 1.83 points—smaller but still meaningful, showing that pure self-training without offline data anchoring leads to measurable degradation.

Appendix D, Figure 5 replicates this sweep for the Llama3 setting with γ ∈ {0.0, 0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1.0}. The inverted-U pattern persists, but the optimal γ shifts to 0.1, achieving 27.55% LC at Iteration 2. At γ = 0.0, LC is 26.93%; at γ = 1.0, LC drops to 22.50%. The shift in optimal γ from 0.5 (Zephyr) to 0.1 (Llama3) is informative: Llama3's base DPO model is stronger (18.20% LC vs. Zephyr's 12.69%), its implicit reward is likely better calibrated, and its generated data is of higher quality—thus less offline anchoring is needed. This model-dependent optimal ratio suggests that γ should be tuned per base model, but that an intermediate value (not 0, not 1) is universally beneficial.

**Multi-iteration robustness across benchmarks**: Appendix F, Tables 9 and 10 verify that the optimal γ identified on one benchmark (AlpacaEval 2 or Arena-Hard) generalizes to the other, for both Zephyr and Llama3. In all four validation-test instances, the γ that performs best on the validation benchmark also achieves the best score on the test benchmark—with the sole exception being Llama3 where γ = 0.0 and γ = 0.1 tie at 39.1% LC on Arena-Hard, both above γ = 0.25 at 33.3% and higher γ values. This cross-benchmark consistency provides evidence that γ is not overfitting to evaluation-specific artifacts.

**DICE algorithm compatibility**: Table 3 (discussed above in Main Results) serves double duty as an ablation showing that DICE-generated data benefits alignment algorithms beyond DPO. The fact that IPO, KTO, and Hinge loss all show improvements (ranging from +0.96 to +3.68 percentage points LC) when trained on DICE datasets versus offline datasets indicates that the dataset construction process—on-policy generation with length-regularized implicit reward labeling—produces universally useful preference pairs, not merely pairs that exploit DPO-specific optimization dynamics.

**Iteration count beyond two rounds**: Appendix B, Table 6 shows that both DICE and the LLM-as-a-Judge baseline degrade at Iteration 3. This is a negative result bounding the method's effective range. The paper discusses this as a limitation (Section 5) and an open question. Notably, both methods degrade, suggesting the saturation is a property of fixed-prompt-set iterative self-training rather than something specific to implicit rewards.

**PPO with implicit rewards (supplementary ablation)**: Appendix D, Table 8 tests whether the implicit reward model can be used with PPO rather than DPO. Starting from zephyr-7B-beta, PPO with the implicit reward achieves only 13.32% LC (vs. 12.69% base, a marginal 0.63 point gain), compared to DPO which achieves 19.03% LC. The paper notes this is not a fully optimized PPO run ("PPO's performance can potentially be enhanced with additional hyperparameter tuning, but such efforts would require substantial resources and time"), but the stark gap suggests that the implicit reward's effectiveness is tightly coupled to the DPO objective, consistent with the fact that the implicit reward parameterization is derived from the DPO loss's analytical structure.

### Critical Assessment

#### Does the Paper Demonstrate That DICE Enables Significant Self-Improvement Without External Feedback?

The core claim—that a DPO-tuned model can improve by more than 8% LC on AlpacaEval 2 using only its implicit reward and initial training data—is **supported by the data in Table 1, with important scope constraints**. Zephyr-DICE achieves +8.02% LC (12.69 → 20.71) and Llama3-DICE achieves +9.35% (18.20 → 27.55) at Iteration 2. These gains are measured against the base DPO-tuned model, which itself was trained on UltraFeedback, meaning the total improvement is from the SFT starting point through initial DPO plus two bootstrapping rounds. A fair interpretation is that DICE extracts additional alignment value from the same preference data, roughly doubling the LC win rate in the Zephyr case and achieving a 51% relative improvement for Llama3. The comparison against Offline DPO degradation (4.96% LC at Iteration 2 for Zephyr) demonstrates that the improvement comes specifically from the on-policy self-labeled data, not from additional training per se.

**However, the "without external feedback" claim requires careful scoping.** DICE does not use external feedback *for preference labeling during bootstrapping*, but it does depend on external feedback for the initial DPO training of the base model (the UltraFeedback dataset). The paper is transparent about this (Section 1: "without relying on external feedback" refers to the bootstrapping phase specifically), but the phrase "self-alignment" in the title could be misread as implying no human preferences at all. More precisely, DICE is a method for maximizing the utility of a *given* set of human preference data, not for alignment from scratch. The base models start at 12.69% and 18.20% LC—substantially above random—because of prior investment in human annotation. DICE amplifies that investment but does not replace it.

The evaluation benchmarks themselves constitute a form of external feedback used for hyperparameter selection (β and γ are selected based on AlpacaEval 2 performance). The cross-validation between AlpacaEval 2 and Arena-Hard (Appendix F) mitigates this concern by showing that parameters selected on one benchmark transfer effectively to the other, but a completely blind deployment would require a different hyperparameter selection strategy (perhaps based on training dynamics or a small held-out validation set from the preference data). The paper does not provide a recipe for hyperparameter selection without evaluation benchmarks, which limits the "no external feedback" framing in practice.

#### Does the Paper Demonstrate That Length-Regularized Reward Shaping Is Necessary and Sufficient?

**The necessity is strongly supported.** Table 4 shows that without length regularization (α = 0.0), the LC win rate is substantially lower (13.32% for γ = 0.0, 15.92% for γ = 0.5) despite higher raw win rates, because the model exploits the evaluator's length bias. The +1031 character average length difference in the vanilla implicit reward dataset (Figure 2, top) quantifies the severity of the bias. DICE would be ineffective without the length correction.

**The sufficiency of a simple additive linear penalty is less thoroughly demonstrated.** The paper shows that α⋆ found by minimizing the average absolute length difference yields the best downstream performance (Table 4), but it does not compare this linear penalty formulation against alternative debiasing approaches—for instance, normalizing implicit rewards by response length, using percentile-based selection rather than argmax/argmin, or applying more complex reward transformations. The linear penalty `−α|y|` implicitly assumes length bias is additive and proportional to character count, which may not capture more complex interactions (e.g., length bias may be stronger for certain response types or prompt categories). The comparison with Park et al. (2024) in Table 7 shows DICE's dataset-level approach outperforms loss-level regularization, but this compares *where* the correction is applied (dataset vs. loss), not *how* the correction is formulated.

**The α⋆ optimization procedure itself is efficient but has an unexamined dependency.** The Bayesian optimization relies on the objective landscape having a clear minimum (which it does, per Appendix C Figure 4, left), but the paper does not investigate whether the optimal α⋆ is stable across different prompt sets, different numbers of generated responses K, or different random seeds in the generation process. If α⋆ varies substantially with these factors, the black-box search would need to be repeated for each deployment context, partially undermining the "no expensive hyperparameter tuning" advantage over loss-level methods.

#### Does the Paper Demonstrate That Experience Replay Prevents Catastrophic Forgetting?

**The evidence is clear that an intermediate γ outperforms extremes, but the mechanism is inferred rather than directly demonstrated.** The inverted-U curves in Figure 3 and Appendix D Figure 5 show that γ = 0.5 (Zephyr) and γ = 0.1 (Llama3) achieve the highest LC win rates, with both lower and higher values degrading performance. The interpretation—that self-generated data provides on-policy supervision while offline data anchors to human preferences—is consistent with these curves.

**However, catastrophic forgetting per se is not directly measured.** The paper does not report how the model's performance on the original UltraFeedback preference pairs changes during bootstrapping (e.g., does its accuracy in predicting held-out human preferences degrade with more self-training rounds?). Nor does it measure whether the model loses general capabilities (e.g., on standard NLP benchmarks) during iterative training. The "forgetting" claim is inferred from the performance drop at γ = 0.0 compared to the optimal γ, but this drop could also be explained by the implicit reward's imperfections leading to suboptimal training signals, rather than by loss of previously learned human preference knowledge. These are related but distinct failure modes: the former is "the model learns the wrong thing from self-labeled data," the latter is "the model forgets what it previously knew from human data." The experience replay mechanism addresses both simultaneously, so the experiment cannot disambiguate which failure mode is the dominant one.

**The optimal γ varies substantially between base models** (0.5 vs. 0.1), indicating that the right mixture ratio depends on model-specific factors—likely the quality of the base model's alignment and the calibration of its implicit reward. This is a practical limitation: γ must be tuned per deployment, and without a validation benchmark, it is unclear how to select it. The paper does not propose a heuristic or automatic method for selecting γ without evaluation data.

#### Does the Comparison Against Scalar Reward Models Support the Advantage of Implicit Rewards?

**The comparison in Table 5 is informative but narrow in scope.** The DPO implicit reward (69.8% alignment rate) outperforms both the IntlRM (62.4%) and ERM-555k (65.6%) on 500 self-generated preference pairs. This supports the claim that implicit rewards are competitive with (or better than) scalar reward models for labeling the policy's own outputs.

**However, the comparison has several constraints that limit the strength of the conclusion:**

- **Single evaluation set**: The 500 pairs are drawn from the first DICE iteration under the Zephyr setting—responses generated by zephyr-7B-beta itself. The advantage of implicit rewards over scalar reward models may be specific to evaluating *self-generated* responses, where the implicit reward benefits from being naturally on-policy. A scalar reward model trained with on-policy data collection (periodically refreshing training data with new policy samples) might close or reverse this gap.

- **GPT-4o as ground truth**: The alignment rate measures agreement with GPT-4o preferences, not with human preferences. If GPT-4o itself has systematic biases (e.g., toward certain response styles), a higher alignment rate may not reflect genuinely better alignment. Both the implicit reward and the scalar reward models are compared against the same GPT-4o labels, so relative comparisons are valid, but the absolute alignment rates should not be interpreted as accuracy against human preferences.

- **Scalar reward model training may not be optimized for this comparison**: The IntlRM is trained using OpenRLHF's recommended hyperparameters, which may not be optimal for this specific data and base model. The paper acknowledges this by noting that PPO performance "can potentially be enhanced with additional hyperparameter tuning," and the same applies to reward model training. The gap of 7.4 points might narrow with more extensive reward model tuning.

**The PPO with implicit rewards result (Table 8, 13.32% LC) is a telling negative result.** It suggests that the implicit reward's utility is not as a general-purpose reward function that can be plugged into any alignment algorithm; rather, it is specifically effective when used within the DPO framework. This makes sense theoretically—the implicit reward parameterization is derived from the DPO analytical solution, so using it with PPO (which optimizes a different objective) may introduce mismatch. But it also means DICE's approach is not modular: you cannot take a DPO-tuned model's implicit reward and use it with your preferred alignment algorithm; you must use DPO.

#### What Would Strengthen the Paper's Claims?

Several experiments are notably absent that would strengthen the paper's conclusions:

- **Scaling with K (number of generated responses per prompt):** All experiments use K = 16. Would fewer responses (K = 4 or 8) still produce useful preference pairs? Would more responses (K = 32 or 64) yield better discrimination, or does the implicit reward's accuracy saturate? This is practically important because generation cost scales linearly with K.

- **Scaling with dataset size:** All experiments use 9,600 preference pairs (from ~10k prompts). How does performance vary with fewer prompts (e.g., 1k or 5k)? Is the benefit of DICE robust to smaller prompt sets, or does it depend on the diversity of the 10k UltraFeedback prompts?

- **Diversity of evaluation:** All main results use AlpacaEval 2 and Arena-Hard—both LLM-as-judge benchmarks. There is no evaluation on standard NLP benchmarks (MMLU, HellaSwag, etc.) to verify that DICE does not degrade general capabilities, and no human evaluation to confirm that the LC win rate improvements correspond to human-perceived quality improvements. LLM-as-judge benchmarks, while widely used, have known limitations including evaluator model biases and imperfect correlation with human judgments.

- **Ablation of the reference model update:** In DICE, the reference model for DPO training is updated each round to the policy from the previous round (πθ(t-1)). What if the reference model were kept fixed at the SFT model throughout all bootstrapping rounds? This ablation would isolate whether the benefit of DICE comes from the on-policy data, from the updated reference, or from their interaction.

- **Stability across random seeds:** The paper does not report error bars or multiple runs with different random seeds for generation or training. With a moderate-sized test set (AlpacaEval 2 has 805 prompts, Arena-Hard has 500) and no reported variance, it is difficult to assess whether the reported differences (e.g., 20.71% vs. 19.03% for different γ values) are statistically reliable.

- **A direct measurement of whether DICE-generated preference labels correlate with human preferences:** The alignment rate against GPT-4o (Table 5) provides an indirect measure. A small-scale human evaluation of the self-labeled preference pairs would ground the method's core assumption—that implicit rewards correspond to genuine quality differences—in human judgment rather than in another model's judgment.

#### Boundary Conditions That the Paper Does Not Explore

The paper's results establish that DICE works for two base models (7B-8B parameters) trained on UltraFeedback and evaluated on benchmark instruction-following tasks. Several boundary conditions remain untested:

- **Model scale:** Would DICE work for smaller models (e.g., 1B parameters) where the implicit reward may be less calibrated? For larger models (70B+) where the base alignment is already strong and further gains may be harder to achieve?

- **Initial alignment quality:** Both base models start from relatively modest LC win rates (12.69% and 18.20%). Would DICE help a model that is already highly aligned (e.g., 30%+ LC), or would the implicit reward have insufficient signal to discriminate among already-high-quality responses?

- **Preference data quality and domain:** UltraFeedback is a carefully curated, diverse preference dataset. Would DICE work with noisier preference data, or with domain-specific preferences (e.g., code generation, medical advice) where the SFT model's knowledge may be more critical to preserve?

- **Prompt diversity:** DICE uses the same prompt set across iterations. Would varying prompts (e.g., using different subsets of UltraFeedback, or synthetic prompts) mitigate the Iteration 3 degradation? The paper hypothesizes this but does not test it.

These untested boundaries do not invalidate the paper's demonstrated results—DICE clearly works in the tested regime—but they define the scope within which the claims should be understood. The paper's title claims "Bootstrapping Language Models with DPO Implicit Rewards" without qualification, but the evidence supports a more specific claim: bootstrapping 7-8B DPO-tuned models on general instruction-following data for one to two additional rounds, with careful hyperparameter selection informed by evaluation benchmarks.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Method Saturates and Degrades After Two Bootstrapping Rounds

**The constraint.** DICE provides approximately two rounds of meaningful improvement before performance collapses. The paper demonstrates this clearly in Appendix B (Table 6): Zephyr-DICE reaches 20.71% LC at Iteration 2 but drops to **14.02% LC at Iteration 3**—below both Iteration 2 and Iteration 1 (19.03%). Llama3-DICE peaks at 27.55% LC at Iteration 2 and drops to **20.61% LC at Iteration 3**, below the Iteration 1 baseline (25.08%). The same pattern holds for the LLM-as-a-Judge baseline: Zephyr decreases from 15.36% (Iteration 1) to 13.95% (Iteration 3), and Llama3 drops from 21.80% (Iteration 2) to 16.86% (Iteration 3).

The paper does not treat this as a surprise—it acknowledges that "this is a known challenge in the literature; both self-rewarding language model (Yuan et al., 2024) and SPPO (Wu et al., 2024) show successful improvement only up to three iterations, which corresponds to two iterations in our approach, as our method begins after one initial iteration of DPO training" (Appendix B). The authors hypothesize that using a fixed prompt set across all iterations may contribute to the degradation: "we use a fixed prompt set in every iteration, whereas other works employ different prompt sets across iterations" (Appendix B).

**The consequence.** DICE is not a method for unbounded self-improvement. After two bootstrapping rounds, the implicit reward's utility as a preference signal is exhausted—further rounds amplify residual errors in the implicit reward rather than surfacing genuine quality improvements. A practitioner cannot simply run DICE for more rounds to get better results; the algorithm has a hard ceiling, and exceeding it actively degrades performance. This constrains the total alignment gain achievable through bootstrapping alone, regardless of how much compute one is willing to spend on additional rounds.

**What evidence exists.** The Iteration 3 degradation is documented in Appendix B (Table 6) for both base models and for both DICE and the LLM-as-a-Judge baseline. The fact that both methods degrade suggests the saturation is a property of fixed-prompt-set iterative self-training rather than something specific to implicit rewards. The paper does not provide diagnostic measurements (e.g., implicit reward calibration at Iteration 3, or the distribution of generated response quality) that would explain *why* performance drops.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not resolve this limitation. It flags varying prompts across iterations as a potential remedy ("We hypothesize that using different prompt sets across iterations could help mitigate the performance drop observed in the third iteration," Appendix B) but does not test this hypothesis. The limitation is explicitly listed in Section 5 ("Limitation and Future Work"): "Another limitation is the lack of continued improvement over many iterations... This issue highlights an open question within this field regarding the iterative enhancement of policy models." No concrete mitigation is proposed.

### Difficulty Estimation via Length-Regularized Reward Shaping Depends on an Unvalidated Assumption About Linearity

**The constraint.** The length-regularized reward shaping formula (Equation 5) assumes that length bias in the implicit reward is **additive and linear in character count**: `r_LR(x, y; α) = β log π_θ(y|x)/π_ref(y|x) − α|y|`. The penalty is a single scalar `α` multiplied by the response's character length, applied uniformly to all responses regardless of prompt type, response style, or content. The automatic optimization of `α⋆` (Equation 6) minimizes the average absolute length difference between winners and losers in the constructed dataset, which implicitly treats length bias as a dataset-level statistic that can be corrected by a global penalty coefficient.

**The consequence.** If length bias is not well-modeled by a linear additive penalty—for instance, if longer responses are disproportionately preferred at some lengths but not others, or if length bias interacts with prompt category (e.g., factual questions vs. creative writing)—then `α⋆` may over-correct for some types of responses and under-correct for others. Table 4 provides suggestive evidence of this tension: the automatic `α⋆ = 0.023` achieves the best LC win rate (18.88–19.03%), but the response length varies substantially depending on the experience replay ratio (2109 chars at γ = 0.0 vs. 1848 chars at γ = 0.5), indicating that `α⋆`'s effect is influenced by factors beyond the length difference statistic it optimizes. Doubling α to 0.046 drives response length down to 876–1185 characters but causes a sharp quality drop (LC win rates of 14.40–14.91%), suggesting that length and quality are not cleanly separable by a single linear coefficient—there exist responses whose length is genuinely informative and penalizing them damages quality.

The paper does not investigate whether the optimal α varies across prompt categories, response types, or iterations. The optimization in Appendix C (Figure 4, left) shows a well-behaved objective landscape for the aggregate dataset, but this aggregate smoothness could mask heterogeneity across subpopulations—some prompts may require stronger length correction, others weaker or none. A global α⋆ is a one-size-fits-all solution to what may be a context-dependent problem.

**What evidence exists.** The paper provides aggregated evidence that the linear penalty works (Table 4 shows α⋆ outperforms α = 0 and α = 2α⋆; Figure 2 shows the length difference distribution becomes approximately unbiased), but provides no disaggregated evidence that it works equally well across all prompt types. The paper does not report length-controlled win rate broken down by prompt category, nor does it analyze whether α⋆ is stable across different subsets of the prompt distribution, different random seeds for response generation, or different values of K (the number of generated responses per prompt). The optimization procedure's sensitivity to these factors is unexplored.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation directly. The choice of a linear additive penalty appears to be motivated by simplicity and by the success of the optimization procedure in finding a well-defined α⋆. The paper frames the dataset-level approach as superior to loss-level regularization (Park et al., 2024) because it decouples length correction from training, but this framing does not address whether the specific functional form of the correction (linear penalty) is appropriate. The paper does not propose or compare against alternative formulations (e.g., length-normalized rewards, non-linear penalties, or per-category α values). This limitation is not explicitly acknowledged.

### The Experience Replay Ratio γ Requires External Benchmark Evaluation to Tune, Undermining the "No External Feedback" Framing

**The constraint.** The paper states that DICE enables self-alignment "without relying on any external feedback" (Section 1, contributions). However, the mixing ratio γ between self-generated and offline data is a critical hyperparameter that must be selected per base model: the optimal γ is **0.5 for Zephyr** (Figure 3) but **0.1 for Llama3** (Appendix D, Figure 5). These values were determined by sweeping γ across a range ({0.0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1.0} for Zephyr; {0.0, 0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1.0} for Llama3) and selecting the value that maximizes AlpacaEval 2 LC win rate (Section 4.1, "Training Details").

**The consequence.** In a deployment scenario without access to an evaluation benchmark, a practitioner has no principled way to select γ. Choosing the wrong value carries a substantial penalty: for Zephyr Iteration 2, using γ = 0.0 yields 18.88% LC vs. the optimal 20.71% (a loss of 1.83 points), while γ = 1.0 yields 13.40% (a loss of 7.31 points). For Llama3 Iteration 2, γ = 0.0 yields 26.93% vs. the optimal 27.55% (a loss of 0.62 points), while γ = 1.0 yields 22.50% (a loss of 5.05 points). The sensitivity is asymmetric—too little offline data (low γ) causes moderate degradation, while too much (high γ) causes severe degradation—but in both cases, selecting γ blindly would leave performance on the table or actively harm it.

The paper's cross-validation between AlpacaEval 2 and Arena-Hard (Appendix F, Tables 9 and 10) demonstrates that γ selected on one benchmark generalizes to the other, but both benchmarks are external evaluation resources. The method provides no intrinsic signal (e.g., training loss, implicit reward calibration, or a held-out validation subset of the preference data) for selecting γ without an evaluation benchmark. This creates a tension with the "without external feedback" framing: the algorithm itself does not require external feedback for preference labeling, but deploying it effectively in practice does require external feedback for hyperparameter selection. The difference between "no external feedback for labeling" and "no external feedback at all" matters for real-world applicability.

**What evidence exists.** The γ sensitivity is documented in Figure 3 (Zephyr) and Appendix D Figure 5 (Llama3). The model-dependence of the optimal γ is implicit in these figures—the fact that the optimum shifts from 0.5 to 0.1 across base models is not highlighted as a limitation by the paper, but it forces per-model hyperparameter search. The cross-benchmark validation in Appendix F provides partial mitigation by showing γ is robust to which external benchmark is used, but does not address the question of how to select γ without *any* external benchmark.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not acknowledge this as a limitation and does not propose an intrinsic method for selecting γ. The "Limitation and Future Work" section (Section 5) focuses on the need for a well-trained initial DPO model and the iteration saturation problem, but does not mention hyperparameter selection. This is a gap between the paper's stated contribution (self-alignment without external feedback) and the practical requirements for deploying the method.

### The Method Is Validated on Only Two Model Families and One Preference Dataset, with No Evidence of Cross-Domain Generalization

**The constraint.** All experiments use models DPO-trained on the UltraFeedback dataset (Cui et al., 2023) and evaluated on AlpacaEval 2 and Arena-Hard—both of which test general instruction-following ability with LLM-based judges. The two base models (Zephyr-7B-beta based on Mistral-7B, and Llama-3-8B-DPO) share key properties: both are in the 7–8B parameter range, both were DPO-trained following the Zephyr pipeline (Tunstall et al., 2023), and both were trained on the same preference dataset. The evaluations are limited to two LLM-as-judge benchmarks; there are no results on standard NLP benchmarks (MMLU, HellaSwag, GSM8K, HumanEval), no human evaluation of response quality, and no domain-specific evaluations (code, math, medical, legal).

**The consequence.** The paper's central claim—that DICE enables significant improvement "across different base models" (Section 1)—is supported only for two models of similar scale trained on the same data with the same pipeline. There is no evidence that DICE would work for: (a) substantially smaller models (e.g., 1–3B parameters) where the implicit reward may be less well-calibrated; (b) substantially larger models (e.g., 70B+) where the base alignment is already strong and further gains may be harder to achieve; (c) models trained on different preference data (e.g., domain-specific preferences, or datasets with different annotation methodologies); (d) models trained with different alignment pipelines (e.g., RLHF-PPO rather than DPO as the initial alignment step).

Moreover, without general capability benchmarks, it is impossible to determine whether DICE's alignment improvements come at the cost of degraded performance on other tasks—a common concern with alignment fine-tuning. The paper does not report whether the bootstrapped models maintain their base models' performance on knowledge recall, reasoning, or code generation. If DICE improves AlpacaEval 2 scores while reducing MMLU accuracy (a known tradeoff in alignment research), that would significantly change the cost-benefit calculus for practitioners.

The exclusive reliance on LLM-as-judge evaluation is a further concern. AlpacaEval 2 uses GPT-4-Turbo as judge, and Arena-Hard uses Mistral-Large-Instruct-2407. Both are model-based evaluations that may have their own biases (length bias, style bias, positional bias). The paper uses length-controlled win rate to address length bias in the evaluator, but other evaluator biases are not controlled for. Without human evaluation, it is unknown whether the reported 8–9% LC win rate improvements translate to human-perceived quality improvements.

**What evidence exists.** The paper's entire experimental section (Section 4) is confined to the UltraFeedback → Zephyr/Llama3 → AlpacaEval 2/Arena-Hard pipeline. The authors do not claim broader generalization; the limitation is one of scope rather than a failed claim. The paper states in the abstract that "DICE enables significant (more than 8% LC win rate increase on AlpacaEval 2) improvement in alignment with different base models" which is factually accurate for the two base models tested, but the phrase "different base models" may imply more diversity than the Mistral-7B and Llama-3-8B comparison provides (both are similarly-sized open-weight models from the same era).

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not acknowledge this as a limitation and does not discuss generalization to other domains, model scales, or training pipelines. The "Future Work" section (Section 5) mentions investigating DPO variants (KTO, IPO) and developing theoretical understanding, but does not mention broader empirical validation. A practitioner considering DICE for a domain-specific application (e.g., medical QA or code generation) or a different model family would need to run their own validation from scratch, as the paper provides no guidance on what properties of the base model or data determine DICE's effectiveness.

### The On-Policy Data Generation Cost Is High and Not Factored into Efficiency Claims

**The constraint.** Each DICE iteration requires the current policy to generate K = 16 responses for each of approximately 10,000 prompts (the subset of UltraFeedback used as the prompt set), producing roughly **160,000 responses** that must each be scored by the implicit reward model (requiring a forward pass through both the policy and reference models to compute log-probabilities). This generation and scoring step is computationally intensive—the paper uses 8 Nvidia A100 GPUs for all experiments (Section 4.1, "Training Details")—and represents a substantial fraction of the total compute budget per iteration. After generation, DPO training proceeds for 300 steps on 9,600 preference pairs with batch size 32—a relatively lightweight training phase compared to the generation phase.

**The consequence.** The headline efficiency claim—that DICE improves alignment "without relying on external feedback"—obscures the computational cost of generating that self-feedback. While DICE eliminates the need for *human annotation* during bootstrapping, it substitutes it with *model inference* cost that scales linearly with the number of prompts, the number of responses per prompt (K), and the number of iterations. For a practitioner with a fixed compute budget, the decision to run DICE involves trading off the cost of generating and scoring 160,000 responses per iteration against the expected alignment gain. The paper provides no analysis of this tradeoff—no scaling curves showing how performance varies with K, with the number of prompts, or with the generation temperature. A practitioner cannot determine from the paper whether the same compute spent on other alignment strategies (e.g., generating more preference data for the initial DPO training, or using a larger base model) would yield better returns.

The fixed K = 16 choice is particularly notable. The paper states that "we sample with different random seeds to get K = 16 diverse responses for each prompt" (Section 4.1) but provides no ablation showing whether K = 8 or K = 4 would produce similarly effective preference pairs at lower cost, or whether K = 32 would yield better discrimination. The implicit reward's ability to identify the best and worst responses likely depends on K—with too few responses, the winner and loser may be similar in quality, producing a weak training signal; with too many, the marginal benefit of additional responses may diminish. This tradeoff is unexplored.

**What evidence exists.** The paper documents the generation parameters (K = 16, temperature settings per model, Section 4.1) and hardware (8 A100 GPUs, Section 4.1), but does not report wall-clock time, FLOP counts, or any cost analysis. There is no ablation over K, no experiment varying the number of prompts, and no comparison of DICE's total compute cost against alternative methods. The paper's efficiency framing is entirely in terms of annotation cost ("without external feedback"), not computational cost.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not acknowledge this as a limitation and does not analyze the cost-performance tradeoff. This is a significant gap for practitioners because the generation phase—not the training phase—likely dominates the total compute budget for DICE, and without guidance on how to set K, practitioners must either replicate the paper's exact setting or guess. A cost analysis (or at minimum an ablation over K) would substantially increase the paper's practical value.

### The Method's Effectiveness Hinges on the Quality of the Initial DPO Training, Which Is Untested as a Variable

**The constraint.** DICE assumes the existence of a DPO-tuned base model and its corresponding reference model from the initial DPO training. The paper explicitly states this as a limitation: "One of the primary limitations of our work is the reliance on the DPO training prior to bootstrapping. If the implicit reward model is not well-trained, it can lead to a collapse of the training pipeline. This challenge is not unique to our approach; the classic RLHF pipeline is also struggling when its reward model is not well-trained" (Section 5).

**The consequence.** The implicit reward's quality as a preference labeler is bounded by the quality of the initial DPO training. If the initial DPO alignment is weak—for instance, because the preference data is noisy, the SFT base model is poorly calibrated, or the DPO training hyperparameters are suboptimal—then the implicit reward will be a poor preference signal, and bootstrapping from it may amplify errors rather than correcting them. The paper demonstrates DICE's effectiveness starting from two specific base models (Zephyr-7B-beta at 12.69% LC and Llama-3-8B-DPO at 18.20% LC) that were carefully trained by their respective creators, but provides no evidence about how DICE performs when the initial DPO training is weaker or stronger than these baselines. In particular: (a) would DICE recover from a poorly-trained initial DPO model, or would it compound the errors? (b) would DICE provide meaningful gains from an already-strong base model (e.g., 30%+ LC), or would the implicit reward lack sufficient signal to discriminate among high-quality responses?

The experience replay mechanism provides partial robustness by mixing in human-labeled offline data, but the paper's acknowledgment that a poorly-trained implicit reward "can lead to a collapse of the training pipeline" suggests that experience replay alone is insufficient to rescue a severely miscalibrated implicit reward. The optimal γ values (0.5 for Zephyr, 0.1 for Llama3) indicate that even with well-trained base models, a substantial fraction of self-labeled data is used—and if those labels are unreliable, training will suffer.

**What evidence exists.** The limitation is explicitly acknowledged in Section 5. The paper does not vary the quality of the initial DPO training—both base models are publicly available, well-regarded checkpoints. There is no ablation where DICE is applied to a deliberately under-trained DPO model, or to a model at different points along the DPO training trajectory. The paper does not measure the implicit reward's calibration as a function of initial DPO training quality, nor does it propose diagnostics for determining whether a given base model's implicit reward is reliable enough for bootstrapping.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges this limitation explicitly but does not mitigate it. The "Future Work" section (Section 5) does not propose methods for assessing or improving implicit reward quality before bootstrapping. A practitioner with a custom DPO-trained model has no guidance on whether DICE will help or harm—they must try it and evaluate externally. This is a practical barrier to adoption, as running DICE is computationally expensive (see previous limitation) and failure is costly. A lightweight diagnostic (e.g., the implicit reward's agreement rate with a small held-out set of human preferences, or the variance of implicit rewards across multiple samples per prompt) would substantially improve deployability, but no such diagnostic is proposed or evaluated.
