Here are the takeaways from this Ryan Peterman post about the best manager he's had (a retrospective on eight managers, distilling what made one stand out):

**Transparent Communication**

The trait Ryan finds most important is that his best manager was transparent with him. This has many benefits but is "especially important for career growth conversations." If your manager doesn't talk about your progress to the next level, it makes it difficult for you to plan your path. Ryan contrasts this with a friend who "never had a clear picture of what he needed to do to get to Senior (IC5) at Meta"—that friend was strung along for years without clear guidance until he called it quits. Ryan never had that experience because his manager made it clear where he stood.

Transparency is also critical for learning. A manager should share feedback to help you grow, and doing it "early and often" makes it much less uncomfortable than letting things build up.

**Technical Competency**

Another trait that stands out is technical competency—"he knows what is going on." Ryan lays out downstream benefits of this. The first is better judgment: in conversations about the team's direction or ongoing work, "what he says is on point in discussions with engineers." The second is better representation: understanding engineering helps him speak about the work during performance reviews, so "engineers doing challenging work in less understood domains still get the ratings they deserve." There are also team health benefits because the engineers respect him more than if he didn't know what was going on.

**Holding a High Bar**

Ryan remembers one of his first halves with the team being frustrated because he got a lower rating than he expected. He compared his work to some peers on a different team who got higher ratings. In retrospect, he realizes his manager was right—"my work deserved the rating I got." Over time he's seen that his manager "doesn't give people more credit than they deserve." This increases the team's talent density because everyone focuses on doing work that has more impact, since that's what gets rewarded. "The bar is high but fair, which keeps high performers motivated and helps them grow."

**Doesn't Oversell**

Ryan's best manager "never oversells anything." This makes him more credible, which increases his influence. If your manager has credibility like this, they'll succeed more often when they advocate for you. At another company Ryan worked at, he had the opposite happen—a manager who oversold things, making engineers "resentful because promotions kept failing to land."

**Focuses on What Matters**

Ryan's best manager "always focuses on what matters." He can think of many cases where politics or processes were slowing engineers on the team down, and at large companies "it's easy for this to happen silently if you don't fight it." His manager always recommended focusing on "making the technology better and improving the user experience." If process or politics got in the way, he'd shield the team from these things to help them focus on engineering.

When you're evaluating a new team, Ryan advises asking people already on that team about your potential manager. "Engineers are usually transparent about their experience which is helpful signal."

---

Ryan's best-manager traits form a self-reinforcing loop: the manager's **technical competency** earns him the respect needed to **hold a high bar** credibly, which in turn fuels the **transparent communication** that keeps engineers aligned on what "high" actually means. His refusal to **oversell** preserves the credibility he needs when he does advocate for someone in a calibration room—the same advocacy that's only possible because he deeply understands the work well enough to **represent it accurately**. The whole system works because he **focuses on what matters**, shielding engineers from noise so they can do the impact-driven work that his high-but-fair bar demands.
