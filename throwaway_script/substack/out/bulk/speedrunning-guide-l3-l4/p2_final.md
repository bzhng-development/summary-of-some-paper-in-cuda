Here are the takeaways from this Ryan Peterman guide on accelerating from Junior (L3) to Mid-Level (L4) engineer:

**Critical logistics: lagging promotions and the manager factor**

Two realities govern big-tech promotions. The first is the **“lagging” promotion**—you must prove you’re already operating at the next level *before* you get the title, which reduces the risk of floundering post-promotion. In practice, that means at least **six months of consistent performance at L4 expectations** before the promo conversation can even be real. The second is that your **manager plays a huge role**—they build your case, advocate for you in calibration, and shape how your work is perceived. Since the L3→L4 bar is relatively straightforward, your manager’s perspective on your readiness is likely accurate. The play, then, is to **align with your manager** on whether your performance already reads as L4-level—don’t guess, and don’t rely on a peer’s assurance.

**The strategy: declare intent and optimize for dev velocity**

The core strategy is simple: meet L4 expectations as fast as you can, then sustain them for six months. Two things unlock speed. First, **be forward with your manager** about wanting to promote—they can tee you up with L4-scope projects, clarify the process, and stress-test your readiness in real time. Second, **optimize for dev velocity**: landing code quickly creates a shorter feedback loop that accelerates your learning, and that compounds into the ability to ship L4-scope work and proactively improve the codebase. The timeline is purely a function of how fast you close the gap. A **one-half promotion** is exceptional—it requires hitting L4 expectations immediately while onboarding, which is most realistic for a high-performing return intern who skips ramp-up and already carries some L4 track record. A **two-half promotion** is the ambitious-but-reasonable target: one half to onboard and start meeting L4 expectations, a second half to build the six-month track record.

**Ryan’s growth path: H1 “Exceeds” to H2 “Greatly Exceeds”**

Ryan’s own arc is a clean illustration of lagging. In H1 (rated Exceeds Expectations as an L3), he took on any task—“nice-to-have features that others didn’t have time for”—finished them quickly, and earned a larger **pipeline rewrite (L4 scope)** from his tech lead. Outside main projects, he gravitated toward removing dead code and speeding up existing code “because I enjoyed it.” He started hitting L4 expectations in the last few months of the half, but without the six-month track record, promotion didn’t happen. In H2 (rated Greatly Exceeds Expectations and promoted L3→L4), he continued driving that L4-scope project independently with high engineering quality, **originated the idea for a test harness to validate the rewrite**—execution described as “comparable to L5 quality”—and led the company in adding static type annotations that MonkeyType couldn’t. By then he’d delivered L4 scope for well over six months, so the promotion “made sense.”

**What he would have changed: steer one-on-ones toward career, not project updates**

Ryan’s biggest regret is how he used one-on-one time—he spent it on project updates instead of discussing what L4 growth actually looked like. That caused two problems. First, he **spent time on work that wasn’t impactful**, taking whatever landed on his plate rather than filtering for promo-relevant scope. Second, he **didn’t have accurate expectations**: a peer told him his work was L4-level and that he’d promote in his first half; he “didn’t know anything at the time so I took their word for it,” and was blindsided when it didn’t happen—a shock that alignment with his manager would have avoided. Even so, the sheer volume of code he wrote still opened doors: his tech lead trusted him with an L4 project because he’d already shown he could handle it, and some of the craft work he did “for my own personal pleasure” ended up becoming promo evidence. “The more work you do, the luckier you get.”

---

Ryan’s **lagging promotion** frame and his **“be forward with your manager”** tactic are two sides of the same coin: the system rewards demonstrated performance, not potential, and your manager is the one who translates that performance into a promotion case. His regret about using one-on-ones for project updates instead of career alignment is a direct consequence of missing that dynamic early. The through-line across both his H1→H2 arc and his advice is that velocity and volume of work create the surface area for promotable scope, but only if you’ve calibrated with your manager on what *counts*—a version of the same theme he’d later develop into his Staff-level **craft-first, impact-conscious** mindset.
