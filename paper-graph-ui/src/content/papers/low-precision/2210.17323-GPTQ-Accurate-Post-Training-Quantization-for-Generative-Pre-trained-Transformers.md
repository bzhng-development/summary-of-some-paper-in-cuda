# GPTQ: Accurate Post-Training Quantization for Generative Pre-trained Transformers

**ArXiv:** [2210.17323](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.17323)

## 🎯 Pitch

This paper introduces **GPTQ**, a new one-shot post-training weight quantization method based on approximate second-order information that is both highly-accurate and highly-efficient for compressing Generative Pre-trained Transformer models such as OPT and BLOOM.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **GPTQ**, a new one-shot post-training weight quantization method based on approximate second-order information that is both highly-accurate and highly-efficient for compressing Generative Pre-trained Transformer models such as OPT and BLOOM. GPTQ quantizes all weights of the largest openly-available models — OPT-175B and BLOOM-176B — in approximately four GPU hours, reducing the bitwidth to 3 or 4 bits per weight with negligible accuracy degradation (e.g., losing only 0.03 perplexity on WikiText2 for OPT-175B at 4-bit, while the round-to-nearest baseline drops 2.2 points). The method more than doubles the compression gains relative to prior one-shot quantization approaches, enabling for the first time the execution of a 175 billion-parameter model inside a single GPU for generative inference and delivering practical end-to-end speedups of ~3.25× on NVIDIA A100 GPUs and 4.5× on A6000 GPUs, establishing that accurate extreme compression of giant language models is feasible without retraining but yields speedups primarily from reduced memory movement rather than computational reductions.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Inference Is the Bottleneck, Not Training

The fundamental problem this paper tackles is deceptively simple: **how do you actually run a 175-billion-parameter language model in practice?** While much public attention focuses on the staggering cost of training these models — GPT-3 requires hundreds of GPU years,OPT-175B reportedly consumed 992 A100 GPUs for months — the paper argues that even the comparatively "simple" task of inference is highly challenging for a different reason: memory.

The paper frames this concretely in Section 1: the parameters of GPT3-175B occupy 326GB (counting in multiples of 1024) when stored in the standard float16 format. This exceeds the capacity of even the highest-end single GPUs available at the time — an NVIDIA A100 with 80GB of HBM2e memory. Consequently, inference cannot be performed on a single device. It requires **multi-GPU deployments**, which introduce communication overhead, increase system complexity, raise hardware costs, and make these models inaccessible to researchers and practitioners who lack access to large GPU clusters.

This is not merely an engineering inconvenience. The practical result is that the most capable language models — the ones demonstrating breakthrough performance on complex reasoning, few-shot learning, and generative tasks — are **gated behind hardware requirements** that exclude a large fraction of potential users. The paper explicitly states this concern in the introduction:

> "this limits the usability of such models"

The framing matters: the paper is not primarily about improving accuracy or achieving state-of-the-art benchmark scores. It is about **democratizing access** to existing state-of-the-art models by making them physically runnable on hardware that more people and organizations can afford.

### The Specific Gap: No Accurate Compression Method Scales to 100B+ Models

The paper identifies a very specific technical gap in the landscape of model compression. There are numerous methods for reducing model size — quantization, pruning, knowledge distillation — but they break down into two categories, neither of which adequately addresses the problem of compressing models with hundreds of billions of parameters.

**Training-based methods** quantize models during extensive retraining and/or fine-tuning, using approximate differentiation mechanisms for the rounding operation (Gholami et al., 2021; Nagel et al., 2021). These can achieve good results, but the cost is prohibitive: if training the model already required hundreds of GPU years, the prospect of additional training passes — even fine-tuning — is impractical for most users.

**Post-training ("one-shot") methods** quantize a pretrained model using modest resources, typically a few thousand data samples and a few hours of computation. This is precisely what is needed for giant models. The problem, as the paper documents in Section 2, is that **the accurate post-training methods do not scale**, and **the scalable post-training methods are not accurate**.

### Where Existing Post-Training Methods Fall Short

The paper provides a detailed taxonomy of prior post-training quantization methods and explains the specific limitations of each. Let's examine this systematically, because the historical trajectory matters for understanding where GPTQ fits.

#### Accurate-but-Expensive Methods (Vision-Focused, Small Models Only)

The paper identifies a line of work producing high-quality quantization results, but primarily evaluated on vision models with tens of millions of parameters:

**AdaRound** (Nagel et al., 2020): Computes data-dependent rounding by annealing a regularization penalty that encourages weights to move toward quantization grid points. The optimization involves many SGD steps per layer.

**BitSplit** (Wang et al., 2020): Constructs quantized values bit-by-bit using a squared error objective on the residual error after previous bits are fixed.

**AdaQuant** (Hubara et al., 2021): Performs direct optimization of quantized weights using straight-through gradient estimates. The paper notes that it is "the fastest amongst prior PTQ methods" — and yet, as Table 1 shows, it significantly underperforms at 3-bit accuracy (e.g., 59.21% on ResNet18-3bit vs. 68.69% for OBQ).

**BRECQ** (Li et al., 2021): Introduces Fisher information into the reconstruction objective and optimizes layers within entire residual blocks jointly, rather than one layer at a time. This improves accuracy but increases computational cost — block-level optimization is substantially more expensive than layer-wise.

**Optimal Brain Quantization (OBQ)** (Frantar et al., 2022): The immediate predecessor to GPTQ, and the method from which GPTQ is directly derived. OBQ generalizes the classic Optimal Brain Surgeon (OBS) second-order weight pruning framework (Hassibi et al., 1993) to quantization. It quantizes weights **one at a time**, in **greedy order** (always picking the weight that currently incurs the least additional quantization error), and **always updates all remaining unquantized weights** to compensate for the error introduced by quantizing a single weight. The paper describes this as solving a quadratic reconstruction problem (Equation 1):

$$\arg\min_{\widehat{W}} ||WX - \widehat{W}X||_2^2$$

OBQ's runtime has **cubic input dependency** $\mathcal{O}(d_{row} \cdot d_{col}^3)$. For context, a single layer in OPT-175B might have $d_{row} = 12288$ and $d_{col} = 12288$ — OBQ would take an impractically long time on such a layer. The paper reports that OBQ can fully quantize ResNet-50 (25M parameters) in approximately one hour on a single GPU. Extrapolating this to OPT-175B (175,000M parameters, or 7,000× larger) would require months of computation.

> "the fact that OBQ's runtime for a $d_{row} \times d_{col}$ matrix $W$ has cubic input dependency $\mathcal{O}(d_{row} \cdot d_{col}^3)$ means that applying it to models with billions of parameters is extremely expensive"

This phrasing — "extremely expensive" — understates the case. The cubic dependency makes OBQ computationally infeasible, not just expensive. A method with cubic scaling cannot be meaningfully applied to models four orders of magnitude larger than the ones it was designed for.

#### Scalable-but-Inaccurate Methods (Large Models, Coarse Quantization)

With the release of open-source large language models like BLOOM (Laurençon et al., 2022) and OPT-175B (Zhang et al., 2022), researchers began developing methods specifically targeting giant networks. However, the paper argues these methods sacrifice too much:

**ZeroQuant** (Yao et al., 2022): Carefully selects quantization granularity (vector-wise rather than layer-wise) and proposes layer-wise knowledge distillation similar to AdaQuant. However, the paper points out a critical scalability limit:

> "the largest model it can apply this approach to has only 1.3 billion parameters. At this scale, ZeroQuant already takes ≈3 hours of compute; GPTQ quantizes models 100× larger in ≈4 hours."

This is a devastating comparison: ZeroQuant cannot even be applied to models at the scale GPTQ targets, and for the models it can handle, it is already slower than GPTQ is for models two orders of magnitude larger.

**LLM.int8()** (Dettmers et al., 2022): Observes that activation outliers in a few feature dimensions break the quantization of larger models, and proposes keeping those dimensions in higher precision while quantizing the rest. This works at 8 bits but, as the paper shows in Figure 1 and Tables 3-4, **fails to preserve accuracy at higher compression rates** (3-4 bits). The paper's RTN (round-to-nearest) baseline corresponds precisely to the quantization scheme used by LLM.int8().

**nuQmm** (Park et al., 2022): Develops efficient GPU kernels for a specific binary-coding based quantization scheme. The paper mentions this as comparable work but does not evaluate against it directly.

The common thread among these scalable methods, as the paper explicitly states, is that **they ultimately just round weights to the nearest quantization level** (RTN). This is computationally trivial — O(n) — but leaves significant accuracy on the table because it makes no attempt to compensate for the errors introduced by quantization. The paper summarizes this limitation bluntly:

> "they fail to preserve accuracy at higher rates"

Figure 1 makes this visually striking: the dashed RTN lines for OPT-175B at 4-bit (10.54 perplexity vs. 8.34 FP16 baseline) and for BLOOM-176B at 3-bit (571 perplexity vs. 8.11 baseline) show catastrophic degradation that renders the quantized models essentially unusable.

### The Fundamental Tension: Accuracy vs. Scalability

The paper's diagnosis of the field is that there exists a **fundamental tension** between accuracy and scalability in post-training quantization. The accurate methods (OBQ, BRECQ, AdaRound) solve a sophisticated optimization problem — they compensate for quantization error by adjusting remaining weights — but this optimization has super-linear computational complexity that prevents scaling to models with hundreds of billions of parameters. The scalable methods (RTN-based approaches) have trivial linear complexity but make no effort to compensate for quantization error, resulting in unacceptable accuracy degradation at compression rates beyond 8 bits.

This tension is not incidental; it is the direct consequence of how these methods are designed. OBQ's cubic complexity comes from the fact that it quantizes weights one at a time and updates the inverse Hessian matrix after each individual weight is quantized. The inverse Hessian has size $d_{col} \times d_{col}$, and updating it for each of the $d_{row} \times d_{col}$ weights produces the $\mathcal{O}(d_{row} \cdot d_{col}^3)$ runtime. This is optimal for a one-at-a-time greedy procedure, but it is fundamentally incompatible with the scale of modern LLMs.

The paper frames this tension as the central open question it addresses:

> "It therefore remains open whether one-shot post-training quantization to higher compression rates is generally-feasible."

The italicized "generally" is important — the question is not just whether a specific model can be compressed, but whether there exists a method that works across model sizes, from millions to hundreds of billions of parameters, at compression rates (3-4 bits) where prior scalable methods fail.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

GPTQ positions itself as **bridging the accuracy-scalability gap**. It does not claim to be more accurate than OBQ on small models — in fact, Table 1 shows it is slightly *less* accurate than OBQ on ResNet18-3bit (67.88% vs. 68.69%) and ResNet50-3bit (74.87% vs. 75.24%) — nor does it claim to be faster than RTN on small models. Instead, the paper's central claim is that it achieves **most of OBQ's accuracy at a tiny fraction of the computational cost**, making it the first method that is simultaneously accurate enough for 3-4 bit compression and fast enough for 100B+ parameter models.

The paper frames this through a direct lineage: GPTQ is OBQ with three major algorithmic modifications that collectively reduce the complexity by three orders of magnitude while preserving the core mechanism — second-order error compensation via the inverse Hessian. The key insight, which we will explore in detail in Section 3, is that OBQ's greedy weight selection order can be replaced with an **arbitrary fixed order** without significant accuracy loss, and this seemingly minor change cascades into massive computational savings because it means the inverse Hessian can be processed column-by-column rather than element-by-element.

The paper also positions itself explicitly in the lineage of practical deployment-oriented research. Unlike prior accurate PTQ methods that demonstrate results on benchmark datasets without showing real-world speedups, GPTQ includes:

1. **End-to-end GPU kernel implementations** that demonstrate wall-clock speedups (Table 6), not just theoretical FLOPs reductions.
2. **Single-GPU deployment** of OPT-175B, which the paper claims as a first (Section 5, "Practical Speedups"): "we can actually fit the entire quantized model into a single 80GB A100 GPU."
3. **A clear statement of limitations**, explicitly noting that speedups come from reduced memory movement, not computational reductions, and that activation quantization is not addressed (Section 6).

This practical orientation distinguishes GPTQ from work that treats quantization as a primarily theoretical or benchmark-focused exercise. The paper is making an argument not just about algorithmic innovation, but about **what kind of compression is actually useful** for making large language models deployable.

### Why Now? The Convergence of Model Scale and Open-Source Releases

The paper's timing is not coincidental. At the time of writing (late 2022, published at ICLR 2023), several factors converged to make this problem urgent:

1. **Open-source releases of giant models**: OPT-175B (Zhang et al., 2022) and BLOOM-176B (Laurençon et al., 2022) were publicly released, making it possible for any researcher to experiment with models at this scale — but only if they could run them. This created a practical demand for compression that did not exist when models of this size were only available behind proprietary APIs.

2. **The established inadequacy of 8-bit quantization**: LLM.int8() (Dettmers et al., 2022) demonstrated that 8-bit quantization works for large models, but at 8 bits, OPT-175B still requires 175GB of memory — still exceeding a single 80GB A100. Pushing to 3-4 bits is necessary to achieve single-GPU deployment, but no method existed that could maintain accuracy at those rates.

3. **OBQ's recent publication** (Frantar et al., 2022, accepted to NeurIPS 2022): The second-order error compensation framework was fresh and demonstrated state-of-the-art accuracy on vision models, but its cubic scaling was a known and acknowledged limitation. The natural question — "can we make this fast enough for LLMs?" — was directly prompted by OBQ's success and limitations.

The paper thus enters a field where the theoretical framework exists (OBQ's second-order compensation), the practical need is acute (deploying OPT-175B and BLOOM-176B), and the competing methods have clearly documented failure modes (RTN collapses at 3-bit; accurate methods cannot scale). The contribution is not inventing the core idea from scratch — it is the set of algorithmic insights that make the core idea computationally tractable at the target scale.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

The paper introduces **GPTQ**, a post-training weight quantization algorithm — a program that takes an already-trained language model and reduces the precision of its weight parameters from 16-bit floating-point to just 3 or 4 bits per weight, without any retraining or fine-tuning. The problem it solves is that existing accurate quantization methods are too slow to run on models with hundreds of billions of parameters, while existing fast methods lose too much accuracy — GPTQ bridges this gap by starting from the accurate-but-slow Optimal Brain Quantization (OBQ) framework and introducing three algorithmic innovations that collectively reduce the runtime by more than three orders of magnitude while preserving OBQ's core mechanism of using second-order Hessian information to compensate for the errors introduced by quantization.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The GPTQ system has four major components that operate in sequence:

1. **Hessian computation**: For each layer (or small block of layers), the system passes a small set of calibration data (128 random 2048-token sequences from C4) through the model to collect the layer inputs `$X$`. From these inputs, it computes the inverse Hessian matrix `$H^{-1} = (2XX^\top + \lambda I)^{-1}$`, which captures second-order information about how sensitive the layer's output is to changes in each weight. This computation happens once per layer before any quantization begins.

2. **Cholesky decomposition of the inverse Hessian**: The inverse Hessian is preprocessed via Cholesky decomposition to extract, in a numerically stable way, exactly the information that will be needed during quantization — specifically, the rows of `$H^{-1}$` corresponding to weights being quantized, which specify how updates should propagate to remaining unquantized weights. This step replaces the original OBQ approach of repeatedly updating `$H^{-1}$` in-place via Gaussian elimination, which accumulated numerical errors.

3. **Column-by-column quantization with lazy batch updates**: Rather than quantizing weights one at a time in a greedy error-minimizing order (as OBQ does), GPTQ quantizes weights in an arbitrary fixed order — left to right, column by column, processing all rows of a column simultaneously. Updates to remaining weights are "lazily batched" in blocks of 128 columns to improve GPU memory access patterns. Within each block, columns are quantized sequentially, with immediate local weight updates; after the entire block is processed, a single global update is applied to all remaining weights to the right of the block.

4. **Block-wise execution across the full model**: To keep GPU memory usage manageable, the algorithm processes the model one Transformer block (6 layers) at a time. After quantizing a block, the calibration data is passed through the freshly quantized block to produce inputs for the next block. This means each subsequent block is quantized based on the *actual* inputs it will receive from the already-quantized preceding layers, not the full-precision layer inputs — a detail the paper reports brings "noticeable improvements at negligible extra cost."

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the layer-wise quantization objective (Equation 1) that both OBQ and GPTQ solve, because this defines what "good" quantization means and what error we are trying to minimize.
- **Second**, OBQ's exact solution mechanics — the weight selection rule, the error update formula, and the inverse Hessian update — because GPTQ is best understood as a series of modifications to OBQ that preserve its error-compensation mechanism while eliminating the computational bottlenecks.
- **Third**, the three-step transformation from OBQ to GPTQ: arbitrary order insight (eliminating per-weight Hessian updates), lazy batch updates (improving GPU utilization), and Cholesky reformulation (solving numerical instability at scale). Each step is motivated by a specific computational or numerical problem that emerges when applying OBQ to layers with thousands of columns.
- **Fourth**, the full Algorithm 1 pseudocode walkthrough, connecting each line of the algorithm back to the mathematical transformations and design decisions.
- **Fifth**, the quantization grid and grouping mechanisms, which define how weights are actually mapped to discrete values and how finer granularity (per-group rather than per-row quantization) trades a small increase in bitrate for improved accuracy.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily an **algorithmic methods paper** whose core idea is that OBQ's accuracy can be preserved — and even slightly improved in some cases — while eliminating its cubic computational dependency, by observing that the greedy one-at-a-time weight selection order (which forces per-weight inverse Hessian updates) can be replaced with an arbitrary fixed order without significant accuracy loss, and that the resulting column-wise processing enables batched GPU operations and a numerically-stable Cholesky-based reformulation.

#### The Layer-Wise Quantization Objective

All post-training quantization methods in the OBQ/GPTQ lineage operate by solving a **layer-wise reconstruction problem**. Rather than trying to minimize the error on the final model output directly — which would require end-to-end optimization through all layers — the problem is decomposed: each layer is quantized independently to minimize the squared error between its full-precision output and its quantized output, given a representative set of inputs.

Formally, let `$W_\ell$` be the weight matrix of a linear layer `$\ell$` and let `$X_\ell$` be the matrix of layer inputs corresponding to a small set of `$m$` data points (the calibration data) running through the network. The objective is:

$$\arg\min_{\widehat{W}} ||WX - \widehat{W}X||_2^2$$

where `$W$` is the full-precision weight matrix, `$\widehat{W}$` is the quantized weight matrix we are searching for, `$X$` is the input activation matrix from the calibration data, and `$||\cdot||_2^2$` is the squared Frobenius norm (sum of squared entries of the matrix).

**What it computes:** the total squared difference between the full-precision layer's output activation vectors `$WX$` and the quantized layer's output `$\widehat{W}X$`, summed over all calibration data points and output dimensions. A value of zero would mean the quantized layer produces exactly the same outputs as the full-precision layer on the calibration data.

**Why this form:** this is a quadratic objective in `$\widehat{W}$`, which is the critical property that makes the entire approach tractable. Because the objective is quadratic, the optimal update to remaining unquantized weights when we change one weight can be computed analytically via the Hessian, without any iterative optimization. If the objective were non-quadratic (as would be the case with activation quantization or with certain nonlinear losses), we would need gradient descent or similar iterative methods, which are much slower and harder to scale. The quadratic form also means the Hessian is constant with respect to `$W$` — it depends only on the inputs `$X$`, not on the weights — so it can be computed once before quantization begins and reused throughout the process.

The paper explicitly notes that this objective is solved independently for each row of `$W$`, because the squared error decomposes as a sum over rows. Each row produces one output neuron, and the error in that neuron's outputs depends only on that row's weights (not on other rows), so the rows can be quantized independently. This row-wise independence is what makes the algorithm's complexity scale with `$d_{row} \cdot d_{col}^2$` rather than something worse — it means we only need one inverse Hessian for all rows, rather than one per row.

The assumption that "individual weights can move freely" means the quantization grid (the set of allowed discrete values) is fixed before the process begins, but there is no constraint that a weight must be rounded to the nearest grid point. The algorithm can round a weight to any value on the grid, and then adjust other weights to compensate. This is what distinguishes OBQ/GPTQ from simple round-to-nearest: round-to-nearest chooses `$\widehat{w}_i = \text{quant}(w_i)$` independently for each weight, while OBQ/GPTQ chooses the quantized value and then modifies `$w_j$` for `$j \neq i$` to offset the error.

#### OBQ's Exact Solution: The Starting Point

To understand GPTQ, we must first understand OBQ because GPTQ is a direct algorithmic transformation of OBQ. OBQ operates on one row `$w$` of the weight matrix at a time. It maintains a set `$F$` of indices corresponding to weights that have not yet been quantized (initially all weights), and a set of already-quantized weights. The key data structure is the inverse Hessian for the unquantized weights, denoted `$H_F^{-1}$`, where `$H_F = 2 X_F X_F^\top$` and `$X_F$` denotes the input rows corresponding to the unquantized weights.

At each step, OBQ selects **one weight** to quantize — specifically, the weight whose quantization will introduce the least additional squared error, accounting for the fact that all remaining unquantized weights can be adjusted to compensate. Then it quantizes that weight, updates all remaining unquantized weights to compensate for the error, and removes the newly-quantized weight from `$F$` (updating `$H_F^{-1}$` accordingly). This repeats until all weights are quantized.

The greedy-optimal weight to quantize next, denoted by `$w_q$`, and the corresponding optimal update to all remaining unquantized weights, denoted by `$\delta_F$`, are given by:

$$w_q = \arg\min_{w_q} \frac{(\text{quant}(w_q) - w_q)^2}{[H_F^{-1}]_{qq}}$$

$$\delta_F = -\frac{w_q - \text{quant}(w_q)}{[H_F^{-1}]_{qq}} \cdot (H_F^{-1})_{:,q}$$

where `$w_q$` is the scalar weight being quantized, `$\text{quant}(w_q)$` rounds `$w_q$` to the nearest value on the quantization grid, `$[H_F^{-1}]_{qq}$` is the diagonal entry of the inverse Hessian corresponding to weight `$q$` (a non-negative scalar representing the "leverage" of that weight — how much the output changes per unit change in the weight), `$(H_F^{-1})_{:,q}$` is the `$q$`-th column of the inverse Hessian, and `$\delta_F$` is a vector of updates applied to all remaining unquantized weights.

**What the first equation computes:** the weight that minimizes the ratio of squared quantization error `$(\text{quant}(w_q) - w_q)^2$` to the inverse Hessian diagonal `$[H_F^{-1}]_{qq}$`. The denominator matters because a weight with a large inverse Hessian diagonal has high "leverage" — changing it causes large changes in the output, and those changes are hard to compensate for by adjusting other weights. By dividing by this leverage term, the selection rule prioritizes quantizing weights where the error is both small *and* easily compensable. This is a form of **uncertainty-weighted error**: weights that are "easy" to compensate (small diagonal) can tolerate larger quantization error, while "influential" weights (large diagonal) are only quantized when their raw error is very small.

**What the second equation computes:** the optimal correction to apply to every remaining unquantized weight to exactly offset the error introduced by quantizing `$w_q$`. The magnitude of the correction to weight `$j$` is proportional to the quantization error `$(w_q - \text{quant}(w_q))$` multiplied by the ratio `$[H_F^{-1}]_{jq} / [H_F^{-1}]_{qq}$`. This ratio captures how weight `$j$` should change to compensate for a unit change in weight `$q$`, given the correlations between their corresponding input dimensions. The negative sign means the correction pushes other weights in the opposite direction of the quantization error, attempting to keep the output unchanged.

**Why this form:** these formulas are the closed-form solution to minimizing a quadratic objective when one variable is constrained to a discrete value. They are derived from setting the gradient of the reconstruction error with respect to the unquantized weights to zero, after fixing `$w_q$` to its quantized value. The inverse Hessian appears because it encodes the pairwise compensation relationships between weights: `$[H_F^{-1}]_{jq}$` tells you how much weight `$j$` must change to offset a unit perturbation in weight `$q$`. This is fundamentally the same mathematics as the Optimal Brain Surgeon (OBS) pruning algorithm, except applied to quantization (moving weights to discrete grid points) rather than pruning (setting weights to zero).

After quantizing `$w_q$`, OBQ removes the `$q$`-th row and column from `$H_F^{-1}$` to reflect that `$w_q$` is now fixed and can no longer be adjusted. This removal is done efficiently in `$O(|F|^2)$` time via one step of Gaussian elimination, rather than recomputing the inverse from scratch:

$$H_{-q}^{-1} = \left( H^{-1} - \frac{1}{[H^{-1}]_{qq}} H^{-1}_{:,q} H^{-1}_{q,:} \right)_{-q}$$

where `$H^{-1}$` is the current inverse Hessian (before removing row/column `$q$`), `$[H^{-1}]_{qq}$` is the `$q$`-th diagonal entry, `$H^{-1}_{:,q}$` is the `$q$`-th column, `$H^{-1}_{q,:}$` is the `$q$`-th row, and the subscript `$-q$` on the outer parentheses means we drop the `$q$`-th row and column from the resulting matrix.

**What it computes:** the inverse of the Hessian submatrix obtained by deleting the `$q$`-th row and column, computed from the full inverse without any matrix factorization. The outer product `$H^{-1}_{:,q} H^{-1}_{q,:}$` is a rank-one matrix; subtracting it and then dropping the `$q$`-th row and column yields exactly the inverse of the reduced Hessian.

**Why this form:** this is the standard Schur-complement formula for rank-one downdating of an inverse matrix, and it costs `$O(|F|^2)$` rather than `$O(|F|^3)$` for recomputing the inverse. This is the key efficiency that makes OBQ practical for medium-sized models. However, OBQ still does this update once *per weight* — for a `$d_{row} \times d_{col}$` matrix, that is `$d_{row} \cdot d_{col}$` updates, each costing `$O(d_{col}^2)$`, yielding the total `$O(d_{row} \cdot d_{col}^3)$` complexity that makes OBQ infeasible for large models. The cubic factor comes from the combination of updating `$O(d_{col}^2)$` matrix entries for each of `$O(d_{col})$` weights per row.

#### Step 1: The Arbitrary Order Insight — Eliminating Per-Weight Hessian Updates

The first and most consequential insight of GPTQ is that OBQ's greedy weight selection order — while theoretically optimal for minimizing greedy reconstruction error — can be replaced with an **arbitrary fixed order** without significant accuracy loss, particularly on large, heavily-parameterized layers. The paper states:

> "we find that, while this quite natural strategy does indeed seem to perform very well, its improvement over quantizing the weights in arbitrary order is generally small, in particular on large, heavily-parametrized layers."

The paper hypothesizes a reason for this: the greedy strategy avoids quantizing weights with large individual error early, but those weights end up being quantized toward the end of the process, when only a few unquantized weights remain that can be adjusted for compensation. So the benefit of deferring difficult weights is diminished because there is less remaining "adjustment capacity" to compensate for their large error. In other words, the total reconstruction error after all weights are quantized is not very sensitive to the order in which quantization decisions are made.

**Why this matters computationally:** if all rows are quantized in the same fixed order (e.g., left to right, column by column), then at any step of the process, the set of unquantized weights `$F$` is **identical for all rows**. This means the inverse Hessian `$H_F^{-1}$` is also identical for all rows, since it depends only on which input dimensions (columns) remain unquantized, not on the specific weight values in each row. Consequently, we only need to compute and update `$H_F^{-1}$` once per column (i.e., `$d_{col}$` times total) rather than once per weight (i.e., `$d_{row} \cdot d_{col}$` times).

This single change reduces the theoretical complexity from `$O(d_{row} \cdot d_{col}^3)$` to `$O(\max\{d_{row} \cdot d_{col}^2, d_{col}^3\})$`. The `$d_{col}^3$` term comes from the initial Cholesky decomposition; the `$d_{row} \cdot d_{col}^2$` term comes from applying the weight updates to all rows for each column. The paper notes that this is a speedup by a factor of `$\min\{d_{row}, d_{col}\}$`. In large Transformer models, `$d_{row} = d_{col} = d$` (the weight matrices are square), so the speedup is `$O(d)$`. For OPT-175B with `$d = 12288$`, this is more than four orders of magnitude improvement in theory.

Figure 2 illustrates the consequence: at any given moment, there is a set of already-quantized columns (left side of the weight matrix), a set of unquantized columns that are currently being updated (right side), and a single column being quantized right now (middle). The block structure emerges naturally from column-wise processing: rather than scattered individual weights being quantized in an order determined by the Hessian, we process columns from left to right, with all rows of a column quantized simultaneously.

#### Step 2: Lazy Batch Updates — Improving GPU Utilization

Even with the arbitrary-order optimization, a direct implementation would be slow in practice because it has a **low compute-to-memory-access ratio**. The problem arises from operations like the inverse Hessian update in Equation (3) (or its multi-column generalization, Equation 5), which update all elements of a potentially huge matrix using only a few floating-point operations per entry. On modern GPUs, these operations are bottlenecked by memory bandwidth rather than compute throughput: the GPU spends most of its time waiting for data to be transferred to and from memory, leaving its arithmetic units idle.

The second key insight addresses this: **the final rounding decision for column `$i$` is only affected by updates performed on that very column, and updates to later columns are irrelevant at this point**. In other words, when we are about to quantize column `$i$`, we do not need to have applied all pending updates to columns `$i+1, i+2, \ldots, d_{col}$`. We only need weights in column `$i$` to be correct. This means we can "lazily batch" updates: process columns in blocks of `$B = 128$`, keeping all updates contained to those 128 columns and the corresponding `$128 \times 128$` block of `$H^{-1}$`. Only once the entire block is processed do we apply a single global update to the remaining columns to the right of the block.

The paper states this explicitly:

> "Concretely, we apply the algorithm to B = 128 columns at a time, keeping updates contained to those columns and the corresponding B × B block of H−1. Only once a block has been fully processed, we perform global updates of the entire H−1 and W matrices."

The mathematical basis for this batching is the multi-weight generalization of the OBQ update formulas. When quantizing a set of indices `$Q$` (rather than a single index `$q$`), the update to remaining unquantized weights and the inverse Hessian downdate are:

$$\delta_F = -(w_Q - \text{quant}(w_Q))([H_F^{-1}]_{QQ})^{-1}(H_F^{-1})_{:,Q}$$

$$H_{-Q}^{-1} = \left( H^{-1} - H^{-1}_{:,Q}([H^{-1}]_{QQ})^{-1}H^{-1}_{Q,:} \right)_{-Q}$$

where `$w_Q$` is a vector of weights being quantized, `$\text{quant}(w_Q)$` is their element-wise quantization, `$[H_F^{-1}]_{QQ}$` is the `$|Q| \times |Q|$` submatrix of the inverse Hessian corresponding to those weights, `$([H_F^{-1}]_{QQ})^{-1}$` is its inverse, `$(H_F^{-1})_{:,Q}$` is the submatrix formed by the columns of `$H_F^{-1}$` corresponding to indices in `$Q$`, and `$H^{-1}_{Q,:}$` is the submatrix formed by the corresponding rows.

**What the first equation computes:** the joint optimal correction to all remaining unquantized weights when multiple weights are quantized simultaneously, rather than one at a time. The key difference from the single-weight case is the matrix `$([H_F^{-1}]_{QQ})^{-1}$`, which accounts for the correlations between the weights being quantized: adjusting weight `$j$` to compensate for quantizing weight `$q_1$` also changes the optimal compensation for quantizing weight `$q_2$`, and this interaction must be accounted for. When `$Q$` is a single index, `$[H_F^{-1}]_{QQ}$` is just the scalar `$[H_F^{-1}]_{qq}$` and its inverse is `$1/[H_F^{-1}]_{qq}$`, recovering the single-weight formula.

**What the second equation computes:** the joint removal of multiple rows and columns from the inverse Hessian, generalizing the single-index Gaussian elimination formula.

**Why the block size of 128:** this is an engineering choice balancing two considerations. Larger blocks mean fewer global updates (which are expensive because they touch all `$d_{col}$` columns) and better GPU utilization (processing 128 columns together allows vectorized operations). However, within a block, columns are processed sequentially (see Algorithm 1), so the block size determines how stale the inverse Hessian information becomes before a full update. The paper does not provide an ablation over block size, but 128 is a natural GPU-friendly number (matching typical warp sizes and cache line widths).

The paper is explicit that this batching "does not reduce the theoretical amount of compute" — the same number of operations are performed. The speedup comes purely from eliminating the memory bandwidth bottleneck. In practice, this provides "an order of magnitude speedup for very large models," making it "a critical component of our algorithm."

#### Step 3: Cholesky Reformulation — Solving Numerical Instability at Scale

The final technical challenge is **numerical instability** in the repeated application of the inverse Hessian downdate formula (Equation 5). The paper reports:

> "it can occur that the matrix $H_F^{-1}$ becomes indefinite, which we notice can cause the algorithm to aggressively update the remaining weights in incorrect directions, resulting in an arbitrarily-bad quantization of the corresponding layer."

**What "indefinite" means here:** the inverse Hessian should theoretically be symmetric positive definite — its eigenvalues should all be strictly positive, and it should define a valid quadratic form. Indefiniteness means that through accumulated floating-point errors, some eigenvalues become negative, causing the matrix to lose its interpretation as a valid inverse covariance. When this happens, the weight update formula can produce corrections that increase the error rather than decrease it, because the formula assumes the matrix defines a valid quadratic minimization problem. The paper notes that "the probability of this happening increases with model size" and that it "almost certainly occurs for at least a few layers on models that are larger than a few billion parameters."

For smaller models, the paper found that **dampening** — adding a small constant `$\lambda$` (specifically, 1% of the average diagonal value) to the diagonal elements of `$H$` before inversion — is sufficient to avoid numerical issues. Dampening shifts all eigenvalues upward by `$\lambda$`, making the matrix more strongly positive definite and less susceptible to floating-point drift. However, the paper states that "larger models require a more robust and general approach."

**The key observation enabling the Cholesky reformulation:** the only information that is ever required from the inverse Hessian `$H_F^{-1}$`, when quantizing weight `$q$`, is **row `$q$`** of that matrix — specifically, the elements in that row starting from the diagonal. The algorithm needs `$[H_F^{-1}]_{qq}$` to select the weight and compute the update magnitude, and `$(H_F^{-1})_{q, j}$` for `$j > q$` to compute how the update propagates to remaining unquantized weights. It does not need any elements above the diagonal (by symmetry), and it does not need any elements in rows other than the one being quantized.

This means we can **precompute all the required rows** before quantization begins, using a numerically stable method, and store them — without ever performing the unstable in-place downdate of Equation (3) or (5). The paper makes the connection to Cholesky decomposition:

> "Indeed, the row removal via (3) for our symmetric H−1 essentially corresponds to taking a Cholesky decomposition, except for the minor difference that the latter divides row q by the square root of the diagonal entry."

**How Cholesky provides the needed information:** The Cholesky decomposition of a symmetric positive definite matrix `$H^{-1}$` (after dampening) factors it as `$H^{-1} = LL^\top$`, where `$L$` is lower triangular with positive diagonal entries. If we form `$L^\top$` (an upper triangular matrix), then row `$q$` of `$L^\top$` contains precisely the information that would have been obtained by repeatedly downdating the original inverse Hessian through Gaussian elimination — it encodes how quantizing the first `$q-1$` weights affects weight `$q$` and all subsequent weights. The minor difference is that Cholesky normalizes by the square root of the diagonal, while Gaussian elimination does not, but this is easily corrected for by multiplying back the diagonal factor.

The practical consequence is that the paper can leverage "state-of-the-art Cholesky kernels" — highly optimized GPU implementations of Cholesky decomposition — to compute all needed inverse Hessian information upfront in a single numerically stable operation. Combined with "mild dampening" (the same 1% of average diagonal), the resulting method is "robust enough to execute on huge models without issues."

**What "Cholesky(H−1)⊤" means in Algorithm 1 line 3:** the notation `Cholesky(H−1)⊤` means compute the Cholesky decomposition `$H^{-1} = LL^\top$` and then take the transpose `$L^\top$`, storing the result in the variable `$H^{-1}$` (overwriting the original inverse). The algorithm then uses this upper-triangular storage: row `$j$` of this matrix, starting from the diagonal at column `$j$`, contains the scaled compensation coefficients for all weights `$j, j+1, \ldots, d_{col}$`. The scaling is such that the diagonal entry `$[H^{-1}]_{jj}$` is the correct inverse Hessian diagonal (after accounting for previously quantized weights), and the off-diagonal entries `$[H^{-1}]_{j,k}$` for `$k > j$` are the correct propagation coefficients scaled by the diagonal.

**Why this solves the numerical problem:** Cholesky decomposition is backward-stable — the computed `$L$` satisfies `$LL^\top = H^{-1} + E$` where `$||E||$` is on the order of machine epsilon times `$||H^{-1}||$`. This means the computed triangular factor is guaranteed to correspond to a nearby symmetric positive definite matrix, even if the original has become slightly indefinite due to rounding. The downdate formula in Equation (5), by contrast, involves subtracting an outer product and then inverting a submatrix — operations that amplify existing errors. The Cholesky approach replaces these repeated rank-one downdates with a single matrix factorization, eliminating the error accumulation.

The paper notes as a bonus that "using a well-optimized Cholesky kernel also yields further speedup." This is because Cholesky on an `$n \times n$` matrix costs `$O(n^3/3)$` — roughly constant-factor faster than the `$O(n^3)$` of repeated Gaussian elimination for all columns, and the constant factor is improved further by highly-tuned GPU implementations.

#### The Full Algorithm Walkthrough

Algorithm 1 in the paper presents the complete GPTQ procedure. Let us walk through it line by line, connecting each operation back to the mathematical concepts:

**Input:** the weight matrix `$W$` of size `$d_{row} \times d_{col}$`, the inverse Hessian `$H^{-1}$` computed as `$(2XX^\top + \lambda I)^{-1}$` (with dampening `$\lambda$` set to 1% of the average diagonal of `$2XX^\top$`), and the block size `$B = 128$`.

**Line 1:** `$Q \leftarrow 0_{d_{row} \times d_{col}}$` — initialize the quantized weight matrix to zeros. This will be filled column by column.

**Line 2:** `$E \leftarrow 0_{d_{row} \times B}$` — initialize a matrix to store the quantization errors for the current block of columns. The size `$d_{row} \times B$` means we track one error value per row per column in the block.

**Line 3:** `$H^{-1} \leftarrow \text{Cholesky}(H^{-1})^\top$` — compute the Cholesky decomposition of the damped inverse Hessian and store the transpose (upper triangular matrix) in place. After this line, `$H^{-1}$` is no longer the full symmetric inverse but an upper-triangular matrix whose rows provide the compensation coefficients.

**Line 4:** `for i = 0, B, 2B, ... do` — iterate over blocks of `$B$` columns. The loop variable `$i$` is the starting column index of the current block.

**Line 5:** `for j = i, ..., i+B-1 do` — within each block, iterate over individual columns. Column `$j$` is the one currently being quantized.

**Line 6:** `$Q_{:,j} \leftarrow \text{quant}(W_{:,j})$` — quantize all weights in column `$j$` (across all `$d_{row}$` rows) by rounding each to the nearest value on the quantization grid. This is a vectorized operation.

**Line 7:** `$E_{:,j-i} \leftarrow (W_{:,j} - Q_{:,j}) / [H^{-1}]_{jj}$` — compute the quantization error for each weight in column `$j$`, divided by the diagonal inverse Hessian entry. This division produces the **scaled error**: `$E_{r, j-i}$` will be multiplied by the off-diagonal entries `$[H^{-1}]_{j, k}$` for `$k > j$` to compute how much weight `$(r, k)$` should change to compensate for the error in weight `$(r, j)$`. The division by `$[H^{-1}]_{jj}$` ensures correct scaling — without it, the propagation formula would need to divide by the diagonal for each update, which is less efficient.

**Line 8:** `$W_{:,j:(i+B)} \leftarrow W_{:,j:(i+B)} - E_{:,j-i} \cdot H^{-1}_{j, j:(i+B)}$` — **immediate local update.** For each row `$r$`, the scaled error `$E_{r, j-i}$` is multiplied by the row vector `$H^{-1}_{j, j:(i+B)}$` (the `$j$`-th row of the upper-triangular `$H^{-1}$`, from column `$j$` to the end of the current block) and subtracted from the corresponding row of `$W$`. This propagates the quantization error for column `$j$` to all columns to its right within the same block, adjusting them to compensate. Note that columns to the *left* of `$j$` are already quantized and cannot be changed; columns beyond the block will be updated later (Line 10).

**Line 9:** `end for` — after this point, all `$B$` columns in the current block have been quantized, and all within-block compensations have been applied.

**Line 10:** `$W_{:, (i+B):} \leftarrow W_{:, (i+B):} - E \cdot H^{-1}_{i:(i+B), (i+B):}$` — **global update.** Apply the batched compensation for the entire block to all remaining columns to the right of the block. `$E$` is the `$d_{row} \times B$` matrix of scaled errors for all columns in the block. `$H^{-1}_{i:(i+B), (i+B):}$` is the submatrix of `$H^{-1}$` consisting of rows `$i`$ through `$i+B-1$` and columns `$i+B$` onward — this is the upper-right block of the Cholesky factor encoding how each column in the current block should compensate columns in the remaining unquantized region. The matrix multiplication `$E \cdot H^{-1}_{...}$` computes, for each row and each remaining column, the total compensation from all errors in the block.

**Why the two-phase update (Lines 8 and 10):** this is the lazy batching mechanism. The local update in Line 8 ensures that when we move to the next column within the block, the weights in that column reflect all compensations from previously-quantized columns in the same block. The global update in Line 10 defers all compensations to columns outside the block until the entire block is finished. This matters because global updates involve a matrix multiplication of size `$d_{row} \times (d_{col} - i - B)$`, which is large enough to achieve good GPU utilization. By contrast, if we applied global updates after every column (i.e., `$B = 1$`), we would perform many small matrix operations that cannot saturate the GPU's memory bandwidth.

#### Quantization Grid and Grouping

While the core GPTQ algorithm focuses on *how* to assign quantized values (the optimization procedure), the paper also specifies *what* values can be assigned (the quantization grid). The default setup is **standard uniform per-row asymmetric quantization on the min-max grid.**

**Uniform quantization** means the representable values are evenly spaced between a minimum and maximum. **Asymmetric** means the grid is not centered at zero — the minimum and maximum are the actual min and max of the weights in that row, so the grid covers the full range `$[w_{\min}, w_{\max}]$` without assuming symmetry. **Per-row** means each row of the weight matrix gets its own `$w_{\min}$` and `$w_{\max}$`, and hence its own scale and zero-point. This is the same quantization granularity used by LLM.int8() (Dettmers et al., 2022).

For `$b$`-bit quantization, there are `$2^b$` representable values (e.g., 16 values for 4-bit, 8 values for 3-bit), spaced uniformly between the row's min and max. The scale factor is `$(w_{\max} - w_{\min}) / (2^b - 1)$` and the zero-point encodes which integer corresponds to the value 0. During inference, dequantization reconstructs the approximate floating-point value as `$\text{scale} \times (\text{integer} - \text{zero_point})$`.

The paper emphasizes that GPTQ is "compatible with essentially any choice of quantization grid." The two important extensions are:

**Grouping:** Instead of computing one scale and zero-point per entire row, the row can be divided into groups of `$g$` consecutive weights, each with its own scale and zero-point. This increases the bitrate slightly because the scale and zero-point parameters themselves must be stored. For example, group-size 128 adds approximately 0.15 bits per weight (assuming FP16 scales and INT zero-points). The benefit is significantly improved accuracy, especially at very low bitwidths (2-3 bits), because the quantization grid adapts to local variations in weight magnitude within a row. The paper shows (Table 5, Figure 4) that grouping can recover a substantial fraction of the accuracy gap to full precision.

**An important interaction with GPTQ:** the group parameters (scale and zero-point) are determined *during* the quantization process of each layer, "always using the most current updated weights." This means that as GPTQ adjusts weights to compensate for errors, the quantization grid for remaining groups can adapt to the new weight distribution, rather than being fixed based on the original full-precision weights. This is a subtle but practically important detail — it means grouping does not interfere with the error compensation mechanism.

---

#### Block-Wise Execution Across the Full Model

The final implementation detail concerns how GPTQ handles the memory constraints of quantizing models with hundreds of billions of parameters. The procedure is as follows.

The model is processed **one Transformer block at a time**, where a block consists of 6 layers (attention projections, feed-forward layers, etc.). For each block, the calibration data is passed through the already-quantized preceding blocks to produce the inputs for the current block. These inputs are used to compute the layer-wise Hessians `$H = 2XX^\top$` for each linear layer in the block. The layers within the block are then quantized using Algorithm 1. Finally, the calibration data is passed through the now-quantized block to produce inputs for the next block, and the process repeats.

This block-wise approach serves two purposes. First, it keeps GPU memory usage low — only one block's weights and Hessians need to be in memory at a time, plus the calibration data activations. Second, and more importantly, it means each block is quantized based on its **actual inputs** in the partially-quantized model, not the inputs in the full-precision model. The paper notes that this "brings noticeable improvements at negligible extra cost." This is because quantization errors in early blocks propagate to later blocks, and by quantizing later blocks based on the already-perturbed inputs, GPTQ can partially compensate for this error propagation. If all layers were quantized independently based on full-precision inputs, the accumulated error across blocks would be larger.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Severing Quantization Order from Accuracy—The Arbitrary-Order Hypothesis

The most consequential intellectual move in this paper is the explicit identification and empirical validation of a **counter-intuitive property of second-order weight quantization**: the order in which weights are quantized does not matter much for the final reconstruction error, especially as models grow larger. This is not an optimization trick; it is a **diagnostic finding about the structure of the error landscape** in heavily over-parameterized layers.

The dominant assumption in prior work, crystallized in OBQ (Frantar et al., 2022), was that **greedy order matters significantly**. OBQ carefully selects the weight that currently incurs the least additional quantization error, quantizes it, updates the remaining weights, and repeats. The rationale is sound: by always picking the "easiest" weight next, you minimize the accumulated compensation burden. This is the same intuition that drives classic greedy algorithms in pruning (Optimal Brain Surgeon; Hassibi et al., 1993) and sparse recovery. The field had carried this assumption forward without testing whether it actually matters in the large-model regime.

GPTQ tests it and finds it does not—or at least, the benefit is marginal enough that it can be discarded in exchange for massive computational savings. The paper's hypothesis for why is insightful: on large layers, the weights that would be deferred by the greedy strategy (those with large individual error) end up being quantized toward the end of the process anyway, when "only a few unquantized weights that can be adjusted for compensation remain." The adjustment capacity of the remaining weights shrinks as more weights become fixed, so the advantage of deferring difficult decisions erodes. Put differently: **the degrees of freedom available for error compensation diminish monotonically, and the greedy ordering only modestly aligns difficult decisions with the moments when those degrees of freedom are still abundant.**

This insight is **fundamental, not incremental**. It reveals that the quadratic error landscape of large linear layers has a flatness property: many different quantization orders lead to basins of similar final error. This is not obvious *a priori*—one could have imagined that ordering matters enormously, as it does in many combinatorial optimization problems. The paper does not prove this theoretically but provides strong empirical evidence across scales: GPTQ matches or slightly exceeds OBQ accuracy on BERT-base and OPT-125M (Appendix Table 8) while being orders of magnitude faster.

The significance extends beyond GPTQ itself. This finding implies that **any second-order quantization method targeting large models can safely ignore order optimization** and instead focus computational effort on numerical stability and throughput. It converts a combinatorial choice (which of `$d_{row} \cdot d_{col}$` weights to quantize next) into a parameter-free fixed schedule, eliminating a major source of algorithmic complexity. This is a conceptual simplification comparable to the move from dynamic to static sparsity masks in pruning—and it likely generalizes to other layer-wise reconstruction objectives beyond the specific quadratic form used here.

---

### Innovation 2: Lazy Batching as a GPU-Aware Algorithm Design Principle

The second key innovation is not merely an implementation optimization but a **GPU-aware reformulation of the error compensation computation** that transforms a memory-bandwidth-bound operation into a compute-bound one without changing the theoretical operation count. The intellectual contribution is recognizing that the **causal structure of the quantization process**—the fact that column `$i$`'s final rounding depends only on updates applied to column `$i$` itself—can be exploited to batch memory accesses without sacrificing any accuracy.

The field's prior approach to GPU acceleration for such algorithms was straightforward: vectorize operations, parallelize across rows, and hope for the best. In OBQ's vectorized implementation, the bottleneck was not theoretical complexity but the **compute-to-memory-access ratio**: operations like rank-one downdating of the full inverse Hessian touch every element of a potentially enormous matrix but perform only a handful of FLOPs per element. On modern GPUs, this means the arithmetic units sit idle while the memory bus is saturated—the algorithm is memory-bandwidth-bound rather than compute-bound.

GPTQ's lazy batching solves this by exploiting a **structural property that is specific to column-wise fixed-order quantization**. Because the algorithm processes columns left-to-right, and updates to weights in column `$j$` from columns `$>j$` don't affect column `$j$`'s quantization decision, those updates can be deferred. The algorithm processes columns in blocks of 128, keeping updates local within the block, and only propagates compensation globally to all remaining columns once the entire block is complete. The crucial detail: **this does not change what compensation is ultimately applied—only when it is applied.** The final quantized weights are mathematically identical to what a fully-up-to-date implementation would produce.

This is an **incremental but practically essential** innovation. It does not advance the theoretical understanding of quantization but it bridges the gap between a theoretically-efficient algorithm (`$O(d_{row} \cdot d_{col}^2)$`) and a practically-fast one. The paper reports an "order of magnitude speedup for very large models" from this technique alone. Without it, the algorithm would still have the same asymptotic complexity but would run so slowly on GPU hardware that it would not be competitive in practice.

The deeper conceptual contribution is demonstrating that **causality constraints in column-wise processing enable aggressive batching without approximation**. This principle—identifying which intermediate results must be immediately propagated versus which can be lazily accumulated—is applicable beyond quantization, to any setting where sequential decisions with global compensation must be made under bandwidth constraints.

---

### Innovation 3: Cholesky Reformulation as Numerical Robustness by Design

The third key insight addresses a problem that the paper explicitly identifies as **scale-dependent**: the inverse Hessian downdate formula (Equation 3 in the original OBQ, Equation 5 in GPTQ's block formulation) accumulates floating-point errors through repeated rank-one updates, and the probability of catastrophic failure—the matrix becoming indefinite—"increases with model size" to the point where it "almost certainly occurs for at least a few layers on models larger than a few billion parameters."

The insight is that **the algorithm never actually needs the full inverse Hessian matrix**. It only needs the rows corresponding to weights being quantized, in the order those weights are processed. These rows can be precomputed in a single numerically-stable operation—Cholesky decomposition—rather than built up incrementally through error-prone Gaussian elimination steps.

The conceptual move here is subtle but powerful: **replacing a dynamic data structure (the evolving inverse Hessian) with a static precomputation (the Cholesky factor) that encodes the same information in a read-only form**. The Cholesky factorization `$H^{-1} = LL^\top$` is mathematically equivalent to what the sequence of downdate operations would produce in exact arithmetic, but it is computed all at once using backward-stable numerical methods that guarantee the result corresponds to a nearby positive definite matrix. The row-by-row Gaussian elimination of OBQ, by contrast, compounds rounding errors at each step, with no guarantee of stability—and empirically fails at scale.

This is significant because it **converts a fragile heuristic (dampening + hope) into a robust guarantee**. For smaller models, mild dampening (adding `$\lambda = 1\%$` of the average diagonal to `$H$`) was sufficient to keep the downdate procedure numerically stable. The paper found this fails on models with billions of parameters. The Cholesky approach solves this without requiring model-specific tuning of the dampening parameter or ad-hoc re-orthogonalization steps. It is a **design principle**: when a dynamic computation is numerically fragile and the information needed is predictable in advance, precompute it statically using backward-stable methods.

The connection the paper draws—that the row removal formula "essentially corresponds to taking a Cholesky decomposition, except for the minor difference that the latter divides row `$q$` by `$\sqrt{[H^{-1}]_{qq}}$`"—suggests that the authors recognized a **deep equivalence between incremental Gaussian elimination on the inverse and Cholesky factorization**. This is not standard textbook material; it required recognizing that the sequence of operations in OBQ's downdate step was implicitly constructing the Cholesky factor, just in an order-sensitive, error-prone way. Making this connection explicit and leveraging it for robustness is the intellectual contribution.

---

### Innovation 4: Verification that Quantization Error Propagation Benefits from Block-Wise Input Correction

A subtler but practically important finding is that **quantizing each layer based on the actual inputs it receives from already-quantized preceding layers, rather than on the full-precision layer inputs, measurably improves accuracy.** The paper mentions this almost in passing ("we find that this brings noticeable improvements at negligible extra cost") but it carries significant implications.

Prior layer-wise quantization methods compute Hessians from calibration data passed through the **full-precision** model, quantize each layer independently to reconstruct those specific inputs, and then assemble the quantized layers into a network. The implicit assumption is that the layer-wise reconstruction objective is sufficient—that minimizing error at each layer given full-precision inputs will minimize end-to-end error. But this ignores **error propagation**: quantization errors in early layers shift the distribution of inputs to later layers, meaning the later layers' Hessians (computed on full-precision inputs) become mismatched to the actual inputs they will receive at inference time.

GPTQ's block-wise execution—processing one Transformer block (6 layers) at a time and re-feeding the calibration data through the quantized block to produce inputs for the next block—**closes this distribution shift loop**. Each block is quantized to reconstruct the inputs it will actually see, not idealized full-precision inputs. This is a form of **sequential greedy optimization**: minimize the reconstruction error for block 1, then accept that error as fixed and optimize block 2 given block 1's actual outputs, and so on.

This finding is significant because it demonstrates that **the layer-wise independence assumption—which makes post-training quantization tractable—can be partially relaxed without significant computational cost**. Full end-to-end optimization through all layers (as in quantization-aware training) would be prohibitive, but block-wise input correction provides a middle ground that captures inter-layer error propagation effects while remaining computationally cheap (just one additional forward pass per block on the calibration data).

The paper does not ablate this finding or quantify its contribution, which is a limitation—we don't know whether it accounts for 0.01 or 0.5 perplexity improvement. But the principle is sound and suggests that future work should consider **input-aware quantization scheduling** as a standard component of post-training compression pipelines for deep networks.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The primary evaluation uses the **WikiText2** language modeling benchmark (Merity et al., 2016), with supplementary results on **Penn Treebank (PTB)** (Marcus et al., 1994) and **C4** (Raffel et al., 2020). For zero-shot evaluation, the paper uses **LAMBADA** (Paperno et al., 2016), **ARC** (Easy and Challenge) (Boratko et al., 2018), **PIQA** (Tata & Patel, 2003), and **StoryCloze**. WikiText2 and PTB are standard perplexity benchmarks known to be "particularly sensitive to model quantization" (Section 5, Language Generation). C4 is notable because GPTQ's calibration data is sampled from the C4 training set, making C4 evaluation not fully zero-shot — though the calibration data is only 128 random segments of 2048 tokens, a tiny fraction of the full dataset. For all language generation tasks, perplexity is computed in standard fashion: the validation set is concatenated using two linebreaks as separators, encoded with the model's default tokenizer, split into non-overlapping segments of 2048 tokens, and the exponentiated average of the log-probabilities for the next-token predictions is reported.

- **Base models.** All experiments use two model families: **OPT** (Zhang et al., 2022) at sizes ranging from 125M to 175B parameters, and **BLOOM** (Laurençon et al., 2022) from 560M to 176B parameters. These are the largest openly-available dense Transformer language models at the time of writing. The paper focuses particularly on the largest variants — OPT-175B and BLOOM-176B — because these are the models for which compression is simultaneously most necessary (they exceed single-GPU memory) and most challenging (prior methods fail at 3-4 bit). All models are used through their HuggingFace integrations.

- **Metrics.** The primary metric is **perplexity (PPL)**, specifically the exponentiated average negative log-likelihood on held-out text. Perplexity is the standard metric in language modeling compression literature (Yao et al., 2022; Dettmers et al., 2022) and is known to be "a very stringent accuracy metric" (Section 1) — small degradations in perplexity correspond to noticeably worse generation quality. For zero-shot tasks, the metric is **accuracy** (fraction of correct predictions), with task-specific scoring following the EleutherAI evaluation harness conventions. For LAMBADA, this means predicting the final word of a passage given the preceding context.

- **Baselines.** The primary baseline is **RTN (Round-to-Nearest)**, which rounds each weight independently to the nearest value on the quantization grid — the same asymmetric per-row grid used by GPTQ. The paper explicitly states that this "corresponds precisely to the state-of-the-art weight quantization of LLM.int8()" (Dettmers et al., 2022) and "is currently the method of choice in all works on quantization of very large language models" (Dettmers et al., 2022; Yao et al., 2022; Park et al., 2022). The **FP16 baseline** is the uncompressed model stored in float16. For small-model comparisons (Table 1), additional baselines include **AdaRound** (Nagel et al., 2020), **AdaQuant** (Hubara et al., 2021), **BRECQ** (Li et al., 2021), and **OBQ** (Frantar et al., 2022). For the small language model comparison (Appendix Table 8), the baseline is the original **OBQ** method applied to BERT-base and OPT-125M.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** For quantization, the relevant "budget" is not generations but **GPU hours** for the compression procedure itself. All quantization runs use a single NVIDIA A100 GPU with 80GB of memory. The calibration data consists of 128 random 2048-token segments from C4 (approximately 262K tokens total). For inference speed measurements, the metric is **average per-token latency** in milliseconds when generating sequences of length 128 at batch size 1, measured on both NVIDIA A100 (80GB) and NVIDIA A6000 (48GB) GPUs. The paper also tracks **GPU count reduction** — how many GPUs are needed to run inference — since this is a key practical motivator.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not employ cross-validation in the traditional sense. The 128 calibration samples are randomly selected from C4 and fixed across all experiments. Because the calibration data is generic web text and not task-specific, and because the benchmark datasets are entirely separate, there is no train/test contamination for WikiText2, PTB, or the zero-shot tasks. For C4 evaluation, the calibration data is sampled from C4's training split, making it not fully zero-shot, but the 128 segments represent such a tiny fraction of C4 that overfitting is not a practical concern. The paper does not report confidence intervals or multiple calibration runs, which is a limitation for assessing result stability.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### GPTQ vs. Accurate Post-Training Methods on Small Models

The paper first establishes that GPTQ is competitive with — though not decisively better than — existing state-of-the-art PTQ methods on the small models where those methods can run. This is a **validation step**, not the main contribution: it demonstrates that the algorithmic simplifications (arbitrary order, lazy batching, Cholesky reformulation) do not sacrifice accuracy relative to the exact greedy OBQ procedure.

**Table 1 (ResNet18 and ResNet50 on ImageNet):**

| Method | RN18-4bit (69.76% FP) | RN18-3bit | RN50-4bit (76.13% FP) | RN50-3bit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| OBQ | 69.56 | 68.69 | 75.72 | 75.24 |
| GPTQ | 69.37 | 67.88 | 75.71 | 74.87 |

At 4-bit, GPTQ matches OBQ within 0.2% on ResNet18 and 0.01% on ResNet50. At 3-bit, the gap widens — GPTQ loses 0.81% on ResNet18 and 0.37% on ResNet50 relative to OBQ — but GPTQ still significantly outperforms AdaQuant (the fastest prior method) by 8.67% on ResNet18-3bit and 9.89% on ResNet50-3bit. GPTQ takes "less than 1 minute rather than approximately 1 hour" for these models. The comparison against BRECQ, the most accurate prior method, shows GPTQ trailing by 0.59% on ResNet18-3bit and 0.45% on ResNet50-3bit — a meaningful gap, but BRECQ cannot scale to models larger than ~100M parameters.

**Appendix Table 8 (BERT-base and OPT-125M):**

| Method | BERT-4bit (88.53 F1 FP) | BERT-3bit | OPT-125M-4bit (27.66 PPL FP) | OPT-125M-3bit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| OBQ | 88.23 | 85.29 | 32.52 | 69.32 |
| GPTQ | 88.18 | 86.02 | 31.12 | 53.85 |

On BERT-base, GPTQ slightly *outperforms* OBQ at 3-bit (86.02 vs. 85.29 F1), a surprising result that the paper hypothesizes may be due to "some of the additional heuristics used by OBQ, such as early outlier rounding, requiring careful adjustments for optimal performance on non-vision models." On OPT-125M, GPTQ substantially outperforms OBQ at 3-bit (53.85 vs. 69.32 perplexity — note lower is better). The paper does not explore this discrepancy deeply, but the result is practically important: GPTQ is not merely approximating OBQ; it is sometimes better, likely because OBQ's greedy order and heuristics were tuned for vision and may not transfer perfectly to language model weight distributions.

**What these results establish:** GPTQ recovers most of OBQ's accuracy while being ~60x faster on small models. The accuracy gap, where it exists, is small enough that GPTQ is the only method in this accuracy tier that can even be *run* on models with billions of parameters. This is the core tradeoff the paper is making.

#### Runtime Scaling to 100B+ Models

**Table 2** reports the wall-clock time for full model quantization on a single A100:

| OPT Model | 13B | 30B | 66B | 175B |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Runtime | 20.9 min | 44.9 min | 1.6 hours | 4.2 hours |

| BLOOM Model | 1.7B | 3B | 7.1B | 176B |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Runtime | 2.9 min | 5.2 min | 10.0 min | 3.8 hours |

The scaling is **super-linear but well-behaved**: OPT-175B takes ~12x longer than OPT-13B despite having ~13.5x more parameters, and BLOOM-176B takes ~22x longer than BLOOM-7.1B despite having ~25x more parameters. The paper explicitly contextualizes these numbers against ZeroQuant-LKD (Yao et al., 2022), which "reports a 3 hour runtime (on the same hardware) for a 1.3B model, which would linearly extrapolate to several hundred hours (a few weeks) for 175B models." This is a ~100x improvement at the target scale. The paper also notes that "adaptive rounding-based methods typically employ a lot more SGD steps and would thus be even more expensive."

**Critical detail:** These runtimes are for the *entire* quantization procedure — Hessian computation, Cholesky decomposition, and the column-by-column quantization loop — running on a single GPU. This is what makes the method practically usable: a researcher can download OPT-175B, run GPTQ for four hours on one A100, and have a compressed model ready for deployment. No multi-GPU setup, no distributed optimization, no weeks-long training procedures.

**4-bit quantization:**
- OPT-175B: GPTQ achieves 8.37 PPL vs. 8.34 FP16 baseline (0.03 degradation) vs. 10.54 for RTN (2.20 degradation). RTN at 4 bits on OPT-175B performs **worse than the 10x smaller FP16 OPT-13B** (10.13 PPL).
- OPT-66B: GPTQ achieves 9.55 vs. 9.34 FP16 (0.21 degradation) vs. 110 for RTN (catastrophic, >100 PPL). This is the model the paper notes has "a significant fraction of dead units in the early layers, which may make it harder to compress."
- OPT-30B: GPTQ achieves 9.63 vs. 9.56 FP16 (0.07 degradation) vs. 10.98 for RTN.
- OPT-13B: GPTQ achieves 10.31 vs. 10.13 FP16 (0.18 degradation) vs. 11.32 for RTN.
- At smaller scales (125M-6.7B), RTN becomes progressively worse, while GPTQ maintains reasonable degradation.

**3-bit quantization:**
- OPT-175B: GPTQ achieves 8.68 PPL vs. 8.34 FP16 (0.34 degradation). RTN achieves 7.3e3 (completely collapsed, ~875x worse than baseline).
- OPT-30B: GPTQ achieves 10.27 vs. 9.56 FP16 (0.71 degradation). RTN achieves 1.6e3.
- OPT-13B: GPTQ achieves 11.61 vs. 10.13 FP16 (1.48 degradation). RTN achieves 3.4e3.

**Figure 1 (left)** visualizes these results: the GPTQ 4-bit curve tracks the FP16 baseline almost perfectly from 125M to 175B, while the RTN 4-bit curve diverges sharply. At 3-bit (Figure 1 right, showing BLOOM), RTN is off the chart at 571 PPL for BLOOM-176B, while GPTQ at 8.64 PPL is close to the FP16 8.11 baseline.

For OPT at 4-bit: 125M drops 3.47 PPL, 1.3B drops 0.84, 13B drops 0.18, 175B drops 0.03. The overparameterization that makes these models expensive to train also makes them **robust to quantization** — there are many near-equivalent weight configurations that produce similar outputs, so the constrained optimization can find good quantized solutions.

#### Zero-Shot Task Performance

**Figure 3** visualizes LAMBADA accuracy across model families and bitwidths. The FP16 curve rises smoothly with model size for both OPT and BLOOM. The key observations:

**At 4-bit:** GPTQ tracks FP16 almost exactly across all model sizes. RTN 4-bit shows moderate degradation, especially on smaller OPT models (OPT-125M drops from 39.16% to 18.34%, a 20.8 percentage point loss; OPT-350M drops from 46.67% to 40.62%). On BLOOM, RTN 4-bit is more competitive, consistent with the perplexity findings.

**At 3-bit:** GPTQ maintains reasonable accuracy — on OPT-175B, it achieves 76.19% vs. 75.59% FP16 (a gain, not a loss); on BLOOM-176B, it achieves 65.10% vs. 67.40% FP16 (a 2.3 point loss). RTN 3-bit collapses to near-zero accuracy on most configurations: OPT-175B at 0.00%, BLOOM-176B at 0.17%. The only models where RTN 3-bit achieves non-trivial accuracy are small OPT variants (125M at 13.93%, 350M at 32.31%), and even these are far below GPTQ.

**Appendix Tables 13-22** provide full zero-shot results across LAMBADA, PIQA, ARC-easy, ARC-challenge, and StoryCloze. The patterns are consistent:
- GPTQ at 4-bit is nearly indistinguishable from FP16 across all tasks and model sizes.
- RTN at 4-bit shows moderate-to-large degradation on ARC-challenge (OPT-66B drops from 40.02% to 22.87%) and PIQA (OPT-66B drops from 79.76% to 60.07%), but holds up better on easier tasks like LAMBADA.
- RTN at 3-bit is catastrophic across almost all configurations — OPT-175B achieves 0.00% on LAMBADA, 51.25% on PIQA (vs. 81.07% FP16), 26.77% on ARC-easy (vs. 71.04% FP16), 23.81% on ARC-challenge (vs. 43.94% FP16), 46.47% on StoryCloze (vs. 79.82% FP16).

**A notable outlier:** OPT-66B shows anomalous behavior across multiple tasks. At 4-bit RTN, it drops to 13.08% on LAMBADA (vs. 74.93% FP16) and 60.07% on PIQA (vs. 79.76% FP16). At 3-bit GPTQ, it drops to 57.02% on LAMBADA (vs. 74.93% FP16). The paper attributes this to "a significant fraction of dead units in the early layers" of the OPT-66B checkpoint, which "may make it harder to compress." This is an important caveat: GPTQ's effectiveness depends on the model being reasonably well-trained, and pathological weight distributions (dead neurons, extreme outliers) can cause degradation even at higher bitwidths.

#### Practical Deployment Metrics: GPU Count Reduction and Latency

**Table 6** presents end-to-end inference latency for OPT-175B at batch size 1, generating sequences of length 128:

| GPU | FP16 (GPU count) | FP16 latency | 3-bit (GPU count) | 3-bit latency | Speedup |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A6000 (48GB) | 8 GPUs | 589 ms | 2 GPUs | 130 ms | 4.53x |
| A100 (80GB) | 5 GPUs | 230 ms | 1 GPU | 71 ms | 3.24x |

On the A100, the 3-bit OPT-175B fits entirely within a single GPU's 80GB memory (63GB for weights, ~9GB for key-value cache at max context length, plus overhead). The FP16 baseline requires 5 GPUs (326GB / 80GB per GPU = 4.1, rounded up to 5 for even distribution). On the more cost-effective A6000, the 3-bit model requires 2 GPUs and delivers 4.53x speedup over the 8-GPU FP16 setup.

**What makes the speedup possible:** The paper states that "almost all of the speedup is due to our kernels, as communication costs are negligible in our standard HuggingFace-accelerate-like setting." The custom kernels perform a quantized-matrix full-precision-vector product by dynamically dequantizing weights as they are loaded from memory. In the generative inference setting (batch size 1, auto-regressive token-by-token generation), the matrix-vector products are memory-bandwidth-bound — the GPU spends most of its time waiting for weight data to be transferred from HBM to compute units. By storing weights at 3 bits instead of 16 bits, the kernel reads ~5.3x fewer bytes from memory. Dequantization adds some compute overhead, but because the operation is memory-bound, the net effect is substantial speedup despite doing extra arithmetic.

**Critical limitation acknowledged by the paper:** These kernels "do not provide speedups for the actual multiplications, due to the lack of hardware support for mixed-precision operands (e.g. FP16 x INT4) on mainstream architectures." The speedup comes entirely from reduced memory traffic. For compute-bound workloads (large batch sizes, non-generative inference), the approach would not provide these speedups — the paper suggests simply decompressing the weight matrix before the matrix-matrix multiplication, which "takes <1.5ms on an A100 and <3ms on an A6000 compared to 76ms/365ms for the subsequent OPT-175B FC2 layer computation with batchsize 16x1024 tokens." So for throughput-oriented scenarios, GPTQ still reduces GPU count (and thus hardware cost) but does not accelerate the computation itself.

| Model | FP16 | g128 (≈2.2 bit) | g64 (≈2.3 bit) | g32 (≈2.6 bit) | 3-bit baseline |

The trend is consistent: grouping provides substantial accuracy improvements at very modest bitrate overhead, with diminishing returns as group size decreases. The paper presents this as an essentially free accuracy boost — "GPTQ is compatible with essentially any choice of quantization grid" and "grouping interacts very well with GPTQ, as the group parameters can be determined during the quantization process of each layer, always using the most current updated weights."

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

- **Greedy order vs. arbitrary order:** The entire algorithm is an ablation of OBQ's core design choice. Section 4 states the finding directly: "While this quite natural strategy does indeed seem to perform very well, its improvement over quantizing the weights in arbitrary order is generally small, in particular on large, heavily-parametrized layers." The empirical evidence is in Appendix Table 8, where GPTQ (arbitrary order) matches or exceeds OBQ (greedy order) on BERT-base and OPT-125M. The paper does not present a dedicated ablation showing side-by-side greedy vs. arbitrary order results on the same model across sizes — this would have strengthened the claim. The evidence is indirect: OBQ *is* the greedy baseline, and GPTQ approximates it closely.

- **Block size (B=128):** The paper states that B=128 is used throughout but does not provide an ablation over block size. This is a notable omission — the block size determines the tradeoff between within-block update accuracy and GPU utilization. The claim that it provides "an order of magnitude speedup" is not supported by a sweep showing how runtime scales with B, nor is there an accuracy vs. B comparison. The choice appears to be an engineering heuristic (128 matches GPU warp sizes) rather than an empirically-optimized parameter.

- **Dampening strength (λ):** The paper uses λ = 1% of the average diagonal of 2XX^T for all models "when dampening is applied." However, the Cholesky reformulation is introduced specifically because dampening alone was insufficient for large models. The paper does not report results with different λ values, nor does it show at what model size dampening alone begins to fail. The transition point between "dampening is sufficient" and "need Cholesky" is claimed at "a few billion parameters" but not precisely characterized.

- **Dampening vs. Cholesky:** This is the most important implicit ablation in the paper. The paper states that dampening alone works for smaller models but "almost certainly" fails for models above a few billion parameters due to the inverse Hessian becoming indefinite. The Cholesky reformulation "combined with mild dampening" is robust at all scales. However, the paper does not present a direct comparison showing: (a) the perplexity of a large model quantized with dampening-only (which presumably fails catastrophically), (b) the perplexity with Cholesky only (no dampening), or (c) the failure rate statistics. Without these, the reader must take on faith that the numerical instability is as severe as claimed and that the Cholesky fix fully resolves it. The evidence is circumstantial: GPTQ *works* on 175B models, while the dampening-only approach is described as producing "arbitrarily-bad quantization."

- **Calibration data size and source:** The paper uses 128 random 2048-token segments from C4. No ablation over number of calibration samples is provided. Given that the Hessian is estimated from this data, too few samples would yield a poor Hessian estimate and worse quantization. The paper does not explore whether 64 or 256 samples would meaningfully change results, nor whether the data source (C4 vs. WikiText2 vs. domain-specific text) matters. The choice of C4 (generic web text) is defended as meaning "GPTQ does not see any task-specific data," but the sensitivity analysis is absent.

- **Block-wise vs. layer-wise input correction:** The paper mentions that processing one Transformer block at a time and re-feeding calibration data through quantized blocks "brings noticeable improvements at negligible extra cost." No ablation is presented — we do not know whether this means 0.05 or 0.5 PPL improvement on OPT-175B. For a paper that quantifies almost everything else, this is a gap.

- **Row-wise vs. group-wise quantization:** Tables 5 and 7, and Figure 4 comprehensively ablate group size. The finding is clear: grouping consistently improves perplexity at low bitwidths, with diminishing returns below group-size 128. At 4-bit on medium models (Figure 4), group-size 1024 recovers most of the FP16 gap; group-size 128 essentially closes it. At 3-bit on 175B models (Table 5), group-size 1024 improves WikiText2 PPL by 0.23 (OPT) and 0.29 (BLOOM); group-size 128 adds a further 0.0 and 0.09 respectively.

- **Model family comparison (OPT vs. BLOOM):** While not a designed ablation, the comparison across two independently-trained model families serves as a robustness check. The qualitative findings (GPTQ >> RTN at 3-bit, larger models easier to quantize, grouping helps) hold for both families. The quantitative differences (BLOOM is easier to quantize at 4-bit, OPT-66B is an outlier) suggest model-specific factors that GPTQ does not control for, but the method's core behavior generalizes.

- **Negative result — OPT-66B dead neurons:** The OPT-66B model consistently underperforms expectations across GPTQ and RTN. The paper identifies "a significant fraction of dead units in the early layers" as the likely cause. This is a useful negative result: GPTQ cannot compensate for fundamentally poor model quality. If a model has pathological weight distributions (zero-gradient neurons, extreme outliers in specific dimensions), second-order compensation cannot fix the underlying problem — those weights carry no useful signal to preserve. This aligns with the broader finding that larger (and presumably better-trained) models are easier to quantize.

### Critical Assessment

#### Does GPTQ actually "more than double compression gains relative to prior one-shot methods, preserving accuracy"?

**The claim is supported, but with an important caveat about what "prior methods" means.** The paper's RTN baseline — which "corresponds precisely to the state-of-the-art weight quantization of LLM.int8()" — collapses at 3-bit (PPL in the thousands) and degrades significantly at 4-bit for OPT models (2.2 PPL loss on OPT-175B). GPTQ at 3-bit achieves 8.68 PPL on OPT-175B — better than RTN at 4-bit (10.54 PPL). This is indeed more than doubling compression: RTN is essentially non-functional at 3-bit, while GPTQ is highly functional. At 4-bit, GPTQ's 0.03 PPL degradation vs. RTN's 2.2 represents a ~73x reduction in error relative to the baseline.

However, the paper compares against **RTN specifically**, not against the strongest possible 3-4 bit quantization baseline. LLM.int8() is designed for 8-bit quantization and the authors acknowledge it "fails to preserve accuracy at higher rates." The fairer framing is: GPTQ is the *first* method to achieve acceptable accuracy at 3-4 bits on 100B+ models, not that it outperforms strong baselines at those bitwidths. For 8-bit, the paper does not compare against LLM.int8() because it is the same as RTN at that bitwidth and RTN works fine at 8 bits. The "double compression" claim is real — GPTQ enables 3-bit where 8-bit was previously the practical floor — but the absolute improvement over a strong 4-bit baseline is harder to quantify because no strong 4-bit baseline exists at this scale.

#### Does GPTQ enable "for the first time execution of a 175 billion-parameter model inside a single GPU"?

**Strongly supported.** The memory arithmetic is straightforward and verified: OPT-175B at 3-bit with FP16 embeddings and output layer takes ~63GB. Adding the full key-value cache for 2048-token context (~9GB) totals ~72GB, fitting within an 80GB A100. The paper reports actually running inference in this configuration. At 4-bit, the model would be ~84GB (weights only), which exceeds the 80GB limit — confirming that 3-bit is necessary for single-GPU deployment on currently available hardware. The FP16 baseline requires 5x A100 GPUs. This is not a theoretical calculation; the paper's custom kernels are deployed and benchmarked. The claim is unambiguous and validated.

#### Do the reported speedups (3.25x on A100, 4.5x on A6000) accurately reflect what users will experience?

**Supported, with a major scope limitation.** The speedups are measured for **batch-size-1 generative inference** using custom dequantization kernels. This is the most practically relevant setting for interactive applications (chatbots, code completion, text generation), where latency determines user experience. The paper is transparent that these speedups come from memory bandwidth reduction and would not apply to throughput-oriented (large batch) inference. For large-batch inference, the paper suggests simple decompression-before-multiplication, which reduces GPU count but not latency per token. The speedups also assume the HuggingFace-accelerate distribution strategy where communication costs are <5% of runtime.

Three caveats weaken this claim slightly:

1. **Speedups measure only matrix-vector products, not full end-to-end generation.** The paper states "all overheads due to HuggingFace, attention or non-quantized operations like residuals or LayerNorms are exactly the same." If attention or layer normalization account for, say, 20% of end-to-end latency, then the 3.25x speedup on matrix operations translates to a lower end-to-end speedup. The paper does not provide a breakdown.

2. **No comparison against optimized FP16 inference.** The FP16 baseline uses standard HuggingFace/accelerate — not FlashAttention (Dao et al., 2022), not tensor parallelism, not kernel fusion. The paper acknowledges that their quantized models "should benefit from more advanced distribution strategies or more efficient attention kernels just as much as our baseline," but the absolute speedup numbers are relative to a baseline that could itself be accelerated.

3. **The kernels are bespoke and not integrated into standard frameworks.** The paper provides code, but adoption depends on integration with PyTorch, HuggingFace, or inference engines like vLLM. The speedups are demonstrated in a research setting, not a production deployment.

#### Do the perplexity results demonstrate that GPTQ preserves accuracy at 3-4 bits?

**Supported, with careful attention to what "preserves accuracy" means.** At 4-bit, GPTQ achieves near-identical perplexity to FP16 across OPT and BLOOM families — the largest degradation on WikiText2 is 0.36 PPL (BLOOM-7.1B), and on the 175B+ models it is 0.03-0.10 PPL. These are negligible by any standard. At 3-bit, degradations are larger (0.34-0.53 on WikiText2 for the 175B+ models) but still remarkably small given 5.3x compression. The zero-shot results corroborate: GPTQ at 3-bit on OPT-175B achieves LAMBADA accuracy *above* FP16 (76.19% vs. 75.59%).

However, "accuracy" is measured exclusively through **perplexity and zero-shot task accuracy** — both of which are aggregate metrics that can mask per-example degradation. The paper does not evaluate:
- **Generation quality** (coherence, factual accuracy, reasoning) through human evaluation or automated metrics like BLEU/ROUGE.
- **Calibration** of the quantized model's confidence scores.
- **Behavioral changes** in sensitive domains (bias, toxicity, safety) that might be amplified by compression.
- **Downstream fine-tuning performance** — does a GPTQ-compressed model fine-tune as well as a full-precision one?

The paper's ethics statement acknowledges this: "We believe a thorough study of the impact of compression upon secondary measures, and in particular bias effects is warranted." This is a responsible caveat, but it means the "accuracy preservation" claim is scoped to the specific metrics evaluated.

#### Is the claim that "larger models are easier to quantize" robust?

**Supported by the data, but the mechanism is not established.** The trend is clear in Tables 3-4 and Figure 1: as model size increases, the gap between GPTQ and FP16 narrows. For OPT at 4-bit: 125M loses 3.47 PPL; 350M loses 2.24; 1.3B loses 0.84; 13B loses 0.18; 175B loses 0.03. The same monotonic trend holds (with OPT-66B as the exception). The paper does not attempt to explain this mechanistically beyond the intuition of overparameterization — larger models have more redundant capacity, so there are more near-equivalent weight configurations available post-quantization. This is plausible but unverified. A more rigorous analysis would examine the Hessian spectrum as a function of model size — one would expect larger models to have more eigenvalues near zero (more flat directions in the loss landscape), which would make them more robust to the constrained optimization.

The OPT-66B exception is informative but underexplored. The paper notes "dead units in the early layers" but does not characterize what proportion of units are dead, how this affects the Hessian, or whether retraining or pruning the dead units would improve quantizability. This is a practically important failure mode: users compressing models they did not train themselves may encounter similarly pathological checkpoints, and GPTQ provides no diagnostic tools for identifying or mitigating such cases.

#### What experiments are missing that would strengthen the paper?

1. **Ablation over the number of calibration samples.** The paper uses exactly 128 samples of 2048 tokens. This is ~262K tokens total — about 10 seconds of GPU time for OPT-175B inference. Could 32 samples work nearly as well? Would 512 samples close the remaining gap to FP16 at 3-bit? Without this ablation, practitioners do not know the calibration data budget required for good results.

2. **Ablation over block size B.** B=128 is stated without justification. For reproducibility and optimization, a sweep over block sizes (32, 64, 128, 256) showing accuracy and runtime tradeoffs would be valuable.

3. **Direct comparison of dampening-only vs. Cholesky at scale.** The paper claims dampening-only fails on large models but never shows the failure. Showing OPT-13B with dampening-only producing, say, 500 PPL would dramatically illustrate the necessity of the Cholesky reformulation and strengthen the paper's argument.

4. **Evaluation on non-perplexity generative metrics.** Running GPTQ-compressed models through standard generation evaluations (e.g., HumanEval for code, GSM8K for math reasoning, summarization benchmarks) would test whether the perplexity preservation translates to maintained capability on structured tasks.

5. **Comparison against a same-budget smaller FP16 model.** If a 3-bit OPT-175B achieves 8.68 PPL, how does this compare to an FP16 model with similar memory footprint? A hypothetical ~11B parameter FP16 model would use roughly the same memory as a 3-bit 175B model (11B × 2 bytes = 22GB vs. 175B × 3/8 = 65.6GB — actually the 3-bit 175B is larger). But the paper doesn't explore whether running a mid-sized FP16 model would be preferable to a heavily compressed large model. This is the inference-time analog of the Chinchilla tradeoff — at fixed memory budget, is it better to have a big compressed model or a smaller uncompressed one?

6. **Sensitivity to weight distribution outliers.** The LLM.int8() paper (Dettmers et al., 2022) identified activation outliers as the primary obstacle to 8-bit quantization, finding that keeping outlier feature dimensions in FP16 was necessary. GPTQ does not address activation outliers — it quantizes only weights. Does GPTQ's second-order compensation implicitly handle the interaction between weight quantization and activation outliers, or would combining GPTQ with LLM.int8()-style outlier handling further improve results? The paper does not investigate this interaction.

7. **Reproducibility across random seeds.** The calibration data is randomly sampled from C4. Running with different random seeds and reporting variance would give confidence that the reported perplexities are stable and not dependent on a lucky calibration draw.

#### Where the claims hold conditionally

The central claim — GPTQ enables accurate 3-4 bit quantization of 100B+ models — holds **unconditionally for the models and metrics tested**. The 4.5x speedup claim holds **for batch-size-1 generative inference on memory-bandwidth-bound GPU architectures**, not for compute-bound inference or non-GPU hardware. The "first time single-GPU execution" claim holds **for the specific hardware (A100 80GB) and model (OPT-175B at 3-bit)**, and would not hold for a 4-bit OPT-175B (exceeds 80GB) or for a 3-bit model on a 48GB GPU (requires 2 GPUs as shown). The "larger models are easier to quantize" trend holds **for well-trained models in the OPT and BLOOM families**, with the OPT-66B counterexample demonstrating that poorly-trained checkpoints break the trend.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### Speedups Are Memory-Bandwidth-Limited, Not Computational

**The assumption or constraint.** GPTQ's practical speedups derive entirely from reduced memory traffic — storing weights at 3-4 bits means the GPU reads fewer bytes from HBM during matrix-vector products — rather than from performing fewer arithmetic operations. The paper is explicit about this in Section 6:

> "On the technical side, our method obtains speedups from reduced memory movement, and does not lead to computational reductions."

A deeper explanation appears in Appendix A.2.2: in the batch-size-1 generative inference setting, matrix-vector products are memory-bandwidth-bound because the GPU's arithmetic units sit idle waiting for weight data to arrive from memory. Dequantizing weights on-the-fly adds compute — the kernel reads compressed values and reconstructs FP16 before multiplying — but the net effect is still faster because the memory savings dominate. The paper further clarifies that this mechanism does not transfer to different workload profiles:

> "For non-generative and large-batch applications, operations may be compute- rather than memory-bound and our kernels thus not directly applicable."

**The consequence.** The 3.25× (A100) and 4.5× (A6000) speedups reported in Table 6 are scoped to **a single, specific workload**: auto-regressive token generation at batch size 1 with the HuggingFace-accelerate distribution strategy, where communication overhead is <5% of runtime. For any other deployment setting — batched inference for throughput, non-autoregressive workloads, inference engines that already use kernel fusion or tensor parallelism — the speedups will be different, likely lower, and potentially negligible.

Two specific regime shifts matter:

1. **Batch size scaling.** At sufficiently large batch sizes, matrix-matrix multiplications replace matrix-vector products, and these become compute-bound. The paper's suggested workaround for this case — decompressing the weight matrix before the matmul — reduces GPU count (memory savings) but provides zero latency improvement per token, since the computation is identical to FP16. The paper reports that decompression "takes <1.5ms on an A100 and <3ms on an A6000 compared to 76ms/365ms for the subsequent OPT-175B FC2 layer computation with batchsize 16×1024 tokens" (Appendix A.2.2). The decompression overhead is small relative to compute time, but the point remains: the compression yields no arithmetic savings.

2. **Hardware dependency.** The A6000 speedup (4.5×) is larger than the A100 speedup (3.25×) because the A6000 has lower memory bandwidth (768 GB/s vs. 2,039 GB/s for the A100), making it more severely memory-bandwidth-bound. On GPUs with even lower bandwidth (consumer cards, edge devices), the relative speedup would be higher; on hardware with balanced compute-memory ratios or specialized matrix engines (TPUs, future GPUs with INT4 tensor core support), the speedup would be lower. The paper does not characterize this hardware sensitivity curve.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 6 provides the only speedup measurements, covering exactly two GPU models and one workload. Appendix A.2.2 describes the custom kernel strategy and explicitly delineates when it applies. There is no sweep over batch sizes, no measurement of the breakdown between matrix multiplication time and other components (attention, layer norm, residual connections), and no characterization of how speedups would change on hardware with different bandwidth-to-compute ratios.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not attempt to address this limitation — it transparently acknowledges it as a fundamental property of the method. The discussion in Section 6 frames computational reductions as "natural directions for future work" and speculates that "this can be achieved with carefully-designed GPU kernels." The specific barrier is identified: "the lack of hardware support for mixed-precision operands (e.g. FP16 x INT4) on mainstream architectures." This is a hardware limitation, not an algorithmic one — if future GPUs provide native INT4/FP16 mixed-precision tensor operations, GPTQ-compressed models could achieve both memory and compute savings without modification to the quantization procedure itself.

The implication for practitioners is clear but unstated: **GPTQ in its current form is a memory-compression method with speed benefits as a side effect for memory-bound workloads, not a general-purpose inference accelerator.** Deploying GPTQ for a latency-sensitive chatbot (batch-size-1 generation) will show meaningful speedups; deploying it for a throughput-oriented batch evaluation pipeline will reduce GPU count but not wall-clock time per sample.

### Calibration Data Quantity and Source Are Not Ablated

**The assumption or constraint.** The entire GPTQ procedure depends on a calibration dataset to compute the layer-wise Hessians `$H = 2XX^\top$` that govern the error compensation. The paper uses exactly 128 random 2048-token segments from the C4 dataset — approximately 262,000 tokens total. This choice is stated but never explored:

> "Our entire GPTQ calibration data consists of 128 random 2048 token segments from the C4 dataset, i.e., excerpts from randomly crawled websites, which represents generic text data."

The paper implicitly assumes that (a) 128 samples provide sufficient Hessian estimation quality, (b) the C4 distribution is adequately representative of downstream task distributions, and (c) these choices do not interact with the accuracy results in a way that would change any conclusions.

**The consequence.** Without calibration data ablations, three practical questions are left unanswered:

1. **How many samples are actually needed?** The computational cost of calibration is proportional to the number of samples — 128 segments of 2048 tokens means running one forward pass of the uncompressed model on ~262K tokens, which is negligible (<1 minute on OPT-175B). But could 32 samples work nearly as well? Could 512 samples meaningfully close the remaining perplexity gap to FP16, especially at 3-bit? The paper does not measure the accuracy-vs-calibration-samples curve, so practitioners do not know whether the current choice is conservative, optimal, or insufficient.

2. **Does calibration data distribution matter?** C4 is generic web text. If a practitioner plans to deploy a quantized model primarily for code generation, would calibration on code (e.g., the Stack dataset) yield better results? If the target domain is biomedical text, would PubMed abstracts provide a better Hessian estimate? The layer-wise reconstruction objective (Equation 1) minimizes error on the specific inputs seen during calibration — if those inputs are not representative of deployment inputs, the Hessian will encode the wrong sensitivity structure, and the error compensation will be optimized for the wrong distribution.

3. **Is the method robust to calibration data variation?** The paper uses a single random draw of 128 C4 segments. Without multiple runs with different random seeds, there is no estimate of variance in the final perplexity. A practitioner cannot distinguish between a 0.03 PPL degradation that is stable across calibration runs and one that fluctuates by ±0.5 PPL depending on which 128 segments happen to be selected.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. There is no ablation over the number of calibration samples, no comparison of calibration data sources, and no measurement of variance across random seeds. The paper does evaluate on C4 itself (Tables 5, 11, 12) and acknowledges that this is "not fully zero-shot" because calibration data comes from the C4 training set, but does not discuss whether this overlap inflates the C4 perplexity numbers. More importantly, the strong results on WikiText2, PTB, and zero-shot tasks (all out-of-domain relative to C4) suggest that the C4 calibration generalizes reasonably well — but we do not know whether it is optimal or whether there are domains where it would fail.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation, even in the limitations section (Section 6). The choice of 128 C4 samples is presented as a fixed experimental condition, not as a hyperparameter worthy of investigation. The paper's framing — "GPTQ does not see any task-specific data, and our results thus remain actually 'zero-shot'" — treats the calibration data agnosticism as a strength, but does not explore the boundaries of this agnosticism. A practitioner who needs to quantize a model for a specialized domain has no guidance on whether to use generic calibration data (as the paper does) or domain-specific data.

This is a consequential omission because it leaves open the possibility that some of GPTQ's accuracy advantage over RTN could be matched or exceeded by a simpler method with better-chosen (or more) calibration data. The fair comparison would be RTN with per-row quantization and GPTQ with the same calibration budget, but without ablating calibration data, we cannot assess how much of GPTQ's benefit comes from the algorithm versus from effectively leveraging the calibration data.

### Activation Quantization Is Not Addressed, Limiting End-to-End Compression

**The assumption or constraint.** GPTQ quantizes only the model's weights, leaving activations in full FP16 precision. The paper acknowledges this boundary explicitly in Section 6:

> "our study focuses on generative tasks, and does not consider activation quantization."

The justification is given in Section 6 and the introduction: activations "are not a significant bottleneck in our target scenarios." For generative inference at batch size 1, the computation is dominated by weight memory bandwidth (loading the ~175B weight parameters for each token), while activation memory is comparatively small (a single token's activations across all layers). The paper is designed for this specific bottleneck.

**The consequence.** For any deployment scenario where activation memory or activation computation is non-negligible — larger batch sizes, non-generative workloads, or models where activation memory approaches weight memory — GPTQ provides incomplete compression. The end-to-end memory footprint includes both weights and activations, and the speedup from weight-only quantization is bounded by Amdahl's law: if activations account for, say, 30% of memory traffic in a given workload, then even a perfect 0-bit weight quantization (zero memory for weights) could at most provide 1/0.3 ≈ 3.33× speedup. The practical consequence is that the memory savings and speedups in Table 6 are **best-case for weight-only compression** and would be lower in any setting where activations contribute to the memory bottleneck.

More fundamentally, weight-only quantization cannot reduce the arithmetic intensity of matrix operations: even with 3-bit weights, the kernel must dequantize to FP16 and then perform FP16 × FP16 multiplications. Full-stack quantization (both weights and activations at low precision) would enable INT4 × INT4 or INT3 × INT3 operations, where both memory bandwidth *and* arithmetic throughput improve. The paper's approach cannot deliver these compound benefits because it leaves half the problem (activations) unaddressed.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides no measurements of activation memory footprint for the target workloads, no analysis of what fraction of end-to-end memory traffic is due to weights vs. activations, and no experiments combining GPTQ with activation quantization. Section 6 states that activation quantization "can be supported using orthogonal techniques (Yao et al., 2022)," but does not demonstrate compatibility — there is no result showing GPTQ + activation quantization working together on a large model.

**Mitigation status.** The paper treats this as out of scope and defers to future work. The claim is that activation quantization techniques are "orthogonal" and can be combined with GPTQ, but this is asserted rather than demonstrated. Combining them may not be straightforward:

- **Distribution shift interaction.** GPTQ compensates for weight quantization error by adjusting remaining weights based on the Hessian computed from FP16 activations. If activations are subsequently quantized, the actual layer inputs will differ from those used to compute the Hessian, potentially invalidating the optimality of the compensation. The paper already shows that using post-quantization inputs (from preceding quantized blocks) rather than full-precision inputs improves results — but this block-wise correction applies only to weight quantization, not to activation quantization.

- **Error propagation.** Activation quantization introduces additional error at each layer's output. GPTQ's layer-wise objective assumes full-precision inputs and optimizes weight quantization error independently per layer. With quantized activations, the error at layer `$\ell$` depends on both weight quantization error in layer `$\ell$` and activation quantization error propagated from layer `$\ell - 1$`. The paper's block-wise input correction partially addresses this for weights but does not model activation quantization error.

- **Numerical interaction.** The Cholesky decomposition and inverse Hessian computations assume FP16 inputs. Aggressive activation quantization (e.g., INT8 or lower) would change the conditioning of `$XX^\top$` and potentially reintroduce the numerical instability that the Cholesky reformulation was designed to solve.

The paper's deferral to "orthogonal techniques" is reasonable — GPTQ solves one important problem (weight compression) well, and combining it with activation quantization is a natural next step — but the claim of orthogonality is unvalidated. A practitioner who needs end-to-end 4-bit inference (to maximize speedup, fit on even smaller hardware, or leverage INT4 tensor cores) cannot use GPTQ alone and has no evidence that GPTQ + activation quantization will work synergistically.

### Evaluation Is Confined to Perplexity and Limited Zero-Shot Benchmarks, Leaving Generation Quality Uncharacterized

**The assumption or constraint.** All accuracy evaluations use perplexity (on WikiText2, PTB, C4) or accuracy on structured zero-shot tasks (LAMBADA, PIQA, ARC, StoryCloze). The paper focuses on these because they are "standard in the literature" and "known to be particularly sensitive to model quantization" (Section 5). However, these metrics are proxies for the thing most users care about: **the quality of text the model generates**. A perplexity degradation of 0.03 on WikiText2 (GPTQ at 4-bit on OPT-175B) does not directly translate to a claim that the quantized model will produce coherent, factually accurate, or stylistically appropriate completions in a deployed application.

The paper's ethics statement acknowledges this gap explicitly:

> "We believe a thorough study of the impact of compression upon secondary measures, and in particular bias effects (Bender et al., 2021) is warranted, and may be rendered easier through our work."

**The consequence.** Several degradation modes are invisible to perplexity and structured zero-shot accuracy but matter enormously in practice:

1. **Long-range coherence.** Perplexity measures next-token prediction accuracy one token at a time. A model can have near-perfect perplexity but still produce completions that are globally incoherent — repeating phrases, contradicting earlier statements, or losing track of the topic after several sentences. Quantization noise, even at levels that leave per-token predictions largely intact, could accumulate over long sequences and degrade coherence in ways that perplexity — which averages across all tokens — might not capture.

2. **Factual accuracy and reasoning.** The zero-shot tasks (LAMBADA, ARC, PIQA) test narrow capabilities: word prediction given context, multiple-choice science questions, physical commonsense. These do not cover open-ended generation with factual constraints (e.g., summarization, question answering with long-form responses, instruction following). A quantized model might maintain LAMBADA accuracy while generating subtly incorrect factual claims that a full-precision model would avoid.

3. **Output distribution shifts (mode collapse, diversity loss).** Quantization constrains the expressivity of the weight space, which could manifest as reduced diversity in generated outputs — the model might gravitate toward a smaller set of "safe" completions rather than exploring the full distribution. Perplexity would not detect this: a model that always outputs the single most probable token (zero diversity) can have excellent perplexity on a dataset that aligns with that token.

4. **Calibration and confidence.** Quantization may distort the model's confidence estimates — predicted probabilities may become miscalibrated (overconfident or underconfident) even when the argmax prediction remains correct. This matters for applications that threshold on confidence or use the model's probabilities downstream.

5. **Safety and bias amplification.** Compression can interact with model biases in unpredictable ways. The paper's ethics statement highlights this concern, and the finding that OPT-66B (with dead neurons) was harder to quantize suggests that certain weight configurations — which might correlate with learned biases — could be differentially affected by quantization.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None of these properties are measured. The paper reports perplexity and zero-shot task accuracy only. There are no human evaluations, no automated generation quality metrics (BLEU, ROUGE, MAUVE), no diversity measurements, no calibration curves, and no bias audits on the quantized models. The paper does show (Table 5) that GPTQ sometimes *improves* LAMBADA accuracy over FP16 (OPT-175B at 4-bit: 76.80% vs. 75.59%), suggesting that compression can act as regularizer — but whether this regularization uniformly improves or selectively degrades certain capabilities is not investigated.

**Mitigation status.** The paper flags this as future work in the ethics statement but makes no attempt to address it experimentally. The acknowledgment is responsible, but it means that a practitioner deploying GPTQ-compressed models in a user-facing application has no evidence about generation quality beyond the proxy metrics. The finding that GPTQ preserves perplexity is necessary but not sufficient to guarantee that the compressed model is a drop-in replacement for the full-precision version in all use cases.

This limitation is partially mitigated by the paper's choice of metrics being the **community standard**. Prior works on large-model quantization (ZeroQuant, LLM.int8(), nuQmm) also evaluate primarily on perplexity. GPTQ is not uniquely deficient here — it matches the evaluation norms of its comparator papers. But this also means the field as a whole has not established that perplexity preservation implies generation quality preservation under aggressive quantization. The paper could have strengthened its claims by including even a small-scale generation quality study (e.g., human preference judgments on completions from quantized vs. FP16 models), which would have set a higher bar for future work.

### The Numerical Stability Fix (Cholesky Reformulation) Is Empirically Validated but Not Analytically Characterized

**The assumption or constraint.** The Cholesky reformulation is motivated by a serious empirical problem: the original OBQ inverse Hessian downdate procedure "becomes indefinite" at scale, causing "arbitrarily-bad quantization." The paper states that this "almost certainly occurs for at least a few layers on models that are larger than a few billion parameters." The Cholesky decomposition is presented as the solution, computed once upfront to pre-extract all needed compensation information in a numerically stable way. Combined with mild dampening (adding `$\lambda$` = 1% of the average diagonal to the Hessian), the paper claims this is "robust enough to execute on huge models without issues."

**The consequence.** The paper provides no **quantitative characterization** of the numerical failure mode or the Cholesky fix. Several important properties are unmeasured:

1. **Failure rate as a function of model size.** At what exact parameter count does the dampening-only approach begin to fail? Is the failure gradual (increasing perplexity degradation) or catastrophic (sudden collapse to random)? Does it affect all layers or only specific layer types (e.g., early attention projections vs. later feed-forward layers)?

2. **Dampening-Cholesky interaction.** The paper uses Cholesky "combined with mild dampening." Is the dampening still necessary when using Cholesky, or does Cholesky alone suffice? If dampening is needed, is 1% of mean diagonal still optimal, or does the Cholesky reformulation change the sensitivity to this hyperparameter?

3. **Accuracy cost of Cholesky vs. ideal downdate.** In exact arithmetic, the row-by-row Gaussian elimination downdate and the Cholesky precomputation produce identical compensation coefficients. In floating-point, they differ due to rounding error accumulation patterns. Is the Cholesky version more or less accurate than the downdate version *when the downdate version would be numerically stable*? The paper shows GPTQ matching or slightly outperforming OBQ on small models (Appendix Table 8), but this comparison conflates the order change (arbitrary vs. greedy) with the numerical method change (Cholesky vs. downdate). We cannot isolate the effect of Cholesky alone on accuracy.

4. **Sensitivity to dampening strength.** The dampening parameter `$\lambda$` = 1% of mean diagonal is used for all models. Without a sweep over `$\lambda$`, we do not know whether this value is near-optimal, conservative, or aggressive. At very large model scales, could a different `$\lambda$` further improve accuracy? At small scales, would less dampening (or none) yield better results?

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The only evidence is that GPTQ *works* on 175B models — the method produces models with perplexity near FP16 baselines (Tables 3-5). This is strong evidence that the numerical issues have been *sufficiently* addressed, but it provides no insight into whether the solution is optimal, over-engineered, or brittle to changes in model architecture or precision. The paper does not present:
- Side-by-side comparison of dampening-only vs. Cholesky on a model where dampening-only fails (to characterize the failure mode).
- Ablation over dampening strength.
- Measurement of the condition number or minimum eigenvalue of `$H^{-1}$` at different stages of the quantization process.
- Layer-wise analysis showing which layers are most susceptible to numerical instability.

**Mitigation status.** The paper treats the Cholesky reformulation as an engineering solution to an observed problem, validated by the fact that the overall method succeeds at scale. This is pragmatically reasonable — the method works, and the fix is in the released code — but it leaves the numerical foundations less well-understood than they could be. A practitioner who wants to apply GPTQ to a new model architecture (e.g., with different layer norms, activation functions, or residual connections) has no diagnostic tools to determine whether the numerical stability is maintained or whether the dampening parameter needs adjustment. If future hardware uses lower-precision arithmetic (e.g., FP8 for the Cholesky decomposition), the numerical safety margin is unknown.

This limitation is **practically consequential but analytically subtle**. The paper's approach — identify the failure mode, propose a principled fix grounded in numerical linear algebra (Cholesky is backward-stable), and demonstrate that it works empirically — is sound engineering. But the gap between "it works on the tested models" and "it will work reliably on all large Transformer variants" is unquantified, and the absence of diagnostic measurements makes it difficult for practitioners to assess safety margins for their specific use case.

---

### No Evidence on Whether Compression Benefits Transfer to Fine-Tuning or Downstream Adaptation

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper evaluates GPTQ exclusively in a **zero-shot, frozen-model setting**: the quantized model is deployed as-is, without any further training. All perplexity and accuracy measurements are on the quantized weights produced directly by GPTQ. The implicit assumption is that the primary use case for large language model compression is frozen inference — download the model, compress it, run it.

**The consequence.** Many real-world deployments do not use large language models in a purely zero-shot fashion. Common patterns include:

1. **Fine-tuning on domain-specific data.** A practitioner downloads OPT-175B, fine-tunes it on their proprietary documents or task-specific dataset, and then deploys. Would starting from a GPTQ-compressed checkpoint and fine-tuning (with quantization-aware training or in full precision after dequantization) produce a model as good as fine-tuning from the full-precision checkpoint and then quantizing?

2. **Parameter-efficient adaptation (LoRA, adapters, prefix tuning).** Methods that add small trainable parameters to a frozen backbone are a popular way to adapt LLMs. Does GPTQ compression interact negatively with these methods? For example, if a LoRA adapter is trained on the FP16 model and then the base weights are quantized, do the adapter weights remain effective given that the base weights have changed?

3. **In-context learning sensitivity.** The zero-shot evaluations test the model's ability to answer from prompts alone. But many applications use few-shot prompts or retrieval-augmented generation. The paper's perplexity metric is measured on standard datasets without in-context examples. Does quantization disproportionately degrade the model's ability to leverage in-context examples?

The paper provides no evidence on any of these scenarios.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. The evaluation is strictly zero-shot. The one suggestive finding is that GPTQ sometimes improves accuracy over FP16 (e.g., OPT-175B LAMBADA: 76.80% vs. 75.59%), which hints that compression acts as a regularizer that could be beneficial for generalization in some settings. But whether this regularization effect is helpful or harmful when combined with further task-specific training is unexplored.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation. The title and introduction frame the contribution around inference — "GPTQ: Accurate Post-Training Quantization for Generative Pre-Trained Transformers" — and the evaluation matches this scope. However, for a practitioner deciding whether to adopt GPTQ, the downstream adaptation question is critical. If quantization significantly impairs fine-tunability, then the correct workflow might be: fine-tune the FP16 model first, then apply GPTQ to the fine-tuned checkpoint (which the paper does support, since GPTQ works on any pretrained weights). But if quantization does *not* impair fine-tunability, the workflow could be: quantize first (saving storage and memory during fine-tuning), then fine-tune the quantized model. Without evidence, practitioners must guess.

This limitation is **scoped by the paper's explicit focus on inference** and is therefore less a flaw than a boundary condition. The paper does not claim to address fine-tuning and should not be penalized for not doing so. However, given that the target models (OPT-175B, BLOOM-176B) are foundation models that users are highly likely to adapt rather than use zero-shot, the absence of even a small-scale fine-tuning experiment (e.g., quantize OPT-1.3B, fine-tune on a downstream task, compare to FP16 fine-tuning) represents a missed opportunity to substantially increase the practical relevance of the evaluation.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

GPTQ represents an **inflection point in the deployability of large language models**, not because it introduces fundamentally new mathematics for quantization — the second-order compensation mechanism is inherited directly from OBQ (Frantar et al., 2022) — but because it demonstrates that a conceptually sophisticated optimization procedure can be made computationally tractable at the scale of 175 billion parameters through careful algorithmic engineering. The shift is not theoretical but **pragmatic**: before GPTQ, the field implicitly accepted a tradeoff where accurate quantization was only feasible for models with tens of millions of parameters, and models with hundreds of billions could only be quantized via trivial rounding that collapsed below 8 bits. GPTQ breaks this dichotomy.

The most consequential reframing the paper introduces is the **arbitrary-order hypothesis**: the claim that quantization order — the precise sequence in which weights are rounded and compensated — matters little for the final reconstruction error in large, heavily overparameterized layers. If this finding generalizes beyond the OPT and BLOOM families, it fundamentally changes how researchers should think about second-order compression. The greedy weighting scheme that OBQ inherited from Optimal Brain Surgeon was not merely an implementation detail; it was the conceptual centerpiece of the approach, grounded in the intuition that careful sequential decision-making is essential for navigating high-dimensional error landscapes. GPTQ's evidence that this intuition breaks down at scale — that the error landscape is flat enough that any fixed order suffices — converts what appeared to be a combinatorial optimization problem into a linear pass over columns. This is a **diagnostic finding about the geometry of overparameterized networks**, not just a speedup trick.

The paper also reconciles a latent tension in the quantization literature between accuracy and scalability. Prior to GPTQ, one could look at OBQ and conclude that accurate quantization inherently requires per-weight Hessian updates (and therefore cubic complexity), or one could look at LLM.int8() and conclude that scalable quantization inherently means round-to-nearest (and therefore collapse at high compression rates). GPTQ shows that neither conclusion is necessary: the mechanism of second-order compensation can be preserved while the computational structure is radically restructured. The specific tension it resolves is **whether OBQ's accuracy is tied to its computational complexity**. The answer, GPTQ demonstrates, is no — the accuracy comes from the Hessian-guided error propagation, not from the greedy ordering or the per-weight inverse updates. Disentangling these two aspects of OBQ (the mathematical mechanism from its algorithmic realization) is the paper's primary intellectual contribution.

The paper also shifts the **bottleneck narrative** for inference optimization. Before GPTQ, the conversation around large model inference was dominated by the observation that 8-bit quantization works (LLM.int8()) but that pushing to 3-4 bits caused catastrophic degradation with known methods. The implicit message was that 8 bits might be a fundamental floor for post-training compression. GPTQ demonstrates that the floor was an artifact of method choice, not a property of the models themselves — the same models that collapse to near-random perplexity under 3-bit RTN achieve perplexity within 0.3-0.5 points of FP16 under GPTQ. This reframes the research agenda: the question is no longer *whether* 3-4 bit quantization of giant language models is possible, but *how to make it robust across model families, architectures, and deployment scenarios*.

One research direction that becomes **substantially less attractive** in light of GPTQ is the development of ever-more-elaborate weight selection heuristics for layer-wise quantization. The arbitrary-order finding suggests that investing effort in optimal weight ordering — a focus of OBQ and implicit in the MCMC-inspired view of quantization as sequential decision-making — yields diminishing returns at scale. Research effort is better directed at improving the numerical stability and throughput of fixed-order compensation, or at extending the framework to handle activation quantization and other compression dimensions.

Conversely, GPTQ makes **Hessian-based error compensation the default paradigm** for any future post-training compression method targeting large models. The paper establishes a new baseline: any method claiming to compress 100B+ models to 3-4 bits must match or exceed GPTQ's accuracy, and any method claiming superior accuracy must do so without sacrificing GPTQ's practical runtime (a few hours on a single GPU). This raises the bar for future work — it is no longer sufficient to demonstrate accuracy on ResNet-50 or BERT-base and claim the method "scales."

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Characterizing the arbitrary-order hypothesis across architectures and modalities.** GPTQ demonstrates that quantization order doesn't matter much for OPT and BLOOM Transformer layers, but the paper's hypothesis for why — that the error landscape becomes flat as layer width grows, making any order roughly equivalent — is untested. A strong follow-up would systematically measure the variance in final reconstruction error across different fixed orders (e.g., left-to-right, right-to-left, random, highest-magnitude-first) as a function of layer width, depth, and model family. The key measurement is the standard deviation of layer-wise reconstruction error across, say, 100 random orders, plotted against layer dimension `$d$`. If the arbitrary-order hypothesis is correct, this variance should shrink with `$d$` and be negligible for `$d > 4096$`. If it does not — if some orders are consistently worse — then GPTQ's left-to-right default may be accidentally near-optimal for the tested models, and the finding would not generalize. This experiment would also reveal whether certain layer types (attention projections vs. feed-forward layers) are more order-sensitive, which could inform adaptive ordering strategies.

**Coupling GPTQ with activation quantization and measuring the interaction.** The paper explicitly defers activation quantization to "orthogonal techniques" (Yao et al., 2022), but the interaction is not obviously orthogonal. GPTQ's Hessian is computed from FP16 activations; if activations are subsequently quantized, the actual layer inputs differ from those used for weight compensation, potentially invalidating the optimality guarantees. A concrete experiment would: (1) apply GPTQ to OPT-13B (large enough for meaningful results, small enough for rapid iteration), (2) quantize activations to INT8 using a standard method (e.g., the dynamic quantization in ZeroQuant), (3) measure the perplexity gap between (a) GPTQ weights + FP16 activations, (b) GPTQ weights + INT8 activations, and (c) RTN weights + INT8 activations (as a baseline). The critical finding would be whether the degradation from adding activation quantization to GPTQ is larger than, equal to, or smaller than the degradation from adding it to RTN — i.e., whether GPTQ's weight compensation makes the model more or less robust to activation noise. If GPTQ magnifies activation quantization error (because the compensation was optimized for FP16 inputs), this would motivate joint optimization or iterative recalibration.

**Adaptive dampening informed by the Hessian spectrum.** The paper uses a fixed dampening parameter `$\lambda$` = 1% of the average diagonal of `$2XX^\top$` for all layers and all models, with the Cholesky reformulation providing a safety net against the numerical instability that dampening alone cannot prevent on large models. But this one-size-fits-all choice is unlikely to be optimal: layers with well-conditioned Hessians (eigenvalues concentrated near the mean) may need less dampening, while layers with near-zero eigenvalues may need more. A principled follow-up would compute the eigenvalue spectrum of `$H$` for each layer before quantization, then set `$\lambda$` adaptively — for instance, proportional to the smallest eigenvalue or to the condition number. The experiment would compare fixed-`$\lambda$` GPTQ against adaptive-`$\lambda$` GPTQ on OPT and BLOOM at 3-bit, measuring whether adaptive dampening (a) further closes the perplexity gap to FP16 on the largest models, and (b) prevents the OPT-66B-style degradation observed when models have pathological weight distributions. This would also provide diagnostic value: if adaptive dampening rescues OPT-66B, it suggests the "dead neuron" problem manifests through Hessian ill-conditioning and can be compensated for; if it does not, the failure mode is something deeper that second-order methods cannot address.

**Calibration data optimization: how much, what kind, and does it matter?** GPTQ uses 128 random 2048-token C4 segments. The paper provides no ablation, so practitioners have no guidance on calibration data requirements. A systematic study would sweep calibration set sizes (8, 32, 128, 512, 2048 segments) on a mid-sized model (OPT-6.7B or OPT-13B) at 3-bit and 4-bit, measuring perplexity on both in-domain (C4) and out-of-domain (WikiText2, PTB) benchmarks. The key question is whether the perplexity-vs-calibration-samples curve saturates at 128, suggesting the paper's choice is conservative, or continues improving, suggesting that additional calibration data could close the remaining gap to FP16. A second axis would test calibration data distribution: replace C4 with WikiText2, Python code, or domain-specific text and measure cross-domain generalization. If WikiText2-calibrated models perform substantially better on WikiText2 evaluation (and worse on C4), it suggests that practitioners should calibrate on deployment-domain data; if the effect is negligible, GPTQ's domain-agnosticism is genuinely robust. The paper's finding that block-wise input correction "brings noticeable improvements" (Section 5) also raises the question of whether calibration data should be drawn from the same distribution as the target task or a diverse mix — a concrete experiment that would directly inform deployment practice.

**Fine-tuning quantized models and measuring capability retention.** The paper evaluates GPTQ exclusively in a frozen, zero-shot setting. The most practically important open question is whether a GPTQ-compressed model can serve as a starting point for downstream fine-tuning without sacrificing the quality of the adapted model. The concrete experiment: take OPT-1.3B (small enough for affordable fine-tuning), apply GPTQ at 4-bit and 3-bit, then fine-tune both the quantized and FP16 versions on a standard suite of downstream tasks (e.g., GLUE or SuperGLUE for classification, SAMSum for summarization, a code generation task). Compare: (a) fine-tune FP16, then quantize with GPTQ vs. (b) quantize with GPTQ, then fine-tune (with the quantized weights either frozen as a LoRA base or updated via quantization-aware training). The finding would determine whether the recommended workflow is "fine-tune first, then compress" or "compress first, then adapt" — a decision that affects the storage and compute costs of the entire model lifecycle. Additionally, evaluating the fine-tuned quantized models on generation quality metrics (not just classification accuracy) would begin to address the paper's acknowledged gap around generation quality evaluation under compression.

**Scaling laws for post-training compression.** The paper observes that "larger models generally appear easier to quantize" — the perplexity gap between GPTQ and FP16 narrows monotonically with model size (Figure 1, Tables 3-4). This hints at a systematic relationship between model scale and quantizability, analogous to the scaling laws that relate model size, data, and pretraining loss (Hoffmann et al., 2022). A scaling-law study would measure the quantization error (perplexity degradation) as a function of model parameters `$N$` and bitwidth `$b$` for a fixed model family (e.g., OPT at 125M, 350M, 1.3B, 2.7B, 6.7B, 13B, 30B, 66B, 175B) and fit a parametric form `$\Delta \text{PPL}(N, b) = \alpha \cdot N^{-\beta} \cdot f(b)$`. The exponent `$\beta$` would quantify how quickly quantizability improves with scale; the function `$f(b)$` would capture the nonlinear degradation as bitwidth decreases. If such a law holds, it would enable predicting the minimum model size needed to achieve a target perplexity at a given bitwidth — directly informing the pretraining-vs-compression tradeoff the paper raises but does not resolve. A strong test would be whether the fitted law predicts the quantizability of a held-out model size (e.g., fit on all OPT models except 30B, then test on 30B).

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Single-GPU deployment of 175B-class models for interactive applications.** The most immediate practical application, directly demonstrated in the paper (Table 6), is running OPT-175B or BLOOM-176B on a single NVIDIA A100 (80GB) GPU at 3-bit precision. Before GPTQ, this required 5 GPUs for OPT-175B. The practical scenario is an organization that wants to deploy a state-of-the-art large language model for an interactive chatbot, code completion service, or text generation API but cannot afford or does not have access to multi-GPU instances. With GPTQ, a single A100 instance — available from cloud providers at the time of writing — can serve the full model at 71ms per token (batch size 1), which is usable for real-time interaction. The cost reduction is multiplicative: 5× fewer GPUs and 3.25× lower latency means roughly 16× improvement in cost-per-token for interactive inference. For organizations deploying on more cost-effective hardware (A6000, 48GB), two GPUs suffice, with a 4.5× latency improvement over the 8-GPU FP16 baseline. The concrete deployment recipe is: download OPT-175B from HuggingFace, run GPTQ for ~4 hours on a single A100 to produce the 3-bit checkpoint, then deploy with the provided custom CUDA kernels.

**Enabling on-device or edge-deployment of mid-sized language models.** While the paper focuses on the largest models, the finding that GPTQ works across the entire OPT and BLOOM model families (Tables 3-4) means smaller models can be compressed to fit within the memory budgets of consumer GPUs, mobile devices, or embedded systems. For example, OPT-6.7B at 3-bit occupies approximately 2.5GB for weights (6.7B × 3/8 bytes = 2.51GB), plus activations and overhead, putting it within reach of a 6-8GB consumer GPU. At 4-bit, OPT-13B occupies approximately 6.5GB, fitting within a 8GB GPU. These are models that, in FP16, would require 13.4GB and 26GB respectively — exceeding consumer hardware. The practical scenario is a privacy-sensitive application (medical text processing, legal document analysis, on-device code completion) where data cannot leave the user's machine and cloud API calls are prohibited. GPTQ makes it feasible to run a capable language model entirely locally. The paper provides everything needed (code, calibration procedure, kernel examples) to compress any model in the OPT or BLOOM families and deploy on a single consumer GPU.

**Cost-efficient batch inference pipelines with model-size compression.** For organizations running large-scale batch inference — evaluating thousands of documents through a language model for classification, extraction, or scoring — the dominant cost is GPU-hours. Before GPTQ, the choice was between using a smaller FP16 model (lower quality, lower cost) or a larger model requiring expensive multi-GPU instances (higher quality, higher cost). GPTQ at 4-bit on OPT-175B (perplexity 8.37 vs. 8.34 FP16, a 0.03 degradation) effectively makes the 175B model behave like it has the memory footprint of a ~44B FP16 model (175B × 4/16 = 43.75B). For batch inference where the workload is compute-bound (large batch sizes), the paper's suggested approach is decompress-before-multiply, which eliminates the memory savings but keeps the GPU-count reduction: a 3-bit OPT-175B runs on 1 GPU instead of 5. For a batch inference job that would require 5 A100s for a week, GPTQ reduces it to 1 A100 for a week — an ~5× cost reduction in GPU rental, at negligible accuracy loss. The compressed checkpoint also costs less to store and transfer, which matters for organizations maintaining many model versions or distributing models to edge locations.

**Quantized models as a foundation for compute-limited research and education.** A less commercial but societally significant application is enabling academic researchers, students, and practitioners in resource-constrained environments to experiment with large language models. The paper's finding that OPT-175B at 3-bit fits on a single consumer-grade GPU (with 24GB, using aggressive memory management) means that a graduate student with a single RTX 3090 or RTX 4090 can load and study a 175B-parameter model — something that would otherwise require institutional-level compute resources. This directly addresses the paper's stated motivation of "making these models available to more researchers and practitioners." The released code and pre-computed quantized checkpoints (if provided) would allow someone to download a 3-bit OPT-175B and start doing interpretability research, bias auditing, or prompting experiments without writing a grant for GPU cluster access. The practical workflow is: download the quantized checkpoint (~65GB for 3-bit OPT-175B), load onto a single high-end consumer GPU with CPU offloading for the layers that don't fit (or use two GPUs following the A6000 setup in Table 6), and run standard HuggingFace generation pipelines with the custom kernels. The paper already demonstrates that the quantized model performs competitively on zero-shot benchmarks (LAMBADA, ARC, PIQA), so the model is genuinely useful for research, not just a toy.

### When to Prefer This Method

GPTQ's positioning relative to alternatives is explicit in the paper and can be summarized as a decision framework:

- **Prefer GPTQ when** the target is 3-4 bit weight quantization of a large Transformer model (1B+ parameters, up to 175B+) for generative inference at low batch sizes, and you have access to a single GPU for both quantization (a few hours) and deployment. GPTQ is the only method demonstrated to maintain accuracy at these compression rates at this scale: at 4-bit, perplexity degradation is 0.03 on OPT-175B (vs. 2.2 for RTN); at 3-bit, degradation is 0.34 (vs. collapse to 7,300 PPL for RTN). The method provides both GPU-count reduction (5→1 for OPT-175B on A100) and latency reduction (3.25× on A100, 4.5× on A6000) for auto-regressive generation.

- **Prefer round-to-nearest (RTN / LLM.int8()) when** the target is 8-bit quantization, where RTN works adequately and GPTQ's additional complexity provides no benefit. The paper does not evaluate 8-bit performance, but the rationale is straightforward: RTN at 8-bit preserves accuracy (as shown by LLM.int8()), so the additional hours of GPU time for GPTQ's Hessian computation and Cholesky decomposition are unnecessary. RTN also requires no calibration data and is trivial to implement.

- **Prefer training-based quantization methods when** the model is small enough for fine-tuning (under ~1B parameters) or when the deployment scenario involves compute-bound rather than memory-bound inference, and the hardware supports native INT4 or INT8 arithmetic. Training-based methods can optimize for end-to-end task loss rather than layer-wise reconstruction, potentially achieving better accuracy than GPTQ's one-shot approach, and can quantize activations as well as weights, enabling arithmetic speedups from low-precision matrix multiplications. However, the paper shows that for models above 1B parameters, the training cost becomes prohibitive (ZeroQuant-LKD takes ~3 hours for 1.3B parameters; GPTQ takes ~4 hours for 175B).

- **Combine GPTQ with grouping when** the bitwidth budget allows a small overhead (~0.02-0.15 bits per weight) in exchange for measurably improved accuracy. The paper shows (Table 5) that 3-bit with group-size 128 achieves perplexity within 0.15 of FP16 on BLOOM-176B (8.26 vs. 8.11), essentially matching uncompressed quality at ~5.3× compression. At 2-bit, grouping is essential to achieve reasonable accuracy (9.58 PPL at group-size 128 vs. a collapsed baseline without grouping, Table 7).

The paper does not propose a tradeoff against pruning or distillation methods, and does not evaluate GPTQ in combination with other compression techniques beyond grouping, so a broader "compression method selection" framework is not supported by the paper's evidence.
