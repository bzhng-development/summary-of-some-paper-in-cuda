Here are the takeaways from this Jordan Cutler interview (front-end specialist who went from L3 to Senior / L5 at Gusto in 2 years, then to Qualified, now building toward Staff):

**Landing at Gusto after a failed Twitter internship**

Jordan started his first job at Gusto in 2019 nursing real imposter syndrome—not getting a return offer from Twitter as an intern “really messed with me” and left him convinced he’d be fired within months. He reflects on what went wrong: he was so heads-down on his intern project that he took no feedback, wasn’t a team player, and “even pushed back on some Staff engineers’ suggestions as an intern.” The core lesson he carried into Gusto was that it’s “incredibly important to be a contributing member of the team and to show you are a ‘sponge’ for growth from the people around you.” That insight became the foundation of his entire first year.

He made three immediate changes: **asking for feedback constantly** from his manager and teammates; **pairing with everyone on the team** and genuinely embracing Gusto’s culture; and **making the team better in any way he could**—documenting what he learned, improving onboarding for future hires, and bringing proposed process improvements directly to his manager with solutions already prepped (“bringing solutions and doing the work for him”). These weren’t grand gestures; they were deliberate habits that signaled a posture shift from his intern days. The very first week on the job, he broke production—and still survived, because the trust-building groundwork was already in motion.

**Junior → Mid-level (L3 to L4) in one year via a scrappy dev-tool project**

The acceleration out of junior wasn’t driven by assigned sprint tickets. Jordan noticed a friction point that frustrated every engineer on the team: loading the local dev environment with test data took at least ten minutes per change cycle. He took a messy one-page Google Doc to his manager with an idea to shrink that to five seconds. The manager pitched it to the PM, got it prioritized, and because Jordan had proposed it, they let him *lead* the project. That single initiative gave him ownership of a cross-functional effort, visibility across the company (he presented it at the all-hands and the CTO came up afterward to compliment the presentation—“mind-blowing” to him at the time), and a productivity boost the team used for years. The promotion to L4 arrived within one year.

The pattern he draws out is that he “probably wouldn’t have had this opportunity so early on if I wasn’t looking for ways to make the team better. Or if I had only stuck to my assigned work.” The assignment was necessary but the visibility and leadership reps came from noticing a team-wide pain point and volunteering a solution.

**A structured feedback cadence for the mid-level year**

After the L4 promotion, his manager was explicit: “don’t expect a promotion so soon again.” That set his expectations but didn’t dial back his ambition. The engine for his growth that year was a **color-coded expectations check-in every six weeks.** Together they mapped the L5 (Senior) expectations green, yellow, or red by his current strengths and gaps. The rhythm was simple: turn reds to yellows and yellows to greens. If everything stayed green for three to six months, they’d make the promotion case. Jordan recommends this process to anyone wanting clear feedback and a manager actively scanning for stretch opportunities—it turns the amorphous “get to senior” into a trackable, mutual project.

**Three pillars of the Senior promotion**

Looking back on what actually powered the jump to L5, he identifies three contributions that cut across the work:

- **Reliability.** He kept shipping and leading team projects with minimal bugs. Not flashy, but it built a reputation as someone whose work didn’t need babysitting.
- **Domain expertise.** He became a GraphQL and front-end subject matter expert across multiple product domains, such that engineers leveled *above* him came to him for advice.
- **Team effectiveness initiatives.** He led an API re-architecture adopted by multiple teams to colocate database ORM queries, and overhauled on-call processes—improving error tracking and rooting out the persistent noisy alerts that made on-call miserable.

Of the three, becoming a domain expert is the one he’d single out as the top piece of advice. The origin story is concrete: the mobile team was exploring GraphQL, he asked his manager to reimburse a book and a Udemy course, and “I read the book overnight and learned a ton about API design using GraphQL.” That small investment let him participate meaningfully in technical discussions and offer real suggestions, which built trust. Soon the team gave him responsibility for redesigning their APIs on GraphQL—creating an ownership area and cementing him as the expert. “Once you’re a subject matter expert, tons of opportunities will come your way.” The teaching, the lead roles on new projects, the requests for pattern adoption—all followed. For Senior, that area of ownership was exactly what the calibration discussion needed.

**Being the main driver of your own growth**

The capstone lesson Jordan returns to is ownership of the trajectory itself. The people around you will help—managers, mentors, peers—but “you should be the one seeking mentorship, getting the answers you need from your manager, and learning from others what your areas of development are.” The promotion to Senior, even amid uncertainty and fear of whether he’d actually get it, only happened because he actively pushed: sought what was needed and advocated for being put up. **Be the main driver.** After reaching Senior, his pace changed—he moved to a front-end-focused team, left Gusto about a year ago to join Qualified as a front-end specialist, and is now aiming for Staff Engineer, a timeline he figures will take a few more years. “Everyone’s career path is different,” he notes, inviting readers to take what resonates and adapt it to their own situation rather than treat his trajectory as a rigid template. That promotion to Senior came about two years ago, making him the youngest Senior Engineer at Gusto among 200+ engineers.

---

His **be-the-main-driver** stance threads through every phase: he didn’t wait for permission to fix the local-dev bottleneck, he didn’t wait for GraphQL expertise to be assigned to him, and he didn’t wait for his manager to guess when he was ready for promotion. The **color-coded expectations check-in** turned that drive into a repeatable mechanism, while the overnight-book-deep-dive pattern turned curiosity into ownership. Both tactics echo his foundational post-Twitter insight that growth comes from being a “sponge”—but a sponge that actively seeks the water, rather than one that passively absorbs whatever splashes its way.
