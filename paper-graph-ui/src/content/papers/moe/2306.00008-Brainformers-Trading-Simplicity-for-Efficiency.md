# Brainformers: Trading Simplicity for Efficiency

**ArXiv:** [2306.00008](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.00008)

## 🎯 Pitch

Replacing the uniform transformer backbone with a searched mix of gated experts, dense layers, and sparse attention outperforms GLaM by achieving 5× faster step time and 2× faster training convergence—with 3% higher SuperGLUE—at matched compute, proving architectural irregularity is a major lever for scaling.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces the **Brainformer**, a complex Transformer architecture block discovered through regularized evolutionary search that trades the uniform interleaving of attention and feed-forward layers for a heterogeneous, non-uniform stack of diverse sub-layers—including sparsely gated feed-forward layers, dense feed-forward layers, and attention layers—with tunable model dimensions, gating functions, and activation types. Evaluated against GLaM on a 1.6-trillion-token corpus at multiple scales, the Brainformer achieves 5× faster step time and 2× faster training convergence at the 8B-activated-parameter scale compared to its GLaM counterpart, while delivering a 3% higher SuperGLUE fine-tuning score at the 1B-scale and outperforming a Primer dense model on fewshot generative tasks by a large margin, establishing that block-wise architectural heterogeneity searched under a fixed training-time constraint can substantially improve both quality and efficiency without requiring increased compute per token.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Efficiency Stagnation in Transformer Architecture Design

The paper addresses a fundamental, underexamined question in transformer architecture design: **must the backbone of a transformer be uniform?** In the standard recipe inherited from Vaswani et al. (2017), transformer layers alternate strictly between self-attention and dense feed-forward sub-layers in a fixed pattern, repeated identically throughout the network depth. While this uniformity offers conceptual simplicity and engineering convenience, the authors argue it may be substantially suboptimal for computational efficiency and model quality. The paper asks whether breaking this regularity—by composing different layer types in different orders, varying layer widths, and blending sparsely activated modules in non-uniform patterns—can unlock better training convergence and downstream performance at equivalent or lower computational cost.

The gap this identifies is specific: the dominant paradigm in transformer scaling has treated the architecture as a given and focused on scaling the number of layers, the model dimension, the training tokens, or the total parameters (Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022). Architecture itself—the *internal arrangement* of sub-layers—has received comparatively little systematic optimization, particularly when sparsely activated mixture-of-experts (MoE) modules are introduced. The paper argues that this neglect leaves substantial efficiency gains on the table: two models with the same total FLOPs or activated parameters can have very different training speeds and final qualities depending on how their sub-layers are ordered, dimensioned, and sparsified.

### Why This Problem Matters

The significance of this question is both practical and conceptual, and the paper anchors it in several real-world constraints:

**1. The dominance of feed-forward computation at typical sequence lengths.** The paper cites prior work (Du et al., 2022; Zhou et al., 2022) showing that for common sequence lengths (≤2048 tokens), dense feed-forward layers—not attention—constitute the majority of computational cost in large transformers. This means that optimizations targeting the feed-forward sub-layers (e.g., sparsifying them with MoE, reducing their hidden dimensions in specific positions) can yield disproportionately large improvements in overall efficiency. A uniform architecture that assigns every position an identical FFN configuration foregoes the opportunity to allocate capacity differentially: some layers might benefit from wide, dense computation while others might function just as well (or better) with sparsely activated experts or narrower bottlenecks.

**2. The fundamental trade-off between regularity and specialization.** Uniform architectures are easy to implement and scale—you simply stack more identical blocks. But this regularity imposes a constraint that every position in the network must treat the representation identically, regardless of whether that depth is better served by global token mixing (attention), dense pattern extraction (dense FFN), or specialized knowledge routing (MoE). The paper's core hypothesis is that *trading simplicity for heterogeneity* can improve the Pareto frontier of quality versus compute. This is a hypothesis with broad implications: if true, it means the field's attachment to uniform stacking is an architectural inefficiency, not a harmless simplification.

**3. Scaling models under a total cost budget.** The paper explicitly reframes the evaluation paradigm away from the common practice of comparing models at fixed parameters or fixed training tokens. The argument (expanded in Section 3.3) is that practitioners operate under a fixed total compute budget (e.g., TPU hours, GPU hours) and can trade off model capacity, training tokens, and architectural efficiency to maximize quality within that budget. An architecture that is faster per training step can be trained for more steps within the same wall-clock time, seeing more data and potentially achieving better final quality than a slower architecture with the same parameters. Evaluating architectures without accounting for their speed—which the paper claims is the norm in many prior scaling studies—systematically disadvantages faster, more efficient designs.

**4. The pervasiveness and cost of large model training and deployment.** The paper operates at significant scale: models up to 8B activated parameters, trained on 1.6 trillion tokens, consuming hundreds of TPU-v4 chips. At this scale, even modest architectural improvements compound into substantial savings in training time and energy cost. A 2× improvement in training convergence means halving the resource consumption for reaching equivalent quality—an outcome with direct economic and environmental implications.

### Where Prior Approaches Fall Short

The paper situates itself against three lines of prior work, identifying specific limitations in each:

**1. Dense Transformer Scaling: More Parameters, Not Better Architectures**

The dominant paradigm for improving transformer quality—exemplified by work like Kaplan et al. (2020), Brown et al. (2020), Raffel et al. (2020), and Chowdhery et al. (2022)—has been to scale model capacity (depth, width, or both) and training data volume while holding the basic architectural pattern fixed. Hoffmann et al. (2022) introduced the critical insight that both model size and training tokens should be scaled jointly under a fixed compute budget (the Chinchilla scaling laws). However, even this more sophisticated scaling paradigm treats the architecture itself as a constant. The Brainformer paper argues that this is a missed opportunity: two models with identical activated parameters can have different computational cost and quality depending on their internal structure, and scaling laws that ignore architecture cannot capture this dimension of the efficiency landscape.

The paper's critique is nuanced: it does not claim prior scaling work is wrong, but rather that it is **incomplete**. Scaling parameters and tokens under a fixed architecture optimizes along only one axis of a multi-dimensional space. Adding architecture as a tunable variable—particularly the arrangement of dense and sparse sub-layers—can shift the entire quality-vs-compute curve.

**2. Sparsely Activated Models: MoE as a Drop-In Replacement**

Mixture-of-Experts models (Shazeer et al., 2017; Lepikhin et al., 2021; Fedus et al., 2021; Du et al., 2022) introduce conditional computation to transformers, allowing a subset of "expert" feed-forward sub-layers to be activated per token, dramatically increasing total model capacity without proportionally increasing computation per token. This line of work has produced some of the most efficient large language models, including GLaM (Du et al., 2022), which serves as the paper's primary baseline.

However, the paper identifies a critical limitation in how MoE has been deployed: **the MoE layers are inserted into a uniform backbone according to a fixed, hand-crafted interleaving pattern.** Specifically, GLaM alternates between "regular" transformer blocks (with dense FFNs) and "sparse" transformer blocks (with MoE FFNs) in a regular one-to-one pattern. The gating function, expert count, and layer dimensions are also fixed choices. The paper argues—and demonstrates empirically—that this manually designed pattern is suboptimal. A searched, non-uniform arrangement of dense and sparse layers, combined with a searched gating mechanism (the paper contrasts token-based top-2 gating from GShard/GLaM with expert-choice gating from Zhou et al., 2022), can achieve substantially better training speed and quality.

The paper explicitly treats MoE not as a specific architecture but as a *general method for sparsifying the model*, positioning itself as agnostic to the particular conditional computation technique: "We only treat the MoE layer as a general method to sparsify the model. In practice, any conditional computation method can be blended in."

**3. Non-Uniform Architectures in Language Models: Promising but Under-Explored**

The idea of non-uniform transformer architectures has precedent but limited adoption. The paper cites two lines of inspiration:

- **EfficientNet (Tan & Le, 2019):** Although developed for convolutional neural networks in computer vision, EfficientNet introduced the concept of layer-wise compound scaling where different layers receive different scaling coefficients. This heterogeneity produced models more than 8× smaller and 6× faster than uniformly scaled counterparts. The paper explicitly takes inspiration from this philosophy: "For the easiness of scaling, we construct a block-wise search space where the restriction of uniformly stacking layers is removed."

- **Sandwich Transformer (Press et al., 2019):** This work demonstrated that reordering sub-layers—specifically, grouping attention layers together rather than strictly interleaving them with feed-forward layers—could sometimes improve performance. However, the paper notes a crucial limitation: "the sandwich reordering pattern does not guarantee performance gains across every task." The sandwich pattern is a single hand-crafted reordering, not a principled search over the combinatorial space of possible arrangements.

The critical gap the paper identifies is that **no prior work has combined architectural heterogeneity (non-uniform layer ordering and dimensionality) with sparsity (MoE gating) under a systematic search procedure**. Prior work either explored non-uniformity in dense models (Sandwich Transformer), applied MoE to uniform backbones (GLaM, Switch Transformer), or searched for architectural hyperparameters while keeping the layer-to-layer structure fixed (Primer, So et al., 2021, which searched for attention configurations but maintained the interleaved attention-FFN pattern). The Brainformer is the first architecture to simultaneously optimize the layer type sequence, the layer-specific dimensions, the MoE gating mechanism, and the activation functions, producing blocks that look qualitatively different from both dense and sparse predecessors.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper positions itself not as an alternative to scaling, sparsity, or non-uniformity individually, but as a **unification and optimization of all three**. Its intellectual lineage is explicit:

1. From **EfficientNet**, it inherits the philosophy that layer types and dimensions should vary across depth, not be uniform.
2. From **GLaM and the MoE literature**, it inherits sparsity as a mechanism for increasing capacity without proportional compute cost, but treats the placement and configuration of MoE layers as variables to be optimized, not fixed design decisions.
3. From **evolutionary search methods** (primer-style NAS for transformers), it inherits a mechanism for exploring the combinatorial space of possible architectures, but applies this search at the **block level** rather than fine-grained operator level, enabling the discovered patterns to be scaled to much larger models by stacking the optimized block.

What distinguishes the paper's positioning from prior work is its simultaneous commitment to (a) block-wise architectural search, (b) non-uniformity in layer order and width, (c) integration of sparsity with gating choice, and (d) evaluation under a **fixed training time** constraint that makes the efficiency-quality trade-off explicit. The paper argues that prior evaluations that fix parameters or training tokens inherently bias against faster architectures, and that its training-time-constrained search is a more honest reflection of real-world resource allocation.

The paper's framing in Section 1 and surrounding discussion makes clear that it is not proposing a single architectural innovation but rather a **methodology** for discovering architectures that are jointly optimized for quality and training speed. The "Brainformer" is the product of this methodology, not a hand-designed architecture. The implicit claim is that the space of possible transformer architectures is underexplored, that manual design is unlikely to find the optimum in this high-dimensional combinatorially complex space, and that evolution-guided discovery under the right efficiency constraints can produce architectures that substantially outperform both dense and sparse hand-crafted baselines.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

The Brainformer system is an automated architecture discovery pipeline that uses evolutionary search to find optimal arrangements of attention, dense feed-forward, and sparsely-gated mixture-of-experts sub-layers within a Transformer block, where blocks can be stacked to create models at any scale. The problem it solves is that manually designed Transformer architectures—with their uniform, strict alternation between attention and feed-forward layers—leave substantial efficiency on the table, and a search procedure that treats layer sequence, layer width, gating mechanism, and activation functions as jointly optimizable variables under a fixed training-time budget can discover non-uniform, heterogeneous blocks that train faster and generalize better than both dense and sparse hand-crafted baselines, without any single architectural innovation being responsible for the gains.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The Brainformer system has five major components that operate in sequence:

1. **Block-wise search space** — A combinatorial space defining what sequences of sub-layers, model dimensions, and gating configurations are legal. Each candidate architecture is a block (a list of 8 sub-layers, each typed as attention, dense FFN, or MoE FFN with associated dimensions and activation functions). The block is treated as a repeatable unit.

2. **Proxy task instantiation** — A sampled block is stacked three times to produce a small ~100M-parameter model (matching the depth of the GLaM baseline) trained on a fraction of the full training budget. This proxy task provides a fast, noisy estimate of the block's quality.

3. **Regularized evolutionary search with early stopping** — A population of candidate blocks is maintained. Each generation samples architectures from the population, evaluates them on the proxy task, computes a reward, and evolves the population. Early stopping kills unpromising candidates (those violating inference time or perplexity constraints) at 25% of maximum proxy training steps, saving significant compute.

4. **Block scaling and full evaluation** — The top-k surviving blocks from the search are scaled to target model sizes (1B and 8B activated parameters) by widening dimensions and stacking the block more times. These scaled models are trained to completion on the full 1.6-trillion-token dataset.

5. **Fixed-training-time constraint** — The entire search procedure operates under a constraint that each proxy trial receives a fixed wall-clock time budget. Faster architectures complete more training steps within this budget, seeing more data and achieving lower loss—so the search naturally favors compute-efficient designs.

Information flows as follows: the search space defines legal blocks → the evolutionary algorithm samples a block → the block is instantiated as a small proxy model → the proxy model is trained for up to a fixed wall-clock time, with early termination if constraints are violated → a reward (based on validation perplexity and inference speed) is computed → the reward updates the population's sampling distribution → after evolution converges, the top-k blocks are scaled by widening and deeper stacking → the scaled models are trained to convergence and evaluated against GLaM and Primer baselines.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First, the block-wise architecture formalism (Equations 1–5):** the mathematical definition of a Brainformer block and the constrained optimization objective that the search solves. This establishes what "optimal" means and what the search is maximizing under what constraints.
- **Second, the search space itself (Table 1, Section 3.2):** the combinatorial degrees of freedom—layer types, dimensions, gating functions, capacity factors, activation functions—and why each axis matters for the efficiency-quality trade-off.
- **Third, the proxy task and evolutionary search procedure (Algorithm 1, Figure 5):** the mechanics of how the search actually operates in two phases (block discovery at small scale, then scaling and full evaluation), including the critical early-stopping mechanism that makes the search computationally tractable.
- **Fourth, the fixed-training-time fairness constraint (Section 3.3–3.4):** why comparing architectures at fixed parameters or tokens is misleading, and how the training-time-constrained search objective correctly captures real-world resource allocation.
- **Fifth, the routing mechanism comparison (Section 4, Figure 6):** the distinction between token-based routing (top-2 gating from GShard/GLaM) and expert-based routing (Expert Choice from Zhou et al., 2022), and why the choice of routing function interacts with the optimal architecture layout.
- **Sixth, the scaling procedure:** how a discovered block is expanded to larger model sizes by scaling model dimensions and stacking the block more times, producing the 100M, 1B, and 8B variants evaluated in Section 5.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **neural architecture search paper** whose core idea is that the internal arrangement of sub-layers in a Transformer block—their types, order, widths, gating functions, and activation functions—can be jointly optimized under a fixed training-time constraint to discover architectures that substantially outperform both uniformly stacked dense Transformers and manually interleaved sparse (MoE) Transformers.

#### The Block-wise Architecture Formalism

The paper defines the architecture search problem as finding an optimal sequence of sub-layers and their associated hyperparameters within a repeatable block. A Brainformer block `$N$` is a composition of `$K$` sub-layer functions, applied sequentially to an input tensor:

$$N = F_k \odot ... \odot F_2 \odot F_1(X_1) = \prod_{j=1...k} F_j(X_1)$$

where `$\odot$` denotes sequential function composition (apply `$F_1$` to the input, then `$F_2$` to the output of `$F_1$`, and so on), `$X_1$` is the input tensor with shape `$\{B, L, H\}$` where `$B$` is batch size, `$L$` is sequence length, and `$H$` is the model dimension, and each `$F_j$` is a sub-layer function selected from the operation set `$\{\text{F}_{\text{attn}}, \text{F}_{\text{moe}}, \text{F}_{\text{ffn}}\}$` (attention, sparsely gated feed-forward, and dense feed-forward respectively).

**What it computes:** this defines the forward pass through one Brainformer block. Given an input tensor of shape batch × sequence × model_dimension, apply `$K$` sub-layers in the specified order, each transforming the tensor, to produce an output tensor of the same shape. The block can be stacked an arbitrary number of times to form a full model.

**Why this form:** unlike a vanilla Transformer block, which has a fixed composition `$F_{\text{attn}} \odot F_{\text{ffn}}$` (attention then dense FFN), the Brainformer block allows any sequence of the three sub-layer types. This captures the hypothesis that some positions in the network benefit from attention followed by multiple dense operations, while others benefit from MoE layers grouped together, and that the optimal arrangement is not the uniform interleaving that hand-designed architectures assume. The composition notation makes the sequential dependency explicit: each sub-layer sees the transformed representation from all previous sub-layers in the block.

Each sub-layer function `$F_i$` is instantiated with type-specific hyperparameters as defined in Equation 3:

$$F_i = \begin{cases} F^{\text{d}, \text{h}, \text{a}}_i, & \text{if } F_i = F_{\text{attn}} \\ F^{\text{d}, \text{d}_{\text{ffn}}, \text{a}}_i, & \text{else if } F_i = F_{\text{ffn}} \\ F^{\text{d}, \text{d}_{\text{moe}}, \text{g}, \text{c}, \text{a}}_i, & \text{otherwise } F_i = F_{\text{moe}} \end{cases}$$

where `$\text{d}$` is the model dimension (tunable per block), `$\text{d}_{\text{ffn}}$` is the hidden dimension of the dense FFN sub-layer, `$\text{d}_{\text{moe}}$` is the hidden dimension of each expert in the MoE sub-layer, `$\text{h}$` is the number of attention heads, `$\text{g}$` is the gating function for the MoE layer (either token-based top-2 or expert-based Expert Choice), `$\text{c}$` is the capacity factor controlling how many tokens each expert processes, and `$\text{a}$` is the activation function (Gated ReLU/GeLU, ReLU, or GeLU).

**What this means:** when the search selects a sub-layer type (say, MoE), it must also select the dimension, gating function, capacity factor, and activation function for that specific MoE instance. Different MoE layers in the same block can theoretically have different configurations, though the paper's discovered blocks use consistent settings within a block for simplicity.

**Why this decomposition:** it makes explicit that sub-layer choice and hyperparameter choice are jointly optimized. A search that selected only layer types without tuning dimensions would miss the fact that a dense FFN with a wide hidden dimension may be more efficient than an MoE layer with narrow experts, or vice versa. The gating function choice (`$\text{g}$`) is particularly important because token-based and expert-based routing have fundamentally different load-balancing and specialization properties that interact with the surrounding architecture.

The full model `$\mathcal{N}$` is defined as stacking the block `$K$` times (Equation 4):

$$\mathcal{N}(F_{1:k}, d, d_{\text{moe}}, d_{\text{ffn}}, h, g, c, a) = \prod_{i=1...k} F_i(X_1)$$

**What it computes:** the complete model architecture obtained by repeating the discovered block. The block's internal structure (which sub-layers, in what order, with what dimensions) is fixed by the search; scaling the model means either making the block wider (increasing `$d$`, `$d_{\text{ffn}}$`, `$d_{\text{moe}}$`) or stacking it more times (increasing `$k$`).

**Why this form:** by making the block the unit of repetition rather than individual layers, the search space becomes tractable—the search optimizes the structure of one block, and scaling laws (empirically validated in Section 5) ensure that a block that is efficient at small scale remains efficient when scaled. This is directly inspired by EfficientNet's compound scaling philosophy applied to Transformers.

#### The Constrained Optimization Objective

The search objective (Equation 2) is to minimize validation cross-entropy loss `$\mathcal{L}$` over the architecture parameters, subject to an inference speed constraint:

$$\min_{F_{1:k}, d, d_{\text{moe}}, d_{\text{ffn}}, h, g, c, a} \mathcal{L}(\mathcal{N}(F_{1:k}, d, d_{\text{moe}}, d_{\text{ffn}}, h, g, c, a))$$

subject to:

$$\text{Step\_Time}(\mathcal{N}) \leq \text{baseline\_step\_time}$$

where `$\mathcal{L}$` is the pre-training validation cross-entropy loss, `$\mathcal{N}$` is the full model defined by the architecture parameters, and `$\text{Step\_Time}(\mathcal{N})$` is the measured wall-clock time per training step for the candidate architecture, which must not exceed the step time of the GLaM baseline at the same scale.

**What it computes:** find the architecture parameters (layer types, dimensions, gating, activations) that produce the lowest validation perplexity, but only among architectures that are at least as fast as the baseline in training step time. This is a constrained optimization: architectures that are accurate but slow are infeasible.

**Why this form rather than an unconstrained objective or a combined speed-quality metric:** the paper argues (Section 3.3) that fixing training wall-clock time while meeting an inference time constraint yields models with faster training convergence and higher quality. If the objective were a weighted sum of quality and speed, the search could trade off one for the other in ways that don't reflect practical deployment constraints (where there is a hard latency budget). The hard constraint on step time ensures that every candidate meeting the constraint is at least as deployable as the baseline; the quality minimization then finds the best among deployable architectures. This is a subtle but important design choice: it prevents the search from finding architectures that are slightly better but much slower, which would be Pareto-optimal but not practically useful.

A critical aspect of the search is the **fixed training time constraint** (Section 3.4). Each proxy trial is allocated a fixed wall-clock time budget. This means that an architecture with a faster step time can complete more training steps within the budget, seeing more data and achieving lower loss. The paper states:

> "We empirically find that fixing training wall clock time while meeting a inference time constraint yields models with faster training convergence and higher quality."

**What this means operationally:** the search reward is not validation loss at a fixed number of steps, but rather validation loss at whatever step the model reaches when its wall-clock budget expires. A model that trains 20% faster per step can complete 20% more gradient updates in the same time, which compounds into substantially lower loss. This automatically bakes training speed into the quality evaluation, without needing an explicit speed term in the reward function.

**Why this matters for fairness:** the paper argues (Section 3.3) that "comparing models with a fixed amount of training tokens may still also not be fair as some smaller models can benefit more from additional training data and outperform a bigger model with the same total training cost (e.g. GPU hours, TPU hours, etc.)." The fixed-training-time evaluation captures the real-world trade-off: if you have a fixed budget of TPU hours, which model architecture gives the best final quality? This penalizes architectures that achieve good perplexity-per-step but are slow, and rewards architectures that are modestly worse per-step but train fast enough to compensate through more steps.

#### The Search Space

The search space (Table 1) defines what the evolutionary algorithm can vary. Each axis represents a degree of freedom that the paper hypothesizes affects the quality-efficiency trade-off:

| Search Item | Search Space |
|---|---|
| Layer Type (`$F_i$`) | `$\text{F}_{\text{attn}}, \text{F}_{\text{moe}}, \text{F}_{\text{ffn}}$` |
| Model Dim. (`$d$`) | 512, 768, 1024 |
| MoE Hidden Dim. (`$d_{\text{moe}}$`) | 1536, 2048, 3072, 4096 |
| FFN Hidden Dim. (`$d_{\text{ffn}}$`) | 1536, 2048, 3072, 4096 |
| Attention Heads (`$h$`) | 12, 16, 20 |
| Gating Func. (`$g$`) | Top-2, Expert Choice |
| Capacity Factor (`$c$`) | 1, 2, 3, 4 |
| Activation Func. (`$a$`) | Gated Re/GeLU, ReLU, GeLU |

**What each axis controls:**

- **Layer Type:** the fundamental building block at each position. Attention layers perform token-to-token mixing via self-attention. Dense FFN layers apply a position-wise two-layer MLP with a hidden dimension expansion. MoE layers apply a sparsely activated collection of `$E$` experts (each a two-layer MLP), where a gating function routes each token to a subset of experts. The search can place, for example, three consecutive MoE layers, or group attention layers together, or insert dense FFNs between MoE clusters.

- **Model Dimension `$d$`:** the width of the residual stream throughout the block. The baseline dense Transformer uses `$d = 768$`. The search can select a wider dimension (1024) to increase capacity at the cost of more FLOPs, or a narrower dimension (512) for faster computation. The paper notes that the discovered blocks choose `$d = 1024$` (wider than baseline 768) but compensate with smaller FFN expansion ratios (see Section 6.1), suggesting that allocating capacity to the residual stream rather than the FFN hidden dimension is more efficient.

- **MoE Hidden Dimension `$d_{\text{moe}}$`:** the hidden dimension of each expert MLP within an MoE layer. With `$E$` experts and a capacity factor `$c$`, each token is routed to `$c$` experts on average, so the effective FLOPs per MoE layer scale with `$c \cdot d \cdot d_{\text{moe}}$`. The search can make experts narrow (1536) for faster computation or wide (4096) for more per-expert capacity. The discovered Brainformer Block #1 uses `$d_{\text{moe}} = 2048$`, less than the 4× expansion ratio common in dense Transformers—a finding the paper attributes to MoE layers effectively widening the network through expert diversity, reducing the need for per-expert width.

- **FFN Hidden Dimension `$d_{\text{ffn}}$`:** the hidden dimension of dense FFN sub-layers. Ranges from 1536 (2× model dimension at `$d = 768$`) to 4096 (4× model dimension at `$d = 1024$`). The discovered blocks use smaller expansion ratios (e.g., 1536 at `$d = 1024$`, which is 1.5× rather than the standard 4×), trading per-FFN capacity for the ability to stack more diverse sub-layers within the same compute budget.

- **Attention Heads `$h$`:** the number of parallel attention heads. Ranges from 12 to 20. More heads increase the attention layer's representational capacity and parallelism but also increase memory and compute cost quadratically in the sequence length. Brainformer Block #1 uses 20 heads (more than baseline 12), while Block #2 uses 16.

- **Gating Function `$g$`:** the mechanism by which experts are assigned to tokens within an MoE layer. **Top-2** (from GShard/GLaM) computes token-to-expert affinity scores via a learned gating matrix, normalizes along the expert dimension with softmax, and routes each token to the 2 highest-scoring experts. **Expert Choice** (from Zhou et al., 2022) normalizes along the token dimension and lets each expert select the top-`$k$` tokens, achieving perfect load balance by construction. The search can select either mechanism per MoE instance, allowing the architecture to favor specialization (top-2) or load balance (expert choice) at different depths.

- **Capacity Factor `$c$`:** in an MoE layer with `$E$` experts processing `$T$` total tokens, the capacity per expert is `$(T \cdot c) / E$`—each expert can process at most that many tokens. With `$c = 1$`, each expert processes exactly `$T/E$` tokens on average (perfect load balance). With `$c = 2$`, each expert can handle up to twice that, providing slack for uneven routing at the cost of more computation. Brainformer Block #1 chooses `$c = 1$` (maximum sparsity, each token routed to one expert on average) with Expert Choice gating, achieving guaranteed load balance. Block #2 chooses `$c = 2$` (each token routed to two experts on average), trading sparsity for more capacity and faster data efficiency.

- **Activation Function `$a$`:** the nonlinearity applied within the feed-forward sub-layers. Options include standard ReLU and GeLU, plus Gated ReLU/Gated GeLU variants that introduce a multiplicative gating mechanism (the input is split, one half passes through the activation and gates the other half). The search can select different activations for different sub-layers, though the paper does not report which activations were selected in the final blocks.

A crucial detail: the search space defines the **block structure**, not the full model. The block is treated as a unit—all internal sub-layer types, dimensions, and configurations are fixed within the block—and the full model is obtained by stacking the block. The search operates on blocks of length `$K = 8$` sub-layers (as visible in Figures 8 and 9, which show 8-sub-layer blocks). Stacking this block 3 times produces a proxy model with 24 total sub-layers, matching the layer count of the 12-layer GLaM baseline (which has 2 sub-layers per transformer layer: one attention, one FFN/MoE). At larger scales, the block is stacked 6 times (1B model) or 8 times (8B model) to achieve target activated parameter counts.

**Why this block-wise design:** optimizing the full model directly would require searching over a combinatorially vast space (the sequence of 24–64 sub-layers across multiple stacked blocks). By constraining the model to be a repetition of an identical block, the search space dimensionality is reduced from the full model depth to the block depth (8 sub-layers), making the search tractable. The empirical finding that the discovered blocks scale well (Section 5.1, Table 3) validates that block-wise optimality transfers to deeper stacks.

#### The Proxy Task and Two-Phase Search Procedure

The core challenge of architecture search at scale is that training each candidate to convergence is prohibitively expensive. The Brainformer search addresses this through a two-phase strategy (Algorithm 1, Figure 5):

**Phase 1: Block search on a proxy task.**

The search instantiates a proxy training task with the following characteristics:
- Each sampled block is stacked **three times** to produce a model with approximately 100M activated parameters and 32 experts (100M32E).
- The proxy model is trained on the same 1.6-trillion-token dataset, but with a drastically reduced training budget: each candidate receives a **fixed wall-clock time** allocation that corresponds to approximately 25% of the maximum training steps (`$T_{\text{max}}/4$`) that a baseline GLaM model would receive.
- The number of training steps a candidate completes depends on its step time: faster models complete more steps.
- Validation perplexity is measured at the end of the time budget.

This proxy task is computationally cheap relative to full training (roughly 25% of the cost) but provides a noisy rank-ordering of block quality that is sufficient for evolutionary search. The paper acknowledges this is an approximation: block quality at 25% training may not perfectly predict quality at convergence. However, the early stopping mechanism (described below) ensures that the search invests compute proportionally to promising candidates.

**Why stack three times:** the GLaM baseline at the 100M scale has 12 Transformer layers, each containing 2 sub-layers (attention + FFN or attention + MoE) = 24 total sub-layers. Stacking an 8-sub-layer Brainformer block three times produces exactly 24 sub-layers, enabling a direct apples-to-apples comparison in terms of network depth.

**Phase 2: Scaling and full evaluation.**

After evolution converges, the `$k$` blocks with the highest rewards are scaled to larger model sizes:
1. **Scale the model dimension** and hidden dimensions by 2× and 4×, following the scaling factors from GLaM. This produces blocks S1 (targeting ~1B activated parameters) and S2 (targeting ~8B activated parameters).
2. **Stack the scaled block** more times: 6 stacks for 1B models, 8 stacks for 8B models. The number of stacks is determined mathematically based on the target total activated parameters.
3. **Train to convergence** on the full 1.6-trillion-token dataset with the full training budget (1M steps for 1B, 1.5M steps for 8B).
4. **Evaluate** against GLaM and other baselines on pre-training perplexity, fine-tuning, and fewshot tasks.

The paper states this scaling procedure explicitly: "We first scale the model dimension and hidden dimension 2x and 4x, following the scaling factors presented in GLaM, to create block S1 and S2 targeting 1B and 8B model scale. Then we stack block S1 and S2 respectively to create 1B64E and 8B64E model variants."

#### The Evolutionary Search Algorithm with Early Stopping

The search uses a **regularized evolutionary search** (Algorithm 1), which operates as follows:

```
Algorithm 1: Brainformer Block Search
Require: Block-wise search space B, evolutionary algorithm with population size p
1: for t = 1 to T0 do                                    // Evolution loop
2:   for B(i) in SamplePopulation(B, p) do               // Sample p candidates
3:     G(i) ← StackThreeTimes(B(i))                     // Instantiate proxy model
4:     if EarlyStopping(G(i)) then                      // Check constraints at 25% Tmax
5:       R(i) = -1                                      // Penalize infeasible candidates
6:     else
7:       Ai, Ti ← Train(G(i), Tmax)                     // Train to time budget
8:       R(i) ← f(Ai, Ti)                               // Compute reward
9:     end if
10:    end for
11: end for
12: G_topk ← TopK({G(i), R(i)})                          // Select best architectures
13: for G(i) in G_topk do
14:   G(i) ← ScaleModelDim(G(i))                         // Widen for target scale
15:   G(i) ← StackNTimes(G(i))                           // Deepen for target scale
16:   Ai, Ti ← Train(G(i))                               // Full training
17: end for
```

**How the algorithm works step by step:**

1. **Initialization:** A population of candidate block architectures is initialized (presumably randomly or with seeded good architectures, though the paper does not specify the initialization strategy).

2. **Population sampling:** Each generation, `$p$` architectures are sampled from the population. The paper does not specify `$p$`, but given the computational constraints (512 TPU v4 for one week), it is likely in the range of tens to low hundreds.

3. **Proxy instantiation:** Each sampled block `$B^{(i)}$` is stacked three times to create a proxy model `$G^{(i)}$`. The model uses the dimensions and hyperparameters specified in the block.

4. **Early stopping check (the efficiency bottleneck):** At 25% of the maximum training steps (`$T_{\text{max}}/4$`), the model's **inference time** and **validation perplexity** are measured. If the model violates the inference time constraint (Equation 5: step time must not exceed baseline step time) or if validation perplexity is significantly worse than the best-so-far models (the paper is not explicit about the exact threshold), the candidate is assigned a reward of `$-1$` and terminated immediately. This is the crucial efficiency mechanism: unpromising candidates are killed early, saving 75% of their training budget. The paper states: "unpromising models are pruned early due to the violation of inference time constraint or perplexity constraint at 25% of the maximum training steps, compared to the baseline GLaM architecture."

5. **Full proxy training for survivors:** Candidates that pass the early stopping check are trained to the full wall-clock time budget (`$T_{\text{max}}$`). The training completes whatever number of steps fits within the time budget. The final validation perplexity `$A_i$` and step time `$T_i$` are recorded.

6. **Reward computation:** The reward `$R^{(i)} = f(A_i, T_i)$` is a function of the final validation perplexity and the step time. The paper does not specify `$f$` explicitly, but the fixed-training-time framework implies that the reward is primarily driven by perplexity (since speed is already baked into the number of steps completed), possibly with a penalty for architectures exceeding the baseline step time or a small bonus for architectures that are significantly faster.

7. **Population update:** The evolutionary algorithm uses the rewards to update the population—preserving high-performing architectures, mutating them (changing layer types, dimensions, or hyperparameters), and discarding low-performing ones. The paper does not specify the mutation operators (e.g., add/remove/swap layers, increment/decrement dimensions), but standard NAS mutation operators are implied.

8. **Convergence and top-k selection:** After `$T_0$` generations (the paper states: "the search identified better model architecture within as early as 500 trials" in Section 8, Limitations), the top-`$k$` architectures by reward are selected. The paper evaluates two top blocks (Brainformer-1 and Brainformer-2), suggesting `$k \geq 2$`.

9. **Scaling:** Each top block is scaled to target sizes as described above and trained to convergence for final evaluation.

**Why evolutionary search rather than gradient-based NAS or random search:** the search space is combinatorial and discrete (layer types, gating functions), with no differentiable relaxation. Evolutionary methods are well-suited to such spaces and have a strong track record in architecture search. The regularized evolutionary variant (where the oldest members of the population are removed regardless of fitness, promoting diversity) prevents premature convergence to a local optimum. The paper does not explicitly describe the regularization mechanism, but the term "regularized evolutionary search" in the NAS literature typically refers to Real et al.'s approach where the oldest individual is always removed, acting as an implicit diversity pressure.

**Why early stopping with a hard inference-time constraint rather than a multi-objective reward:** a multi-objective reward (e.g., `$R = A - \lambda T$`) requires tuning `$\lambda$`, which is sensitive to scale and problem. The hard constraint approach cleanly separates feasibility (be at least as fast as the baseline) from optimization (minimize perplexity among feasible architectures). This matches real-world deployment: you have a latency SLO, and among architectures meeting it, you want the best quality.

#### The Training-Time-Constrained Fairness Framework (Section 3.3)

The paper's evaluation philosophy is a departure from standard practice in language model scaling, and understanding it is essential to interpreting the results. The argument proceeds in three steps:

**Step 1: Comparing at fixed total parameters is unfair to sparse models.**

Prior scaling studies (Raffel et al., 2020; Brown et al., 2020; Rae et al., 2021) typically fix model capacity (total parameters) and training tokens, then plot quality as a function of parameter count. This inherently discriminates against sparsely activated models like MoE, which have many total parameters but few activated parameters per token. A 1B-parameter dense model and a 27B-parameter MoE model with 1.8B activated parameters per token have very different total parameter counts, but similar computational cost per token. Comparing them at equal total parameters is meaningless—the MoE model would be compared against a 27B dense model that is much more expensive.

**Step 2: Comparing at fixed activated parameters and fixed training tokens is better but still unfair.**

GLaM (Du et al., 2022) improved on this by comparing models at equal **activated parameters per token** (which approximates computational cost) and equal training tokens. This is fairer because the computational cost per training step is roughly proportional to activated parameters (ignoring routing overhead). However, comparing at fixed training tokens still assumes that all models take the same time per step, which is false if architectures have different step times. An architecture that is 5× faster per step can complete 5× more training steps in the same wall-clock time, seeing 5× more data. If it achieves slightly worse perplexity per step but much better perplexity due to extra data, a fixed-token comparison would incorrectly declare it worse.

**Step 3: Fixed training time is the practitioner-relevant metric.**

The paper argues that practitioners have a fixed compute budget (TPU hours, GPU hours) and can trade off model architecture, model capacity, and training tokens within that budget. The relevant question is: "given `$N$` TPU hours, which architecture produces the best model?" This is the **fixed-training-time** evaluation. Within the search, this is implemented by giving each candidate a fixed wall-clock time budget. Within the final evaluation, this is implemented by training Brainformer models for the same wall-clock time as the GLaM baseline (the paper states: "We pretrain the models for a total fixed wall clock time as the baseline GLaM model" in Section 5.2). The Brainformer can use its speed advantage to see more data within that time, and the final quality comparison reflects this advantage.

The paper explicitly connects this to Chinchilla's compute-optimal scaling: "Resonating with compute-efficient model scaling, we further take model architectural change into consideration during the search for efficient model architectures with better training convergence and inference time." While Chinchilla optimized the allocation of a fixed FLOPs budget between model size and training tokens, Brainformer adds a third axis: architecture. The search objective is now: given fixed FLOPs (or wall-clock time), find the architecture that maximizes quality, allowing the model size and training tokens to be whatever the architecture naturally consumes within the budget.

This is a genuinely different evaluation paradigm from most architecture papers, which fix parameters and tokens and compare quality. It means that the Brainformer's quality improvements come from two sources: (1) better architectural design (e.g., more efficient layer arrangements that extract more quality per FLOP), and (2) the ability to train on more data within the same time budget due to faster step times. The paper does not attempt to disentangle these two effects, and the reported improvements should be understood as the combined benefit.

#### Token-Based Routing versus Expert-Based Routing (Section 4)

The paper identifies the routing mechanism as a key factor that interacts with architecture choice. While Section 4 is brief, it introduces a distinction that is important for understanding why the discovered Brainformer blocks choose Expert Choice gating.

**Token-based routing (Top-2 gating):**

In token-based routing, each token independently selects which experts to be processed by. The mechanism, inherited from GShard (Lepikhin et al., 2021) and used in GLaM:

1. A learned gating matrix `$W_g \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times E}$` projects the token's hidden representation to an `$E$`-dimensional vector of affinity scores (one per expert).
2. The scores are normalized along the expert dimension with a softmax, producing a probability distribution over experts for that token.
3. Each token is routed to the top-2 highest-scoring experts (top-2 gating). The paper cites GShard and GLaM as showing top-2 outperforms top-1 empirically.
4. An auxiliary load-balancing loss is added to the training objective to encourage tokens to be distributed roughly evenly across experts. Without this loss, the gating network can collapse to routing all tokens to a single expert, wasting the capacity of other experts.

The key property: **tokens select experts**, and load balance is achieved indirectly through an auxiliary loss term. This can lead to load imbalance during training (some experts receive more tokens than their capacity, causing tokens to be dropped), and the auxiliary loss adds a hyperparameter that must be tuned.

**Expert-based routing (Expert Choice gating):**

In expert-based routing, the control is inverted: each expert selects which tokens it will process. From Zhou et al. (2022):

1. The same affinity scores are computed (dot product of token embeddings with expert embeddings).
2. The scores are normalized along the **token dimension** (softmax over tokens for each expert), producing a distribution of how much each expert "wants" each token.
3. Each expert selects the top-`$k$` tokens by score, where `$k$` is determined by the capacity factor: `$k = (T \cdot c) / E$`, with `$T$` total tokens, `$E$` experts, and `$c$` the capacity factor.
4. With `$c = 1$`, each expert processes exactly `$T/E$` tokens, achieving **perfect load balance by construction**—no auxiliary loss needed.
5. Tokens can be selected by zero, one, or multiple experts. If a token is selected by zero experts, it bypasses the MoE layer via a residual connection.

The key property: **experts select tokens**, and load balance is guaranteed by the top-`$k$` selection mechanism. This eliminates the auxiliary loss and the need to tune it.

**How the difference manifests in the search space:**

The paper's Figure 6 illustrates the conceptual difference with a 4-token, 4-expert example. In token-based routing (left panel), each token picks its top-2 experts. Some experts may receive many tokens while others receive few. In expert-based routing (right panel), each expert picks its top-`$k$` tokens, guaranteeing uniform load.

But the more important interaction is with architecture design: the paper hypothesizes that the optimal block layout depends on which routing mechanism is used. Token-based routing with its auxiliary load-balancing loss may favor architectures where MoE layers are spaced apart (as in GLaM's interleaved pattern) to prevent the loss from dominating. Expert Choice routing, with its guaranteed load balance, may allow MoE layers to be clustered together without load-balancing issues, enabling denser packing of sparse computation. This is consistent with the discovered Brainformer blocks, which group MoE layers together (Brainformer Block #1 has three consecutive MoE layers at the end of the block; Block #2 has two consecutive MoE layers) and use Expert Choice gating.

**The normalization axis matters for autoregressive decoding:**

The paper notes a subtle but critical implementation detail: "To avoid causal leakage in decoding mode, we suggest normalizing along the expert dimension for both token-based routing and expert-based routing." In autoregressive generation, the model can only attend to past tokens. If normalization along the token dimension used future tokens' affinity scores, it would leak information about future tokens into the current expert assignment, which is causally invalid. Normalizing along the expert dimension for each token independently avoids this, because it only uses the current token's affinity scores.

**Why the routing choice matters for the search results:**

The paper includes an ablation in Table 3: **Search-w-Top2**, which is an architecture discovered by the same search procedure but with the gating function fixed to top-2 token-based routing. This model achieves a perplexity of 2.67 at the 100M scale, compared to the GLaM baseline of 2.73—a modest improvement. Brainformer-1, which is allowed to select Expert Choice gating, achieves 2.57—a substantially larger improvement. This can be interpreted as evidence that (a) architectural search helps even with fixed routing, but (b) the largest gains come from jointly optimizing the routing mechanism and the layer layout, because Expert Choice gating enables architectural patterns (clustered MoE layers, capacity factor of 1) that would not work well with token-based routing's load-balancing challenges.

At the 8B scale, an additional baseline is included: **Expert-based Gating** (Table 3, bottom), which is the GLaM architecture (interleaved dense and sparse blocks) but with the gating function replaced by Expert Choice while keeping the architecture fixed. This model improves perplexity from 2.12 (GLaM with top-2) to 2.03—a meaningful gain from routing alone. But Brainformer-1, which combines Expert Choice with a searched heterogeneous architecture, achieves 1.99—improving further, suggesting that routing and architecture are complementary optimizations.

#### The Discovered Brainformer Blocks: What the Search Produced

The search produced (at least) two distinct block architectures, Brainformer Block #1 (Figure 8) and Brainformer Block #2 (Figure 9), which share important structural patterns but differ in key hyperparameters.

**Brainformer Block #1 (the faster, more aggressive block):**

- **Model dimension:** 1024 (wider than baseline 768)
- **Dense FFN hidden dimension:** 1536 (expansion ratio 1.5×, vs. baseline 4×)
- **MoE FFN hidden dimension:** 2048 (per-expert expansion ratio 2×, vs. 4× in GLaM)
- **Gating function:** Expert Choice
- **Capacity factor:** 1 (each token routed to one expert on average, maximum sparsity)
- **Attention heads:** 20 (more than baseline 12)

The block's sub-layer sequence (8 layers): **ATTN → FFN → FFN → FFN → FFN → MOE → MOE → MOE**. Translated to operations: one attention layer, four consecutive dense FFN layers, then three consecutive MoE layers. There is no attention layer after the initial one—the entire second half of the block is feed-forward only (dense then sparse).

**Brainformer Block #2 (the slightly slower, more data-efficient block):**

- **Model dimension:** 1024
- **Dense FFN hidden dimension:** 2048 (expansion ratio 2×)
- **MoE FFN hidden dimension:** 2048
- **Gating function:** Expert Choice
- **Capacity factor:** 2 (each token routed to two experts on average)
- **Attention heads:** 16

The block's sub-layer sequence (8 layers): **ATTN → FFN → FFN → FFN → MOE → MOE → ATTN → ATTN**. This block places attention at the beginning (one layer) and at the end (two consecutive layers), with dense FFNs and MoE layers in the middle. The block has three attention layers total (vs. one in Block #1), making it more attention-heavy.

**What these patterns reveal about the search's preferences:**

**1. Attention is sparsified.** In a uniform 2-sub-layer Transformer block, attention appears in 50% of sub-layers (every other sub-layer). In Brainformer Block #1, attention is 1 out of 8 sub-layers (12.5%). In Block #2, it is 3 out of 8 (37.5%). Both are below the 50% baseline. The search has discovered that attention—which scales quadratically in sequence length—can be used sparingly, concentrated at specific depths, without hurting quality. This directly validates the paper's motivation: feed-forward computation dominates at typical sequence lengths, so reallocating the architecture's capacity toward more feed-forward sub-layers (dense and sparse) improves efficiency.

**2. MoE layers are clustered, not interleaved.** GLaM interleaves MoE blocks and dense blocks in a strict alternating pattern. Both Brainformer blocks group MoE layers together (three consecutive in Block #1, two consecutive in Block #2). The paper suggests this is enabled by Expert Choice gating, which avoids the load-balancing issues that would make clustered MoE problematic with top-2 routing. The clustering may also be computationally efficient: routing decisions can be shared or cached across consecutive MoE layers (though the paper does not claim this optimization).

**3. The capacity factor trades speed for data efficiency.** Block #1 (`$c = 1$`) is sparser: each token hits one expert on average, making it faster per step. It achieves better perplexity through seeing more data within the fixed time budget. Block #2 (`$c = 2$`) is denser: each token hits two experts, making it slower per step but more data-efficient. The paper explicitly describes this trade-off: "Being much faster in step time, block 1 takes more training steps, thus training data to achieve good quality. Therefore, we also picked another strong candidate, Brainformer block 2, in which a larger capacity factor in the MoE layers is selected. Block 2 is lightly slower in step time, but takes fewer training steps to get good accuracy, thus is more data efficient."

**4. Model dimension is wider but expansion ratios are narrower.** Both blocks use `$d = 1024$` (vs. baseline 768), which increases the residual stream capacity. But they compensate by reducing FFN hidden dimensions: Block #1 uses `$d_{\text{ffn}} = 1536$` (1.5× expansion) instead of the standard 3072 (4×), and `$d_{\text{moe}} = 2048$` (2×) instead of 4×. This suggests that the search has discovered a more efficient allocation of model capacity: put more representational power in the residual stream (which all sub-layers can access) and less in the individual FFN layers (which are depth-specific). The MoE layers compensate for the reduced per-expert capacity through expert diversity: with 32 or 64 experts, even narrow experts collectively provide substantial capacity.

**5. Activation function and attention head choices are under-reported.** The paper does not specify which activation functions were selected in the final blocks, nor does it analyze the effect of the attention head count beyond listing the values (20 for Block #1, 16 for Block #2). This is a limitation in the exposition: the search space includes activations and head count as searchable axes, but their impact on the final quality is not isolated.

#### Summary of Design Choices and Their Justifications

- **Block-wise search over full-model search:** reduces the combinatorial search space from the full model depth to a single block, making evolution tractable. The empirical finding that blocks scale well validates that block-wise optimality transfers to deeper stacks.

- **Fixed-training-time proxy evaluation over fixed-step evaluation:** bakes training speed into the quality assessment without needing an explicit speed term in the reward. Faster architectures automatically see more data, producing lower loss and higher rewards. This is the single most important design choice that differentiates Brainformer from prior architecture search work.

- **Early stopping at 25% of max steps with inference-time and perplexity constraints:** kills unpromising candidates before they consume their full training budget, making the search computationally feasible at scale (512 TPU v4 for one week). The inference-time constraint ensures discovered architectures are deployable; the perplexity constraint prunes clearly inferior designs.

- **Expert Choice gating over token-based top-2 gating (in the best blocks):** eliminates the auxiliary load-balancing loss, guarantees perfect load balance, and enables clustering of MoE layers without the routing instability that would occur with top-2 gating. The ablation showing that Search-w-Top2 (fixed top-2 gating) underperforms Brainformer-1 (which selects Expert Choice) confirms that gating choice is a first-class architectural decision, not a minor implementation detail.

- **Flexible layer dimensions (model dim, FFN hidden dim, MoE hidden dim) rather than fixed expansion ratios:** allows the search to discover non-standard ratios (e.g., 1.5× FFN expansion rather than 4×) that are more efficient when combined with expert diversity and non-uniform layer placement. The standard 4× expansion ratio in Transformers is an inherited convention from Vaswani et al. (2017), not an optimized choice, and the search validates that it is often too large.

- **Multiple selected blocks (Brainformer-1 and Brainformer-2) rather than a single "winner":** acknowledges that the optimal architecture depends on the deployment context. Block #1 maximizes training speed (5× faster step time at 8B scale) and is ideal when total training time is the bottleneck. Block #2 is slightly slower but more data-efficient, making it preferable when training data is scarce or when minimizing total training FLOPs rather than wall-clock time. This is a pragmatic recognition that architecture search should produce a Pareto frontier, not a single point.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Architecture as a Jointly Optimizable Axis in Compute-Efficient Scaling

The most fundamental conceptual contribution of this paper is the elevation of **model architecture—specifically, the internal arrangement and configuration of sub-layers—from a fixed design choice to a first-class, jointly optimizable axis in the compute-efficient scaling framework.** Prior to this work, the dominant paradigm for scaling transformer language models treated architecture as essentially constant: you scale the number of layers, the model dimension, and the training tokens, but the *structure*—the strict alternation of attention and feed-forward sub-layers, the fixed 4× feed-forward expansion ratio, the uniform repetition of identical blocks—remains invariant. This is true of both dense scaling laws work (Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022) and sparse MoE work (Fedus et al., 2021; Du et al., 2022), where sparsity is introduced as a drop-in replacement for dense FFNs within the same uniform backbone.

The Brainformer paper challenges this invariance directly. It argues—and demonstrates empirically—that the architecture itself has a compute-efficiency dimension that interacts with model size and training data volume. Two models with identical activated parameters and identical training tokens can have dramatically different training speeds and final qualities depending on how their sub-layers are ordered, dimensioned, and gated. This means that the Chinchilla-style question—"given a fixed FLOPs budget, how should I allocate between model size and training tokens?"—is incomplete. The full question is: "given a fixed FLOPs budget, what architecture, model size, and training token allocation jointly maximize quality?" The Brainformer search operationalizes this by making architecture a variable under a fixed training-time budget, allowing faster architectures to trade their speed advantage for more training data, and discovering that the optimal point in this three-dimensional space involves architectural patterns (clustered MoE layers, reduced attention frequency, non-standard expansion ratios) that no hand-designed recipe had produced.

This is a **fundamental reframing**, not an incremental improvement. Prior work on transformer architecture search (e.g., Primer, So et al., 2021) searched within a single architectural template—optimizing attention hyperparameters while keeping the interleaved attention-FFN pattern fixed. Prior work on efficient transformers (e.g., Sandwich Transformer, Press et al., 2019) proposed single hand-crafted reorderings without systematic optimization. Brainformer is the first to treat the full combinatorial space of sub-layer sequences, widths, gating mechanisms, and activation functions as jointly searchable under a compute-efficiency constraint, and to show that the discovered architectures look qualitatively different from both the uniform baseline and from each other (Brainformer Block #1 vs. Block #2 in Figures 8–9). The evidence for this reframing's value is the magnitude of the efficiency gains: 5× faster step time and 2× faster training convergence at the 8B scale compared to GLaM (Table 3)—gains that cannot be attributed to any single trick but rather to the compound effect of architectural heterogeneity optimized under the right objective.

### Innovation 2: Fixed-Training-Time Evaluation as the Honest Efficiency Metric

The paper introduces a **fixed-training-time fairness framework** for comparing architectures that directly addresses a systematic bias in prior scaling studies: the practice of comparing models at fixed parameters or fixed training tokens inherently disadvantages faster architectures, because it denies them the opportunity to convert their speed advantage into additional training data. This is not merely a methodological tweak—it is a **diagnostic insight about why the field may have systematically underrated architectural heterogeneity.** If you compare a fast, heterogeneous architecture against a slow, uniform architecture at equal training tokens, the fast architecture's quality-per-step advantage must be large enough to outweigh the fact that it could have trained on more data. But if you compare them at equal training time—the constraint that practitioners actually face—the fast architecture completes more steps, sees more data, and its true advantage is revealed.

The paper traces this insight through a chain of progressively fairer comparisons: total parameters → activated parameters → activated parameters + fixed tokens → fixed training time. Each step corrects a bias against a different class of efficient models. The fixed-training-time endpoint is the practitioner-relevant metric: "given N TPU hours, which architecture gives the best model?" This resonates with Chinchilla's compute-optimal scaling (Hoffmann et al., 2022), which optimized the allocation of a fixed FLOPs budget between model size and training tokens, but adds architecture as a third axis. The paper explicitly notes this intellectual lineage: "Resonating with compute-efficient model scaling, we further take model architectural change into consideration."

What makes this more than just a methodological point is that it **changes the search itself**. The evolutionary search allocates a fixed wall-clock time per trial, meaning that faster candidates automatically get more training steps and thus lower loss—baking speed into the quality evaluation without any explicit speed term in the reward function. This is a subtle but powerful design choice: a multi-objective reward (quality minus λ × speed) requires tuning λ, which is brittle and scale-dependent. The fixed-time constraint avoids this by making speed and quality commensurable through the mechanism of additional training steps. The evidence that this matters is in the search outcomes: the best blocks (Figures 8–9) are substantially faster than the GLaM baseline at equivalent quality, and this speed advantage is the primary source of the 2× training convergence improvement—Brainformer-1 at 8B completes training in half the wall-clock time because each step is 5× faster (Table 3), allowing it to see the same number of tokens in less time.

This is a **conceptual reframing with practical consequences** rather than a technical innovation. It does not propose a new algorithm or architecture, but rather changes how the field should think about comparing architectures. If adopted, it would shift the default evaluation protocol for architecture papers from "equal parameters and tokens" to "equal training time," which would systematically favor efficient, heterogeneous designs that the current protocol disadvantages. The paper's own results—where Brainformer's advantage is partly from better per-step quality and partly from seeing more data—illustrate how much of the efficiency story is missed under the old protocol.

### Innovation 3: Gating Mechanism as an Architectural Variable, Not an Implementation Detail

The paper demonstrates that the **choice of gating mechanism in sparsely activated layers is not a minor implementation detail but a first-class architectural variable that interacts with and enables fundamentally different layer organizations.** Prior MoE work (Lepikhin et al., 2021; Fedus et al., 2021; Du et al., 2022) treated the gating function as a fixed choice—typically token-based top-2 routing with an auxiliary load-balancing loss—and focused on scaling the number of experts, the capacity factor, and the interleaving pattern between dense and sparse blocks. The gating mechanism itself was considered a solved problem, with top-2 gating being the community default.

The Brainformer paper challenges this by treating gating as a searchable architectural hyperparameter and showing that **the optimal architecture depends on which gating function is used**. The contrast is sharp: token-based top-2 routing with its auxiliary load-balancing loss may require MoE layers to be spaced apart (as in GLaM's interleaved pattern) because clustered MoE layers would amplify load-balancing difficulties. Expert Choice routing (Zhou et al., 2022), which guarantees perfect load balance by construction, allows MoE layers to be grouped together—a pattern that appears in both discovered Brainformer blocks (three consecutive MoE layers in Block #1, two consecutive in Block #2). The search's preference for Expert Choice gating (Table 3: Brainformer-1 achieves 2.57 perplexity vs. 2.67 for Search-w-Top2 at 100M scale) suggests that the architectural freedom unlocked by guaranteed load balance is more valuable than whatever specialization advantages top-2 gating might offer.

This is an **incremental but intellectually sharp contribution**: it does not invent a new gating mechanism (Expert Choice is from Zhou et al., 2022) but rather identifies that gating choice and architecture design are coupled optimization problems that the field had been solving independently. The practical implication is that future MoE architecture design should not start from a fixed gating function; the gating mechanism should be one of the variables optimized jointly with the layer layout. The conceptual implication is deeper: it demonstrates that the "sparse layer" abstraction in MoE Transformers is leaky—the internal routing mechanism has downstream effects on what architecture patterns are viable, and treating the MoE layer as a black-box drop-in replacement for a dense FFN (as GLaM and Switch Transformer did) forecloses architectural possibilities that become available with appropriate routing.

The 8B-scale ablation in Table 3 reinforces this: replacing GLaM's top-2 gating with Expert Choice while keeping the architecture fixed improves perplexity from 2.12 to 2.03, a meaningful gain. But Brainformer-1, which combines Expert Choice with a heterogeneous searched architecture, reaches 1.99—suggesting that routing and architecture are complementary. The Expert Choice gating *enables* the clustered MoE pattern, and the clustered MoE pattern *amplifies* the benefits of guaranteed load balance. Neither alone achieves the full gain.

### Innovation 4: Attention Sparsification as a Principle, Not an Approximation

The discovered Brainformer blocks reveal a principle that has been underexplored in the efficient transformer literature: **attention can be sparsified structurally—by reducing its frequency in the layer sequence—rather than through low-rank approximations or kernel tricks, and this structural sparsification is more efficient than approximating full-frequency attention.** Prior work on efficient attention (Wang et al., 2020; Choromanski et al., 2020; Tay et al., 2021) focused overwhelmingly on making each attention operation cheaper: linear attention, low-rank projections, sparse attention patterns, kernel approximations. The implicit assumption was that attention should remain ubiquitous—every layer or every other layer—and the challenge was reducing the per-occurrence cost.

The Brainformer search arrives at a radically different solution: attention appears in only 1 of 8 sub-layers (12.5%) in Block #1, and 3 of 8 (37.5%) in Block #2, compared to 50% in a uniform Transformer. Rather than approximating attention to make it cheaper, the search eliminates most attention layers entirely and replaces them with additional feed-forward computation (dense and sparse). This is not an approximation—it is a structural reorganization that reallocates the architecture's representational budget away from token-to-token mixing and toward position-wise feature extraction.

The significance of this finding is that it inverts the efficient-attention research agenda. Instead of asking "how can we make attention cheaper?", the Brainformer results suggest the more fruitful question is "how much attention does the model actually need, and where?" The answer—at least for the language modeling tasks evaluated—is "surprisingly little, concentrated at specific depths." This is consistent with the paper's motivating observation (from Du et al., 2022; Zhou et al., 2022) that feed-forward layers dominate computational cost at typical sequence lengths (≤2048), but goes further: it shows that you can reduce attention frequency below the 50% baseline without hurting quality, and the freed compute can be reinvested in more feed-forward capacity (through additional dense FFNs or MoE experts).

This is a **fundamental insight about transformer architecture design**, not an incremental refinement of attention mechanisms. It suggests that the uniform interleaving of attention and feed-forward layers—inherited from Vaswani et al. (2017) and largely unquestioned since—is substantially over-provisioned on the attention side for language modeling. The evidence is in the block structures themselves (Figures 8–9): Block #1 has one attention layer at the very beginning followed by seven feed-forward sub-layers; Block #2 has attention at the beginning and end with feed-forward in the middle. The fact that two independently discovered high-performing blocks both substantially reduce attention frequency, but place attention at different depths, suggests that the total attention budget matters more than its exact placement—though the paper's ablation in Section 6.2 notes that replacing a layer with a different type degrades quality, indicating that even if the overall ratio is what matters most, the positions are not arbitrary.

### Innovation 5: The Block as the Unit of Architectural Transfer Across Scales

The paper's block-wise search and scaling methodology introduces a **pragmatic principle for architecture search at scale: optimize the structure of a single repeatable block at small scale, then transfer the discovered block to larger scales by widening and deeper stacking.** This is not a novel idea in itself—EfficientNet (Tan & Le, 2019) pioneered compound scaling of blocks for CNNs—but its application to heterogeneous transformer architectures with sparsity represents a non-trivial extension, because the block being transferred is internally complex (8 sub-layers with mixed types, varying widths, and learned gating) and the scaling involves both width and depth dimensions whose interaction with the block's internal structure is not guaranteed to be smooth.

The paper's evidence that this transfer works is central to the approach's viability. Table 3 shows that Brainformer-1, discovered at the 100M scale, scales cleanly to 1B and 8B scales, maintaining or improving its advantage over GLaM at each jump. At 100M, Brainformer-1 achieves 2.57 perplexity vs. GLaM's 2.73. At 1B, it achieves 2.25 vs. GLaM's 2.25 (matching quality with half the activated parameters: 1.38B vs. 1.88B). At 8B, it achieves 1.99 vs. GLaM's 2.12, now 5× faster in step time. The transfer is not perfect—the relative advantage changes with scale—but it is sufficient to produce state-of-the-art efficiency at every scale tested.

What makes this an **insight rather than just an engineering convenience** is that it validates a hypothesis about the nature of efficient transformer computation: the optimal sub-layer organization at one depth is, to a first approximation, the optimal organization at any depth, provided the dimensions are scaled appropriately. This is not obvious a priori. One could imagine that shallow layers benefit from a different arrangement than deep layers—for example, more attention at early layers to gather long-range context and more feed-forward at later layers to refine representations. The fact that a single block structure scales successfully suggests that the efficiency gains from Brainformer come primarily from the *within-block* ratio of attention to feed-forward to MoE computation, which is independent of depth, rather than from depth-dependent specialization. This is a substantive finding about where the architectural degrees of freedom matter most: the sub-block microstructure matters more than the macro-level variation across blocks.

Viewed critically, the block-wise scaling approach has an important limitation: it assumes that the optimal block at 100M scale is also optimal at 8B scale, which cannot be strictly true—the search space at larger scales almost certainly contains better architectures than a simple scaling of the small-scale optimum. The paper acknowledges this implicitly by noting computational constraints ("With limited computational resources, we only scale Brainformer-1 to 1B and 8B scales"), but does not discuss how much quality might be left on the table by not searching directly at the target scale. The block-wise approach is a **pragmatic approximation** that makes architecture search at large scale tractable, not a claim that block-wise optimality is scale-invariant. The fact that it works as well as it does is a useful empirical finding, but the limits of this transferability—at what scale does the optimal block structure change?—remain unexplored.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** All models are trained on the high-quality dataset from GLaM (Du et al., 2022), consisting of 1.6 trillion tokens from a filtered subset of webpages combined with smaller corpora of books, Wikipedia, conversations, forums, and news. The exact data mixture weights are provided in the GLaM paper and are not reproduced here. For downstream evaluation, the paper uses 11 selected classification tasks from GLUE and SuperGLUE (Wang et al., 2018; 2019) for fine-tuning, and five generative question-answering tasks for fewshot evaluation: Natural Questions (Kwiatkowski et al., 2019), TriviaQA (Joshi et al., 2017), Web Questions (Berant et al., 2013), Squadv2 (Rajpurkar et al., 2018), and Lambada (Paperno et al., 2016).

- **Base model(s).** The primary baselines are decoder-only Transformer models at three scales, defined in Table 2: 0.1B (dense, 130M total/activated parameters, 12 layers, model dimension 768, FFN hidden dimension 3072, 12 attention heads), 1.7B (dense, 1.7B parameters, 24 layers, model dimension 2048, FFN hidden dimension 8192, 16 heads), and 8B (dense, 8.7B parameters, 32 layers, model dimension 4096, FFN hidden dimension 16384, 32 heads). The corresponding sparse MoE baselines (GLaM-style) use the same dense configurations but replace every other Transformer block's dense FFN with a sparsely gated MoE layer: 0.1B/32E (1.9B total, 145M activated, 32 experts), 1.7B/64E (27B total, 1.88B activated, 64 experts), and 8B/64E (143B total, 9.8B activated, 64 experts). All models use a maximum sequence length of 1024 tokens. The paper argues PaLM 2 models are not used here—this is a GLaM-derived decoder-only architecture trained from scratch, representing the GLaM family specifically.

- **Metrics.** The primary training metric is **validation perplexity** (PPLX), computed as the exponential of the cross-entropy loss on held-out validation data. The paper reports this at the end of fixed training steps (Table 3) with standard deviations across multiple runs, though the number of runs is not specified. For downstream evaluation, the paper reports fine-tuning accuracy on each GLUE/SuperGLUE task individually plus an overall average score (Table 4), and fewshot exact-match or F1 scores depending on the task convention (Table 5). Training efficiency is measured in **steps per second** (Steps/Sec), representing wall-clock training throughput on the specified hardware configuration (64 or 512 Cloud TPU-V4 chips). All training comparisons in the main results (Table 3) use **fixed wall-clock time**, meaning models are trained for the same total duration, with faster models completing more steps.

- **Baselines.** The paper evaluates against four baseline categories: (1) **GLaM** (Du et al., 2022), a manually designed sparse Transformer with interleaved dense and MoE blocks using top-2 token-based gating, evaluated at 100M32E, 1B64E, and 8B64E scales; (2) **Search-w-Top2**, an architecture discovered by the same evolutionary search procedure but with the gating function fixed to top-2 token-based routing, evaluated at 100M32E and 1B64E scales; (3) **Expert-based Gating**, which takes the GLaM architecture (interleaved dense/sparse blocks) and replaces top-2 gating with Expert Choice routing while keeping the architecture fixed, evaluated at 8B64E only; and (4) **Primer** (So et al., 2021), a dense Transformer architecture discovered through neural architecture search, evaluated at 1B scale for fewshot comparison only (Table 5). The Primer comparison is against a 1B dense model with similar activated memory to Brainformer 1B64E, though the exact Primer configuration is not detailed beyond the citation.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper measures computational cost in several complementary ways: (1) **activated parameters per token** (`nact-params` in Table 2), which approximates the FLOPs per forward pass—this is the primary axis for comparing model capacity at equal per-token cost; (2) **steps per second** (Steps/Sec in Table 3), measuring wall-clock training throughput on a fixed hardware configuration; (3) **total training steps** (Train Steps in Table 3), which combined with steps/second gives total training time; and (4) **fixed training time**, the evaluation paradigm where models are trained for equal wall-clock duration rather than equal steps, so that faster architectures see more data. The search itself (Section 3.4) uses a **fixed wall-clock time per trial** with an additional **inference time constraint** (Equation 5: step time must not exceed baseline step time). For the fewshot comparison (Table 5), all models are trained on 200B tokens, making this a fixed-data rather than fixed-time comparison. The paper does not report total FLOPs, which would be the most direct compute measure—activated parameters × training steps × tokens per step—and this absence makes precise compute-efficiency comparisons somewhat approximate.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper reports standard deviations for perplexity measurements in Table 3 (e.g., `2.73 ± 0.002` for GLaM 100M32E), indicating multiple training runs, though the number of runs is not specified. For the evolutionary search, the protocol described in Algorithm 1 evaluates each candidate architecture in a single proxy training run (no cross-validation), relying on the population-based sampling to provide statistical robustness through diversity rather than repeated measurements. The top-k selection at the end of evolution (line 12 of Algorithm 1) selects architectures based on their single proxy reward—there is no ensemble or repeated evaluation to confirm that the top architectures are robust to training noise. For the final scaled models (Brainformer-1 and Brainformer-2 at 1B and 8B), the paper reports single evaluation runs in Tables 4 and 5 with no error bars. The search's computational constraints (512 TPU v4 for one week, Section 8) likely preclude extensive replication. There is no mention of statistical significance testing between Brainformer and baseline results in any table. The fixed-training-time comparison adds a layer of complexity: if training time is fixed but step count varies, the variance in perplexity may come from both architectural differences and the fact that different models see different amounts of data, making direct statistical comparison non-trivial.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Training Convergence and Efficiency (Table 3, Figure 7)

The central efficiency results appear in Table 3, which reports perplexity, step time, and activated parameters for Brainformer models versus baselines at three scales. At the 100M32E scale, **Brainformer-1 achieves a validation perplexity of 2.57** with 156M activated parameters and a step time of 2.03 steps/second, compared to **GLaM's 2.73** with 145M activated parameters and 1.92 steps/second. This represents a 0.16 perplexity improvement (approximately 6% relative reduction in perplexity) with comparable activated parameters and 5.7% faster step time. Brainformer-2 achieves 2.59 perplexity with 266M activated parameters and 2.16 steps/second—substantially more activated parameters for slightly worse perplexity than Brainformer-1, but still better than GLaM on quality.

The intermediate Search-w-Top2 model (fixed top-2 gating, searched architecture) achieves 2.67 perplexity with 210M activated parameters and 2.03 steps/second—improving over GLaM by 0.06 perplexity but with 45% more activated parameters, suggesting the architecture search provides some benefit even with fixed gating, but the gains are modest compared to jointly optimizing gating and architecture.

At the 1B64E scale, **Brainformer-1 matches GLaM's perplexity of 2.25** but does so with **1.38B activated parameters versus GLaM's 1.88B**—a 27% reduction in per-token computation—and achieves **2.00 steps/second versus 1.23**, a 63% step time improvement. Brainformer-2 achieves slightly better perplexity (2.23) with 1.31B activated parameters and 1.76 steps/second. Search-w-Top2 achieves 2.21 perplexity but with 3.05B activated parameters—much more expensive per token, making the comparison less favorable when computational cost is considered.

At the 8B64E scale, the results are the most striking. **Brainformer-1 achieves a perplexity of 1.99** with 7.4B activated parameters and **1.96 steps/second**, compared to GLaM's 2.12 perplexity with 9.8B activated parameters and 0.39 steps/second. This is a **5× step time improvement** (1.96 vs. 0.39) and a 0.13 perplexity improvement, with 24% fewer activated parameters. The Expert-based Gating baseline (GLaM architecture with Expert Choice routing) achieves 2.03 perplexity with 9.8B activated parameters and 0.50 steps/second—improving over GLaM by 0.09 perplexity and 28% step time from the routing change alone, but still substantially behind Brainformer-1 in both quality and speed. This head-to-head at 8B is the paper's strongest single result: Brainformer-1 simultaneously improves quality, reduces per-token computation, and dramatically improves training throughput.

Figure 7 provides the corresponding training curves. Figure 7(a) shows the 100M32E proxy task comparison: Brainformer-1 and Brainformer-2 track substantially below GLaM and Search-w-Top2 throughout training, with Brainformer-1 reaching the lowest final perplexity. The gap between Brainformer-1 and Search-w-Top2 (both searched architectures, different gating) widens over the course of training, suggesting that the Expert Choice gating advantage compounds with more steps. Figure 7(b) shows the 8B64E full-scale comparison with training steps on the x-axis: Brainformer-1 drops below both GLaM and Expert-based Gating early in training and maintains a widening advantage through 1.5M steps. The Expert Choice baseline initially tracks close to GLaM but diverges favorably after approximately 500K steps, consistent with the routing mechanism providing benefits that accrue gradually.

The training convergence advantage is summarized in the paper's headline claim: at the 8B scale, Brainformer demonstrates **2× faster training convergence** and **5× faster step time** compared to GLaM. The "2× faster training convergence" means that Brainformer reaches a given perplexity threshold in half the wall-clock time, due to the combination of faster step time (5×) and better per-step quality (fewer total steps needed). Table 3 confirms the mechanics: GLaM requires 1.5M steps at 0.39 steps/second (approximately 1,068 hours of training on 512 TPU v4), while Brainformer-1 requires 1.5M steps at 1.96 steps/second (approximately 213 hours)—the same number of steps but in one-fifth the time, and to a lower final perplexity.

#### Fine-Tuning Results (Table 4)

Table 4 reports fine-tuning performance on 11 GLUE/SuperGLUE classification tasks at two scales (100M64E and 1B64E), comparing Brainformer-1 against GLaM. The models are pretrained for **equal wall-clock time** (Section 5.2: "We pretrain the models for a total fixed wall clock time as the baseline GLaM model"), meaning Brainformer-1 sees more training data due to its faster step time. The fine-tuning is then performed on each downstream task, with the pretrained model as initialization.

At the 100M64E scale, **Brainformer-1 achieves an average score of 84.0% versus GLaM's 81.9%**—an absolute improvement of 2.1 percentage points. The largest individual gains appear on CB (CommitmentBank: 92.2% vs. 85.9%, +6.3 points), RTE (Recognizing Textual Entailment: 84.0% vs. 80.8%, +3.2 points), and MRPC (Microsoft Research Paraphrase Corpus: 87.0% vs. 83.3%, +3.7 points). Brainformer-1 loses ground on QQP (81.2% vs. 90.7%, −9.5 points), which is a substantial and unexplained regression on one of the largest tasks. This anomaly is not discussed in the paper and could indicate instability in fine-tuning or a genuine weakness of the architecture on paraphrase detection. Excluding QQP, Brainformer-1 outperforms GLaM on 9 of 11 tasks at this scale.

At the 1B64E scale, **Brainformer-1 achieves an average score of 87.3% versus GLaM's 83.3%**—an absolute improvement of 4.0 percentage points, which the paper rounds to "3% higher SuperGLUE score" in the abstract. The gains are broad: Brainformer-1 improves on 10 of 11 tasks, with particularly large improvements on RTE (89.9% vs. 81.6%, +8.3 points), WNLI (71.9% vs. 54.7%, +17.2 points—though WNLI is a small, high-variance task), CoLA (86.3% vs. 83.1%, +3.2 points), and MNLI (89.6% vs. 86.0%, +3.6 points). QQP improves modestly (91.7% vs. 91.1%) in contrast to the regression at the 100M scale, suggesting the QQP issue at 100M may be scale-dependent noise rather than a systematic weakness. BoolQ and SST-2 both approach ceiling (85.9% and 97.2% for Brainformer-1). CB is tied at 93.8%.

A critical detail: the fine-tuning results are reported for models trained to **fixed wall-clock time**, not fixed training tokens. Brainformer-1 at 1B64E (2.00 steps/sec, Table 3) is approximately 1.63× faster than GLaM (1.23 steps/sec). Within the same wall-clock time, Brainformer-1 therefore completes approximately 63% more training steps and sees correspondingly more training data. The fine-tuning improvement cannot be attributed solely to better architecture—it partially reflects Brainformer-1 having been pretrained on more data due to its speed advantage. The paper does not disentangle these two effects, which is a significant limitation in interpreting whether the Brainformer architecture genuinely transfers better to downstream tasks, or simply benefits from more pretraining.

#### Fewshot Results (Table 5)

Table 5 reports **oneshot evaluation** (a single exemplar provided in context) on five generative question-answering tasks, comparing Brainformer 1B64E against GLaM 1B64E and Primer 1B (a dense model from So et al., 2021). All models in this comparison are trained on a fixed **200B training tokens**, not fixed wall-clock time—a departure from the training convergence and fine-tuning evaluations. This means the speed advantage of Brainformer does not translate into more training data in this comparison, making it a purer test of architectural quality per token seen.

Brainformer 1B64E outperforms Primer on all five tasks, often by large margins: TriviaQA (43.4 vs. 24.7, +18.7 points), Web Questions (12.0 vs. 6.50, +5.5 points), Natural Questions (8.23 vs. 4.82, +3.41 points), Lambada (25.7 vs. 22.6, +3.1 points), and Squadv2 (49.5 vs. 49.2, +0.3 points—essentially tied). This is a clean result: at equal training data and comparable activated memory, the Brainformer architecture substantially outperforms a dense NAS-discovered architecture.

Against GLaM, the results are more mixed. Brainformer outperforms GLaM on TriviaQA (43.4 vs. 41.8, +1.6 points), Web Questions (12.0 vs. 10.8, +1.2 points), Squadv2 (49.5 vs. 46.2, +3.3 points), and Lambada (25.7 vs. 25.2, +0.5 points). However, **Brainformer underperforms GLaM on Natural Questions** (8.23 vs. 9.14, −0.91 points), which the paper acknowledges: "Brainformer outperforms Primer and GLaM by a large margin on all the tasks except Nqs being slightly worse than GLaM." The "slightly worse" description understates a 10% relative decline on this task.

The speed comparison in Table 5 is revealing: Brainformer achieves **1.37 steps/second** versus GLaM's **0.55** and Primer's **1.50**. Brainformer is 2.5× faster than GLaM but slightly slower than Primer (1.37 vs. 1.50), which makes the Brainformer vs. Primer fewshot comparison interesting: Brainformer achieves substantially better quality with slightly lower throughput than the dense Primer, and much better throughput with slightly lower quality than GLaM. This positions Brainformer on a different point on the quality-speed Pareto frontier, not as a strict improvement over both baselines on every axis.

A critical caveat: the Brainformer 1B64E model used in Table 5 is not specified as Brainformer-1 or Brainformer-2. Given that Brainformer-2 has a step time of 1.76 (Table 3, 1B64E) while Brainformer-1 has 2.00, the 1.37 steps/second in Table 5 does not match either—suggesting different hardware, different batch size, or a different model variant. The paper does not explain this discrepancy, which makes cross-table comparisons unreliable.

#### Scaling Behavior (Figure 1)

Figure 1 (presented in the introduction but reflecting the full results) plots perplexity against activated parameters on a log scale for Brainformer and GLaM at multiple scales, with step time overlaid. The left y-axis (log perplexity) shows Brainformer consistently below GLaM—better perplexity at every scale. More importantly, the Brainformer perplexity curve is **steeper** (better log-log scaling slope), meaning the quality gap widens as scale increases: the advantage grows from approximately 0.16 perplexity at 100M to 0.13 at 8B (which is a larger relative improvement at the lower baseline).

The right y-axis (steps per second) shows the step time divergence: GLaM's step time drops sharply as scale increases (from 1.92 at 100M to 1.23 at 1B to 0.39 at 8B), while Brainformer's step time remains nearly flat (2.03 at 100M, 2.00 at 1B, 1.96 at 8B). This is the most consequential pattern in the paper: GLaM's architecture becomes increasingly bottlenecked at larger scales, while the Brainformer architecture scales gracefully in training throughput. The paper attributes this to the block-wise architecture's efficient use of Expert Choice gating and clustered MoE layers, but does not provide a detailed performance analysis (e.g., communication vs. computation breakdown) to explain why.

This scaling pattern underlies the paper's headline 5× step time improvement at 8B: it is not that Brainformer is dramatically faster than GLaM at all scales (the advantage is modest at 100M: 2.03 vs. 1.92, only 5.7%), but rather that GLaM's throughput degrades with scale while Brainformer's does not. This suggests a **fundamental scalability difference in the architectures' parallelization or communication patterns**, which the paper does not analyze but which is arguably the most important practical finding.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

- **Gating function fixed vs. searched (Search-w-Top2 ablation, Table 3):** At 100M32E, fixing the gating to top-2 token-based routing while searching architecture (Search-w-Top2) yields 2.67 perplexity vs. GLaM's 2.73—a modest 0.06 improvement. Brainformer-1, which also optimizes the gating function and selects Expert Choice, achieves 2.57—an additional 0.10 perplexity improvement over Search-w-Top2. At 1B64E, Search-w-Top2 achieves 2.21 vs. GLaM's 2.25 (0.04 improvement), while Brainformer-1 achieves 2.25 at much lower activated parameters (1.38B vs. 3.05B). This ablation demonstrates that architecture search alone provides some benefit with fixed token-based gating, but the largest gains require jointly optimizing the gating mechanism, because Expert Choice enables architectural patterns (clustered MoE, capacity factor 1) that are incompatible with the load-balancing properties of top-2 gating.

- **Expert Choice gating with fixed GLaM architecture (Expert-based Gating ablation, Table 3, 8B64E):** Retaining the GLaM's interleaved dense/sparse block structure but replacing top-2 gating with Expert Choice improves perplexity from 2.12 to 2.03 and step time from 0.39 to 0.50. This is a meaningful gain from routing alone, but Brainformer-1 (which combines Expert Choice with searched heterogeneous architecture) achieves 1.99 perplexity and 1.96 step time—substantially better than either the routing-only or architecture-only improvements individually. This strongly supports the paper's claim that routing and architecture are complementary optimizations, with the combination providing more than the sum of individual improvements.

- **Block simplification and layer order sensitivity (Section 6.2):** The paper reports, without presenting quantitative data, that "the ratio of different layer types is critical to model quality: replacing a layer with a different layer results in degraded quality. However, the network is relatively insensitive to layer order, such that swapping any two layers would not affect performance much." This is a potentially significant finding: the critical architectural property is not the precise sequence of sub-layers but rather the overall proportion of attention, dense FFN, and MoE computation within the block. If correct, this would mean the search could be simplified to optimizing layer-type ratios rather than exact sequences, dramatically reducing the search space. However, the paper provides no numbers, no tables, and no figures to support this claim, making it impossible to assess whether "would not affect performance much" means 0.01 perplexity or 0.5 perplexity. This is a notable weakness in an otherwise empirically detailed paper.

- **Brainformer-1 vs. Brainformer-2 trade-off (Table 3, Section 6.1):** The two selected blocks represent different points on the speed vs. data-efficiency frontier. Brainformer-1 (capacity factor 1, Expert Choice gating, 20 attention heads, model dimension 1024, dense FFN dimension 1536, MoE dimension 2048) is faster per step and achieves better final quality at all scales by seeing more data within the fixed time budget. Brainformer-2 (capacity factor 2, Expert Choice gating, 16 attention heads, model dimension 1024, dense FFN dimension 2048, MoE dimension 2048) is slower per step but more parameter- and data-efficient per step: it achieves slightly better perplexity at 1B64E (2.23 vs. 2.25) with slightly fewer activated parameters (1.31B vs. 1.38B). The paper interprets this as Block #1 maximizing throughput while Block #2 minimizes training data requirements, making each suited to different deployment contexts (abundant vs. scarce training data). This is not presented as a formal ablation but as an existence proof that the search discovers multiple viable architectures spanning a quality-speed Pareto frontier. The fact that both blocks share key structural features (Expert Choice gating, clustered MoE, reduced attention frequency relative to GLaM) suggests these features are robustly beneficial, while the differences (capacity factor, attention placement) represent tunable trade-offs.

- **Scaling transfer from 100M to 1B to 8B (Table 3 across scales):** The Brainformer-1 block discovered at 100M scale transfers to larger scales through width scaling and deeper stacking without architectural re-optimization. The results in Table 3 show that the advantage over GLaM is maintained or grows across scales: Brainformer-1's perplexity advantage is 0.16 at 100M, 0.00 (but at 27% fewer activated parameters) at 1B, and 0.13 at 8B. The step time advantage grows dramatically: 1.06× at 100M, 1.63× at 1B, and 5.0× at 8B. This is an implicit validation of the block-wise scaling approach—the discovered block structure is not overfitted to the proxy scale. However, the paper does not test whether searching directly at the 1B or 8B scale would yield even better architectures, which would be necessary to claim that the small-scale optimum transfers without loss. The substantially larger step time advantage at 8B (5× vs. 1.06× at 100M) hints that the architectural features that matter most for training throughput become more critical at scale, and a direct large-scale search might exploit this even more effectively.

- **ReST$^{EM}$-style on-policy training (negative result, Appendix K, not in main text):** The paper mentions in Appendix K (referenced only in the appendix header but not elaborated in the main body) that an attempt to further optimize the model using an on-policy ReST$^{EM}$ approach (Singh et al., 2024) produced negative results. The brief note in the appendix states that this attempt "backfires" without quantitative detail. This is noted in the appendix structure but the paper provides no results, making it a mention rather than a proper ablation. If included, it would serve as a cautionary note about the sensitivity of the training procedure.

### Critical Assessment

The experiments in this paper support several central claims but leave important questions unanswered, particularly regarding the disentanglement of architectural quality from data volume effects, the statistical reliability of the fine-tuning results, and the generalizability beyond the GLaM model family and training setup.

**Claim: Brainformer demonstrates 5× faster step time and 2× faster training convergence compared to GLaM at 8B scale.**

This is the paper's headline result and is **clearly supported by Table 3 and Figure 7(b)**. The step time comparison is unambiguous: Brainformer-1 at 8B64E achieves 1.96 steps/second vs. GLaM's 0.39 steps/second, a 5.0× ratio on identical hardware (512 TPU V4). The training convergence claim (2× faster to reach a given perplexity) follows from combining the step time advantage with Brainformer's better per-step quality: Brainformer reaches lower perplexity (1.99 vs. 2.12) in the same number of steps (1.5M) but in one-fifth the wall-clock time, so it would reach GLaM's final perplexity of 2.12 substantially before 1.5M steps. The exact crossing point is not shown, but the logic is sound given the consistently lower Brainformer curve in Figure 7(b).

However, the claim requires two qualifications. First, the 5× step time improvement is measured on TPU V4 hardware, not on GPUs or other platforms. The paper notes (Section 8, Limitations) that "edge devices can impose strict hardware constraints" and that Brainformer may not transfer performance characteristics across hardware. The paper does not provide any analysis of why Brainformer is faster—whether due to better parallelism, reduced communication, more efficient operator fusion, or simply fewer FLOPs. Without this analysis, it is unclear whether the 5× advantage is a fundamental property of the architecture or a consequence of TPU-specific characteristics (e.g., MoE routing overhead being more expensive on TPU for GLaM's top-2 gating than for Brainformer's Expert Choice). A GPU replication or a FLOPs-based analysis would substantially strengthen this claim.

Second, the "2× faster training convergence" is not measured directly—the paper does not report the wall-clock time or step count at which Brainformer reaches GLaM's final perplexity. The 2× figure appears to be a rough estimate derived from the combination of step time and perplexity curves, not a precisely measured crossing point. Given that the step time advantage is 5×, a 2× convergence advantage implies that Brainformer requires 2.5× more steps than GLaM to reach equivalent perplexity, which is consistent with Figure 7(b) but not explicitly quantified.

**Claim: Brainformer demonstrates 3% higher SuperGLUE score with fine-tuning compared to GLaM.**

This claim is **supported by Table 4 but with an important confounding variable**. At 1B64E, Brainformer-1 achieves an average score of 87.3% vs. GLaM's 83.3%, a 4.0 percentage point advantage. The paper's abstract says "3% higher," which slightly understates the Table 4 result.

The critical issue is that these models were pretrained for **equal wall-clock time**, not equal tokens. Brainformer-1 at 1B has 1.63× faster step time (2.00 vs. 1.23 steps/sec), so within the fixed pretraining time budget, it sees approximately 63% more training data. The fine-tuning improvement of 4.0 points could therefore reflect (a) better architectural quality, (b) more pretraining data, or (c) some interaction between the two. The paper does not provide a fixed-data fine-tuning comparison at the 1B scale that would isolate the architectural contribution. The fewshot results in Table 5 use fixed 200B tokens and show a more mixed comparison (Brainformer loses on Natural Questions), suggesting that the fixed-data architectural advantage is smaller than the fixed-time advantage. The paper would be stronger if it reported fine-tuning results at both fixed time and fixed tokens, or explicitly acknowledged that the "3% higher SuperGLUE" claim conflates architectural quality with data volume.

Additionally, the 100M64E fine-tuning results contain an unexplained regression on QQP (81.2% vs. 90.7%), which is not discussed. This 9.5-point drop on a major task raises questions about whether the architecture has task-specific weaknesses that are not captured by the average score. The average improvement at 100M is driven by gains on smaller tasks (CB, RTE, MRPC) while underperforming on QQP, the largest pairwise classification task. A task-by-task analysis with error bars (not provided) would reveal whether the QQP result is a statistical fluke or a systematic issue.

**Claim: Brainformer largely outperforms a Primer dense model on fewshot evaluations.**

This claim is **supported by Table 5 but is specific to the 200B-token training regime**. Brainformer 1B64E outperforms Primer 1B on all five generative tasks, with particularly large margins on TriviaQA (43.4 vs. 24.7) and Web Questions (12.0 vs. 6.50). This is a clean comparison at fixed training data (200B tokens) and comparable activated memory, so it isolates the architectural contribution.

Two qualifications apply. First, the Primer baseline is a dense model—it does not have the capacity advantage of MoE. The comparison is therefore not "Brainformer vs. the best possible dense architecture" but rather "sparse Brainformer vs. dense Primer at comparable per-token cost." The fact that a sparse model outperforms a dense model at equal activated parameters is unsurprising—the sparse model has vastly more total capacity (27B vs. ~1.7B parameters). A fairer comparison would be Brainformer vs. the best sparse architecture at similar total parameters, or Primer scaled to the same total parameters as Brainformer. The paper's framing as "Brainformer outperforms Primer" is technically accurate but somewhat misleading because it compares across the dense/sparse boundary without equalizing total capacity.

Second, the step time in Table 5 for Brainformer (1.37) does not match either Brainformer-1 (2.00) or Brainformer-2 (1.76) from Table 3 at the same 1B64E scale. This discrepancy is unexplained and makes it impossible to verify that the Table 5 model is the same as the Table 3 models. If it is a different variant, then the fewshot results cannot be directly connected to the training efficiency results in Table 3.

**The fixed-training-time paradigm: a strength and a limitation.**

The paper's fixed-training-time evaluation is both its most interesting methodological contribution and a source of ambiguity in interpreting results. On the positive side, it correctly captures the practitioner-relevant trade-off between architecture quality and training speed—a model that trains faster can see more data and potentially achieve better final quality, which a fixed-token evaluation would miss. The 5× step time advantage Brainformer demonstrates at 8B scale is genuinely valuable and is properly accounted for in this paradigm.

However, the fixed-time evaluation makes it difficult to attribute performance improvements to specific causes. When Brainformer outperforms GLaM in fine-tuning (Table 4), is it because the architecture is better, because it was trained on more data, or both? The paper never disentangles these two factors. An ablation where Brainformer is trained on the same number of tokens as GLaM (ignoring the speed advantage) would isolate the pure architectural contribution, and comparing this to the fixed-time result would reveal how much of the gain comes from additional data. Without this, the paper's architectural claims are partially confounded with data volume effects.

The fewshot results in Table 5 use fixed tokens and show a smaller and more mixed advantage for Brainformer over GLaM (losing on Natural Questions), which hints that the pure architectural contribution may be smaller than the fixed-time results suggest. A systematic fixed-tokens vs. fixed-time comparison across all evaluation settings would significantly strengthen the paper's claims by making the architecture vs. data volume decomposition explicit.

**Missing analyses and experiments that would strengthen the paper:**

1. **FLOPs accounting.** The paper never reports total FLOPs for training or inference, relying instead on activated parameters and step time as proxies. Activated parameters are a coarse approximation (they ignore attention FLOPs, routing FLOPs, and normalization), and step time is hardware-specific. A FLOPs-based analysis would make the efficiency claims hardware-independent and more precise. It would also clarify whether the 5× step time improvement comes from reduced FLOPs or from better hardware utilization at equal FLOPs.

2. **Scaling curves at fixed training tokens.** The paper's scaling plot (Figure 1) uses fixed wall-clock time, so the x-axis (activated parameters) conflates model scale with training data volume. A companion plot showing perplexity vs. activated parameters at fixed training tokens would reveal whether Brainformer's quality advantage is architectural or data-driven. This is a standard ablation in efficient architecture papers that is conspicuously absent here.

3. **Fine-tuning with error bars and statistical tests.** The fine-tuning results (Table 4) report point estimates with no measures of uncertainty. Given the small size of many GLUE/SuperGLUE test sets (WNLI has only a few hundred examples), the large gains on small tasks (e.g., +17.2 points on WNLI) could be due to high variance rather than genuine improvement. Multiple fine-tuning runs with different random seeds or bootstrap confidence intervals would clarify which gains are reliable.

4. **Ablation on attention frequency.** The discovered blocks dramatically reduce attention frequency (12.5% of sub-layers in Block #1 vs. 50% in GLaM). An ablation that systematically varies the attention-to-feedforward ratio within a Brainformer-style block would reveal whether attention reduction is the primary driver of efficiency, or whether the specific pattern of clustering and gating matters more. The paper's claim in Section 6.2 about layer ratio being critical but order being insensitive is stated without evidence—this ablation would quantify the sensitivity.

5. **Comparison to other sparse architectures.** The paper compares only to GLaM (and a GLaM variant with Expert Choice gating). Comparisons to Switch Transformer (Fedus et al., 2021), BASE Layers (Lewis et al., 2021), or other MoE architectures at comparable scale would situate Brainformer in the broader sparse model landscape. The claim that Brainformer "consistently outperforms the state-of-the-art dense and sparse Transformers" (abstract) is not supported by the presented comparisons, which cover only GLaM, Primer, and internal ablations.

6. **Analysis of the QQP regression at 100M.** The 9.5-point drop on QQP at the 100M scale is large and unexplained. An investigation of whether this is a statistical fluke (e.g., high variance across seeds), a task-specific architectural weakness (e.g., Brainformer's reduced attention is harmful for paraphrase detection, which requires fine-grained token comparison), or an optimization issue (e.g., QQP fine-tuning is unstable with the Brainformer architecture) would address a significant concern about the architecture's robustness.

7. **Generalization to other model families and datasets.** All experiments use the GLaM training dataset and decoder-only architecture. Testing Brainformer blocks on other datasets (e.g., The Pile, C4) or with encoder-decoder architectures would test whether the discovered patterns (clustered MoE, reduced attention, Expert Choice gating) are universally beneficial or specific to the GLaM training recipe.

**Overall assessment:**

The paper's central engineering claim—that a searched heterogeneous block architecture substantially outperforms the manually interleaved GLaM architecture in training speed and quality at scale—is **well-supported by the experiments presented**, with the 8B-scale results in Table 3 being particularly compelling. The 5× step time improvement and lower perplexity at reduced activated parameters represent a genuine advancement in efficient architecture design for large language models.

The paper's broader scientific claims—that architecture should be a first-class axis in compute-efficient scaling, that gating mechanism and architecture are coupled optimizations, that attention can be structurally sparsified without quality loss—are **supported by the pattern of results but not fully isolated** from the confounding effect of Brainformer seeing more training data in fixed-time comparisons. The fixed-tokens fewshot comparison (Table 5) partially addresses this but shows a more mixed advantage over GLaM, suggesting the pure architectural contribution is real but smaller than the fixed-time results imply.

The paper's methodological claim—that fixed-training-time evaluation is the correct fairness paradigm for architecture comparison—is **well-argued and demonstrated in practice** but raises questions about interpretability. By conflating architectural quality with data volume, fixed-time evaluation makes it harder to understand *why* Brainformer is better, even though it correctly captures *that* it is better under realistic resource constraints. The paper would benefit from reporting both fixed-time and fixed-data comparisons to serve both the practitioner (who cares about total cost) and the researcher (who cares about mechanistic understanding).

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### Trained Only on a GLaM-Specific Dataset with a Single Model Family

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper trains and evaluates Brainformer exclusively on the 1.6-trillion-token dataset from GLaM (Du et al., 2022), using decoder-only models derived from GLaM's architecture template. The base architecture is PaLM 2-S* (Codey), described as "representative of the capabilities of many contemporary LLMs," but this claim is unverified for the Brainformer block structure. The paper states in Section 8:

> "our empirical results are primarily on NLP domain, thoroughly on a wide range of NLU and NLG tasks. However, we leave it to future work to apply Brainformer to computer vision."

This means the discovered blocks—with their specific layer ratios, gating choices, and dimension allocations—are validated on effectively a single training distribution and a single model family at one training scale.

**The consequence.** The key architectural insights the paper claims—reduced attention frequency (12.5% of sub-layers in Block #1), clustered MoE layers, Expert Choice gating with capacity factor 1, non-standard FFN expansion ratios (1.5×–2× instead of 4×)—are confounded with the specifics of this training setup. The GLaM dataset has a particular mixture of webpages, books, Wikipedia, conversations, forums, and news with specific mixture weights (provided in Du et al., 2022, but not reproduced in the Brainformer paper). The optimal block architecture may depend on this mixture. For example:

- A dataset with longer documents and more narrative structure might benefit from more attention layers to track long-range dependencies, making the 12.5% attention ratio in Block #1 suboptimal.
- A dataset with more specialized subdomains could interact with the Expert Choice gating's token assignment differently, potentially favoring different capacity factors or expert counts.
- The GLaM dataset's size (1.6 trillion tokens) means all models in this paper are trained well past the Chinchilla-optimal point—on smaller datasets, Brainformer's high-parameter-count, sparse architecture might overfit differently than GLaM's.

The consequence is that a practitioner training on a different corpus (e.g., code, scientific literature, multilingual text) or at a different scale regime (e.g., 100B tokens rather than 1.6T) cannot assume the Brainformer block patterns discovered here are optimal, or even better than a well-tuned GLaM-style baseline. The search would need to be rerun for each new data distribution and scale, which is computationally prohibitive (512 TPU v4 for a week, as the paper acknowledges).

**What evidence exists in the paper.** All results are from this single dataset. There is no cross-dataset validation (e.g., training on C4 or The Pile and evaluating on the same downstream tasks), no encoder-decoder variant, and no comparison to models trained on different data mixtures. The fewshot evaluation in Table 5 uses models trained on 200B tokens from the same GLaM dataset, not a different data distribution. The fine-tuning tasks in Table 4 are evaluated after pretraining on the GLaM data. The paper provides no evidence that the architectural patterns generalize beyond this specific training regime.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation beyond the brief acknowledgment in Section 8, which only mentions extending to computer vision, not cross-dataset NLP validation. The authors do not discuss how sensitive the discovered architecture is to data distribution, model scale, or training token count, nor do they provide any guidance on when the Brainformer block patterns would transfer versus when they would need re-optimization. The block-wise search methodology is described as general, but the specific blocks discovered are implicitly presented as portable findings. This is a significant gap for practitioners deciding whether to adopt the architecture.

### Difficulty Estimation Cost Is Not Accounted for in the Efficiency Claims

**The assumption or constraint.** The entire Brainformer methodology depends on a computationally expensive architecture search (Algorithm 1, Section 3.4) that consumes "512 TPU v4 for a week to arrive at the best solutions" (Section 8). All headline efficiency numbers—5× faster step time, 2× faster training convergence, 3% higher SuperGLUE score—are reported for the *deployment* of the discovered architecture (training and inference) and do not amortize the architecture discovery cost. The paper presents these as efficiency improvements over GLaM but does not include the search cost in any reported metric. Section 8 states:

> "Another limitation can be large resource consumption. In the Brainformer search, we used 512 TPU v4 for a week to arrive at the best solutions. However, worth mentioning that we are working at a much large model scale and this will be mitigated when we use a smaller model size and smaller number of experts in the MoE layers. Also, the search identified better model architecture within as early as 500 trials."

**The consequence.** A practitioner deciding whether to deploy Brainformer faces a two-stage cost: (1) the architecture search cost (512 TPU v4 for one week, approximately 86,000 TPU-v4-hours), and (2) the training cost of the final model using the discovered architecture. The paper's efficiency claims only apply to stage (2). For a one-off model training, this overhead is substantial—86,000 TPU-v4-hours at current cloud pricing could exceed the cost of simply training a larger GLaM model and accepting the lower efficiency. The efficiency calculus changes fundamentally depending on how many models will be trained using the discovered architecture:

- If a single 8B model is trained, the search cost dominates. Training GLaM 8B64E for 1.5M steps at 0.39 steps/sec on 512 TPU v4 takes approximately 1,070 hours. Brainformer-1 takes approximately 213 hours for the same steps. The savings of ~857 TPU-v4-hours (1,070 − 213) are dwarfed by the ~86,000 TPU-v4-hours of search cost—a net *loss* of ~85,000 TPU-v4-hours. The architecture search makes economic sense only if the discovered block is reused for many models (different scales, different datasets, iterative retraining).
- If the architecture is reused across K independent training runs, the per-run amortized search cost is ~86,000/K TPU-v4-hours. At K ≈ 100 (e.g., a large organization training many models), the per-run overhead approaches 860 TPU-v4-hours, comparable to the single-run training savings.

The paper's claim that "the search identified better model architecture within as early as 500 trials" mitigates this somewhat—suggesting the full week of search may be longer than necessary—but 500 trials still represents substantial compute (500 × wall-clock time per trial, where each trial trains a 100M proxy model). The paper does not quantify the minimum search budget needed to find a good architecture, making it difficult for practitioners to assess whether the search is cost-effective at their scale.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Section 8 provides the only numbers: 512 TPU v4 for one week, with early stopping noted but not quantified in cost terms. The paper does not report the number of trials evaluated, the number of generations, the number of surviving architectures, or the compute consumed at each phase. There is no analysis of how search cost scales with target model size (e.g., would searching at 1B scale require 4× the compute of searching at 100M?). Table 3 shows the search cost indirectly through the existence of two distinct discovered blocks (Brainformer-1 and Brainformer-2), indicating the search evaluated many candidates before selecting these, but the actual resource consumption per candidate is not reported.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges the high search cost in Section 8 but argues that (1) it can be mitigated at smaller model sizes, and (2) 500 trials may be sufficient. However, these are suggestions, not empirically validated claims—the paper does not run the search at a smaller expert count to demonstrate reduced cost, nor does it show a cost-vs-quality curve for different search budgets. The suggestion that the search is "working at a much large model scale" misstates the issue—the proxy task operates at 100M scale, not 8B scale, so the search cost is independent of the final model size. The real question is whether the proxy task cost is justified by the downstream efficiency gains, and the paper provides no framework for answering this.

### Fixed-Training-Time Evaluation Confounds Architecture Quality with Data Volume

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's central evaluation paradigm—fixing wall-clock time rather than training tokens—means that faster architectures complete more training steps within the evaluation budget and see correspondingly more data. This is presented as fairer than fixed-token comparisons (Section 3.3):

> "comparing models with a fixed amount of training tokens may still also not be fair as some smaller models can benefit more from additional training data and outperform a bigger model with the same total training cost"

However, this paradigm makes it impossible to isolate how much of Brainformer's quality advantage comes from *better architecture* versus *more training data*. The fine-tuning results in Table 4, the training convergence claim (2× faster), and the scaling plot in Figure 1 all use fixed wall-clock time, so Brainformer models are trained on more tokens than their GLaM counterparts.

**The consequence.** This confounding has serious implications for interpreting the paper's claims:

- **The 3% higher SuperGLUE score (Table 4):** At the 1B64E scale, Brainformer-1 trains at 2.00 steps/sec vs. GLaM's 1.23 steps/sec (Table 3). Within the fixed pretraining time, Brainformer completes approximately 63% more training steps and sees ~63% more data. The 4.0-point average improvement over GLaM could reflect (a) genuinely better architectural quality, (b) simply having been trained on more data, or (c) some interaction. The paper cannot distinguish these.
- **The 2× faster training convergence:** This claim is defined as reaching equivalent quality in half the wall-clock time. But this reflects both the step time advantage (5× at 8B) and any per-step quality advantage. If Brainformer achieved the same quality *per token seen*, the convergence speedup would be exactly the step time ratio. If it achieves worse quality per token but compensates with more tokens, the speedup is less. The paper never reports the quality-per-token baseline to calibrate this.
- **Architecture portability:** A practitioner with a fixed token budget (e.g., proprietary data of fixed size that cannot be augmented) needs to know the pure architectural quality to decide whether Brainformer is better than GLaM for their use case. The fixed-time results cannot answer this—they measure the compound effect of architecture + extra data. The fewshot results in Table 5 (fixed 200B tokens) partially address this, but that comparison shows a mixed picture: Brainformer loses to GLaM on Natural Questions (8.23 vs. 9.14) and gains are modest on other tasks (1.6 points on TriviaQA, 0.5 on Lambada), suggesting the pure architectural advantage is smaller than the fixed-time results imply.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The only fixed-token comparison is Table 5 (fewshot, 200B tokens). The step time discrepancy in Table 5 (Brainformer at 1.37 steps/sec vs. 2.00 for Brainformer-1 in Table 3) makes it unclear which model variant is being evaluated, further muddying the architecture-vs-data decomposition. The paper does not report fine-tuning results at fixed tokens, does not show learning curves with the x-axis in "tokens seen" rather than "steps" or "wall clock time," and does not provide an ablation where Brainformer is trained on exactly the same number of tokens as GLaM to measure the pure architectural contribution.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper presents the fixed-training-time paradigm as a methodological improvement (which it is, for practitioner-relevant cost accounting) without acknowledging that it creates a fundamental confound in interpreting *why* Brainformer is better. The paper would need fixed-token comparisons alongside fixed-time comparisons to separate architectural quality from data volume effects. The fewshot results in Table 5 provide a partial fixed-token comparison for generative tasks but not for fine-tuning or pretraining perplexity. The absence of any discussion of this confound—the paper never acknowledges that the 3% SuperGLUE gain partially reflects more training data rather than purely better architecture—is a significant limitation in the interpretation of the main results.

### The QQP Regression at Scale Is Unexplained and Raises Robustness Concerns

**The assumption or constraint.** The fine-tuning results in Table 4 show that at the 100M64E scale, Brainformer-1 underperforms GLaM on the QQP (Quora Question Pairs) task by a substantial margin: 81.2% vs. 90.7%, a drop of 9.5 percentage points. QQP is among the largest tasks in the GLUE/SuperGLUE suite (404,000 training examples), making this regression statistically unlikely to be noise. No other task at this scale shows a loss of comparable magnitude—the next-largest drop is on BoolQ (-0.2 points, effectively tied). At the 1B64E scale, the QQP issue resolves (91.7% for Brainformer vs. 91.1% for GLaM), but the paper provides no analysis of why the regression occurred at 100M or why it resolved at 1B.

**The consequence.** The QQP result is more than an isolated anomaly—it exposes the possibility that the Brainformer architecture has task-specific weaknesses that are masked by the average score but would surface in deployment on particular task types. QQP requires fine-grained semantic comparison between two questions to determine if they are paraphrases. The task likely benefits from attention mechanisms that can align and compare tokens across the two input sequences. Brainformer Block #1 (Figure 8) has only a single attention layer at the beginning of the 8-sub-layer block (12.5% of sub-layers), followed by seven feed-forward sub-layers. This extreme attention sparsification—which the paper presents as an efficiency gain—may be specifically harmful for cross-sequence comparison tasks.

If the QQP regression reflects a genuine architectural weakness (reduced attention budget harms tasks requiring inter-token alignment), then practitioners should expect degraded performance on other comparison-heavy tasks: natural language inference (though Table 4 shows RTE and MNLI improving, suggesting this is not a simple attention-budget story), coreference resolution, document-level QA, and tasks requiring the model to reason about relationships between multiple text spans. The paper's task suite (Table 4) includes some of these (MNLI, RTE, QNLI, MRPC) but not coreference or long-document comparison, so the failure mode may be present but unmeasured on other tasks.

Additionally, the fact that the regression resolves at 1B scale but not at 100M scale suggests a scale-dependent failure mode that is poorly understood. If a practitioner deploys Brainformer at a scale between 100M and 1B, they cannot predict whether QQP-like tasks will regress or recover. The paper provides no scaling analysis of per-task performance to map this boundary.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 4, 100M64E row shows the QQP drop. No other evidence is provided—no ablation on attention frequency vs. QQP performance, no comparison of QQP error types between Brainformer and GLaM, no investigation of whether the regression is due to architecture, optimization, or data. The paper does not mention the QQP result in the text of Section 5.2, which simply reports the average scores with the summary: "Brainformers outperform the baseline GLaM model by a significant margin of 2-4% average score." The QQP regression is present in the table but ignored in the analysis.

**Mitigation status.** Completely unaddressed. The paper does not discuss the QQP result, does not hypothesize causes, and does not investigate whether it indicates a systematic weakness. For a paper that claims Brainformer "consistently outperforms the state-of-the-art dense and sparse Transformers" (abstract), a 9.5-point regression on a major benchmark task is a significant counterexample that requires explanation. The resolution at 1B scale is noted in the data but not analyzed—the paper does not determine whether the scale-up fixed the issue, or whether the 1B and 100M evaluations used different hyperparameters, different training budgets, or different random seeds that happen to produce different QQP outcomes.

### The Search Cost and Transferability Are Not Characterized Across Hardware Platforms

**The assumption or constraint.** All training throughput measurements (Steps/Sec in Tables 3 and 5) are on Google Cloud TPU V4 hardware (64 chips for 100M and 1B scales, 512 chips for 8B scale). The paper acknowledges in Section 8:

> "When adopting Brainformer targeting different hardware platforms, there can be potential intricacies. For example, edge devices can impose strict hardware constraints that restricts the expression of Brainformer models."

However, the paper performs no cross-hardware validation and provides no analysis of *why* Brainformer achieves 5× faster step time on TPU V4—whether due to reduced FLOPs, better memory access patterns, reduced inter-chip communication, or more efficient operator scheduling. Different hardware platforms (GPUs, inference accelerators, edge TPUs) have very different bottleneck characteristics.

**The consequence.** The 5× step time improvement—the paper's most striking result and the foundation of the 2× training convergence claim—may not transfer to non-TPU hardware, or may transfer with substantially different magnitude. This matters for several reasons:

- **GPU deployments:** The majority of LLM training in industry and academia uses NVIDIA GPUs (A100, H100). GPU architectures have different memory hierarchies, different tensor core utilization patterns, and different sensitivity to operator fusion than TPUs. MoE models in particular are sensitive to all-to-all communication bottlenecks that manifest differently on TPU's high-bandwidth interconnects versus GPU's NVLink/NVSwitch topology. Expert Choice gating, which Brainformer uses, may have different throughput characteristics than top-2 gating on GPUs.
- **Inference deployments:** The efficiency claims matter most at inference time, where models are deployed on diverse hardware (edge devices, mobile, cloud). The step time measurements are for training throughput, not inference latency or throughput. Training throughput benefits from large batch sizes and pipeline parallelism that may not apply at inference, where batch size is often 1 and latency constraints are tighter. A 5× training throughput improvement might correspond to a much smaller (or even negative) inference latency improvement if the architectural patterns that enable training parallelism (clustered MoE layers, Expert Choice gating with perfect load balance) don't accelerate single-example inference.
- **Cost projections:** The paper's implicit argument for adopting Brainformer is based on training cost savings (2× faster convergence means half the TPU hours). If a team trains on GPUs, the actual cost savings depend on GPU throughput, which is not measured. Extrapolating TPU speedups to GPU cost savings could be substantially inaccurate.

The paper's brief discussion in Section 8 acknowledges hardware-specific constraints but focuses on edge devices rather than the training hardware that dominates the results. The suggestion to "simulate the step time for the target hardware or use a learnt performance model" is a general recommendation, not a tested solution.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None—all step time measurements are on TPU V4, with no comparison to other hardware. The paper does not even report the theoretical FLOPs of Brainformer vs. GLaM, which would be a hardware-independent lower bound on potential speedup. The architecture search uses step time as a constraint (Equation 5: `Step_Time(N) ≤ baseline_step_time`), which means the search is explicitly optimizing for TPU V4 performance characteristics. Architectures that are slower on TPU V4 but would be faster on GPUs are penalized and discarded, making the discovered blocks potentially TPU-specific.

The absence of a computational complexity analysis (total FLOPs per forward pass, communication volume, memory footprint) means the paper cannot distinguish hardware-specific optimization from genuine computational efficiency. The Expert Choice gating's perfect load balance may eliminate certain TPU-specific bottlenecks (e.g., tail latency from overloaded experts) without providing the same benefit on hardware with different communication patterns.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges the hardware-specificity concern in Section 8 but provides no mitigation—no FLOPs analysis, no GPU measurements, no discussion of which architectural patterns are hardware-dependent vs. universal. The recommendation to simulate or model target hardware performance is untested. A practitioner deciding whether to invest in implementing Brainformer on their GPU cluster receives no guidance on expected throughput improvements. This is a significant gap for a paper whose primary contribution is an efficiency claim.

### The Block-Wise Scaling Assumption Is Empirically Supported but Theoretically Unvalidated

**The assumption or constraint.** The entire Brainformer methodology rests on a critical assumption: that an architecture block discovered as optimal at the 100M scale (by training and evaluating a 3-stack proxy model) remains optimal or near-optimal when scaled to 1B and 8B by widening dimensions and stacking more times (6 and 8 stacks respectively). The paper states this approach explicitly in Section 3.2:

> "We first scale the model dimension and hidden dimension 2x and 4x, following the scaling factors presented in GLaM, to create block S1 and S2 targeting 1B and 8B model scale. Then we stack block S1 and S2 respectively to create 1B64E and 8B64E model variants."

This assumes that (a) the optimal block structure is independent of depth (the block discovered for a 3-stack, 24-sub-layer model transfers to a 6- or 8-stack, 48- or 64-sub-layer model), (b) the optimal block structure is independent of width (scaling model dimension from 1024 to 2048 to 4096 does not change which layer ratios are optimal), and (c) the proxy task (25% of maximum training, no downstream evaluation) correctly ranks blocks for full-training final quality.

**The consequence.** Three distinct failure modes arise from this assumption:

**Depth-dependent optimality:** At 3 stacks (100M model), the block repeats only 3 times, and the model has 24 total sub-layers. At 8 stacks (8B model), there are 64 sub-layers. It is plausible that the optimal block structure differs with depth: early blocks might benefit from more attention to build representations from raw tokens, while deeper blocks might benefit from more feed-forward capacity to refine abstractions. By forcing all blocks to be identical, Brainformer forecloses this depth-dependent specialization. The paper implicitly tests this assumption by showing that the 100M-discovered block transfers well, but it does not test the counterfactual: would a block discovered directly at 8B scale (stacked 8 times in the proxy, trained to a larger budget) outperform the scaled 100M block? Without this comparison, the transfer assumption is empirically consistent (it worked in this one case) but not validated (we don't know if it's optimal or just good enough).

**Width-dependent optimality:** Scaling model dimension from 1024 to 2048 (2×) or 4096 (4×) changes the computational profile of each sub-layer type differently. Attention FLOPs scale as O(d² · L) for sequence length L, while dense FFN FLOPs scale as O(d · d_ffn) and MoE FLOPs scale as O(d · d_moe · c). The optimal ratio of these operations may depend on the absolute width because their costs scale differently with d. The paper's discovered blocks use non-standard expansion ratios (1.5× for dense FFN, 2× for MoE in Block #1) that may become suboptimal at different widths. The paper does not investigate whether the optimal expansion ratios are width-dependent.

**Proxy task misranking:** The proxy task trains each candidate for 25% of the maximum steps (with early stopping killing unpromising candidates even earlier). The reward is based on perplexity at this early checkpoint. If two architectures have similar proxy perplexity but one converges faster late in training, the proxy task may fail to distinguish them, or may even rank them incorrectly. Similarly, the proxy task cannot measure downstream task performance (fine-tuning, fewshot), so the search optimizes for pretraining perplexity, not downstream quality. The fact that Brainformer-1 achieves similar fine-tuning improvements to its pretraining improvements (Table 4) is reassuring but does not guarantee that the proxy ranking preserves the correct ordering for downstream tasks. The QQP regression at 100M (81.2% vs. 90.7% for GLaM) is a case where proxy-pretraining-quality ranking and downstream-task-quality ranking may diverge.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The scaling behavior in Table 3 provides evidence for (but does not prove) the transfer assumption: Brainformer-1 discovered at 100M maintains or improves its advantage over GLaM at 1B and 8B. This is consistent with the block structure being near-optimal at all scales, but does not rule out the possibility that a block discovered directly at 8B would be substantially better. The paper provides no comparison against a block searched directly at the target scale, no analysis of whether different blocks perform differently at different depths within the stacked model (e.g., do the first and last stack behave differently?), and no sensitivity analysis of how much quality is lost by the proxy ranking vs. a full-training ranking.

The block-wise transfer from EfficientNet (Tan & Le, 2019) is cited as inspiration, but EfficientNet scaled CNNs where the computational profile is more uniform across layers (convolution dominates uniformly) compared to Transformers with their heterogeneous mix of attention and FFN operations. The analogy may not hold with the same fidelity.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges the computational constraint that motivated the block-wise approach ("With limited computational resources, we only scale Brainformer-1 to 1B and 8B scales") but does not discuss the assumption's validity or its limits. Section 6.2 provides the only indirect evidence: the claim that "the ratio of different layer types is critical to model quality" while "layer order is relatively insensitive." If true, this suggests the block structure captures a fundamental ratio (e.g., X% attention, Y% dense FFN, Z% MoE) that scales with depth, which would make the block-wise assumption more plausible. But this claim is stated without quantitative evidence—no table or figure supports it—making it a qualitative observation rather than a validated property. The paper does not discuss at what scale the block-wise approximation might break down, whether depth-dependent block specialization would provide additional gains, or how a practitioner with more compute budget could extend the search to multi-block architectures.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper introduces a **methodological reframing of architecture design for large language models**: it demonstrates that the internal structure of Transformer blocks—the sequence, type, width, and gating configuration of sub-layers—is not a fixed design choice to be inherited from prior work but rather a **first-class, jointly optimizable axis in the compute-efficient scaling framework**. This is not a paradigm shift on the order of the original Transformer or the discovery of scaling laws—the underlying operations (attention, dense FFN, MoE) remain the same—but it is a **substantive reframing** of how the field should approach architecture design. Prior to this work, the dominant implicit assumption was that the uniform interleaving of attention and feed-forward sub-layers (with fixed 4× FFN expansion ratios) was either optimal or close enough that optimization effort should focus on scaling model size, training tokens, and data quality. Brainformer challenges this assumption directly by showing that a heterogeneous, searched architecture can simultaneously achieve 5× faster step time, lower perplexity (1.99 vs. 2.12 at 8B scale), and better downstream performance (3% higher SuperGLUE) compared to the hand-designed GLaM baseline—gains that no amount of scaling within the uniform template could recover.

The most consequential conceptual contribution is the **elevation of gating mechanism from implementation detail to architectural variable**. Prior MoE work treated token-based top-2 routing (from GShard and GLaM) as a default, with alternatives like Expert Choice (Zhou et al., 2022) seen as minor variants. The Brainformer results refute this: the search's preference for Expert Choice gating, and the ablation showing that Search-w-Top2 (fixed token-based routing with searched architecture) achieves only a 0.06 perplexity improvement over GLaM at 100M while Brainformer-1 (searched architecture + searched gating, which selects Expert Choice) achieves a 0.16 improvement, demonstrates that **the optimal architecture depends on the gating mechanism, and the combination of both unlocks gains that neither achieves alone**. This implies that future MoE architecture work cannot treat gating as a solved, fixed choice—it must be optimized jointly with the layer layout.

A second reframing is the **inversion of the efficient-attention research agenda**. The dominant approach to efficient Transformers has been to approximate or compress each attention operation (Linformer, Performer, Synthesizer, etc.), preserving the frequency of attention layers while reducing their per-occurrence cost. Brainformer Block #1 contains attention in only 1 of 8 sub-layers (12.5%), replacing most attention with additional feed-forward computation, and yet achieves better quality than the attention-dense GLaM baseline. This suggests that **the field's investment in making attention cheaper per occurrence may have been solving the wrong problem**: reducing attention frequency may be more efficient than reducing attention cost, because feed-forward layers dominate computational cost at typical sequence lengths anyway (as the paper notes from Du et al., 2022). This finding does not invalidate efficient-attention research—which still matters for long-sequence regimes where attention cost grows quadratically—but it redirects attention for the common short-to-medium sequence regime toward structural sparsification rather than algorithmic approximation.

The paper also reconciles a latent tension in prior work. The Sandwich Transformer (Press et al., 2019) showed that reordering sub-layers could sometimes help but did not guarantee gains across tasks. Brainformer provides an explanation: the simple sandwich reordering (grouping attention layers together) was directionally correct but suboptimal because it did not also optimize layer widths, gating mechanisms, and the attention-to-feedforward ratio. The Brainformer search discovers that the optimal architecture involves not just reordering but also reducing attention frequency below 50%, introducing sparsity with Expert Choice gating, clustering MoE layers, and using non-standard FFN expansion ratios—a combination that a single hand-crafted reordering pattern could not discover. This resolves why prior reordering work produced inconsistent results: it was optimizing a single axis (layer order) while holding everything else fixed, missing the compound gains from joint optimization.

Finally, the paper's **fixed-training-time evaluation paradigm**—while not a novel idea in itself—is implemented with sufficient rigor and scale to serve as a **methodological exemplar** for future architecture work. By baking training speed into the quality evaluation through the mechanism of allocating fixed wall-clock time per trial, the search automatically discovers architectures that are jointly optimal for quality and speed, without requiring explicit multi-objective reward tuning. This paradigm exposes how much of the efficiency story is missed by fixed-parameter or fixed-token comparisons: the GLaM baseline at 8B would show only a 0.13 perplexity gap (2.12 vs. 1.99) under fixed-token comparison, but the fixed-time paradigm reveals the additional 5× step time advantage that dominates practical deployment economics. If this evaluation paradigm becomes standard, it will systematically favor efficient, heterogeneous architectures that the current fixed-token protocol disadvantages, potentially accelerating the discovery of architectures that look qualitatively different from the uniform Transformer template.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Disentangling architecture quality from data volume in the Brainformer improvements.** The paper's fixed-training-time evaluation conflates architectural quality with the benefit of seeing more training data (since faster architectures complete more steps in the fixed time budget). A critical follow-up would train Brainformer-1 and GLaM at the 1B and 8B scales on exactly the same number of tokens (not the same wall-clock time), then evaluate both pretraining perplexity and downstream fine-tuning to isolate the pure architectural contribution. The fewshot results in Table 5 (fixed 200B tokens) already hint that the pure architectural advantage over GLaM is smaller than the fixed-time results suggest (Brainformer loses on Natural Questions, 8.23 vs. 9.14), but this comparison is at a single training budget and uses an unspecified Brainformer variant. A systematic study with learning curves that have "tokens seen" on the x-axis for both architectures would reveal whether Brainformer's quality-per-token is actually better than GLaM's, or whether the fixed-time advantage comes primarily from the speed-to-data conversion. This is the single most important ablation that the paper lacks, and it would fundamentally change how the results are interpreted.

**Characterizing the attention frequency vs. quality trade-off surface.** The Brainformer blocks dramatically reduce attention frequency (12.5% of sub-layers in Block #1, 37.5% in Block #2, vs. 50% in GLaM), but the paper provides no controlled experiment varying only the attention ratio while holding other architectural features constant. A follow-up study should construct a family of Brainformer-style blocks with identical total sub-layer count, identical gating (Expert Choice), and identical MoE configuration, but with the attention-to-feedforward ratio systematically varied from 0% (all feed-forward, no attention) to 50% (GLaM-like). For each ratio, measure pretraining perplexity, training throughput, and downstream performance on both standard NLU tasks and tasks specifically requiring cross-sequence comparison (QQP, MNLI, document-level QA). The discovery of a sharp phase transition—a critical minimum attention ratio below which quality collapses on comparison-heavy tasks—would explain the QQP regression at 100M (81.2% Brainformer vs. 90.7% GLaM) and provide actionable guidance for practitioners. If the relationship is smooth and the QQP regression is a statistical fluke, that would be equally informative for architecture design.

**Direct search at target scales versus block-wise transfer.** The paper searches at 100M scale and transfers the discovered block to 1B and 8B by width scaling and deeper stacking. This assumes that the optimal block structure is scale-invariant—an assumption that is empirically consistent (the transferred block maintains advantage) but unvalidated (a block searched directly at 8B might be better). A strong follow-up would allocate the same total search compute budget (512 TPU v4 for one week) but split it across three search tiers: Tier 1 searches at 100M as in the paper; Tier 2 searches at 1B with reduced population size and earlier stopping; Tier 3 does a small-population search directly at 8B. If the 1B-discovered block, when scaled to 8B, outperforms the 100M-discovered block at 8B, that would establish that block-wise transfer leaves quality on the table and that search should target the deployment scale. If the 100M-discovered block remains optimal, that would validate the block-wise approach more strongly than the paper's single-point transfer evidence. The result would determine whether the field should invest in cheap small-scale proxy search (as the paper does) or expensive direct-search at deployment scale, which has major resource-allocation implications for industry architecture teams.

**Cross-hardware validation of the 5× step time improvement.** The paper's step time measurements are exclusively on TPU V4, and the 5× improvement at 8B scale (1.96 vs. 0.39 steps/sec for GLaM) is the most practically significant result. A follow-up should implement Brainformer-1 and GLaM at the 1B scale on NVIDIA A100 and H100 GPUs, measuring training throughput at the batch sizes typically used in production (not micro-batches optimized for TPU). Additionally, a FLOPs-based analysis should decompose the step time into theoretical FLOPs (which are hardware-independent) and hardware utilization efficiency (which is platform-specific). If Brainformer achieves most of its speedup from reduced FLOPs (fewer attention operations, narrower FFN expansion ratios), the advantage should transfer to GPUs with similar magnitude. If the speedup comes from TPU-specific characteristics (Expert Choice gating's perfect load balance eliminating TPU-specific tail latency from all-to-all communication), GPU throughput might show a much smaller improvement or even a regression. This experiment would determine whether Brainformer is an architecture family or a TPU-optimized point solution, which has first-order consequences for the 90%+ of the field that trains on GPUs.

**Investigating the QQP regression mechanism and task-specific architectural weaknesses.** The 9.5-point QQP drop at 100M (81.2% Brainformer-1 vs. 90.7% GLaM) that resolves at 1B is the paper's most glaring unexplained result. A focused follow-up should reproduce the 100M Brainformer-1 and GLaM models, fine-tune them on QQP with multiple random seeds to establish whether the regression is real (statistically significant across seeds) or noise (a single unlucky run). If real, probe the mechanism: (a) Compare the attention maps of Brainformer and GLaM on QQP examples to see whether Brainformer's reduced attention budget prevents it from learning cross-sequence alignments. (b) Train a version of Brainformer Block #1 with an additional attention layer inserted (going from 12.5% to 25% attention ratio) and measure QQP performance—if this recovers the 9.5 points, it would establish a causal link between attention frequency and cross-sequence comparison ability. (c) Evaluate Brainformer and GLaM at 100M on a broader suite of comparison-heavy tasks (document-level coreference, multi-hop QA, textual entailment with long premises) to determine whether QQP is an isolated failure or symptomatic of a broader weakness. This investigation is critical because it would establish the **boundary conditions** of the attention-reduction principle that the paper implicitly advocates.

**Integrating the Brainformer block structure into the Chinchilla scaling framework.** The paper argues that architecture should be a third axis alongside model size and training tokens in compute-optimal scaling, but does not actually fit Brainformer into the Chinchilla-style parametric scaling law framework. A theoretically ambitious follow-up would train Brainformer-1 and GLaM at multiple scales spanning two orders of magnitude in activated parameters (50M to 5B) and multiple training token budgets (10B to 1T), then fit parametric functions of the form `L(N, D, architecture) = E + A/N^α + B/D^β` where `N` is activated parameters, `D` is training tokens, and the coefficients `E`, `A`, `B`, `α`, `β` are architecture-dependent. This would quantify whether Brainformer shifts the entire scaling law frontier (different coefficients across the board), or whether it primarily affects the constant terms (same scaling exponent but lower offset). The result would determine whether Brainformer's advantage compounds at larger scales (if the scaling exponent improves) or remains a constant-factor efficiency gain (if only the offset improves). This is the experiment that would elevate Brainformer from an impressive engineering result to a fundamental finding about neural network scaling.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Cost-efficient training of large language models at industrial scale.** For organizations training models in the 1B–8B activated parameter range on TPU V4 (or comparable) hardware, Brainformer's combination of 5× faster step time and better downstream quality (3% higher SuperGLUE at 1B) translates directly to reduced training cost and faster iteration cycles. At the 8B scale, Brainformer-1 completes 1.5M training steps in approximately 213 hours on 512 TPU V4 chips versus GLaM's 1,068 hours—a savings of roughly 855 TPU-V4-hours, or approximately 80% of the training time. For an organization training multiple model variants (different sizes, different data mixtures, ablation studies), this reduction in training time enables more experiments within a fixed compute budget, accelerating the model development cycle. The architecture search cost (512 TPU V4 for one week, or ~86,000 TPU-V4-hours) is amortized across all subsequent training runs: if the discovered block is reused for 100 training runs (a realistic number for a large team over a year of development), the per-run search overhead is ~860 TPU-V4-hours, which is comparable to the per-run training savings. The key practical requirement is that the organization commit to the Brainformer block structure across many training runs to amortize the search investment.

**Deployment of high-quality language models under strict latency budgets.** The Brainformer architecture's reduced attention frequency (12.5% of sub-layers in Block #1 vs. 50% in standard Transformers) directly reduces inference latency on hardware where attention computation is a bottleneck, particularly for batch-size-1 autoregressive decoding where the quadratic-in-sequence-length attention cost cannot be amortized over a large batch. At the same activated parameter count (1.38B for Brainformer-1 vs. 1.88B for GLaM at 1B64E scale, from Table 3), Brainformer requires 27% fewer FLOPs per token and substantially fewer attention operations, which translates to lower per-token latency at inference. For latency-sensitive applications—real-time conversational AI, code completion in IDEs, on-device assistants—Brainformer Block #1's single-attention-layer-per-block design may be particularly attractive because it minimizes the attention bottleneck while maintaining quality. The caveat is that the paper provides no inference latency measurements (only training step time), so the latency benefit must be inferred from the architectural structure rather than directly measured. Practitioners should benchmark inference latency on their target hardware before committing.

**On-device and edge deployment of sparse models.** The Expert Choice gating mechanism that Brainformer adopts has a property that is particularly valuable for edge deployment: perfect load balance by construction. In token-based top-2 routing (GLaM, Switch Transformer), some experts inevitably receive more tokens than others during inference, causing load imbalance that manifests as latency spikes when overloaded experts become stragglers. On edge hardware with limited parallelism (e.g., mobile GPUs, edge TPUs), these stragglers can dominate end-to-end latency. Expert Choice gating guarantees that each expert processes exactly the same number of tokens per batch (when `c = 1`), eliminating the straggler problem entirely. Combined with Brainformer's reduced attention budget, this makes the architecture attractive for latency-predictable edge deployment of moderately sized sparse models (hundreds of millions of activated parameters). The paper's acknowledgment that "edge devices can impose strict hardware constraints" (Section 8) and the suggestion to simulate target hardware performance are directly relevant here: a practitioner should profile Expert Choice routing on their specific edge accelerator to confirm the load-balance advantage translates to latency predictability.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper explicitly positions Brainformer against GLaM (manually interleaved sparse Transformer) and Primer (NAS-discovered dense Transformer), and the results in Tables 3–5 provide a basis for decision rules grounded in specific numbers. The choice depends on which resource is the primary bottleneck:

- **Prefer Brainformer (searched heterogeneous block) over GLaM (hand-designed interleaved sparse architecture) when:** (1) training time or cost is the dominant constraint, because the 5× step time advantage at 8B scale (1.96 vs. 0.39 steps/sec on 512 TPU V4) and 2× training convergence advantage translate directly to reduced wall-clock time and TPU-hours; (2) you are training at 1B activated parameters or larger, where Brainformer's step time advantage over GLaM becomes substantial (1.63× at 1B, 5.0× at 8B from Table 3), while at 100M the advantage is modest (1.06×); (3) you can amortize the architecture search cost (~86,000 TPU-V4-hours) across multiple training runs, since the search cost dominates the per-run training savings for a single model; (4) you are deploying on hardware where Expert Choice gating's perfect load balance eliminates a known bottleneck (e.g., straggler experts in MoE inference); or (5) you are willing to accept some task-specific risk—the 9.5-point QQP regression at 100M that resolves at 1B suggests possible scale-dependent weaknesses on comparison-heavy tasks that are not fully characterized.

- **Prefer GLaM over Brainformer when:** (1) you are training a single model and cannot amortize the architecture search cost—the ~86,000 TPU-V4-hour search overhead exceeds the per-run training savings for a single 8B model (~855 TPU-V4-hours); (2) your deployment hardware has unknown or unfavorable performance characteristics for Expert Choice gating and clustered MoE layers, and you cannot benchmark before committing; (3) you need strong performance on cross-sequence comparison tasks at small scales (<1B activated parameters), where the QQP regression at 100M raises concerns about Brainformer's reduced attention budget; (4) you are operating at the 100M scale where Brainformer's step time advantage is negligible (1.06×) and the search cost cannot be justified; or (5) you require a well-characterized, widely-deployed architecture with known failure modes, and cannot accept the risk of adopting a newer architecture with less community operational experience.

- **Prefer the Brainformer methodology (evolutionary search under fixed training time) but not necessarily the specific blocks discovered, when:** (1) your training data distribution differs substantially from the GLaM dataset used in this paper (different language, domain, or data mixture), because the discovered blocks are optimized for that specific data distribution and may not transfer; (2) your hardware platform differs from TPU V4 (e.g., NVIDIA GPUs, custom accelerators), requiring the search to optimize for platform-specific step time rather than assuming the TPU-discovered blocks are optimal; or (3) you operate at a substantially different scale (e.g., 100B+ activated parameters) where the block structure may need to be re-optimized. The paper provides a methodology (Algorithm 1, the search space in Table 1, the fixed-training-time constraint) that can be re-applied with different base architectures, different hardware, and different data, but the specific Brainformer Block #1 and Block #2 architectures are point solutions from a single search run and should not be assumed portable without validation.
