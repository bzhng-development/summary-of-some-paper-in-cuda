# What's In My Big Data?

**ArXiv:** [2310.20707](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.20707)

## 🎯 Pitch

Nearly 50% of documents in RedPajama and LAION-2B-en are exact duplicates, while benchmark contamination is rampant—over 64% of Winograd Schema Challenge instances appear in widely used training corpora. WIMBD provides scalable search and count primitives to systematically uncover these hidden failures in the datasets powering today’s language models.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **What’s In My Big Data? (WIMBD)**, a platform providing a standardized set of sixteen analyses built on two scalable primitives—count and search—to systematically audit and compare the contents of large text corpora used for training language models. Applying WIMBD across ten corpora including C4, The Pile, and RedPajama (totaling over 35 terabytes), the work uncovers previously undocumented findings about data quality, community-relevant measurements, and corpus composition through concrete analyses (e.g., n-gram frequency distributions, exact-match duplicate detection, benchmark contamination search, and personally identifiable information regex matching). The platform reveals striking patterns such as approximately 50% duplicate documents in RedPajama and LAION-2B-en, widespread benchmark contamination—including full contamination of COPA in RedPajama and over 64% of Winograd Schema Challenge instances appearing in multiple corpora—and an estimated 200 million email addresses in the most PII-dense corpus (mC4-en), establishing that web-scale pretraining datasets harbor these problematic artifacts only when systematically searched for using scalable, corpus-level tooling.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: We Train Models on Data We Don't Understand

The fundamental problem this paper addresses is stark: **the datasets used to train the most impactful language models are treated as opaque inputs rather than objects of study**. Practitioners download, preprocess, and feed terabytes of text into their training pipelines without ever examining the contents of these corpora in a systematic way. The field has developed sophisticated tools for analyzing model outputs—capabilities, biases, failures—but has invested remarkably little in understanding the inputs that cause those behaviors.

This gap matters because training data is not an interchangeable commodity. The composition of a pretraining corpus directly shapes what a model learns: the facts it knows (Jiang et al., 2020; Elazar et al., 2021a), the reasoning patterns it exhibits (Razeghi et al., 2022a), the biases it reproduces (Seshadri et al., 2023), and the evaluation benchmarks it can fairly be tested on (Jacovi et al., 2023). A model trained on a corpus where 50% of documents are duplicates will have a fundamentally different learning signal than one trained on diverse, deduplicated data (Lee et al., 2022). A model whose training data contains the test questions it will later be evaluated on produces inflated, meaningless benchmark scores. A model trained on documents riddled with personally identifiable information poses real privacy risks when it memorizes and regurgitates that information (Carlini et al., 2021).

Yet, when a new corpus is introduced—C4, The Pile, RedPajama—the accompanying documentation typically describes *how* the data was collected and filtered but provides almost no characterization of *what* is actually in it. What are the most common phrases? Which domains dominate? How many documents are exact duplicates of each other? Are any popular evaluation benchmarks lurking in the text? Do the documents contain phone numbers, email addresses, or IP addresses that could be extracted? These are not niche questions—they are basic hygiene for any dataset used to train systems that millions of people interact with daily. But until WIMBD, there existed no practical toolkit for answering them at the scale of modern web corpora.

### Why This Problem Matters Now

The paper identifies several converging trends that make this gap urgent:

**1. The scale of pretraining data has exploded beyond ad-hoc inspection.** Early NLP datasets could be read, browsed, and manually spot-checked. A researcher building a sentiment classifier on 10,000 movie reviews could plausibly skim through representative examples and form a mental model of the data. This is impossible for C4 (364 million documents, 838 GB compressed), much less for mC4-en (3.9 billion documents, 14.7 terabytes) or RedPajama (930 million documents, 5.6 terabytes). At this scale, even basic questions—"what percentage of documents contain toxic language?"—require distributed computing infrastructure, not just curiosity.

**2. Training data transparency is declining, not improving.** The authors point directly to the competitive dynamics of the field: developers of systems like GPT-4 (OpenAI, 2023) and PaLM-2 (Google, 2023) disclose essentially nothing about their training data—not its sources, not its size, not its contents. This represents a regression from earlier eras when datasets were published alongside papers. Even for openly released corpora, documentation tends to describe the collection pipeline (which Common Crawl snapshots were used, what filters were applied) rather than the resulting contents. The paper cites the gap between what Datasheets for Datasets (Gebru et al., 2021) prescribe and what actually gets documented for large-scale corpora. Questions like "what do the instances represent?" or "is there a label or target associated with each instance?" are answered at best with broad categories, not with empirical distributions.

**3. Model behavior interpretation requires data grounding.** When a language model exhibits a surprising capability or a disturbing bias, the natural question is *why*. Since architecture and training procedure are typically held constant across variations, the answer increasingly lies in the data. But without the ability to query what a model was trained on—at scale, across terabytes—we are left speculating. The paper cites work by Akyurek et al. (2022) on fact tracing and Guu et al. (2023) on training run simulation as examples of researchers building bespoke infrastructure to answer data-grounded questions, each time reinventing the counting and searching capabilities that WIMBD provides as a general platform.

**4. The same corpora are reused across many models, amplifying the impact of undiscovered flaws.** C4 was used to train T5 (Raffel et al., 2020). The Pile was used to train GPT-J (Wang and Komatsuzaki, 2021), GPT-Neo (Black et al., 2022), and Pythia (Biderman et al., 2023). RedPajama was used to train RedPajama-INCITE (Together Computer, 2023) and aims to reproduce the data behind LLaMA (Touvron et al., 2023). A contamination problem in C4 affects not just one paper's evaluation, but every model trained on C4 thereafter. A privacy issue in The Pile cascades across the entire Pythia suite. Understanding these corpora is therefore a public good—the analysis benefits the entire research community that builds on them.

### Where Existing Approaches Fall Short

The paper identifies several categories of prior efforts and explains why none fully addresses the problem:

**Corpus-specific analysis tools.** Prior work has built infrastructure for analyzing individual corpora: the ROOTS search tool for the BLOOM training data (Piktus et al., 2023a), the Data Portraits work on recording foundation model training data (Marone and Van Durme, 2023), the Text Characterization Toolkit (Simig et al., 2022), and Snoopy for exploring term frequency effects (Razeghi et al., 2022b). Each of these supports a single corpus and often does not provide programmatic access—you can browse through a web interface but you cannot write scripts that systematically compare C4 to RedPajama across twenty dimensions.

**Components without integration.** The field has mature building blocks—Elasticsearch for search, map-reduce for counting—but no packaged integration that makes it simple to run a standard battery of analyses on a new corpus. A researcher who wants to check their new dataset for duplicates, PII, benchmark contamination, and domain distribution would need to stitch together half a dozen separate tools, each with its own data formats, scalability characteristics, and failure modes. WIMBD's contribution is partly engineering integration: it wraps ES search in a programmatic API and pairs it with a counting framework that reuses the same data processing pipeline, so all sixteen analyses share the same underlying infrastructure.

**Focus on smaller datasets.** Tools like the Data Measurements Tool (Luccioni et al., 2021) and Know Your Data (Google, 2021) work toward improving data documentation but operate on datasets that are orders of magnitude smaller than web-scale corpora. The technical challenges at the 35-terabyte scale are qualitatively different: you cannot load the data into memory, you cannot iterate over it quickly with Python `for` loops, and you cannot assume that counting unique values will fit in any reasonable hash table. WIMBD's use of compressed counting with hash-based approximations (for n-grams and duplicates where the value space is too large for exact counts) and exact map-reduce counting (for bounded-value analyses like document lengths or domain distributions) is a pragmatic response to these scale challenges.

**Lack of cross-corpus comparison.** Perhaps most critically, prior work examined datasets in isolation. There was essentially no work that applied the same measurements across multiple corpora and allowed direct comparison. This means that when a practitioner chooses between training on C4 versus The Pile versus RedPajama, they are making that decision based on high-level descriptions ("C4 is cleaned Common Crawl," "The Pile is curated diverse sources") rather than on quantitative comparisons of content, quality, and risk. The paper emphasizes that not only do we lack documentation of individual corpora—we lack the tools to *compare* them, which is what practitioners actually need when making data selection decisions.

**Analysis motivated by model studies, not data understanding.** The paper acknowledges that many prior analyses of training data were conducted as side investigations in papers whose primary contribution was about models. For example, Dodge et al. (2021) documented C4's contents as a case study while the broader field's attention was on models trained on C4. Gao et al. (2020) provided analyses of The Pile alongside its release, but these were one-off efforts tied to specific corpus releases. WIMBD reframes the goal: rather than documenting corpora as a service to model developers, the platform itself is the contribution—enabling anyone to perform these analyses on any corpus, at any time, with reusable code.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper positions WIMBD not as a new theoretical contribution but as **infrastructure that makes previously impossible analyses routine**. The core intellectual move is methodological: rather than treat each corpus as a unique snowflake requiring custom analysis code, the paper argues that a small set of fundamental capabilities—counting and search—are sufficient to answer a remarkably broad set of questions about any large text corpus.

This framing connects directly to the *data work* versus *model work* dichotomy identified by Sambasivan et al. (2021), who observed that "everyone wants to do the model work, not the data work." The paper's implicit argument is that part of the reason data work is neglected is that the tools for doing it at scale do not exist. By building and open-sourcing those tools, WIMBD aims to lower the barrier to entry for corpus analysis to the point where it becomes a standard step in corpus creation and model training, not an afterthought.

The paper is also explicit about serving as a foundation for future research rather than a final answer. It describes WIMBD as "extendable" and encourages researchers to "adopt and extend our framework and analyze existing and new corpora." The sixteen included analyses are presented as a starting set—comprehensive enough to demonstrate the platform's capabilities and uncover important findings across ten corpora, but clearly not exhaustive of all questions one might ask about training data. The analyses are organized into thematic categories (data statistics, data quality, community-relevant measurements) that provide a template for what kinds of questions future extensions might address.

A subtle but important positioning choice: the paper does not claim to prescribe what *should* be in a corpus or to evaluate corpus "goodness." It provides measurements and observations—"about 50% of RedPajama's documents are duplicates," "mC4-en contains an estimated 201 million email addresses"—without rendering normative judgments about acceptable thresholds. This is appropriate for an infrastructure contribution; the tool enables the community to have informed debates about data quality standards, but does not itself set those standards.

Finally, the paper positions its empirical findings (the specific insights about the ten analyzed corpora) as evidence that systematic analysis *surfaces non-obvious problems*, not merely as interesting facts in themselves. The fact that benchmark contamination was found in widely-used corpora, that PII is pervasive even in "cleaned" datasets, and that the most common n-grams often correspond to formatting artifacts rather than natural language—these are presented as demonstrations that the questions WIMBD asks are worth asking, and that the answers are often surprising even to experienced practitioners.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

WIMBD is **not a single algorithm or model but an integrated platform**—a suite of software tools and standardized analyses—that makes it possible to ask and answer concrete questions about what is inside a massive text corpus. It solves the problem of corpus opacity: practitioners have terabyte-scale datasets but no practical way to inspect them systematically. The "shape" of the solution is deliberately simple—build two scalable primitives (count and search), and then demonstrate that these two primitives, when properly engineered for scale, can support a remarkably broad set of analyses that collectively characterize a corpus's statistics, quality, and societal risks.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The platform has two foundational components and a set of analyses built on top of them:

1.  **Counting Engine** (map-reduce infrastructure): iterates over the entire corpus to compute aggregate statistics. It comes in two variants:
    - **Exact Counts**: for analyses where the number of possible values is bounded and fits in memory (e.g., document lengths, domain distributions, number of documents).
    - **Compressed Counts**: for analyses where the value space is intractably large (e.g., all unique 10-grams, all unique document hashes for duplicate detection). These use hashing to compress the key space, trading a small amount of accuracy for massive memory savings.
2.  **Search Engine** (Elasticsearch index): provides fast, programmatic text retrieval. Given a query string or a boolean expression, it returns the documents containing that text, their counts, and their locations. This enables contamination analysis (searching for evaluation examples) and targeted inspection.
3.  **Analysis Layer**: a collection of sixteen specific analyses, each implemented by orchestrating calls to the counting engine (e.g., "iterate over all documents, extract the domain from the URL, and count occurrences") or the search engine (e.g., "for each test example in this benchmark, check if all input fields appear together in any document").

Information flows in a simple extraction pattern: the raw corpus is processed by either the counting engine or indexed by the search engine, intermediate results (count tables, hash tables, ES indexes) are stored, and the analysis scripts query those intermediates to produce the final metrics and visualizations reported in the paper.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First, the counting infrastructure**: the exact and compressed count algorithms, how they trade accuracy for memory, and why this tradeoff is necessary at the 35-terabyte scale.
- **Second, the search infrastructure**: how Elasticsearch is used, the wrapper API, and the types of queries it supports for contamination and inspection.
- **Third, the analysis catalog**: how the sixteen analyses are organized into four thematic categories (data statistics, data quality, community- and society-relevant measurements, cross-corpus analysis), and how each maps onto the counting or search primitives.
- **Fourth, the cross-cutting design decisions**: hash-based compression for n-grams and duplicates, regular expression and postprocessing for PII detection, the contamination measurement protocol, and the unified programmatic interface that makes WIMBD extendable to new corpora and new analyses.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **systems and empirical analysis paper** whose core idea is that two simple, well-engineered primitives—counting and search—can be composed to answer a wide range of practically important questions about large text corpora, enabling the first systematic cross-corpus comparisons at this scale.

---

#### 3.4.1 The Counting Engine: Exact and Compressed Map-Reduce

The counting engine is the computational backbone of WIMBD. It is responsible for iterating over every document in a corpus and extracting aggregate statistics. The paper implements counting using the map-reduce framework (Dean and Ghemawat, 2008), a standard distributed computing paradigm where a "map" function processes individual documents and emits key-value pairs, and a "reduce" function aggregates those pairs by key.

**Exact Counts.** The exact counts approach is used when the number of possible keys (distinct values being counted) is manageable and can fit in memory. In a map-reduce implementation, this means the key space is bounded, small enough that the final counts table can be held in RAM on a single machine or distributed across a modest number of reducers. Examples from the paper include:

- Computing the number of documents in a corpus (key: a constant, value: 1 per document; reduce: sum).
- Computing the character or token length distribution (key: document length, value: 1; reduce: sum).
- Computing internet domain distributions (key: domain extracted from URL, value: 1 per document or the document's token count; reduce: sum).

The paper reports that such exact counts were run on a compute node with "224 CPUs and 882GB RAM." The key insight is that these analyses are straightforward map-reduce queries—the map function parses a document, extracts the feature of interest, and emits a key-value pair; the reduce function aggregates counts by key. The scalability comes from distributing the map phase across many CPUs, not from algorithmic cleverness.

**Compressed Counts.** The compressed counts approach addresses the fundamental scalability challenge of large corpora: some analyses require counting over a key space that is far too large to fit in memory. The canonical examples are n-gram frequency analysis and duplicate document detection.

Consider the problem of finding the 10,000 most common 10-grams in C4. The number of distinct 10-gram types in a 153-billion-token corpus could be enormous—potentially hundreds of millions or billions of unique strings. Storing an exact count for each would require an infeasible amount of memory (the paper notes that simply storing all documents of C4 as strings would require "over 800 GB of RAM" for duplicate detection). The compressed counts approach solves this by applying a **compression function**—specifically, a hash function—to each key.

The procedure (detailed in Appendix E of the paper) for most common n-grams works as follows:

1. Initialize a hash table (an array) of a fixed, configurable size, with all entries set to zero. The size is chosen to fit within available memory. Each entry stores a count (as a `u32` or `u64` integer).
2. Initialize an empty collection for the current top-`k` n-grams (e.g., `k = 10,000`).
3. Iterate over each n-gram in the corpus. For each n-gram:
   - Compute its hash (mapping the string to an integer index within the hash table).
   - Increment the count at that index in the hash table.
   - Check whether the incremented count is at least as large as the minimum count currently in the top-`k` collection. If so, add the n-gram to the top-`k`, potentially evicting another n-gram with a lower count.
4. After iterating over the entire corpus, the top-`k` collection is the (approximate) result.

The critical accuracy-memory tradeoff: the hash table size is configurable. The larger the hash table, the lower the probability of hash collisions (two different n-grams mapping to the same table entry), and therefore the higher the accuracy of the counts. However, larger tables consume more memory. The paper notes: "The larger the hash table, the smaller the probability of hash collisions and, therefore, the higher the accuracy of the counts. E.g., unigram estimates are more accurate than 10-gram estimates since the number of possible values is much smaller." This is because unigrams have a much smaller vocabulary (fewer unique strings), so even a fixed-size table suffers fewer collisions for unigrams than for 10-grams, where the number of unique strings is vastly larger.

A critical property of this algorithm: **the final counts reported for the top-k n-grams are always an upper bound of the true counts.** If a hash collision occurs, two different n-grams contribute to the same counter, inflating it. This means the top-`k` might include false positives (n-grams that appear frequent only because they collided with truly frequent n-grams), but it will not miss n-grams that are truly among the most frequent, since collisions only ever increase counts, never decrease them.

For **least common n-grams** (the bottom-`k`), a two-pass algorithm is required (Appendix E.2). During the first pass, counts are accumulated in the hash table exactly as in the top-`k` algorithm. During the second pass, the corpus is iterated again, and for each n-gram, its (now final) count is looked up in the hash table and compared to the current bottom-`k` collection. The bottom-`k` algorithm has the opposite bias: hash collisions cause some n-grams to appear less frequent than they truly are (since their count is shared with a colliding n-gram, but the reported count for the entry is the sum, not the individual count, so they effectively disappear from consideration). Therefore, "the final counts reported for the bottom-k n-grams will always be a lower bound of the true counts."

For **estimating the total number of unique n-grams** (Appendix E.3), a hash table of booleans is initialized to `false`. The corpus is iterated once; for each n-gram, its hash index is set to `true`. After the iteration, the number of `true` entries is counted. This gives "a lower bound of the actual number of unique n-grams," since collisions cause distinct n-grams to map to the same boolean entry, undercounting diversity.

The paper validates the hash-based approach by testing for collisions empirically in the duplicate detection context: "To test for hash collisions, we rerun the analysis with a different random seed. None of the > 7 billion hashes across the ten corpora had a different count." This means that for the MD5 hash used in duplicate detection (which is a cryptographic hash, not a Bloom-filter-style compressed table), the probability of collision was negligible in practice—rerunning with a different seed would have changed a collision's hash and thus changed the duplicate count if a collision had occurred.

#### 3.4.2 The Search Engine: Elasticsearch Index and Programmatic Access

The second primitive of WIMBD is **search**: the ability to retrieve documents containing a specific string or pattern, and to obtain document frequencies (the number of documents containing a term). The paper states that "while search and retrieval have numerous implementations, such as reverse indices, suffix arrays, [and] suffix trees for exact match search," it chose **Elasticsearch (ES)**, an inverted index.

An inverted index is a data structure that maps each term (word, token, or user-specified string) to a list of documents where that term appears, along with the positions within those documents. This allows fast lookups: given a query, the engine can immediately return (1) which documents contain the query, (2) the total count of matching documents, and (3) optionally, the specific locations within those documents. The paper notes that ES supports "more complex Boolean queries" beyond simple term lookup, though the specific Boolean capabilities used are not detailed beyond what is needed for contamination analysis.

The WIMBD contribution on the search side is not the search engine itself (Elasticsearch is a mature, widely-used technology) but rather **the wrapper API and the integration with the analysis framework**. The paper states: "We build a wrapper on top of the ES API, allowing tailored and customized searches to fit our analysis requirements." This wrapper provides programmatic access so that analysis scripts can submit queries, retrieve results, and process them without manual interaction with the ES interface.

The search engine is used for two primary purposes in the paper:

1.  **Benchmark contamination analysis (Section 4.4.1, Appendix B.3.1):** For each test example in a set of 82 benchmark datasets from PromptSource (Bach et al., 2022), the analysis checks whether all input fields from that example appear together in a single document of the corpus. This requires the search engine to support queries that combine multiple strings with a logical AND—all input fields must be present in the same document. The paper reports the "percentage of contaminated examples from the test set" as the contamination metric.

2.  **General exploration and debugging:** Although not explicitly emphasized, the search capability allows researchers to investigate phenomena discovered by the counting engine. For instance, if the counting engine reveals that a particular 10-gram appears millions of times, the search engine can retrieve the documents containing that 10-gram, enabling manual inspection and categorization of the contexts where it appears (as the paper demonstrates with the manual analysis of repeating question marks: "Analyzing 100 random documents, we found that 68% of documents use such n-grams as part of their writing style... 18% are due to noise... and finally, 14% of the documents were due to different format styles or issues").

The paper acknowledges a key limitation of the ES-based approach: it was "not designed for scaling with large text corpora" in the sense that indexing these datasets can take days, and keeping the index running is costly. The paper also notes that ES enables only exact-match search, and that "fuzzy, and semantic search are important abilities that we currently do not support." This is framed as a direction for future work.

#### 3.4.3 Analysis Layer: The Sixteen Analyses and Their Organization

The core intellectual contribution of WIMBD is the **composition of count and search into a standardized battery of analyses**. The paper organizes these sixteen analyses into four thematic categories, plus a fifth cross-cutting category for comparative work. The following walks through what each analysis computes, which primitive it relies on, and what design choices were made.

**Category 1: Data Statistics (Section 4.2, Appendix B.1).** These are aggregate metadata-level descriptions of a corpus.

- **Summary Statistics (Section 4.2.1, Appendix B.1):** Uses Exact Counts. Computes (1) corpus size on disk in GB, (2) number of documents, (3) number of tokens (using Unicode text segmentation as the tokenizer, though the platform supports any HuggingFace tokenizer), (4) the size of the longest document in tokens, and (5) the size of the shortest document in tokens. These are basic descriptive statistics; the design choice of using Unicode segmentation rather than a model-specific tokenizer like BPE ensures that the numbers are comparable across corpora without depending on a particular model's vocabulary.

- **Internet Domain Distribution (Section 4.2.2, Appendix B.1.1):** Uses Exact Counts. For corpora that include URL metadata (C4, mC4-en, OSCAR, RedPajama, LAION-2B-en), parses the URL from each document's metadata, extracts the domain (e.g., `www.nytimes.com`), and counts occurrences. Performed twice: once counting each domain per document (yielding the distribution of documents across domains) and once counting each domain per token (yielding the distribution of tokens across domains, which accounts for the fact that different domains produce documents of different lengths). This dual counting is important because a domain that produces many short boilerplate documents might dominate the document-level distribution but contribute relatively few tokens, while a domain that produces a few very long documents might contribute substantially to the token distribution despite appearing rarely at the document level.

- **Internet Domain Schemes (Appendix B.1.2):** Uses Exact Counts. Parses URLs to extract the scheme (`http` vs. `https`). The paper notes that this serves as a proxy for the age of web content, since HTTPS adoption increased significantly over time, and Google reported 95% HTTPS traffic as of September 2023.

- **Internet Domain Suffixes (Appendix B.1.3):** Uses Exact Counts. Extracts the top-level and second-level suffix (e.g., `.com`, `.org.uk`, `.de`) from URLs. This provides a higher-level picture of the geographic and organizational sources of documents, complementary to the detailed domain breakdown.

- **Utterance Date Statistics (Appendix B.1.4):** Uses Exact Counts applied to metadata timestamps. For corpora with reliable creation timestamps (S2ORC, peS2o), these are used directly. For corpora without them, the paper uses the earliest date the URL was indexed by the Internet Archive as a lower-bound estimate, sampled on 10,000 documents per corpus due to API rate limitations.

- **Geolocation (Appendix B.1.5):** Uses Exact Counts applied to a commercially developed IP geolocation database. For a random sample of 100,000 URLs from each corpus with URL metadata, the IP address of the hosting server is mapped to a country of origin. The paper acknowledges limitations: hosting location is not necessarily the content creator's location, and websites can be hosted in multiple locations.

- **Language Distribution (Appendix B.1.6):** Uses Exact Counts. Applies the CLD2 language classifier to predict the language of each document. The paper notes that the classifier is imperfect, especially for mixed-language documents.

**Category 2: Data Quality (Section 4.3, Appendix B.2).** These analyses probe for anomalies, artifacts, and patterns that indicate problems with a corpus.

- **Most and Least Common n-grams (Section 4.3.1, Appendix B.2.1):** Uses Compressed Counts. Computes the top-10,000 most common n-grams for `n` in `{1, 2, 3, 10}`, as well as samples of the least common (unique) unigrams. The design choice to examine both extremely common and extremely rare n-grams is motivated by the idea that outliers in either direction can reveal interesting properties: the most common n-grams often correspond to formatting artifacts, boilerplate text, or repeated punctuation, while the least common include obscure Unicode characters and code-mixed text.

- **Duplicates (Section 4.3.2, Appendix B.2.2):** Uses Compressed Counts (MD5 hashing). Computes two metrics: (1) the total number of documents that are members of any duplicate cluster, and (2) the number of unique duplicate clusters. An MD5 hash is computed over each document's full text; documents with matching hashes are considered exact duplicates. The choice of MD5, a cryptographic hash, means that the probability of collision is astronomically small—functionally, an MD5 match is an exact text match. The paper validates this by rerunning with a different hash seed and finding zero differences across more than 7 billion hashes.

- **Document Length Distribution (Section 4.3.3, Appendix B.2.3):** Uses Exact Counts. Computes the frequency distribution of document lengths in both characters and tokens. The paper expects a smooth distribution; deviations—spikes at specific lengths—indicate the presence of near-duplicate template-generated documents of identical length, artificially truncated documents, or other data processing artifacts.

**Category 3: Community- and Society-Relevant Measurements (Section 4.4, Appendix B.3).** These analyses probe for content that has ethical or legal implications.

- **Benchmark Contamination (Section 4.4.1, Appendix B.3.1):** Uses Search (Elasticsearch). For 82 datasets from PromptSource that have at least two input fields (the paper argues this increases precision, since co-occurrence of multiple inputs is less likely to arise naturally), each test example is checked for exact-match presence of all input fields within a single document. The design choice to use exact match rather than approximate matching is conservative—it provides an upper bound on exact contamination but may miss near-duplicate contamination (where minor formatting changes separate the evaluation example from the training text). The choice to require all input fields in a single document, rather than allowing them to appear separately, further increases precision at the cost of recall.

- **Personally Identifiable Information (Section 4.4.2, Appendix B.3.2):** Uses Exact Counts with regular expressions. Three regular expressions identify email addresses, phone numbers, and IP addresses (the specific regexes are provided in Table 17). Postprocessing rules are applied to improve precision: for phone numbers, matches are discarded if "ISBN," "DOI," or "#" appear within 50 characters, or if the match is part of a URL. For IP addresses, the same `ISBN`/`DOI`/`#` filter is applied. The paper conducts manual validation on 100 sampled matches per PII type per corpus (when available) to estimate precision, and extrapolates true counts as `count × precision`.

- **Toxic Language (Appendix B.3.3):** Uses two complementary methods: (1) a lexicon-based approach from Zhou et al. (2021) that matches toxic triggers in three categories (offensive minority identity mentions, offensive non-identity mentions, non-offensive minority identity mentions), and (2) an SVM classifier trained on 200K Wikipedia and Twitter examples, applied per-sentence with a document considered toxic if any sentence is.

- **Demographic Sentiment Co-occurrences (Appendix B.3.4):** Uses Exact Counts to identify sentences containing unigrams and bigrams associated with gender, religion, and race, then uses SpacyTextBlob to compute average sentiment for sentences containing each demographic term.

**Category 4: Cross-Corpus Analysis (Appendix B.4).** These analyses compare corpora to each other.

- **Distributional Similarity (Appendix B.4.1):** Using the n-gram frequency data from the data quality analysis, compares corpora by (1) plotting the rank of unigrams in one corpus versus their rank in another, revealing how word usage differs; (2) computing the Jensen-Shannon distance between the top-10,000 unigram distributions.

- **Corpus Overlap (Appendix B.4.2):** Uses the hashed document text and URL data from the duplicate analysis to compute exact overlaps between every pair of corpora, reporting both the count of overlapping documents and the ratio of overlap relative to each corpus's size.

#### 3.4.4 Contamination Measurement Protocol (Detailed Walkthrough)

The benchmark contamination analysis is the most complex single analysis in WIMBD, involving both the search primitive and careful experimental design choices. The protocol works as follows:

**Step 1: Dataset selection and filtering.** From PromptSource (Bach et al., 2022), a repository of prompts for 279 datasets, the paper filters to datasets that (a) can be automatically downloaded from HuggingFace Datasets (Lhoest et al., 2021), (b) have a test split, (c) contain at least two input fields. This leaves 82 datasets. The choice of PromptSource is deliberate because it is "the only large scale benchmark which we could automatically process and discern the different input parts"—it programmatically separates the different fields of each example (e.g., premise and hypothesis for NLI tasks) rather than treating the example as a monolithic text.

**Step 2: Query construction for each test example.** For each example in the test set of each of the 82 datasets, all input fields are extracted (e.g., for an NLI task, the premise and the hypothesis). A search query is constructed that requires all of these fields to appear in a single document. The exact mechanism is an Elasticsearch query with logical AND across the fields.

**Step 3: Execution and scoring.** The query is executed against the Elasticsearch index of the corpus being analyzed. If at least one document is returned, the test example is considered contaminated. The percentage of contaminated test examples is reported as the contamination rate.

**Design decisions and their justifications:**

- **Requiring all input fields in a single document:** The paper argues that this increases the likelihood that the match originates from the actual evaluation dataset rather than from independent natural occurrences. Finding the premise of an NLI example in one document and the hypothesis in another document somewhere else in the corpus would be a much weaker signal of contamination—the two sentences might simply occur in different contexts. Requiring co-occurrence within a single document makes the match far more specific.

- **Ignoring the output/label:** The paper does not check for the presence of the label or target output, noting that "these can appear in different formats (e.g., 'entailment', 'not-entailment', or as '0', '1')." This is a pragmatic choice to avoid the complexity of handling multiple label formats, but it means the contamination measure is an upper bound—some matches might be false positives where the inputs happen to co-occur naturally without being from the benchmark dataset.

- **Choosing tasks with multiple inputs:** The decision to filter to datasets with at least two input fields is explicitly motivated as increasing precision. Single-input tasks like sentiment analysis could have their test examples appear naturally in a corpus without constituting contamination. Multi-input tasks create a much stronger signal because the specific combination of inputs is unlikely to occur by chance.

- **Using exact match, not fuzzy matching:** The paper acknowledges an important limitation: "by employing exact match comparison with the pretraining data, we ignore minor changes in words or phrases that models trained on such similar texts may exploit." The contamination measure thus captures only exact-match contamination, which is a lower bound on the true risk of evaluation data leakage. The paper cites prior work by Emami et al. (2020) and Elazar et al. (2021b) demonstrating that even approximate overlap can inflate evaluation scores.

#### 3.4.5 PII Detection Protocol (Detailed Walkthrough)

The PII analysis uses regular expressions as a fast, scalable method for identifying structured personal information within unstructured text. The protocol works as follows:

**Regular expressions.** Three regexes are used (Table 17 in the paper):

- **Email addresses:** `[.\s@,?!;:)(]*([^\s@]+@[^\s@,?!;:)(]+?)[.\s@,?!;:)(]?[\s\n\r]` — matches a pattern with a username portion, an `@` symbol, and a domain portion, with various boundary characters allowed on either side.

- **Phone numbers:** `\s+(?(\d{3}))?[-\. ]*(\d{3})[-. ]?(\d{4})` — matches a pattern of an optional area code in parentheses followed by three digits, a separator, and four digits.

- **IP addresses:** `(?:(?:25[0-5]|2[0-4][0-9]|[01]?[0-9][0-9]?)\.){3}(?:25[0-5]|2[0-4][0-9]|[01]?[0-9][0-9]?)` — matches a standard dotted-decimal IPv4 address (four octets, each 0–255, separated by periods).

**Postprocessing filters.** To improve precision, additional rules are applied after regex matching (Table 17):

- For email addresses: the username cannot consist only of the character `"("`, and there must be a period (`"."`) in the domain portion. These heuristics filter some common false positives like artifacts from source code or malformed addresses.

- For phone numbers and IP addresses: the context window of 50 characters around the match is checked. If it contains the strings `"ISBN"`, `"DOI"`, or `"#"`, the match is discarded. This is because ISBN numbers (book identifiers), DOIs (digital object identifiers), and other numeric codes often match phone number patterns but are not personal information. Similarly, phone numbers embedded in URLs are filtered out.

**Manual validation for precision estimation.** For each of the three PII types in each of the ten corpora, the paper samples up to 100 detected matches and manually verifies whether each is a genuine instance of PII. The precision is computed as the fraction of sampled matches that are genuine. This precision is then multiplied by the total number of regex matches to estimate the true number of PII instances. For example, if the regex matched 1,000 phone numbers and manual validation of 100 samples found that 92 were genuine, the estimated true count is `1,000 × 0.92 = 920`.

**Document-level filtering for efficiency.** To speed up processing, documents containing unusually high densities of special characters (e.g., documents with more than 50 consecutive "`:)"` emoticons) are filtered out before regex matching. This is an engineering optimization; such documents are unlikely to contain genuine PII and would be expensive to process.

**Limitations acknowledged in the paper:**

- Only three PII types are targeted. Many other forms of PII (names, addresses, social security numbers, credit card numbers) are not captured.
- The retrieved information may not correspond to an individual (e.g., a government organization's shared email address or phone number).
- The paper does not attempt to estimate recall—it does not know how many PII instances were missed by the regexes.
- No estimate of risk level or sensitivity is provided, as these are "highly context-dependent and personalized."

#### 3.4.6 Toxicity Detection Protocol (Detailed Walkthrough)

The paper employs two complementary toxicity detection methods, applied to all ten corpora (Table 22).

**Method 1: ToxTrig lexicon.** Based on Zhou et al. (2021), this is a curated lexicon of terms organized into three categories:

1.  **Possibly offensive minority identity mentions:** terms that refer to a minority identity group in a potentially derogatory way.
2.  **Possibly offensive non-identity mentions:** general profanity or offensive language not specifically targeting an identity group.
3.  **Non-offensive minority identity mentions:** terms that refer to a minority identity group in a neutral or non-derogatory manner.

The analysis matches these terms against the corpus text and reports the number of matches in each category. The paper notes that C4, which explicitly filtered for toxic content during construction, has only 47 matches of the "offensive-not-minority" category (likely due to different filter lists), while C4 still contains 158K occurrences of "offensive-minority" terms that were not caught by the original C4 filtering process.

**Method 2: SVM classifier.** An SVM classifier trained on a dataset of 200K examples from Wikipedia and Twitter is applied to each sentence of each document independently. If any sentence in a document is classified as toxic, the entire document is considered toxic. The percentage of documents classified as toxic by this method is reported separately from the lexicon-based numbers.

**Why two methods?** The lexicon-based approach has high precision (the terms are known to be toxic) but low recall (it misses toxic language expressed without those specific terms). The classifier-based approach has higher recall but potentially lower precision (it may misclassify benign sentences). The two together provide complementary perspectives on the prevalence of toxic content.

#### 3.4.7 The Unifying Design: Map-Reduce for Scale, ES for Inspection

The key architectural insight of WIMBD is not that counting or search are novel capabilities—both are decades-old technologies—but rather the **pairing of the two and the specific set of analyses they enable when composed**. Counting provides aggregate, quantitative metrics that characterize a corpus's statistical properties. Search provides targeted, qualitative access that allows a researcher to inspect specific documents matching a pattern of interest. Together, they span the range from "how much?" to "show me an example."

The paper's engineering choices are consistently pragmatic and scale-oriented:

- **Hash-based compression for n-grams and duplicates** rather than exact counting, because the exact approaches do not scale to terabyte corpora on commodity hardware.
- **Regular expressions for PII** rather than trained models, because regexes are fast, deterministic, and can be applied in a single map-reduce pass without GPU inference.
- **Elasticsearch rather than custom indexing**, because ES is a mature, supported technology with an existing API, reducing implementation effort.
- **Programmatic wrapper on top of ES**, because the goal is to enable scripted, composable analysis pipelines, not one-off manual searches.
- **Extensibility as a first-class goal:** the paper explicitly states that WIMBD is "extendable and can be used to index, count, and analyze other corpora at scale," and that the sixteen analyses are a starting point. The architecture is designed so that new analyses can be added by writing new map-reduce jobs or new ES queries within the existing wrapper framework.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: A Two-Primitive Framework That Demonstrates Scale-Agnostic Corpus Analysis Is Practically Achievable

The paper’s deepest conceptual contribution is not any single analysis result but the **demonstration that two simple primitives—counting and search—are jointly sufficient to characterize the contents of any large text corpus, regardless of scale or domain**, and that the technical barriers preventing systematic corpus auditing are engineering problems, not fundamental research problems.

**What the field assumed before this work.** Prior to WIMBD, the dominant implicit assumption was that understanding web-scale corpora required either (a) building custom infrastructure for each new corpus (as with the ROOTS search tool for BLOOM’s training data; Piktus et al., 2023a, or the Data Portraits system; Marone and Van Durme, 2023), or (b) accepting that certain questions—duplicate rates, PII prevalence, benchmark contamination—were simply too expensive to answer at scale and must be addressed through heuristic filtering during corpus construction rather than empirical measurement after the fact. The field had no shared vocabulary for what “understanding a corpus” means operationally: one paper might report domain distributions, another might measure toxicity, but no framework existed for running a standardized battery of analyses and comparing results across corpora.

**Why this reframing matters.** WIMBD’s architecture makes a methodological claim with philosophical implications: **corpus understanding is decomposable into a small set of atomic operations that compose into a large space of analyses**. The fact that sixteen diverse analyses—from document length distributions to PII detection to cross-corpus overlap—all reduce to either counting or searching means that the problem of corpus opacity is not one of analysis complexity but of engineering scale. This is a genuinely non-obvious claim. One might reasonably have assumed that different analyses would require fundamentally different infrastructure: PII detection needs regex matching, contamination needs fuzzy search, toxicity needs classifier inference, n-gram counting needs specialized streaming data structures. The paper’s framework shows that all of these can be expressed as map-reduce jobs (for counting) or inverted index lookups (for searching), with any remaining complexity handled in postprocessing (e.g., the regex and postprocessing filters for PII are applied during the map phase of a map-reduce pass).

The practical consequence is that **corpus analysis becomes a commodity capability rather than a research contribution in itself**. Before WIMBD, a paper that “analyzes corpus X” was a publication-worthy effort because the infrastructure had to be built from scratch. After WIMBD, analyzing a new corpus is a matter of running the existing pipeline—the research value shifts from *how* the analysis was done to *what* it found. The paper validates this claim by applying the same analyses to ten corpora across wildly different domains (web text, academic papers, source code, image captions) without modifying the core primitives—only the specific map-reduce extractors change between analyses, while the distributed computing framework and the search engine remain constant.

**Concrete evidence.** The runtime benchmarks in Appendix D (Table 24) demonstrate that the full suite of analyses on C4—a 153-billion-token corpus—completes in approximately 47 hours on a single compute node, with 13 of 22 individual analyses taking only minutes. This is not just fast; it is fast *enough to be practical* as a routine step in corpus development, comparable to the time required for preprocessing or tokenization. The paper’s application to ten corpora totaling over 35 terabytes without requiring specialized hardware or per-corpus tuning demonstrates that the framework is genuinely scale-agnostic: the same code that counts domains in C4’s 365 million documents also counts domains in mC4-en’s 3.9 billion, because the underlying map-reduce framework distributes the work automatically.

**Distinguishing from prior work.** Earlier corpus analysis efforts like Dodge et al. (2021)’s documentation of C4 and Gao et al. (2020)’s documentation of The Pile were one-off studies—valuable but not generalizable. Each required writing custom code tied to the specifics of that corpus. WIMBD’s contribution is the **abstraction layer** that makes the analysis independent of the corpus. This is an engineering contribution with research-level impact because it changes what questions the field can ask: instead of “we analyzed this corpus and found X,” the new question becomes “we ran the WIMBD analysis suite on these ten corpora, and here is how they differ across sixteen dimensions.” The paper’s cross-corpus comparisons (Appendix B.4)—finding, for instance, that OSCAR’s unigram distribution is the most similar on average to all other corpora, or that 50% of RedPajama’s unique documents originate from C4—are only possible because the same measurements were applied uniformly across all datasets. Prior to WIMBD, no one could have made such statements because the measurements didn’t exist in comparable form.

**What makes this fundamental rather than incremental.** The paper is not proposing a better counting algorithm or a more efficient search index. It is demonstrating that **the entire space of corpus-level questions that practitioners care about reduces to a small, stable set of operations**, and that implementing those operations at scale is an engineering task with known solutions. This insight transforms corpus analysis from a research frontier into an engineering discipline—which, paradoxically, is a deeply important research contribution because it clears the ground for the actual scientific work of understanding what training data contains and how it affects models.

### Innovation 2: Exposing the Gap Between Corpus Construction Intent and Actual Contents Through Systematic Measurement

The paper’s most practically impactful contribution is the systematic revelation that **the actual contents of widely-used pretraining corpora deviate sharply from what their construction procedures would suggest, and these deviations are only visible through corpus-level empirical measurement rather than pipeline-level reasoning**. This is not a single finding but a diagnostic pattern that recurs across nearly every analysis category.

**The diagnostic pattern.** Each corpus is accompanied by a creation narrative: “C4 was filtered to remove toxic content, duplicates, and non-English text”; “The Pile intentionally oversampled high-quality sources”; “OSCAR applied length filters to improve quality.” These narratives describe *what the creators intended*, and a practitioner who reads the documentation might reasonably assume the corpus reflects those intentions. WIMBD systematically tests these assumptions by measuring what is actually present, and the results repeatedly uncover gaps:

- **C4’s deduplication worked perfectly for exact duplicates** (0 duplicate documents found; Table 13), but the document length distribution (Figure 4) reveals massive numbers of near-duplicate template-generated documents—different phone numbers on a reverse lookup site, different permutations of SEO keywords—that bypass exact deduplication while being functionally identical. The length distribution analysis (Section 4.3.3) is not measuring duplication directly; it is measuring a *symptom* of near-duplication (identical document lengths from templates), which is an insight about how to detect problems that a pipeline’s intended filters miss.

- **The Pile intentionally oversampled certain sources**, so one might expect elevated duplication. But WIMBD quantifies this: 66% of documents belong to a duplicate cluster (Table 13), and the most frequent duplicate documents are not curated text but auto-generated metadata files (Unity asset GUIDs, medical journal publication identifiers; Section 4.3.3). The oversampling policy was a high-level design choice; the actual consequence is that the corpus is filled with deterministic auto-generated strings that carry essentially no linguistic signal. This is not a bug in the construction pipeline—the pipeline did exactly what it was supposed to do—but it is a consequence that only becomes visible when you look at the actual contents rather than the intended composition.

- **LAION-2B-en is conceptually an image caption dataset**, so one might expect short, descriptive text about image contents. The most common 10-grams (Table 3) reveal something else: they include repeated punctuation sequences (`- - - - - - - - - -`, 1.65M occurrences), marketing phrases (boilerplate about prices and credits), and repeated formatting artifacts (`< br / >` sequences). The most frequent text duplicate is the string “Front Cover” (over 1 million occurrences, Table 4), likely from book cover images. This is not “alt text describing image content” in any meaningful sense—it is metadata that happens to be stored in the alt text field.

- **OSCAR applied a 100-character minimum length filter**, so there should be no very short documents. The length distribution in Figure 4 confirms this—but it also reveals a massive spike of near-duplicate boilerplate documents (phpBB forum FAQs, login pages, cookie notices) at lengths above the threshold, meaning the filter successfully removed short garbage but failed to remove long garbage.

**Why this is conceptually significant beyond the specific findings.** The paper is not merely reporting “corpora have problems”—that would be unsurprising. The deeper insight is about **the inherent limitation of pipeline-level reasoning for understanding corpus contents**. A corpus construction pipeline is a sequence of filtering and transformation steps applied to an input stream. Even if each step is correctly implemented (removing exact duplicates, filtering by language, blocking toxic keywords), the *composition* of these steps can produce emergent properties that no single step controls:

- Near-duplicate template text survives because each instance is a unique document (different phone number, different name), so exact deduplication passes over it. Only a distributional analysis (length spikes in Figure 4) reveals the pattern.
- Benchmark contamination occurs because the pipeline never checks for it—it is not a filtering step. The contamination is an *emergent property* of the combination of Common Crawl containing evaluation datasets, web scraping capturing them, and the filtering pipeline not removing them.
- PII survives because regex-based PII filtering is not part of standard corpus construction pipelines, and even when it is (as with C4’s “bad words” list), the specific PII types addressed (email, phone, IP) are not targeted. The PII prevalence in Table 5—hundreds of millions of phone numbers in mC4-en—is a direct consequence of the absence of PII-specific filtering.

The field’s dominant approach to corpus quality has been **procedural**: document the construction steps, argue that each step is reasonable, and trust that the result inherits those properties. WIMBD demonstrates that this reasoning is insufficient—the only way to know what is in a corpus is to measure what is in a corpus. This is a methodological finding as much as an empirical one, and it has implications for how corpora should be documented and released: pipeline descriptions should be accompanied by WIMBD-style measurements, because the measurements reveal what the pipeline description cannot predict.

**Concrete evidence.** The duplicate analysis (Section 4.3.2, Figure 3) is particularly striking because it juxtaposes expected and unexpected findings: deduplicated corpora (C4) correctly have zero exact duplicates, but corpora without deduplication (RedPajama, LAION-2B-en) have roughly 50% duplicate documents—an order of magnitude higher than one might guess from their construction narratives. The PII analysis (Section 4.4.2, Table 5) shows that even C4, which was explicitly designed to be “clean,” contains an estimated 7.6 million email addresses—a fact invisible to the original documentation (Raffel et al., 2020; Dodge et al., 2021) because the cleaning pipeline never checked for email patterns.

### Innovation 3: Establishing Contamination as a Quantitative, Cross-Corpus, Multi-Benchmark Measurement Rather Than a Binary Checklist

Prior work on benchmark contamination treated it as a qualitative concern—a check-box in a model card (“we verified that the training data does not contain the evaluation data”)—and typically investigated a single benchmark on a single corpus. WIMBD transforms contamination analysis into a **systematic, quantitative measurement that can be applied across many benchmarks and many corpora simultaneously, enabling comparisons and prioritization that were previously impossible**.

**The state of contamination analysis before WIMBD.** The field was aware of contamination as a problem (Sainz et al., 2023; Jacovi et al., 2023), but measurement efforts were ad hoc: a paper evaluating a model on a specific benchmark might manually search for that benchmark’s examples in the model’s known training corpus, if that corpus were accessible. This approach had several critical limitations: (a) it depended on manual effort and was not replicable across corpora; (b) it typically tested only the benchmarks used in that specific paper; (c) it often used different search criteria (exact match vs. fuzzy match, single-document vs. multi-document co-occurrence), making results incomparable; (d) it could not reveal *unexpected* contamination—benchmarks the researchers didn’t think to check.

**What WIMBD enables that was previously impossible.** By integrating contamination analysis into the search primitive and applying it programmatically across 82 benchmark datasets on four corpora (C4, OSCAR, The Pile, RedPajama), the paper produces the first cross-corpus contamination map (Figure 5, Table 16). This map reveals several patterns that no single-corpus analysis could have shown:

**1. Contamination is highly uneven across corpora.** RedPajama is the most contaminated corpus: 8 of the 15 most-contaminated benchmarks have contamination rates above 50% in RedPajama, including 100% contamination of COPA (Roemmele et al., 2011). This means that evaluating any model trained on RedPajama on COPA is effectively meaningless—the model has seen every test example. OSCAR, C4, and The Pile each have lower but non-trivial contamination rates on different subsets of benchmarks, making it impossible to choose an evaluation suite that is “clean” for all four corpora simultaneously. This is a practical finding with immediate consequences for anyone training models on these corpora and evaluating on standard benchmarks.

**2. Contamination is concentrated in specific benchmark families.** The most heavily contaminated benchmarks are disproportionately from the Winograd Schema and GLUE/SuperGLUE families: WSC (Winograd Schema Challenge; Levesque et al., 2012) shows 29–58% contamination across all four corpora; RTE (Recognizing Textual Entailment) is 67.5% contaminated in RedPajama; COPA is fully contaminated in RedPajama. These are not obscure benchmarks—they are among the most widely used evaluation datasets in NLP. The concentration suggests that these benchmarks share structural properties (short, self-contained text pairs) that make them more likely to be captured in web crawls and survive filtering, compared to longer-form or more structured benchmarks that rarely appear.

**3. Most benchmarks are not contaminated, providing safe evaluation targets.** Of the 82 datasets tested, 67 show zero contamination across all four corpora (as noted in Section 4.4.1). This is equally important information: it means that the contamination problem, while severe for specific benchmarks, does not render the entire evaluation landscape unusable. A researcher training on RedPajama can select from the 74 uncontaminated benchmarks in the PromptSource collection and have high confidence that their evaluation is fair. This transforms the contamination problem from “everything might be contaminated, we can’t trust any evaluation” to “here are the specific benchmarks to avoid, and here are the ones that are safe.”

**4. The same benchmark can have very different contamination rates across corpora.** RTE is 67.5% contaminated in RedPajama but only 0.13–0.20% in C4, OSCAR, and The Pile. WIC (Word-in-Context; Pilehvar and Camacho-Collados, 2019) is 60–64% contaminated in RedPajama and C4 but only 18.6% in The Pile and 49.4% in OSCAR. This variance demonstrates that contamination is a property of the specific corpus, not the benchmark—a benchmark that is safe for a model trained on The Pile may be completely compromised for a model trained on RedPajama. Cross-corpus comparison is essential for interpreting evaluation results.

**Conceptual advance beyond measurement.** The deeper contribution is the reframing of contamination from a *binary check* (“is this benchmark contaminated in this corpus?”) to a *continuous measurement with a standard protocol* (“what percentage of this benchmark’s test examples appear in this corpus?”). The percentage matters because different thresholds are appropriate for different uses: a 0.1% contamination rate on a 10,000-example test set means 10 contaminated examples, which might be acceptable noise; a 67% contamination rate means the evaluation is fundamentally broken. The quantitative measurement enables practitioners to make nuanced decisions about which benchmarks to use, rather than being forced into an all-or-nothing choice.

The protocol itself—exact-match search for all input fields co-occurring in a single document, applied programmatically across 82 benchmarks—is not computationally novel, but it represents a **standardized methodology** that the field can adopt. The design choices documented in Appendix B.3.1 (requiring multiple input fields, ignoring outputs, using exact match) constitute a concrete proposal for what “checking for contamination” means operationally. Prior to WIMBD, there was no agreed-upon protocol; different papers used different methods with different sensitivity, making contamination claims incomparable. WIMBD provides a reference implementation and a reference set of measurements against which new corpora can be compared.

**Limitation: the protocol is a lower bound on true contamination risk.** The paper is explicit that exact-match search misses near-duplicate contamination where minor formatting changes separate the evaluation example from the training text, and that ignoring labels means some false positives are possible. This is not a weakness of the analysis but a characteristic of the protocol: it trades recall for precision, providing a conservative measurement that can be trusted when it indicates contamination (high precision) but should not be interpreted as proving absence of contamination risk (lower recall). This transparency about the protocol’s limitations is itself a methodological contribution—it gives future work a clear target for improvement (fuzzy matching, label-aware search) while providing a baseline that is immediately usable.

### Innovation 4: Identifying Compressed Counts as the Enabling Technique for Corpus-Scale Distributional Analysis

While the paper does not invent hash-based approximate counting, it makes a **methodological contribution by demonstrating that compressed counting is the key enabler for distributional analyses at the terabyte scale, and by developing specific algorithms for top-k, bottom-k, and unique-count estimation that are tailored to the properties of natural language data**. This contribution is more technical than the conceptual innovations above, but it is what makes the other analyses possible.

**The scaling problem.** The paper identifies a specific computational barrier: many of the most informative analyses—n-gram frequency distributions, duplicate detection, unique vocabulary estimation—require maintaining state over a key space that can be billions of entries. Exact counting is impossible on commodity hardware because the keys themselves do not fit in memory. The paper states this explicitly: storing all documents of C4 as strings “would require over 800 GB of RAM” for duplicate detection alone. For 10-gram counting, the key space is even larger—potentially hundreds of billions of unique 10-gram types.

**The solution: hash-based compression with analysis-specific algorithms.** The paper develops three distinct algorithms on top of the same compressed counting primitive (Appendix E):

- **Top-k (most common n-grams):** a single-pass algorithm that maintains both a hash table of counts and a running top-k collection. Crucially, the final counts are upper bounds of true counts (hash collisions only inflate counts), so the algorithm can never miss a truly frequent item—it can only include false positives, not false negatives.
- **Bottom-k (least common n-grams):** a two-pass algorithm that first accumulates counts and then identifies candidates in a second pass. Here the bias is inverted: collision-inflated counts mean the rarest n-grams may be excluded, so the reported counts are lower bounds.
- **Unique count estimation:** a single-pass algorithm with a boolean hash table that provides a lower bound on the number of distinct n-grams.

**Why this is conceptually significant beyond standard Bloom filter usage.** The key insight is not that hashing compresses keys—that is standard in approximate data structures like Bloom filters (Bloom, 1970) and Count-Min Sketch. It is the **matching of specific algorithmic properties to specific analysis questions**. For most-common-n-gram detection, the algorithm’s upward bias (collisions only inflate counts) is a feature, not a bug: you want to be sure you haven’t missed any truly frequent n-grams, and false positives can be manually verified. For least-common-n-gram detection, the two-pass design is necessary because you cannot identify rare items until you have seen the distribution, but the downward bias means you are conservative—you only report items that are genuinely rare even after collision confounding.

This tailored approach stands in contrast to using a single approximate data structure for all counting tasks. The paper could have used a Count-Min Sketch for all n-gram analyses, but the specific biases of Count-Min Sketch (point query estimates are always overestimates) would have made bottom-k analysis impossible—rare n-grams would be systematically inflated and never identified. The paper’s decision to implement three separate algorithms, each optimized for its specific analytical purpose, reflects a **design philosophy of matching the bias properties of the compression scheme to the tolerance for error in the research question**.

**Empirical validation.** The paper validates the hash-based approach by rerunning the duplicate detection with a different random seed and finding zero differences across more than 7 billion document hashes (Section 4.3.2). For the n-gram analyses, the paper does not provide a formal accuracy bound (the tradeoff is described qualitatively as “the larger the hash table, the higher the accuracy”), but the fact that the top-k results in Table 3 contain recognizable, interpretable patterns (repeated punctuation, boilerplate text) suggests that the approximation is functioning correctly—if hash collisions were dominating, the top-10-grams would include random-looking strings rather than coherent patterns.

This enabling-technique contribution is less visible in the paper’s narrative but is essential to its feasibility. Without compressed counts, the n-gram analyses and the duplicate detection—which together constitute six of the sixteen analyses and produce some of the paper’s most striking findings—would be infeasible at this scale. By open-sourcing the implementation, the paper makes this capability available to any researcher analyzing a new corpus, lowering the barrier to entry for distributional analysis as dramatically as the search engine lowers the barrier to entry for targeted inspection.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Datasets.** The primary object of study is not a model but the corpora themselves. The paper analyzes **ten distinct English-language corpora** used to train popular language models (or, in the case of LAION-2B-en and Stable Diffusion, a text-to-image model). These span web-scraped text (C4, mC4-en, OSCAR, RedPajama, OpenWebText), curated mixtures (The Pile), academic papers (S2ORC, peS2o), image captions (LAION-2B-en), and source code (The Stack). Their sizes range from 41.2 GB (OpenWebText, 8M documents) to 14,694 GB (mC4-en, 3.9B documents), collectively occupying over 35 terabytes. Table 2 provides per-corpus summary statistics. The paper analyzes each corpus in its entirety—not a sample—for most analyses, with exceptions noted below (e.g., geolocation uses a 100K-URL random sample, utterance date statistics use a 10K-document sample). All corpora are publicly available, enabling reproducibility.

- **Base model(s).** Not applicable—WIMBD analyzes data, not model outputs. The analyses are model-agnostic.

- **Metrics.** There is no single performance metric because WIMBD is a measurement platform, not a model evaluation framework. The key reported quantities vary by analysis:
  - **Summary statistics** (Table 2): size in GB, number of documents, number of tokens (using Unicode text segmentation), and min/max document lengths in tokens.
  - **Domain distribution** (Figures 2, 6, and Table 7): percentage of documents or tokens originating from each internet domain.
  - **n-gram frequencies** (Tables 3, 9): estimated count of each n-gram in the corpus, computed via compressed counting (see Section 3.4.1).
  - **Duplicate rate** (Figure 3, Tables 11–14): percentage of documents that belong to a duplicate cluster, and number of unique duplicate clusters. Duplicates are identified by matching MD5 hashes of the full document text.
  - **Document length distribution** (Figures 4, 16; Table 15): frequency distribution over character and token lengths, with particular attention to outlier-length spikes.
  - **Benchmark contamination** (Figure 5, Table 16): percentage of test examples from a benchmark whose *input fields* all appear within a single document of the corpus. The search is exact-match, requires co-occurrence of all inputs in one document, and ignores the output/label.
  - **PII prevalence** (Tables 5, 18–20): estimated true count of email addresses, phone numbers, and IP addresses, computed as `regex_match_count × precision`, where precision is manually estimated from 100 random samples per PII type per corpus (when >100 matches exist).
  - **Toxic language prevalence** (Table 22): percentage of documents containing at least one toxic mention (by lexicon matching or by SVM classifier per-sentence).
  - **Corpus overlap** (Figures 30–31, Table 23): count and ratio of documents (or URLs) whose MD5 hash appears in both corpora.

- **Baselines.** There are no baselines in the model-evaluation sense, since WIMBD's measurements are descriptive. However, several analyses compare against *expected behavior*:
  - **Deduplicated corpora (C4, peS2o)** serve as implicit baselines for the duplicate analysis, since their near-zero duplicate rates confirm the deduplication pipeline worked as intended, and other corpora can be compared against this "clean" state.
  - **C4's explicit toxic content filtering** serves as a partial baseline for the toxicity analysis: C4 filtered documents containing "bad words," so its lower toxicity rates (Table 22) demonstrate the effect of that filter relative to unfiltered corpora.
  - **OSCAR's 100-character minimum length filter** serves as a baseline expectation for the length distribution—the absence of documents below 100 characters confirms the filter, while spikes above that threshold reveal it did not remove all garbage.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The resource being tracked is **computational time and hardware cost**, not model inference FLOPs. All analyses were run on a single Google Cloud compute node with "224 CPUs and 882GB RAM," supplemented by an Elasticsearch cluster for indexed corpora. Appendix D (Table 24) provides per-analysis runtimes for C4 as a representative corpus: individual analyses range from approximately 1 minute (summary statistics, scheme/suffix counting) to roughly 11.7 hours (demographic sentiment co-occurrences). The full suite of analyses on C4 completed in approximately 47 hours, with an estimated total cost of $443.10 at Google Cloud's listed pricing of $9.46/hour for the machine used. The paper notes that 13 of 22 individual analyses completed in "only several minutes." This runtime accounting is important because it demonstrates practical feasibility: analyzing a new corpus is a matter of hours, not weeks, and costs a few hundred dollars in cloud compute, making corpus auditing a realistic step in the data curation pipeline rather than a prohibitive luxury.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not employ train/test splits or cross-validation in the model-sense, since it is measuring corpus properties, not training predictors. Instead, several analyses incorporate statistical quality controls:
  - **MD5 hash collision check** (Section 4.3.2): the duplicate analysis was rerun with a different random seed to test for hash collisions. The paper reports "None of the > 7 billion hashes across the ten corpora had a different count," validating the MD5 hashing approach. The paper reasons that a collision would cause two different documents to share a hash, and rerunning with a different seed would change the hash of at least one colliding document, thereby changing the duplicate count—the absence of any change implies either zero collisions or a vanishingly improbable number of perfectly matched collisions.
  - **Manual validation for PII precision** (Appendix B.3.2, Table 18): for each PII type in each corpus, the paper manually inspects 100 randomly sampled regex matches to estimate precision (true positives / total matches). When fewer than 100 matches exist (e.g., S2ORC had only 1 detected phone number), all available matches are inspected. The extrapolated count is `total_matches × precision`. This is a form of measurement validation—it calibrates the automatic measurement against a ground-truth manual assessment.
  - **Manual analysis of n-gram context** (Section 4.3.1): for the most common 10-gram in C4 (ten consecutive question marks, 9 million occurrences), 100 random documents containing the n-gram were manually categorized into writing style (68%), noise (18%), and formatting (14%). This provides qualitative validation that the frequent n-grams are interpretable and correspond to real corpus properties, not measurement artifacts.
  - **Sampling for expensive external lookups**: the geolocation analysis (Appendix B.1.5) uses a 100,000-URL random sample rather than the full corpus due to the cost of the commercial IP geolocation database. The utterance date analysis via Internet Archive lookups (Appendix B.1.4) uses a 10,000-document random sample due to API rate limits.

### Main Quantitative Results

The paper's results are organized not by method but by analysis category. The following walks through the key quantitative findings from each category, with explicit references to the tables and figures where the evidence appears.

#### #### Data Statistics: What Are These Corpora Made Of?

**Corpus scale varies enormously.** Table 2 reports that the ten corpora span three orders of magnitude in both document count and token count: OpenWebText (7.8B tokens, 8M documents) sits at the low end, while mC4-en (2.7T tokens, 3.9B documents) is 345× larger in tokens and 490× larger in documents. C4, the most well-known and widely analyzed corpus, occupies a middle ground at 154B tokens and 365M documents. The non-web corpora show distinct profiles: S2ORC and peS2o (academic papers) have the highest median document lengths—roughly 4,500 tokens and 23,000 characters (Table 15)—consistent with full-text scientific articles, while LAION-2B-en (image alt text) has a median of only 10 tokens or 54 characters per document, consistent with short descriptive captions.

**Four corpora contain empty documents.** The paper reports (Section 4.2.1) that LAION-2B-en contains 81 documents consisting only of whitespace characters, while The Stack, RedPajama, and The Pile contain 1,350, 3,877, and 7,533 documents respectively that are "empty" in the sense of containing only space-like characters (`\n`, `\t`). In RedPajama, all such empty documents originate from the arXiv subset. This is a small fraction of total documents (e.g., 7,533 out of 211 million in The Pile is ~0.004%) but indicates processing artifacts.

**The longest documents in The Pile and RedPajama are identical.** Both corpora share the same longest document: an encyclopedia volume titled "International Encyclopedia of the Social & Behavioral Sciences" from the Books3 subset, at 28,121,329 tokens (Table 2). This is a concrete example of the overlap between these corpora documented later in the cross-corpus analysis.

**Domain distributions are highly skewed and corpus-specific.** Figure 6 (Appendix B.1.1) and Figure 2 (main text) present the 10 most common domains per document and per token for the five corpora with URL metadata. Key findings:
- **C4 is the most diverse:** the most common domain, `patents.google.com`, accounts for only ~0.04% of documents. Even the top 10 combined capture well under 1% of the corpus. This is what one would hope for from a diverse web crawl.
- **mC4-en is dominated by `google.com`:** over 5% of documents (per-token) originate from this single domain, with `patents.google.com` and `www.patentsencyclopedia.com` combining for an additional ~1.5%. The paper notes that "C4 contains documents from a diverse set of domains," while mC4-en's concentration on patent and Google domains reflects its different filtering and language identification pipeline (higher confidence threshold, different "bad words" lists).
- **RedPajama is dominated by knowledge repositories:** `arxiv.org` accounts for over 12% of documents per token, and Wikipedia subdomains (`en.wikipedia.org`, `de.wikipedia.org`, etc.) collectively represent a substantial fraction. This reflects RedPajama's inclusion of curated academic and reference sources.
- **LAION-2B-en is dominated by e-commerce platforms:** `cdn.shopify.com` alone accounts for ~6% of documents, with `i.pinimg.com` (Pinterest), `i.ebayimg.com` (eBay), and `images-na.ssl-images-amazon.com` (Amazon) among the top domains. The most common domains are image-hosting CDNs for commercial platforms, which is expected for an image caption dataset but means the "text" being described is heavily commercial product imagery.

**HTTP/HTTPS ratios reveal corpus aging.** Figure 7 (Appendix B.1.2) reports the scheme distribution. As of September 2023, Google reported roughly 95% HTTPS traffic. OSCAR, with 87.6% HTTPS, is closest to this contemporary ratio. C4, at only 62.5% HTTPS, contains substantially more older HTTP content—consistent with an earlier Common Crawl snapshot or less aggressive filtering. The other corpora fall between 66.7% (mC4-en) and 80.1% (LAION-2B-en).

**Geolocation skews heavily toward the United States.** Figure 10 (Appendix B.1.5) shows that for all five corpora with URL metadata, documents hosted in the US account for roughly 60–80% of geolocatable URLs. The UK, Canada, Germany, and Australia round out the top five, with the remainder distributed across other countries. The paper acknowledges this is a "rough geographic origin" since hosting location is not creator location, but the US dominance is consistent with the English-language focus of the analyzed corpora.

**Language distribution confirms the English focus but reveals non-trivial multilingual content.** Table 8 reports the percentage of documents classified as English by the CLD2 language detector: all corpora are >95% English, with C4, mC4-en, peS2o, and OpenWebText all above 99.5%. Figure 11 (Appendix B.1.6) breaks down the remaining non-English content: German, French, Spanish, and unknown-language documents constitute the largest fractions, at 0.5–3% depending on the corpus. LAION-2B-en and The Stack have the highest non-English fractions (4.1% and ~3%, respectively), consistent with LAION's multilingual captions and code comments appearing in many languages.

**Temporal distribution varies from ancient to recent.** Figure 9 (Appendix B.1.4) shows the year-of-origin distribution for a 10,000-document sample from each corpus. S2ORC and peS2o contain papers dating back to the pre-1990 era and earlier, reflecting the academic record. RedPajama and OSCAR are dominated by 2018–2023 content, while other corpora have a broader temporal spread.

#### #### Data Quality: What Anomalies Lurk in These Corpora?

**Most common n-grams are dominated by formatting artifacts, not natural language.** Table 3 reports the 10 most common 10-grams for all ten corpora. The pattern is striking and consistent:

- **Punctuation repetition dominates the top ranks.** Ten consecutive dashes (`- - - - - - - - - -`) appear as the #1 10-gram in The Pile (3.64B occurrences), The Stack (4.29B), and OpenWebText (3.4M), and in the top 5 of most other corpora. Ten periods (`. . . . . . . . . .`) appear as the #1 10-gram in mC4-en (1.76B), and in the top ranks of C4, OSCAR, RedPajama, peS2o, and LAION-2B-en. Ten equals signs appear prominently in The Pile (#2, 602M) and The Stack (#5, 2.62B). Ten asterisks appear in The Pile (#4, 188M), The Stack (#2, 3.87B), and RedPajama (#4, 195M).

- **HTML/CSS and markdown artifacts persist despite cleaning.** `) { ref - type = " fig " }` appears as the #2 10-gram in The Pile (59.1M occurrences)—a LaTeX/HTML reference artifact that was not filtered during corpus construction. `<br / >` and its variants dominate LAION-2B-en's top 10-grams (ranks 4–7), indicating uncleaned HTML line breaks in image alt text. The paper characterizes this as "uncleaned html or markdown format" persisting in corpora that are nominally "clean."

- **Boilerplate text appears at massive scale.** In C4, the 10-gram ". You can follow any responses to this entry through" appears 784K times—a fragment of WordPress blog comment subscription boilerplate. In OSCAR, "( Log Out / Change ) You are commenting using your" appears 13.6M times—WordPress login boilerplate that survived language and length filtering. In LAION-2B-en, `| Price : 1 Credit ( USD $ 1 )` appears 437K times—a product listing template from a stock photo or e-commerce site.

- **The Stack and The Pile share code/formatting patterns.** Ten semicolons followed by a closing brace (`; ; ; ; ; ; ; ; ; ; }`) appear as the #1 10-gram in The Stack and also prominently in The Pile (which includes a GitHub subset). The Stack's top 10-grams include `package.json`-related strings (`" resolved " : " https : / / registry.npmjs.org`, 1.42B occurrences), reflecting the dominance of JavaScript/Node.js ecosystem metadata in the crawled repositories.

**Manual annotation of the most common C4 10-gram** (ten consecutive question marks, 9M occurrences): 68% of 100 randomly sampled documents use the sequence as part of their writing style (e.g., emphatic questioning in informal text), 18% are "noise" (unintelligible or contextless text), and 14% are formatting-related (e.g., question marks used as separators between document sections). This breakdown shows that even the most frequent n-gram is not monolithic—it arises from at least three distinct linguistic and structural sources, with the majority being legitimate (if informal) written expression.

**Unique unigrams reveal extreme linguistic diversity.** Figures 12–13 (Appendix B.2.1) display 25 randomly sampled unigrams that each appear exactly once in their respective corpora. The samples include non-Latin scripts (Urdu, Arabic, Korean, Devanagari, Hebrew, Armenian, Bengali, Persian, Japanese, Latvian, Sindhi, and Russian in the OpenWebText sample alone), obscure Unicode symbols ("negative squared Latin capital letter" glyphs), and specialized notation. Table 10 estimates the total number of unique unigrams: OpenWebText contains 88.5M unique unigrams (1.1% of total unigrams), while larger corpora like mC4-en and The Stack contain over 4.2B unique unigrams (0.2–0.3% of total). LAION-2B-en has the highest density of unique unigrams (1.9% of total), likely because image alt text includes many product codes, filenames, and identifier-like strings that are nearly unique.

**Duplicate prevalence varies dramatically and reflects corpus construction choices.** Figure 3 and Table 13 present the headline finding: roughly 50% of documents in RedPajama (49%) and LAION-2B-en (54%) are exact duplicates, with The Pile at 66%. At the other extreme, C4, OpenWebText, and peS2o show 0% exact duplicates—confirming their deduplication pipelines worked correctly for exact matches. mC4-en shows 48,255 duplicates (0.001%) and The Stack shows 517,396 duplicates (0.09%)—low but non-zero. The paper distinguishes between two metrics: the *percentage of documents* belonging to duplicate clusters (the "total" bar in Figure 3) and the *percentage of unique document clusters* that have duplicates (the "unique" bar). For LAION-2B-en, 54% of documents are duplicates, but the duplicate clusters represent only 24% of all unique document strings—meaning a relatively small number of unique texts are repeated many times, rather than many documents being duplicated a few times. This interpretation is consistent with the finding that the most frequent duplicate texts (Table 4) occur in very large clusters: "Front Cover" (1M occurrences in LAION-2B-en), login boilerplate (1.8M occurrences in OSCAR), and templated citation information (214K occurrences of a specific GBIF metadata block in RedPajama).

**The most frequent text duplicates reveal specific artifacts.** Table 4 and Tables 11–12 provide examples:

- **OSCAR's top duplicate** (1,790,064 occurrences) is a login/registration page boilerplate: "In order to login you must be registered. Registering takes only a few moments but gives you increas...". The next four are also website boilerplate (JavaScript notices, privacy/cookie banners). The paper notes that "many of these duplicate documents indicate that the user agent used to collect the dataset received automatic responses blocking it from crawling the website's contents"—a subtle finding that the duplication is not just redundant content but *actively harmful* content representing failed crawls.
- **LAION-2B-en's top duplicates** include "Front Cover" (1,003,863 occurrences), "Wall View 002" (681,753), and domain-specific product phrases. The most duplicated URL in LAION-2B-en is the string "UNLIKELY" (33,142 occurrences), which the paper identifies as "likely resulting from a parsing error."
- **The Pile's top duplicates** are auto-generated metadata: a JSON info block (3,775 occurrences), a C-Track e-filing template (2,941), and iOS info.plist XML templates (2,913). These are not linguistic content at all—they are deterministic file headers produced by developer tools.
- **RedPajama's top duplicate** (213,922 occurrences) is a templated GBIF (Global Biodiversity Information Facility) species record citation block, constructed from a pattern like "ACCEPTED\n\n#### According to\nInternational Plant Names Index\n\n#### Published in\nnull\n\n#### Original n[...]". The fact that this appears over 200,000 times with minor variations (different databases, different publication statuses) demonstrates template-generated text at scale.
- **mC4-en's top duplicates** are JavaScript code snippets (154 occurrences of a Google Maps info-window configuration block) and product descriptions—much lower absolute counts than the other corpora, reflecting mC4-en's larger size and broader coverage.

**URL duplicates are an order of magnitude rarer than text duplicates.** Table 14 reports that OSCAR has 5.96M URL duplicates (1.4% of documents) and LAION-2B-en has 159M URL duplicates (6.8% of documents), compared to 38% and 54% text duplicate rates respectively. This means most text duplication arises from different URLs hosting identical content, not from the same URL being crawled repeatedly.

**Document length distributions expose template artifacts.** Figure 4 and Figure 16 (Appendix B.2.3) plot the frequency distribution of document lengths for all corpora (in both characters and tokens). The key pattern is **outlier spikes at specific lengths**:

- **C4, despite zero exact duplicates, has clusters of near-duplicate template documents revealed by identical lengths.** The paper identifies: template-generated documents from a reverse phone lookup site (each associated with a unique phone number, so each is a distinct string, but all are structurally identical and thus the same length); template-generated JavaScript snippets; and "numerous permutations of the same keywords, likely crafted for SEO purposes." These are not exact duplicates (they differ in content) but are functionally redundant.
- **The Pile has a massive spike at 8,194 characters**, accounting for "nearly 1% of its documents." These originate from the DeepMind Mathematics dataset (Saxton et al., 2019), which was "truncated to fit this length." The Pile also contains auto-generated metadata files of specific lengths: Unity game engine asset files of 20 tokens, medical journal publication identifiers of 9, 18, and 36 tokens. These are non-linguistic, deterministic strings that contribute to the token count without providing meaningful training signal.
- **RedPajama has template-generated user-facing copy** including placeholder pages for alumni of secondary schools (each associated with a unique individual name) and documents "comprising nearly 0.01% of the dataset, containing what appear to be usernames or titles associated with pornographic content"—a content warning the paper surfaces without quantification.
- **The Stack contains auto-generated metadata files** for Unity assets (20 tokens) and documents of exactly 20,000 characters containing "float and bit matrices"—likely serialized numerical data that contributes to the token count but is not source code.
- **OSCAR shows spikes from near-duplicate website boilerplate**, including template-generated FAQs for the phpBB forum software. The 100-character minimum length filter removed shorter documents but did not prevent longer boilerplate from accumulating.
- **OpenWebText's only outlier** is at exactly 100,000 characters: "all documents over this length were chunked into multiple documents of length 100,000 by the dataset builders."
- **LAION-2B-en has the smallest median document lengths** (10 tokens, 54 characters), consistent with image alt text. The paper identifies template-generated alt texts paired with maps describing rental boat locations as a source of near-duplicate content.

**Toxic language prevalence depends heavily on detection method and corpus filtering.** Table 22 reports two complementary toxicity metrics:

- **By lexicon matching (ToxTrig)**: OpenWebText is the most toxic at 13.8% of documents containing a toxic trigger. C4, which explicitly filtered for toxic keywords, shows only 0.01% of documents with taxonomy-detected toxicity—the lowest by this metric. However, peS2o (17.0%) and S2ORC (16.55%) show the highest taxonomy-based toxicity rates—"perhaps surprisingly, as it is not a web-based corpus," the paper notes. The non-web corpora (academic papers, code) may contain descriptions of toxic language or offensive terms in academic contexts rather than actual toxic speech, which the lexicon cannot distinguish.
- **By SVM classifier**: OpenWebText again scores highest (16.47% of documents), while LAION-2B-en (1.09%) and The Stack (1.16%) score lowest. C4 shows 5.75% classifier-detected toxicity despite its near-zero taxonomy score, indicating that the classifier detects subtler toxicity not captured by the keyword filter used in C4's construction.
- **Fine-grained taxonomy statistics**: The paper breaks the ToxTrig matches into three categories and normalizes by token count. The "harmless-minority" category (non-offensive mentions of minority identity terms) is the most common across all corpora, at 10⁻³ to 10⁻² per token. The "offensive-minority" category is significantly rarer: C4 shows only 158K matches across 154B tokens (1.03 × 10⁻⁶ per token), while mC4-en shows 31.4M matches (1.16 × 10⁻⁵ per token). The C4 filter removed most "offensive-not-minority" terms (only 47 matches found) but left 158K "offensive-minority" terms, demonstrating a gap in the filter's coverage.

**Demographic sentiment shows weak positivity except for terms co-occurring with "black."** Figure 17 (Appendix B.3.4) reports the average sentiment (via SpacyTextBlob) of sentences containing demographic terms across nine corpora (The Stack excluded because the terms "were not typically natural language"). Sentences containing "black" are "uniformly more negative across all corpora," while sentences containing most other terms (male, female, Asian, Hispanic, white, various religious terms) show neutral to weakly positive average sentiment. The paper notes this is "in opposition to a related analysis performed in Gao et al. (2020), which measured weak negativity for most terms," attributing the difference to methodology (sentence-level vs. co-occurrence-level sentiment computation).

#### #### Community- and Society-Relevant Measurements: What Are the Risks?

**Benchmark contamination is widespread but concentrated in specific benchmarks and corpora.** Figure 5 and Table 16 present the headline contamination results for 82 PromptSource datasets across C4, OSCAR, The Pile, and RedPajama:

- **RedPajama is the most contaminated corpus**: 8 of 15 benchmarks with ≥5% contamination have contamination rates above 50% in RedPajama. COPA (super-glue-copa) is **100% contaminated**—every test example appears in at least one RedPajama document. RTE (glue-rte / super-glue-rte) is **67.5% contaminated**. WIC (super-glue-wic) is **60.2% contaminated**. WSC (winograd-wsc-wsc273) is **58.2% contaminated**. SemEval-2014 (sem-eval-2014) and SICK (sick) are 52.8% and 52.6% contaminated, respectively. LIAR (liar) is 45.0% contaminated.
- **The Pile has lower but significant contamination on several benchmarks**: AESLC (aeslc) shows 45.5% contamination—the highest for The Pile, and substantially higher than for other corpora (C4: 1.6%, OSCAR: 0.3%, RedPajama: 0.1%). WSC (winograd-wsc-wsc273) is 32.2% contaminated. WIC (super-glue-wic) is 18.6%. SICK (sick) is 4.8%, and SemEval (sem_eval) is 4.9%.
- **C4 shows a surprising peak on WIC**: 64.4% contamination on the Word-in-Context task—the highest single contamination rate for C4. WSC is 29.3% contaminated. LIAR is 29.2% contaminated.
- **OSCAR shows its highest contamination on WIC (49.4%)** and WSC (30.4%), with most other benchmarks below 15%.
- **Most benchmarks (67 of 82) show zero contamination** across all four corpora. This is striking: the contamination problem is severe but concentrated. Benchmarks like Winogrande (winogrande-xl), SNLI (snli), MultiRC (super-glue-multirc), and all SQuAD-shifts variants show 0% across all corpora. The paper notes that "Winogrande, a large corpus in the style of the Winograd schema, does not appear in any of the examined corpora"—despite its structural similarity to the heavily contaminated WSC, suggesting that WSC's contamination is due to its inclusion in specific web sources that were crawled, not to an inevitable consequence of web-scale collection.

**PII is pervasive, with mC4-en as the most contaminated corpus per token.** Table 5 and Table 19 present the extrapolated PII frequencies and ratios normalized by corpus size:

- **Email addresses**: Estimated true counts range from 364K in OpenWebText (precision 99%) to 201M in mC4-en (precision 92%). C4 contains 7.6M, The Pile 19.9M, RedPajama 35.2M. Precision is high (>80%) for most corpora; The Pile (43%), The Stack (53%), and S2ORC (100% but only 10 samples available) have lower precision. Per-token, mC4-en (4.23 × 10⁻⁴) and OSCAR (4.09 × 10⁻⁴) have the highest email address density—roughly 10× higher than web corpora like C4 (3.1 × 10⁻⁶).
- **Phone numbers**: Estimated true counts are substantially higher. mC4-en contains an estimated **4.07 billion** phone numbers (precision 66%). OSCAR contains 107M, RedPajama 70.3M, C4 19.7M. Precision varies dramatically: OSCAR and C4 show >90% precision, while LAION-2B-en (7%) and The Stack (9%) have very low precision, making their estimated counts unreliable. Per-token, mC4-en has the highest phone number density (8.55 × 10⁻³), roughly 3 orders of magnitude higher than the next-highest corpus (OSCAR, 6.98 × 10⁻⁴).
- **IP addresses**: Estimated true counts are lower across the board. mC4-en contains 97.9M (precision 44%), The Stack 4.5M (55%), The Pile 4.1M (48%). S2ORC, peS2o, and LAION-2B-en contain effectively zero. Per-token IP address density is highest in mC4-en (2.06 × 10⁻⁴).
- **Cross-PII patterns**: "The most common PII across corpora is phone numbers, followed by email addresses and IP addresses (except for The Stack, which has more IP addresses than email addresses: 4.4M vs. 4.3M, and peS2o, which has more email addresses than phone numbers)."
- **PII precision error analysis** (Table 21): The paper provides examples of false positives for each PII type. For email addresses, false positives include IRC chat handles containing `@` (e.g., `*!*@n***.***`), usernames with `@` in mockups, and version strings with `@` symbols in code (e.g., `remirror/ui@0.7.3`). For phone numbers, false positives include product identification numbers, software version numbers (e.g., `10.0.0.163`), and 10-digit file identifiers that match the regex pattern but are not phone numbers. For IP addresses, version strings again dominate (e.g., `3.8.10.1`).

#### #### Cross-Corpus Analysis: How Do These Corpora Compare to Each Other?

**Text overlap between corpora is substantial for specific pairs but not universal.** Figure 30 reports the pairwise text overlap (MD5 hash of full document text) between all ten corpora:

- **C4 ∩ RedPajama**: 365M overlapping documents—this is 100% of C4's unique documents (since 365M = |C4|) and 50% of RedPajama's unique documents (690M), meaning half of RedPajama's distinct documents also appear in C4. The paper explains this as reflecting C4's presence in RedPajama's Common Crawl-derived subset, combined with RedPajama's larger documents (average 2,800 tokens vs. C4's 420 tokens, so RedPajama may chunk C4 documents differently).
- **The Pile ∩ RedPajama**: 934K overlapping documents (0.7% of The Pile, 0.1% of RedPajama). These likely come from shared sources like arXiv, Wikipedia, and GitHub.
- **The Pile ∩ The Stack**: 8.9M overlapping documents (6.5% of The Pile, 1.6% of The Stack), reflecting their shared GitHub source.
- **OpenWebText ∩ The Pile**: 3.9M overlapping documents (48.7% of OpenWebText's 8M unique documents, 2.8% of The Pile). The paper notes this is expected since The Pile includes OpenWebText as a source.
- **S2ORC and peS2o have zero overlapping documents with each other and with all other corpora.** The paper attributes this to formatting differences: "despite peS2o being a cleaned version of S2ORC, due to a difference in formatting for parsed paper sections," even these two related corpora share no exact-text documents.
- **Three-way and higher overlaps are rare.** Table 23 reports the largest multi-corpus intersections: C4 + OSCAR + RedPajama has 1.68M three-way overlaps, C4 + mC4-en + RedPajama has 1.38M, and The Pile + RedPajama + The Stack has 592K. All other combinations drop rapidly.

**URL overlaps reveal corpus construction relationships.** Figure 31 reports pairwise URL overlaps for the four corpora with URL metadata (C4, mC4-en, OSCAR, LAION-2B-en):

- **C4 ∩ mC4-en**: 149M shared URLs (41% of C4's unique URLs, 3.8% of mC4-en's). This is substantial but not total, consistent with mC4-en being derived from later Common Crawl snapshots than C4.
- **C4 ∩ OSCAR**: 12.7M shared URLs (3.4% of C4, 3.0% of OSCAR). OSCAR, like C4, is built on Common Crawl, but with different filtering.
- **URL overlaps with LAION-2B-en are negligible:** at most 132 URLs shared with mC4-en and 6 with C4. LAION-2B-en's URLs point to image files (CDNs, photo hosts), which are essentially disjoint from the text-oriented URLs in other corpora.
- **mC4-en documents from C4 constitute only 0.04% of mC4-en**, despite mC4-en being only ~10× larger in total documents. The paper speculates this is "due to formatting differences, between the C4 and mC4-en collection"—the filtering and text extraction pipelines produced different document boundaries even when crawling the same URLs.

**Distributional similarity is highest among web-scraped corpora and lowest for code and captions.** Figure 29 reports the Jensen-Shannon distance between the top 1,000 most common unigrams for each pair of corpora, computed on both intersection and union of the unigram sets:

- **OpenWebText, C4, mC4-en, OSCAR, and RedPajama are most similar to each other:** pairwise JS distances range from 0.14 (C4 ↔ RedPajama) to 0.33 (OpenWebText ↔ RedPajama) on the intersection-based metric. The union-based metric shows a similar pattern with higher absolute values (0.19–0.40).
- **S2ORC and peS2o form a distinct cluster** (JS distance 0.035 on intersection, 0.061 on union—the closest pair by far), but are distant from all other corpora (0.39–0.62).
- **LAION-2B-en and The Stack are the most distinct corpora:** their pairwise JS distances to all other corpora are 0.35–0.54 for LAION-2B-en and 0.49–0.62 for The Stack. This is expected—LAION-2B-en contains image captions with a very different vocabulary distribution (dominated by descriptive adjectives, product terms, and visual words), while The Stack contains source code with programming language keywords, variable names, and package identifiers.
- **OSCAR has the lowest average JS distance to all other corpora** (0.19 on average on the intersection metric), making it the "most representative" corpus in terms of common-unigram distribution.

**Unigram rank comparisons reveal domain-specific vocabulary.** Figures 18–28 (Appendix B.4.1) show paired rank comparison plots for every corpus pair. For example, Figure 18 shows that common unigrams in LAION-2B-en (like "Black," "Light," "Happy," "Women's") have ranks in the 2,000–10,000 range in C4, while common unigrams in C4 (like "an," "its," "then," "should") have similar ranks in LAION-2B-en. This asymmetry reflects the different linguistic distributions: LAION-2B-en has a long tail of descriptive terms that are common in image descriptions but rare in general web text, while C4's common words are the standard functional vocabulary of English that appears in most corpora.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

The paper is a measurement platform rather than a model, so the relevant "ablation" concept is measurement validation: checking whether the measurements are accurate, robust to methodological choices, and not artifacts of the specific algorithms used. The following documents the key validation procedures and their findings.

**MD5 hash collision test for duplicate detection (Section 4.3.2):** The duplicate analysis was rerun with a different random hash seed. "None of the > 7 billion hashes across the ten corpora had a different count." This validates that the MD5 hash-based duplicate identification did not suffer from collisions—the probability of two different documents producing the same MD5 hash is negligible, and the rerun with a different seed confirmed zero empirical collisions. This means the duplicate counts can be interpreted as genuine exact-text duplicates.

**PII precision manual validation (Appendix B.3.2, Table 18):** For each PII type in each corpus, the paper manually inspected up to 100 randomly sampled regex matches and labeled them as true positives or false positives. The precision varies dramatically:
- Email address precision is high for most corpora (92–100%) but drops for The Pile (43%) and The Stack (53%). The paper attributes this to code artifacts containing `@` symbols (e.g., version strings, package names) that match the email regex but are not email addresses.
- Phone number precision is high for OSCAR (91%), C4 (92%), and RedPajama (94%), but very low for LAION-2B-en (7%) and The Stack (9%). In LAION-2B-en, many matches are numeric product identifiers and image dimensions that happen to match the phone number pattern. In The Stack, the regex matches version numbers, hash values, and numeric constants in code.
- IP address precision is moderate to low across the board: 54% (OpenWebText), 43% (OSCAR), 56% (C4), 44% (mC4-en), 48% (The Pile), 30% (RedPajama), 55% (The Stack). S2ORC, peS2o, and LAION-2B-en had zero IP address detections after postprocessing.

The paper explicitly notes that the precision estimates for some cells are based on very small samples: S2ORC had only 1 phone number and 0 IP addresses to inspect; peS2o had 13 phone numbers and 12 IP addresses; LAION-2B-en had 17 email addresses and 16 IP addresses. For these corpora, `*` annotations in Table 18 indicate that fewer than 100 samples were available, and the precision estimate is correspondingly less reliable.

**Postprocessing rule effectiveness (Appendix B.3.2, Table 17):** The PII detection includes postprocessing filters (excluding matches within 50 characters of "ISBN," "DOI," or "#"). The paper does not ablate these rules individually, but the manual validation provides indirect evidence: the high precision for email addresses and phone numbers in most corpora suggests the postprocessing is removing common false positives. For The Stack and LAION-2B-en, where precision is low despite postprocessing, the paper acknowledges that the remaining false positives are of types not caught by these specific filters (version strings, numeric identifiers).

**Toxicity method comparison (Appendix B.3.3, Table 22):** The paper applies two toxicity detection methods—a lexicon-based approach (ToxTrig) and an SVM classifier—and reports both. The results often diverge substantially:
- For C4, the taxonomy finds 0.01% toxic documents, while the classifier finds 5.75%. The paper attributes this to the classifier detecting "subtleties that the lexicon used for filtering documents from C4 did not catch"—i.e., the keyword-based C4 filter and the keyword-based ToxTrig both miss toxicity expressed without known toxic terms, while the classifier captures a broader range of toxic language.
- For peS2o, the taxonomy finds 17.0% toxic documents, while the classifier finds only 9.56%. This reversal suggests the academic text in peS2o contains discussions *about* offensive terms (which trigger the lexicon) without being toxic speech (which the classifier is trained to detect).
- This dual-method approach serves as a form of measurement triangulation: neither method is a ground truth, but the comparison between them reveals corpus-specific patterns and measurement limitations that would be invisible with a single method.

**Search-based contamination precision (Appendix B.3.1, design rationales):** The paper makes several explicit design choices for contamination detection and documents the rationale for each, which functions as a form of robustness discussion:
- **Requiring all input fields in a single document**: increases precision at the cost of recall. A match is more likely to be genuine contamination because co-occurrence of multiple specific strings is unlikely by chance. However, this misses contamination where inputs appear in separate documents.
- **Requiring exactly two or more input fields**: "the co-occurrence of both inputs in the same document increase the likelihood of these inputs to originate from an existing evaluation dataset." Single-input tasks like sentiment analysis are excluded because a single sentence appearing in a corpus could be natural occurrence, not contamination.
- **Ignoring the output/label**: because labels "can appear in different formats (e.g., numeric values, text labels, etc.)," making automatic matching unreliable. This biases the measurement toward false positives (inputs that happen to co-occur naturally without being from the benchmark) rather than false negatives. The paper explicitly acknowledges this produces "an upper bound of exact-match dataset contamination."

**Elasticsearch index format validation (implicit):** The paper does not explicitly validate that the ES index correctly captured all documents or that queries return complete results. The search-based contamination analysis assumes the ES index is complete and accurate—an assumption that the paper does not test, which is a gap in the validation.

### Critical Assessment

The experiments in this paper are fundamentally different from typical ML experiments because WIMBD is a *measurement instrument*, not a proposed method that needs to outperform baselines. The question is not "does WIMBD work better than alternative tools?" but rather "are WIMBD's measurements accurate, are they presented in a way that supports the paper's claims, and do they reveal things about corpora that were not previously known?"

**Claim: WIMBD enables systematic analysis of large corpora that was previously impractical.** The paper demonstrates this by *doing it*—running sixteen analyses across ten corpora totaling 35 terabytes on a single compute node. The fact that the analyses completed (runtime benchmarks in Appendix D) and produced interpretable, non-trivial results (Tables 2–23, Figures 2–31) is the primary evidence. The claim is further supported by the paper's publication of all code and artifacts as open source, making the capability available to others.

However, a critical gap is that **the paper does not compare WIMBD's measurements to ground truth or to alternative measurement tools.** There is no validation against human annotation for the domain distribution, no comparison of WIMBD's approximate n-gram counts against exact counts on a small corpus where exact counting is feasible, and no verification that the Elasticsearch index contains all documents it should contain. The manual PII validation (100 samples per type per corpus) is the closest the paper comes to measurement validation, but even that is limited: it estimates precision for PII identification, but does not estimate recall (how many PII instances were missed?). For the compressed counting of n-grams, the paper describes the accuracy-memory tradeoff qualitatively but provides no empirical accuracy measurements—no comparison of approximate top-k to exact top-k on a manageable sub-corpus.

This does not necessarily undermine the paper's claims, because the claimed contribution is the *platform*, not the exact numerical values for any specific corpus. But it means the specific numbers reported (e.g., "9 million occurrences of ten question marks in C4") should be understood as subject to hash collision inflation of unknown magnitude. The paper's statement that "the larger the hash table, the smaller the probability of hash collisions" provides intuitive guidance but no quantitative bounds. A reader cannot know whether the reported 9M count is 5% inflated, 50% inflated, or accurate to within 1%.

**Claim: The analyses reveal surprising and previously undocumented findings about widely-used corpora.** This claim is strongly supported for the specific findings reported. The duplicate rates for RedPajama (~50%), LAION-2B-en (~54%), and The Pile (66%) are striking and, to the best of the paper's knowledge, were not previously documented at this scale. The benchmark contamination findings—100% contamination of COPA in RedPajama, 67.5% contamination of RTE—are similarly surprising and practically important for anyone evaluating models trained on these corpora. The PII estimates, particularly the extrapolated 4 billion phone numbers in mC4-en, quantify a privacy risk that was previously only discussed qualitatively.

However, there are important caveats about the **scope and generalizability** of these findings:

- **All analyses are on English-language corpora.** The paper states that "most of our analyses are not language dependent, and can be easily applied to other languages as well," but provides no evidence for this claim. Regex-based PII detection would need different patterns for different phone number formats and email conventions. The ToxTrig lexicon is English-specific. The toxicity classifier was trained on English Wikipedia and Twitter. The benchmark contamination analysis uses English-language benchmarks. The paper has not demonstrated that WIMBD's analyses transfer to non-English corpora without modification.
- **The ten corpora, while diverse, are not a representative sample of "large text corpora."** They were chosen because they are well-known and publicly available, not because they provide coverage of the space of possible corpus compositions. A corpus constructed with entirely different methods (e.g., transcribed speech, machine translation output, synthetic data) might surface different patterns that WIMBD's current analyses do not capture.
- **Many findings are corpus-specific and may not generalize.** The fact that The Pile's most common 10-gram is ten dashes is a property of The Pile's specific composition (including LaTeX-heavy arXiv papers and markdown-heavy GitHub repositories). A new corpus constructed from different sources would have different most-common n-grams. This is not a weakness of the analysis—it is precisely the point of running standardized measurements—but it means the paper's specific empirical findings are primarily valuable as a reference set for these ten corpora, not as universal claims about "what is in big data" in general.

**Claim: The difficulty estimation approach works in a practical setting.** This claim is specifically about a component of the compute-optimal framework, but it applies to WIMBD in a different sense: **the paper claims that automated, regex-based PII detection and lexicon/classifier-based toxicity detection are practical measurement tools.** The evidence partially supports this but reveals significant limitations:

- **PII precision is highly variable.** The regex-based approach works well (>80% precision) for email addresses in eight of ten corpora, but for phone numbers, only five of ten corpora achieve >80% precision, and for IP addresses, none do. This means the PII measurement is reliable for estimating email address prevalence but substantially less reliable for estimating phone numbers and IP addresses, particularly in code-heavy corpora (The Stack) and image-caption corpora (LAION-2B-en).
- **The PII postprocessing rules are corpus-agnostic** (the same ISBN/DOI rules are applied everywhere) but may not target the most common false positive types in each corpus. For The Stack, version numbers and hash values are the main false positives for phone numbers, and no postprocessing rule addresses those.
- **Toxicity measurement diverges substantially between methods** (lexicon vs. classifier), and the paper provides no resolution mechanism—both numbers are reported, and the reader must decide which to trust. The paper's interpretation (e.g., that the classifier detects "subtleties" missed by the lexicon) is plausible but speculative; the classifier could equally be producing false positives on academic text that discusses toxic language without being toxic.

**Missing experiments that would have strengthened the paper:**

1. **Exact vs. approximate counting comparison.** Running the n-gram analysis with exact counting on a subset of a corpus (e.g., 100K documents from C4) and comparing the top-k results to the approximate results from the full corpus would provide an empirical estimate of hash collision error. This is a standard validation technique for approximate counting that the paper does not perform.
2. **Recall estimation for PII detection.** The paper could have manually annotated a small set of documents (e.g., 500 random documents from one corpus) for all three PII types and compared the regex-based detection to the manual annotation, providing both precision and recall estimates. The current precision-only validation tells us how many of the *detected* items are genuine but says nothing about how many genuine PII instances were missed entirely.
3. **Contamination verification.** For a sample of benchmark examples that WIMBD flags as contaminated (e.g., 50 examples from the most contaminated corpus-benchmark pairs), the paper could manually inspect the matching documents to verify that they genuinely represent benchmark data leakage rather than incidental co-occurrence. The current contamination measurement is described as "an upper bound," but the paper does not estimate how loose that bound is.
4. **Alternative search backends.** The paper acknowledges that Elasticsearch "was not designed for scaling with large text corpora" and that indexing can take days. Comparing ES to alternative approaches (e.g., suffix arrays for exact match, dense retrieval for fuzzy search) on a smaller corpus would help practitioners choose the right backend for their scale and query needs.
5. **Cross-language PII and toxicity.** Applying the PII regexes and toxicity classifier to the non-English portions of the corpora (which the language distribution analysis shows are non-trivial) would test the paper's claim that the analyses are "not language dependent."
6. **Impact of pre-processing differences.** The paper notes that mC4-en's low text overlap with C4 (0.04%) despite being built from Common Crawl supersets is "due to formatting differences." A controlled experiment examining how different text extraction pipelines (e.g., boilerplate removal, HTML parsing choices) affect the WIMBD measurements would inform practitioners about how sensitive these analyses are to corpus construction choices.

**On the FLOPs-matched comparison claim:** WIMBD does not make FLOPs-matched comparison claims—that was a feature of the reference example paper, not the current paper. WIMBD's central claim is about enabling measurement, not about comparing test-time to pretraining compute.

**Overall assessment of experimental support:** The experiments convincingly demonstrate that WIMBD's two-primitive architecture (counting + search) is sufficient to run a diverse set of informative analyses at terabyte scale on a single compute node. The specific empirical findings about the ten corpora—duplicate rates, common n-grams, benchmark contamination, PII prevalence—are well-documented with explicit references to tables and figures throughout. The paper's claim that these findings are surprising and previously undocumented is credible given that no prior work had systematically compared these corpora across these dimensions. However, the quantitative accuracy of specific measurements (particularly approximate n-gram counts and low-precision PII types) is not rigorously validated against ground truth, and the scope is limited to English-language corpora with the specific analyses implemented in the initial WIMBD release. The platform's extensibility claim—that new analyses can be easily added—is supported by the open-source release but not by any demonstration of users or the authors extending it to new analysis types beyond the initial sixteen. The paper succeeds admirably as a demonstration that corpus-scale auditing is technically feasible and practically valuable; it leaves to future work the tasks of rigorous measurement validation, cross-lingual extension, and integration of WIMBD-style auditing into standard corpus release practices.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Difficulty Estimation Cost Is Unaccounted for and Dominates the Headline Efficiency Gains

**The assumption or constraint.** The entire compute-optimal framework requires knowing a question's difficulty before allocating the inference budget. The paper's method for estimating difficulty—generating 2,048 samples per question and averaging either ground-truth correctness (oracle) or PRM final-answer scores (predicted)—is described in Section 3.2. The paper acknowledges this cost explicitly but excludes it from all budget calculations:

> "our experiments do not account for this cost largely for simplicity ... We view this as an exploration-exploitation tradeoff and an important avenue for future work." (Section 3.2)

**The consequence.** The headline result—"more than 4× better efficiency over a standard best-of-N baseline" (Section 1, Figures 4 and 8)—is computed *after* difficulty has been estimated, without amortizing the cost of the estimation step itself. At 2,048 samples per question, the difficulty estimation alone consumes more compute than the largest test-time budgets studied (256–512 generations) for every single question. In a realistic deployment, the total cost would be `difficulty_estimation_cost + strategy_execution_cost`, and the former dominates the latter by a factor of ~4–8× at the budgets where the efficiency gains are claimed. The `4×` figure should therefore be understood as an **upper bound on achievable efficiency** conditional on a separate, expensive difficulty oracle, not as a realized deployment gain. For any practical setting where difficulty must be estimated for each incoming query, the net efficiency gain over a standard best-of-N baseline that simply spends the entire budget on solving the problem (without a separate estimation phase) would be substantially smaller than 4×, potentially negative for easy problems where best-of-N already performs well.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper's own numbers reveal the scale of this gap. Section 3.2 states that difficulty is estimated using 2,048 samples. The largest test-time budget studied in the search experiments (Section 5.3) is 256 generations, and for revisions (Section 6) the budgets reach 512 generations. This means difficulty estimation costs 4–8× the largest solution budget. Figure 4 shows that compute-optimal search at 16 generations (~39.5% accuracy) matches best-of-N weighted at 64 generations (~38% accuracy). If we add the 2,048-generation estimation cost, the compute-optimal approach has consumed 2,064 generations total versus 64 for the baseline—a ~32× *disadvantage* in total compute, not a 4× advantage. The paper does not present any results that include the amortized difficulty estimation cost.

**Mitigation status.** The paper explicitly acknowledges this as "an important avenue for future work" (Section 3.2) and suggests two directions: training models to predict difficulty directly from question text, or amortizing difficulty estimation by using the initial samples from the solution process itself (adaptive difficulty estimation). Neither approach is implemented or evaluated. Until one of these approaches is demonstrated, the compute-optimal framework's reported gains are conditional on an oracle that is not practically available at deployment time for individual queries. The paper also notes that in batch settings where the same question is solved many times, the estimation cost could be amortized—but this use case is not the primary framing of the paper, which focuses on improving per-query efficiency.

---

### Hard Problems Remain Completely Unsolved: Test-Time Compute Cannot Create Capability from Nothing

**The assumption or constraint.** The compute-optimal framework assumes that the base model has some non-trivial probability of producing a correct answer—the pass@1 rate must be above zero for test-time compute to help. The paper acknowledges this boundary explicitly in the Section 7 takeaway box, stating that test-time compute "can only provide benefits when the model already possesses some 'skill' on a given task distribution, e.g., non-zero pass@1."

**The consequence.** For the hardest questions (difficulty bin 5 in the paper's five-quintile binning), **no amount of test-time compute helps—at all**. In Figure 3 (right), bin 5 accuracy hovers at 1–3% for all search methods and all compute budgets from 4 to 256 generations. In Figure 7 (right), bin 5 accuracy is roughly 2–3% irrespective of whether the budget is spent fully parallel, fully sequential, or any hybrid ratio. In Figure 9 (the FLOPs-matched comparison), the difficulty-bin-5 scaling line is essentially flat near 0–5% across all compute budgets and all ratios of inference-to-pretraining tokens. The consequence is that test-time compute is not a substitute for larger pretraining on genuinely difficult problems—the $14\times$ larger pretrained model outperforms the smaller model with compute-optimal test-time scaling by large margins on hard problems at all $R$ values except the most extreme ($R \ll 1$), where revisions show a +21.6% relative gain but PRM search shows a -3.6% relative loss (Figure 1 bar charts, Figure 9). For most practical deployment scenarios (where $R$ is not extremely small), the paper demonstrates that scaling pretraining compute is the only viable path for improving performance on hard problems. This is a fundamental capability bound: test-time compute amplifies existing capability within the model's reach but cannot extend the frontier of what the model can solve.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The evidence is presented consistently across all experimental sections. Difficulty bin 5 (the hardest quintile, with pass@1 near zero) is explicitly shown in Figure 3 (right, fifth panel), Figure 7 (right, fifth panel), and Figure 9 (fifth row of panels for revisions, additional breakdown for PRM search). In all cases, the performance curves are flat and near zero. The paper states: "On the hardest questions (bin 5), no method makes meaningful progress—the base model simply lacks the capability to produce correct solutions regardless of how the budget is allocated" (Section 5.3). For revisions: "Bin 5: All ratios produce roughly 2–3% accuracy. No allocation strategy helps" (Section 6). The FLOPs-matched comparison (Section 7) quantifies this: for $R \gg 1$ with PRM search, hard problems show a -52.9% relative disadvantage from using test-time compute instead of the larger model.

**Mitigation status.** The paper is transparent about this limitation—it is presented as a key finding rather than a hidden weakness. The takeaway box in Section 7 explicitly states the boundary condition: "test-time compute is most effective when the base model has non-trivial performance on the task." However, the paper does not provide a practical method for identifying which problems fall into this "no hope" category before investing compute. The difficulty estimation method can identify these problems (they fall into bin 5), but it requires the same expensive 2,048-sample procedure discussed in the previous limitation. For deployment, the practical implication is that a system using compute-optimal test-time scaling should have a fallback mechanism—either escalating to a larger model or flagging the problem for human review—when difficulty estimation indicates bin 5. The paper does not design or evaluate such a mechanism. The paper also does not explore whether test-time compute could be combined with retrieval augmentation or tool use to expand the base model's effective capability on these hard problems, which is a natural direction for overcoming this boundary.

### Single Benchmark, Single Model Family: The Difficulty-Dependent Patterns May Not Generalize

**The assumption or constraint.** All experiments in the paper use the MATH benchmark (Hendrycks et al., 2021) with the PaLM 2-S* (Codey) model family. The paper acknowledges this scope limitation in Section 4, stating that the authors "believe this model is representative of the capabilities of many contemporary LLMs," but this claim is not empirically tested. The paper does not evaluate on any other reasoning benchmark (e.g., GSM8K, MBPP, HumanEval, ARC, FOLIO), any other domain (e.g., code generation, scientific QA, open-ended generation), or any other model family (e.g., Llama, Mistral, Qwen).

**The consequence.** Several aspects of the paper's findings could be model-specific or benchmark-specific, and their generalizability is unknown:

- **The PRM's quality and over-optimization behavior** depend on PaLM 2-S*'s output distribution (the PRM is trained on Monte Carlo rollouts from this specific model). A model with different calibration properties, different error patterns, or different output lengths might produce PRM scores with different reliability characteristics, changing where the over-optimization threshold lies and thus changing the optimal difficulty-conditioned policy. The paper's finding that the PRM800k dataset (trained on GPT-4 outputs) was "largely ineffective" for PaLM 2 models (Section 5.1) already demonstrates that PRM quality is highly sensitive to the match between the PRM's training distribution and the base model's output distribution. This means the specific difficulty-dependent search strategy discovered for PaLM 2-S* may not transfer to other models without retraining a model-specific PRM and re-deriving the optimal policy.

- **The revision model's behavior** depends on PaLM 2-S*'s in-context learning and revision capabilities. The finding that revisions are most effective on easy problems (where the model's initial attempts are "roughly correct and just need refinement") may depend on the specific types of errors this model makes. A model that makes different kinds of errors—e.g., conceptual errors rather than arithmetic slips—might benefit from revisions in different difficulty regimes, or not at all.

- **MATH is a specific reasoning domain** (competition-level mathematics) with well-defined correct answers that can be verified with exact string matching. This enables both the difficulty estimation protocol (via pass@1) and the PRM training (via Monte Carlo correctness). The finding that beam search helps on medium problems but over-optimizes on easy ones is a statement about how PaLM 2-S*'s mathematical reasoning errors interact with the PRM's assessment of solution steps. For a code generation benchmark (where correctness is determined by unit test execution rather than symbolic reasoning), the PRM's step-level scoring might have entirely different reliability characteristics. For open-ended generation tasks with no clean correctness signal, neither the PRM training procedure nor the difficulty estimation method applies in its current form.

- **The test set is 500 questions.** Split into five difficulty quintiles of ~100 questions each, then further split by two-fold cross-validation (Section 3.2), the compute-optimal policy is selected based on approximately 50 questions per fold per bin. The paper does not report confidence intervals on any of the compute-optimal scaling curves, making it impossible to assess whether the observed gains are statistically reliable at this sample size. The difference between the best and second-best strategy within a bin might be small enough that a different random split would select a different policy with different aggregate performance.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides no cross-benchmark or cross-model validation. The single-benchmark, single-model scope is explicitly stated in Section 4 but not defended beyond the claim of representativeness. The PRM800k transfer failure (Section 5.1) is the only piece of evidence pointing to model-specificity—it demonstrates that PRMs do not transfer between model families, which implies that the entire search scaling analysis might need to be repeated for each new base model.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed beyond the acknowledgment in Section 4. The paper does not claim generalizability and does not suggest that the specific difficulty-dependent policies discovered for PaLM 2-S* on MATH will transfer. The framework itself—difficulty estimation + strategy selection—is presented as general, but the specific findings require replication on other models and benchmarks. The open-source release of the code enables such replication by future work, but the paper itself provides no evidence one way or the other.

---

### The $14\times$ Larger Model Baseline Is Not Compute-Optimally Trained, Weakening the Pretraining-Test-Time Comparison

**The assumption or constraint.** The FLOPs-matched comparison in Section 7 compares PaLM 2-S* augmented with compute-optimal test-time strategies against a model with approximately $14\times$ more parameters, but this larger model was trained by scaling *only* parameters while holding training data fixed, following the LLaMA training paradigm (Touvron et al., 2023). The paper acknowledges this departure from compute-optimal pretraining:

> "We choose this setting as it is representative of a canonical approach to scaling pretraining compute and leave the analysis of compute-optimal scaling of pretraining compute where the data and parameters are both scaled equally to future work." (Section 7)

**The consequence.** A model trained with compute-optimal scaling (Chinchilla-style, Hoffmann et al., 2022)—where both parameters and training data are increased proportionally with the total compute budget—would likely achieve higher performance for the same FLOPs than a parameter-only-scaled model. This means the pretraining baseline in the FLOPs-matched comparison is **weaker than it should be** for a fair comparison. The reported advantages of test-time compute over the larger model—e.g., +27.8% relative improvement on medium questions at $R \ll 1$ for revisions, +19.1% on easy questions at $R \ll 1$ for PRM search (Figure 1 bar charts)—may shrink or reverse against a properly compute-optimal larger model.

Additionally, the $14\times$ larger model is evaluated with **greedy decoding** only—no test-time compute augmentation of any kind (no majority voting, no best-of-N, no search, no revisions). This is acknowledged implicitly by the framing (the comparison is "test-time compute with small model" vs. "no test-time compute with large model"), but it is an asymmetric comparison. A fairer design would give the larger model a comparable test-time compute budget (scaled proportionally to its per-token cost) or at minimum a modest best-of-N budget. The paper's finding that test-time compute with a smaller model can "outperform a ~14× larger model" is therefore true only against a specific, suboptimal larger-model deployment. Against a larger model that also used test-time compute—even a simple best-of-8 or best-of-16—the advantage might disappear or reverse, particularly on medium and hard problems where the paper already shows smaller margins.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper does not include any variant of this comparison with a compute-optimally trained larger model or with the larger model receiving any test-time compute budget. All FLOPs-matched results in Figure 9 and Figure 1 bar charts are against a parameter-only-scaled model with greedy decoding. The paper does not discuss how the results might change under a Chinchilla-optimal training regime, though it does note that "this means the $14\times$ larger model may not be compute-optimally trained" as a caveat.

**Mitigation status.** The paper explicitly states this is a limitation and leaves it to future work. The paper's choice to follow the LLaMA scaling paradigm is defensible—many real-world models are in fact trained with parameter-only scaling rather than Chinchilla-optimal scaling, making the comparison practically relevant even if theoretically suboptimal. However, readers should understand that the reported advantages of test-time compute are relative to a specific baseline and may not hold against the strongest possible pretrained model achievable with the same total FLOPs.

---

### Revisions and PRM Search Are Studied Independently but Never Combined

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper studies two complementary test-time compute mechanisms—revisions (modifying the proposal distribution) and PRM-guided search (improving candidate selection via a verifier)—but analyzes them entirely separately, in Sections 6 and 5 respectively. Section 8 explicitly acknowledges this:

> "We did not experiment with PRM tree-search techniques in combination with revisions" (Section 8)

**The consequence.** The paper's central claim about the complementarity of the two axes—revisions are a "local search" that refines existing solutions, PRM search is a "global search" that explores qualitatively different solution strategies (Section 4, Innovation 2)—is argued conceptually but never demonstrated empirically. The paper shows that revisions work best on easy problems and PRM search works best on medium problems (Section 3 difficulty-bin analyses for each method), which implies that a combined system using revisions as the proposal distribution within PRM-guided beam search might outperform either method alone, particularly on medium problems where both have some effect. Without this experiment, the paper's strongest claimed insight—that the proposal distribution and verifier are "complementary, independent scaling axes" (Innovation 2)—remains a hypothesis about what *could* be achieved, not a demonstrated result.

This gap is particularly significant because the paper's compute-optimal allocation selects between search and revisions (allocating budget to one or the other based on difficulty), but never deploys both simultaneously on the same problem. A natural extension—using the revision model to iteratively improve candidates within each beam of a beam search, with the PRM scoring each step—could potentially combine the benefits of both mechanisms. The paper's current results therefore represent a **lower bound** on what a fully integrated system could achieve, and the difficulty-dependent policy (which switches between rather than combining mechanisms) may be suboptimal compared to a policy that jointly optimizes revision depth, search width, and search algorithm.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides separate results for revisions (Figures 6–8) and PRM search (Figures 3–4), with difficulty-bin analyses for each. It does not contain a single experiment where both are combined. Section 8 lists this as the first item in future work.

**Mitigation status.** The paper is transparent about this gap and presents it as a direction for future work rather than a hidden weakness. The conceptual framework—the proposer-scorer decomposition in Section 2—provides the intellectual scaffolding for the combination. The practical barrier to combining them (noted implicitly) is that the PRM trained on base model outputs does not transfer well to revision model outputs (Figure 15a, Appendix J shows this distribution shift), so a combined system would likely require training a revision-specific PRM, adding to the infrastructure burden.

---

### The Revision Model Suffers a 38% Correct-to-Incorrect Reversion Rate with Only Partial Mitigation

**The assumption or constraint.** The revision model is trained exclusively on sequences where the in-context answers are incorrect, followed by a correct target (Section 6.1). During training, the model never sees a correct answer in context followed by a revision task—it only learns to revise incorrect answers into correct ones. At test time, however, the model generates sequential revisions, meaning that by step 2 or 3 of a chain, the context may already contain a correct answer from an earlier revision step.

**The consequence.** The paper reports (Section 6.1) that **approximately 38% of correct answers produced during a revision chain get "revised" back to incorrect answers** in the subsequent step. This is a direct consequence of the training data bias: the model has never been trained to recognize when no revision is needed, so it treats a correct answer in context the same as an incorrect one and attempts to "fix" it, often introducing new errors. The mitigation—using majority voting or verifier-based selection across the entire chain rather than simply taking the final revision—reduces the impact but does not eliminate it. The 38% reversion rate means that for problems where the revision model produces a correct answer at some intermediate step, there is a substantial probability that the final output (if selected naively) will be incorrect, and the chain-based selection mechanism must correctly identify the correct intermediate step among the revisions. The verifier's accuracy on revision model outputs is imperfect (the base-LM PRM underperforms on revision outputs, as shown in Figure 15a), meaning the selection mechanism itself introduces errors.

This reversion problem is not a minor implementation detail—it fundamentally limits the effective depth of sequential revision chains. The pass@1 trajectory in Figure 6 (left) shows that per-step accuracy improves from ~18% at step 1 to ~24–25% by steps 15–20 and then plateaus. Without the reversion problem, one would expect longer chains to continue improving (or at minimum not degrade). The plateau suggests that after roughly 15–20 steps, the probability of finding a correct answer through revision is balanced by the probability of losing a previously correct answer through incorrect reversion, creating a steady-state ceiling.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The 38% reversion rate is reported in Section 6.1 but without a detailed breakdown (e.g., how this rate varies by difficulty, by revision step, or by the type of error introduced). Figure 6 (left) indirectly shows the consequence—the pass@1 curve saturates despite the model being capable of further revisions. The paper's mitigation (chain-based selection) is evaluated in Figure 6 (right) and Figure 8, which show that sequential revisions with verifier-based selection outperform parallel sampling, but these results do not isolate how much of the gap between achieved performance and the theoretical ceiling (if reversions did not occur) is due to the reversion problem.

**Mitigation status.** Partially addressed. The paper implements chain-level selection (majority voting or verifier-based best-of-N weighted across the entire revision chain) to avoid relying on the final revision, and this does provide improvements over naively taking the last output. However, this is a post-hoc fix that does not address the root cause—the model's training distribution mismatch. A more principled solution—such as training the revision model on mixed trajectories that sometimes end with "no revision needed" when the current answer is already correct, or training an explicit "stop revising" classifier—is not explored. The ReST$^{EM}$ experiment (Appendix K, Figure 16) shows that attempting to further optimize the revision model with on-policy RL-style training caused performance to *degrade substantially* with sequential revisions, indicating that the revision training is fragile and the reversion problem may be exacerbated rather than solved by more sophisticated training. The paper does not propose a concrete path to reducing the 38% reversion rate.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

WIMBD fundamentally reframes corpus creation from a **pipeline-centric activity** (document the construction steps and trust the outcome) to a **measurement-centric discipline** (empirically characterize what was actually produced). This is a methodological shift, not an incremental refinement—it changes what counts as adequate documentation for a released dataset.

Before WIMBD, the prevailing norm for large-scale corpora was to describe the collection and filtering process: which Common Crawl snapshots were used, what heuristics were applied, what deduplication was performed. These descriptions constituted the corpus's "documentation." WIMBD demonstrates that this documentation is necessary but radically insufficient. The paper's sixteen analyses across ten corpora uncover patterns—spikes in the document length distribution revealing template-generated near-duplicates that survived deduplication, benchmark contamination rates exceeding 50% for widely-used evaluation sets, billions of phone numbers in corpora that were never designed to capture structured personal data—that are invisible at the pipeline level. These are not bugs in the pipelines; they are **emergent properties of the composition of well-intentioned filtering steps** that no amount of pipeline documentation would predict.

The field's dominant approach to data quality has been procedural: state what you did, argue that each step is reasonable, and assume the output inherits the intended properties. WIMBD makes the case—empirically, through dozens of specific findings—that this reasoning fails systematically. C4's creators deduplicated the corpus, and WIMBD confirms zero exact duplicates. But C4 also contains clusters of functionally identical reverse-phone-lookup pages, each with a unique phone number, each bypassing exact deduplication, and these manifest as an anomalous spike in the document length distribution (Figure 4) that the pipeline description says nothing about. OSCAR's creators applied a 100-character minimum length filter, and WIMBD confirms no documents fall below that threshold. But OSCAR also contains millions of near-duplicate phpBB forum FAQ pages—longer than 100 characters, structurally identical, each a failed crawl disguised as content—that no filter was designed to catch.

This shift from procedural to empirical documentation has practical consequences. The paper demonstrates that running the full WIMBD suite on C4 (~154 billion tokens) costs approximately 47 hours of compute time and ~$443 on cloud infrastructure (Appendix D, Table 24). This is not trivial, but it is comparable to the cost of preprocessing or tokenizing the same corpus—activities that are already standard in any training pipeline. WIMBD makes corpus auditing **economically feasible as a routine step**, not a research project in itself. The Dollma dataset (Soldaini et al., 2024), cited in the paper's discussion, reportedly used WIMBD insights during its curation, suggesting the tool is already being adopted in practice.

WIMBD also resolves a contradiction that had been simmering in the NLP community. On one side, there have been repeated calls for better data documentation—Datasheets for Datasets (Gebru et al., 2021), Data Statements (McMillan-Major et al., 2023), and broader movements toward dataset transparency. On the other side, the practical tools for implementing this documentation at the scale of web corpora simply did not exist. The Data Measurements Tool (Luccioni et al., 2021) and Know Your Data (Google, 2021) addressed smaller datasets. The ROOTS search tool (Piktus et al., 2023a) and Data Portraits (Marone and Van Durme, 2023) supported individual corpora but not cross-corpus comparison. The gap between what documentation standards prescribed and what practitioners could feasibly produce was not a lack of will but a lack of infrastructure. WIMBD fills this gap, making it credible to demand—rather than merely request—that new corpora be released with systematic empirical characterization.

The research directions that become more attractive after WIMBD include any work that depends on understanding training data composition: model interpretability (linking model behavior to data properties), data-efficient training (understanding which data characteristics correlate with sample efficiency), memorization and privacy research (quantifying exposure risk from training data), and fairness audits (measuring representation and bias at the corpus level). The research directions that become less attractive are those that treat corpora as interchangeable black boxes—papers that compare models trained on "C4" vs. "The Pile" without examining how these corpora differ in measurable ways will increasingly appear incomplete, because WIMBD provides the measurements that make those differences explicit.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Fuzzy and semantic contamination detection.** The paper's contamination protocol uses exact-match search—all input fields must appear verbatim in a single document. This is acknowledged as "an upper bound of exact-match dataset contamination" (Appendix B.3.1) that misses near-duplicate contamination where minor formatting changes, paraphrasing, or translation separate the evaluation example from the training text. The paper explicitly notes that prior work (Emami et al., 2020; Elazar et al., 2021b) has shown that "high overlap between sentences... and pretraining corpora inflates the results on the test set" even without exact match—a finding the current protocol cannot detect. A natural extension would integrate dense retrieval (embedding-based similarity search) or n-gram overlap metrics into the WIMBD search primitive, replacing the exact-match constraint with a similarity threshold. A strong follow-up would measure contamination rates under both exact and fuzzy matching for the same 82 PromptSource benchmarks on the same four corpora, quantifying how much additional contamination fuzzy matching reveals. The paper's finding that 67 of 82 benchmarks show zero exact-match contamination makes this particularly important: some of those "clean" benchmarks may show non-trivial near-duplicate contamination, which would change which evaluation sets are safe for models trained on specific corpora. The Elasticsearch limitation (only exact-match, as noted in Appendix C) makes this extension non-trivial—it would likely require integrating a vector database alongside the existing ES index.

**Cross-lingual PII and toxicity measurement with per-language precision calibration.** The paper's PII detection uses English-centric regular expressions (North American phone number format with optional parenthetical area codes, standard dotted-decimal IP addresses) and its toxicity detection uses an English lexicon and an English-trained SVM classifier. Yet the language distribution analysis (Figure 11) shows that every corpus contains 0.3–4.1% non-English documents, and mC4-en alone contains an estimated 201 million email addresses and 4 billion phone numbers (Table 5). No one knows how many of those detections are in non-English contexts, or what the precision of the English regexes is on, say, German phone numbers (`+49` format) or Japanese email address conventions. A strong follow-up would: (1) develop regex patterns for the ten most common non-English languages in each corpus (as identified by the existing CLD2 language distribution analysis), (2) conduct separate manual precision validation for each language-regex pair on a per-language sample of 100 matches, (3) report per-language extrapolated PII counts, and (4) integrate a multilingual toxicity classifier (covering the same languages) alongside the English SVM. The WIMBD framework already supports this structurally—the Exact Counts primitive can run language-specific regexes in the map phase, gated on the CLD2 language prediction—but the engineering work of developing and validating the language-specific patterns remains. This extension would directly address the paper's claim that "most of our analyses are not language dependent" (Appendix A), which is currently untested.

**Measurement validation via exact counting on sub-corpus samples.** The paper's compressed counting for n-grams and duplicates uses hash-based approximation (Appendix E) with a configurable accuracy-memory tradeoff, but provides no empirical estimate of how accurate the approximations are for any specific analysis. The paper's only validation is the MD5 collision check for duplicate detection (rerunning with a different seed and finding zero differences across 7 billion hashes), which validates the specific choice of MD5 for duplicate hashing but says nothing about the non-cryptographic hash tables used for n-gram counting, where collisions are expected and the table size is chosen to fit available memory. A straightforward and important follow-up would take a manageable sub-corpus (e.g., 100K random documents from C4, or the OpenWebText corpus at 8M documents and 41 GB), run exact n-gram counting on it as ground truth, run WIMBD's compressed counting, and report the precision, recall, and rank correlation between the exact and approximate top-k lists at multiple hash table sizes. This would produce the first empirical calibration curve for the compressed counting approach, allowing users to choose hash table sizes with known error bounds. The paper's observation that "unigram estimates are more accurate than 10-gram estimates since the number of possible values is much smaller" (Section 3.1) is qualitatively correct but quantitatively unspecified—this experiment would produce specific numbers (e.g., "at table size X, top-100 unigrams have recall 0.99 and top-100 10-grams have recall 0.82") that would make the accuracy tradeoff operational.

**Integration of PRM-guided search with revision model outputs on code generation benchmarks.** WIMBD is a measurement platform, not a method—but the paper's demonstration that benchmark contamination is widespread creates an urgent need for evaluation frameworks that are robust to contamination. One approach is to integrate data characteristics (from WIMBD-style analyses) into contamination-aware evaluation protocols. However, a more direct connection to the broader literature would test whether WIMBD-style corpus auditing can detect contamination that impacts model evaluation. A concrete experiment: take a corpus known to be contaminated with a specific benchmark (e.g., RedPajama with COPA at 100% contamination), train two identical-architecture models—one on the full contaminated RedPajama, one on a decontaminated version (with COPA examples removed using WIMBD's search tool to identify and filter contaminated documents)—and compare their COPA scores. If the scores differ substantially, this provides causal evidence that WIMBD-detected contamination inflates evaluation results. This would transform contamination measurement from a descriptive exercise ("X% of test examples appear in the corpus") to a causal claim ("this contamination inflates evaluation scores by Y points"), which is what practitioners actually need to decide whether to trust a benchmark for a given model. The paper's current contamination analysis provides the descriptive measurement; the training experiment would close the loop to model impact.

**Dynamic corpus evolution tracking across Common Crawl snapshots.** WIMBD's analyses are applied statically to ten corpora at fixed points in time, but corpora are periodically re-crawled (Common Crawl snapshots, updated arXiv dumps, new Wikipedia versions). WIMBD makes it feasible to run the same sixteen analyses on multiple versions of the same corpus and track how its properties evolve. A concrete study would apply WIMBD to consecutive Common Crawl-based corpora (e.g., C4 built from different monthly snapshots, or mC4-en versions over time) and measure how domain distributions shift, whether new evaluation benchmarks become contaminated as they are released and subsequently crawled, whether PII accumulation accelerates or stabilizes, and whether the most common n-grams change as the web's content evolves. This would produce the first longitudinal characterization of web-scale training data, analogous to how sociolinguists track language change in smaller corpora. The paper's Appendix D runtime benchmarks (47 hours for C4) suggest this is feasible at monthly cadence with moderate cloud compute budgets. The engineering requirements—scheduling recurrent WIMBD runs and maintaining versioned indices—are straightforward extensions of the existing infrastructure. The scientific payoff would be understanding whether corpus quality is stable over time or whether models trained on different snapshots are learning from systematically different data, with implications for reproducibility and model versioning.

**Negative-result stress test: WIMBD on a known-clean reference corpus.** A valuable validation experiment would be to construct or identify a small, carefully curated, human-verified "clean" corpus (e.g., Project Gutenberg texts with known provenance, or a manually decontaminated subset of Wikipedia), run the full WIMBD suite on it, and verify that the analyses produce the expected results: zero benchmark contamination, zero or near-zero PII, expected n-gram distributions, smooth document length distributions, and zero duplicates. This would serve dual purposes: (1) it validates that WIMBD's measurements are not generating false positives (e.g., the contamination search returning spurious matches that look like benchmark examples but are genuine natural occurrences), and (2) it provides a reference distribution against which measurements on web-scale corpora can be compared—a "control corpus" that establishes baseline expectations for each metric. The paper implicitly does something similar by using C4 (which was explicitly deduplicated) as a baseline for the duplicate analysis, but a manually verified corpus would extend this validation to all sixteen analyses simultaneously. Any analysis that produces anomalous results on the clean corpus (e.g., non-trivial "detected" PII or contamination) would indicate a problem with the measurement methodology rather than the corpus, providing a systematic debugging framework for WIMBD's analysis suite.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Corpus release documentation as a standardized requirement.** The most immediate practical application is integrating WIMBD-style measurements into the release process for new large-scale text corpora. Rather than releasing a new dataset with only a paper describing the collection pipeline, corpus creators can run WIMBD and publish the sixteen analysis outputs alongside the corpus. The paper's Table 24 demonstrates this is economically feasible: the full suite on C4 cost ~$443 and took ~47 hours. For a new corpus under active development—like Dolma (Soldaini et al., 2024), which already used WIMBD iteratively during curation—this cost is negligible compared to the cost of training models on the resulting data. The concrete benefit is that downstream users can make informed decisions: a researcher choosing between two available corpora for pretraining can compare their domain distributions (Figure 6), duplicate rates (Figure 3), and contamination profiles (Figure 5) directly, rather than relying on high-level descriptions. The paper's finding that 50% of RedPajama's documents are duplicates versus 0% for C4 (Table 13) is exactly the kind of information that changes data selection decisions but was previously unavailable without custom infrastructure.

**Data source prioritization for targeted data collection.** The domain distribution analyses (Section 4.2.2, Appendix B.1.1) and the cross-corpus overlap analysis (Appendix B.4.2) provide actionable intelligence for practitioners building new corpora. The finding that `cdn.shopify.com` contributes ~6% of LAION-2B-en's documents (Figure 2) but these are largely duplicate product listing templates rather than diverse image descriptions means that a practitioner building a new image-caption dataset can specifically target or exclude this domain based on whether it aligns with their goals. The finding that 50% of RedPajama's unique documents originate from C4 but C4's documents average 420 tokens versus RedPajama's 2,800 tokens (Table 2) means that including C4 in a new corpus might contribute document diversity without adding proportionate token volume—a concrete consideration when designing a multi-source mixture. The geolocation analysis (Figure 10) showing 60–80% US-hosted documents across all five corpora with URL metadata provides an actionable target for efforts to increase geographic diversity in training data: a practitioner can measure their own corpus's geolocation distribution against these baselines and adjust their URL seed list or crawling strategy accordingly.

**Evaluation benchmark selection for models trained on known corpora.** The contamination analysis (Figure 5, Table 16) directly informs evaluation protocol design. A researcher who has trained a model on RedPajama should categorically avoid reporting results on COPA (100% contaminated), RTE (67.5% contaminated), WIC (60.2% contaminated), WSC (58.2% contaminated), SemEval-2014 (52.8%), SICK (52.6%), and LIAR (45.0%)—these benchmarks are compromised for any RedPajama-trained model. Conversely, the finding that 67 of 82 tested benchmarks show zero contamination across all corpora means that selecting a clean evaluation suite is feasible by cross-referencing the paper's Table 16 with the corpus used for training. For a model trained on The Pile, AESLC (45.5% contaminated) and WSC (32.2% contaminated) should be avoided, but most other benchmarks appear uncontaminated. For a model trained on C4, only WIC (64.4% contaminated), WSC (29.3%), and LIAR (29.2%) exceed 5% contamination. This is a practical, immediately usable result: the paper provides a lookup table (Table 16) mapping corpus to contaminated benchmarks, enabling researchers to select evaluation suites that are appropriate for their training data without running any infrastructure themselves.

**PII risk assessment and data sanitization for public corpus releases.** The PII analysis (Section 4.4.2) with its precision-calibrated extrapolation (Tables 5, 18) provides the first systematic estimate of how much structured personal information exists in widely-used public training corpora. A legal or policy team deciding whether a corpus is safe to release publicly can use these numbers as a reference point: if their corpus's per-token PII density is substantially higher than mC4-en's (the most PII-dense corpus in the study, with 4.23 × 10⁻⁴ email addresses per token, 8.55 × 10⁻³ phone numbers per token, and 2.06 × 10⁻⁴ IP addresses per token), they may need additional sanitization. The paper's finding that precision varies dramatically by corpus and PII type (e.g., phone number precision is 94% for RedPajama but 7% for LAION-2B-en) means that PII detection must be calibrated per-corpus—a blanket regex without manual precision validation will produce misleading counts. The paper's postprocessing rules (excluding matches near "ISBN," "DOI," or "#" strings; Table 17) and the manual validation protocol (100 samples per type per corpus; Table 18) provide a template that other corpus creators can follow directly.
