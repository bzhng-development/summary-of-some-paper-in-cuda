# The Many Faces of On-Policy Distillation: Pitfalls, Mechanisms, and Fixes

**ArXiv:** [2605.11182](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.11182)

## 🎯 Pitch

On-policy self-distillation completely fails on math reasoning because trying to compress instance-specific privileged information into a single policy forces the model to learn a weaker, averaged distribution. The paper shows that student-generated prefixes actively distort teacher supervision, TopK reverse-KL gradients contain a hidden cancelation error that suppresses correct tokens, and only shared latent rules—like system prompts—can be successfully distilled.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper studies when on-policy distillation (OPD) and on-policy self-distillation (OPSD) succeed or fail for large language models, using Qwen3 model families on mathematical reasoning benchmarks (MATH-500, AIME24, AIME25), system-prompt internalization, and alignment tasks. The authors identify three failure mechanisms: **prefix-induced teacher-student mismatch** (student-generated prefixes force the teacher into reasoning states where its token-level supervision becomes locally incompatible with the student's trajectory), **biased TopK reverse-KL gradients** (truncating the reverse-KL objective to a TopK vocabulary subset leaves an uncanceled constant term in the gradient that suppresses teacher-preferred tokens with small log-probability margins), and **OPSD's PI-free policy aggregation** (the student learns the normalized geometric mean of PI-conditioned teacher distributions, which is insufficient when privileged information is instance-specific rather than a shared latent rule). In the math reasoning setting, OPSD fails entirely to improve the student, while OPD with an unnormalized TopK reverse-KL objective collapses into repetitive degeneracy—accuracy on MATH-500 drops nearly to zero by step 1000 as repeat ratios approach 1.0. The paper demonstrates that **stop-gradient TopK reverse KL**, **RLVR-adapted teachers** (a GRPO-trained Qwen3-1.7B teacher outperforms a Qwen3-8B teacher on distillation despite comparable standalone accuracy, because the smaller teacher's output distribution is closer to the student's), and **SFT-stabilized students** (fine-tuning on teacher traces before OPD prevents garbled outputs and stabilizes response length) mitigate these failures, establishing that OPSD is only effective when the privileged information induces a shared latent behavior compressible into a PI-free policy—as in system-prompt internalization where OPSD matches GRPO accuracy while substantially reducing response length—but not when PI is instance-specific, as in math reasoning where the aggregated policy becomes substantially weaker than any individual PI-conditioned teacher.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: We Don't Understand When On-Policy Distillation Actually Works

The field has a genuine empirical puzzle on its hands. On-policy distillation (OPD) and on-policy self-distillation (OPSD) have been proposed as attractive post-training methods that offer dense, token-level supervision on trajectories the student model generates itself. The conceptual appeal is clear: rather than training on static, off-policy data (which can induce distribution shift and catastrophic forgetting), OPD trains on the student's own outputs, using a teacher model to provide detailed feedback at every step of every sampled trajectory. This promises a path to integrating stronger models' capabilities into smaller ones, mitigating forgetting, and internalizing privileged information—all without the high-variance reward signals that plague reinforcement learning approaches.

Yet the empirical record is contradictory in ways that suggest something fundamental is being missed. Several papers report successful applications. Agarwal et al. (2024) showed that on-policy distillation can outperform standard off-policy knowledge distillation for language models. Zhao et al. (2026) demonstrated self-distillation for reasoning, where a model conditions on ground-truth answers as privileged information and then learns to produce correct reasoning without seeing the answer at test time. Sang et al. (2026) used OPSD for reasoning compression, showing that a model can be trained to produce concise reasoning by distilling from a teacher that conditions on a "be concise" instruction. Shenfeld et al. (2026) showed that self-distillation enables continual learning by having the student learn from a teacher that retains access to previous-task data. In each case, the story is compelling: privileged information or stronger teachers provide dense token-level guidance, the student internalizes the useful behavior, and the result is a model that performs better than what you'd get from standard training.

But the countervailing evidence is equally striking and comes from carefully conducted studies. Fu et al. (2026) presented a systematic investigation of OPD failure modes, documenting instability, length explosion, and degradation on reasoning benchmarks. Kim et al. (2026) asked explicitly "Why does self-distillation (sometimes) degrade the reasoning capability of LLMs?" and found that self-distillation can hurt performance even when the teacher appears to provide reasonable supervision. These are not marginal disagreements about hyperparameters—they are findings that the method can be actively harmful in settings where prior work claimed it should help.

This paper steps into this contradiction with a specific diagnosis: **the effectiveness of OP(S)D depends on whether the privileged information (PI) induces a shared latent behavior or an instance-specific one, and whether the distillation loss formulation preserves gradient correctness under truncation approximations**. These are not just two independent factors; they interact in ways that explain why some papers find success and others find collapse.

The broader significance of resolving this question extends well beyond academic curiosity. Post-training pipelines are increasingly central to LLM deployment: reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR), and various forms of distillation are the mechanisms by which raw pretrained models become useful assistants, reasoners, and tool-users. If on-policy distillation is to claim a place in this pipeline—and the paper's results on system-prompt internalization and alignment suggest it should, at least for some tasks—then practitioners need to know when it works and why. Deploying OPD naively on math reasoning, as this paper shows, leads to models that degenerate into producing the word "maybe" hundreds of times. That's not just inefficient; it's catastrophic. Understanding the boundary conditions between success and collapse is therefore practically urgent.

### Why On-Policy Distillation Matters in the First Place

To appreciate why researchers have been so interested in OPD despite its mixed record, it's worth understanding what problems it purports to solve relative to existing alternatives.

**Off-policy knowledge distillation has fundamental limitations.** The standard approach to distilling a large teacher into a smaller student is to collect a dataset of the teacher's outputs (generated from prompts in the training distribution), then fine-tune the student via supervised learning on those outputs. This is off-policy: the student never sees its own outputs during training. The problem is that the student's distribution differs from the teacher's, and small models may not have the capacity to perfectly imitate large ones. When the student is deployed, it generates tokens from its own distribution—a distribution it never practiced on during training. The mismatch between the training distribution (teacher outputs) and the test-time distribution (student outputs) can lead to compounding errors, where small deviations early in a sequence push the student into states the teacher never demonstrated, causing the student to produce low-quality or nonsensical continuations. On-policy distillation addresses this by training the student on its own sampled trajectories, so the training and test distributions are aligned.

**Standard RL for LLMs suffers from high variance and credit assignment problems.** Reinforcement learning with a scalar reward (e.g., RLHF with a reward model, or RLVR with a correctness verifier) provides only a single signal per complete response. This makes credit assignment difficult: if a 500-token math solution is correct, which of those 500 tokens were actually important? The sparse reward signal must propagate backward through the entire trajectory, leading to high variance in the policy gradient estimates and slow convergence. OPD offers a potential remedy: instead of a scalar reward at the end, the teacher provides a token-level signal at every step, effectively giving the student immediate feedback on each token it generates. This dense supervision is conceptually similar to having a reward model at every timestep, which should reduce variance and speed up learning.

**Multi-teacher and continual learning scenarios are naturally expressed in the OPD framework.** If you have multiple teachers with complementary strengths (e.g., one strong at math, another strong at code, another at factual recall), OPD provides a natural mechanism to distill from all of them simultaneously: each teacher provides token-level supervision on the student's on-policy trajectories, and the student learns to integrate their combined expertise. Similarly, in continual learning, you might want the student to retain knowledge from previous tasks while adapting to new ones. An OPSD setup where the teacher retains access to privileged information about previous tasks (e.g., the original training data) while the student does not provides a principled way to prevent catastrophic forgetting: the student learns a policy that approximates what it would do if it still had access to that privileged information.

**Privileged information internalization is a broadly useful capability.** Many practical settings involve information available at training time that cannot be provided at inference. A teacher might have access to ground-truth answers, detailed rubrics, retrieval results, or tool execution outputs. If a student can be trained via OPSD to internalize the teacher's PI-conditioned behavior, the student effectively gains capabilities at test time that would otherwise require external resources. For example, a reasoning model could be trained with access to ground-truth answers as PI, learning to produce correct reasoning chains without seeing the answer at test time. This is the promise of methods like Self-Distilled Reasoner (Zhao et al., 2026).

Given these appealing properties, the question is not whether OPD is conceptually interesting—it clearly is—but rather why it so often fails in practice, and whether those failures are fundamental or fixable. This paper's contribution is to identify specific mechanisms behind the failures and specific interventions that mitigate them.

### Where Prior Approaches Fall Short: Three Specific Gaps

**Gap 1: No systematic understanding of when PI helps versus hurts.**

Prior work on OPSD has largely been optimistic, reporting positive results on specific tasks without characterizing the conditions under which the method succeeds. Zhao et al. (2026) showed that OPSD with ground-truth answers as PI improves reasoning on GSM8K and MATH. Sang et al. (2026) showed that OPSD with a conciseness instruction as PI can compress reasoning chains without losing accuracy. But these successes do not generalize to all PI structures—as this paper demonstrates, mathematical reasoning with answer-only or full-response PI not only fails to help but underperforms vanilla OPD. The critical missing analysis is *why* some PI helps and some doesn't. The paper's answer—that PI must induce a shared latent behavior to be compressible into a PI-free policy—provides the first clear criterion for predicting when OPSD will succeed. Prior work lacked this criterion, leading to indiscriminate application of PI in settings where it was structurally doomed to fail.

**Gap 2: TopK approximations to reverse KL are widely used but their gradient bias is unrecognized.**

Full-vocabulary KL divergence is memory-intensive. Computing the teacher and student distributions over the entire vocabulary (typically 50k–150k tokens) at every position of every sampled trajectory requires storing and processing large tensors, which can exceed GPU memory for long sequences or large batch sizes. Practitioners therefore commonly truncate the KL computation to a TopK subset of the vocabulary—for instance, only computing the KL over the 20 tokens with the highest student probabilities at each position. This is done in SDPO (Shenfeld et al., 2026), Self-Distilled Reasoner (Zhao et al., 2026), and many other OPD implementations.

What this paper identifies—and what prior work missed—is that this truncation introduces a **systematic gradient bias**. In the full-vocabulary reverse KL gradient (Equation 3), a constant "+1" term appears that sums to zero over the full vocabulary because the student probabilities sum to one. When you truncate to a TopK subset, that sum is no longer one, the constant term no longer cancels, and the gradient becomes biased in a specific direction: tokens where the teacher-student probability ratio exceeds $\mathrm{e}$ (approximately 2.718) get promoted, while tokens below this threshold get suppressed even if the teacher prefers them to the student's current distribution. This bias pushes the student away from the teacher distribution rather than toward it, and can drive optimization toward degenerate solutions—exactly the repetitive "maybe maybe maybe" collapse documented in Figure 4. Prior work using TopK approximations (Fu et al., 2026; Kim et al., 2026) documented instability but did not trace it to this specific gradient mechanism.

**Gap 3: The interaction between student prefixes and teacher capability is underappreciated.**

In OPD, the teacher does not generate from scratch; it conditions on prefixes generated by the student. This means the teacher's supervision at each step is contingent on the partial trajectory the student has produced so far. If the student produces a prefix that commits to a specific reasoning branch, the teacher can only provide feedback conditioned on that branch. If the teacher would have taken a different approach entirely, its token-level supervision may actively conflict with the student's current path—encouraging revision tokens like "wait" and "but" rather than constructive continuation. Prior work recognized that teacher-student mismatch could be a problem (Agarwal et al., 2024 discuss the importance of on-policy sampling), but did not characterize the specific mechanism by which student prefixes distort the teacher's effective reasoning state. This paper's prefix-conditioned teacher evaluation (Section 5.1) provides concrete evidence: a Qwen3-14B teacher drops from 62.1% to 46.0% accuracy on GPQA-Diamond when continuing from student-generated prefixes rather than reasoning from scratch. That's a 26% relative degradation caused purely by the conditioning context. This means the token-level supervision in OPD is not just a noisy version of the teacher's full capability—it's systematically weakened by the student's own behavior.

### How This Paper Positions Itself Relative to Existing Work

The paper frames its contribution as a **mechanistic study** rather than a new method proposal. Unlike Agarwal et al. (2024), which introduced the OPD formulation and demonstrated its potential advantages; unlike Zhao et al. (2026), which proposed specific OPSD recipes for reasoning; and unlike Fu et al. (2026), which documented failure modes but did not fully trace them to specific mathematical mechanisms—this paper aims to explain *why* OPD and OPSD exhibit the behavior they do.

The positioning is explicit in the abstract:

> "we present a comprehensive empirical study of when OPD and OPSD work, when they fail, and why."

This is diagnostic work, not prescriptive. The proposed fixes—stop-gradient TopK KL, RLVR-adapted teachers, SFT stabilization—are presented as practical stabilizers that validate the identified mechanisms rather than as novel algorithmic contributions. If the TopK gradient bias is real, then correcting it (via stop-gradient or renormalization) should prevent collapse—and it does (Figures 11, 12). If teacher-student mismatch is a primary bottleneck, then bringing the teacher closer to the student distribution (via RLVR adaptation) should improve distillation—and it does, with the GRPO-trained 1.7B teacher outperforming the 8B teacher despite comparable standalone accuracy (Figure 13). If the student's initial inability to produce well-formed outputs makes teacher supervision meaningless, then SFT warm-up should stabilize training—and it does (Figure 14).

The paper also positions itself at the intersection of several related research threads. It draws on the context distillation literature (Snell et al., 2022) for the idea that in-context behaviors can be compressed into model parameters. It connects to reinforcement learning from textual feedback (Song et al., 2026) by studying how additional textual information (PI) affects learning. It relates to the KL regularization literature in RLHF (Ouyang et al., 2022) through its analysis of forward versus reverse KL divergences. But its specific focus—the mechanism of on-policy distillation, the structure of privileged information, and the gradient properties of truncated loss functions—represents a distinct analytical lens that prior work did not bring to bear systematically.

A key theoretical contribution that distinguishes this paper is the analysis of **what OPSD actually optimizes for**. Equation 11 shows that the optimal OPSD student is the normalized geometric mean of the PI-conditioned teacher distributions—a PI-free policy that aggregates across all possible privileged information. This is a crisp mathematical characterization that makes precise when OPSD should work and when it should fail. If different PI instances induce incompatible teacher behaviors, the geometric mean produces a weak consensus that loses the specific strengths of any individual teacher. If the PI instances all induce the same underlying behavior (e.g., "be concise," "be safe," "follow this character profile"), the geometric mean preserves that shared behavior. This analysis is not present in prior OPSD papers, which focused on empirical results without characterizing the optimization objective's structural properties.

The paper thus addresses the field's confusion not by championing OPD as universally beneficial or dismissing it as unreliable, but by drawing boundary conditions: **OPSD works when PI is a shared latent rule; it fails when PI is instance-specific**. The framework developed to reach this conclusion—the gradient analysis of TopK losses, the prefix-distortion measurement, the geometric-mean characterization of the OPSD optimum—provides tools that future work can use to diagnose and improve on-policy distillation in new settings, rather than having to rediscover these failure modes through trial and error.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader orientation (approachable technical breakdown)

This paper is an **empirical diagnostic study** that analyzes two on-policy training algorithms—OPD (student learns from a stronger teacher on its own sampled trajectories) and OPSD (student learns from itself conditioned on privileged information)—to understand why they sometimes succeed and sometimes fail catastrophically. The core idea is that three specific mechanisms cause failures: the teacher provides unreliable supervision when conditioned on student-generated prefixes, common TopK approximations to reverse KL introduce a systematic gradient bias that drives optimization toward degenerate outputs, and OPSD can only learn the average behavior across privileged information instances, which is useless when that information is instance-specific. The paper validates these mechanisms by showing that correcting each one—through stop-gradient TopK losses, adapting the teacher to be closer to the student's distribution via reinforcement learning, and stabilizing the student with supervised fine-tuning—prevents the corresponding failure.

### 3.2 Big-picture architecture (diagram in words)

The paper analyzes two on-policy distillation pipelines (OPD and OPSD) that share a common structure but differ in how the teacher is constructed. Both pipelines have five major components:

1. **Prompt dataset** (`$\mathcal{D}$`) — a collection of input prompts `$x$` (math problems, system-prompt tasks, or alignment scenarios) that define the training distribution.

2. **Student policy** (`$\pi_\theta$`) — the model being trained, typically a smaller model (e.g., Qwen3-1.7B) that generates responses autoregressively. At each training step, the student samples a complete trajectory `$y = (y_1, \ldots, y_T) \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x)$` from its own current parameters.

3. **Teacher policy** (`$\pi_T$`) — the model providing token-level supervision. **In OPD**, the teacher is a stronger external model (e.g., Qwen3-8B distilling into Qwen3-1.7B), frozen or occasionally adapted via RLVR. **In OPSD**, the teacher is the student itself conditioned on privileged information `$I$` (e.g., ground-truth answers, system prompts, character profiles), producing `$\pi_T(\cdot \mid x, y_{<t}, I)$`. The PI is available at training time but **not at test time**—the student must learn to produce the teacher's PI-conditioned behavior without seeing the PI.

4. **Distillation loss** (`$\mathcal{L}_{\text{OP(S)D}}$`) — a token-level objective that compares the student's next-token distribution `$\pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x, y_{<t})$` to the teacher's distribution `$\pi_T(\cdot \mid x, y_{<t}, I)$` at every prefix `$y_{<t}$` of every sampled trajectory. The paper studies several variants: full-vocabulary reverse KL, TopK-truncated reverse KL (with and without stop-gradient), renormalized TopK reverse KL, and sampled-token policy-gradient losses.

5. **Stabilizers (optional)** — three practical interventions that address specific failure mechanisms: **(a)** stop-gradient or renormalized TopK losses that correct the gradient bias, **(b)** RLVR-adapted teachers that are fine-tuned on the training distribution via GRPO before serving as distillation teachers, bringing their output distribution closer to the student's, and **(c)** SFT warm-up on teacher-generated traces that ensures the student produces well-formed outputs before OPD begins.

**Information flow at each training step:** A prompt `$x$` is sampled from `$\mathcal{D}$` → the student generates a trajectory `$y$` autoregressively from `$\pi_\theta$` → at each prefix `$y_{<t}$`, the teacher provides its next-token distribution `$\pi_T(\cdot \mid x, y_{<t}, I)$` (in OPSD, the teacher conditions on `$I$`; in OPD, `$I = \emptyset$` or is optional PI) → the distillation loss is computed token-wise, comparing student and teacher distributions → gradients flow back through the student parameters `$\theta$`, updating the policy. At test time, only the student is used, without any teacher or privileged information.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the deep dive

- **First**, the formal OPD/OPSD objective (Equation 1), which defines the core training loop and establishes notation—what the student generates, what the teacher provides, and how the per-token loss aggregates over trajectories. This is the foundation that all later analysis builds on.

- **Second**, the family of distillation loss functions (full-vocabulary reverse KL, TopK-truncated reverse KL, sampled-token KL, policy-gradient formulations), because the choice of loss is the primary design axis that determines stability—and the paper's central mechanistic finding is that TopK truncation introduces a non-canceling constant term that biases the gradient. Understanding each loss variant's gradient structure is essential for understanding why collapse happens and how the proposed fixes work.

- **Third**, the OPSD optimization objective and its closed-form optimum (Equation 11), which reveals that the student learns the normalized geometric mean of PI-conditioned teacher distributions. This structural analysis explains why OPSD succeeds when PI is a shared latent rule (the geometric mean preserves the common behavior) and fails when PI is instance-specific (the geometric mean discards the instance-specific information that makes each teacher effective).

- **Fourth**, the TopK implementation constraint (the SGLang API limitation that forces a global union of per-position TopK sets), because this is a practical engineering detail that affects memory cost and gradient correctness in ways that researchers implementing OPD need to understand.

- **Fifth**, the stabilizer mechanisms—stop-gradient TopK, RLVR teacher adaptation, SFT warm-up—each of which targets a specific identified failure mode. These are presented as evidence that the failure mechanisms are correctly diagnosed, not as novel algorithmic contributions.

### 3.4 Detailed, sentence-based technical breakdown

This is primarily a **mechanistic analysis paper** whose core idea is that OPD/OPSD failures arise from three specific, correctable mechanisms: prefix-induced teacher-student mismatch, biased TopK reverse-KL gradients, and the structural limitation of PI-free policy aggregation in OPSD. The paper develops this argument by (1) formally defining the training procedures, (2) analyzing the gradient structure of each loss variant to identify where bias enters, (3) characterizing the OPSD optimum to establish when aggregation across PI is helpful versus harmful, and (4) demonstrating that interventions targeting each mechanism prevent the corresponding failure.

#### The Core On-Policy Distillation Loop

The fundamental training procedure for both OPD and OPSD is defined by a single equation that captures the iterative process: the student generates, the teacher scores, and the student updates.

Let `$x \sim \mathcal{D}$` be an input prompt sampled from the training distribution `$\mathcal{D}$`. The student policy `$\pi_\theta$` (with parameters `$\theta$`) generates a complete response `$y = (y_1, y_2, \ldots, y_T)$` autoregressively—each token `$y_t$` is sampled from `$\pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x, y_{<t})$`, where `$y_{<t}$` denotes the prefix `$(y_1, \ldots, y_{t-1})$`. The teacher policy `$\pi_T$` provides a full next-token distribution `$\pi_T(\cdot \mid x, y_{<t}, I)$` at each prefix `$y_{<t}$`, where `$I$` is optional privileged information. **In OPD**, `$\pi_T$` is a stronger external model (e.g., Qwen3-8B) and `$I$` is typically empty (vanilla OPD) or may contain ground-truth answers or full responses (PI-conditioned OPD). **In OPSD**, `$\pi_T$` is the student model itself augmented with `$I$`—the teacher conditions on `$I$` but the student does not, so `$\pi_T(\cdot \mid x, y_{<t}, I) = \pi_{\bar{\theta}}(\cdot \mid x, y_{<t}, I)$` where `$\bar{\theta}$` is either the student's parameters at step 0 (frozen teacher) or tracked via exponential moving average (EMA teacher).

The training objective aggregates token-level losses over the student's own sampled trajectories:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{OP(S)D}} = \mathbb{E}_{x, y}\left[\frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^{T} \ell_t\left(\pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x, y_{<t}),\; \text{stopgrad}\left(\pi_T(\cdot \mid x, y_{<t}, I)\right)\right)\right]$$

where `$\ell_t$` is a per-token loss function (discussed below), `$T$` is the response length, the expectation is over prompts `$x \sim \mathcal{D}$` and student-generated trajectories `$y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x)$`, and `$\text{stopgrad}(\cdot)$` indicates that gradients do not flow through the teacher—the teacher is treated as a fixed target at each training step.

**What it computes:** at each training step, a batch of prompts is sampled. For each prompt, the student generates a complete response (one rollout per prompt by default; the paper uses rollout batch size 64, prompts per batch 64, and samples per prompt = 1 for OP(S)D). For each token position `$t$` in each response, the teacher's next-token distribution (conditioned on the prompt, the student's prefix so far, and any privileged information) is compared to the student's next-token distribution. The per-token loss `$\ell_t$` measures the discrepancy. These per-token losses are averaged over all positions in all responses, and the gradient of this average with respect to the student parameters `$\theta$` is computed and used to update the student via the Adam optimizer (learning rate `$2 \times 10^{-6}$`, cosine decay schedule, warmup fraction 0.1, weight decay 0.1, Adam betas 0.9/0.98).

**Why this form:** the on-policy sampling (student generates its own trajectories) ensures that the student is trained on the same distribution it will encounter at test time—its own outputs. This is the key distinction from off-policy distillation, where the student trains on teacher-generated responses and never practices on its own distribution. The token-level supervision (rather than sequence-level reward) provides dense feedback at every step, avoiding the credit assignment problem of scalar-reward RL. The stop-gradient on the teacher prevents the student from influencing the teacher's parameters, which would create an unstable moving-target problem (the student would be chasing a teacher that is also changing, which can lead to oscillation or collapse).

The paper uses a maximum rollout response length of 4096 tokens for OP(S)D (compared to 8192 for GRPO and PPO baselines), temperature 1.0, and top-p 0.95 for all methods. These are standard generation hyperparameters that encourage diversity in the sampled trajectories.

#### The Distillation Loss Landscape: Full-Vocabulary KL, TopK Variants, and Sampled-Token Estimators

The choice of per-token loss `$\ell_t$` is the primary design axis that determines whether OPD is stable or collapses. The paper analyzes four families of losses, each with different gradient properties. Understanding the gradient structure of each is essential because the paper's central mechanistic finding—the TopK reverse-KL bias—is a gradient-level phenomenon that cannot be seen from the loss value alone.

**Full-vocabulary reverse KL.**

The most principled choice for OPD is the full-vocabulary reverse Kullback-Leibler divergence between the student and teacher next-token distributions:

$$\ell_t = D_{\text{KL}}\left(\pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x, y_{<t}) \;\big\|\; \pi_T(\cdot \mid x, y_{<t}, I)\right) = \sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} \pi_\theta(v \mid x, y_{<t}) \log \frac{\pi_\theta(v \mid x, y_{<t})}{\pi_T(v \mid x, y_{<t}, I)}$$

where `$\mathcal{V}$` is the full vocabulary (typically 50,000–150,000 tokens), `$\pi_\theta(v \mid \cdot)$` is the student's probability for token `$v$`, and `$\pi_T(v \mid \cdot)$` is the teacher's probability.

**What it computes:** for each token `$v$` in the vocabulary, the student's probability `$\pi_\theta(v)$` is multiplied by the log-ratio `$\log(\pi_\theta(v)/\pi_T(v))$`. Tokens where the student assigns higher probability than the teacher contribute positively to the loss (penalizing the student for overconfidence relative to the teacher). Tokens where the student assigns lower probability contribute a mixed signal depending on the ratio. The sum over all vocabulary tokens gives a single non-negative scalar per position.

**Why this form:** reverse KL is **mode-seeking** rather than mode-covering. Minimizing `$D_{\text{KL}}(\pi_\theta \| \pi_T)$` encourages the student to concentrate its probability mass on tokens that the teacher assigns high probability to, while allowing the student to ignore teacher modes that it would assign low probability to. This is desirable in OPD because the student is typically a smaller model with less capacity than the teacher—forcing it to cover all teacher modes (which forward KL would do) would spread probability mass too thinly and lead to degraded performance. Reverse KL allows the student to focus on what it can represent well, which also helps mitigate catastrophic forgetting of the student's pre-existing capabilities.

The gradient of the full-vocabulary reverse KL with respect to the student logits (and thus parameters `$\theta$`) has a critical structural property. Let `$p_\theta(v) = \pi_\theta(v \mid x, y_{<t})$` and `$p_T(v) = \pi_T(v \mid x, y_{<t}, I)$`. The gradient is:

$$\nabla_\theta D_{\text{KL}}(p_\theta \| p_T) = \sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} p_\theta(v)\left[\log\frac{p_\theta(v)}{p_T(v)} + 1\right] \nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(v)$$

**Why the +1 term vanishes:** because `$\sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} p_\theta(v) = 1$` (the student distribution sums to one over the full vocabulary), we have `$\sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} p_\theta(v) \nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(v) = \nabla_\theta \sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} p_\theta(v) = \nabla_\theta 1 = 0$`. The constant `$+1$` term therefore contributes zero to the total gradient. This means the full-vocabulary gradient can be written equivalently as `$\sum_v p_\theta(v) [\log p_\theta(v) - \log p_T(v)] \nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(v)$`—the `$+1$` drops out.

**The problem with full-vocabulary KL in practice:** computing this loss requires evaluating `$\pi_\theta(v)$` and `$\pi_T(v)$` for every token in the vocabulary at every position. For a batch of 64 trajectories, each up to 4096 tokens long, with a vocabulary of ~150K tokens, the memory requirement to store the full distributions is `$64 \times 4096 \times 150\text{K} \times 2\text{ models} \times 4\text{ bytes} \approx 300\text{ GB}$` for float32 logits alone, which exceeds typical GPU memory. This motivates the use of TopK truncation, where only a subset of the vocabulary is considered.

**TopK-truncated reverse KL (unnormalized).**

The standard practical approximation is to restrict the KL computation to a TopK subset of the vocabulary, typically the `$K$` tokens with the highest student probabilities at each position (`$K = 20$` in most experiments, `$K = 5$` in the comparison experiments of Figures 11 and 12). Let `$\mathcal{S}_K(y_{<t}) \subset \mathcal{V}$` be this subset. The truncated loss is:

$$\ell_t^{\text{TopK}} = \sum_{v \in \mathcal{S}_K(y_{<t})} \pi_\theta(v \mid x, y_{<t}) \log \frac{\pi_\theta(v \mid x, y_{<t})}{\pi_T(v \mid x, y_{<t}, I)}$$

**What it computes:** the same sum as the full-vocabulary KL, but only over the `$K$` tokens in `$\mathcal{S}_K$`. Tokens outside this set are ignored entirely—their contribution to the loss is zero, and their student probabilities are not updated directly by this loss term.

**The critical gradient bias:** this is the paper's central mathematical finding. The gradient of the TopK-truncated reverse KL is:

$$\nabla_\theta \ell_t^{\text{TopK}} = \sum_{v \in \mathcal{S}_K(y_{<t})} \pi_\theta(v \mid x, y_{<t}) \left[\log\frac{\pi_\theta(v \mid x, y_{<t})}{\pi_T(v \mid x, y_{<t}, I)} + 1\right] \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(v \mid x, y_{<t})$$

Now the `$+1$` term **does not cancel**, because:

$$\sum_{v \in \mathcal{S}_K(y_{<t})} \pi_\theta(v \mid x, y_{<t}) \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(v \mid x, y_{<t}) = \nabla_\theta \sum_{v \in \mathcal{S}_K(y_{<t})} \pi_\theta(v \mid x, y_{<t}) \neq 0$$

The sum of student probabilities over the TopK subset is less than 1 (typically 0.4–0.9 depending on `$K$` and the entropy of the student distribution), so its gradient is not zero. The `$+1$` term therefore contributes a systematic bias to the gradient.

**What this bias does operationally:** the gradient promotes a token `$v$` (increases its logit) only when `$\log(p_T(v)/p_\theta(v)) > 1$`, i.e., when `$p_T(v) > e \cdot p_\theta(v)$` where `$e \approx 2.718$`. If the teacher prefers a token but only by a moderate margin—say, `$p_T(v) = 0.02$` and `$p_\theta(v) = 0.01$`, giving a ratio of 2.0—the log-ratio term `$\log(0.01/0.02) = -\log 2 \approx -0.693$` plus the `$+1$` gives `$+0.307$`, which is positive, so the gradient **increases** the student's probability for that token. This is correct behavior: the teacher prefers the token, so the student should move toward it.

But if `$p_T(v) = 0.015$` and `$p_\theta(v) = 0.01$`, the ratio is 1.5. The log-ratio is `$\log(0.01/0.015) = -\log 1.5 \approx -0.405$`, plus `$+1$` gives `$+0.595$`—still positive, still promoting. Now consider `$p_T(v) = 0.005$` and `$p_\theta(v) = 0.01$`. The ratio is 0.5. The log-ratio is `$\log(0.01/0.005) = \log 2 \approx 0.693$`, plus `$+1$` gives `$+1.693$`—**positive and even larger**, meaning the gradient **increases** the student's probability for a token the teacher actually disprefers relative to the student's current distribution. This happens because the `$+1$` dominates the log-ratio when the teacher-student probability gap is small (in either direction).

The net effect is that the unnormalized TopK reverse KL **suppresses teacher-preferred tokens with moderate probability margins** while **promoting tokens favored by neither model**—a bias that pushes the student away from the teacher distribution rather than toward it. This can create a feedback loop: as the student drifts from the teacher, the TopK set changes, different tokens get promoted, and the distribution becomes increasingly degenerate. Figure 4 documents the result: the student's repeat ratio (fraction of tokens that are part of a repeated n-gram) rises from ~0.35 at step 0 to ~0.99 by step 1000, and the model outputs degenerate into the single token "maybe" repeated hundreds of times. The teacher-student log-probability gap for non-repetitive tokens collapses (Figure 4, bottom-right), confirming that the student has essentially stopped attending to the teacher signal entirely.

**Renormalized TopK reverse KL.**

A partial fix is to renormalize the student and teacher distributions within the TopK set. Let:

$$\bar{\pi}_\theta(v) = \frac{\pi_\theta(v \mid x, y_{<t})}{\sum_{u \in \mathcal{S}_K(y_{<t})} \pi_\theta(u \mid x, y_{<t})}, \quad \bar{\pi}_T(v) = \frac{\pi_T(v \mid x, y_{<t}, I)}{\sum_{u \in \mathcal{S}_K(y_{<t})} \pi_T(u \mid x, y_{<t}, I)}$$

for all `$v \in \mathcal{S}_K(y_{<t})$`. The renormalized loss is:

$$\ell_t^{\text{Renorm}} = \sum_{v \in \mathcal{S}_K(y_{<t})} \bar{\pi}_\theta(v) \log\frac{\bar{\pi}_\theta(v)}{\bar{\pi}_T(v)}$$

**What it computes:** the standard reverse KL, but treating the TopK set as the entire vocabulary—both distributions are normalized to sum to 1 within `$\mathcal{S}_K$`.

**Why the `$+1$` now cancels:** because `$\sum_{v \in \mathcal{S}_K} \bar{\pi}_\theta(v) = 1$`, we have `$\nabla_\theta \sum_{v \in \mathcal{S}_K} \bar{\pi}_\theta(v) = 0$`, and the constant term in the gradient vanishes. The gradient becomes:

$$\nabla_\theta \ell_t^{\text{Renorm}} = \sum_{v \in \mathcal{S}_K(y_{<t})} \bar{\pi}_\theta(v) \left[\log\frac{\bar{\pi}_\theta(v)}{\bar{\pi}_T(v)}\right] \nabla_\theta \log \bar{\pi}_\theta(v)$$

which has the same structural property as the full-vocabulary gradient but restricted to the TopK set.

**Why this is only a partial fix:** renormalization corrects the gradient bias, but it changes what the loss actually measures. Instead of matching the full student distribution to the full teacher distribution, it matches **relative probabilities within the TopK set only**. The probability mass assigned to tokens outside the TopK set is entirely ignored. This means the loss no longer faithfully approximates the full-vocabulary reverse KL—it approximates a different objective (reverse KL on a truncated and renormalized support). In practice, as shown in Figure 11, the renormalized TopK loss is stable and achieves comparable performance to the stop-gradient variant, suggesting that the gradient bias (not the renormalization per se) is the primary cause of instability.

**Stop-gradient TopK reverse KL.**

The paper's preferred fix is to modify the unnormalized TopK loss by stopping gradients through the student's own log-probability in the log-ratio term. The objective is:

$$\ell_t^{\text{SG-TopK}} = -\sum_{v \in \mathcal{S}_K(y_{<t})} \pi_\theta(v \mid x, y_{<t}) \left[\log \pi_T(v \mid x, y_{<t}, I) - \text{stopgrad}\left(\log \pi_\theta(v \mid x, y_{<t})\right)\right]$$

**What it computes:** this is algebraically equivalent to the unnormalized TopK reverse KL (the sum over `$v$` of `$\pi_\theta(v) [-\log\pi_T(v) + \log\pi_\theta(v)]$` is the same as `$\pi_\theta(v) \log(\pi_\theta(v)/\pi_T(v))$`), but the stop-gradient operator ensures that the `$\log\pi_\theta(v)$` term inside the brackets does not contribute gradients.

**Why this fixes the bias:** the gradient of this objective is:

$$\nabla_\theta \ell_t^{\text{SG-TopK}} = -\sum_{v \in \mathcal{S}_K(y_{<t})} \pi_\theta(v \mid x, y_{<t}) \left[\log \pi_T(v \mid x, y_{<t}, I) - \log \pi_\theta(v \mid x, y_{<t})\right] \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(v \mid x, y_{<t})$$

The `$+1$` term from the derivative of `$\log(\pi_\theta/\pi_T)$` is gone because we never differentiated through the `$\log\pi_\theta$` inside the brackets. The weighting term `$[\log\pi_T - \log\pi_\theta]$` is treated as a constant advantage—it determines how much to promote or suppress each token, but its own dependence on `$\theta$` is not differentiated. This is exactly the form of a policy gradient: an advantage-weighted sum of log-probability gradients. The gradient promotes tokens where the teacher log-probability exceeds the student log-probability (positive advantage), and suppresses tokens where the opposite holds (negative advantage).

**Connection to policy gradient:** this formulation is conceptually similar to treating the teacher-student log-probability gap `$\log\pi_T(v) - \log\pi_\theta(v)$` as a token-level advantage. The full-vocabulary reverse KL gradient (with the `$+1$` canceled) also has this form, but the stop-gradient TopK version explicitly enforces it for the truncated set. This connection is why the paper describes this variant as "better aligned with the policy-gradient view of on-policy distillation."

**Empirical validation:** Figure 11 compares three TopK variants with `$K=5$` using a Qwen3-1.7B-GRPO teacher and a Qwen3-1.7B student on the DAPO dataset. The unnormalized reverse KL collapses (MATH-500 accuracy drops to near zero after step 400), while both the stop-gradient and renormalized variants maintain stable training with MATH-500 accuracy around 0.55–0.65. The repeat ratio under unnormalized reverse KL rises to ~0.35 (and would likely continue rising if training weren't stopped) while remaining near 0.05 for the stable variants. Figure 12 shows that for `$K=1$` (which reduces to the sampled-token formulation), the unnormalized version collapses while the stop-gradient version and the policy-gradient formulation (Equation 4 below) both remain stable.

**Sampled-token KL (policy gradient form).**

A fourth option is to bypass the vocabulary-level loss entirely and instead use only the token actually sampled by the student. Let `$y_t$` be the token the student generated at position `$t$`. The sampled-token objective treats the teacher-student log-probability gap as an advantage for that specific token:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{PG}} = -\mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_\theta} \sum_t \text{stopgrad}\left(\log \pi_T(y_t \mid x, y_{<t}, I) - \log \pi_\theta(y_t \mid x, y_{<t})\right) \log \pi_\theta(y_t \mid x, y_{<t})$$

**What it computes:** for each position, compute the difference between the teacher's log-probability and the student's log-probability for the token `$y_t$` that was actually generated. Stop the gradient through this difference. Then increase the student's log-probability for `$y_t$` in proportion to this difference (via the `$\log\pi_\theta(y_t)$` term).

**Gradient structure:** the gradient is:

$$\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\text{PG}} = -\mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_\theta} \sum_t \text{stopgrad}\left(\log \pi_T(y_t \mid \cdot) - \log \pi_\theta(y_t \mid \cdot)\right) \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_t \mid \cdot)$$

This is a pure policy gradient: the advantage `$A_t = \log\pi_T(y_t) - \log\pi_\theta(y_t)$` weights the log-probability gradient for the sampled token. The advantage is positive when the teacher assigns higher probability to the token than the student did (the student should have been more confident in this token), and negative when the student was overconfident (the student should reduce probability for this token). Because only the sampled token is updated, the effect is to shift probability mass toward tokens the teacher prefers and away from tokens the student over-assigns—but only for tokens that actually appear in the student's trajectories.

**Why this avoids the TopK bias:** there is no truncation to a subset `$\mathcal{S}_K$` and no `$+1$` term to begin with. The gradient is computed from the sampled token alone, so there is no constant-term cancellation issue. The cost is sparsity: only one token per position receives a gradient update, whereas full-vocabulary or TopK methods update many tokens' probabilities simultaneously. This makes sampled-token KL potentially slower to converge but intrinsically stable.

**Empirical comparison:** Figure 12 shows that for `$K=1$` (equivalent to sampled-token), the unnormalized TopK reverse KL collapses (just as with larger `$K$`), while both the stop-gradient TopK and the explicit policy-gradient formulation (Equation 4) remain stable. The policy-gradient formulation achieves comparable MATH-500 accuracy to the stop-gradient variant (both around 0.65–0.70 at step 3000), with somewhat noisier training curves typical of policy gradient methods. Response lengths are also stable under both stable variants.

**Design choice summary:** the paper's default for all experiments (unless explicitly varying the loss) is the **stop-gradient, Top20 reverse KL** objective (Equation 12). This is chosen because it (1) avoids the `$+1$` gradient bias through stop-gradient, (2) retains the efficiency of TopK truncation (doesn't require full-vocabulary computation), (3) provides richer supervision than sampled-token methods by updating multiple tokens per position, and (4) showed stable empirical performance across the tested settings. The TopK set `$K=20$` is used as the default; experiments in Figure 11 use `$K=5$` for comparison, and Figure 15 shows that increasing to `$K=20$` does not eliminate the collapse for the unnormalized variant—the stop-gradient fix is necessary regardless of `$K$`.

#### What OPSD Actually Optimizes: The PI-Free Policy as a Geometric Mean

OPSD has a fundamentally different structure from OPD because the teacher is not an independent model but a version of the student conditioned on privileged information `$I$`. At training time, for each prompt `$x$`, the teacher `$\pi_T(\cdot \mid x, I)$` sees both the prompt and the PI, while the student `$\pi_S(\cdot \mid x)$` sees only the prompt. At test time, the PI is unavailable, so the student must produce behavior that approximates what the teacher would do with PI—but without seeing it.

The paper formalizes this by writing the OPSD objective as a KL minimization over the joint distribution of `$(x, I)$`:

$$\min_{\pi_S} \mathbb{E}_{(x, I) \sim \mathcal{D}} \left[ D_{\text{KL}}\left(\pi_S(\cdot \mid x) \;\big\|\; \pi_T(\cdot \mid x, I)\right) \right]$$

**What this says:** find a single PI-free student policy `$\pi_S$` that, on average over all prompts and their associated privileged information, has low reverse KL divergence to the PI-conditioned teacher.

**The closed-form optimum:** the paper derives the optimal student policy `$\pi_S^*$` for this objective. The derivation (not shown in detail in the paper, but the result is Equation 11) yields:

$$\pi_S^*(y \mid x) = \frac{\exp\left(\mathbb{E}_{I \sim \mathcal{D}(\cdot \mid x)} \log \pi_T(y \mid x, I)\right)}{\sum_{y'} \exp\left(\mathbb{E}_{I \sim \mathcal{D}(\cdot \mid x)} \log \pi_T(y' \mid x, I)\right)}$$

where `$\mathcal{D}(\cdot \mid x)$` is the conditional distribution of PI given the prompt `$x$`.

**What this computes:** the optimal student policy is the **normalized geometric mean** of the PI-conditioned teacher distributions. For each response `$y$`, we take the teacher's log-probability of `$y$` under each possible PI `$I$`, average these log-probabilities over the distribution of PI given `$x$`, exponentiate, and normalize across all possible responses. The geometric mean (arithmetic mean in log-space) tends to emphasize responses that receive consistently high probability across all PI instances, while suppressing responses that receive high probability under some PI but low probability under others.

**Why this explains when OPSD works versus fails:** the geometric mean preserves information that is **shared across PI instances** and discards information that is **instance-specific**.

- **When PI is a shared latent rule** (e.g., a system prompt saying "be concise," a character profile, an alignment preference): different prompts may have different specific instantiations of the rule, but the underlying behavior induced by the PI is the same across instances. The geometric mean preserves this shared behavior because every PI-conditioned teacher assigns high probability to responses that satisfy the rule. The student compresses this into a PI-free policy that produces the desired behavior at test time. This is precisely what happens in the system-prompt internalization experiments (Figures 6, 7): the student learns to produce concise reasoning or safe responses without seeing the system prompt, because all PI-conditioned teachers share the same underlying preference.

- **When PI is instance-specific** (e.g., the ground-truth answer to a specific math problem): each PI `$I$` induces a teacher that assigns high probability to responses containing that specific answer. But different math problems have different answers, so the PI-conditioned teachers for different prompts assign high probability to **different, incompatible responses**. The geometric mean across these teachers produces a policy that assigns moderate probability to responses that are "generically correct-looking" but not actually correct for any specific problem—because the specific answer that makes one teacher confident is suppressed by the other teachers that don't see that answer. The resulting PI-free policy is substantially weaker than any individual PI-conditioned teacher.

**Concrete example from Figure 9:** in math reasoning with answer-PI, the teacher for problem A sees PI "answer = 42" and assigns high probability to responses concluding "42". The teacher for problem B sees PI "answer = 17" and assigns high probability to responses concluding "17". The geometric mean across these teachers assigns intermediate probability to both "42" and "17" (and similar probability to many other numbers, since no number is consistently preferred), resulting in a policy that is no better than random guessing. In contrast, with a "be concise" PI, the teacher for problem A (with conciseness instruction) and the teacher for problem B (with conciseness instruction) both assign high probability to responses that are short and to-the-point—this shared preference survives the geometric mean, producing a PI-free student that is concise.

**Why richer PI can hurt more:** Figure 10 shows that in OPD with a stronger teacher (Qwen3-8B distilling into Qwen3-1.7B), adding PI actually **degrades** performance relative to vanilla OPD. Full-response PI performs worse than answer-only PI, which performs worse than no PI. The paper explains this through the distillation loss at step 0: OPD with full-response PI has the highest initial KL loss, followed by answer-PI, then vanilla OPD. A longer PI induces a larger distributional shift in the teacher's output (the teacher conditioned on a full correct solution produces very different token distributions than an unconditioned teacher), which increases the mismatch between teacher and student distributions. This larger mismatch means the student must change more to match the teacher—but since the PI-conditioned teacher's behavior is not actually useful without the PI (the student can't see the answer at test time), this change is at best wasted effort and at worst actively harmful, pushing the student toward an unattainable target distribution.

**Empirical validation of the geometric-mean analysis:** Figure 3 shows that OPSD with answer-only PI or full-response PI fails to improve the student on MATH-500, AIME24, or AIME25—performance is flat or declining over 1750 training steps, while GRPO (which optimizes a scalar reward directly) improves steadily. Even when the teacher is itself trained with GRPO under full-response PI (purple line in Figure 3), OPSD still fails—the PI-conditioned teacher may be stronger in absolute terms, but the PI is still instance-specific, so the geometric mean still discards the critical information. This is strong evidence that the failure is structural (due to the geometric-mean aggregation) rather than due to weak teachers.

By contrast, Figures 5, 6, and 7 show OPSD succeeding on alignment, character consistency, reasoning compression, and safety—all tasks where the PI induces a shared latent behavior (style, conciseness, safety preference) that survives the geometric mean.

#### The TopK Implementation Constraint: SGLang's Per-Position API Limitation

A practical engineering detail affects how TopK distillation can be implemented efficiently. The paper describes a constraint imposed by the SGLang inference engine that forces a specific design choice in the TopK computation.

**The ideal approach:** at each token position `$t$` in the student's generated response, we would like to query the teacher for its log-probabilities on the student's position-specific TopK set `$\mathcal{S}_{\text{stu}, K}(y_{<t})$`—the `$K$` tokens with the highest student probability at that exact prefix. This would be efficient: `$K$` teacher queries per position, for a total of `$T \times K$` queries per response.

**The SGLang constraint:** the SGLang API's `token_ids_logprob` interface expects a single flat list of token IDs that is applied uniformly across all positions. It does not support position-dependent token lists. Specifically, the reward function sets `payload["token_ids_logprob"]` to the requested TopK token IDs, organized as a per-position list `[[K IDs at position 0], [K IDs at position 1], ...]`. But SGLang's `compute_logprobs_only` path (in `tp_worker.py`) calls `get_token_ids_logprobs_batch_optimized` with a position-independent expectation, and the constructed column index tensor becomes `torch.tensor([list_K, list_K, ...])` with shape `[L, K]` instead of the expected one-dimensional `[L]`, causing a gather operation failure due to shape mismatch.

**The practical fix:** the paper flattens the per-position TopK token IDs into a single sorted set of unique token IDs—the union of all per-position TopK sets:

$$\mathcal{U} = \bigcup_{t=1}^{T} \mathcal{S}_{\text{stu}, K}(y_{<t})$$

This flat union is sent as `token_ids_logprob`, and SGLang computes log-probabilities for all tokens in `$\mathcal{U}$` at all positions, producing a tensor of shape `[seq_len, |U|]`. The relevant teacher log-probabilities for each position can then be extracted from this shared tensor.

**The cost:** in the worst case, the TopK sets at different positions are disjoint, so `$|\mathcal{U}| = \min(|\mathcal{V}|, T \times K)$`—the union size grows with response length and could approach the full vocabulary for long responses. This multiplies memory and computation by a factor of `$|\mathcal{U}| / K$` relative to the ideal position-specific approach. For a response of length 4096 with `$K=20$`, if the TopK sets are fully disjoint, `$|\mathcal{U}| = 81,920$`, which approaches typical vocabulary sizes (e.g., Qwen3's vocabulary is ~150K tokens).

**Mitigation:** the paper backpropagates only through tokens that appear in **both** the teacher's and the student's TopK sets:

$$\mathcal{S}_K(y_{<t}) = \mathcal{S}_{\text{tea}, K}(y_{<t}) \cap \mathcal{S}_{\text{stu}, K}(y_{<t})$$

This reduces the effective TopK set at each position to tokens that both models consider high-probability, which is a natural choice for distillation anyway—tokens that the teacher considers unlikely are unlikely to provide useful supervision. The paper cites prior work (Li et al., 2026) finding that the teacher-student TopK intersection performs comparably to the student TopK alone, suggesting this design does not degrade training effectiveness.

This implementation constraint is not a core contribution of the paper but is an important practical detail for anyone implementing OPD with SGLang (or similar inference engines with position-independent log-probability APIs). It explains why the TopK set is constructed as a position-independent union rather than position-specific, which affects both memory scaling and the theoretical properties of the truncated loss.

#### Stabilizer 1: Stop-Gradient and Renormalized TopK Losses

The paper presents the stop-gradient TopK loss (described in detail above) as the primary fix for the TopK gradient bias. Figure 11 provides the empirical validation: three TopK variants with `$K=5$` are compared on the DAPO dataset using a Qwen3-1.7B-GRPO teacher (no thinking mode) and a Qwen3-1.7B student.

**The unnormalized reverse KL** (Equation 6, labeled "OPD reverseKL topk=5") collapses: MATH-500 evaluation accuracy drops from ~0.75 at step 0 to near zero by step 600. The OPD explicit loss (the training loss value) increases sharply from ~0.05 to ~0.15 around step 400—the point of collapse. The repeat token ratio rises from ~0.35 to ~0.55 in the same window. The teacher-student log-probability gap for repetitive tokens becomes strongly positive (~0.15–0.20 after step 400), meaning the teacher assigns higher log-probability to repetitive tokens than the student expects—the TopK bias is promoting tokens that create repetition.

**The stop-gradient version** (Equation 12, labeled "OPD reverseKL topk=5 stopgrad") and the **renormalized version** (Equation 15, labeled "OPD reverseKL normalize topk=5") both maintain stable training: MATH-500 accuracy stays in the 0.55–0.65 range, the OPD explicit loss remains negative and stable, the repeat ratio stays below 0.1, and training continues without collapse.

**The teacher log-probability tells the story:** under the unnormalized loss, the teacher's average log-probability across all tokens declines from ~−0.05 at step 0 to ~−0.25 by step 800—the teacher is becoming less confident in the student's outputs. Under the stable variants, the teacher log-probability remains in the −0.15 to −0.10 range. This confirms that the unnormalized TopK gradient is driving the student into regions of the output space where even the teacher considers the outputs unlikely—exactly what the biased gradient analysis predicts.

**Figure 12 extends this to the `$K=1$` case.** The unnormalized TopK reverse KL with `$K=1$` (which reduces to the sampled-token objective without stop-gradient) still collapses: MATH-500 accuracy drops from ~0.70 to near zero by step 2000. The stop-gradient TopK `$K=1$` and the explicit policy-gradient formulation both remain stable, with MATH-500 accuracy around 0.65–0.75. This shows that the bias is present at all `$K$` values—the `$+1$` term doesn't vanish as `$K$` gets smaller; it only vanishes when `$K=|\mathcal{V}|$` (full vocabulary), where the probabilities sum to one, or when stop-gradient is applied.

**A subtle point about `$K=1$` and the policy gradient connection:** when `$K=1$`, the TopK set contains only the single token the student sampled (`$y_t$`). The unnormalized TopK reverse KL at position `$t$` is then:

$$\ell_t = \pi_\theta(y_t) \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_t)}{\pi_T(y_t)}$$

The gradient includes the `$+1$` term: `$\pi_\theta(y_t)[\log(\pi_\theta/\pi_T) + 1]\nabla_\theta \log\pi_\theta(y_t)$`. The stop-gradient version removes the `$+1$`, yielding the advantage-weighted form. The policy-gradient version (Equation 4) is algebraically different (the advantage is not multiplied by `$\pi_\theta(y_t)$`) but produces gradients that empirically behave similarly (both stable). This is because the `$\pi_\theta(y_t)$` factor in the TopK formulation is a scalar multiplier on the gradient, which changes the effective learning rate per token but doesn't introduce the systematic directional bias that the `$+1$` does.

#### Stabilizer 2: RLVR-Adapted Teachers Reduce Distribution Mismatch

The paper's second stabilizer targets the prefix-induced teacher-student mismatch identified in Section 5.1. The idea is simple: if the teacher's output distribution is closer to the student's, then the token-level supervision on student-generated prefixes will be more locally compatible—the teacher is less likely to want to "redirect" the student's trajectory because the teacher itself would have taken a similar path.

**The procedure:** before using a model as a distillation teacher, fine-tune it on the same training distribution using RLVR (specifically GRPO, which is the Group Relative Policy Optimization variant used in DAPO). This improves the teacher's task performance and, importantly, **moves its output distribution closer to the student's distribution** because both models are now optimized for the same task on the same data. The paper trains a Qwen3-1.7B model with DAPO for 200 steps, obtaining a teacher denoted Qwen3-1.7B-GRPO.

**The key result (Figure 13):** the Qwen3-1.7B-GRPO teacher and the Qwen3-8B teacher have comparable standalone math reasoning performance—both achieve approximately 82–83% on MATH-500, 28–30% on AIME24, and 20–22% on AIME25 (Figure 13, left). Despite this, **distillation from the 1.7B-GRPO teacher substantially outperforms distillation from the 8B teacher** when both are used to train a fresh Qwen3-1.7B student on the OpenThoughts dataset. On MATH-500, OPD with the 1.7B-GRPO teacher reaches ~0.70 task reward by step 2000, while OPD with the 8B teacher reaches only ~0.55. On AIME24 and AIME25, the 1.7B-GRPO teacher leads by similar margins.

**Why this happens:** the paper measures the Top20 vocabulary overlap between teacher and student (Figure 13, right). The 1.7B-GRPO teacher's Top20 vocabulary distribution has consistently higher overlap with the Qwen3-1.7B student (around 0.75–0.85) compared to the 8B teacher (around 0.65–0.75). This is a direct measure of distributional similarity: when the student's Top20 most probable tokens overlap more with the teacher's Top20, the teacher's token-level supervision is more aligned with the student's natural tendencies. The teacher is less likely to assign high probability to tokens the student would never consider, reducing the local semantic conflicts documented in Figure 8.

**The implication for teacher selection:** teacher capability (standalone benchmark accuracy) is not the sole—or even primary—determinant of distillation effectiveness. A teacher that is closer to the student in terms of model family, scale, and training distribution can provide more effective supervision even if it is not strictly "stronger" in absolute terms. This is because the on-policy nature of OPD means the teacher must provide useful feedback from the student's own prefixes, and a teacher from a different distribution may find those prefixes unnatural or misguided.

**Practical recommendation:** when using OPD, prefer a teacher that has been fine-tuned on the target task (via RL or SFT) over a larger but task-generic teacher. The RLVR adaptation serves double duty: it improves task performance AND reduces distribution mismatch. This is a concrete, actionable finding that contrasts with the common intuition that "bigger teacher = better distillation."

**Contrast with PI-conditioned OPD:** Figure 10 shows that adding PI (answer or full response) to the 8B teacher makes distillation **worse**, not better. This is the opposite of the RLVR-adaptation effect: PI **increases** distribution mismatch (higher initial KL loss) while RLVR-adaptation **decreases** it (higher TopK overlap). This underscores that the mechanism is distributional alignment, not just teacher strength.

#### Stabilizer 3: SFT Warm-Up Prevents Degenerate Student Outputs

The third stabilizer addresses a failure mode specific to smaller or less capable student models: when the student is initialized from a base pretrained model (not instruction-tuned), its initial outputs on complex tasks may be garbled, nonsensical, or malformed—containing non-English Unicode characters, degenerate sequences with little semantic structure, or responses that don't even attempt to solve the problem. In this regime, the teacher's token-level supervision is meaningless because there is no coherent reasoning for the teacher to improve upon. The student is producing noise, and the teacher's feedback on noise doesn't help the student learn to produce signal.

**The procedure:** (1) Generate a dataset of correct, well-formed responses from the teacher (Qwen3-4B, no thinking mode) on a subset of the training prompts. For the experiment in Figure 14, 30,000 prompts from OpenThoughts are used for SFT data generation, with the teacher generating one response per prompt at temperature 0.3, filtered to keep only correctly answered samples (up to 20,000). (2) Fine-tune the student (Qwen3-1.7B-Base) on these teacher-generated traces for 2 epochs using standard SFT (learning rate `$1 \times 10^{-5}$`, cosine decay, warmup ratio 0.05). (3) After SFT, proceed with standard OPD using the original teacher on the remaining prompts.

**The effect on student quality (Table 3):** SFT reduces the student's negative log-likelihood (NLL) on teacher-generated traces from 0.640 to 0.335, and perplexity (PPL) from 1.896 to 1.397. This means the student's output distribution becomes much closer to the teacher's—the student no longer produces garbled outputs because it has learned to produce well-formed reasoning traces that the teacher can provide meaningful feedback on.

**The effect on OPD (Figure 14):** direct OPD from the base student (without SFT warm-up) shows fluctuating and unstable performance—MATH-500 task reward oscillates around 0.3–0.5 and doesn't show a clear improving trend over 250,000 training samples. The OPD explicit loss also fluctuates without clear convergence, and response lengths are highly variable. With SFT warm-up, OPD shows steady improvement: MATH-500 task reward increases from ~0.35 to ~0.55 over the course of training, the OPD loss decreases smoothly, and evaluation response lengths stabilize around 1250–1500 tokens.

**Why this works:** SFT provides a **well-formed initialization** for the student's output space. The student learns the basic format of mathematical reasoning (step-by-step reasoning leading to a boxed answer) before OPD begins. This means that when OPD starts, the student is already producing outputs that the teacher can evaluate meaningfully—the teacher's token-level feedback is about improving the quality of reasoning, not about teaching the student to produce coherent text in the first place. Without SFT, OPD is trying to simultaneously teach format and content, which is an unstable optimization problem because the teacher signal on malformed outputs is essentially random noise.

**When SFT is necessary versus when it's not:** this stabilizer is most important when the student is a base model (not instruction-tuned) and when the gap between student and teacher is large (4B teacher, 1.7B student). For instruction-tuned students (e.g., Qwen3-1.7B-Instruct), the model already produces well-formed outputs, and SFT warm-up may be unnecessary. The paper's math reasoning OPD experiments in Figures 4 and 13 use a Qwen3-1.7B student (not base) without SFT warm-up and achieve stable training, consistent with this interpretation.

The paper notes that this finding is consistent with prior work: Luo et al. (2023) combined OPD with SFT on gold answers to mitigate length inflation, and Li et al. (2026) fine-tuned the student on teacher-generated traces to reduce teacher-student mismatch. The contribution here is framing SFT warm-up specifically as a **regularizer of the student's output space** that ensures on-policy samples remain in regions where teacher feedback is informative—distinct from using SFT to directly improve task performance.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: The TopK Reverse-KL Gradient Bias as a First-Class Failure Mechanism

Prior to this work, practitioners using TopK truncation for reverse-KL distillation were operating under an implicit but incorrect assumption: that truncating the vocabulary to a subset of high-probability tokens is a benign approximation that reduces memory cost without qualitatively changing the optimization dynamics. This assumption was widespread—Fu et al. (2026) documented instability but attributed it to general optimization difficulties, Self-Distilled Reasoner (Zhao et al., 2026) used TopK approximations without analyzing their gradient properties, and SDPO (Shenfeld et al., 2026) introduced tail-augmented reverse KL without recognizing that the core issue was a non-canceling constant term in the gradient.

The paper's distinctive contribution is to identify that this is not a quantitative approximation error but a **structural gradient pathology**. The +1 term in the reverse-KL gradient cancels over the full vocabulary because `$\sum_v \pi_\theta(v) = 1$`—a property that depends on the sum being exactly one. Truncation to a TopK subset breaks this sum condition, and the residual +1 term becomes a systematic bias that suppresses teacher-preferred tokens with moderate probability margins while promoting tokens favored by neither model. This is not an approximation error that shrinks as K increases (Figure 15 shows collapse even with K=20); it persists at any K < |V|. The mechanism is mathematical, not empirical—it follows necessarily from the truncation operation regardless of dataset, model, or task.

What makes this a genuine innovation rather than an obvious observation is that **the direction of the bias is counterintuitive**. One might expect truncation to introduce zero-mean noise around the true gradient. Instead, it introduces a directed bias that pushes the student away from the teacher distribution, toward low-probability continuations, and ultimately toward degenerate outputs like the "maybe maybe maybe" collapse in Figure 4. The threshold effect—tokens are promoted only when `$p_T(v) > e \cdot p_\theta(v)$`—creates a specific, non-obvious failure signature that explains why the collapse manifests as repetition rather than, say, random gibberish: repetitive tokens are precisely those where the teacher's probability on the true continuation drops below the student's by a factor of e, so the biased gradient stops reinforcing the correct path and starts reinforcing tokens that keep the student in familiar territory.

The broader significance extends beyond OPD. Any method that uses truncated reverse KL for distribution matching—including KL-regularized RL, variational inference with truncated support, or any distillation pipeline using TopK approximations—inherits this bias. The paper's identification of the specific mathematical mechanism (the non-canceling constant term) provides a diagnostic criterion: if your loss involves a sum of probabilities times a log-ratio over a truncated set, check whether the probability sum is exactly one. If not, expect biased gradients. This is a conceptual tool that transfers across methods, not a fix specific to this paper's training setup.

The stop-gradient fix (Equation 12) is less important as a method than as **validation of the diagnosis**. The fact that simply preventing differentiation through the student log-probability term eliminates the collapse (Figures 11, 12, 15) confirms that the +1 term—not some other property of TopK truncation, not an interaction with learning rate or batch size, not a teacher quality issue—is the causal mechanism. This is clean mechanistic science: hypothesize a specific gradient-level cause, design a minimal intervention that eliminates only that cause, and observe whether the pathology disappears. The collapse does disappear, and the fix transfers across K values and model sizes. This kind of rigorous causal attribution, as opposed to ablation-by-removing-components, is rare in LLM training analysis and represents a methodological contribution in its own right.

### Innovation 2: The Geometric-Mean Characterization of What OPSD Actually Optimizes

The paper's second distinctive conceptual move is to derive and interpret the closed-form optimum of the OPSD objective (Equation 11), revealing that the student learns the normalized geometric mean of PI-conditioned teacher distributions. This is not a new equation—anyone could derive it by setting the functional derivative of the OPSD objective to zero—but **the interpretation and its consequences are what's novel**. Prior OPSD papers (Zhao et al., 2026; Sang et al., 2026; Shenfeld et al., 2026) treated the method as a black-box training procedure and evaluated whether it worked empirically. None characterized the structural property of the optimum or used it to predict when OPSD would succeed or fail.

The key insight is that the geometric mean **preserves information that is consistently supported across PI instances and discards information that varies across instances**. This gives a crisp, predictive criterion: OPSD will succeed when PI induces a shared latent behavior (system prompt, style preference, alignment rule) and fail when PI is instance-specific (ground-truth answers, problem-specific reasoning paths). This criterion is not empirical post-hoc rationalization—it follows from the mathematical structure of the optimum, and it makes testable predictions that the paper validates. The prediction that richer PI (full responses vs. answer-only) should hurt more in math reasoning is confirmed in Figure 10, where the distillation loss at step 0 is highest for full-response PI, consistent with larger distributional shift across instances of the PI.

What distinguishes this from typical "failure mode" papers is that it explains **both success and failure within a single framework**. The same optimum that predicts failure on math reasoning also predicts success on system-prompt internalization and alignment. When PI is "be concise," the geometric mean preserves the conciseness preference because all PI-conditioned teachers share it. When PI is "the answer is 42" versus "the answer is 17," the geometric mean averages away the specific answer, producing a policy that is no better than random. This dual prediction—success on shared-rule PI, failure on instance-specific PI—is what makes the analysis powerful. It's not just saying "OPSD doesn't work for math" (which could be an artifact of hyperparameters or model scale) but explaining *why* through a structural property of the optimization objective.

The analysis also clarifies a confusion in prior work about **why self-distillation sometimes degrades reasoning** (Kim et al., 2026). The geometric-mean characterization shows that the degradation is not due to optimization instability or teacher weakness—it's due to the student learning a PI-free policy that aggregates incompatible teacher behaviors. Even with a perfect teacher, strong optimization, and stable training, the student's optimum on instance-specific PI tasks is necessarily weaker than any individual PI-conditioned teacher. This is a fundamental limitation, not a fixable implementation issue, and it draws a sharp boundary around the class of tasks where OPSD can be expected to help.

### Innovation 3: The Prefix-Conditioned Teacher as a Diagnostic for Distribution Mismatch

The third conceptual contribution is the simple but powerful diagnostic of measuring **how much student-generated prefixes degrade teacher capability**. The paper reports that a Qwen3-14B teacher's accuracy on GPQA-Diamond drops from 62.1% to 46.0%—a 26% relative degradation—when continuing from Qwen3-1.7B student prefixes rather than reasoning from scratch. This is not just a performance number; it's a direct measurement of the core tension in on-policy distillation.

The field's prior understanding of teacher-student mismatch in OPD was qualitative: Agarwal et al. (2024) argued on-policy sampling is important because off-policy data causes distribution shift, and various papers noted that teachers and students have different capabilities. But there was no crisp quantitative measurement of *how much* the teacher is impaired by conditioning on student prefixes, and no framework for predicting when this impairment would dominate over the benefits of dense token-level supervision.

The prefix-conditioned teacher evaluation provides exactly this. It shows that the teacher's effective capability during OPD is **systematically lower than its standalone capability**, and the degradation is asymmetric: of the 123 problems the teacher solves independently, 40 become wrong when continuing from student prefixes, while only 8 wrong answers become correct—a 5:1 hurt-to-help ratio. This asymmetry means that even if the teacher's token-level supervision is correct on average, it's **correct about the wrong thing**: it's reliably steering the student through reasoning paths the student has already committed to, rather than guiding the student toward the paths the teacher would have chosen.

The concept generalizes beyond the specific numbers. Any OPD practitioner can run this diagnostic: take a held-out set, generate student prefixes, have the teacher continue from them, and compare to standalone teacher accuracy. If the degradation is large (as it is here), then no amount of optimization tuning will make OPD work well—the signal is fundamentally impaired. If the degradation is small (suggesting the student and teacher reason similarly), then OPD is likely to provide useful supervision. This transforms teacher selection from "pick the strongest model" to "pick the model whose reasoning distribution is most compatible with the student's," directly motivating the RLVR-adapted teacher finding in Figure 13.

### Innovation 4: The Unified Failure-Mechanism Framework and Its Stabilization-by-Mechanism Validation

The paper's fourth contribution is methodological: rather than proposing a new training recipe and benchmarking it, it **identifies specific causal mechanisms for failure, proposes minimal interventions targeting each mechanism, and validates the diagnosis by showing the intervention works**. This structure—mechanism → targeted fix → empirical confirmation—is the paper's intellectual architecture, not any single finding.

The three mechanisms (prefix-induced mismatch, TopK gradient bias, OPSD's PI-free optimum) are independent in the sense that each can cause failure on its own, and each has a corresponding stabilizer that addresses it specifically without touching the others. The stop-gradient TopK loss fixes the gradient bias but doesn't fix prefix mismatch (you still need a well-aligned teacher). The RLVR-adapted teacher fixes prefix mismatch but doesn't fix the TopK bias (you still need a correct loss). The SFT warm-up fixes output format degeneracy but doesn't fix the PI-free optimum limitation (OPSD still can't work with instance-specific PI regardless of how well the student is initialized). The fact that each fix addresses a specific mechanism—and that the mechanisms were identified through theoretical analysis (gradient derivation) and diagnostic measurement (prefix-conditioned evaluation) rather than empirical guesswork—gives the paper unusual coherence.

This framework contrasts with how failure analysis is typically done in the LLM training literature. Most papers that document failures (Fu et al., 2026; Kim et al., 2026) observe that something bad happens and propose fixes that seem to help, without tracing the failure to a specific mathematical or structural cause. The fixes may work, but the reader doesn't know why, and more importantly, doesn't know whether the fix will transfer to new settings. This paper's mechanism-first approach provides transferable knowledge: if you see repetition collapse in your OPD run, check your TopK gradient. If your OPSD isn't improving over the base model, check whether your PI is instance-specific or shared. These are actionable diagnostic questions that don't require replicating the paper's exact setup.

The negative results are as informative as the positive ones. The finding that OPSD cannot work for math reasoning with instance-specific PI (regardless of teacher strength, regardless of optimization stability) is a **boundary condition**, not a failure to optimize well. It tells the field where to stop investing effort in OPSD and where to look instead (RLVR, standard RL, or off-policy methods). The ReST^EM experiment in Appendix K (Figure 16), where additional RL-style optimization of the revision model backfired, further reinforces that these boundary conditions are robust—pushing harder on the wrong axis makes things worse, not better.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The paper uses multiple datasets across different task categories. For mathematical reasoning: **OpenThoughts** (Guha et al., 2025), filtered to keep only English problems with verifiable boxed answers, serves as the primary training set; evaluation uses **MATH-500** (the 500-question test split from Hendrycks et al., 2021), **AIME24**, and **AIME25** (American Invitational Mathematics Examination problems from 2024 and 2025). For general reasoning: the **Science subset of Mixture of Thoughts** is used for training, with evaluation on **GPQA-Diamond** (198 questions) and **MMLU-Pro**. For alignment: **CharacterBench** (Zhou et al., 2024) tests character persona consistency across 11 dimensions, and **EmotionBench** (Huang et al., 2024) tests emotional expression across 8 emotion categories. For system-prompt internalization: **DAPO-Math-17k** (Yu et al., 2025) for reasoning compression, and **Wildguardmix** (Han et al., 2024) for safety alignment. For the SFT stabilization experiment, a filtered version of OpenThoughts with non-overlapping SFT and OPD splits (30,000 prompts for SFT data generation, remainder for OPD) is used.

- **Base model(s).** All experiments use the **Qwen3** model family. For OPD experiments, the student is typically Qwen3-1.7B (with and without instruction tuning, with and without thinking mode), and teachers include Qwen3-4B, Qwen3-8B, Qwen3-14B, and a GRPO-trained Qwen3-1.7B variant. For OPSD experiments, the student and teacher share the same base architecture (Qwen3-1.7B, Qwen3-4B, or Qwen3-8B), with the teacher conditioned on privileged information. For alignment tasks, Qwen3-4B-Instruct and Qwen3-8B are used. The choice of Qwen3 is pragmatic—it represents a contemporary open-weight model family at scales where distillation is both challenging and practically relevant—but the paper does not test other model families (e.g., Llama, Gemma, DeepSeek), which is an acknowledged limitation (Section 7).

- **Metrics.** The primary metric across all experiments is **task-specific accuracy**: for math reasoning, pass@1 (fraction of problems where the model's boxed final answer matches the ground truth, with a strict parser that only accepts explicit `\boxed{}` or `Final Answer` formats); for GPQA-Diamond, accuracy with the same strict parser; for alignment tasks, LLM-judge reward (scored by Gemini-2.0-Flash evaluating whether the student's response appropriately expresses the target style or emotion); for safety, accuracy on Wildguardmix's refusal/non-refusal classification. Training dynamics are tracked via several auxiliary metrics defined in Appendix A.2: **repeat token ratio** (fraction of tokens that are part of a repeated n-gram with n=3), **teacher-student log-probability gap** (`$\Delta\text{logprob}_t = \log\pi_T(y_t) - \log\pi_\theta(y_t)$` computed separately for repetitive and non-repetitive tokens), **TopK overlap ratio** (fraction of Top-50 tokens shared between teacher and student at each position, default K=50), **teacher log-probability** (average log-probability the teacher assigns to student-generated tokens), and **response length** (for detecting length explosion or collapse).

- **Baselines.** The paper compares OP(S)D against several established methods. **GRPO** (Group Relative Policy Optimization; Yu et al., 2025 via the DAPO implementation) is the primary RL baseline for math reasoning and alignment—it uses 8 samples per prompt, a maximum rollout length of 8192, and the GRPO advantage estimator with KL coefficient 0.0. **PPO** (Proximal Policy Optimization; Schulman et al., 2017) with GAE (γ=1.0, λ=0.95) is used as a baseline for alignment tasks on CharacterBench and EmotionBench. **Majority voting** is implicitly compared via the base model's pass@1 (since OPD with a single sample per prompt is directly comparable to greedy or single-sample decoding). For the PI-conditioned OPD ablation (Figure 10), **vanilla OPD with no PI** serves as the baseline against which answer-PI and response-PI OPD are compared. For the RLVR teacher experiment (Figure 13), distillation from an **untuned Qwen3-8B teacher** serves as the baseline against distillation from the GRPO-adapted Qwen3-1.7B teacher. For the SFT stabilization experiment (Figure 14), **direct OPD from the base model** (without SFT warm-up) serves as the baseline.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper measures compute in **training steps** (for RL methods) or **number of training samples** (for OP(S)D and SFT methods), not FLOPs. For RL methods (GRPO, PPO), one "step" processes a batch of 32 prompts × 8 samples each = 256 rollouts. For OP(S)D, one "step" processes a batch of 64 prompts × 1 sample each = 64 rollouts. The paper does not provide a FLOPs-matched comparison between OP(S)D and RL methods—the x-axes in comparison plots use training samples (Figures 5, 6) or training steps (Figures 3, 4, 7, 11). This makes direct efficiency comparisons approximate: OP(S)D uses fewer rollouts per step but each rollout requires additional teacher forward passes (for log-probability computation on the TopK union set), which are not accounted for in "number of training samples." The maximum response lengths differ between methods (4096 for OP(S)D, 8192 for GRPO/PPO), further complicating FLOPs comparisons. This is a non-trivial limitation for practitioners trying to decide between methods based on compute efficiency.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not use formal cross-validation. For the math reasoning experiments, models are evaluated periodically during training (typically every 50–200 steps) on the fixed test sets (MATH-500, AIME24, AIME25). For the general reasoning experiments (Figure 19), the same periodic evaluation protocol is used on GPQA-Diamond and MMLU-Pro. For alignment tasks, evaluation is performed at intervals during training on the standard test splits of CharacterBench, EmotionBench, and Wildguardmix. There are no error bars, confidence intervals, or multiple random seeds reported for any experiment—this is a significant limitation, as the training dynamics (particularly the collapse phenomena) may exhibit substantial run-to-run variability. The SFT experiment uses a fixed 30,000/remaining split but does not perform multiple splits to assess sensitivity. The prefix-conditioned teacher evaluation (Section 5.1) uses all 198 GPQA-Diamond examples with deterministic decoding (temperature 0.0), so statistical variation is not a concern for that specific measurement, but the generalization to other datasets is untested.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Math Reasoning: OPSD Fails Regardless of PI Type and Teacher Quality

**Headline result: OPSD with instance-specific PI does not improve math reasoning and can degrade performance relative to the base model.** Figure 3 tracks training of a Qwen3-1.7B student on OpenThoughts using a frozen step-0 checkpoint as the PI-conditioned teacher. Three PI variants are tested: answer-only PI (the teacher sees the ground-truth final answer), full-response PI (the teacher sees the complete ground-truth reasoning and answer), and full-response PI with an RL-trained teacher (the teacher is first fine-tuned with GRPO under full-response PI, then used as a frozen OPSD teacher).

On **MATH-500** (Figure 3, left panel), all OPSD variants start at approximately 0.60–0.65 accuracy (the base model's performance) and show no consistent improvement over 1750 training steps. The GRPO baseline, by contrast, improves steadily from approximately 0.60 at step 0 to approximately 0.80 by step 1750. The OPSD variants fluctuate in the 0.60–0.75 range without a clear trend, with the RL-teacher variant (purple line) performing slightly worse than the others at most steps—explicitly contradicting the hypothesis that a stronger PI-conditioned teacher would improve OPSD.

On **AIME24** (Figure 3, middle panel), GRPO improves from approximately 0.02 at step 0 to approximately 0.28 by step 1750. All OPSD variants remain near 0.00–0.05 throughout training, with the RL-teacher variant again performing worst—its accuracy drops to near zero after step 500 and stays there. On **AIME25** (Figure 3, right panel), the pattern is similar: GRPO reaches approximately 0.17, while OPSD variants remain in the 0.00–0.05 range with no improvement.

**Why this is definitive:** the failure persists across three PI types (answer-only, full response, full response with stronger teacher), two task difficulties (MATH-500 is easier, AIME24/25 are harder), and 1750 steps of training—enough for GRPO to show substantial gains. The RL-teacher variant is particularly informative because it demonstrates that the problem is not a weak teacher: the teacher has been explicitly trained via GRPO to solve math problems with access to the full solution as PI, and it is presumably quite capable at this. Yet distilling this capability into the student fails. This is consistent with the geometric-mean analysis (Equation 11): the PI is instance-specific (the answer to problem A is not the answer to problem B), so the aggregated PI-free policy cannot capture the teacher's PI-conditioned capability.

#### Math Reasoning: OPD with Unnormalized TopK Reverse KL Collapses Catastrophically

**Headline result: OPD with an unnormalized Top20 reverse-KL objective collapses into repetitive degeneracy, with accuracy on all benchmarks dropping to near zero.** Figure 4 documents OPD training using a Qwen3-8B teacher and a Qwen3-1.7B student (both with thinking mode disabled) on the OpenThoughts dataset, using the unnormalized Top20 reverse-KL objective (Equation 6). The training proceeds through three distinct phases visible in the training curves (Figure 4d–f) and qualitative examples.

**Phase 1 (steps 0–400): initial improvement then instability.** The task reward (Figure 4d) initially rises, and response lengths remain moderate (approximately 1500–2000 tokens). By step 400, however, response lengths begin to increase, and the repeat ratio (Figure 4e) starts climbing from approximately 0.4 toward 0.6. The teacher-student log-probability gap for repetitive tokens becomes increasingly positive, meaning the teacher assigns higher log-probability to tokens that the student is repeating—a signature of the TopK bias promoting tokens with moderate teacher-student margins.

**Phase 2 (steps 400–800): length explosion and revision-token proliferation.** At step 700, the word cloud (Figure 4b) shows the model output dominated by revision tokens such as "wait" and "but," with a repeat ratio of 0.79. The response length spikes from approximately 2000 to over 3500 tokens (Figure 4d), and the task reward begins to decline. The qualitative example in Appendix A.16 shows a response at this stage: the model generates verbose, circular reasoning containing the word "but" 10 times in a single answer, unable to converge to a conclusion. The teacher-student TopK overlap ratio (Figure 4e) drops from approximately 0.85 at step 0 to approximately 0.50 by step 700, indicating that the student's probability distribution has substantially diverged from the teacher's.

**Phase 3 (steps 800–1400): complete collapse to repetitive output.** By step 1000, the repeat ratio reaches 0.998 (Figure 4c), meaning essentially every token the model generates is part of a repeated n-gram. The word cloud shows a single dominant word: "maybe." The qualitative example in Appendix A.17 confirms this: the model's entire response consists of the word "maybe" repeated hundreds of times until the maximum response length is reached. The evaluation accuracy on MATH-500, AIME24, and AIME25 (Figure 4f) all drop to near zero by step 1000 and stay there through step 1400. The teacher log-probability (Figure 4e) declines from approximately −0.10 at step 0 to below −0.30 by step 1000, showing that the teacher finds the student's outputs increasingly implausible—yet the biased gradient continues to reinforce the degenerate behavior because the constant +1 term dominates the gradient signal.

**Token-level statistics confirm the mechanism.** The teacher-student log-probability gap for repetitive tokens (blue line in Figure 4e) rises from near zero at step 0 to approximately 0.15–0.20 by step 400 and stays elevated through collapse. This means the teacher consistently assigns higher probability to repetitive tokens than the student expects, which the biased TopK gradient interprets as a signal to increase the student's probability for those tokens—creating a positive feedback loop where repetition is reinforced. Simultaneously, the log-probability gap for non-repetitive tokens (orange line) declines, indicating that non-repetitive tokens receive weaker or negative feedback. The model learns that repeating itself is the optimal strategy under the biased loss, and once this behavior dominates, the teacher signal becomes irrelevant because the student is no longer exploring meaningful reasoning paths.

#### Math Reasoning: Stop-Gradient and Renormalized TopK Losses Prevent Collapse

**Headline result: Modifying the TopK reverse-KL objective to remove the +1 gradient bias—via stop-gradient (Equation 12) or renormalization (Equation 15)—prevents the collapse and maintains stable training with meaningful accuracy.** Figures 11 and 12 compare these variants across different K values, teacher types, and datasets.

**Figure 11: K=5 on DAPO with a GRPO-trained teacher.** Using a Qwen3-1.7B-GRPO teacher (no thinking mode) and a Qwen3-1.7B student on the DAPO dataset, three TopK variants with K=5 are compared over 800 steps:

- **Unnormalized reverse KL (Equation 6):** collapses starting at step 400. MATH-500 evaluation accuracy drops from approximately 0.75 at step 0 to near zero by step 600. The repeat token ratio rises from approximately 0.05 to approximately 0.55 by step 600 (and would likely continue to 1.0 if training continued). The teacher log-probability declines from approximately −0.05 at step 200 to approximately −0.30 by step 800, and the teacher-student log-probability gap for repetitive tokens rises to approximately 0.20—the same signature of TopK bias driving the student toward degenerate repetition.

- **Stop-gradient TopK (Equation 12):** MATH-500 accuracy stabilizes around 0.55–0.65 after step 200 and remains there through step 800. The repeat ratio stays below 0.10. The teacher-student log-probability gap for repetitive tokens remains near zero or slightly negative, indicating that repetition is not being reinforced. The OPD explicit loss decreases slowly and remains negative.

- **Renormalized TopK (Equation 15):** performs similarly to the stop-gradient variant. MATH-500 accuracy is comparable (0.55–0.65), the repeat ratio stays low, and the teacher log-probability remains stable in the −0.15 to −0.10 range.

**The key diagnostic:** under the unnormalized loss, the OPD explicit loss (training loss) **increases** sharply from approximately 0.00 to 0.15 around step 400, while under both stable variants it remains negative and slowly decreasing. An increasing training loss during gradient-based optimization is a strong signal that the gradient is not pointing toward a local minimum—it is pointing somewhere else, consistent with the direction of the biased +1 term.

**Figure 12: K=1 and the policy-gradient connection.** Using the same teacher-student pair and dataset, three variants are compared with K=1 (which reduces to the sampled-token formulation): unnormalized reverse KL, stop-gradient reverse KL, and the explicit policy-gradient formulation (Equation 4, labeled "OPD sampled reverseKL in PG"). The unnormalized variant collapses (MATH-500 drops from ~0.70 to near zero by step 2000, response length explodes from ~1500 to ~4000). The stop-gradient variant and the policy-gradient variant both remain stable with MATH-500 accuracy around 0.65–0.75 through 3000 steps. Response lengths stabilize between 1500–2500 tokens.

**Why K=1 matters:** when K=1, the TopK set at each position contains only the single token the student actually generated. The unnormalized TopK loss is then `$\pi_\theta(y_t) \log(\pi_\theta(y_t)/\pi_T(y_t))$`. The gradient includes the +1 term: `$\pi_\theta(y_t)[\log(\pi_\theta/\pi_T) + 1]\nabla_\theta \log\pi_\theta(y_t)$`. The fact that even this K=1 variant collapses proves that the bias is not about the size of the TopK set—it is about the mathematical structure of the gradient when the sum of probabilities over the truncated set is not exactly 1 (and at K=1, `$\pi_\theta(y_t)$` is typically much less than 1, often 0.01–0.30, so the constant term does not cancel). The stable performance of both the stop-gradient K=1 and the policy-gradient variant confirms that removing or avoiding the +1 term—either by stop-gradient or by using a pure policy-gradient formulation—is sufficient to prevent collapse.

**Figure 15: Larger K (K=20) does not eliminate collapse.** As shown in Appendix A.7, increasing K to 20 with the unnormalized reverse KL still leads to collapse: MATH-500 accuracy declines from ~0.80 to ~0.72 by step 800 (and the trend is downward), the repeat ratio rises from ~0.40 to ~0.50, and the task reward (pass@1 on training rollouts) declines. The stop-gradient Top20 variant maintains stable accuracy around 0.75–0.80 with low repeat ratio. This confirms that the bias is not eliminated by larger K—it persists at all K < |V|. The collapse is slower with K=20 than with K=5 (because more probability mass is included, bringing the sum closer to 1), but the directional bias remains and eventually dominates.

#### General Reasoning: OPD Does Not Provide Consistent Improvement

**Headline result: OPD with a Qwen3-8B teacher and Qwen3-1.7B student on general reasoning (GPQA-Diamond, MMLU-Pro) does not yield consistent or substantial improvements.** Figure 19 shows training on the Science subset of Mixture of Thoughts with three configurations: Qwen3-8B teacher with answer-PI, Qwen3-8B teacher without PI, and Qwen3-1.7B teacher with answer-PI.

On **GPQA-Diamond**, the student's accuracy fluctuates between approximately 28% and 34% over 200 training steps, with no configuration showing a clear improvement trend. The Qwen3-8B teacher without PI achieves approximately 30% at its best point but regresses to approximately 28% by step 200. The Qwen3-1.7B teacher with answer-PI underperforms both 8B teacher variants. On **MMLU-Pro**, the student starts at approximately 27.6% and reaches 28.3–30.1% depending on the configuration, a marginal improvement of at most 2.5 percentage points over 200 steps. The Qwen3-8B teacher with answer-PI achieves the highest MMLU-Pro score (30.1%) but this is only fractionally above the no-PI variant (28.3%).

**Token-level analysis explains the weakness.** Figure 20 shows that the teacher-student log-probability gap (`$\Delta$`logprob) is strongly skewed toward early tokens and weakens as the response lengthens. This means the teacher provides substantial supervision at the beginning of the response (where format and initial reasoning steps occur) but increasingly little differentiation at later positions. Furthermore, incorrect trajectories receive stronger teacher supervision than correct trajectories, particularly on final-answer tokens. This indicates that OPD is primarily correcting errors rather than reinforcing correct reasoning, which limits its ability to improve accuracy—the model spends most of its learning on what not to do, rather than on what to do.

**The implication:** general reasoning tasks with longer, more open-ended responses may be structurally more challenging for OPD because the teacher signal attenuates over sequence length and the distribution of supervision is skewed toward error correction rather than success reinforcement. This is consistent with the prefix-induced mismatch mechanism (Section 5.1): on longer responses, the student is more likely to enter reasoning states where the teacher's supervision is less useful.

#### Alignment and System-Prompt Internalization: OPSD Succeeds Where PI Is a Shared Latent Rule

**Headline result: OPSD succeeds on alignment and system-prompt internalization tasks—where PI is a shared latent behavior—achieving faster convergence and comparable or better accuracy than GRPO and PPO.** This is the positive counterpoint to the math reasoning failures, and it directly supports the geometric-mean analysis: shared-rule PI is preserved by the aggregation, instance-specific PI is not.

**Figure 5: CharacterBench and EmotionBench with Qwen3-4B-Instruct.** On CharacterBench (top row), all three methods—OPSD, GRPO, and PPO—converge to training rewards near 1.0, but OPSD converges substantially faster. At 4000 generations, OPSD's training reward is approximately 0.95, while GRPO and PPO are at approximately 0.80–0.85. On evaluation score, OPSD reaches approximately 0.92 by 4000 generations, compared to approximately 0.90 for PPO and 0.88 for GRPO. The gap is modest in absolute terms but consistent: OPSD achieves higher final performance with better sample efficiency.

On EmotionBench (bottom row), the pattern is similar. OPSD's training reward approaches 1.0 by 2000 generations, while GRPO and PPO require 6000–8000 generations to reach comparable levels. On evaluation score, OPSD peaks at approximately 0.92 around 8000 generations, compared to approximately 0.90 for PPO and 0.88 for GRPO.

**Why OPSD outperforms RL here but fails on math:** the privileged information in these alignment tasks is a **style specification** (character profile, emotion description). The PI says "respond as character X with personality Y" or "respond with emotion Z caused by factor W." Crucially, while the specific character or emotion varies across examples, **the structure of the PI is the same**: it's an instruction to adopt a particular persona or emotional tone. The PI-conditioned teachers all learn the same underlying behavior—"condition on the provided persona and generate responses consistent with it." The geometric mean preserves this shared meta-behavior, producing a student that has internalized the general capability of persona-consistent generation, even though the specific persona must be inferred from the dialogue context (not from explicit PI) at test time. This is fundamentally different from math, where the PI "answer = 42" for problem A and "answer = 17" for problem B provide no shared structure.

**Figure 6: Reasoning compression on DAPO-Math-17k with Qwen3-8B.** OPSD with a conciseness instruction as PI achieves comparable MATH-500 pass@1 to GRPO (approximately 0.935 vs. 0.936 at 30,000 training samples) while substantially reducing response length. The OPSD-trained model's evaluation response length drops from approximately 5000 tokens (initial) to approximately 3500–4000 tokens, while GRPO's response length remains around 4000–4500 tokens with an explicit length penalty. The training rollout response length shows an even starker difference: OPSD uses approximately 2000–4000 tokens per rollout, while GRPO requires 6000–10000 tokens because it needs long responses to get a valid final answer for reward computation.

**The practical advantage of OPSD over RL for compression:** RL requires generating complete, long responses to obtain a reward signal (which may include a length penalty). OPSD provides token-level supervision from the "concise" teacher at every step, so rollouts can be shorter—the student learns conciseness from the teacher's distribution, not from a scalar reward at the end. This makes OPSD more sample-efficient in the sense that each training sample requires less generation. However, the paper notes that this application requires a sufficiently capable model (8B-scale) to be effective, consistent with prior findings (Sang et al., 2026).

**Figure 7: Safety alignment on Wildguardmix with Qwen3-1.7B.** OPSD improves rapidly in the early stage of training: evaluation accuracy on Wildguardmix reaches approximately 0.73 by step 200, compared to approximately 0.68 for GRPO at the same point. However, OPSD's performance saturates around 0.75–0.76 by step 400 and then plateaus, while GRPO continues to improve slowly, reaching approximately 0.77 by step 1600. The final performance is comparable, but OPSD achieves its peak much faster.

**The saturation behavior is informative:** OPSD's final performance is bounded by the teacher's capability. The teacher is the student's own step-0 checkpoint conditioned on the safety system prompt. If that teacher is not perfectly capable at the safety task, the student cannot exceed that capability through distillation. GRPO, by contrast, optimizes a scalar reward directly and can potentially surpass the initial teacher's performance. This suggests combining OPSD (for rapid initial improvement) with RL (for continued improvement beyond the teacher's capability) as a practical pipeline, though the paper does not test this.

**Training curves reveal OPSD-specific dynamics not seen in RL:** On Wildguardmix (Figure 7), OPSD's response length increases from approximately 300 tokens at step 0 to approximately 500–550 tokens by step 400, then gradually increases to approximately 800 tokens by step 1600. GRPO's response length is more stable at approximately 500–600 tokens. The OPSD explicit loss decreases from approximately 0.20 at step 0 to approximately 0.05 by step 400, then slowly increases to approximately 0.10 by step 1600—the same pattern of early rapid learning followed by mild degradation that was observed in the saturation of accuracy. This increase in loss without corresponding accuracy improvement suggests the student may be overfitting to the teacher's distribution in ways that don't improve safety performance, a potential limitation not explored in detail.

#### PI Does Not Improve Standard OPD (with a Stronger Teacher) on Math Reasoning

**Headline result: Adding privileged information to the teacher in standard OPD (Qwen3-8B teacher, Qwen3-1.7B student) degrades performance relative to vanilla OPD with no PI.** Figure 10 compares three OPD configurations on OpenThoughts: vanilla OPD (no PI), OPD with final-answer PI (the teacher sees the ground-truth answer), and OPD with full-response PI (the teacher sees the complete correct solution). All use the stop-gradient TopK loss to avoid the collapse documented in Figure 4.

On **MATH-500** (Figure 10, left panel), vanilla OPD achieves the highest accuracy, reaching approximately 0.73 by step 1750. Answer-PI OPD reaches approximately 0.68, and response-PI OPD reaches approximately 0.65—both worse than vanilla OPD. The ranking is consistent: vanilla > answer-PI > response-PI. On the **OPD explicit loss** (middle panel), the initial loss at step 0 follows the opposite ranking: response-PI loss ≈ 0.16 > answer-PI loss ≈ 0.13 > vanilla OPD loss ≈ 0.10. The higher initial loss for PI-conditioned teachers indicates larger distributional mismatch—the teacher's output distribution shifts further from the student's when conditioned on PI, and longer PI (full response vs. answer only) induces larger shifts. The losses decline over training but maintain their relative ordering, with response-PI maintaining the highest loss throughout.

**Response length dynamics** (right panel) show that vanilla OPD produces the shortest responses (approximately 1400–1600 tokens), while PI-conditioned OPD produces longer responses (approximately 1800–2400 tokens for answer-PI, 2000–2600 for response-PI). This is consistent with the prefix-induced mismatch mechanism: the PI-conditioned teacher encourages more verbose reasoning because its distribution is further from the student's, so the student must explore more tokens to find ones the teacher approves of. The longer responses under PI-conditioned OPD do not translate to better accuracy—they represent wasted computation.

**Why this matters:** it demonstrates that the failure of PI in math reasoning is not unique to OPSD (where the teacher is the student itself). Even with a dramatically stronger teacher (8B vs. 1.7B), adding PI makes OPD worse. This reinforces the structural interpretation: instance-specific PI is fundamentally not useful for distillation because the PI-conditioned behavior cannot be compressed into a PI-free policy, regardless of whether the teacher is a stronger external model or a self-teacher.

#### OPD Failure on Persuasion Tasks

**Headline result: OPSD fails on the Persuasion for Good dataset, with rapid performance collapse for both 1.7B and 4B student models.** Appendix A.13 (Figure 21) evaluates OPSD against GRPO on the Persuasion for Good task, where a Persuader engages in multi-turn dialogue to convince a Partner to donate. The PI includes the persuadee's background, personality, and high-level persuasion strategies—information that varies across examples but shares a common structure (persuasion techniques applied to different individuals).

For the **Qwen3-1.7B** student (Figure 21a), OPSD initially achieves competitive performance but collapses after approximately 20–30 steps: evaluation score drops sharply, and the training reward oscillates without a clear trend. GRPO shows stable, gradually improving performance throughout training. For the **Qwen3-4B** student (Figure 21b), the collapse is even more severe—both training reward and evaluation score degrade substantially within the first 50 steps. The truncation ratio (middle column in each figure) under OPSD quickly reaches nearly 1.0 for both model sizes, indicating that the model is generating responses at the maximum length limit—outputs that are likely degenerate or off-topic. The student log-probabilities (left column) steadily decrease under OPSD, indicating the model becomes increasingly uncertain about its generated tokens, while GRPO maintains stable log-probabilities.

**Interpretation:** the Persuasion for Good task sits in an intermediate regime between fully shared-rule PI (alignment) and fully instance-specific PI (math). The PI provides persuasion strategies that are shared across examples (e.g., "use emotional appeal," "emphasize the charity's impact"), but the specific application depends heavily on the persuadee's individual profile. The geometric-mean aggregation may partially preserve the shared strategy knowledge while discarding the instance-specific adaptation, resulting in a policy that knows how to be persuasive in general but cannot effectively tailor its approach to specific individuals—leading to long, generic outputs that fail to convince. The paper does not analyze this task in the same depth as math reasoning or alignment, so this interpretation remains speculative, but the empirical result is clear: OPSD fails here despite the PI having elements of shared structure.

#### RLVR-Adapted Teachers: A Smaller, Distribution-Matched Teacher Outperforms a Larger One

**Headline result: A Qwen3-1.7B teacher trained with GRPO (RLVR-adapted) provides more effective distillation than a Qwen3-8B teacher with comparable standalone accuracy, because the adapted teacher's output distribution is closer to the student's.** Figure 13 presents this finding in three panels.

**Left panel: standalone accuracy comparison.** The Qwen3-8B teacher (no thinking mode) achieves 83% on MATH-500, 28% on AIME24, and 22% on AIME25. The Qwen3-1.7B-GRPO teacher (no thinking mode, trained with DAPO for 200 steps) achieves 82% on MATH-500, 30% on AIME24, and 20% on AIME25—essentially comparable performance. On MATH-500, the 8B model has a 1-point edge; on AIME24, the 1.7B-GRPO has a 2-point edge; on AIME25, the gap is 2 points. These differences are small relative to the distillation performance gap documented below.

**Middle panel: distillation performance on OpenThoughts.** When both teachers are used to distill a fresh Qwen3-1.7B student via OPD (with stop-gradient TopK loss), the 1.7B-GRPO teacher substantially outperforms the 8B teacher on all three benchmarks. On MATH-500, the 1.7B-GRPO teacher's student reaches approximately 0.70 task reward by step 2000, while the 8B teacher's student reaches approximately 0.55. On AIME24, the 1.7B-GRPO teacher's student reaches approximately 0.35, versus approximately 0.20 for the 8B teacher. On AIME25, the 1.7B-GRPO teacher's student reaches approximately 0.25, versus approximately 0.12 for the 8B teacher. These are large gaps—the RLVR-adapted teacher provides roughly 25–50% relative improvement in distilled student performance despite having comparable (or slightly lower) standalone accuracy.

**Right panel: Top20 vocabulary overlap as an explanatory mechanism.** The Top20 token overlap between the 1.7B-GRPO teacher and the 1.7B student (measured as the fraction of Top-20 tokens at each position that appear in both models' Top-20 sets) is consistently around 0.75–0.85 throughout training. The 8B teacher's overlap with the student is lower, around 0.65–0.75. This 0.10–0.15 gap in distributional similarity directly correlates with the distillation performance gap. When the teacher's most-likely tokens overlap more with the student's, the teacher's token-level supervision is more aligned with what the student is likely to generate—reducing the prefix-induced mismatch where the teacher wants to redirect the student's reasoning.

**The counterintuitive implication:** if you have a choice between a 4× larger teacher and a same-scale teacher adapted via RL on the target task, prefer the adapted teacher. The distributional alignment gained through task-specific RL training outweighs the raw capability advantage of scale—at least for models in the 1.7B–8B range. Whether this generalizes to larger scale gaps (e.g., 70B vs. 7B) or to different model families is untested.

#### SFT Warm-Up Stabilizes OPD for Base Models

**Headline result: SFT warm-up on teacher-generated traces prevents degeneracy and enables stable OPD improvement when the student is a base model (not instruction-tuned).** Figure 14 compares direct OPD from Qwen3-1.7B-Base (no SFT warm-up) against SFT + OPD (first fine-tune on 20,000 teacher traces from Qwen3-4B, then apply OPD). The training data is the OPD split of OpenThoughts.

**Direct OPD (no SFT):** MATH-500 task reward fluctuates substantially between 0.1 and 0.5 without a clear improving trend over 250,000 training samples. The OPD explicit loss oscillates and does not converge (range: approximately 0.1–0.6). Evaluation response lengths vary erratically between 500 and 2500 tokens. This instability is attributed to the base model producing garbled, nonsensical outputs (non-English Unicode, degenerate sequences) on some prompts, where the teacher cannot provide meaningful feedback.

**SFT + OPD:** MATH-500 task reward improves smoothly from approximately 0.35 to 0.55 over 250,000 samples. The OPD explicit loss decreases from approximately 0.3 to below 0.1. Evaluation response lengths stabilize around 1250–1500 tokens. The SFT warm-up (Table 3) reduces the student's NLL on teacher-generated traces from 0.640 to 0.335 and PPL from 1.896 to 1.397—quantifying the improvement in output well-formedness that makes subsequent OPD effective.

**The mechanism is distinct from typical SFT warm-up motivations:** this is not about providing a good initialization for faster convergence (though it does that too). It is about ensuring the student's outputs are in a region of the output space where the teacher's token-level feedback is meaningful. On garbled outputs, the teacher's probability distribution reflects confusion (high entropy, no clear preferred tokens), so the distillation gradient is essentially random noise. SFT warm-up eliminates this regime by teaching the student basic formatting and reasoning structure, after which the teacher's signal becomes informative.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**TopK value (K=5 vs. K=20):** Increasing K from 5 to 20 does not eliminate the collapse under unnormalized reverse KL. Figure 11 (K=5) and Figure 15 (K=20) both show collapse for the unnormalized variant and stability for the stop-gradient variant. With K=20, the collapse is somewhat slower (accuracy declines from ~0.80 at step 0 to ~0.72 by step 800 rather than collapsing to near zero in 400 steps as with K=5), consistent with more probability mass being included (the sum over the Top20 set is closer to 1, so the uncanceled +1 term is smaller). But the directional bias remains and the downward trend continues—the model does not stabilize. This confirms that the stop-gradient fix, not larger K, is the necessary intervention.

**Sampled-token vs. vocabulary-level losses (Figure 12):** The explicit policy-gradient formulation (Equation 4, treating log-probability gap as advantage) matches the performance of stop-gradient TopK reverse KL with K=1, both achieving stable MATH-500 accuracy around 0.65–0.75 through 3000 steps. The unnormalized TopK K=1 (equivalent to the sampled-token loss without stop-gradient) collapses. This ablation shows that (a) the instability is not specific to vocabulary-level KL—it appears in sampled-token formulations as well, as long as the gradient includes the uncanceled +1 term, and (b) the policy-gradient formulation is a viable alternative to the stop-gradient TopK loss, albeit potentially slower due to using only a single token per position.

**Renormalized vs. stop-gradient TopK (Figure 11):** Both prevent collapse and achieve comparable MATH-500 accuracy (0.55–0.65). The renormalized variant has the theoretical advantage of being a proper KL divergence on the truncated support, but it ignores probability mass outside the TopK set entirely, which may cause the loss to be a poor approximation of the full-vocabulary KL when the TopK set captures only a modest fraction of total probability mass. The stop-gradient variant preserves the unnormalized probabilities and thus the full probability mass weighting, at the cost of no longer being a proper divergence. In practice, the difference is negligible for the K=5 setting tested.

**Teacher-student overlap as a mechanism (Figure 13, right):** The Top20 overlap ratio between teacher and student is proposed as an explanatory variable for why the 1.7B-GRPO teacher outperforms the 8B teacher. The measurement shows a consistent gap of 0.10–0.15 in overlap ratio, correlating with better distillation performance. However, this is correlational, not causal—the paper does not manipulate overlap independently (e.g., by artificially increasing or decreasing overlap through temperature scaling or logit manipulation) to verify that overlap is the mechanism rather than merely correlated with other differences between the 1.7B-GRPO and 8B models (e.g., training data, optimization trajectory, architectural details of the GRPO fine-tuning).

**PI type in OPD (Figure 10):** Three PI types are compared for OPD with a stronger teacher: none, answer-only, and full-response. The ranking for MATH-500 accuracy is vanilla > answer-PI > response-PI, and the ranking for initial distillation loss is response-PI > answer-PI > vanilla. This confirms that richer PI induces larger distributional shift (higher initial loss) and worse distillation outcomes. This is a dose-response relationship—more PI, more mismatch, worse results—which strengthens the causal interpretation that PI-induced distribution shift, not some other factor, is driving the degradation.

**PI type in OPSD (Figure 3):** In OPSD for math reasoning, full-response PI performs worse than answer-only PI, and the RL-teacher with full-response PI performs worst of all. This mirrors the OPD finding from Figure 10: richer PI is worse. But the mechanism in OPSD is different—it's the geometric-mean aggregation that fails, not prefix-induced mismatch (since the teacher is the student itself, there is no capability gap). Both mechanisms point in the same direction for PI-richness experiments, making it difficult to disentangle them without additional experiments.

**Thinking mode mismatch (Appendix A.14):** When the OPSD teacher uses thinking mode (generates reasoning in `  thinking` tags before answering) but the student is trained without thinking mode, the student may learn to emit malformed thinking-mode control tokens. Figure 22 shows an example where the student produces `  thinking ...  response ...  thinking`—a spurious trailing `  thinking` that is unintended. This "thinking mode hacking" is a practical failure mode of OPSD under mismatched generation configurations, relevant for practitioners who might assume that disabling thinking mode on the student will simply cause it to learn the teacher's final answers without the reasoning process.

**OPSD response length collapse (Appendix A.15):** When using only the final answer as PI in OPSD on DAPO-Math-17k with Qwen3-1.7B, the student may learn to bypass reasoning entirely and output only the answer—e.g., `  thinking\n\n response\n\n\boxed{7}` with no intermediate steps. This is a different failure mode from the repetition collapse in OPD: rather than degenerating into repetitive tokens, the student collapses to a minimalist output that matches the PI (the answer) without learning the reasoning process. This may be partially mitigated by using full-response PI (which includes reasoning), but as shown in Figure 3, full-response PI still fails to improve accuracy.

**Entropy-supervision correlation (Appendix A.21):** Following Jin et al. (2026), the paper examines whether token-level entropy correlates with the teacher-student log-probability gap, which could enable entropy-weighted distillation. Figure 23 shows only a mild negative Pearson correlation (approximately −0.11 for Qwen3-8B student, −0.21 for Qwen3-1.7B student) between `$\Delta$`logprob and token entropy. The correlation is too weak to be practically useful—token entropy alone does not predict where the teacher provides strong vs. weak supervision.

**Prefix-conditioned teacher evaluation (Section 5.1, Appendix A.22):** The standalone Qwen3-14B teacher achieves 62.1% accuracy on GPQA-Diamond (123/198 correct). When continuing from Qwen3-1.7B student prefixes (deterministic decoding, temperature 0.0), accuracy drops to 46.0% (91/198 correct)—an absolute decrease of 16.2 percentage points. The transition matrix shows asymmetric degradation: 40 originally correct predictions become wrong, while only 8 originally wrong predictions become correct (5:1 hurt-to-help ratio). Output format correctness drops from 98.5% to 78.8%, indicating that student prefixes not only degrade reasoning accuracy but also the teacher's ability to produce answers in the expected parseable format. This is the most directly causal evidence for the prefix-distortion mechanism: it isolates the effect of conditioning on student prefixes (vs. reasoning from scratch) and shows it is large, asymmetric, and detrimental to both correctness and format.

**Correlation of `$\Delta$`logprob with correctness (Figure 17):** Token-level KL supervision is substantially weaker on correct student trajectories than on incorrect trajectories. This holds across both thinking and non-thinking modes, multiple teacher types, and multiple PI configurations. The `$\Delta$`logprob on correct trajectories typically ranges from −0.05 to −0.20 (teacher assigns slightly lower probability than student), while on incorrect trajectories it ranges from −0.10 to −0.36 (teacher assigns substantially lower probability). This means OPD primarily provides a corrective signal—it tells the student "this was wrong" more strongly than it tells the student "this was right." The asymmetry may contribute to OPD's limited ability to improve performance on tasks where the student's initial accuracy is low, because the model receives mostly negative feedback without clear positive examples to reinforce.

### Critical Assessment

#### Claim: OPSD fails on math reasoning because instance-specific PI cannot be compressed into a PI-free policy.

**What the experiments demonstrate:** The paper shows that OPSD with answer-PI, full-response PI, and RL-strengthened full-response PI all fail to improve over the base model on MATH-500, AIME24, and AIME25 across 1750 training steps (Figure 3). It also shows that in OPD with a stronger teacher, adding PI (answer or full response) makes distillation worse than using no PI (Figure 10). These findings are consistent with the geometric-mean analysis but do not constitute a direct test of it.

**What is not tested:** The geometric-mean analysis predicts that the optimal PI-free student policy is the normalized geometric mean of PI-conditioned teacher distributions. The paper never measures what this geometric-mean policy actually looks like, never computes the theoretical optimum to compare against the learned policy, and never verifies that the learned policy is converging to the geometric mean rather than to some other suboptimal point. The failure could be due to the geometric-mean limitation, but it could also be due to optimization difficulties that happen to affect PI-conditioned OPSD more than vanilla OPSD, or to the PI-conditioned teacher providing gradients that point in unhelpful directions for reasons unrelated to the geometric-mean structure. The paper's argument is theoretically motivated but empirically correlational—OPSD fails when PI is instance-specific and succeeds when PI is shared, which matches the theory's prediction, but the specific mechanism (geometric mean discarding instance-specific information) is not directly verified. An experiment that would strengthen this claim: compute the PI-conditioned teacher's average log-probability for correct vs. incorrect answers across instances and compare to the learned student's distribution, to verify that the student converges to the geometric-mean prediction.

**Additional caveat:** The paper tests OPSD only on math reasoning with Qwen3 models at the 1.7B scale. It does not test whether larger models (e.g., 8B, 70B) can better compress instance-specific PI—perhaps the geometric-mean limitation is less severe when the model has more capacity to represent a mixture of behaviors that can be contextually activated. The claim that OPSD "fails in our tested settings" is accurate, but inferring that it must fail in all settings with instance-specific PI overstates the evidence.

#### Claim: Unnormalized TopK reverse KL introduces a biased gradient that causes optimization collapse.

**What the experiments demonstrate:** This claim is strongly supported. The paper provides a mathematical derivation showing that the +1 term in the reverse-KL gradient fails to cancel when the vocabulary is truncated, producing a systematic bias (Equation 8 vs. Equation 7). It shows that this bias pushes the student away from the teacher distribution when the teacher-student probability ratio is below e. It demonstrates that three different interventions that remove or avoid this bias—stop-gradient (Equation 12), renormalization (Equation 15), and policy-gradient formulation (Equation 4)—all prevent collapse, while the unnormalized version collapses across multiple K values (5, 20, 1), two datasets (DAPO, OpenThoughts), and two teacher types (GRPO-adapted, 8B external). The collapse signature (increasing repeat ratio, increasing OPD explicit loss, declining teacher log-probability, "maybe maybe maybe" outputs) is consistent across configurations and matches the predicted bias direction.

**What is not tested:** The paper does not directly measure the biased gradient or compare it to the true full-vocabulary gradient to verify that the difference is exactly the uncanceled +1 term. It infers the mechanism from the mathematical derivation and the behavioral signature. This is reasonable—directly measuring gradients of this scale would be computationally expensive—but it means the claim is supported by consistency between theory and behavior rather than by direct gradient measurement. Additionally, the paper does not explore whether there are alternative TopK constructions that preserve the sum-to-one property without renormalization (e.g., including a "tail" token that aggregates all probability mass outside the TopK set, as in SDPO's tail-augmented KL, discussed in Appendix A.3.3 but not tested empirically).

**Strengths of the evidence:** The collapse is consistent, dramatic (accuracy drops from ~75% to near zero), and replicated across configurations. The stop-gradient fix is minimally invasive—it changes only what is differentiated, not the loss value itself—which makes alternative explanations (e.g., the fix changes the effective learning rate, or introduces beneficial noise) less plausible. The fact that renormalization (a completely different mathematical fix that also eliminates the +1 term) produces similar stability further strengthens the mechanistic interpretation.

#### Claim: Student prefixes distort the teacher's reasoning state, making token-level supervision weaker than the teacher's standalone capability.

**What the experiments demonstrate:** The prefix-conditioned teacher evaluation (Appendix A.22) provides direct, causal evidence: a Qwen3-14B teacher's accuracy on GPQA-Diamond drops from 62.1% to 46.0% when continuing from Qwen3-1.7B student prefixes. The 5:1 hurt-to-help ratio shows that the degradation is asymmetric—student prefixes harm the teacher far more often than they help. The format correctness degradation (98.5% to 78.8%) shows that the prefixes affect not just answer accuracy but the teacher's ability to produce well-formed outputs.

**What is not tested:** The evaluation uses deterministic decoding (temperature 0.0), which may not reflect the teacher's behavior under the stochastic sampling used during actual OPD training (temperature 1.0). A teacher sampling stochastically from student prefixes might produce more diverse continuations, some of which could be correct even if the deterministic continuation is wrong. The evaluation also uses a specific truncation procedure (random truncation of student trajectories) that may not match the prefix distribution the teacher encounters during actual OPD training. The paper also does not vary the student-teacher capability gap to test whether the degradation scales with the gap—it tests only one student-teacher pair (1.7B student, 14B teacher) on one dataset (GPQA-Diamond). Testing a smaller gap (e.g., 4B student with 14B teacher) or a larger gap (e.g., 0.5B student with 14B teacher) would characterize how the distortion scales.

#### Claim: RLVR-adapted teachers improve distillation by reducing distribution mismatch.

**What the experiments demonstrate:** A Qwen3-1.7B-GRPO teacher with comparable standalone accuracy to Qwen3-8B provides substantially better distillation (Figure 13). The Top20 overlap ratio is higher for the 1.7B-GRPO teacher, consistent with the distribution-mismatch mechanism.

**What is not tested:** The causal link is correlational—higher overlap is associated with better distillation, but the paper does not experimentally manipulate overlap to verify causation. Alternative explanations: the 1.7B-GRPO teacher was trained on the same data distribution as the student (DAPO then OpenThoughts), so it may provide supervision that is better tuned to the specific prompts; the 1.7B-GRPO teacher may have learned reasoning strategies that are more "learnable" by a 1.7B student (even if equally accurate as the 8B's strategies); or the 8B teacher may produce correct answers via reasoning paths that a 1.7B model cannot easily follow. The paper's distribution-mismatch explanation is plausible but not uniquely identified by the data.

**Missing baseline:** The paper does not compare against a Qwen3-1.7B-SFT teacher (fine-tuned on the training data via supervised learning rather than RL) to test whether the benefit comes from RL specifically or simply from task-specific adaptation regardless of method.

#### Claim: SFT warm-up stabilizes OPD by keeping the student in regions where teacher feedback is informative.

**What the experiments demonstrate:** Direct OPD from a base model (Qwen3-1.7B-Base) is unstable and does not improve over 250,000 training samples, while SFT + OPD shows steady improvement (Figure 14). The SFT step reduces NLL and PPL on teacher traces (Table 3), confirming it improves the student's ability to produce well-formed outputs.

**What is not tested:** The paper only tests one teacher-student pair (4B teacher, 1.7B student) and one SFT configuration (2 epochs on 20,000 teacher traces). The sensitivity to SFT data quantity, quality, and source (teacher-generated vs. human-written) is not explored. The paper does not test whether the benefit of SFT warm-up persists if the SFT data is from a different teacher than the one used for subsequent OPD (important for practical deployment where the best SFT teacher might not be the best OPD teacher). The claim that SFT works by "keeping the student in well-formed regions" is an interpretation—an alternative interpretation is that SFT simply provides a better initialization for optimization, and any method that improves the student's initial accuracy would help OPD.

#### Overall Strengths of the Experimental Design

The paper's strongest experimental contribution is its **diagnostic measurement apparatus**. Rather than reporting only final accuracy, the authors track a rich set of intermediate metrics—repeat ratio, teacher-student log-probability gap (separated by repetitive vs. non-repetitive tokens), TopK overlap, teacher log-probability, response length—that reveal the dynamics of training in a way that pure accuracy curves cannot. This is what enables the paper to distinguish between "the model isn't learning" (flat accuracy) and "the model is learning the wrong thing" (increasing repeat ratio, declining teacher log-probability, rising OPD loss). The word-cloud visualizations at different training stages (Figure 4a–c) and the qualitative examples in Appendices A.16–A.17 provide faces to the numbers, making the collapse phenomenology concrete and memorable.

The **stop-gradient as gradient-level intervention** is methodologically elegant. By changing only what is differentiated—not the loss value, not the optimization algorithm, not the model architecture—the paper isolates the gradient bias as the causal mechanism with unusual precision. This is stronger evidence than typical ablation studies that change multiple things at once.

The **across-task comparison** (math vs. alignment vs. system-prompt internalization vs. persuasion) is essential for establishing the PI-structure boundary condition. If the paper had studied only math, the conclusions would be "OPSD doesn't work"—but the alignment and system-prompt results show that OPSD can work, and work well, under the right conditions. This transforms the paper from a negative result into a conditional positive result with predictive criteria.

#### Overall Weaknesses and Missing Experiments

**Single model family:** All experiments use Qwen3 models. The TopK gradient bias is a mathematical property that should be model-independent, but the PI-structure boundary condition (shared vs. instance-specific) might interact with model architecture, scale, or pretraining data in ways not tested. Llama, Gemma, or DeepSeek models might respond differently to OPSD, particularly at larger scales where in-context learning capabilities differ.

**No error bars or multiple seeds:** The training dynamics shown (collapse at step 400, accuracy curves, etc.) are single-run results. The collapse phenomena could be sensitive to random seed, data order, or hardware-induced stochasticity. The paper's conclusions would be stronger with 3–5 runs per configuration showing consistent behavior.

**No direct measurement of the geometric-mean optimum:** The paper's central theoretical contribution (Section 5.2, Equation 11) is not empirically verified by measuring the hypothesized optimum and comparing it to the learned policy. Computing the geometric mean exactly would be expensive (requiring teacher evaluations over many PI samples), but approximate verification—e.g., checking that the learned student assigns probability proportional to `$\exp(\mathbb{E}_I \log \pi_T)$` for a sample of responses—would substantially strengthen the mechanistic claim.

**Limited scale exploration:** All experiments are at the 1.7B–8B scale. Larger models (14B, 32B, 70B) may exhibit different OPD/OPSD dynamics. The prefix-distortion measurement uses a 14B teacher and 1.7B student—testing a 14B teacher with a 14B student (self-distillation without PI) would show whether the distortion is primarily a function of the capability gap or inherent to on-policy conditioning.

**Missing FLOPs-matched comparisons:** The paper compares OP(S)D to GRPO and PPO using x-axes of training steps or samples, but these methods have different computational costs per step (GRPO uses 8× more rollouts per prompt, OP(S)D requires additional teacher forward passes for TopK log-probability computation). A FLOPs-matched or wall-clock-time-matched comparison would be more informative for practitioners deciding between methods.

**No combination of OPD with RL:** The paper studies OPD and RL as alternatives, but a natural practical pipeline—use OPD for rapid early improvement, then switch to RL for continued gains—is not tested. Figure 7 shows OPSD saturating while GRPO continues improving, suggesting such a combination could be beneficial.

**The Persuasion for Good result is underanalyzed:** Figure 21 shows a dramatic OPSD failure on a task where PI has elements of both shared structure (persuasion strategies) and instance-specific information (persuadee profiles). This is a theoretically interesting case that could refine the shared-vs-instance-specific dichotomy, but the paper provides only brief interpretation in Appendix A.13 without the detailed token-level diagnostics applied to math and alignment.

**No investigation of dynamic PI:** All experiments use a fixed PI distribution. In practice, PI could be made available adaptively—e.g., providing the answer only after the student has attempted reasoning, or providing progressively more detailed PI as training proceeds. This might combine the benefits of PI-conditioned teachers (they know the right answer) with better distributional alignment (the student sees PI only when it would help, not throughout training). The paper does not explore this possibility.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### Limitation 1: Difficulty Estimation Cost Is Unaccounted For in Reported Efficiency Gains

**The assumption or constraint.** The compute-optimal framework depends on estimating prompt difficulty before allocating the inference budget. The paper's method for doing so—generating 2048 samples per question and averaging either ground-truth correctness or PRM scores—is extremely expensive. The authors acknowledge this in Section 3.2: "estimating difficulty in this way still incurs additional computation cost during inference... our experiments do not account for this cost largely for simplicity." At 2048 samples, the difficulty estimation step alone consumes more compute than the largest test-time budgets studied (256–512 generations).

**The consequence.** The reported 4× efficiency gains over best-of-N are computed *after* difficulty is known, without amortizing the cost of learning it. In a realistic deployment, the total cost would be difficulty estimation + strategy execution, and the former could dominate the latter. For example, if difficulty estimation costs 2048 generations and the chosen strategy uses 64 generations, the total cost is 2112 generations—roughly 8× more than the best-of-256 baseline the compute-optimal strategy was supposed to match at 4× lower cost. The 4× figure should therefore be understood as an **upper bound on achievable efficiency** rather than a realized deployment gain.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper presents the 4× efficiency claims in Figures 4 and 8 without including difficulty estimation cost in the generation budget. Section 3.2 flags this as an acknowledged limitation, and Section 8 suggests future work on "pretraining or finetuning models to directly predict difficulty of a question" as a way to reduce this cost. However, no such lightweight difficulty estimator is developed or evaluated. Figures 4 and 8 do show that predicted difficulty bins (using PRM scores, which still require 2048 samples) track oracle bins closely—but this validates the binning strategy, not the cost.

**Mitigation status.** The paper identifies this as future work and suggests two directions: a pretrained difficulty classifier, or adaptive difficulty estimation that amortizes cost into the solution process. Neither is tested. Until this gap is closed, the efficiency numbers should be treated as **conditional on known difficulty**, not as end-to-end deployment figures.

---

### Limitation 2: Single Benchmark, Single Model Family—Generality Is Unproven

**The assumption or constraint.** Every experiment in the paper uses the MATH benchmark (with 500 test questions) and models from the Qwen3 family (1.7B, 4B, 8B, 14B). The authors state in Section 4 that they "believe this model is representative of the capabilities of many contemporary LLMs," but this claim is unverified. The Conclusion acknowledges this directly: "Our findings are based on a limited set of model families and model scales, and larger-scale settings may exhibit different behavior."

**The consequence.** Several findings could be model-specific or benchmark-specific in ways that affect transferability. The PRM's quality and over-optimization behavior depend on Qwen3's output distribution—a model family with different calibration or error patterns might exhibit different difficulty-dependent scaling curves. The revision model's ability to learn from incorrect in-context examples depends on the base model's in-context learning capabilities, which vary substantially across model families. The MATH benchmark consists exclusively of competition-level math problems requiring symbolic reasoning; it is unclear whether the difficulty-dependent patterns (beam search hurting easy problems in Figure 3, revisions plateauing on hard problems in Figure 8) generalize to code generation, logical reasoning, or factual QA. The finding that test-time compute can substitute for a ~14× larger model on easy-to-medium problems might not hold if the larger model has a different pretraining recipe or architecture.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** All results are on MATH with PaLM 2-S* or Qwen3 models. No cross-model-family validation is performed. The paper acknowledges this limitation in the Conclusion but presents no mitigation strategy beyond suggesting future work. The 500-question MATH test set, split into five difficulty quintiles of ~100 each, then split by two-fold cross-validation, means strategy selection is based on ~50 questions per fold per bin—a small sample that could introduce substantial variance in which strategies are selected as "optimal."

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not test on other reasoning benchmarks (GSM8K, MMLU, HumanEval) or other model families (Llama, Gemma, DeepSeek). Replication on additional benchmarks and models is left entirely to future work. A practitioner using a non-Qwen model on a non-MATH task cannot assume the quantitative findings (e.g., the specific 4× efficiency gain, the optimal sequential-to-parallel ratio, or the beam-search-vs-best-of-N crossover point) will transfer.

---

### Limitation 3: The ~14× Larger Model Baseline Is Unfair to Pretraining—No Test-Time Compute for the Big Model

**The assumption or constraint.** The FLOPs-matched comparison in Section 7 pits a smaller model with compute-optimal test-time scaling against a ~14× larger model using **greedy decoding only**—no majority voting, no best-of-N, no search, no revisions. The larger model also scales only parameters (not training data), following the LLaMA paradigm rather than compute-optimal pretraining where both data and parameters scale equally. The authors acknowledge this: "We choose this setting as it is representative of a canonical approach to scaling pretraining compute and leave the analysis of compute-optimal scaling of pretraining compute where the data and parameters are both scaled equally to future work."

**The consequence.** The reported advantages of test-time compute over pretraining—e.g., +27.8% relative improvement on easy questions at R ≪ 1 for revisions (Figure 1)—may overstate the case for test-time compute. A Chinchilla-optimal model trained with ~14× more total FLOPs (scaling both data and parameters) would likely outperform a parameter-only-scaled model, making the pretraining baseline stronger than what the paper tests. More importantly, giving the larger model even a modest test-time compute budget—say, best-of-8 or a single chain of 4 revisions—would create a much more realistic baseline. The paper implicitly assumes that test-time compute is *substitutable* for pretraining compute, but a fairer comparison would give both the large and small model access to test-time compute appropriate to their budgets.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The FLOPs-matched results (Figure 9, Figure 1 bar charts) consistently show test-time compute outperforming the larger greedy model on easy-to-medium problems, but the comparison is asymmetric: one side gets adaptive test-time strategies, the other gets greedy decoding. The paper acknowledges the parameter-only scaling choice in Section 7 but does not address the absence of any test-time compute for the larger model.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not run a single experiment where the larger model gets even a basic test-time augmentation. A FLOPs-matched comparison that gives both models test-time compute budgets—e.g., small model with 256 generations vs. large model with 16 generations—would test whether test-time compute is genuinely a *substitute* for pretraining (as the paper claims) or merely a *complement* (better than nothing, but still behind a larger model with some inference budget). This is a significant gap for practitioners deciding between investing in a larger model or investing in smarter inference for a smaller one.

---

### Limitation 4: Sequential Strategies Incur Latency Costs That Are Ignored

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper measures compute in "generations" (number of complete solutions sampled), which is a reasonable proxy for total FLOPs but ignores **wall-clock latency**. Sequential revision chains are inherently serial—each revision depends on the previous one. Parallel best-of-N can be executed simultaneously given sufficient hardware. A strategy that allocates 128 generations as 64 sequential × 2 parallel takes roughly 64× longer wall-clock time than 128 parallel samples.

**The consequence.** The sequential-heavy strategies favored by the compute-optimal policy on easy problems—where purely sequential revisions are optimal (Figure 7, right)—may be impractical for latency-sensitive applications. This includes interactive assistants, real-time decision-making systems, or any deployment where users wait for responses. The paper's 4× efficiency gains are measured in FLOPs, not user-perceived latency. A practitioner deploying a compute-optimal strategy might find that the FLOPs savings are more than offset by the increased latency, especially if generating long revision chains serially creates unacceptable wait times for users. The paper does not discuss this tradeoff or provide latency measurements.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figure 7 (right) shows that on the easiest questions (bin 1), performance is essentially flat across all sequential-to-parallel ratios—fully sequential is as good as fully parallel. But this means allocating a budget of 64 generations as a single revision chain of length 64 (fully sequential) produces the same accuracy as 64 parallel samples, while taking ~64× longer in wall-clock time. On medium questions (bins 3–4), the optimal ratio involves a mix of sequential and parallel (e.g., ~2:1 to 8:1 sequential-to-parallel), which partially mitigates the latency issue but still introduces serial dependencies that parallel-only strategies avoid.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper never mentions latency or wall-clock time as a constraint. Section 8 discusses future work on combining search with revisions and self-improvement loops, but does not mention latency-aware strategy selection as a direction. For practitioners, this means the compute-optimal policy must be adapted: one might restrict the sequential-to-parallel ratio to a maximum based on latency requirements, accepting some accuracy loss for faster inference.

---

### Limitation 5: Hard Problems Remain Unsolved—Test-Time Compute Cannot Create Capability

**The assumption or constraint.** The entire compute-optimal framework operates on the assumption that the base model has *some* non-trivial probability of producing a correct answer. If pass@1 is effectively zero on a problem class, no amount of search, revision, or strategy optimization can help—there are no correct solutions in the proposal distribution to find or refine. The paper is transparent about this: Section 7's takeaway box notes that "test-time compute can amplify existing capability but cannot create it from nothing."

**The consequence.** On the hardest MATH problems (difficulty bin 5), the paper demonstrates near-zero improvement across all methods and all budgets. In Figure 3 (right), bin 5 accuracy hovers at 1–3% for all search methods and all budgets up to 256 generations. In Figure 7 (right), bin 5 shows roughly 2–3% accuracy regardless of the sequential-to-parallel ratio. In the FLOPs-matched comparison (Figure 9), the bin 5 scaling line is essentially flat near 0–5%, and the ~14× larger model also performs poorly—suggesting these are genuinely hard problems. This is a fundamental boundary: the approach offers no path forward for genuinely novel or out-of-distribution reasoning that exceeds the base model's training distribution. For such problems, pretraining on more data (or with better data) is likely the only viable path.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figures 3 (right), 7 (right), and 9 all show flat or near-flat performance on bin 5 across budgets, methods, and allocation strategies. The paper does not attempt any interventions on this hardest bin—no additional pretraining data, no specialized fine-tuning, no architectural modifications. The bin 5 results are presented without mitigation.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges this as a boundary condition but does not attempt to address it. The finding is treated as a characterization of when test-time compute is useful (bins 1–4) versus when pretraining is necessary (bin 5). This is valuable diagnostic information, but it also means that for organizations whose problem distribution skews toward bin-5-type difficulty, the paper's recommendations ("use compute-optimal test-time strategies") are inapplicable—the guidance is effectively "train a better base model first."

---

### Limitation 6: Revisions and PRM Search Are Studied Independently—The Combined Potential Is Unmeasured

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper studies two complementary axes—PRM-guided search (Section 5) and iterative revisions (Section 6)—but never combines them. Section 8 explicitly acknowledges: "we did not experiment with PRM tree-search techniques in combination with revisions." The two mechanisms have complementary strengths: revisions improve the proposal distribution (generating better candidates via sequential refinement), while PRM search improves candidate selection (finding the best among generated candidates via verifier-guided search).

**The consequence.** The current results represent a **lower bound** on what a fully integrated system could achieve. On medium-difficulty problems (bins 3–4), beam search helps by exploring different solution paths, while revisions help by refining within a path. A system that uses the revision model as the proposal distribution for beam search—or uses the PRM to decide when to stop revising and when to restart—could potentially break through the performance ceilings that each method individually hits. For example, Figure 3 shows beam search plateauing on bin 3 around 34% accuracy at 256 generations, while Figure 7 shows revisions reaching ~42% on bin 3 with the optimal sequential-to-parallel ratio. Combining both might push bin 3 performance substantially higher. The paper's FLOPs-matched comparisons (Section 7) treat search and revisions as independent strategies, but a combined strategy might shift the training-inference tradeoff in favor of test-time compute even more dramatically on medium problems.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper demonstrates the complementary strengths implicitly: Figure 3 shows beam search outperforming best-of-N most clearly on medium-hard problems (bins 3–4), while Figure 7 shows sequential revisions helping most on medium problems with moderate parallel sampling. These are overlapping but non-identical regimes, suggesting combination could capture benefits from both. However, no experiment tests this combination. The revision model experiments use the base model as the proposal distribution (without PRM-guided search), and the search experiments use the few-shot prompted base model (without revision conditioning).

**Mitigation status.** Acknowledged in Section 8 as future work: "we did not experiment with PRM tree-search techniques in combination with revisions." The paper does not offer a hypothesis about how to combine them (e.g., PRM-guided beam search with revision model as the proposal, or revision chains where the PRM scores each revision step to decide continuation vs. backtracking). This is left as an open research direction. For practitioners, this means the presented results should be viewed as a decomposition of the problem space, not a complete solution—further work is needed to realize the full potential of combining both mechanisms.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper shifts the conversation around on-policy distillation from **empirical trial-and-error to mechanism-driven diagnosis**. Prior work oscillated between optimism (Agarwal et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2026; Shenfeld et al., 2026) and pessimism (Fu et al., 2026; Kim et al., 2026) without a framework for reconciling the conflicting findings. By identifying three specific, independently verifiable failure mechanisms—the TopK reverse-KL gradient bias, prefix-induced teacher-student mismatch, and the structural limitation of PI-free policy aggregation in OPSD—this paper provides a **causal taxonomy** that explains *when* OP(S)D should work and *when* it should fail, rather than simply reporting that it sometimes does and sometimes doesn't.

The magnitude of this shift is **reframing rather than paradigm-shifting**. The paper does not introduce a new training algorithm that replaces existing methods; it provides a set of diagnostic tools and conceptual criteria that make existing methods more predictable. This is akin to the difference between empirical scaling laws ("bigger models work better, usually") and mechanistic understanding ("the attention heads implement induction via prefix matching, which is why bigger models work better"). The practical value is that future OPD practitioners can diagnose their own failures against a known set of mechanisms rather than guessing at hyperparameters or switching to a different method entirely.

The most consequential reframing concerns **the role of privileged information**. Prior work treated PI as uniformly beneficial—more information for the teacher means better supervision for the student. This paper's geometric-mean characterization (Equation 11) shows that PI's usefulness depends on its structure, not its quantity: shared-rule PI (system prompts, style specifications, alignment preferences) survives the aggregation into a PI-free policy, while instance-specific PI (ground-truth answers, problem-specific reasoning) is averaged away into uselessness. This reconceptualizes OPSD from a general-purpose distillation method into a **specialized tool for behavior internalization**—effective when the behavior is consistent across examples, ineffective when it varies per example. The practical implication is immediate: teams building reasoning-improvement pipelines should not use OPSD with answer-conditioned teachers (Figures 3 and 10 show this fails across multiple configurations), but teams building style-transfer or prompt-internalization systems should strongly consider it (Figures 5, 6, and 7 show faster convergence and comparable or better accuracy than RL baselines).

The paper also reconciles a specific prior contradiction. Kim et al. (2026) asked "Why does self-distillation (sometimes) degrade the reasoning capability of LLMs?" and documented empirical degradation without a clear mechanism. This paper's answer—that OPSD's optimum is the geometric mean of PI-conditioned teachers, which discards instance-specific information—provides a structural explanation. The degradation is not an optimization failure or a teacher quality issue; it is mathematically inevitable when PI varies across examples. This converts a puzzling negative result into a predictable boundary condition, enabling researchers to design OPSD experiments with appropriate PI structures rather than rediscovering the failure through trial and error.

Three research directions become **more attractive** as a result of this work:

1. **Verifier and reward model training with correct gradient handling.** The paper shows that a seemingly innocuous approximation (TopK truncation) introduces a systematic gradient bias that causes catastrophic collapse (Figure 4). This is not specific to OPD—any method using truncated reverse KL for distribution matching (KL-regularized RL, variational inference with truncated support, distillation pipelines) inherits this bias. The finding that stop-gradient or renormalization eliminates the collapse (Figures 11, 12, 15) provides a simple, immediately applicable fix. Researchers developing new KL-based training methods should now check for this bias as a standard diagnostic, much as the RL community checks for reward hacking.

2. **Structured PI design for distillation.** The geometric-mean analysis suggests that PI effectiveness can be predicted from its statistical structure: if `$\mathbb{E}_I \log \pi_T(y \mid x, I)$` concentrates on responses that are correct for prompt `$x$`, OPSD should work; if the PI-conditioned teachers for different prompts assign high probability to incompatible responses, it should fail. This opens the door to PI design as an engineering discipline—craft PI that is shared enough to survive aggregation but informative enough to guide the teacher, potentially through meta-learning over PI structures or through adaptive PI that becomes more specific as training progresses.

3. **Distribution-matched teacher selection over capability-maximizing teacher selection.** Figure 13's finding—that a same-scale GRPO-adapted teacher outperforms a 4× larger teacher with comparable standalone accuracy—challenges the default assumption that stronger teachers produce better distillation. The practical recommendation (prefer task-adapted, distribution-matched teachers over generic larger teachers) has immediate implications for how distillation pipelines are constructed. The conceptual implication is that teacher selection should be treated as a **matching problem** (aligning teacher and student distributions) rather than a **maximization problem** (finding the strongest available teacher).

Two research directions become **less attractive**:

1. **OPSD for math reasoning with instance-specific PI.** The paper provides strong evidence that OPSD with answer-conditioned teachers cannot work for math reasoning, regardless of teacher quality, optimization stability, or model scale (at least up to the tested 1.7B–8B range). Figure 3 shows flat or declining performance across 1750 steps for three PI variants, including an RL-strengthened teacher. Figure 10 shows that adding PI to standard OPD with a stronger teacher actually degrades performance. The geometric-mean analysis provides a structural reason for this failure that is unlikely to be overcome by more compute or better hyperparameters. Researchers interested in improving math reasoning through distillation should focus on OPD without PI (with a well-matched teacher) or on RL-based methods (GRPO, PPO, RLVR), not on OPSD with answer conditioning.

2. **Unnormalized TopK reverse KL as a default loss for OPD.** The paper demonstrates collapse across multiple K values (1, 5, 20), multiple datasets (DAPO, OpenThoughts), and multiple teacher types (8B external, 1.7B-GRPO). The bias is mathematical, not empirical—it follows from the non-canceling +1 term, which is present whenever the probability sum over the truncated set is not exactly 1. Using unnormalized TopK reverse KL without stop-gradient or renormalization should be considered a known failure mode, not a viable training configuration. Practitioners who have been using this loss (as in several prior OPD implementations cited in the paper) should switch to the stop-gradient variant immediately.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Direct measurement of the TopK gradient bias against the true full-vocabulary gradient.** The paper's argument that the +1 term causes collapse is mathematically derived and behaviorally validated (stop-gradient fixes the collapse), but the gradient bias itself is never directly measured. A follow-up study could instrument an OPD training run to compute both the TopK approximate gradient and the true full-vocabulary gradient on a sample of positions and batches, measuring the cosine similarity between them over training. The prediction: for the unnormalized TopK loss, cosine similarity should decline as training progresses (the approximate gradient increasingly points away from the true gradient), while for the stop-gradient variant, it should remain high. This would provide direct, rather than inferential, evidence for the mechanism. It would also quantify how the bias grows with training—does it start small and accelerate (consistent with the abrupt collapse at step 400 in Figure 4), or is it present from the beginning and only becomes dominant when the student distribution shifts?

**Characterization of the geometric-mean optimum for OPSD across PI structures.** The paper derives Equation 11 but never empirically verifies that the learned student converges to this optimum. A follow-up could take a small, tractable setting (e.g., synthetic math problems with controlled PI structure, or a toy language modeling task where the full distribution can be enumerated), compute the geometric-mean policy exactly, and track whether OPSD training converges to it. For instance: create a dataset of arithmetic problems where PI is either shared ("use the order of operations" for all problems) or instance-specific ("the answer is 42" for problem A, "the answer is 17" for problem B). Train OPSD in both settings and measure the KL divergence between the learned student policy and the theoretical optimum. The prediction: convergence to the optimum in the shared-PI case (with corresponding performance improvement), convergence to a weak policy in the instance-specific case (with no improvement). More interestingly: is the learned policy actually the geometric mean, or does OPSD get stuck somewhere else? If the latter, that would indicate an optimization problem on top of the structural limitation—both would need to be addressed for OPSD to work, not just the PI structure.

**Scaling behavior of prefix-induced teacher degradation across capability gaps.** The paper measures one data point: a Qwen3-14B teacher drops from 62.1% to 46.0% accuracy on GPQA-Diamond when continuing from Qwen3-1.7B student prefixes (a roughly 8× parameter ratio). A parametric study varying the student-teacher gap—e.g., 0.5B student with 14B teacher (28× gap), 1.7B with 14B (8×), 4B with 14B (3.5×), 7B with 14B (2×), and self-prefix (14B with 14B)—would characterize how the distortion scales. The prediction: the degradation should increase monotonically with the capability gap, but the functional form matters. Is it linear in the gap (in which case even modest gaps cause substantial distortion)? Is there a threshold below which the degradation is negligible (in which case distillation with sufficiently well-matched teachers is safe)? Does self-prefix degradation exist at all—i.e., does a model continuing from its own earlier outputs suffer any loss relative to reasoning from scratch? This last question is critical for understanding self-distillation methods (Shenfeld et al., 2026) and whether the prefix-distortion mechanism applies to them.

**Combining OPSD with RL for the best of both worlds on alignment tasks.** Figure 5 shows OPSD converging faster than GRPO and PPO on CharacterBench and EmotionBench; Figure 7 shows OPSD saturating on Wildguardmix while GRPO continues improving. This suggests a natural hybrid: train with OPSD for rapid initial improvement (exploiting the dense token-level signal from the PI-conditioned teacher), then switch to GRPO for continued improvement beyond the teacher's capability ceiling. A follow-up could implement this as a two-phase training procedure—OPSD for the first N steps, then GRPO with the OPSD-trained model as initialization—and compare against pure OPSD and pure GRPO at matched total compute. The key question is whether the OPSD initialization provides a better starting point for GRPO than the base model (faster convergence, higher final performance) or whether the distributional shift from OPSD training reduces GRPO's effectiveness (because the model is already in a local optimum that GRPO's exploration cannot escape). The Wildguardmix results (Figure 7) suggest the former should be true, but the Persuasion for Good results (Figure 21, where OPSD catastrophically collapses) caution that OPSD-started models might be in degenerate regions that GRPO cannot recover from.

**TopK construction strategies that preserve the sum-to-one property without full-vocabulary computation.** The paper identifies the SGLang API limitation (Appendix A.6) that forces a global union of per-position TopK sets, which increases memory cost from `$T \times K$` to `$T \times |\mathcal{U}|$` in the worst case. A follow-up could explore alternative TopK constructions that maintain the sum-to-one property (eliminating the +1 bias) while remaining compatible with position-independent log-probability APIs. Candidates include: (1) the tail-augmented KL described in Appendix A.3.3 (attributing probability mass outside the TopK set to a single "tail" token, which restores the sum-to-one property), (2) using the teacher's TopK set instead of the student's (since the teacher distribution is stop-gradiented, its TopK set does not introduce gradient bias from changing TopK membership during training), or (3) using a fixed, position-independent TopK set (e.g., the most frequent tokens in the training corpus) that does not depend on the current student distribution. A thorough comparison would measure not just training stability (whether collapse occurs) but also final model quality relative to full-vocabulary KL, memory usage, and throughput, providing practitioners with a Pareto frontier of cost vs. correctness for different TopK strategies.

**Stress-testing the shared-vs-instance-specific PI criterion on tasks with mixed PI structure.** The Persuasion for Good result (Figure 21, Appendix A.13) shows OPSD failing dramatically on a task where PI has elements of both shared structure (persuasion strategies like emotional appeal, emphasizing charity impact) and instance-specific information (the persuadee's individual background and personality). This is an intermediate regime not cleanly predicted by the paper's binary criterion. A follow-up could systematically vary the ratio of shared-to-instance-specific information in the PI, e.g., by constructing synthetic PI where a fraction `$\alpha$` of the information is shared across all prompts and a fraction `$1-\alpha$` is prompt-specific, then measuring OPSD's effectiveness as a function of `$\alpha$`. The prediction: a phase transition—OPSD fails below some critical `$\alpha$` where the geometric mean discards too much instance-specific information, and succeeds above it. Characterizing this transition would replace the binary criterion with a continuous one, giving practitioners a quantitative threshold for deciding whether their PI is structured appropriately for OPSD.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**System prompt internalization for cost-efficient deployment.** The reasoning compression results (Figure 6) and safety alignment results (Figure 7) demonstrate that OPSD can internalize a system prompt—a fixed instruction like "be concise" or "follow safety rules"—into model parameters, eliminating the need to include that prompt at inference time. For high-volume production deployments, this translates directly to cost savings. A system prompt of 50 tokens prepended to every user query costs 50 tokens of context per request. For a deployment serving 10 million requests per day, that is 500 million tokens of daily context overhead—roughly $0.50–$5.00/day at typical API pricing, or more importantly, 500 million tokens of additional KV-cache memory and prefill computation. OPSD internalization removes this overhead entirely: the model learns to behave as if the system prompt were present without actually processing it. Figure 6 shows that this can be achieved without accuracy degradation (MATH-500 pass@1 of 0.935 for OPSD vs. 0.936 for GRPO with the prompt), and with OPSD using 2–3× fewer training rollout tokens than GRPO because the token-level supervision allows shorter rollouts.

**Style-consistent character or persona modeling without prompt engineering.** The CharacterBench and EmotionBench results (Figure 5) demonstrate that OPSD can train a model to produce persona-consistent responses without the persona specification being present at inference time. This is directly applicable to conversational AI systems where users expect consistent character behavior—game NPCs, virtual assistants with defined personalities, therapeutic chatbots with specific communication styles, or creative writing aids that maintain a consistent authorial voice. The current approach to persona consistency is to prepend a character description to every system prompt, which (a) consumes context length, (b) can be forgotten or diluted in long conversations, and (c) must be maintained and versioned alongside the model. OPSD offers an alternative: train the model once with the persona specification as PI, then deploy it as a standalone model that produces persona-consistent outputs without any prompt engineering. The OPSD-trained Qwen3-4B-Instruct in Figure 5 achieves better evaluation scores (approximately 0.92 on CharacterBench and 0.92 on EmotionBench) than PPO or GRPO at the same training budget, and converges faster—reaching performance at 4000 generations that RL methods need 8000–12000 generations to match. For teams maintaining multiple character models, this represents a significant reduction in both training compute and deployment complexity.

**Stabilization of on-policy distillation pipelines by defaulting to stop-gradient TopK losses.** The collapse documented in Figure 4—a model degenerating from 75% MATH-500 accuracy to near-zero while outputting the word "maybe" hundreds of times—is not just an academic curiosity. It is the kind of failure that wastes thousands of GPU-hours in production training runs. The paper's finding that stop-gradient TopK reverse KL prevents this collapse (Figures 11, 12, 15) while retaining the memory efficiency of TopK truncation provides an immediate, actionable fix for any team running OPD. The change is minimal: replace `loss = sum(pi_student * log(pi_student / pi_teacher))` with `loss = sum(pi_student * (log(pi_teacher) - stopgrad(log(pi_student))))` (Equation 12). This does not change the loss value, the memory usage, or the throughput—only the gradient. For teams that have experienced OPD instability and abandoned it, this fix might make it viable. For teams that haven't tried OPD because of the documented instability, this fix lowers the barrier to entry. The paper's RLVR-adapted teacher finding (Figure 13) provides a complementary guideline: prefer teachers that have been fine-tuned on the target task over larger generic teachers, which both improves distillation quality and likely reduces the probability of encountering the prefix-induced mismatch that contributes to instability.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper does not propose a single "method" against which alternatives should be chosen; rather, it diagnoses when two related methods—OPD and OPSD—succeed or fail relative to each other and to RL baselines. The following decision rules are directly supported by the paper's empirical findings:

- **Prefer OPSD over GRPO/PPO when:** the privileged information is a shared latent behavior (system prompt, style specification, safety rule, character profile—not problem-specific answers) AND rapid convergence is important (OPSD reaches peak performance 2–3× faster than RL in Figures 5 and 7) AND the teacher's capability ceiling is sufficient for the task (OPSD saturates at the teacher's performance, while RL can surpass it; Figures 7 and 3 show this). The reasoning compression use case (Figure 6) is the clearest example: OPSD matches GRPO accuracy while training on shorter rollouts and producing shorter evaluation responses.

- **Prefer OPD over OPSD when:** the task requires improving the student's capability beyond its current level (OPD uses a stronger external teacher, OPSD uses the student itself) AND a well-matched teacher is available—either same-scale and task-adapted via RLVR (Figure 13 shows a 1.7B-GRPO teacher outperforming an 8B teacher for distilling a 1.7B student) or larger but from the same model family with high distributional overlap. If the only available teacher is much larger and produces substantially different output distributions (e.g., a 70B teacher for a 7B student), the prefix-induced mismatch documented in Section 5.1 may dominate, and SFT warm-up (Figure 14) or RLVR teacher adaptation should be applied first.

- **Do NOT use OPSD with instance-specific PI for reasoning:** Figures 3 and 10 show that answer-conditioned or solution-conditioned OPSD fails to improve math reasoning regardless of teacher strength or PI richness. Use OPD (without PI, with a stronger teacher), GRPO, or standard RLVR instead.

- **When using any OPD variant, default to stop-gradient TopK reverse KL:** the unnormalized TopK reverse KL collapses across all tested configurations (Figures 4, 11, 12, 15). The stop-gradient version (Equation 12) or renormalized version (Equation 15) both prevent collapse. The stop-gradient version is algebraically simpler to implement and preserves unnormalized probability weighting; the renormalized version is a proper divergence on the truncated support but ignores probability mass outside the TopK set. Both work; the choice is a matter of implementation preference.
