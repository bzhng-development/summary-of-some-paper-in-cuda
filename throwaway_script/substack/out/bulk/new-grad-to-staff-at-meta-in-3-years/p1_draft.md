Here are the takeaways from this Evan King interview (grew from new grad to Staff Engineer at Meta in 3 years, now co-founder of HelloInterview):

**Speed is your multiplier**

From day one at Meta, Evan could grasp problems and crank out code faster than most people around him. His first project involved migrating a legacy system that ingested ISIS and Al-Qaeda propaganda content and hashed it to prevent copies from appearing on the platform. The timeline was set for "a month plus." He finished it in under a week. That left him three weeks to make it better, so he redesigned the entire system, taking ingestion times from multiple days down to seconds. The timing was critical: when terror organizations post new media, most views happen within the first few hours. The original pipeline, taking days to process, meant they were always too late. By reducing ingestion time to seconds, they could catch and block content before it gained traction, detecting an order of magnitude more harmful content. The core insight: "When you can complete your normal workload with 70% of your time, that extra 30% becomes your secret weapon. That's where the real growth happens." If you're not yet blazing through your core responsibilities, he says, stop reading and put all your energy into mastering fundamental skills first.

**Operating above your level**

Freeing up 30% of your time changes everything. You can use it to develop the perspective of someone a level above you. As a junior engineer, Evan wasn't just focused on his tasks—he was studying how his senior teammates approached problems, evaluated trade-offs, and thought about systems. By the time he hit senior, he had shifted his lens to understanding how staff engineers connected broader technical strategies across teams. As he approached staff, he was thinking about how technical decisions impacted entire organizations and shaped long-term architecture. "Each step up wasn't just about doing more—it was about seeing more." He leaned heavily on his manager and trusted senior engineers to understand how they thought about problems at their level, then worked to develop that same perspective. The beauty of this approach: at worst, you develop a deeper understanding of your organization's challenges; at best, you start operating at that next level, making promotion a recognition of how you already think rather than a bet on your potential. He notes this came naturally as a junior but as he grew more senior, he needed his manager to push him to think even bigger.

**No one has all the answers**

Many engineers join FAANG companies with crippling impostor syndrome, which alone can sink or significantly stall most careers. For whatever reason, Evan didn't have it. Seeing the vulnerability of others around him reinforced a powerful truth: "No one has all the answers. Not the senior engineers, not the directors, not even the VPs." Once he internalized this, he was suddenly free to share ideas broadly and without hesitation. Meta was uniquely suited for this mindset—Workplace, their internal communication platform, allowed employees to share work and ideas across the company, surfacing valuable insights regardless of an employee's level. Sometimes his posts got no reaction; sometimes the reaction was even negative (a veteran employee once publicly stated all the reasons why his proposal was "idiotic"). But "nobody remembered or cared the next day, including me." Other times, posts went viral and led to meetings with directors or VPs to discuss his ideas one-on-one. "It was meritocracy at its finest—but you could only take advantage of it if you weren't paralyzed by the fear of looking stupid."

**Simple solutions to complex problems**

There's a tendency in tech, especially at Meta, to reach for complex technical solutions. Evan learned early that his strength wasn't in being the most technically sophisticated engineer in the room, but in seeing the clearest path to solving the actual problem. A defining example came during his work on suicide prevention. The team faced the challenge of detecting potential suicides in live videos in real time, with staggeringly high stakes—every false negative meant a potentially missed opportunity to save a life. They had a team of brilliant ML PhDs working alongside Meta's unified AI org to develop state-of-the-art models for video and audio. But after watching enough false negatives, Evan noticed something that seemed almost too obvious: while sophisticated models were still processing frame sequences and temporal features, the viewers in the comments section had already identified the crisis. Comments like "don't do it" or "it's not worth it" were appearing consistently. He suggested adding comments as a modality to the model. The results were immediate and dramatic: recall jumped from 9% to over 50%. With further iterations—including incorporating comment embeddings as temporal features in sequence models and an expanding training dataset—they pushed recall into the 90% range. "Sometimes the best solution isn't the most technically sophisticated one. Taking a step back, questioning our assumptions, and being willing to see the problem from different angles does wonders."

**The favor economy**

Conventional wisdom says don't get distracted by things that don't drive direct impact. Evan took a different path, and it paid off. He helped everyone, especially in the beginning—ops, data engineering, data science, other engineers, regardless of level. "I went out of my way to help." There's a fine line here, which he struggled with as he grew more senior since time is finite and valuable. But those early favors compound. By the time he hit Staff, others he'd helped had grown too. That manager from another team he helped was now a director. That ops person he assisted became director of ops. While he never expected favors in return, they trusted him. That goodwill meant he was in all the right conversations. People came to him first with their ideas and opportunities. If someone outside the org wanted to talk about Content Integrity, there was a good chance his name came up and they were routed to him. "Those cross-discipline relationships I built meant people associated my name with my organization. And now those people were in positions of power." He reflects candidly: "Say what you want about whether this is good for an organization, but it was definitely good for my career."

**The power of positive motion**

The tech industry breeds cynicism, but Evan made a conscious decision to never talk badly about anyone—"not even in my thoughts." This wasn't about ignoring problems; he saw the flaws and felt the frustrations like everyone else. But unless he could meaningfully address an issue, he wouldn't focus on it. This mindset shift changed everything: he genuinely looked forward to coming to work, found himself excited by challenges rather than drained by them, and fully bought into the mission. "I discovered that work became more fun—and there's no limit to what you can accomplish when you're enjoying what you do. Happiness and positivity are wild accelerants in life, not just work."

**The reality check**

Evan is upfront about the context: "I hit the timing jackpot. Right org, explosive growth phase, and a manager who saw something in me before I saw it myself. If you're looking for a pure meritocratic story, this isn't it. Nobody lands promotions this fast without the stars aligning." The six principles he describes weren't strategies he consciously developed; they emerged naturally as he reflected on what worked. They're not prescriptive steps to follow but patterns he noticed in retrospect. His closing advice: if you're reading looking for a blueprint to replicate his timeline, you're focusing on the wrong thing. "The timeline isn't what matters. What matters is building sustainable habits that compound over time. Start with mastering your core responsibilities. Use that mastery to create bandwidth. Deploy that bandwidth strategically. Build relationships. Stay positive. The rest will follow."
