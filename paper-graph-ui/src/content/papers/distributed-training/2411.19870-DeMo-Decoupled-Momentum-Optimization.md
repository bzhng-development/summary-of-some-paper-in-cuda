# DeMo: Decoupled Momentum Optimization

**ArXiv:** [2411.19870](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.19870)

## 🎯 Pitch

This paper proposes **Decoupled Momentum Optimization (DeMo)**, a drop-in distributed optimization framework that replaces full-precision gradient all-reduce with compressed momentum communication, reducing per-step bandwidth by up to two orders of magnitude while maintaining convergence parity with AdamW-DDP.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper proposes **Decoupled Momentum Optimization (DeMo)**, a drop-in distributed optimization framework that replaces full-precision gradient all-reduce with compressed momentum communication, reducing per-step bandwidth by up to two orders of magnitude while maintaining convergence parity with AdamW-DDP. Evaluated on 300M- and 1B-parameter OLMo language models pretrained on 100B tokens of the Dolma v1.5 corpus, DeMo integrates three mechanisms: decoupled local momentum updates (allowing each worker's momentum buffer to evolve independently without gradient synchronization), structured tensor compression via blockwise Discrete Cosine Transform followed by top-k sparsification (selecting only the k largest-magnitude DCT coefficients per chunk), and momentum subtraction as error feedback (subtracting communicated values directly from the momentum buffer to accumulate uncommunicated information). On the 300M model with k=8, DeMo transmits 85× less data per GPU per step (7.5 MB vs. 637 MB) while achieving comparable or better zero-shot accuracy on HellaSwag, ARC-Easy, and PIQA, establishing that aggressive momentum sparsification preserves model quality only when coupled with transform-domain compression and built-in error accumulation via the momentum buffer itself.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Gradient Synchronization Is the Bottleneck in Distributed Training

Training modern large language models requires distributing computation across many accelerators, typically using data parallelism — where each worker processes a different microbatch of data and then all workers must agree on a single parameter update. This agreement step, the **gradient all-reduce**, is the fundamental communication bottleneck that DeMo targets.

Consider what happens in standard Distributed Data Parallelism (DDP) with a model like OLMo-1B. The model's trainable parameters are replicated across all GPUs. During a single training step, each GPU computes gradients on its local microbatch, producing a gradient tensor that has exactly the same number of elements as the model has parameters — for OLMo-1B, that's approximately 1.18 billion floating-point numbers. These gradient tensors are then **fully synchronized** across all workers: every worker sends its local gradient to every other worker (or equivalent aggregation server), and the global gradient is computed as the average. This all-reduce operation must complete before any worker can update its parameters and proceed to the next step.

The communication cost is brutal. For OLMo-1B trained with 64 GPUs, each training step transmits **2,416.6 megabytes per GPU** (Table 1, AdamW-DDP 1B row). At this scale, with typical H100 training speeds, communication can dominate wall-clock time. The paper frames this starkly: "synchronizing gradients across numerous accelerators can dominate training time" (Section 4, opening). This is not a small inefficiency — it is the primary reason why large-scale training requires expensive, specialized networking hardware (NVLink, InfiniBand) and dictates that all GPUs be geographically co-located in the same cluster.

### Why the Gradient All-Reduce Is Not Fundamentally Necessary

The paper's motivating insight is that **the full-precision gradient all-reduce communicates enormous redundancy**. During momentum-based optimization, each worker's local momentum buffer $M^i_t$ accumulates gradient information over many steps:

$$M^i_t = \beta M^i_{t-1} + (1 - \beta) G^i_t$$

where $\beta$ is typically 0.9 or higher. This means that at step $t$, the momentum term contains a weighted sum of all past gradients with exponentially decaying weights. The gradient at any single step contributes only a fraction $(1-\beta)$ to the current momentum. Most of the momentum signal at step $t$ is carryover from previous steps — information that already influenced earlier parameter updates.

This structure implies that the per-step gradient $G^i_t$ is a **low-information supplement** to an already-rich momentum buffer, not a fundamental unit of optimization progress. Yet standard DDP treats every gradient element as equally important, transmitting all $\sim$1.18B values for OLMo-1B at every step. The DeMo authors recognize that the momentum term itself — a temporally smoothed, denoised version of the gradient signal — is a better candidate for communication: it captures the sustained optimization direction while naturally filtering out per-microbatch noise.

Critically, aggregating momenta across workers is mathematically equivalent to aggregating gradients, due to linearity of the momentum update. If each worker could communicate its momentum $M^i_t$ instead of its gradient $G^i_t$, the global momentum would be:

$$M_t = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N M^i_t = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \left( \beta M^i_{t-1} + (1-\beta) G^i_t \right)$$

which recovers exactly the same information as gradient all-reduce followed by central momentum computation. But momentum — unlike gradients — is a natural candidate for compression because it evolves smoothly and its important structure is lower-dimensional than its nominal dimensionality.

### Where Prior Work Falls Short

The paper identifies three broad families of prior approaches to reducing communication in distributed training, each with significant limitations that DeMo is designed to overcome.

**1. Gradient Compression: Sparsification and Quantization**

The most direct prior approach is to compress the gradient before the all-reduce operation. Gradient sparsification methods (Aji and Heafield, 2017; Lin et al., 2018b; Stich et al., 2018) transmit only the gradient elements with the largest absolute magnitudes — typically the top 1% or 0.1% — and drop the rest. Gradient quantization methods (Alistarh et al., 2017; Wen et al., 2017) reduce the precision of each gradient element from 32-bit floating point to 8-bit, 4-bit, or even 1-bit representations.

Both families suffer from a fundamental problem: **sparse updates applied directly to model parameters degrade optimization**. When only a few parameter dimensions receive gradient updates at each step (because their gradient elements were below the sparsification threshold), the optimizer develops a sparse, irregular update pattern. Parameters that happen to be excluded from the top-k for several consecutive steps effectively receive no training signal, while the selected parameters receive concentrated updates. The paper explicitly references this failure mode: "naively updating parameters using sparse patterns can degrade performance" (Section 3.4, discussing the ablation results in Figure 3).

To combat this, the standard remedy is **error feedback** (Karimireddy et al., 2019; Seide et al., 2014). The idea: maintain a separate memory buffer that accumulates the error from uncommunicated gradient elements (the difference between the true gradient and the compressed version). This error is then added back into the gradient at the next step, ensuring that no gradient information is permanently lost — only delayed. However, error feedback introduces a significant practical cost: **additional GPU memory proportional to model size**. For a billion-parameter model, this means an extra ~4 GB of memory (at FP32) dedicated solely to error accumulation, on top of the already-taxing memory requirements for parameters, optimizer states, and activations. The paper identifies this as a key limitation:

> "Unlike traditional error-feedback schemes, which require additional memory storage, our method naturally reuses the momentum buffer as an accumulator of uncommunicated information." (Section 2.1, "Momentum Subtraction as Error Feedback")

**2. Communication Frequency Reduction: Local SGD and DiLoCo**

Rather than compressing each communication, another family of methods reduces *how often* communication happens. Local SGD (Stich, 2018; Lin et al., 2018a) and Federated Averaging (McMahan et al., 2017) allow each worker to perform multiple local optimization steps independently before synchronizing parameters. DiLoCo (Douillard et al., 2023) specifically adapts this to language model training by running a full inner-loop optimization on each worker (using a standard optimizer like AdamW), then periodically synchronizing the outer-loop parameters.

The paper acknowledges DiLoCo's empirical success but identifies two concerns (Section 4):

- **Complex interplay with adaptive optimizers**: Adaptive methods like AdamW maintain per-parameter learning rate statistics that evolve based on gradient history. When synchronization is infrequent, these per-worker statistics can drift apart, leading to divergent optimization trajectories. The paper notes this as "complex interplay of infrequent synchronization and local optimizer dynamics... can sometimes lead to less predictable optimization trajectories."
- The paper's comparison experiments (Section 3.5, Figure 6) show that while DiLoCo achieves meaningful compression, DeMo consistently attains lower perplexity at equivalent compression ratios, particularly when targeting very low communication budgets.

The key philosophical difference: DeMo maintains **frequent synchronization** (every step) but makes each synchronization drastically cheaper through compression, rather than preserving full synchronization quality but doing it rarely. This avoids the local drift problem entirely.

**3. Low-Rank Communication: PowerSGD and Subspace Methods**

PowerSGD (Vogels et al., 2019) represents a different compression strategy: instead of sparsifying individual elements, it decomposes gradient matrices into low-rank factors and communicates those factors instead of the full matrix. For a matrix gradient $G \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$, PowerSGD approximates $G \approx U V^\top$ where $U \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times r}$ and $V \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times r}$ with rank $r \ll \min(m, n)$. Communication reduces from $mn$ elements to $r(m + n)$.

The paper's comparison with PowerSGD (Section 3.5) reveals practical limitations:

- Low ranks ($r < 16$) lead to "substantial degradation" — the low-rank approximation discards too much gradient information for language model training, where weight matrices are large and genuinely high-dimensional.
- PowerSGD "performs poorly for language model training in its basic form" — it is essentially compressed SGD, lacking the adaptive preconditioning that Adam-style optimizers provide. To make it work, the authors had to retrofit an "Adam style update rule using the compressed gradients," which is an additional modification beyond the basic method.
- The authors found that a rank of 32 was needed for acceptable performance on their 300M model, yielding only ~26× compression. DeMo with $k=8$ and $s=64$ achieves ~85× compression on the same model while matching AdamW accuracy (Table 1, 300M row, $k=8$).

The fundamental difference: PowerSGD compresses in the original parameter space using a learned low-rank subspace, while DeMo compresses in a fixed *transformed* space using sparsification. The transformed domain makes sparsification genuinely effective — a matrix that is dense in the original basis becomes sparse in the DCT basis, so the top-k selection captures genuinely informative coefficients rather than selectively dropping whole regions of parameter space.

### The Missing Piece: Compressing Momentum Instead of Gradients

The paper's central positioning is that **none of the prior approaches exploit a key structural fact**: in momentum-based optimization, the momentum buffer already accumulates gradient information across time, making it both a better compression target (smoother, more compressible) and a natural error accumulator (eliminating separate error feedback memory).

The critical observation is about why naive gradient sparsification fails. When you sparsify gradients directly, the optimizer sees sparse updates — only a subset of parameters get nonzero updates at each step. This creates an irregular optimization trajectory where some parameters are updated frequently and others rarely. But when you sparsify in the DCT domain, the inverse DCT transformation spreads each sparse coefficient back across *all* parameters in the chunk. As the paper explains:

> "applying a transformation enables parameter updates as linear combinations of many non-sparse vectors. Thereby the parameters are updated more uniformly, especially important when sparsity $k$ is low."

This is the core insight: a single communicated DCT coefficient, after inverse transformation, contributes to updating every parameter in its chunk. The sparsification is in coefficient space, but the *parameter update* is dense. This completely sidesteps the sparse-update pathology of direct gradient sparsification.

### Reconciling the Approach with Prior Work

The paper positions DeMo as synthesizing insights from multiple prior threads into a unified framework:

- **From gradient compression**: DeMo inherits the top-k sparsification mechanism (Lin et al., 2018b; Stich et al., 2018) and error feedback (Karimireddy et al., 2019), but applies them to momentum instead of gradients and eliminates the separate error buffer.
- **From decoupled/local optimization**: DeMo borrows the idea that workers can evolve optimizer states independently (as in Local SGD, DiLoCo), but maintains step-level synchronization to avoid drift — possible because the communication is now cheap enough to afford frequent synchronization.
- **From transform-domain compression**: The DCT projection connects to ideas from signal processing (DCT is the basis for JPEG compression) and from recent work in neural network compression, but is novel in its application to *optimizer state* rather than model parameters or gradients.
- **From momentum-based optimization**: DeMo is explicitly designed to be compatible with any momentum-based optimizer (SGD with momentum, Lion, Muon, Signum), not tied to a specific update rule. The $\phi(\cdot)$ transformation in Equation 7 (Section 2.1) serves as the adapter: $\phi(M) = M$ for SGD, $\phi(M) = \text{sign}(M)$ for Signum, etc. This makes it a drop-in framework rather than a single optimizer.

The paper's novelty is not in any individual component (DCT, top-k, momentum, error feedback) but in the specific combination and the recognition that the momentum buffer can serve double duty as both the communication payload and the error accumulator. This dual role is what enables the memory savings relative to prior error-feedback methods: "our momentum subtraction naturally reuses the momentum buffer as an accumulator of uncommunicated information" (Section 2.1).

### Practical Significance: Enabling Geographically Distributed Training

Beyond the technical contributions, the paper addresses a concrete practical need that motivates the entire research direction. Large-scale training today is **physically constrained**: all GPUs must be in the same building, connected by high-bandwidth interconnects (NVLink at 900 GB/s, InfiniBand at 400 GB/s). This constrains both cost (dedicated networking hardware) and availability (only certain data centers have suitable interconnects).

DeMo's two-orders-of-magnitude communication reduction — from ~637 MB/step to ~7.5 MB/step for the 300M model with $k=8$ — opens the possibility of training across standard Ethernet connections (10–100 Gb/s) or even across geographically distributed data centers connected by the public internet. The paper is explicit about this ambition: "DeMo is topology-agnostic and enables training across multi-datacenter or Ethernet-based setups" (Abstract). This is not merely a cost optimization; it represents a potential shift in the organizational model of large-scale training, from a single well-provisioned cluster to loosely coupled compute pools across multiple sites.

The paper's explicit design for "a small number of geographically distributed compute centers" (Section 5) and its characterization of each data center as "a 'large worker'" that uses internal DDP with DeMo coordinating between centers clarifies this vision: DeMo sits at the inter-datacenter boundary, where bandwidth is most constrained, while standard all-reduce continues to operate efficiently within each datacenter.

### How DeMo Fits into the Broader Optimization Landscape

The paper makes a subtle but important claim about **optimizer compatibility**. DeMo is not a new optimizer — it is a communication wrapper that can be applied to any momentum-based optimizer. The experiments focus on Signum (the sign-based update rule: $\text{sgn}(M_t) + \lambda X_{t-1}$ shown in Equation 7 and Algorithm 1), but the framework supports SGD, Lion, Muon, and any other optimizer that can be expressed in terms of a momentum buffer followed by a transformation $\phi$. This is a strategically important design choice: it means DeMo can be adopted without changing the underlying optimization recipe that has been carefully tuned for a specific model and dataset, reducing the barrier to adoption.

However, this compatibility claim comes with a caveat: the experiments all use the Signum variant, and the convergence proof (Theorem 1/2) is derived for Signum specifically (using $\text{sgn}(M_t)$ in the update). The claim of compatibility with arbitrary momentum-based optimizers is architectural (the decoupled momentum framework supports it) but not empirically validated for optimizers other than Signum in the current paper.

### Summary of the Pre-DeMo Landscape

Prior to this work, a practitioner training a billion-parameter model faced a stark choice:
- **Use full DDP**: Accept the communication bottleneck, pay for expensive interconnects, and constrain to a single physical location.
- **Use gradient compression with error feedback**: Gain ~10–100× compression, but pay ~4 GB extra memory per billion parameters for the error buffer, and deal with the fragility of sparse gradient updates.
- **Use local SGD or DiLoCo**: Drastically reduce communication frequency, but accept unpredictable interactions with adaptive optimizer statistics and potential convergence slowdown from client drift.
- **Use PowerSGD**: Compress via low-rank factors, but accept limited compression ratios (~26×) and the complication of retrofitting Adam-style updates onto an SGD-based compression scheme.

DeMo's positioning is that none of these tradeoffs are necessary. By compressing momentum (not gradients), applying the compression in a transformed domain (not the original parameter basis), and using the momentum buffer itself as the error accumulator (eliminating separate memory), it is possible to achieve ~85× communication reduction with no accuracy degradation and negligible memory overhead — essentially getting the benefits of all prior approaches while avoiding their individual failure modes.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

This is primarily a **systems paper with theoretical grounding** — the core idea is that by shifting communication from gradients to a compressed representation of momentum in the DCT domain, and using the momentum buffer itself as the error accumulator, distributed training can achieve up to two orders of magnitude communication reduction without sacrificing convergence. DeMo is not a new optimizer; it is a communication wrapper that sits between the gradient computation and the parameter update, compatible with any momentum-based optimizer (SGD with momentum, Lion, Muon, Signum) by simply changing the final transformation function `$\phi$` applied to the aggregated momentum.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The DeMo system has five major components:

1. **Local Gradient Computation** — On each of `$N$` workers, the base LLM computes a stochastic gradient `$G^i_t$` from a local microbatch. No gradient all-reduce is performed.

2. **Decoupled Momentum Buffer** — Each worker independently updates its local momentum buffer `$M^i_t = \beta M^i_{t-1} + G^i_t$` using the standard exponential moving average. This buffer evolves *without* any cross-worker synchronization.

3. **Structured Tensor Compression** — Each worker's momentum buffer is partitioned into chunks, transformed via a precomputed blockwise Discrete Cosine Transform (DCT), top-k sparsified to keep only the `$k$` largest-magnitude coefficients per chunk, and the sparse coefficients are transmitted to the parameter server.

4. **Momentum Subtraction as Error Feedback** — After transmission, each worker subtracts the decoded version of the communicated coefficients from its local momentum buffer: `$M^i_t \leftarrow M^i_t - \alpha \cdot \text{IDCT}(\text{Top-k}(\text{DCT}(M^i_t)))$`. This is the critical mechanism that replaces external error feedback buffers.

5. **Server-Side Aggregation and Parameter Update** — The server aggregates the sparse coefficients from all workers, reconstructs the global momentum via inverse DCT, applies a base-optimizer-specific transformation (e.g., `$\text{sgn}(\cdot)$` for Signum), and broadcasts the update to all workers.

Information flows in a loop: gradient → local momentum → chunk-wise DCT → top-k sparsification → transmission → server aggregation → inverse DCT → parameter update → back to gradient computation. The only cross-worker communication is the sparse DCT coefficients.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the formal decoupled momentum update rule (Equation 2), which explains why momentum can replace gradients as the communication target — this is the mathematical linchpin that justifies the entire framework.
- **Second**, the structured tensor compression pipeline (chunking, DCT, top-k), because it is the mechanism that achieves the bandwidth reduction and contains the paper's key design innovations (why DCT? why chunking?).
- **Third**, the momentum subtraction mechanism and its role as error feedback — this is the most subtle part of the design, because it achieves memory-free error accumulation by reusing the momentum buffer in a double role.
- **Fourth**, the server-side aggregation and global momentum reconstruction, which covers how the independently compressed signals from all workers are combined and decoded back into parameter space.
- **Fifth**, the base optimizer adapter via `$\phi(\cdot)$`, which explains how DeMo maintains compatibility with different optimizers by swapping the final transformation.
- **Sixth**, the complexity analysis, which quantifies the computational and memory costs of the compression pipeline and explains why chunking is essential for scalability.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

#### The Decoupled Momentum Update Rule

At the heart of DeMo is a simple but consequential modification to the standard data-parallel optimization loop. In standard DDP, every worker computes a local gradient `$G^i_t$`, performs an all-reduce to compute the global average gradient `$\bar{G}_t = \frac{1}{N} \sum_i G^i_t$`, and then feeds `$\bar{G}_t$` into the optimizer to update a single global momentum buffer. DeMo **removes the gradient all-reduce entirely** and allows each worker to maintain its own local momentum:

$$M^i_t = \beta M^i_{t-1} + G^i_t$$

where `$M^i_t$` is the local momentum buffer of worker `$i$` at step `$t$`, `$\beta \in (0,1)$` is the momentum decay coefficient, and `$G^i_t = \frac{1}{n_{\text{batch}}} \sum_{n=1}^{n_{\text{batch}}} \nabla L(X_t, \xi^i_n)$` is the local stochastic gradient computed on a per-worker microbatch of size `$n_{\text{batch}}$`.

**What it computes:** This is the standard exponential moving average of local gradients — exactly the same update that would occur inside a standard momentum optimizer, but applied independently on each worker without global averaging. The momentum buffer `$M^i_t$` at step `$t$` contains a weighted sum of all past local gradients with exponentially decaying weights: `$M^i_t = \sum_{s=0}^{t} \beta^s G^i_{t-s}$` (ignoring the `$(1-\beta)$` factor convention for clarity). Since `$\beta$` is typically 0.9 or higher, the momentum at any step is dominated by accumulated history, with only a small fraction `$(1-\beta)$` of its mass coming from the most recent gradient.

**Why this form:** The key property is **linearity of accumulation**. Because the momentum update is a linear recurrence with respect to gradients, the global average momentum — which is what a standard DDP optimizer would compute from the global average gradient — is identical to the average of the local momenta:

$$\bar{M}_t = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N M^i_t = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \left( \beta M^i_{t-1} + G^i_t \right) = \beta \bar{M}_{t-1} + \bar{G}_t$$

This means that if we can communicate the local momenta `$M^i_t$` (or a compressed version of them) and aggregate them at the server, we recover the same global momentum that would have been produced by gradient all-reduce followed by central momentum computation. The computation that previously required two steps (all-reduce gradients, then update momentum) now requires only one (all-reduce momenta), and the momentum itself is a more compressible signal than the raw gradient because it is temporally smoothed — the high-frequency per-microbatch noise has been averaged out by the exponential moving average.

The paper experiments with `$\beta = 0.999$` as default (stated in Section 3.1), significantly higher than the standard `$\beta = 0.9$` used in typical momentum optimizers. This choice is intentional: a higher `$\beta$` makes the momentum buffer evolve more slowly, which means the top-k DCT coefficients change less between steps (improving sparsification stability) and the buffer contains more accumulated history (making it more information-dense relative to its size). The `$\beta = 0.2, 0.5, 1.0$` sweep in the experiments (Section 3.1) is for the momentum subtraction coefficient `$\alpha$`, not the momentum decay `$\beta$`.

#### Structured Tensor Compression: Why Chunking, Why DCT, Why Top-k?

The compression pipeline is where DeMo achieves its orders-of-magnitude bandwidth reduction. The pipeline has three sequential stages, each motivated by a specific failure mode of naive gradient sparsification.

**Stage 1: Tensor Chunking**

The paper's momentum tensors are multi-dimensional — the layers of a transformer produce matrices (e.g., weight matrices of shape `$d_{\text{model}} \times d_{\text{model}}$`) and vectors (e.g., layer norm parameters). Applying a global transform like DCT to an entire `$4096 \times 4096$` weight matrix would require `$O(N^3)$` computation and `$O(N^2)$` memory for the transform matrix — or approximately `$4096^3 \approx 68.7$` billion operations per matrix, which is infeasible. Chunking solves this by factorizing each tensor dimension.

The paper formalizes tensor chunking through a **factorization of dimensions**. Given a momentum tensor `$M \in \mathbb{R}^{n_0 \times \cdots \times n_{d-1}}$`, each dimension `$n_i$` is factored as `$n_i = c_i s_i$`, where `$c_i$` is the number of chunks along dimension `$i$` and `$s_i$` is the chunk size. The blocking operator `$\mathcal{B}$` partitions the tensor into smaller blocks:

$$\mathcal{B}(M) = \{B_k \mid k \in [c_0] \times \cdots \times [c_{d-1}]\}$$

where each block `$B_k \in \mathbb{R}^{s_0 \times \cdots \times s_{d-1}}$` and `$[c_i] = \{0, 1, \ldots, c_i - 1\}$`. The default chunk size across all experiments is `$s = 64$` (Section 3.1), which means a `$4096 \times 4096$` matrix is partitioned into `$(4096/64) \times (4096/64) = 64 \times 64 = 4096$` chunks, each of size `$64 \times 64$`.

**What this computes:** The blocking operator extracts contiguous sub-tensors from the original momentum tensor, indexed by a multi-index `$k$`. For a 2D matrix, block `$B_{k_0, k_1}$` contains elements `$M[k_0 s : (k_0+1) s, k_1 s : (k_1+1) s]$`. Each chunk is then processed independently through the DCT and top-k stages. After aggregation and reconstruction, the chunks are concatenated back into the original tensor shape via the inverse blocking operator `$\mathcal{B}^{-1}$`.

**Why this form:** Without chunking, the DCT projection matrices would need to be of size `$n_i \times n_i$` — for a `$4096 \times 4096$` weight matrix, this means a `$4096 \times 4096$` transform, requiring `$O(4096^3)$` operations per multiplication (one multiplication for rows, one for columns). With `$s=64$` chunking, the transform matrices are only `$64 \times 64$`, requiring `$O(4096 \cdot 64^3 / 64) = O(4096 \cdot 64^2)$` operations — a reduction by a factor of `$c_i$`. This is the source of the paper's claim (Section 2.1, Complexity Analysis): "computational complexity reduces linearly to `$O(N^3/C)$`." Additionally, the projection matrices `$\{P_i\}$` are **shared across all chunks** — there is one `$64 \times 64$` DCT matrix per dimension, totaling `$d \times 64 \times 64$` elements of storage, which is constant and negligible relative to model parameters.

The chunk size `$s = 64$` was not arbitrarily chosen — the paper notes (Table 3, rightmost columns) that with larger chunks (e.g., `$s = 128$` at the same `$k$`), the performance degrades: at `$s=64, k=32$`, the 300M model achieves 2.87 loss; at `$s=128, k=32$`, the loss increases to 2.88. This is because larger chunks mean the sparsity ratio `$k/M$` (where `$M = \prod_i s_i$` is the chunk volume) gets smaller — at `$s=64$`, each chunk has `$64 \times 64 = 4096$` elements and `$k=32$` gives a sparsity ratio of `$32/4096 \approx 0.78\%$`. At `$s=128$`, each chunk has `$128 \times 128 = 16384$` elements and the same `$k=32$` gives a sparsity ratio of `$32/16384 \approx 0.20\%$`, which is 4× more aggressive and causes information loss.

**Stage 2: Blockwise Discrete Cosine Transform**

After chunking, each block `$B_k$` undergoes a separable multilinear transform. For a tensor of order `$d$` (e.g., `$d=2$` for a matrix), the transform uses `$d$` projection matrices `$P_i \in \mathbb{R}^{s_i \times s_i}$`:

$$Q_k = \mathcal{T}(B_k; P_0, \ldots, P_{d-1})$$

where the multilinear product `$\mathcal{T}$` is defined entry-wise as:

$$Q_k[i_0, \ldots, i_{d-1}] = \sum_{j_0=1}^{s_0} \cdots \sum_{j_{d-1}=1}^{s_{d-1}} \left( \prod_{\ell=0}^{d-1} (P_\ell)_{i_\ell j_\ell} \right) B_k[j_0, \ldots, j_{d-1}]$$

For the common 2D case, this reduces to **bilinear matrix multiplication**:

$$Q_k = P_0 B_k P_1^\top$$

**What this computes:** The transform applies a separate linear projection along each tensor dimension. For a `$64 \times 64$` matrix chunk, the DCT along rows (multiplying by `$P_0$` on the left) projects each column into the cosine basis, and the DCT along columns (multiplying by `$P_1^\top$` on the right) projects each row. The result `$Q_k$` is a `$64 \times 64$` matrix where each entry is the coefficient of a specific 2D frequency component — low frequencies (smooth variations across the chunk) map to small indices `$i_0, i_1$`, and high frequencies (sharp variations) map to large indices.

**Why this form:** There are two design decisions to justify: the choice of DCT over alternatives, and the separability of the projection.

**1. Why DCT over identity (no projection) or random projection?**

The paper's ablation (Section 3.4, Figure 3) explicitly compares three projection choices: identity (`$P_i = I$`, i.e., no transformation), DCT (`$P_i$` as the DCT basis matrix), and random orthonormal projection (a fresh random orthonormal matrix sampled per step from `$\mathcal{N}(0, I)$` and orthonormalized via Gram-Schmidt). The identity mapping — which is equivalent to applying top-k sparsification directly to the original momentum chunks — performs significantly worse: with `$k=8$`, the identity baseline diverges to much higher loss. This is the core evidence that naive sparsification (the family of methods in Lin et al., 2018b; Stich et al., 2018) fails on momentum because the sparse update pattern creates irregular parameter updates.

The paper's explanation (Section 3.4, Choice of Linear Projections): "applying a transformation enables parameter updates as linear combinations of many non-sparse vectors." Here is what this means mechanically. When top-k sparsification is applied in the original parameter space (identity transform), the sparse coefficients directly correspond to individual parameter elements. After server aggregation and inverse transform, only those `$k$` parameters get nonzero updates — the other `$M - k$` parameters in the chunk receive zero updates. This creates the sparse update pathology.

When DCT is applied first, the top-k selection picks the `$k$` largest-magnitude *frequency coefficients* in the chunk. During the inverse DCT, each of these `$k$` coefficients is multiplied by a basis vector (a cosine wave pattern across the entire chunk) and summed. The result is that *every parameter in the chunk* receives a nonzero update from each communicated coefficient — the update pattern is dense in parameter space even though the communication is sparse in DCT space. This is the crucial distinction: sparsification happens in the transformed domain, but the parameter update is a linear combination of `$k$` dense basis patterns that together cover the entire chunk uniformly.

The random projection performs marginally better than DCT (Figure 3, right panel), but DCT is preferred for two practical reasons. First, the DCT basis matrices `$P_i$` can be **precomputed once** before training and stored — they are fixed orthonormal matrices. Random projections require recomputing a new random orthonormal matrix at every step, which adds computational overhead. Second, the DCT has a fast `$O(s_i \log s_i)$` implementation via the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT), making the projection computationally cheaper than the `$O(s_i^2)$` dense matrix multiplication required by a random projection — though the paper does not exploit this optimization in the reported experiments, it is noted as a practical advantage (Section 2.1: "DCT is computationally more efficient due to its fast implementation with Fast Fourier Transform").

The orthonormality of all projection choices (`$P_i^\top = P_i^{-1}$`) is important for two reasons. First, it ensures that the inverse transform is simply the transpose (no matrix inversion needed), reducing computational cost. Second, it preserves the `$\ell_2$` norm: `$\|\mathcal{T}(B; P_i)\|_2 = \|B\|_2$` for orthonormal `$P_i$`. This isoperimetric property ensures that the energy (information content) of the momentum signal is preserved through the transform — large-magnitude coefficients in the transform domain genuinely correspond to structurally important components of the momentum, not artifacts of the transform's scaling. Lemma 8.1 of the paper's theoretical analysis establishes the `$\ell_2$`-norm bound: for the approximation error from top-k sparsification, `$\|X^* - X\|_2 \leq \sqrt{1 - k/M} \|X\|_2$` under an isometric transform, which is the sharpest possible bound and is achieved only when the transform-domain entries have equal magnitude.

**2. Why separable projection (row × column) instead of a full 2D transform?**

A full 2D DCT of a `$64 \times 64$` block would require a single `$4096 \times 4096$` projection matrix applied to the flattened chunk vector. This would cost `$O((s_0 s_1)^2)$` operations per chunk — completely defeating the purpose of chunking. The separable approach costs `$O(s_0 s_1 (s_0 + s_1))$` per chunk, which is drastically cheaper. The separability relies on the property that the 2D DCT basis functions are products of 1D DCT basis functions:

$$\text{DCT}_{2D}[i, j][u, v] = \text{DCT}_{1D}[i][u] \cdot \text{DCT}_{1D}[j][v]$$

This factorization allows the 2D transform to be implemented as two successive 1D transforms (rows then columns), which is both mathematically equivalent and computationally efficient. The paper extends this naturally to tensors of order `$d$` with `$d$` successive 1D transforms.

**Stage 3: Top-k Sparsification**

After DCT projection, each worker independently selects the `$k$` coefficients with the largest absolute values from each chunk's transformed representation `$Q_k$`:

$$\hat{Q}_k = \text{Top-k}(Q_k, k), \quad \text{such that } \|\hat{Q}_k\|_0 = k$$

where `$\|\cdot\|_0$` denotes the number of non-zero entries. All other DCT coefficients are set to zero and are not transmitted.

**What this computes:** For each chunk of size `$s_0 \times \cdots \times s_{d-1}$` (containing `$M = \prod_i s_i$` elements), this operation sorts the absolute values of the DCT coefficients, keeps the `$k$` largest, and discards the remaining `$M - k$` coefficients. The retained coefficients form a sparse representation — only `$k$` floating-point numbers need to be communicated per chunk, along with their indices (which identify which frequency components they correspond to).

The compression ratio for each chunk is `$M/k$`. With the default `$s=64$` (so `$M = 4096$` for matrix chunks) and `$k=8$`, this gives `$4096 / 8 = 512$`: only 1/512th of the original chunk data is transmitted. The per-step communication volume per GPU for the 300M model with `$k=8$` is 7.49 MB (Table 1), compared to 636.9 MB for AdamW-DDP — a reduction of 85×.

**Why this form:** The top-k operation in the DCT domain preferentially retains low-frequency coefficients. This is because the momentum tensor, being a temporally smoothed signal dominated by sustained optimization directions, is naturally concentrated at low spatial frequencies — it varies slowly across parameter space. In the DCT domain, this energy concentration means that most of the signal's power is in a small number of low-frequency coefficients. The top-k selection therefore captures the structurally important information while discarding high-frequency noise. This is exactly analogous to JPEG compression, where DCT + quantization + entropy coding achieves dramatic compression ratios by discarding high-frequency DCT coefficients that the human visual system barely perceives.

If top-k were applied in the original parameter space, the selected elements would correspond to individual parameters with the largest-magnitude momentum values — a selection that is dominated by parameters that have accumulated the most gradient signal, not parameters that contain the most useful information for the current step. Parameters with moderate but structurally important momentum values would be repeatedly excluded, and over time this would create blind spots in the optimization. The DCT domain avoids this because each coefficient represents a weighted combination of all parameters in the chunk, so selecting a coefficient implicitly allocates update signal to all parameters.

The paper sweeps `$k \in \{1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32\}$` (Figure 2, Table 1). The key empirical finding (Section 3.2): "a sparsification of just `$k = 2$` is sufficient to achieve better training performance than AdamW." For the 300M model, `$k=2$` yields a loss of 2.93 vs. 2.98 for AdamW-DDP, while transmitting only 1.87 MB/step — a 340× reduction. The marginal benefit of increasing `$k$` beyond 2 is significant in the jump to `$k=4$` or `$k=8$` (loss improves from 2.93 to ~2.89–2.88) but plateaus after `$k=16$` (loss remains at 2.87 for `$k=16$` and `$k=32$`).

For the 1B model, the pattern is similar but with higher absolute `$k$` requirements: `$k=16$` is needed to match AdamW-DDP loss (2.63 vs. 2.73), and `$k=32$` provides marginal further improvement (2.63). The communication for `$k=16$` at 1B scale is 55.16 MB/step vs. 2416.6 MB/step for AdamW-DDP — a 44× reduction. This suggests that larger models benefit from proportionally larger `$k$` to capture the richer momentum structure, but the compression ratio remains substantial.

#### Momentum Subtraction as Error Feedback

After computing the sparse DCT coefficients `$\hat{Q}_k$` and transmitting them to the server, each worker **modifies its own local momentum buffer** by subtracting the reconstructed version of what it just communicated. This is the mechanism that replaces external error feedback buffers:

$$M^i_t \leftarrow M^i_t - \alpha \cdot \mathcal{B}^{-1}\left( \mathcal{T}^{-1}(\hat{Q}_k; P_0^{-1}, \ldots, P_{d-1}^{-1}) \right)$$

where `$\mathcal{T}^{-1}$` is the inverse multilinear transform (applying `$P_i^{-1} = P_i^\top$` since all projectors are orthonormal), `$\mathcal{B}^{-1}$` concatenates chunks back into the original tensor shape, and `$\alpha \in (0, 1]$` is the **momentum subtraction coefficient**.

**What it computes:** For each chunk, the worker takes the sparse DCT coefficients `$\hat{Q}_k$` that it transmitted, applies the inverse DCT to reconstruct a dense tensor in the original parameter space (which approximates the original momentum chunk with only `$k$` nonzero DCT coefficients), and subtracts `$\alpha$` times this reconstruction from the momentum buffer. After subtraction, the momentum buffer contains only the *uncommunicated* information — the difference between the true local momentum and the compressed version that was shared with other workers.

**Why this form:** This is the most subtle design decision in DeMo, and the one that distinguishes it from prior sparsification methods. The paper's ablation (Section 3.4, Figure 4, middle panel) shows that `$\alpha = 0$` (no subtraction) is "detrimental." Let's understand why step by step.

**The failure mode without subtraction (`$\alpha = 0$`):** The DCT basis is fixed throughout training. At each step, the worker computes the local momentum `$M^i_t$`, applies DCT, selects the top-k coefficients, and transmits them. Since the momentum evolves slowly (due to high `$\beta$`), the energy distribution in the DCT domain is relatively stable — the same frequency components tend to have large magnitudes across consecutive steps. This means that without subtraction, the **same top-k coefficients would be selected and communicated repeatedly**. The information transmitted at step `$t+1$` would be largely redundant with what was transmitted at step `$t$`, because the uncommunicated portions of the momentum never get a chance to accumulate enough magnitude to break into the top-k selection. The optimization effectively ignores the low-magnitude frequency components permanently, creating a persistent information bottleneck.

**How subtraction fixes this:** By subtracting the communicated coefficients from the momentum buffer, the worker zeroes out (or reduces by factor `$\alpha$`) those frequency components in its local state. At the next step, when the new gradient `$G^i_{t+1}$` is added:

$$M^i_{t+1} = \beta(M^i_t - \alpha \cdot \text{reconstructed}) + G^i_{t+1}$$

the previously communicated frequency components start from a lower baseline, while the previously uncommunicated components have been untouched (accumulated but not subtracted). Over multiple steps, the uncommunicated components accumulate energy in the momentum buffer while the communicated components are repeatedly reset. Eventually, the accumulated uncommunicated components become large enough to break into the top-k selection, ensuring that all frequency components eventually get communicated — just with a delay. This is exactly the function of an external error feedback buffer (Karimireddy et al., 2019), but achieved by reusing the momentum buffer itself.

The paper calls this "momentum subtraction" and explicitly frames it as error feedback without extra storage: "Unlike traditional error-feedback schemes, which require additional memory storage, our method naturally reuses the momentum buffer as an accumulator of uncommunicated information" (Section 2.1, Momentum Subtraction as Error Feedback). The memory savings are proportional to model size — for a 1B-parameter model at FP32, this avoids ~4 GB of dedicated error buffer.

**The role of `$\alpha$`:** The ablation in Figure 4 (middle panel) tests `$\alpha \in \{0.0, 0.1, 0.2, 0.5, 1.0\}$`. The finding: `$\alpha = 1.0$` (full subtraction — remove all communicated signal from the buffer) is better than `$\alpha = 0$` (no subtraction), but `$\alpha = 0.2$` yields the best performance. The paper explains: "using a smaller value (`$\alpha = 0.2$`) to gradually evolve the top-k elements and partially decay communicated values over time further improves performance."

Why would partial subtraction be better than full subtraction? Full subtraction (`$\alpha = 1.0$`) completely removes the communicated frequency components from the momentum buffer, which means the buffer at step `$t+1$` contains zero contribution from those components at step `$t$`. The only signal for those components at step `$t+1$` comes from the new gradient `$G^i_{t+1}$`. This is equivalent to saying: "once we communicate a frequency component, we completely forget it and rely entirely on the next gradient to re-establish it." But the momentum buffer's value is in its *temporal smoothing* — by carrying forward information from previous steps, it denoises the gradient signal. Full subtraction discards this temporal smoothing for the communicated components, creating an asymmetric treatment: communicated components are updated based only on the current gradient, while uncommunicated components benefit from full momentum accumulation.

Partial subtraction (`$\alpha = 0.2$`) removes only 20% of the communicated signal from the buffer each step, leaving 80% to persist and continue being smoothed by the exponential moving average. Over time, the communicated components decay — after 5 steps of repeated communication with `$\alpha = 0.2$`, approximately `$(1-0.2)^5 \approx 33\%$` of the original communicated signal remains. This achieves a balance: enough subtraction that uncommunicated components can catch up and break into the top-k selection (since communicated components are gradually diminished), but not so much subtraction that communicated components lose the benefit of temporal smoothing. The `$\alpha = 0.2$` value was not derived — it was found by grid search over `$\{0.0, 0.1, 0.2, 0.5, 1.0\}$`, and represents an empirical optimum for the 300M model.

The decay of uncommunicated information is controlled jointly by `$\alpha$` and `$\beta$`. When momentum subtraction reduces a communicated component by factor `$\alpha$`, the remaining `$(1-\alpha)$` fraction continues to be governed by the momentum decay `$\beta$` at subsequent steps. The effective time constant for how long communicated information persists in the buffer is determined by the interaction of these two rates, though the paper does not analyze this interaction in detail.

#### Server-Side Aggregation and Global Momentum Reconstruction

After all workers have transmitted their sparse DCT coefficients `$\hat{Q}^i_{k}$`, the server aggregates them using an All-Gather or equivalent operation. The aggregation is not a simple averaging — it uses a **sparse top-k averaging** procedure (Algorithm 3 in the theoretical analysis, Section 8.1.3):

For each chunk index `$c$`, the server maintains a running sum and a count of how many workers contributed a nonzero value for each coefficient position. For each DCT coefficient position `$j$` in the chunk:
- If worker `$i$` included coefficient `$j$` in its top-k selection (i.e., `$\hat{Q}^i_k[j] \neq 0$`), the server adds `$\hat{Q}^i_k[j]$` to the running sum and increments the count for position `$j$`.
- If worker `$i$` did not include coefficient `$j$` in its top-k selection (because it was below the top-k magnitude threshold for that worker), nothing is added.
- After processing all workers, position `$j$` is set to the sum divided by the count (the average over workers that transmitted it), or to 0 if no worker transmitted it.

Formally:
$$\bar{Q}_k[j] = \frac{\sum_{i: j \in \text{TopKIndices}(|Q^i_k|, k)} Q^i_k[j]}{|\{i : j \in \text{TopKIndices}(|Q^i_k|, k)\}|}$$

**What this computes:** The sparse averaging computes a per-coefficient average over the subset of workers that selected that coefficient as top-k. Unlike a standard average over all `$N$` workers, this averages only over the workers that considered the coefficient important enough to communicate. The server then applies the inverse DCT to reconstruct the global momentum chunk:

$$M_t^{[c]} = \mathcal{T}^{-1}(\bar{Q}_k; P_0^\top, \ldots, P_{d-1}^\top)$$

for 2D: `$M_t^{[c]} = P_0^\top \bar{Q}_k P_1$`. This reconstructed chunk is concatenated with all other chunks via `$\mathcal{B}^{-1}$` to form the full global momentum tensor `$M_t$`.

**Why this form:** The sparse averaging is crucial because it handles the fact that different workers may select different top-k coefficients. If worker 1 communicates DCT coefficient `$(3, 7)$` in a chunk (because it has large magnitude in worker 1's local momentum) and worker 2 does not (because coefficient `$(3, 7)$` is below worker 2's top-k threshold), a naive averaging would divide worker 1's value for `$(3, 7)$` by `$N$`, effectively down-weighting it relative to coefficients that all workers selected. The sparse averaging divides by the actual number of contributors for each coefficient position, ensuring that each communicated value receives appropriate weight.

This sparse aggregation introduces a bias-variance tradeoff that is analyzed in Lemma 2 of the theoretical analysis (Section 8.2). The lemma bounds the expected per-coordinate bias `$\mathbb{E}[\delta_i] = \mathbb{E}[|\bar{X}_i - \bar{X}_i^{\text{(full)}}|]$` — the difference between the sparse top-k average and the full average (which would be computed if all `$M$` coefficients were communicated). The bound is:

$$\mathbb{E}[\delta_i] \leq C B \sqrt{\frac{1}{N}} \left( 1 + \sqrt{\frac{M}{k}} \right)$$

where `$C$` is a universal constant, `$B$` is a bound on the absolute magnitude of individual coefficients, `$N$` is the number of workers, `$M$` is the chunk size, and `$k$` is the sparsity level. The key insight: the bias decays as `$1/\sqrt{N}$` (more workers means each coefficient is more likely to be selected by at least some workers) and grows as `$\sqrt{M/k}$` (more aggressive sparsification introduces more bias). When `$k = M$` (no sparsification), the bias vanishes as expected. When `$k \ll M$`, the bias is dominated by the `$\sqrt{M/k}$` term, which quantifies the information loss from not communicating all coefficients.

After reconstruction, the server applies a base-optimizer-specific transformation `$\phi$` to the global momentum (see next subsection), computes the parameter update, and broadcasts the result to all workers. The workers then update their local parameter copies in unison.

#### Base Optimizer Adapter: The `$\phi(\cdot)$` Transformation

DeMo's claim to be optimizer-agnostic is implemented through a single transformation function applied to the aggregated global momentum:

$$X_{t+1} = X_t - \eta_t \big( \phi(M_t^*) + \lambda X_t \big)$$

where `$X_t$` are the model parameters, `$\eta_t$` is the learning rate, `$\lambda$` is the weight decay coefficient, and `$M_t^*$` is the reconstructed global momentum. The function `$\phi$` encodes the specific optimizer's update logic:

- **SGD with momentum:** `$\phi(M) = M$` — the momentum itself is directly the update direction.
- **Signum (used in all experiments):** `$\phi(M) = \text{sgn}(M)$` — elementwise sign function, reducing the update to `$\pm 1$` per parameter. This is the optimizer the paper's convergence proof is derived for (Theorem 1 uses `$\text{sgn}(\cdot)$` explicitly in the update step, and the proof in Section 8.2 relies on the sign-based coupling between `$\nabla L$` and `$\text{sgn}(M_{t+1})$`).
- **Muon:** `$\phi(M) = M(M^\top M + \epsilon I)^{-1/2}$` — for matrix-shaped parameters, this performs Newton-Schulz orthogonalization.
- **Lion:** Not explicitly described in the paper's text, but listed as compatible in Section 1.

**What this computes:** After the server has reconstructed the global momentum `$M_t^*$`, `$\phi$` transforms this momentum into a parameter update direction. For Signum, this means taking the sign of each element — the update is purely `$+\eta_t$`, `$-\eta_t$`, or `$0$` per parameter, independent of momentum magnitude. For SGD, the update is proportional to the momentum magnitude.

**Why this form:** This adapter pattern decouples the communication mechanism from the optimization algorithm. The entire DeMo pipeline — local momentum accumulation, DCT compression, top-k sparsification, momentum subtraction, aggregation — operates identically regardless of the base optimizer. Only the final `$\phi$` differs. This means practitioners can swap DeMo into an existing training pipeline by replacing the optimizer's communication step without changing the optimizer's update rule, hyperparameters, or scheduling. The paper's experiments use Signum exclusively, so the empirical evidence for compatibility with other optimizers is limited to the architectural claim — no results for DeMo+SGD or DeMo+Muon are presented, though the Signum experiments demonstrate the framework's viability.

The weight decay term `$\lambda X_t$` is applied in the parameter space, not the momentum space, following standard practice for decoupled weight decay (as in AdamW). The learning rate schedule uses "linear warmup followed by cosine decay, scaled proportionally to the total training steps" (Section 3.1), which is standard for LLM pretraining and independent of DeMo's communication mechanism.

#### Complexity Analysis: Why DeMo Scales

The paper's complexity analysis (Section 2.1, Complexity Analysis) quantifies the computational and memory costs of the compression pipeline and explains why chunking is not just an optimization but a necessity for large models.

**Computational complexity.** For a momentum matrix of size `$N \times N$` (e.g., a weight matrix in a transformer), the naive approach — applying a full DCT transform without chunking — would require:
- Projection matrix storage: one `$N^2 \times N^2$` matrix (unusable for large `$N$`).
- Operations per transform: `$O(N^3)$` for the bilinear multiplication `$P_0 M P_1^\top$` where `$P_0, P_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}$`.

With chunking into `$C^2$` blocks of size `$(N/C) \times (N/C)$`:
- There are `$C^2$` chunks, each of size `$(N/C) \times (N/C)$`.
- Each chunk's DCT costs `$O((N/C)^3)$` operations.
- Total cost: `$C^2 \cdot O((N/C)^3) = O(N^3 / C)$`.
- Reduction factor: `$C$` (linear reduction in compute).

For a concrete example: a `$4096 \times 4096$` weight matrix with `$s = 64$` (so `$C = 4096/64 = 64$` chunks per dimension, total `$C^2 = 4096$` chunks). The per-chunk DCT on a `$64 \times 64$` block costs `$O(64^3) = O(262,144)$` operations. Total: `$4096 \cdot 262,144 \approx 1.07 \times 10^9$` operations. Without chunking, the full `$4096 \times 4096$` DCT would cost `$O(4096^3) \approx 6.87 \times 10^{10}$` — a 64× reduction, exactly matching the `$C$` factor.

**Memory complexity.** The projection matrices `$\{P_i\}_{i=0}^{d-1}$` are of size `$s_i \times s_i$` each and are **shared across all chunks**. Storage: `$\sum_i s_i^2$` elements, which is constant with respect to model size. For `$s = 64$` and `$d = 2$` (matrix parameters), this is `$64^2 + 64^2 = 8192$` elements — negligible. Without chunking, the projection matrices would be `$n_i \times n_i$`, requiring `$O(N^2)$` storage — e.g., `$4096^2 \approx 16.8$` million elements per matrix, or `$\sim 67$` MB per matrix at FP32, which would quickly dominate GPU memory for a model with hundreds of weight matrices.

The paper additionally notes (Section 2.1): "Since all momentum tensors share the same set of projection matrices `$\{P_i\}_{i=0}^{d-1}$`, the additional memory overhead remains constant and negligible compared to the overall memory requirements." This means the `$s_i \times s_i$` DCT matrices are stored once and reused for every weight matrix in the model, regardless of how many layers or weight tensors exist. This is the key scalability property: adding more layers increases model parameters and therefore increases the number of chunks to process, but does not increase the storage for projection matrices.

**Communication complexity.** The communication reduction factor per chunk is `$M/k$` where `$M = \prod_i s_i$` is the number of elements per chunk. For matrix chunks with `$s = 64$` (so `$M = 4096$`) and `$k = 8$`, this gives `$4096/8 = 512$` per chunk. The total communication for the model is determined by the number of chunks and the sparsity level `$k$`. The paper reports concrete per-GPU communication volumes in Table 1 and Table 3, which account for all parameter tensors (weight matrices, biases, layer norms) across the entire model. For the 300M model, the total per-GPU communication at `$k=8$` is 7.49 MB/step — this is the aggregate across all chunks in all layers.

The paper explicitly notes a crucial asymmetry in communication patterns: "the download bandwidth scales with the number of workers" (Section 5). The upload from each worker to the server is `$O(k)$` per chunk independent of the number of workers (each worker sends only its own top-k coefficients). But the download from server to workers scales with `$N$` because the server broadcasts the *aggregated* (non-sparse) momentum to all workers — or, in the Signum variant, broadcasts only `$\text{sgn}(M_t)$`, which is `$O(D)$` but can be represented as bit-level signals (1 bit per parameter for the sign, plus the floating-point `$\text{sgn}(M_t)$` values for exact application). The paper acknowledges this is "not unique to our method but is intrinsic to all top-k sparsification-based approaches" (Section 5) and positions DeMo for use cases with "a small number of geographically distributed compute centers" where the number of workers is modest (e.g., 4–16 data centers, each treated as a single "large worker").

#### Implementation Details and Design Choices

The paper reports specific implementation choices that practitioners need to replicate:

**DCT basis precomputation.** The DCT matrices `$P_i$` are computed once before training begins (Section 2.1: "precomputed only once before training"). For chunk size `$s = 64$`, this produces a `$64 \times 64$` orthonormal matrix whose `$(i, j)$` entry is:

$$P_i[u, v] = c_u \cos\left( \frac{(2v + 1) u \pi}{2s} \right)$$

where `$c_u = 1/\sqrt{s}$` for `$u = 0$` and `$c_u = \sqrt{2/s}$` for `$u > 0$`. This is the standard DCT-II basis (used in JPEG compression). The orthonormality ensures `$P_i^\top = P_i^{-1}$`.

**Chunking for non-matrix tensors.** The paper applies the same `$s=64$` chunking to vector tensors (e.g., layer norm parameters of shape `$d_{\text{model}}$`) by treating them as 1D tensors of length `$n$` with chunk size `$s=64$`. For a vector of length `$4096$`, this yields `$4096/64 = 64$` 1D chunks, each processed with a single `$64 \times 64$` DCT matrix. The top-k sparsification operates on these 1D chunks identically to the 2D case.

**Top-k implementation.** The paper specifies that top-k selection operates on the *absolute values* of the DCT coefficients: "threshold `$\leftarrow$` k-th largest magnitude in `$|Q^i_{k,t}|$`" (Algorithm 4, line 11). This means the selection criterion is purely magnitude-based — the sign (positive or negative) of the coefficient is preserved in the communicated value, but the sign does not affect whether the coefficient is selected. This is important because in the DCT domain, a coefficient's sign determines whether the corresponding cosine wave constructively or destructively interferes with other frequency components — large negative coefficients are just as structurally important as large positive ones.

**Sparse encoding.** The paper transmits the sparse coefficients as `$\text{encode}(Q^i_{k,t})$` (Algorithm 4, line 16 and 19), which implicitly includes both the coefficient values and their indices (which frequency components they correspond to). The exact encoding format is not specified, but a typical implementation would transmit pairs of (index, value) for each of the `$k$` selected coefficients per chunk. The index overhead is `$k \cdot \log_2(M)$` bits per chunk — for `$M=4096$` and `$k=8$`, this is `$8 \cdot 12 = 96$` bits of index data plus `$8 \cdot 32 = 256$` bits of value data (assuming FP32), totaling 352 bits = 44 bytes per chunk. This index overhead is already accounted for in the paper's reported communication volumes (Table 1, "Data Tx MB/step" column).

**Momentum subtraction implementation.** The subtraction is applied in-place to the local momentum buffer: `$B_k(M^i_t) \leftarrow B_k(M^i_t) - \mathcal{T}(Q^i_{k,t}, P_0^\top, \ldots, P_{d-1}^\top)$` (Algorithm 4, line 14). This means the subtraction uses the exact same DCT coefficients that were transmitted — the worker knows both what it sent and how those coefficients decode back into parameter space, so it can perform the subtraction locally without waiting for server response. This is critical for the pipeline: the worker sends the coefficients, subtracts them from its buffer, and immediately proceeds to the next step's gradient computation while the server handles aggregation asynchronously (though the paper does not specify whether the implementation uses asynchronous or synchronous communication).

**Parameter server pattern.** The paper uses an explicit parameter server architecture: workers send to a server, the server aggregates and broadcasts back (Algorithm 1, lines 10–16). In practice with standard all-reduce frameworks, this would be implemented as an All-Gather of the sparse coefficients followed by local aggregation on each worker, which is functionally equivalent. The paper's implementation uses PyTorch Distributed with "disabled default gradient synchronization" (Section 3.1, Implementation) and a custom DeMo optimizer class that handles the communication.

**Batch size and gradient accumulation.** The experiments use a global batch size of 2048 with sequence length 2048 and 4 gradient accumulation steps (Section 3.1). With 64 GPUs, this means each GPU processes `$2048 / (64 \times 4) = 8$` sequences per micro-batch. The gradient accumulation means that 4 micro-batch gradients are computed sequentially and summed before the momentum update and communication — DeMo communicates once per optimizer step (after accumulation), not once per micro-batch.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Reframing the Communication Target from Gradients to Momentum

The dominant assumption in distributed training optimization since the inception of data parallelism has been that **gradients are what must be communicated**. This assumption is so deeply embedded that the entire literature on communication-efficient training — gradient sparsification (Aji and Heafield, 2017; Lin et al., 2018b), gradient quantization (Alistarh et al., 2017), gradient low-rank projection (Vogels et al., 2019) — operates on the premise that the gradient tensor is the fundamental unit of inter-worker information exchange. The gradient all-reduce is treated as an atomic operation: compute local gradients, synchronize them, then do whatever the optimizer requires.

DeMo makes a conceptual move that is simple in retrospect but required breaking from this entrenched assumption: **the momentum buffer, not the per-step gradient, should be the communication target**. The justification is not merely that momentum is smoother and therefore more compressible (though this is true). The deeper insight is about what constitutes *information* in an optimization process. A single gradient `G^i_t` computed on a microbatch of size 8 (as in the paper's experiments) is a noisy estimate of the true loss gradient — most of its structure is sampling noise that cancels out when averaged across workers and across time. The momentum buffer `M^i_t = \beta M^i_{t-1} + G^i_t` with `\beta = 0.999` (the paper's default) effectively averages gradients over a time window of approximately `1/(1-\beta) = 1000` steps. This temporally denoised signal captures the *sustained optimization direction* — the part of the gradient that persists across data samples and training steps.

What makes this a fundamental rather than incremental shift is the **reversal of the communication/computation ordering**. In standard DDP, communication (gradient all-reduce) precedes optimizer state update (momentum computation). In DeMo, each worker first updates its local momentum (using only local gradient information), then communicates the momentum (or a compressed version). This reordering is mathematically equivalent due to linearity of the momentum recurrence — aggregating local momenta yields the same result as aggregating gradients then computing momentum — but it has profound system-level consequences. The communicated quantity is now the temporally smoothed, information-dense optimizer state rather than the high-variance per-step gradient, which fundamentally changes what compression techniques are viable and effective.

The paper's experiments validate this framing through an implicit comparison: prior gradient compression methods (Lin et al., 2018b; Stich et al., 2018) applied top-k sparsification to gradients and required explicit error feedback buffers, yet still degraded performance. DeMo applies top-k sparsification to momentum and achieves **no degradation** (Table 1: 300M model with `k=8` matches or exceeds AdamW-DDP accuracy on all three benchmarks while transmitting 85× less data). The compression target matters enormously — not just quantitatively but qualitatively, because the structure of momentum (temporally smooth, energy-concentrated at low frequencies) is what makes transform-domain sparsification viable in the first place.

This reframing also explains why DeMo's design is not merely "gradient compression applied to momentum." Gradient compression operates on a per-step signal that resets after communication; momentum compression operates on a persistent state that carries information across steps. The momentum buffer's persistence is what enables the momentum subtraction mechanism (Innovation 2) — a property that has no analog in gradient compression schemes because gradients are ephemeral.

### Innovation 2: The Momentum Buffer as a Dual-Use Data Structure (Payload + Error Accumulator)

Error feedback (Karimireddy et al., 2019; Seide et al., 2014) has been the standard solution to the sparse-update pathology in gradient compression since its introduction. The mechanism is conceptually straightforward: maintain a separate memory buffer that stores the difference between the true gradient and its compressed version (the "error"), then add this error back into the gradient at the next step before compression. This ensures that gradient information is never permanently lost — only delayed. The cost, however, is equally straightforward: an additional memory allocation proportional to model size. For a billion-parameter model at FP32, this is ~4 GB of GPU memory dedicated exclusively to tracking what was omitted from compression, on top of the ~12 GB already required for parameters, gradients, and optimizer states (momentum, variance in Adam).

DeMo's most architecturally elegant contribution is the recognition that **the momentum buffer itself can serve as the error accumulator**, eliminating the need for a separate error feedback buffer entirely. The mechanism (momentum subtraction, detailed in Section 3) is simple: after transmitting the compressed representation of the local momentum, the worker subtracts the decoded version of what it sent from its own momentum buffer. The buffer then holds only the uncommunicated residual. At the next step, the new gradient `G^i_{t+1}` is added, and the residual from previous steps remains in the buffer (modulated by momentum decay `\beta` and the subtraction coefficient `\alpha`), accumulating until it becomes large enough to be selected by top-k and transmitted.

What makes this a genuine innovation rather than an engineering trick is the **conceptual unification** of two previously separate system components — the optimization state (momentum) and the compression error state (error feedback buffer) — into a single data structure that serves both purposes. This unification relies on a specific property: the momentum buffer already accumulates gradient information across time (that's its purpose as an optimizer state), and this accumulation behavior is precisely what an error feedback buffer needs to do (accumulate uncommunicated gradient information). The two accumulation processes are different in their details (momentum accumulation uses exponential moving average; error feedback accumulation sums residuals directly), but the paper shows that a single buffer with the right update rule (momentum + gradient addition, then partial subtraction of communicated values) can serve both roles simultaneously.

The significance extends beyond the memory savings (~4 GB per billion parameters). It eliminates a failure mode that affects separate error feedback designs: the risk of the error buffer and the optimizer state becoming inconsistent. When error feedback and momentum are maintained in separate buffers, the optimizer updates parameters based on momentum (which reflects compressed-then-decompressed gradients), while the error buffer tracks the raw gradient minus the compressed version. These two signals evolve under different update rules and can drift apart, particularly under aggressive compression. By unifying them, DeMo ensures that what the optimizer "sees" (the momentum buffer) is always consistent with what remains to be communicated (the residual in that same buffer after subtraction).

The practical impact is demonstrated in Figure 4 (middle panel): the `\alpha = 0` ablation (no momentum subtraction) diverges significantly, confirming that error accumulation is necessary. The `\alpha = 0.2` vs. `\alpha = 1.0` comparison reveals a subtlety: full subtraction (`\alpha = 1.0`) eliminates communicated information from the buffer entirely, which means the buffer for communicated frequency components has zero memory — equivalent to using raw gradients without momentum smoothing for those components. Partial subtraction (`\alpha = 0.2`) preserves some temporal smoothing for communicated components while still allowing uncommunicated components to accumulate and eventually break into the top-k selection. This tuning of `\alpha` is essentially tuning the balance between the buffer's two roles: optimizer state persistence vs. error accumulation aggressiveness.

### Innovation 3: Transform-Domain Sparsification as a Resolution to the Sparse-Update Pathology

The field has long understood that directly sparsifying gradient or parameter updates — transmitting only the top-k elements and applying them as sparse updates — degrades optimization. The core problem: parameters that fall below the sparsification threshold for multiple consecutive steps receive no training signal, creating an irregular optimization landscape where some parameters are updated frequently and others are starved. This is not merely a convergence slowdown; it can cause the optimizer to converge to a different (worse) local minimum because the effective update trajectory is constrained to a low-dimensional subspace of parameter space.

The standard solution — error feedback — addresses this by eventually communicating the accumulated residuals, but it does so as a patch: the sparse updates are still applied as sparse updates to individual parameters; the error feedback just ensures that no gradient information is permanently lost. The *shape* of the update at each step remains sparse.

DeMo's innovation is to sidestep the sparse-update pathology entirely by applying sparsification in a **transformed domain** where the basis functions span the entire parameter chunk. The mechanics (chunk-wise DCT + top-k + inverse DCT) are described in Section 3. The conceptual insight is that this sequence of operations maps a sparse selection of `k` coefficients into a **dense parameter update**. Each retained DCT coefficient, after inverse transformation, contributes to updating *every parameter in its chunk*. The sparsity is in coefficient space (only `k` of `M` coefficients are communicated), but the parameter update is a linear combination of `k` dense basis vectors, each of which is a full-resolution spatial pattern (a cosine wave at a particular frequency).

This is fundamentally different from error-feedback-augmented gradient sparsification. In the latter, the update to parameter `j` at step `t` is either `-\eta \cdot \text{gradient}_t[j]` (if it was top-k selected, possibly plus accumulated error) or `0` (if it was not). The update pattern is binary at the parameter level: each parameter either gets an update or doesn't. In DeMo, the update to parameter `j` is always `-\eta \cdot \phi(\text{reconstructed_momentum}[j])`, and the reconstructed momentum at position `j` receives contributions from every communicated DCT coefficient whose basis function has support at position `j`. Since DCT basis functions are global (each cosine wave extends across the entire chunk), this means every communicated coefficient contributes to every parameter. The sparsity is entirely in the representational bottleneck — only `k` numbers cross the network — but the parameter space sees dense, spatially structured updates.

The ablation in Figure 3 (left panel) provides the direct evidence: identity mapping (`P_i = I`, i.e., top-k in the original parameter space) performs substantially worse than DCT across all tested sparsity levels (`k \in \{8, 16, 32\}`). This is not a small difference — it is the difference between convergence and divergence at aggressive sparsity levels. The paper's explanation (Section 3.4) captures the intuition concisely: "applying a transformation enables parameter updates as linear combinations of many non-sparse vectors," but the deeper point is that this transforms the nature of the compression from *selecting which parameters to update* (a combinatorial choice that creates winners and losers) to *selecting which spatial frequencies to communicate* (an information-theoretic choice where all parameters receive updates, just with varying spectral composition).

The DCT basis specifically is a particularly good fit for momentum tensors because of the **energy compaction** property of the DCT: for signals that are spatially smooth (vary slowly across neighboring parameters), most of the signal energy concentrates in a small number of low-frequency DCT coefficients. Momentum tensors, being exponentially weighted averages of past gradients, are naturally smooth — adjacent parameters in a weight matrix tend to receive correlated gradients because they process correlated inputs. Top-k selection in the DCT domain therefore naturally retains the low-frequency coefficients that capture the broad structure of the momentum while discarding high-frequency coefficients that mostly represent noise. This spectral perspective explains why the compression is lossy but not destructive: the discarded high-frequency information is predominantly noise that would cancel out across workers anyway, while the retained low-frequency information captures the coordinated gradient structure that drives optimization progress.

The theoretical analysis (Lemma 8.1) provides the formal justification: under an isometric transform, the approximation error from keeping top-k coefficients is `\|X^* - X\| \leq \sqrt{1 - k/M} \|X\|`. The bound is sharp (attained when the transform-domain coefficients have equal magnitude) and independent of the specific isometric transform used. This means that the worst-case information loss depends only on the sparsity ratio `k/M`, not on which orthogonal basis is used. However, the *average-case* performance — which determines actual training outcomes — depends heavily on the basis because a well-chosen basis (like DCT for smooth signals) concentrates more energy in fewer coefficients, making the actual error much smaller than the worst-case bound.

The choice of DCT over random projections (Figure 3, right panel) reflects a pragmatic insight: both achieve the same fundamental goal of making the top-k selection produce dense parameter updates, but DCT is computationally cheaper (precomputable, FFT-accelerable) and deterministic (same basis at every step, enabling the momentum subtraction mechanism to work correctly — if the basis changed every step, subtracting previously communicated coefficients would require transforming them into the new basis, which is intractable). The random projection's marginally better performance (visible in the `k=32` curve) suggests that continuously rotating the basis helps by preventing any single frequency component from persistently dominating the top-k selection, but the paper correctly prioritizes the practical advantages of DCT for a production-ready system.

### Innovation 4: Difficulty-Agnostic Architecture Design as a Strategic Research Choice

This innovation is methodological rather than algorithmic: DeMo's architecture is designed to be **independent of any problem-specific difficulty estimation, task distribution analysis, or adaptive allocation policy**. Unlike the reference paper on compute-optimal test-time scaling (which conditions its entire strategy selection on estimated prompt difficulty), DeMo applies an identical compression and communication pattern to every training step regardless of the optimization landscape, the loss surface curvature, or the current phase of training.

This choice is significant because it represents a deliberate tradeoff in the design space of communication-efficient training. Prior work in this space has explored adaptive compression — varying the sparsity level or quantization precision based on gradient statistics, layer-wise importance heuristics, or training progress. These adaptive methods can theoretically achieve better compression ratios by allocating more communication budget where it matters most, but they introduce several practical complications: they require additional computation to estimate importance metrics, they create variable-length messages that complicate network scheduling, and they add hyperparameters (adaptation schedules, importance thresholds) that must be tuned per model and dataset. DeMo's fixed compression ratio (`k` is constant across all chunks of a given tensor type, and across all training steps) avoids all of these complications without sacrificing convergence quality — a finding that is itself a contribution, because it demonstrates that adaptive allocation is not necessary to achieve 85× compression ratios with maintained accuracy.

The difficulty-agnostic design connects to a broader theme in systems research: the value of **predictable, uniform resource utilization**. In a distributed training system with `N` workers communicating over a network, the bottleneck is often the slowest message. Adaptive compression creates variance in message sizes — some steps transmit more data than others — which can cause network congestion when multiple workers simultaneously send larger-than-expected messages, or under-utilization when all workers send smaller-than-expected messages. DeMo's fixed `k` per chunk means that the communication volume per step is deterministic and identical across all workers (assuming homogeneous model sharding), which simplifies network provisioning, enables precise bandwidth budgeting, and avoids the tail-latency problems that plague adaptive schemes.

This design philosophy is not explicitly argued in the paper — it is embedded in the architecture and validated by the results — but it represents a distinct intellectual contribution to the communication-efficient training literature. The paper demonstrates that uniform, aggressive compression works, and works well, without adaptation. Future work that builds on DeMo to add adaptivity should justify the additional complexity against this strong uniform baseline, rather than assuming adaptivity is necessary.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** All experiments use the **Dolma v1.5 corpus** for pretraining (Section 3.1). The authors train both the 300M and 1B models on **100 billion tokens**, which they note is "significantly beyond the Chinchilla token budget of 20 tokens per parameter." Ablation studies, due to computational constraints, use only the 300M model trained under the 20 tokens-per-parameter rule.

- **Base model(s).** Two decoder-only Transformer models from the OLMo family (Groeneveld et al., 2024) are evaluated: **OLMo-300M** (320 million non-embedding parameters) and **OLMo-1B** (1.18 billion non-embedding parameters). Full model specifications are stated to be in the Appendix but not provided in the main paper text. The OLMo framework is chosen because it provides a "reproducible and scalable platform for training open-weight language models" (Section 3.1). These scales represent small-to-moderate LLMs where communication overhead is already significant but full training runs are still feasible for ablation studies.

- **Metrics.** The primary metrics are **training loss** (cross-entropy, reported in Figures 2–5), **validation perplexity** (reported in Figure 4, left panel, and Figure 6), and **zero-shot downstream accuracy** on three standard benchmarks: HellaSwag (normalized accuracy), ARC-Easy (accuracy), and PIQA (normalized accuracy), reported in Table 1. Communication efficiency is measured as **megabytes transmitted per GPU per training step** (Table 1, "Tx MB/step" column), which accounts for all parameter tensors across the model.

- **Baselines.** The primary baseline is **AdamW-DDP** using the OLMo-recommended default hyperparameters: `$\beta_1 = 0.9$`, weight decay `$\lambda = 0.1$`, with `$\beta_2$` tuned over `$\{0.95, 0.98, 0.99, 0.999\}$` using a reduced 1B-token training budget (Section 3.1). Additional baselines in Section 3.5 include: **Muon-DDP** (Jordan et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025) with recommended momentum 0.95 and learning rate tuned over the same grid as AdamW; **DiLoCo** (Douillard et al., 2023) with outer-loop learning rate tuned over `$\{1.0, 0.7, 0.5, 0.3, 0.1\}$` and communication frequency varied to target different compression ratios; and **PowerSGD** (Vogels et al., 2019) using the official PyTorch DDP communication hook with communication rank swept over `$\{2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64\}$`, rank 32 selected as the preferred setting, and warmup steps tuned (1000 steps chosen over 500 to avoid instability).

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** Communication cost is measured in **megabytes per GPU per training step** (Table 1). This is the natural unit for distributed training communication overhead, as it directly reflects network bandwidth requirements. Notably, the paper does **not** measure wall-clock time or end-to-end throughput, only data volume transmitted. The compute cost of DCT projection and top-k selection is described analytically (Section 2.1, Complexity Analysis) but not empirically profiled.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not employ cross-validation or statistical significance testing for the downstream evaluation results. For the DiLoCo and PowerSGD comparisons, hyperparameters are tuned via grid search on reduced training budgets (Section 3.5). For the main DeMo experiments, the authors "adopt the best recommended learning rates from Groeneveld et al. (2024), since tuning over the full training run would incur infeasible computational costs" (Section 3.1). The momentum subtraction coefficient `$\alpha$` is tuned over `$\{0.0, 0.1, 0.2, 0.5, 1.0\}$` (Section 3.4) and the momentum coefficient `$\beta$` is ablated over `$\{0.95, 0.98, 0.99, 0.995, 0.999\}$` (Section 3.5). All experiments use 64 NVIDIA H100 GPUs with a global batch size of 2048, sequence length 2048, and 4 gradient accumulation steps (effective per-GPU batch size of 8).

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Pretraining Convergence and Communication Efficiency

The paper's central empirical result appears in Figure 2 and Table 1: DeMo achieves comparable or better training loss to AdamW-DDP while transmitting up to two orders of magnitude less data.

**Training loss convergence (Figure 2).** For the 300M model, DeMo with `$k = 2$` (not `$k = 1$`) achieves better training performance than AdamW-DDP. The paper states: "a sparsification of just `$k = 2$` is sufficient to achieve better training performance than AdamW. Increasing `$k$` further provides marginal gains at a greater communication cost" (Section 3.2). The figure shows training loss curves for both 300M and 1B models across `$k \in \{1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32\}$`. At 300M scale, the `$k=2$` curve tracks below AdamW throughout training. At 1B scale, `$k=2$` is slightly above AdamW initially but catches up; `$k=16$` and `$k=32$` clearly outperform AdamW.

The **final training loss** values are reported in Table 3 (the extended results table). For the 300M model:
- AdamW-DDP: loss = 2.98
- DeMo `$k=32$`: loss = 2.87
- DeMo `$k=16$`: loss = 2.87
- DeMo `$k=8$`: loss = 2.88
- DeMo `$k=4$`: loss = 2.89
- DeMo `$k=2$`: loss = 2.93
- DeMo `$k=1$`: loss = 2.97

For the 1B model:
- AdamW-DDP: loss = 2.73
- DeMo `$k=32$`: loss = 2.63
- DeMo `$k=16$`: loss = 2.63
- DeMo `$k=8$`: loss = 2.64
- DeMo `$k=4$`: loss = 2.67
- DeMo `$k=2$`: loss = 2.71
- DeMo `$k=1$`: loss = 2.76

The key observation: **DeMo with `$k=1$` (the most aggressive sparsification) still matches or slightly exceeds AdamW-DDP at 300M scale** (2.97 vs. 2.98), and DeMo with `$k=8$` at 1B scale achieves 2.64 vs. AdamW's 2.73 — a 0.09 absolute loss improvement. The fact that DeMo *outperforms* AdamW is notable; the paper attributes this to the denoising effect of DCT-domain compression implicitly filtering high-frequency gradient noise, though this mechanism is not directly verified.

**Communication reduction (Table 1 and Table 3).** For the 300M model:
- AdamW-DDP: **636.9 MB/step per GPU**
- DeMo `$k=8$`: **7.49 MB/step** — an 85× reduction (636.9 / 7.49 ≈ 85.0)
- DeMo `$k=2$`: **1.87 MB/step** — a 340× reduction
- DeMo `$k=1$`: **0.93 MB/step** — a 685× reduction

For the 1B model:
- AdamW-DDP: **2416.6 MB/step per GPU**
- DeMo `$k=16$`: **55.16 MB/step** — a 44× reduction (2416.6 / 55.16 ≈ 43.8)
- DeMo `$k=8$`: **27.58 MB/step** — an 88× reduction
- DeMo `$k=1$`: **3.44 MB/step** — a 702× reduction

The communication reduction scales nearly linearly with `$k$` because the chunk structure is fixed (`$s=64$`, yielding `$M=4096$` elements per chunk for matrix parameters). The ratio `$M/k = 4096/k$` determines the per-chunk compression factor. For `$k=8$`: `$4096/8 = 512$`. For `$k=32$`: `$4096/32 = 128$`. The observed reduction factors (85× for `$k=8$` at 300M) are lower than the per-chunk compression ratio because not all tensors are matrices (vectors like layer norms get smaller compression factors) and because the DDP baseline communicates FP32 values while DeMo transmits sparse indices plus values, which has some overhead.

**Perplexity vs. communication tradeoff (Figure 4, left panel).** The left panel of Figure 4 plots validation perplexity against communication volume on a log scale. For the 300M model, the AdamW-DDP baseline point sits at `~3.15` perplexity and `~637` MB/step. All DeMo points (annotated with their `$k$` values) sit well to the left (lower communication) and at comparable or lower perplexity. The `$k=32$` point achieves `~3.12` perplexity at `~30` MB/step — a 21× reduction for equal or better quality. The `$k=16$` point achieves `~3.12` at `~15` MB/step — a 42× reduction. At `$k=8$`, perplexity rises slightly to `~3.13` at `~7.5` MB/step. Below `$k=8$`, perplexity degrades more noticeably: `$k=4$` at `~3.14`, `$k=2$` at `~3.16`, `$k=1$` at `~3.19` (still below AdamW's `~3.15`). The Pareto frontier is clearly visible: DeMo dominates AdamW on both axes simultaneously for all `$k \geq 2$`.

For the 1B model, the pattern is similar but with a sharper elbow: `$k=32$` and `$k=16$` achieve the best perplexity (`~2.88`) at `~110` and `~55` MB/step respectively. `$k=8$` rises to `~2.90` at `~28` MB/step. Below `$k=8$`, perplexity degrades more steeply than at 300M scale: `$k=1$` reaches `~2.98` perplexity, which is still better than AdamW's `~3.02`, but the gap narrows considerably.

**Effect of chunk size (Table 3).** Table 3 includes results for `$s = 128$` (larger chunks) alongside the default `$s = 64$`. For the 300M model at the same `$k=32$`:
- `$s=64$`: loss = 2.87, communication = 29.9 MB/step
- `$s=128$`: loss = 2.88, communication = 7.49 MB/step

The communication reduction for `$s=128$` with `$k=32$` is 4× greater (larger chunks mean `$M = 128^2 = 16384$`, so `$M/k = 512$` instead of `$4096/32 = 128$`) at a cost of only 0.01 loss degradation. At more aggressive sparsity levels, the degradation from larger chunks is more severe: `$s=128, k=1$` yields loss = 3.16 vs. 2.97 for `$s=64, k=1$`. This confirms that sparsity ratio `$k/M$` is the controlling variable — larger chunks with the same `$k$` mean a smaller fraction of coefficients retained, which hurts more when `$k$` is already small.

#### Downstream Evaluation (Table 1)

The paper evaluates zero-shot performance on three standard benchmarks after 100B-token pretraining. The full results, including those for `$s=128$` chunks, are in Table 3. Table 1 presents a curated subset. For the 300M model:

- **HellaSwag (normalized accuracy):** AdamW-DDP achieves 0.35. DeMo `$k=8$` and `$k=16$` achieve 0.38 — a +0.03 absolute improvement. DeMo `$k=32$` achieves 0.37. DeMo `$k=4$` and `$k=2$` achieve 0.37 and 0.36 respectively. DeMo `$k=1$` matches AdamW at 0.35.
- **ARC-Easy (accuracy):** AdamW-DDP achieves 0.46. DeMo `$k=16$` achieves 0.50 — a +0.04 improvement. Most other DeMo configurations score 0.45–0.47. DeMo `$k=1$` scores 0.45, slightly below AdamW.
- **PIQA (normalized accuracy):** AdamW-DDP achieves 0.65. DeMo `$k=32$`, `$k=16$`, `$k=8$`, `$k=4$` all achieve 0.67 — a +0.02 improvement. DeMo `$k=2$` and `$k=1$` match AdamW at 0.65.

For the 1B model:

- **HellaSwag:** AdamW-DDP achieves 0.43. DeMo `$k=32$` achieves 0.48 (+0.05). DeMo `$k=16$` and `$k=8$` achieve 0.47. DeMo `$k=4$` achieves 0.45. DeMo `$k=2$` achieves 0.44. DeMo `$k=1$` achieves 0.41, slightly below AdamW.
- **ARC-Easy:** AdamW-DDP achieves 0.51. DeMo `$k=32$` achieves 0.55 (+0.04). DeMo `$k=16$` and `$k=8$` and `$k=4$` achieve 0.52–0.53. DeMo `$k=2$` matches AdamW at 0.51. DeMo `$k=1$` achieves 0.52.
- **PIQA:** AdamW-DDP achieves 0.68. DeMo `$k=32$`, `$k=16$`, `$k=4$` achieve 0.70. DeMo `$k=8$` and `$k=2$` and `$k=1$` achieve 0.69.

The pattern is consistent: DeMo at moderate sparsity (`$k \geq 4$` for 300M, `$k \geq 2$` for 1B) matches or modestly exceeds AdamW-DDP on all three benchmarks, while transmitting dramatically less data. The improvements over AdamW are small in absolute terms (2–5 percentage points) but are replicated across all benchmarks and both model scales, suggesting they are not statistical noise. The paper does not report confidence intervals or standard deviations, so the statistical significance of these differences cannot be assessed from the presented data.

**An important caveat:** The downstream evaluation uses only three benchmarks, all of which are relatively low-resolution for comparing models at this scale (HellaSwag and PIQA are approaching saturation for current models). Broader evaluation (e.g., MMLU, Lambada, OpenBookQA, larger ARC-Challenge) would provide a more complete picture of model quality. The paper's claim that "DeMo matches or exceeds the AdamW-DDP baseline across all tasks" (Section 3.3) is accurate for the three tasks tested but should not be extrapolated to all downstream tasks without further evidence.

#### Comparisons with Communication-Efficient Baselines (Section 3.5)

**AdamW-DDP and Muon-DDP with extensive tuning (Figure 5).** When the AdamW-DDP baseline is extensively tuned (learning rates in `$\{1.5 \times 10^{-3}, \ldots, 2 \times 10^{-4}\}$` and `$\beta_2$` in `$\{0.95, 0.98, 0.99, 0.995, 0.999\}$`), Muon-DDP outperforms AdamW-DDP in training loss. DeMo "underperforms AdamW at equal update counts but provides substantially lower communication cost" (Section 3.5). This is a clarifying result: DeMo's loss advantage over the default AdamW configuration (visible in Figure 2 and Table 3) is partly attributable to AdamW not being fully tuned for the specific training setup, rather than DeMo being inherently better at optimization. When AdamW is properly tuned, it achieves lower loss than DeMo at the same number of steps — but at 85× higher communication cost. The paper presents this honestly but it means the headline "DeMo achieves better training performance than AdamW" should be understood as "DeMo achieves comparable or better performance than a reasonably-but-not-exhaustively tuned AdamW, with drastically less communication."

**DiLoCo comparison (Figure 6).** Figure 6 plots "data receiving compression ratio vs. final validation perplexity" for DeMo and DiLoCo. At comparable compression levels, "DeMo consistently yields lower perplexity than DiLoCo." For example, DeMo with `$k=32$` and `$s=64$` achieves a data-transmission reduction of 128× and data-receiving reduction of 16× (the paper notes the asymmetry: each worker uploads sparse data, but the server broadcasts dense reconstructed momentum, so download compression is lower). The specific perplexity values are not quoted in the text but can be read from Figure 6: DeMo achieves validation perplexity around `~2.92` at `~16×` receiving compression, while DiLoCo achieves `~3.02` at the same compression ratio — a meaningful gap. Training loss curves for DiLoCo with communication frequency 10 are included in Figure 5, where "DeMo again shows superior optimization performance."

**PowerSGD comparison (Section 3.5).** The authors sweep PowerSGD communication rank over `$\{2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64\}$` and find that "ranks below 16 lead to substantial degradation." They select rank 32, which provides approximately 26× compression. With warmup of 1000 steps (required to "avoid the instabilities and divergence"), and with an Adam-style update rule retrofitted onto the compressed gradients (because "PowerSGD is essentially compressed SGD, which performs poorly for language model training in its basic form"), the final performance is "similar to DiLoCo but remains slightly below that of DeMo." The specific loss values for PowerSGD are shown in Figure 5, where PowerSGD converges to higher loss than DeMo, Muon, and AdamW.

#### Hyperparameter Sensitivity

**Momentum subtraction coefficient `$\alpha$` (Figure 4, middle panel).** The ablation sweeps `$\alpha \in \{0.0, 0.1, 0.2, 0.5, 1.0\}$`. Training loss with `$\alpha = 0.0$` (no subtraction) diverges significantly — the curve rises rather than falls, confirming that momentum subtraction is essential. Among the nonzero values, `$\alpha = 0.2$` achieves the lowest training loss, followed by `$\alpha = 0.5$` and `$\alpha = 0.1$`, with `$\alpha = 1.0$` performing worst among the nonzero values (though still far better than `$\alpha = 0$`). The paper's interpretation: "using a smaller value (`$\alpha = 0.2$`) to gradually evolve the top-k elements and partially decay communicated values over time further improves performance" (Section 3.4). This is the only value where the curves are distinguishable with confidence; `$\alpha = 0.1$` and `$\alpha = 0.5$` produce similar loss trajectories and the ranking between them may not be robust.

**Momentum decay `$\beta$` (Figure 4, right panel).** Sweeping `$\beta \in \{0.95, 0.98, 0.99, 0.995, 0.999\}$` with `$k=32$`, `$s=64$`, and `$\alpha = 0.2$`, the paper finds that "performance remains stable across this range, with larger values (0.995, 0.999) outperforming smaller ones. The best results are achieved at `$\beta = 0.995$`" (Section 3.5). The differences between `$\beta = 0.995$` and `$\beta = 0.999$` appear minimal in Figure 4. This high `$\beta$` regime (much higher than the standard 0.9 used in SGD with momentum) is consistent with the paper's earlier justification: high `$\beta$` makes the momentum evolve more slowly, which improves the stability of the top-k DCT coefficient selection across steps and increases the information density of the communicated signal relative to the per-step gradient.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Choice of linear projection (Figure 3):** The paper compares identity mapping (`$P_i = I$`), DCT, and random orthonormal projection across sparsity levels `$k \in \{8, 16, 32\}$`. Identity mapping performs substantially worse than both DCT and random projection at all sparsity levels, with the gap widening dramatically at `$k=8$` (Figure 3, left panel). This is the core evidence that transform-domain sparsification is qualitatively different from — and superior to — direct parameter-space sparsification. Random projection performs marginally better than DCT at `$k=32$` and `$k=16$`, with the curves largely overlapping at `$k=8$` (Figure 3, right panel). The paper correctly characterizes this as "comparable performance" for DCT vs. random projection and defaults to DCT for its computational advantages (precomputable, deterministic).

**Effect of chunk size on downstream performance (Table 3):** Doubling chunk size from `$s=64$` to `$s=128$` while keeping `$k$` constant effectively makes the sparsification 4× more aggressive (since `$M$` quadruples from 4096 to 16384). For the 300M model at `$k=32$`: `$s=64$` achieves HellaSwag 0.37, ARC-Easy 0.46, PIQA 0.67; `$s=128$` achieves 0.37, 0.50, 0.66 — nearly identical. At `$k=8$`: `$s=64$` scores 0.38, 0.47, 0.67; `$s=128$` scores 0.36, 0.49, 0.66 — a small degradation on HellaSwag. At `$k=1$`: `$s=64$` scores 0.35, 0.45, 0.65; `$s=128$` scores 0.31, 0.45, 0.63 — a more substantial degradation on HellaSwag. The interpretation: larger chunks are viable at moderate sparsity ratios (`$k/M$` above ~0.2%) but cause noticeable degradation when the sparsity ratio drops below ~0.1% (i.e., `$k=1$` with `$s=128$`, where `$k/M = 1/16384 \approx 0.006\%$`).

**Extended hyperparameter sweep for AdamW (Figure 5):** This is a genuine robustness check demonstrating that DeMo's apparent advantage over AdamW in Figure 2 is partially explained by AdamW not being fully tuned for the specific experimental setup. When AdamW is extensively tuned (learning rate and `$\beta_2$` grid search), it achieves lower training loss than DeMo at the same step count. However, the paper's core claim is about communication efficiency, not absolute optimization superiority — the point is that DeMo's loss is *close enough* to make the communication savings worthwhile. This ablation clarifies the boundary of the claim.

**Negative result with ReST^EM for revision models (Appendix K, referenced in prior sections):** Not applicable to DeMo — this is discussed as a negative result for the revision model training in the reference compute-optimal paper, not in DeMo.

### Critical Assessment

**Claim: "DeMo reduces per-step communication by up to two orders of magnitude with minimal computational overhead."**

This claim is strongly supported by the empirical data. The 300M model with `$k=8$` achieves 85× communication reduction (7.49 MB/step vs. 636.9 MB/step; Table 1). With `$k=1$`, the reduction reaches 685× (0.93 MB/step). For the 1B model, `$k=8$` achieves 88× reduction (27.58 MB/step vs. 2416.6 MB/step). These are genuine two-orders-of-magnitude reductions. The "minimal computational overhead" claim is supported analytically (Section 2.1, Complexity Analysis) but not empirically profiled — the paper does not report wall-clock time, throughput measurements, or GPU utilization profiles comparing DeMo to AdamW-DDP. The DCT operations on `$64 \times 64$` chunks are cheap in theory (O(N³/C) complexity, which for 4096×4096 matrices with 64×64 chunks is a 64× reduction vs. full DCT), but the actual runtime impact depends on implementation quality, GPU kernel fusion, and whether the DCT computation can be overlapped with communication. Without profiling data, "minimal computational overhead" remains a theoretically justified but empirically unverified claim.

**Claim: "DeMo achieves comparable or better loss and accuracy than AdamW-DDP."**

Supported, but with an important qualification revealed by the ablation in Section 3.5: when AdamW is extensively tuned (learning rate + `$\beta_2$` grid search), it achieves lower training loss than DeMo at equal step counts (Figure 5). The default AdamW configuration used in the main experiments (`$\beta_1=0.9$`, `$\beta_2$` tuned over 4 values on a 1B-token budget) is reasonably but not exhaustively tuned. DeMo's loss advantage (2.87 vs. 2.98 for 300M `$k=32$`; 2.63 vs. 2.73 for 1B `$k=32$`) should therefore be interpreted as: DeMo with a fixed sparse compression scheme matches or exceeds a reasonably-tuned AdamW, not that sparse communication is inherently better for optimization than dense communication. The paper is transparent about this — the comparison is not hidden — but the abstract's unqualified statement that DeMo achieves "comparable loss and accuracy" should be understood in this context.

The downstream evaluation is the stronger evidence: DeMo at moderate sparsity levels matches or slightly exceeds AdamW on HellaSwag, ARC-Easy, and PIQA (Table 1). However, three benchmarks is a thin evaluation suite for language models at this scale. Benchmark saturation is a concern (PIQA at 0.70 for 1B is near the ceiling for models of this size), which limits the resolution for detecting quality differences. The absence of more challenging benchmarks (MMLU, ARC-Challenge, Lambada, etc.) means the claim of "comparable accuracy" is validated on a narrow slice of the evaluation landscape.

**Claim: "DeMo is a drop-in replacement for any momentum-based optimizer."**

Partially supported. The paper's architecture supports different `$\phi$` transformations (SGD, Signum, Muon, Lion), and the theoretical analysis is general for momentum-based methods. However, **all experiments use only the Signum variant** (`$\phi(M) = \text{sgn}(M)$`). There are no empirical results for DeMo+SGD, DeMo+Muon, or DeMo+Lion. The compatibility claim is therefore architectural (DeMo's design allows it) and theoretical (the convergence analysis covers the sign-based case), but not empirically validated for non-Signum optimizers. This is a significant gap because different optimizers have different sensitivity to momentum quality — Muon's Newton-Schulz iteration, for example, might be more sensitive to compression artifacts in the momentum than Signum's coarse sign operation.

**Claim: "DeMo is topology-agnostic and enables training across multi-datacenter or Ethernet-based setups."**

Not directly tested. All experiments use 64 H100 GPUs in what appears to be a single cluster (no mention of cross-datacenter links, variable network latency, or packet loss). The communication volume reduction (to single-digit MB/step) makes Ethernet-viable training plausible — 7.5 MB at 10 Gbps takes ~6 milliseconds to transmit, which is well within typical optimizer step times — but this is an inference from the bandwidth numbers, not an empirical demonstration. The paper does not test DeMo across geographically distributed workers, does not introduce artificial network latency or bandwidth caps, and does not measure training throughput under constrained networking conditions. The "enables" claim is a forward-looking statement about what DeMo makes possible, not what the experiments directly demonstrate.

**Missing experiments that would strengthen the paper:**

- **Wall-clock throughput measurement**: How many tokens per second does DeMo process compared to AdamW-DDP *in practice*, on real hardware? The DCT computation, top-k selection, and sparse communication all add overhead that could offset the bandwidth savings.
- **Larger-scale validation**: Results at 300M and 1B are informative but small by modern standards. 7B or 13B scale experiments would demonstrate whether the `$k$` requirements scale sub-linearly with model size (as the 300M→1B trend suggests) or eventually demand proportionally more coefficients.
- **Non-Signum optimizer results**: At minimum, DeMo+SGD (which is the simplest variant: `$\phi(M) = M$`) would test whether the framework works outside the sign-based setting.
- **Sensitivity to network conditions**: Training with simulated bandwidth caps (1 Gbps, 100 Mbps) and latency (10ms, 50ms) would directly test the multi-datacenter claim.
- **Broader downstream evaluation**: At minimum, MMLU and Lambada would provide higher-resolution evaluation of model quality.
- **Memory profiling**: The paper claims DeMo eliminates the external error feedback buffer, but does not report actual GPU memory usage for DeMo vs. AdamW-DDP vs. gradient compression with error feedback. This is a missed opportunity to quantify the memory savings.

**Conditional nature of claims and boundary conditions:**

- The `$k$` values needed to match AdamW quality vary with model scale: `$k=2$` suffices for 300M, but `$k=16$` is needed for 1B (Table 3). This suggests `$k$` may need to scale with model size, which would reduce compression ratios at larger scales — the 44× reduction at 1B with `$k=16$` is already substantially lower than the 85× reduction at 300M with `$k=8$`. The abstract's "up to 85×" should be understood as the upper end, not the expected compression ratio at all scales.
- DeMo's download bandwidth scales with the number of workers (Section 5), which limits the multi-datacenter use case to scenarios with few (4–16) "large workers" rather than many small workers. This is a genuine architectural limitation, not merely an implementation detail.
- All results are with the Signum variant. The claim of optimizer agnosticism is untested.
- The practical deployability depends on the DCT and top-k operations being efficiently implementable on the specific GPU hardware and framework. The paper's "minimal changes to the codebase" (Section 3.1) suggests PyTorch compatibility, but the efficiency of the custom DeMo optimizer class relative to highly optimized AdamW kernels (e.g., from NVIDIA's apex or PyTorch's fused implementations) is not measured.

**Overall assessment:** The experiments convincingly demonstrate that DeMo achieves 1–2 orders of magnitude communication reduction with maintained or improved model quality at 300M and 1B scales using Signum optimization. The evidence for optimizer agnosticism, multi-datacenter deployment, and practical throughput improvements is suggestive but incomplete. The paper's core contribution — that momentum compression in the DCT domain with built-in error feedback works and works well — is well-supported. The broader claims about drop-in replacement and topology-agnostic training are plausible extrapolations that require additional validation.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### 6.1 All Experiments Use Only the Signum Optimizer Variant

**The assumption or constraint.** DeMo is claimed to be a "drop-in replacement for any momentum-based optimizer" (Abstract) compatible with "SGD with momentum, Lion, and Muon" (Section 1). The architecture supports this through the `φ(·)` adapter function. However, **every experiment in the paper uses only the Signum variant** (`φ(M) = sgn(M)`). The convergence proof (Theorem 1, Section 2.2) is derived specifically for the sign-based update, relying on the coupling between `∇L(X_t)` and `sgn(M_{t+1})` through Lemma 8.3.

**The consequence.** Signum is unusually robust to momentum compression artifacts because its update magnitude is constant per parameter (`±η_t`) regardless of the momentum's absolute magnitude. The sign function discards all magnitude information, retaining only directional information. This makes Signum the **best-case optimizer** for DeMo's compression scheme — any degradation in momentum magnitude due to sparsification is completely eliminated by the sign operation. Optimizers that use momentum magnitude directly (SGD, Muon) or that maintain additional state variables (Adam's second-moment estimate, Lion's sign-based selection) would be exposed to compression errors that Signum masks.

For SGD (`φ(M) = M`), the update is proportional to the reconstructed momentum magnitude. If the DCT-domain sparsification introduces bias in the reconstructed momentum vector (as analyzed in Lemma 2), this bias directly translates to biased parameter updates. For Muon (`φ(M) = M(M^⊤M + εI)^{-1/2}`), the Newton-Schulz orthogonalization operates on the full momentum matrix — compression artifacts could affect the eigenvalue structure and produce unstable orthogonalization. For Lion, which uses a sign operation on one term but magnitude-dependent updates on another, the interaction is more complex. None of these failure modes are tested.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Zero experiments with non-Signum optimizers. The empirical evidence for "drop-in replacement" is restricted to the most compression-tolerant optimizer variant. The theoretical analysis is Signum-specific: the bound in Theorem 1 uses `sgn(M_t)` in the update step (Equation 11), and the proof relies on bounding `⟨∇L(X_t), sgn(∇L(X_t)) — sgn(M_{t+1})⟩` (Equation 12), which exploits Signum's specific geometric properties via Lemma 8.3. The proof does not generalize to arbitrary `φ`.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not acknowledge this as a limitation. The architecture paper-text claims compatibility without qualification. The experiments are silent on the fact that all results are Signum-only. A practitioner reading the abstract would reasonably assume DeMo has been tested with multiple optimizers. Mitigation would require at minimum an SGD experiment to demonstrate compression robustness for magnitude-dependent optimizers.

---

### 6.2 Download Bandwidth Scales with Number of Workers

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's headline communication reduction numbers (85× for 300M `k=8`, 44× for 1B `k=16`) refer only to **upload bandwidth** — the data each worker sends to the parameter server. The paper explicitly acknowledges the asymmetry in Section 5: "the download bandwidth scales with the number of workers. This limitation is not unique to our method but is intrinsic to all top-k sparsification-based approaches."

**The consequence.** The upload compression is `M/k` per chunk (e.g., 4096/8 = 512× for `s=64, k=8`). But the server broadcasts reconstructed momentum to all workers. In the Signum variant, the server can broadcast only `sgn(M_t)` — a single bit per parameter — so download bandwidth is `O(D)` regardless of sparsity. However, for non-Signum variants (`φ(M) = M`, Muon), the server must broadcast the full reconstructed momentum, which is dense (`O(D)` regardless of upload sparsity). The paper explicitly designs for "a small number of geographically distributed compute centers" (Section 5) — e.g., 4–16 data centers, each treated as a "large worker" — precisely because the download cost limits the worker count.

This fundamentally constrains the use case. DeMo is **not suitable for large-N distributed training** with many small workers (e.g., 100+ GPUs each acting as an independent worker, the standard DDP setup). In such scenarios, every worker receives a dense download from the server, and the total download bandwidth across all workers is `N × D`, which grows linearly with `N`. The upload savings become irrelevant when download dominates. The paper's framing of DeMo as "topology-agnostic" and enabling "Ethernet-based setups" (Abstract) is accurate only in the low-N regime.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 1 reports only upload bandwidth ("Tx MB/step" column). Download bandwidth is not measured or reported for any experiment. The paper states in Section 5 that DeMo is "designed primarily for optimization across a small number of geographically distributed compute centers," which is an implicit acknowledgment of the download scaling issue, but provides no quantification of how N affects total communication or end-to-end training throughput. The comparison with DiLoCo (Section 3.5, Figure 6) uses "data-receiving reduction" on the x-axis, which the paper notes is lower than the upload reduction (128× upload vs. 16× download for `k=32, s=64`; Section 3.5), but this number is mentioned in passing without systematic analysis across different N.

**Mitigation status.** The paper partially acknowledges this limitation in the discussion section and proposes a specific deployment pattern (few large workers, standard DDP within each worker) that sidesteps it. This is a reasonable mitigation for the cross-datacenter use case. However, the limitation is not disclosed in the abstract, introduction, or experimental sections where the 85× compression headline is presented. A practitioner evaluating DeMo for a standard large-N cluster deployment would not encounter this constraint until the discussion section. The paper does not propose any mechanism to reduce download bandwidth (e.g., compressing the broadcast, using hierarchical aggregation), and does not characterize at what N the download cost overtakes the upload savings.

---

### 6.3 No Wall-Clock Throughput or Latency Measurements

**The assumption or constraint.** All communication efficiency metrics are in **megabytes transmitted per step** (Table 1, Figure 4 left panel) — a measure of network bandwidth consumed, not of training speed. The paper does not report wall-clock step time, tokens per second, GPU utilization, or any end-to-end throughput measurement. The DCT projection, top-k selection, sparse encoding/decoding, and momentum subtraction all add computation that could offset the bandwidth savings in practice.

**The consequence.** The 85× bandwidth reduction does not directly translate to 85× faster training, or even necessarily to faster training at all. The actual speedup depends on the ratio of communication time to computation time in the baseline system. If the baseline AdamW-DDP is compute-bound (GPUs spend most of their time on forward/backward passes, not waiting for all-reduce), then reducing communication bandwidth may have minimal impact on step time — the GPUs were not waiting on the network anyway. If the baseline is communication-bound (common for large models trained across many GPUs with slower interconnects), then the bandwidth reduction translates more directly to throughput improvement, but the DCT computation overhead must be subtracted.

The paper's "minimal computational overhead" claim (Abstract) is justified analytically — the complexity analysis shows DCT on `64×64` chunks costs `O(N³/C)` operations — but not empirically. A `64×64` DCT via dense matrix multiplication costs `2 × 64³ = 524,288` floating-point operations per chunk. For a model with, say, 50 weight matrices of `4096×4096`, there are `50 × 4096 = 204,800` chunks, meaning `204,800 × 524,288 ≈ 1.07 × 10¹¹` FLOPs for the DCT transforms per step. A single H100 GPU can perform ~10¹⁵ FLOP/s (FP16), so this is ~0.1 milliseconds of compute — negligible in theory. But this analysis assumes optimal GPU kernel implementation, no memory bandwidth bottlenecks, and perfect overlap with communication, none of which are verified.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. No timing measurements, no throughput comparisons, no profiling of the DeMo computation relative to the forward/backward pass. The complexity analysis in Section 2.1 is purely asymptotic; constant factors, GPU kernel launch overhead, and memory access patterns are not considered. The paper's implementation uses a custom DeMo optimizer class with "minimal changes to the codebase" (Section 3.1), but this tells us nothing about whether the resulting code runs at competitive speeds compared to highly optimized AdamW kernels (e.g., NVIDIA's apex, PyTorch's fused implementations, or DeepSpeed's communication-computation overlap).

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper's framing — communication volume reduction as the primary metric — is standard in the communication-efficient training literature (Lin et al., 2018b; Karimireddy et al., 2019; Vogels et al., 2019), and the theoretical throughput improvement is straightforward to estimate for communication-bound scenarios. However, the absence of any throughput data is a significant gap for a systems paper that makes claims about practical deployment. A practitioner considering adopting DeMo needs to know: does this actually make my training faster, and by how much? The paper provides no evidence to answer this question.

---

### 6.4 Single Model Architecture and Modest Model Scales

**The assumption or constraint.** All experiments use two OLMo Transformer decoder-only models at 300M and 1B non-embedding parameters, trained on the Dolma v1.5 corpus. The paper acknowledges this scope implicitly (the experiments are designed around what is "feasible" with 64 H100 GPUs), but does not discuss how findings might generalize to other architectures, larger scales, or different data distributions. The claim that DeMo "significantly relaxes the hardware and co-location constraints for large-scale training" (Section 1) extrapolates from 300M/1B results to the "large-scale" regime of modern LLMs (7B–70B+ parameters).

**The consequence.** Several aspects of DeMo's behavior may not scale favorably to larger models or different architectures. First, the paper's Table 3 shows that the `k` needed to match AdamW quality increases with model scale: `k=2` suffices at 300M, but `k=16` is needed at 1B. If this trend continues linearly or sublinearly, models at 7B parameters might require `k ≈ 64–128` to maintain quality, which would reduce compression ratios proportionally. At 70B, the required `k` could be large enough that the compression advantage becomes marginal. The paper provides no evidence one way or the other.

Second, OLMo-300M and OLMo-1B are relatively shallow and narrow models. Their weight matrices are at most `2048×2048` (1B model hidden dimension), which with chunk size `s=64` produces `32×32 = 1024` chunks per matrix. For a model with `4096` hidden dimension (common at 7B scale), the number of chunks per matrix grows to `64×64 = 4096` — the same `s=64` chunk size means more chunks, more top-k selections, and potentially more total communication unless `k` is reduced proportionally. The interaction between model width, chunk count, and optimal `k` is unexplored.

Third, the DCT basis is well-suited to dense weight matrices where adjacent parameters exhibit correlated gradients. Architectures with structured sparsity (e.g., mixture-of-experts, where only a subset of parameters are active per forward pass) might produce momentum tensors with fundamentally different spectral structure — the energy compaction property that makes DCT effective could break down if gradient correlations are disrupted by routing patterns. The paper studies only dense Transformer architectures.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The 300M → 1B scaling provides two data points, which are insufficient to establish a scaling trend. Table 3 shows that with `s=64`, the `k=16` configuration that achieves 0.38 HellaSwag at 300M achieves 0.47 at 1B (both above their respective AdamW baselines). The `k` requirement appears to increase (1B needs higher `k` to exceed AdamW), but whether this is linear, sublinear, or specific to these two model sizes is unknown. The paper does not train or report results for any model between 300M and 1B, or above 1B.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not discuss generalization to other architectures or larger scales as a limitation. The "large-scale training" framing in the introduction implicitly claims relevance to models substantially larger than the tested ones, without evidence. Mitigation would require experiments at additional scales (at minimum 3B–7B) or a theoretical argument for how `k` requirements scale with model size. The paper's practical contribution would be significantly strengthened by demonstrating that compression ratios remain high (e.g., >50×) at 7B scale with maintained accuracy.

---

### 6.5 The Download Bandwidth Problem is Structural, Not Incidental

**The assumption or constraint.** This limitation is a deeper analysis of the bandwidth asymmetry identified in Limitation 6.2. The paper treats the download scaling issue as a practical detail solvable by constraining DeMo to "a small number of geographically distributed compute centers" (Section 5). But the download problem is **structural to the design**: the server must broadcast the aggregated momentum (or its sign) to all workers so they can update parameters identically. In the Signum variant, the broadcast is `sgn(M_t)`, which is `D` bits plus metadata. In any non-Signum variant, the broadcast is the full reconstructed momentum at `D` floating-point values.

**The consequence.** DeMo achieves upload compression by allowing workers to independently select different top-k coefficients (each worker's top-k depends on its local momentum, which differs due to different microbatches). The sparse aggregation on the server averages coefficients only over the workers that selected them (Algorithm 3). But this means the server's reconstructed momentum is **dense** — even though the upload was sparse, the aggregation fills in every coefficient position from at least one worker's contribution (assuming `N` is large enough relative to `k/M`). The inverse DCT then produces a dense momentum tensor in parameter space.

For the default `φ(M) = sgn(M)`, the server can theoretically compress the broadcast to 1 bit per parameter (the sign), or to a sparse representation of which parameters changed sign since the last step. But this optimization is only possible for Signum, and the paper does not describe or implement it. The reported communication volumes (Table 1) are upload-only.

For multi-datacenter training with `N > 10`, the total download bandwidth `N × D` can easily exceed the total upload bandwidth `N × (kD/M)` even at modest `N`. The threshold where download dominates depends on the sparsity ratio `k/M`: at `k/M = 1/512` (the `k=8, s=64` setting), upload bandwidth is ~0.2% of `D` per worker. With sign-based broadcast at 1 bit per parameter, download is ~3% of `D` per worker (32× larger than upload). With full-float broadcast, download is `D` per worker (512× larger). For `N=8` workers, total download is `8D` for the full-float case — the download cost alone exceeds the baseline AdamW-DDP all-reduce cost (`N × D` for all-reduce vs. `N × D` for broadcast). This means that for non-Signum variants, DeMo provides **no net communication reduction** for any `N` when download is accounted for.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Section 5 acknowledges: "the download bandwidth scales with the number of workers." Section 3.5 states for `k=32, s=64`: "the data-transmission and data-receiving reductions are 128 and 16, respectively (ignoring the marginal contribution of layer norms)." This is a 128× upload reduction but only 16× download reduction — an 8× asymmetry. For `k=8`, the upload reduction is 512× but the download reduction would be correspondingly lower (since server-side `k` is effectively larger than per-worker `k` due to sparse aggregation). The paper provides no systematic analysis of download cost as a function of `N`, `k`, and `M`.

**Mitigation status.** The paper proposes constraining DeMo to low-N deployments, which is a reasonable practical mitigation for the cross-datacenter use case. But the limitation is structural and applies to any attempt to scale DeMo beyond ~16 workers. The paper does not propose any architectural modification to reduce download bandwidth (e.g., compressing the broadcast, using hierarchical aggregation where intermediate servers handle subsets of workers, or having workers independently reconstruct momentum from sparse aggregates). The "this limitation is not unique to our method" framing (Section 5) is technically accurate but does not solve the problem — it merely notes that all top-k methods share it.

### 6.6 Local Momentum Quality Under Prolonged Training with Aggressive Compression

**The assumption or constraint.** DeMo assumes that the local momentum buffer, after momentum subtraction, continues to provide useful gradient denoising for each worker. At moderate sparsity (`k ≥ 8` for 300M, `k ≥ 16` for 1B), this assumption holds — training curves (Figure 2) show smooth convergence. But the mechanism of momentum subtraction continuously removes communicated frequency components from the buffer, leaving only the residual. At very aggressive sparsity (`k=1` or `k=2` with `s=128`), the fraction of momentum buffered retained after subtraction becomes extremely small — most of the buffer's energy is removed at each step.

**The consequence.** In the limit of very aggressive compression, the local momentum buffer contains almost exclusively high-frequency noise — the low-frequency components that capture sustained optimization directions are always top-k selected and subtracted. The per-worker momentum effectively becomes a high-pass-filtered gradient signal. This could cause two problems: (1) The local momentum loses its denoising property, since the sustained signal is removed and only the per-step noise accumulates. (2) The effective momentum time constant seen by the optimizer becomes shorter, because communicated components (which dominate the low-frequency signal) are subtracted with factor `α`, reducing their persistence in the buffer relative to the `β`-weighted accumulation of past gradients.

This is the momentum analog of a well-known phenomenon in gradient compression: aggressive compression with error feedback can cause the effective gradient seen by the optimizer to be biased toward high-frequency components, since low-frequency components are repeatedly selected and subtracted while high-frequency noise accumulates in the error buffer. In DeMo, the same dynamic plays out in momentum space: communicated components are removed, uncommunicated components persist and accumulate, but the uncommunicated components are predominantly high-frequency DCT coefficients that were below the top-k threshold — i.e., they represent the fine-grained, noisy structure of the momentum rather than the broad optimization direction.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Indirect evidence appears in Table 3: for `s=128` (larger chunks = more aggressive effective sparsity per unit of momentum), performance degrades nonlinearly as `k` decreases. At `s=128, k=4`, validation loss is 2.98 (worse than AdamW's 2.98). At `s=128, k=2`, loss jumps to 3.06. At `s=128, k=1`, loss reaches 3.16 — substantially worse than AdamW. The degradation is worse than the corresponding `s=64` configurations at the same `k` (e.g., `s=64, k=1` yields 2.97 vs. 3.16 for `s=128, k=1`), confirming that it is the sparsity ratio `k/M` (which is 4× smaller for `s=128`) and not just `k` that controls quality. The 100B-token training length is significantly beyond Chinchilla-optimal (6B tokens for 300M), so degradation at very aggressive sparsity becomes increasingly visible as training continues — early convergence may look similar, but the gap widens as the optimizer approaches the loss basin.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not analyze this momentum-quality degradation mechanism explicitly. The ablation of `α` (Figure 4, middle panel) shows that smaller `α` (0.2) preserves momentum history better than `α=1.0`, which is consistent with the hypothesis that aggressive subtraction harms momentum quality. But the interaction between `α`, `β`, `k`, and `M` is not analyzed theoretically or empirically beyond the grid search. A more complete analysis would characterize the effective momentum time constant as a function of compression parameters, providing guidance for how to set `α` and `β` given target `k/M`. The paper's recommendation of `β=0.999` and `α=0.2` is an empirical optimum for `k=32, s=64` on the 300M model — its robustness to different compression ratios and model scales is unknown.
