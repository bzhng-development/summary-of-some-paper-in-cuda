# MoE-Mamba: Efficient Selective State Space Models with Mixture of Experts

**ArXiv:** [2401.04081](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.04081)

## 🎯 Pitch

This paper proposes that State Space Models can be scaled more efficiently by combining them with Mixture of Experts, and introduces **MoE-Mamba**, a model that interleaves Mamba layers with Switch Transformer MoE feed-forward layers to separate unconditional sequence processing from conditional per-token expert computation.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper proposes that State Space Models can be scaled more efficiently by combining them with Mixture of Experts, and introduces **MoE-Mamba**, a model that interleaves Mamba layers with Switch Transformer MoE feed-forward layers to separate unconditional sequence processing from conditional per-token expert computation. On the C4 dataset using models up to 2.4B total parameters (121M active parameters), MoE-Mamba reaches the same log perplexity as vanilla Mamba in 2.35× fewer training steps while preserving Mamba's linear-time inference advantages, and consistently outperforms a comparable Transformer-MoE baseline — establishing that the efficiency gains from MoE are complementary to and amplify those of SSM architectures, with the improvement increasing as training proceeds.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: How Do We Scale Language Models Efficiently Beyond Transformers?

The fundamental question this paper tackles sits at the intersection of two major trends in language model architecture design. On one side, the Transformer architecture (Vaswani et al., 2017) has been spectacularly successful, driving virtually all modern large language models from GPT-4 to Llama to Gemini. On the other side, a growing body of work suggests that Transformers may not be the only viable architecture — and may not be the most efficient one, particularly for long sequences or resource-constrained deployment scenarios.

The paper identifies a specific gap: **State Space Models have shown promise as alternatives to Transformers, but they lack a demonstrated path for scaling to the massive parameter counts (hundreds of billions to trillions) that make modern LLMs so capable.** The authors frame this as the natural next step in the evolution of SSM-based architectures. Mamba (Gu & Dao, 2023) demonstrated that SSMs can compete with Transformers on quality at moderate scales, but the question of how to push SSMs further — how to make them economically viable at the extreme scales where Transformers operate — remained open.

This matters for several practical reasons that the paper implicitly addresses:

**The quadratic cost of attention is a fundamental bottleneck.** Transformers scale poorly with sequence length — attention computation grows quadratically with context size, which creates both FLOPs and memory bandwidth bottlenecks during training and inference. As applications demand longer context windows (document-level understanding, multi-turn conversations, codebase-level reasoning), this quadratic cost becomes increasingly burdensome. Mamba and other SSMs offer linear-time sequence processing, which means that if they could match Transformer quality, they would be strictly superior for long-context deployment.

**Inference efficiency is becoming economically critical.** The cost of deploying LLMs at scale is dominated by inference, not training. A model that processes tokens with linear complexity during inference — and with memory usage independent of context length — offers substantial cost advantages over Transformers in production settings. The paper explicitly notes that "the advantages of Mamba over the attention mechanism are especially prominent during inference, as not only is the computational complexity lowered, but also the memory usage is not dependent on the context length" (Section 3.1). This is not merely a theoretical advantage; it translates directly to lower latency, lower hardware requirements, and lower per-query costs.

**The MoE scalability recipe is proven for Transformers but untested for SSMs.** The Mixture of Experts approach has become a standard technique for scaling Transformers: by sparsely activating subsets of parameters for each token, MoE models can grow to trillions of parameters while keeping the per-token computational cost roughly constant. The Switch Transformer (Fedus et al., 2022) demonstrated this for Transformers, and Mixtral 8×7B (Jiang et al., 2024) showed that the approach works in practice, matching Llama 2 70B quality while using approximately 1/6 of the inference compute. But no one had systematically applied MoE to SSM architectures. The paper positions itself as filling exactly this gap: can the same sparse-parameter scaling technique that works for Transformers also work for Mamba, and if so, what are the right design choices?

### Where Prior Approaches Fall Short

The paper identifies specific limitations along several axes:

**Mamba lacks feed-forward layers, which are the natural target for MoE.** In Transformers, the standard playbook for applying MoE is to replace the dense feed-forward layers (typically containing 8d² parameters per layer, or roughly 2/3 of the model) with sparsely-activated expert feed-forward networks. But Mamba — by design — does not use feed-forward layers. A single Mamba block contains approximately 6d² parameters, all within the SSM computation itself. This means the standard MoE recipe cannot be applied directly; there is no obvious "vanilla dense layer" to replace with experts. The paper addresses this by proposing to *interleave* entire MoE feed-forward layers between Mamba blocks (Section 3.2), creating a hybrid architecture where unconditional sequence processing (Mamba's job) is separated from conditional per-token computation (the MoE's job).

**Prior attempts to add feed-forward layers to Mamba hurt performance.** The paper explicitly states (Section 4.5) that "Mamba interleaved with feed-forward layers (which corresponds to a single-expert MoE layer) is worse than vanilla Mamba," citing Gu & Dao (2023). This is a critical finding: simply inserting a dense feed-forward network between Mamba blocks — which would be the naive way to introduce MoE — actually degrades performance. The paper's contribution includes discovering that this degradation can be overcome by making those feed-forward layers *sparse* (multi-expert MoE), such that MoE-Mamba with 4 or more experts outperforms vanilla Mamba. The mechanism for this reversal is not fully explained but is an important empirical result.

**Attention-free architectures have known weaknesses that MoE might compensate for.** The paper observes (Appendix C) that SSM-based models like Mamba may struggle with tasks requiring verbatim token copying or precise recall of past information, because the entire sequence history is compressed into a fixed-size hidden state. Transformers, by contrast, can attend directly to any previous token, making them strong at exact copying and in-context learning (Elhage et al., 2021; Olsson et al., 2022). The paper hypothesizes that Mamba compensates for this limitation in other ways to achieve comparable perplexity, but the weakness remains. MoE could potentially help here: by dedicating different experts to different types of information retrieval or copying patterns, the model might allocate its limited state capacity more effectively. The paper does not test this hypothesis directly, but the observed discrepancy between perplexity and accuracy (Section 4.6) — where Transformer-MoE achieves higher accuracy than MoE-Mamba despite higher perplexity — provides suggestive evidence.

**The scaling properties of SSMs are poorly understood compared to Transformers.** The Transformer architecture has been the subject of extensive scaling laws research (Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022; Clark et al., 2022), giving practitioners clear guidance on how to allocate compute between model depth, width, and training data. For SSMs, this knowledge base is much thinner. The paper does not attempt to derive scaling laws, but the investigation of how MoE-Mamba's performance varies with model size (25M vs. 100M active parameters), number of experts, and training duration provides empirical groundwork for such laws. The finding that speedup *increases* with training duration (Appendix D, Figure 8) — meaning MoE-Mamba's advantage over vanilla Mamba grows as both models train longer — is a non-trivial scaling observation that would not be predicted by simply extrapolating early-training behavior.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper positions MoE-Mamba not as a fundamentally new architecture, but as a **synthesis of two proven techniques from different architectural lineages**: Mamba (the state-of-the-art SSM from the efficient-sequence-modeling line) and Switch Transformer MoE (the state-of-the-art sparsity technique from the Transformer scaling line). The key intellectual move is recognizing that these two techniques address *different* efficiency bottlenecks and can be combined without conflict:

- **Mamba addresses the sequence-length bottleneck**: attention is quadratic in context length; SSMs are linear. This benefit is most pronounced during inference on long sequences.
- **MoE addresses the parameter-count bottleneck**: dense models activate all parameters for every token; MoE activates only a fraction. This benefit applies during both training and inference, reducing the FLOPs-per-token cost of scaling model capacity.

The paper's central claim is that these benefits are **complementary and additive**. MoE-Mamba preserves Mamba's linear-time inference property (because the MoE layers are feed-forward and thus also linear in sequence length), while adding the parameter-efficiency benefits of sparse activation. This is not obvious a priori — one could imagine that adding MoE layers disrupts the careful hardware optimizations that make Mamba fast, or that the interleaved architecture creates bottlenecks that negate the SSM advantages. The paper's experiments are designed to test exactly this.

The paper also positions itself relative to a contemporaneous work, BlackMamba (Anthony et al., 2024), which appeared after the first version of MoE-Mamba was released and similarly combines Mamba with MoE. The authors note this validates their core direction but distinguish their contribution by emphasizing their "extensive ablations on the model architecture, number of experts, and other design choices" (Section 2) — positioning MoE-Mamba as the more thorough empirical investigation.

The framing in Section 5's limitations and future work section reveals the paper's ambition: this is presented as an **opening move in a new research direction**, not a final solution. The authors explicitly call for scaling laws, exploration of different MoE designs, distillation of MoE into Mamba, and investigation of synergies between Mamba's long-context processing and MoE's parameter scaling. This suggests they see MoE-Mamba as establishing the viability of the SSM+MoE combination, with substantial optimization and theoretical work remaining.

A subtle but important aspect of the positioning: the paper uses the Switch Transformer design (top-1 routing, capacity factor of 1) specifically because it is "well-established and easy-to-implement" (Section 3.1), not because it is necessarily optimal for SSMs. The authors explicitly leave consideration of other MoE designs for future work. This is a pragmatic choice that makes the paper's claims about the SSM+MoE *principle* cleaner — by using the simplest, most standardized MoE variant, they reduce the risk that any observed gains are due to MoE design cleverness rather than the fundamental compatibility of SSMs and sparse computation.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader orientation (approachable technical breakdown)

MoE-Mamba is a neural network architecture for language modeling that combines a sequence-processing backbone (Mamba) with a conditional computation mechanism (Mixture of Experts) into a single decoder-only model. The system solves the problem of efficiently scaling State Space Models to large parameter counts by enabling the model to grow its total capacity dramatically through sparse expert layers while keeping per-token active computation roughly constant and preserving the linear-time sequence processing advantages that make Mamba attractive in the first place.

### 3.2 Big-picture architecture (diagram in words)

The architecture has three major component types arranged in a repeating stack:

- **Mamba layers** — the unconditional, all-token processing backbone. Every token passes through every Mamba layer. These layers perform the core sequence modeling, integrating context across the full sequence into each token's representation using linear-time selective state space computations. Each Mamba layer contains approximately `$6d_{\text{model}}^2$` parameters (where `$d_{\text{model}}$` is the model's hidden dimension) and outputs vectors of size `$d_{\text{model}}$` that are added to the residual stream.

- **Switch MoE feed-forward layers** — the conditional, per-token expert computation. These are interleaved between Mamba layers in a sequential pattern. Each MoE layer contains `$N_{\text{experts}}$` separate feed-forward networks (called experts), each with `$d_{\text{expert}}$` hidden units. For each token, exactly one expert is selected via a learned router, and only that expert's computation is performed. This means the total parameter count can be massive (hundreds of millions to billions) while the per-token active computation remains proportional to a single expert's size. The MoE layer output is added to the residual stream.

- **Residual stream (implicit)** — the standard Transformer-style residual pathway. Every Mamba layer and every MoE layer reads from and writes back to this accumulating stream. Embeddings enter at the bottom, layer outputs are added incrementally, and the final representation is projected to vocabulary logits through an unembedding layer.

Information flows as follows: token embeddings enter the stack → a Mamba layer processes the full sequence and adds its output to the residual stream → an MoE layer routes each token position to its selected expert, computes the expert output, and adds it to the residual stream → the process repeats for the total number of blocks (8 blocks for 25M-active-parameter models, 16 blocks for 100M-active-parameter models, where each block contains one Mamba layer and one MoE layer) → the final residual representation is decoded through the output vocabulary projection.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the deep dive

- **First**, the Mamba layer mechanics — the selective SSM computation, the expansion-projection design, and the hardware optimizations — because Mamba is the unconditional processing backbone that every token passes through, and understanding its internal structure is prerequisite to understanding why MoE is added rather than integrated into the Mamba block itself.
- **Second**, the Switch MoE layer — routing, expert selection, load balancing, and capacity — because this is the conditional computation mechanism that enables the parameter scaling gains, and its design choices (top-1 routing, capacity factor 1, load balancing loss) determine the training dynamics.
- **Third**, the MoE-Mamba interleaving pattern — why MoE layers are placed sequentially after Mamba layers rather than in parallel or inside the Mamba block — because this architectural decision is the paper's central design contribution and the ablations in Section 4 validate it against alternatives.
- **Fourth**, the alternative architectures explored (Parallel MoE-Mamba and Inner MoE modifications) and why they underperform — because these negative results are important for understanding the design space and the constraints that make the sequential interleaving effective.
- **Fifth**, the parameter allocation strategy — how the paper distributes active parameters between Mamba layers and MoE layers, including the expansion factor `$E$` and the optimal 3:3 ratio — because this ratio is a crucial hyperparameter that determines the architecture's efficiency frontier.
- **Sixth**, the training configuration and the speedup metric — how the paper measures its core claim of 2.35× training step reduction and what "speedup" means operationally — because this is the paper's headline result and the methodology for computing it matters for interpreting the claim.

### 3.4 Detailed, sentence-based technical breakdown

This is primarily an **empirical architecture design paper** whose core idea is that Mamba's unconditional sequence processing and MoE's conditional per-token computation are complementary mechanisms that can be composed by simply interleaving their respective layers in a residual stack, and that this composition yields multiplicative efficiency gains — the linear-time sequence processing of SSMs combined with the parameter-efficiency of sparse activation.

#### Mamba Layer Mechanics

The Mamba layer (Gu & Dao, 2023) is the fundamental building block that processes sequences with linear complexity in sequence length. Before explaining how MoE layers are added, we must understand what the Mamba layer does internally and why it contains no obvious dense feed-forward component to replace with experts.

**Selective State Space Model core.** At its heart, the Mamba layer implements a state space model (SSM) — a mathematical framework originating in control theory and signal processing that models a system through a latent state vector that evolves over time based on inputs. In the context of language modeling, "time" corresponds to token position in the sequence. The SSM is defined by two equations: a state evolution equation that describes how the hidden state updates as each new token arrives, and an output equation that describes how the current state maps to an output vector for that position. The standard SSM formulation uses fixed (non-input-dependent) state transition matrices, which makes them efficient to compute but limits their expressiveness — they cannot selectively attend to or ignore specific inputs based on content.

**Selective mechanism — the key innovation of Mamba.** What distinguishes Mamba from prior SSMs (such as S4, H3, and DSS) is the **selective mechanism**: the state transition parameters `$\mathbf{A}$` (state matrix), `$\mathbf{B}$` (input projection), and `$\mathbf{C}$` (output projection) are made functions of the input token at each position. Specifically:

- The **input-dependent discretization** makes the effective state transition depend on the current token's content. A small projection network takes the current input and outputs the discretization timestep `$\Delta$`, which controls how much the state is updated versus retained. When `$\Delta$` is large, the state incorporates more of the current input; when small, the state retains more of the past. This allows the model to selectively "pay attention" to certain tokens and "ignore" others based on content — analogous to how attention weights work in Transformers, but without the quadratic cost.

- The **input-dependent B and C matrices** allow different input tokens to project onto the state space (and be read out from it) in different ways. This means the model learns which features of each token to store in the state and which to retrieve, conditioned on the token's content.

The selective mechanism is what gives Mamba its Transformer-competitive performance on language modeling while maintaining linear-time sequence processing. Without it, SSMs are essentially fixed-content filters that cannot adapt their behavior based on whether the current token is a noun, verb, number, or punctuation — a fundamental limitation for language understanding.

**Expansion-projection design.** The Mamba layer follows a specific computational pattern (Figure 3, right panel):

1. The input vector `$\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}}}$` is projected through two separate linear layers: the **Gate Projection** and the **Conv Projection**. Both project the input to a higher-dimensional space of size `$E \cdot d_{\text{model}}$`, where `$E$` is the expansion factor. Vanilla Mamba uses `$E = 2$` as default (Gu & Dao, 2023), meaning the internal representations are twice the model dimension.

2. The Conv path processes the expanded representation through a 1D convolution (for local context) followed by the selective SSM operation (for long-range context). The convolution provides short-range position-dependent processing — essentially a learned local filter over a small window of adjacent tokens.

3. The Gate path applies a SiLU (Sigmoid Linear Unit) activation function — also called Swish — to its expanded projection, producing a gating signal.

4. The SSM output from the Conv path and the gating signal from the Gate path are multiplied element-wise (the "gating" operation), producing a gated representation of size `$E \cdot d_{\text{model}}$`.

5. The Output Projection maps this gated representation back down to `$d_{\text{model}}$`, and the result is added to the residual stream.

**Parameter count.** A single Mamba layer contains "slightly over `$6d_{\text{model}}^2$`" parameters (Section 4.1). This comes from the three projections: the Gate and Conv projections together account for `$2 \times d_{\text{model}} \times (E \cdot d_{\text{model}})$`, the Output projection accounts for `$(E \cdot d_{\text{model}}) \times d_{\text{model}}$`, plus the SSM parameters (A, B, C matrices and the `$\Delta$` projection). For `$E = 2$`, this gives approximately `$2 \times d_{\text{model}} \times 2d_{\text{model}} + 2d_{\text{model}} \times d_{\text{model}} = 4d_{\text{model}}^2 + 2d_{\text{model}}^2 = 6d_{\text{model}}^2$` parameters per layer.

**Critical observation for MoE integration: no dense feed-forward network.** Unlike the Transformer architecture, where a standard block consists of attention (approximately `$4d_{\text{model}}^2$` parameters) followed by a feed-forward network (approximately `$8d_{\text{model}}^2$` parameters), the Mamba layer combines sequence processing and nonlinear transformation in a single integrated computation. There is no separate, replaceable feed-forward component. This means that applying the standard MoE recipe — replacing dense FFN layers with sparse expert FFNs — is not directly applicable. The paper's solution, as we will see, is to add MoE layers *in addition to* the Mamba layers rather than *replacing* components within them.

**Hardware-aware implementation.** Mamba achieves its practical speed through several implementation optimizations that are important to understand because they constrain how MoE layers can be integrated without disrupting the efficiency gains:

- **Work-efficient parallel scan:** Although the SSM state update is sequential in principle (each position's state depends on the previous position's state), Mamba uses a parallel scan algorithm that computes all positions' states simultaneously using a divide-and-conquer approach. This enables training parallelism comparable to Transformers.

- **Kernel fusion:** Multiple GPU operations are fused into single custom CUDA kernels, avoiding the need to materialize the full expanded state `$(B, L, E \cdot d_{\text{model}})$` in GPU memory. Instead, the intermediate expanded representations are consumed immediately by the SSM computation. This is what makes Mamba memory-efficient despite the internal expansion.

- **Activation recomputation:** During the backward pass, intermediate states necessary for gradient computation are not saved but recomputed from saved inputs. This trades additional computation (re-running the forward pass) for reduced memory usage, analogous to gradient checkpointing in Transformers but more tightly integrated with the SSM-specific operations.

These optimizations mean that simply inserting additional computation between Mamba layers could, in principle, disrupt memory flow or kernel fusion boundaries. However, the paper finds empirically that interleaving MoE feed-forward layers does not negate Mamba's inference advantages — the MoE layers use standard matrix multiplications that run efficiently on GPUs, and the Mamba layers retain their optimized kernels.

#### Switch MoE Layer Mechanics

The Switch Transformer MoE design (Fedus et al., 2022) is the conditional computation mechanism that MoE-Mamba uses to scale total parameters without scaling per-token FLOPs proportionally. The paper adopts this specific design because it is "well-established and easy-to-implement" (Section 3.1), not because it is claimed to be optimal for SSMs.

**Expert architecture.** Each expert `$E_i$` is a standard feed-forward network with an identical architecture across all experts:

- Input dimension: `$d_{\text{model}}$` (the same as the residual stream dimension)
- Hidden dimension: `$d_{\text{expert}}$`
- Activation function: not explicitly stated but presumably GELU or ReLU (standard in Switch implementations, using two linear transformations with a nonlinearity between them)
- Output dimension: `$d_{\text{model}}$` (to add back to the residual stream)

For the paper's main experiments, `$d_{\text{expert}} = 1536$` for the 25M-scale models (with `$d_{\text{model}} = 512$`) and `$d_{\text{expert}} = 2304$` for the 100M-scale models (with `$d_{\text{model}} = 768$`). The expert size is deliberately set smaller than the standard Transformer FFN size (`$4 \times d_{\text{model}} = 2048$` for 25M and `$3072$` for 100M) to accommodate multiple experts while keeping per-token active parameters comparable to baselines. Specifically, Section 4.1 states: "we scale down the size of each expert in our model (we set `$d_{\text{expert}} = 3d_{\text{model}}$`). This way, we can keep both the number of blocks and the number of active parameters per token roughly the same in all models of similar size."

**Router mechanism — top-1 gating.** For each token embedding `$\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}}}$`, the router computes:

$$h(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{W} \mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{N_{\text{experts}}}$$

where `$\mathbf{W} \in \mathbb{R}^{N_{\text{experts}} \times d_{\text{model}}}$` is a trainable weight matrix. This is a simple linear projection from the model dimension to the number of experts.

The router logits `$h(\mathbf{x})$` are normalized into a probability distribution over experts using softmax:

$$p_i(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{\exp(h(\mathbf{x})_i)}{\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\text{experts}}} \exp(h(\mathbf{x})_j)}$$

where `$p_i(\mathbf{x})$` is the softmax probability assigned to expert `$i$` for token `$\mathbf{x}$`.

**What this computes:** For each token position in the sequence, the router takes the current token's representation (which has been accumulated through the residual stream from all previous layers) and produces a categorical distribution over the `$N_{\text{experts}}$` available experts. The softmax ensures these are valid probabilities that sum to 1.

**Why this form:** The linear-projection-plus-softmax router is the simplest learnable gating mechanism that produces differentiable expert assignments. The softmax is essential because it provides a smooth, differentiable approximation to hard routing — during training, the probability distribution `$p_i(\mathbf{x})$` can backpropagate gradients to the router weights `$\mathbf{W}$`, encouraging the router to learn which token representations should be routed to which expert. An alternative would be a hard threshold (if `$h_i > \tau$`, route to expert `$i$`), but this would not be differentiable and would require reinforcement learning (REINFORCE) gradient estimates, which have higher variance.

**Expert selection — the "Switch" simplification.** The key insight of Switch Transformers (and the source of the name) is that you do not need to route each token to multiple experts. Prior MoE work (Shazeer et al., 2017; Lepikhin et al., 2020) typically used top-k routing with `$k > 1$`, meaning each token was processed by the 2 or more highest-scoring experts, and their outputs were combined via weighted averaging. Fedus et al. (2022) showed that `$k = 1$` works just as well while being simpler and more computationally efficient. The MoE layer output for token `$\mathbf{x}$` is therefore:

$$\mathbf{y} = p_I E_I(\mathbf{x})$$

where `$I = \arg\max_i p_i(\mathbf{x})$` is the index of the expert with the highest router probability, `$E_I(\mathbf{x})$` is the feed-forward computation of that selected expert, and `$p_I$` is the router probability for that expert.

**What this computes:** The token is sent to exactly one expert. That expert performs its feed-forward computation on the token, and the output is multiplied (scaled) by the router's confidence in that expert assignment. The scaling by `$p_I$` is important: it means that if the router is uncertain (probabilities spread across several experts), the chosen expert's output is downweighted, which provides a smooth gradient signal for the router to become more decisive.

**Why this form with `$k = 1$`:** The top-1 routing keeps the per-token FLOPs independent of the number of experts. With top-1, each token activates exactly one expert's worth of feed-forward computation (approximately `$2 \times d_{\text{model}} \times d_{\text{expert}}$` FLOPs per token per MoE layer, for the two linear projections in the FFN). With top-2, this would double. The `$p_I$` scaling factor is what makes top-1 routing work despite the apparent information loss — even though only one expert processes the token, the router's confidence score modulates how much that expert's output contributes to the residual stream, providing a soft mechanism for blending expertise.

**Capacity factor and token dropping.** During training with batches, an important practical issue arises: the router might assign many more tokens to some experts than others, creating load imbalance. Following Switch Transformer, MoE-Mamba uses a **capacity factor of 1**. This means each expert has a fixed capacity: it can process at most `$\lceil \frac{N_{\text{tokens}}}{N_{\text{experts}}} \rceil$` tokens per batch (the ceiling accounts for non-divisible batch sizes). If more tokens are routed to an expert than its capacity, the excess tokens are **dropped** — they bypass the MoE layer entirely, with their representations passing through the residual connection unchanged. This is called "token dropping."

The capacity factor is 1, which is the minimum value that can process the full batch if routing is perfectly balanced. A capacity factor of 2 would allow each expert to process up to twice its fair share before dropping, at the cost of more computation and memory. The choice of capacity factor 1 means MoE-Mamba aggressively enforces load balancing — if routing is unbalanced, tokens get dropped, which acts as an implicit penalty on the router for poor load distribution.

**Load balancing loss.** To further encourage even token distribution across experts, an auxiliary load balancing loss is added to the training objective, following the Switch Transformer formulation:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{aux}} = \alpha \cdot N_{\text{experts}} \cdot \sum_{i=1}^{N_{\text{experts}}} f_i \cdot P_i$$

where `$\alpha = 0.01$` is a weighting coefficient, `$f_i$` is the fraction of tokens in the batch that were routed to expert `$i$` (the empirical load), and `$P_i$` is the average router probability assigned to expert `$i$` (the model's preference). This loss is minimized when `$f_i = 1/N_{\text{experts}}$` for all `$i$` (uniform load) and the model's routing probabilities match the empirical assignment.

**What this computes:** For each expert, the loss multiplies the fraction of tokens actually sent there (`$f_i$`) by the average probability the router assigns to that expert (`$P_i$`). If an expert receives many tokens but has low average probability, the product is moderate. If an expert receives few tokens but has high average probability, the product is again moderate. The loss penalizes the combination of high probability and low actual usage (or vice versa). The factor `$N_{\text{experts}}$` normalizes so the loss is comparable across different numbers of experts. The `$\alpha = 0.01$` coefficient ensures this auxiliary loss does not dominate the primary language modeling objective.

**Why this form:** The load balancing loss is a differentiable surrogate for the non-differentiable token dropping operation. Token dropping itself is a hard constraint that cannot propagate gradients, so the load balancing loss provides a soft signal that encourages the router to produce approximately uniform assignments. The specific `$f_i \cdot P_i$` form (rather than, say, a squared difference) is inherited from Switch Transformer and was designed to be stable in practice. The choice of `$\alpha = 0.01$` is a standard value from the literature; the paper does not ablate this hyperparameter.

**Number of experts.** The paper experiments with varying numbers of experts (1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 42) and finds monotonic improvement in final log perplexity as the number of experts increases, with 32 experts giving the best results among those tested (Figure 4). The main experiments use 42 experts for the 25M-scale model and 42 experts for the 100M-scale model (Table 1). The exact number 42 appears to be an engineering choice rather than a principled optimum — it is "fewer than the 48 experts explored in some ablations" and provides roughly the target active parameter count per expert.

#### MoE-Mamba Interleaving Pattern

The central architectural decision in the paper is *where* to place MoE layers relative to Mamba layers. The chosen design — interleaving them sequentially — is not the only possibility, and the paper explicitly ablates alternatives, but it produces the strongest results.

**Sequential interleaving architecture.** In MoE-Mamba, the model consists of a stack of "blocks," where each block contains one Mamba layer followed by one MoE feed-forward layer. For the 25M-scale model, there are 8 such blocks (8 Mamba layers, 8 MoE layers), while Mamba25M — the vanilla baseline with similar active parameters — uses 16 Mamba layers and no MoE layers. The total number of non-embedding layers is thus 16 for both models, making the depth comparable. For the 100M-scale, MoE-Mamba has 16 blocks (32 total layers) compared to vanilla Mamba100M's 32 Mamba layers — again, comparable depth at 32 non-embedding layers.

The sequence of computation for each token position is:

1. **Embedding lookup:** Token ID → learned embedding vector `$\mathbf{e} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}}}$`, initializing the residual stream.
2. **First Mamba layer:** Processes the sequence through the selective SSM, producing an output added to the residual stream.
3. **First MoE layer:** Routes the current residual representation through the router, selects the top-1 expert, computes the expert's FFN output scaled by `$p_I$`, and adds it to the residual stream.
4. **Repeat** for all 8 (or 16) blocks.
5. **Unembedding:** Final residual representation → projection to vocabulary logits → softmax → loss.

**Why sequential interleaving — the paper's rationale.** The paper frames this design as separating "unconditional processing of every token by the Mamba layer — which can efficiently integrate the whole sequence context into an internal representation — and conditional processing by an MoE layer that can apply the most relevant expert (and thus the subset of parameters) for each token" (Section 3.2). The idea is that:

- The Mamba layer performs sequence-level reasoning — understanding how tokens relate to each other across the full context — and updates each token's representation with that contextualized information.
- The MoE layer then performs token-level specialized processing — applying knowledge or computation specific to the type of token (e.g., a numeric reasoning expert vs. a factual recall expert vs. a syntactic structure expert), now that the token's representation has been enriched with sequence context.

This separation mirrors a pattern used in some MoE-Transformer models (Lepikhin et al., 2020; Fedus et al., 2022), where vanilla dense feed-forward layers are alternated with MoE feed-forward layers. The intuition is that some processing steps benefit from the full diversity of all parameters (dense), while others benefit from specialized, high-capacity but conditionally-activated computation (sparse).

**Parameter allocation between Mamba and MoE — the optimal ratio.** A critical design choice is how to distribute the active parameter budget between Mamba layers and MoE layers. The paper investigates this in Section 4.3 by varying the **expansion factor** `$E$` of the Mamba layer (which controls its parameter count) and correspondingly adjusting the number and size of MoE experts to keep the total parameter count fixed at 542M and active parameters per token at 26M.

The relationship between these quantities is:

- **Mamba active parameters per layer:** approximately `$6d_{\text{model}}^2 + 2(E-2)d_{\text{model}}^2$` (the extra term accounts for expansion beyond `$E=2$`).
- **MoE active parameters per layer:** `$2 \times d_{\text{model}} \times d_{\text{expert}}$` for the two linear projections in the selected expert.
- **MoE total parameters:** `$N_{\text{experts}} \times 2 \times d_{\text{model}} \times d_{\text{expert}}$` plus router parameters.
- **Constraint:** Total active parameters = Mamba active + MoE active = fixed (26M for the 25M-scale experiment).

Table 2 sweeps five ratios, from Mamba-heavy to MoE-heavy, by adjusting `$E$`, `$d_{\text{expert}}$`, and `$N_{\text{experts}}$` while maintaining the fixed total parameter budget:

| Ratio (Mamba active : MoE active) | E | `$d_{\text{expert}}$` | `$N_{\text{experts}}$` |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 : 5 | 2 | 3 × 512 = 1536 | 19 |
| 2 : 4 | 1 2/3 | 1536 | 24 |
| 3 : 3 | 2 | 1536 | 32 |
| 4 : 2 | 2 2/3 | 1024 | 48 |
| 5 : 1 | 3 1/3 | 512 | 96 |

The results (Figure 5) show that performance improves as more active parameters are allocated to Mamba, reaching a peak around the 3:3 ratio, after which gains become marginal. The paper defaults to 3:3 for all other experiments because "higher ratios are impractical due to inefficient hardware utilization and high routing costs caused by a large number of experts" (Section 4.3).

**Why the 3:3 ratio matters conceptually.** This finding suggests that Mamba's unconditional sequence processing requires a substantial fraction of the active parameter budget — roughly half — to be effective, and that the MoE layers provide complementary value that saturates if too many parameters are shifted into them. At the extreme of 5:1 (Mamba-heavy), the model has 96 very small experts (each only 3×512×512 = 786K parameters for the two linear layers), and the routing cost across so many experts begins to dominate. At the 1:5 extreme (MoE-heavy), the Mamba layers are starved of capacity and cannot effectively perform sequence-level integration.

**Comparison to vanilla Mamba's depth scaling.** MoE-Mamba uses half as many Mamba layers as vanilla Mamba at the same active parameter count (8 instead of 16, or 16 instead of 32). The MoE layers compensate for this reduction in Mamba depth by providing specialized per-token computation. The fact that this works — indeed, that MoE-Mamba *outperforms* vanilla Mamba — indicates that the MoE layers are providing genuinely useful computation beyond what additional Mamba layers would provide, not merely compensating for lost depth.

#### Parallel MoE-Mamba

As an alternative to sequential interleaving, the paper explores placing the MoE feed-forward layer in parallel with the Mamba layer within the same block (Figure 3, left panel). This design is "inspired by Wang (2021) and Chowdhery et al. (2023)" (Section 3.3).

**Parallel architecture.** In this variant, instead of the computation being sequential — Mamba first, then MoE feeding on Mamba's output — the token's current residual representation is fed simultaneously to both the Mamba layer and the MoE layer. Their outputs are then summed and added to the residual stream:

$$\mathbf{y} = \text{Mamba}(\mathbf{x}) + \text{MoE}(\mathbf{x})$$

where both `$\text{Mamba}(\mathbf{x})$` and `$\text{MoE}(\mathbf{x})$` are computed independently from the same input `$\mathbf{x}$`.

**Why this might be attractive.** The parallel design has two potential advantages:

1. **Hardware efficiency through fused matrix multiplications (Chowdhery et al., 2023):** The input projections for the Mamba layer and the MoE router (and possibly the initial expert projections) can be fused into a single large matrix multiplication, reducing kernel launch overhead and improving GPU utilization. This is analogous to how the attention QKV projection in Transformers is often implemented as a single fused operation.

2. **Reduced communication in model-parallel settings (Wang, 2021):** In distributed training with expert parallelism (different experts on different devices), the parallel design allows the Mamba computation (which is local to the sequence) and the MoE computation (which requires all-to-all communication for token routing) to overlap, potentially hiding communication latency.

**Results and why it underperforms.** Figure 6 and Table 7 show that Parallel MoE-Mamba consistently underperforms the sequential variant across all numbers of experts tested (1 to 32). At 32 experts, sequential MoE-Mamba achieves log perplexity 3.66 after 1B tokens, while parallel achieves 3.69. The gap is larger at fewer experts: at 8 experts, sequential achieves 3.69 vs. parallel 3.72.

The paper does not provide a mechanistic explanation for the gap, but a plausible interpretation (consistent with the separation-of-roles rationale in Section 3.2) is that the sequential design allows the MoE layer to operate on representations that have already been *contextualized* by the Mamba layer. In the parallel design, both Mamba and MoE operate on the same pre-contextualized input, meaning the MoE routing decision cannot leverage the sequence-level information that the Mamba layer would have computed. This is potentially critical because effective expert routing likely depends on understanding what role a token plays in its sequence context — for example, the token "bank" should be routed to a financial expert if the context mentions "river" vs. a geography expert if the context mentions "money," and the Mamba layer is responsible for capturing that context.

#### Inner MoE — Modifying the Mamba Block Itself

The paper also experiments with integrating MoE *inside* the Mamba block by replacing one or more of its three internal linear projections (Gate Projection, Conv Projection, Output Projection) with MoE layers (Section 3.4, Table 3).

**Design space.** There are `$2^3 - 1 = 7$` possible combinations (replacing any non-empty subset of the three projections), because the paper discards the combination with no MoE (which would just be vanilla Mamba). For each combination, the 24 total experts are split evenly among the MoE'd projections (e.g., if two projections are converted to MoE, each gets 12 experts; if all three, each gets 8 experts). This maintains constant total expert FFN capacity across designs.

Additionally, the paper experiments with applying these modifications to only *half* the Mamba blocks (every other layer) while doubling the number of experts per MoE'd layer (from 24 to 48) to maintain total parameters. This is "inspired by Fedus et al. (2022)," who found that MoE layers need not be applied at every position in the stack.

**Results — none outperform MoE-Mamba.** Table 3 reports the outcomes across all 14 configurations (7 combination choices × 2 placement frequencies). Three designs achieve results marginally better than vanilla Mamba (log perplexity around 3.70 after 1B tokens vs. vanilla Mamba's 3.72), but none approach MoE-Mamba (3.67 with 16 experts at 1B tokens). The best internal MoE configuration — replacing the Gate Projection only, applied to every other layer — achieves 3.70, compared to MoE-Mamba sequential's 3.67.

**Patterns in the results.** Several patterns emerge from Table 3:

- **Gate Projection replacement is the least harmful.** Replacing the Gate Projection alone achieves 3.71 (every layer) and 3.70 (every other layer), the best among the internal MoE designs. This is consistent with the Gate Projection being a relatively simple nonlinear transformation (project-up + SiLU activation) that is less critical to the SSM's sequence-processing role.

- **Output Projection replacement is the most harmful.** Replacing the Output Projection alone achieves 3.70 (every other layer) but with combinations including it, results degrade significantly — e.g., Conv + Output achieves 3.76, Gate + Output achieves 3.88. The Output Projection is responsible for compressing the expanded representation back to `$d_{\text{model}}$`, and routing this compression through different experts likely disrupts the consistency of the residual stream representation.

- **Multiple-projection MoE is consistently worse.** As the number of MoE projections increases (from 1 to 2 to 3), performance degrades monotonically for any fixed placement frequency. For example, with all layers modified: Gate only (3.71) > Conv + Gate (3.95) > Conv + Gate + Output (4.39). This suggests that the three projections in the Mamba block are tightly coupled — they need to be consistent with each other because they respectively expand, process, and compress the same information — and making the choice of projection parameters conditional on the input token (via routing) breaks this consistency.

- **Every-other-layer application is sometimes better.** For several configurations, applying MoE to only half the layers outperforms applying it to all layers (e.g., Output only: 3.70 vs. 4.05; Gate + Output: 3.88 vs. 4.16). This pattern mirrors the Fedus et al. (2022) finding and suggests that not every layer benefits from conditional computation — some layers perform generic transformations that work well with dense parameters.

**Why this matters despite negative results.** The internal MoE experiments are important because they show that the natural place for MoE in a Mamba architecture is *outside* the Mamba block, not *inside* it. The Mamba block's design — where the three projections form an integrated pipeline of expansion, selective processing, and compression — does not decompose cleanly into replaceable components. This validates the paper's decision to add MoE layers as separate, interleaved blocks rather than trying to integrate them into the existing Mamba computation.

#### Training Configuration and Speedup Metric

The paper's headline claim — "MoE-Mamba reaches the same performance as Mamba in 2.35× fewer training steps" — requires understanding exactly what is being compared and how the metric is computed.

**Models compared.** Both models (MoE-Mamba100M and Mamba100M) have comparable active parameters per token: MoE-Mamba100M has 117M active parameters, while Mamba100M has 121M. The total parameter counts are vastly different: 2,439M for MoE-Mamba vs. 121M for Mamba. The training data, sequence length, batch size, and learning rate schedule are matched (Table 6). Both are trained on the C4 dataset with the GPT2 tokenizer using cross-entropy loss for next-token prediction.

**What "speedup" means operationally.** The speedup is defined as:

$$\text{speedup}(l) = \frac{\text{training steps}_{\text{Mamba}} \text{ to reach loss } l}{\text{training steps}_{\text{MoE-Mamba}} \text{ to reach loss } l}$$

This is measured by training both models, recording the EMA-smoothed training log perplexity at each step (with smoothing factor `$\alpha = 0.001$`), and finding the step at which each model first achieves a given loss value. The ratio of those steps is the speedup. A speedup of 2.35× means that if Mamba takes, say, 23,500 steps to reach log perplexity `$l$`, MoE-Mamba reaches the same `$l$` in only 10,000 steps.

**Why EMA smoothing:** The authors use "EMA-smoothed (`$\alpha = 0.001$`) training log perplexity as the comparison metric for both final loss and speedup measurements as it is a more fine-grained comparison metric than test log perplexity" (Section 4.1). EMA smoothing applies an exponential filter to the per-step loss values, reducing noise and making it possible to identify exactly when a given loss threshold is first crossed. The test log perplexity comparison (in Appendix G, Figure 9) shows the same qualitative ordering of models but with higher variance.

**Hyperparameters (Tables 5 and 6).** Key training settings for the 100M-scale models:

- **Training steps:** 30,000
- **Context length:** 1024 tokens
- **Batch size:** 1024 sequences (total batch of ~1M tokens per step, so ~30B tokens processed total)
- **Optimizer:** AdamW (Loshchilov & Hutter, 2019) with `$\beta_1 = 0.9$`, `$\beta_2 = 0.95$`
- **Maximum learning rate:** `$1 \times 10^{-3}$` for Mamba100M, `$5 \times 10^{-4}$` for MoE-Mamba100M, `$2.5 \times 10^{-4}$` for Transformer-MoE100M (Transformer-MoE required halving the learning rate due to "repeated instabilities")
- **Learning rate schedule:** Cosine decay with 1% warmup, final LR ratio = 0.1
- **Weight decay:** 0.1
- **Gradient clipping:** 0.5
- **Hardware:** Multi-GPU setup using PyTorch FSDP (Zhao et al., 2023b)

The 25M-scale models use a smaller batch size (64 sequences) and train for 150K steps (processing ~10B tokens), with maximum learning rates of `$1 \times 10^{-3}$` (Mamba25M) and `$5 \times 10^{-4}$` (MoE-Mamba25M, Transformer-MoE25M).

**Parameter counting convention.** All models report *non-embedding* parameters — that is, parameters excluding the input embedding table and output unembedding (vocabulary projection) matrix. This follows the convention proposed by Kaplan et al. (2020) and used by Lan et al. (2020). The rationale is that embedding parameters scale with vocabulary size rather than model capacity, and including them obscures scaling relationships. For the models in this paper, the embedding and unembedding layers account for a fixed `$V \times d_{\text{model}}$` parameters where `$V$` is the GPT2 vocabulary size (50,257), which is significant for small `$d_{\text{model}}$` but negligible relative to the total for larger models.

**Active parameters per token.** This is computed as the sum of parameters that are actually used to compute the output for a single token. For Mamba layers (dense), this equals all Mamba parameters divided by the number of layers. For MoE layers (sparse), this equals the parameters of a single expert (since top-1 routing activates exactly one expert per token) plus the router parameters. For MoE-Mamba25M, this gives 26M active parameters out of 542M total — approximately 4.8% of total parameters are active for any given token.

**Relation to FLOPs.** The paper reports active parameters rather than FLOPs, "following Zhou et al. (2022)" (Appendix B). The justification is that "the number of FLOPs is both harder to calculate and less relevant for hardware-aware architecture like Mamba with its optimizations, especially during inference." This is reasonable because Mamba's optimized CUDA kernels mean that FLOPs counts from standard profilers may not accurately reflect actual runtime — kernel fusion and memory access patterns dominate practical throughput. However, for training, FLOPs and active parameters are "roughly proportional" (Kaplan et al., 2020).

**Why the learning rate differs between models.** The paper tunes learning rates separately for each model architecture, finding that Transformer-MoE requires a lower learning rate to avoid training instability. This is a known phenomenon with MoE models — the sparse gradient updates (experts only receive gradients from their assigned tokens) can lead to noisier optimization, and lower learning rates help stabilize training. The fact that MoE-Mamba does not require learning rate reduction (using `$5 \times 10^{-4}$` at both scales) while Transformer-MoE does (requiring halving to `$2.5 \times 10^{-4}$` for the 100M model) suggests that Mamba's continuous state updates provide more stable gradient flow through the MoE layers compared to attention's discrete token interactions.

**Speedup growth during training.** Appendix D (Figure 8) shows that the speedup of MoE-Mamba over vanilla Mamba *increases* as training progresses. For the 25M models, speedup oscillates between 1.6× and 1.9× after the initial training period. For the 100M models, speedup "rises steadily," reaching 2.35× by the end of the 30K-step training run. This growing gap is significant: it means that MoE-Mamba does not just train faster initially but continues to pull ahead, suggesting that the benefits of conditional computation compound over longer training. Extrapolating this trend, one would expect even larger speedups at industrial-scale training durations (hundreds of billions of tokens), though the paper does not test this.

**The special case of small numbers of experts.** An important finding from the expert count ablation (Table 4): MoE-Mamba with 1 expert (which is equivalent to Mamba interleaved with a single dense feed-forward layer) achieves log perplexity 3.75 after 1B tokens, *worse* than vanilla Mamba's 3.72. With 2 experts, the model reaches 3.74, still worse than vanilla Mamba. Only at 4 experts does MoE-Mamba begin to outperform (3.72, matching vanilla Mamba with speedup 1.03×). At 8 experts, the gap widens to 3.70 vs. 3.72 (speedup 1.10×), and it continues improving to 32 experts (3.67, speedup 1.23×). The monotonic improvement with more experts (Figure 4) suggests that 32 experts is not a ceiling — the authors "expect further gains with even more experts" (Section 4.5). This is consistent with the MoE scaling literature: more experts provide more total capacity and finer-grained specialization, and the per-token cost remains constant (since only one is activated), limited only by memory and routing overhead.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: MoE and SSMs Address Orthogonal Efficiency Bottlenecks — Their Combination Is Multiplicative, Not Additive

The paper's most fundamental conceptual contribution is the recognition that Mixture of Experts and State Space Models solve *different* scaling problems, and that combining them yields efficiency gains that neither technique can achieve alone — not because they make each other stronger, but because they target independent resource constraints. This is not an obvious claim. The standard approach to improving architectures is to optimize a single bottleneck: make attention faster (by approximating it), make feed-forward layers sparser (by adding MoE), or make sequence processing linear (by switching to SSMs). Each line of work typically competes against the others, implicitly asking "which architecture is best?" rather than "can we compose these?" The paper's key reframing is that these are not competing solutions to the same problem, but complementary solutions to different problems:

- **SSMs solve the sequence-length bottleneck.** Transformer attention costs `$O(L^2)$` in both time and memory for sequence length `$L$`. Mamba reduces this to `$O(L)$`, which is a qualitative change in how cost scales with context — it means doubling the context length doubles the cost, rather than quadrupling it. This benefit is architectural and cannot be achieved by MoE, which leaves the `$O(L^2)$` attention cost unchanged in Transformers.

- **MoE solves the parameter-count bottleneck.** Dense models activate all parameters for every token, making per-token cost proportional to total model size. MoE with top-1 routing makes per-token cost proportional to active expert size, decoupling total capacity from inference compute. This benefit is independent of sequence length — it applies equally to short and long contexts — and cannot be achieved by SSMs, which are typically dense architectures.

What makes this insight distinctive is that it was not predicted by prior work. The Transformer-MoE literature (Shazeer et al., 2017; Lepikhin et al., 2020; Fedus et al., 2022) had established that MoE improves Transformer efficiency, but the combination with SSMs was unexplored. The SSM literature (Gu et al., 2022b; Gu & Dao, 2023) had focused on matching Transformer quality with better asymptotic complexity, but had not addressed the parameter-scaling axis. The paper's framing — that SSMs and MoE are "orthogonal efficiency axes" — implies that the optimal architecture for a given compute budget should optimize *both* simultaneously, not choose between them. This is a shift from the dominant "architecture competition" framing (Transformer vs. SSM vs. RWKV vs. Hyena) toward a "composability" framing where architectural innovations from different lineages can be combined.

The evidence supporting this as a genuine synergy (rather than just two techniques that happen to not interfere) comes from the scaling behavior. MoE-Mamba's speedup over vanilla Mamba *increases* with training duration (Appendix D, Figure 8), reaching 2.35× for the 100M models by the end of training. This growing gap suggests that MoE is not merely providing a one-time capacity boost but is interacting with the SSM's learning dynamics in a way that compounds over time. If the benefits were purely additive (MoE adds capacity, Mamba provides linear-time processing, they don't interact), one would expect a relatively constant speedup throughout training. The increasing speedup instead suggests that the conditional computation enables more efficient use of the Mamba layers' sequence-processing capacity — possibly because the MoE layers can offload specialized token-level computation that would otherwise consume Mamba state capacity — which manifests more strongly as training progresses and the model learns to specialize its experts.

### Innovation 2: Discovering That Sparsity Transforms a Harmful Architectural Addition into a Beneficial One

One of the paper's most intriguing findings is not a new architecture but a diagnostic observation about *why* MoE works when combined with Mamba: simply adding dense feed-forward layers to Mamba degrades performance (consistent with Gu & Dao, 2023), but making those layers *sparse* via MoE reverses this, turning a harmful modification into a beneficial one. This is not a predicted result. The standard MoE playbook — replace dense FFNs with sparse expert FFNs — works in Transformers because the dense FFN is already a large, beneficial component (typically 2/3 of parameters). Making it sparse preserves its function while scaling capacity. But in Mamba, the dense FFN is *absent* by design (Gu & Dao, 2023 found it hurts performance), so there is no existing beneficial component to make sparse. The paper is therefore not replacing a good dense layer with a better sparse one — it is taking something that is actively harmful (a dense FFN interleaved with Mamba) and, by making it sparse, turning it into something beneficial.

This pattern is visible in Table 4. With 1 expert (equivalent to a dense FFN), MoE-Mamba achieves log perplexity 3.75 after 1B tokens, worse than vanilla Mamba's 3.72. With 2 experts, the loss improves to 3.74 — still worse than vanilla Mamba. At 4 experts, it matches Mamba (3.72, speedup 1.03×). At 8 experts, it pulls ahead (3.70, speedup 1.10×). And at 32 experts, the gap widens to 3.67 (speedup 1.23×). The crossover point — where sparsity becomes beneficial — is at 4 experts, which means the model needs at least a 4-way choice of which parameters to apply to each token before the FFN stops being harmful and starts being helpful.

This has important conceptual implications. It suggests that the problem with adding dense FFNs to Mamba is not that additional per-token computation is inherently wasteful, but that *applying the same parameters to all tokens* is counterproductive. When parameters are shared across all tokens (dense FFN), the FFN's transformations interfere with the Mamba layers' sequence processing — perhaps by forcing token representations into a shared space that discards information the SSM needs, or by adding noise that the residual stream must compensate for. When parameters are specialized per-token (sparse MoE), the FFN can adapt its transformation based on the token's content, avoiding this interference. The fact that 4 experts are needed for the crossover suggests that a minimum level of specialization granularity is required before the benefits outweigh the costs.

This finding is a fundamental advance in understanding MoE, not just an incremental architecture tweak. It reveals that sparsity has a *qualitative* effect on what kinds of architectural additions are viable — it is not merely a capacity-scaling technique, but can also rescue architectural choices that would otherwise degrade performance. This has implications beyond Mamba: it suggests that other architectures that lack dense FFNs (or where FFNs are harmful) might still benefit from sparse MoE layers, and that the "starting point" for MoE integration need not be an existing dense component that is simply made sparse.

### Innovation 3: The Mamba-to-MoE Parameter Ratio Represents a New Architectural Degree of Freedom with a Non-Trivial Optimum

In standard Transformer-MoE architectures, the ratio of attention parameters to FFN parameters is largely determined by the Transformer's original design: attention accounts for roughly `$4d^2$` per layer, FFN for `$8d^2$`, so the ratio is approximately 1:2 regardless of how many experts are used (since experts replace the dense FFN but preserve its per-expert size). The paper's exploration of the Mamba-to-MoE active parameter ratio (Section 4.3, Figure 5) reveals that this ratio is not a fixed architectural constraint but a tunable hyperparameter with a meaningful optimum. By varying the Mamba expansion factor `$E$` and correspondingly adjusting expert count and size while holding total parameters constant, the paper discovers that the optimal split is approximately 3:3 (equal active parameters in Mamba layers and MoE layers), with sharply diminishing returns on either side.

This is conceptually important because it establishes that SSM-based architectures have a *different internal resource allocation optimum* than Transformers. In Transformers, the attention-to-FFN ratio has been studied by Kaplan et al. (2020) and others, but it is rarely treated as a design hyperparameter — it is mostly inherited from the original Transformer design and only occasionally varied (e.g., in ALBERT, Lan et al., 2020). In MoE-Mamba, the paper shows that the ratio *must* be chosen carefully and that it differs from the Transformer's implicit ratio. The 3:3 optimum means Mamba needs proportionally more of the active parameter budget than attention does — roughly half — to perform its sequence-processing role effectively. This likely reflects that the Mamba layer's internal computation (`$6d^2$` parameters for a single layer combining state space processing, gating, and local convolution) is doing more diverse work than a Transformer attention layer (`$4d^2$` parameters focused solely on token interaction), and thus benefits more from parameter allocation.

The practical upshot is that future SSM+MoE architectures have a new design dimension to optimize, and the 3:3 ratio provides a starting point for scaling. At larger scales, this optimum might shift — the paper does not test whether it holds for billion-parameter models — but the existence of a well-defined optimum at the tested scale suggests it is a genuine architectural property rather than an artifact of hyperparameter tuning.

### Innovation 4: Establishing Viability of SSM+MoE as a Research Direction Through Negative Results That Bound the Design Space

While MoE-Mamba's positive results — 2.35× training speedup, outperforming Transformer-MoE — are the headline, the paper's negative results are equally valuable as intellectual contributions. The comprehensive ablations of alternative designs (Parallel MoE-Mamba, Inner MoE modifications) do not merely show that the sequential interleaving is best; they establish *constraints* on why it is best, pruning large swaths of the design space and providing guidance for future work.

**Parallel integration fails** (Section 4.4, Figure 6): placing MoE alongside Mamba rather than after it consistently underperforms, and requires 2–4× more experts to match the sequential variant. This implies that the MoE layer benefits from operating on Mamba-contextualized representations — the routing decision needs the sequence-level information that Mamba has just computed. This is a non-trivial finding because parallel integration has hardware advantages (kernel fusion, communication overlap) and would have been a reasonable default design choice. Showing that it underperforms, and quantifying how much, prevents future work from wasting compute on this direction.

**Internal MoE modifications fail** (Section 4.4, Table 3): none of the 14 tested configurations of MoE inside the Mamba block outperforms MoE-Mamba, and many substantially degrade performance (up to 4.39 log perplexity vs. 3.67). The pattern of which projections tolerate MoE substitution (Gate Projection is least harmful, Output Projection is most harmful, and multiple substitutions compound damage) reveals that the Mamba block's three projections form a tightly coupled computational pipeline that resists modular decomposition. This is a mechanistic insight: the Mamba block is not a collection of independent linear transformations that can be individually sparsified, but an integrated computation where the expansion, selective processing, and compression steps must be consistent. This finding is not obvious from the Mamba architecture's description and represents a genuine diagnostic contribution.

**Small numbers of experts hurt** (Table 4): MoE-Mamba with 1 or 2 experts underperforms vanilla Mamba, crossing over to beneficial only at 4 experts. This establishes a minimum viable sparsity for the technique to work, which is important for practitioners who might consider using fewer experts to reduce memory overhead. The result also connects to the paper's broader message: sparsity is not just a capacity knob but a qualitative requirement for the architecture to function.

Together, these negative results do more than validate the chosen design — they map the boundaries of the SSM+MoE design space, distinguishing directions that are merely suboptimal (parallel, requiring more experts to match) from those that are fundamentally incompatible (internal MoE, which breaks the Mamba block's coherence). For a new research direction, this kind of negative-result cartography is as valuable as positive benchmarks, because it prevents the community from collectively exploring dead ends.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** All experiments use the C4 dataset (Raffel et al., 2020), a large-scale corpus of cleaned English web text consisting of hundreds of gigabytes of text data. The models are trained on the next-token prediction task using the standard cross-entropy loss function. For the main experiments, approximately 10B tokens are used for 25M-scale models and approximately 30B tokens for 100M-scale models. The authors note that this processes "less than 20% of the tokens" in C4, so while documents may be encountered multiple times during training, the test set performance should remain representative (Appendix G).

- **Base model(s).** The architectures studied are: **Mamba** (Gu & Dao, 2023), a selective state space model with linear-time sequence processing; **Transformer** (Vaswani et al., 2017), the standard decoder-only attention-based architecture; **Transformer-MoE**, a Transformer with Switch MoE feed-forward layers replacing dense FFNs; and **MoE-Mamba**, the proposed architecture. All models are decoder-only and are trained from scratch — there are no pretrained checkpoints involved. Two model scales are used: approximately 25M active parameters (BERT-MEDIUM configuration, 8 blocks for Transformers, 16 layers for vanilla Mamba, 8 Mamba + 8 MoE layers for MoE-Mamba, `d_model = 512`) and approximately 100M active parameters (BERT-BASE-inspired with 16 blocks and `d_model = 768`). The 25M scale is used for extensive ablations; the 100M scale validates the main findings.

- **Metrics.** The primary metric is **EMA-smoothed training log perplexity** with smoothing factor `α = 0.001`. The authors state this "is a more fine-grained comparison metric than test log perplexity" (Section 4.1) because it reduces noise and enables precise identification of when specific loss thresholds are crossed. Perplexity is computed as `exp(cross-entropy loss)`. The secondary metric is **speedup**, defined as the ratio of training steps (or equivalently, processed tokens) required by the baseline to reach a given log perplexity, divided by the steps required by MoE-Mamba to reach the same perplexity. Test log perplexity is reported in Appendix G (Figure 9) for the 100M models and shows the same ordering as training perplexity. Appendix C also reports **accuracy** (fraction of next-token predictions that are exactly correct), which reveals an interesting metric discrepancy discussed below.

- **Baselines.** Four baselines are compared throughout: **(1) Vanilla Mamba** — the Mamba architecture as described by Gu & Dao (2023) with expansion factor `E = 2`, no feed-forward layers. **(2) Vanilla Transformer** — a standard decoder-only Transformer with Rotary Position Embedding (Su et al., 2023), using dense feed-forward layers with `d_ff = 2048` (25M scale). **(3) Transformer-MoE** — a Transformer where dense feed-forward layers are replaced with Switch MoE layers, matching MoE-Mamba's expert configuration (same `d_expert`, same number of experts, same total parameters). Transformer-MoE serves as the critical baseline for testing whether MoE benefits are specific to SSMs or generic across architectures. **(4) Majority voting / best-of-N** is not used in this paper (unlike the example summary) — the evaluation is purely based on training perplexity curves.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** Compute is measured in **training steps** (or equivalently, **processed tokens**, since batch size and context length are held constant across comparable models). The batch size for 25M models is 64 sequences of 1024 tokens (~65K tokens per step). For 100M models, batch size is 1024 sequences of 1024 tokens (~1M tokens per step). The 2.35× speedup is measured in training steps, not wall-clock time. Active parameters per token are reported rather than FLOPs, following Zhou et al. (2022), because "the number of FLOPs is both harder to calculate and less relevant for hardware-aware architecture like Mamba with its optimizations, especially during inference" (Appendix B). However, active parameters and FLOPs are "roughly proportional" (Kaplan et al., 2020). The paper does not report wall-clock training times or FLOPs-per-step comparisons.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not employ cross-validation or multiple training runs with different random seeds. All training runs are single-instance. The speedup metric is computed from these single training curves, meaning there are no error bars, confidence intervals, or variance estimates on the reported speedup values (2.35× for 100M, 1.76× for 25M). Statistical significance is not assessed. This is a notable limitation — the results could be sensitive to initialization, data ordering, or other random factors, and the paper provides no way to assess this sensitivity.

- **Hyperparameter tuning.** Learning rates are tuned separately for each 25M-scale model architecture and then reused (divided by 2) for the 100M counterparts (Section 4.1). For Transformer-MoE100M, the learning rate is further halved "due to repeated instabilities." This means the learning rate was adjusted post-hoc based on observed training behavior, introducing a potential confound in the comparison — Transformer-MoE may not have received the same level of hyperparameter optimization as the other architectures. The paper uses the AdamW optimizer (Loshchilov & Hutter, 2019) with cosine learning rate decay, 1% warmup, final LR ratio of 0.1, weight decay of 0.1, and gradient clipping of 0.5 for all models (Tables 5 and 6). The GPT2 tokenizer (Radford et al., 2019) is used throughout.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Aggregate Architecture Comparison (Table 1, Figure 1)

The headline result is presented in Table 1 and Figure 1: **MoE-Mamba100M reaches the same log perplexity as Mamba100M in 2.35× fewer training steps.** Specifically, MoE-Mamba100M achieves a final EMA-smoothed training log perplexity of 2.81, compared to Mamba100M's 2.99, with a speedup of 2.35×. At the 25M scale, MoE-Mamba25M achieves log perplexity 3.19 versus Mamba25M's 3.34, with a speedup of 1.76×.

The comparison across all architectures for the 100M scale is:
- **Mamba100M:** 121M active parameters, log perplexity 2.99, speedup 1× (reference baseline)
- **MoE-Mamba100M (ours):** 2,439M total / 117M active parameters, log perplexity 2.81, speedup 2.35×
- **Transformer-MoE100M:** 2,454M total / 114M active parameters, log perplexity 2.88, speedup 1.79×

MoE-Mamba100M outperforms Transformer-MoE100M by 0.07 in log perplexity (2.81 vs. 2.88) despite having comparable active parameters (117M vs. 114M). For the 25M models, the corresponding gap is 3.19 vs. 3.23 — a 0.04 advantage for MoE-Mamba.

The vanilla Transformer25M achieves log perplexity 3.43, which is worse than all other models, consistent with the known advantages of both Mamba and MoE over dense Transformers at this scale. The paper does not report Transformer100M (dense) results, presumably because training a dense Transformer with the same active parameter count would have been computationally prohibitive given the total parameter counts of the MoE models.

Figure 1 visualizes the training dynamics: MoE-Mamba consistently maintains lower log perplexity than both Mamba and Transformer-MoE throughout training, with the gap widening over time. The Mamba baseline (top line) converges more slowly and to a higher final perplexity than both MoE models.

#### Speedup Growth During Training (Appendix D, Figure 8)

An important temporal dynamic: **the speedup of MoE-Mamba over vanilla Mamba increases as training progresses.** For the 25M models, speedup oscillates between 1.6× and 1.9× after an initial training period. For the 100M models, speedup "rises steadily" throughout the 30K training steps, reaching 2.35× at the end. This means the speedup is not constant — quoting a single number (2.35×) refers to the final value at the end of training, and the advantage would likely be smaller at earlier checkpoints.

The increasing speedup trend is significant because it suggests that MoE-Mamba's benefits compound over longer training. If the speedup were constant, one would expect the ratio of steps to reach any given perplexity to be roughly fixed. The growing ratio implies that MoE-Mamba achieves proportionally larger gains at lower (better) perplexity values — the model is not just learning faster but learning *differently*, with the benefits of conditional computation becoming more pronounced as the model converges to lower loss.

#### Optimal Ratio of Mamba to MoE Active Parameters (Figure 5, Table 2)

Section 4.3 investigates how the allocation of active parameters between Mamba layers and MoE layers affects performance, holding total parameters (542M) and active parameters per token (26M) constant across all configurations.

**The key finding: a 3:3 ratio of Mamba active to MoE active parameters is roughly optimal, with sharply diminishing returns for allocating more to Mamba.** The five tested ratios range from 1:5 (MoE-dominant) to 5:1 (Mamba-dominant), achieved by adjusting the Mamba expansion factor `E`, expert size `d_expert`, and number of experts (Table 2). The results (Figure 5, y-axis shows final log perplexity after training on ~10B tokens):

From the figure (reading left to right as Mamba-to-MoE ratio increases):
- **1:5 (E=2, d_expert=1536, 19 experts):** log perplexity is highest (worst) among all ratios — around 3.24–3.25 by visual estimate from Figure 5.
- **2:4 (E=1 2/3, d_expert=1536, 24 experts):** performance improves, reaching perhaps ~3.22.
- **3:3 (E=2, d_expert=1536, 32 experts):** log perplexity drops to approximately 3.19 — this is the optimum and the configuration used in all other experiments.
- **4:2 (E=2 2/3, d_expert=1024, 48 experts):** marginal further improvement, reaching perhaps ~3.18.
- **5:1 (E=3 1/3, d_expert=512, 96 experts):** similar or very slightly better than 4:2, but "impractical due to inefficient hardware utilization and high routing costs caused by a large number of experts" (Section 4.3).

The paper defaults to the 3:3 ratio even though the 4:2 and 5:1 ratios appear marginally better in Figure 5, because the gains are small and the practical costs (96 experts with very small individual capacity, high routing overhead, poor hardware utilization from small matrix multiplications) outweigh them. This is a pragmatic engineering choice rather than a claim that 3:3 is the theoretically optimal ratio — it represents the best balance of performance and practicality at this scale. Whether the optimum shifts at larger model sizes is not tested.

#### Number of Experts Ablation (Figure 4, Table 4)

**The performance of MoE-Mamba improves monotonically with the number of experts**, with the crossover to outperforming vanilla Mamba occurring at 4 experts.

Table 4 reports log perplexity after 1B tokens for MoE-Mamba25M variants with different numbers of experts, all maintaining 26M active parameters per token by adjusting expert size and count proportionally:

- **Vanilla Mamba:** 27M parameters, log perplexity 3.72
- **1 expert (dense FFN):** 26M parameters, log perplexity 3.75 — *worse than vanilla Mamba*
- **2 experts:** 64M total, log perplexity 3.74 — *still worse than vanilla Mamba*
- **4 experts:** 114M total, log perplexity 3.72 — *matches vanilla Mamba*, speedup 1.03×
- **8 experts:** 215M total, log perplexity 3.70 — *outperforms*, speedup 1.10×
- **16 experts:** 416M total, log perplexity 3.67 — *substantially outperforms*, speedup 1.21×
- **32 experts:** 416M total (adjusted to maintain active parameters), log perplexity 3.67 — *matches 16 experts*, speedup 1.23×

Two patterns emerge. First, there is a clear monotonic improvement from 1 to 16 experts, after which the performance gain saturates (16 and 32 experts achieve the same log perplexity 3.67, though 32 experts has a slightly higher speedup — 1.23× vs. 1.21× — suggesting faster convergence despite the same final value). Second, the crossover from harmful to beneficial occurs at 4 experts — this is the minimum number needed for the sparsity to overcome the negative effect that dense FFNs have on Mamba.

Figure 4 visualizes these training curves and confirms that the ordering is consistent throughout training, not just at the final checkpoint. Models with more experts (8, 16, 32) separate from those with fewer experts (2, 4) relatively early in training, and the gap widens over time.

#### Sequential vs. Parallel MoE-Mamba (Figure 6, Table 7)

**Sequential MoE-Mamba consistently outperforms Parallel MoE-Mamba across all numbers of experts tested,** with the gap narrowing as the number of experts increases.

Table 7 provides the direct comparison after 1B tokens:

| # Experts | MoE-Mamba Sequential | MoE-Mamba Parallel |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 3.76 | 3.79 |
| 2 | 3.74 | 3.77 |
| 4 | 3.71 | 3.74 |
| 8 | 3.69 | 3.72 |
| 16 | 3.67 | 3.70 |
| 32 | 3.66 | 3.69 |

At every expert count, sequential achieves lower (better) log perplexity. The gap is approximately 0.03 at all counts. Importantly, Parallel MoE-Mamba with 8 experts (3.72) matches vanilla Mamba (3.72), but only with "between 2 and 4 times as many experts and total parameters to match the performance of the sequential variant" (Section 4.4). For example, sequential MoE-Mamba with 8 experts achieves 3.69, while parallel requires 16 experts (3.70) or 32 experts (3.69) to reach comparable performance. This makes the parallel variant substantially less parameter-efficient.

The paper notes that parallel MoE-Mamba "may be an attractive alternative at larger scales due to potentially enabling more efficient use of hardware due to different communication (Wang, 2021) or fused input matrix multiplications (Chowdhery et al., 2023)" (Section 4.4), but this is speculative — no large-scale experiments with the parallel variant are reported.

#### Inner MoE Modifications (Table 3)

**None of the 14 tested configurations of MoE within the Mamba block matches MoE-Mamba's performance**, and most substantially degrade it. Table 3 reports log perplexity after 1B tokens for all variants, compared to vanilla Mamba (3.72) and MoE-Mamba with 16 experts (3.67):

The best-performing internal MoE configurations are:
- **Gate Projection only (every other layer):** 3.70 — the single best internal MoE design
- **Gate Projection only (all layers):** 3.71
- **Output Projection only (every other layer):** 3.70
- **Conv Projection only (every other layer):** 3.71

These three designs achieve modest improvements over vanilla Mamba (3.72) but fall short of MoE-Mamba (3.67). The worst configurations involve multiple-projection MoE combinations, with the all-three-projections variant reaching 4.39 (all layers) and 3.88 (every other layer) — substantially worse than even the dense FFN baseline (3.75 for 1 expert in Table 4).

The key pattern: replacing any single projection with MoE is tolerable (performance near or slightly better than vanilla Mamba), but replacing multiple projections causes compounding degradation. The Output Projection is most sensitive to MoE replacement (4.05 for all layers, vs. 3.71 for Gate only). The every-other-layer application strategy is generally better than applying MoE to all layers for a given projection combination.

#### Accuracy-Perplexity Discrepancy (Appendix C, Figure 7)

**Despite achieving lower perplexity, MoE-Mamba can exhibit lower next-token prediction accuracy than Transformer-MoE.** This is documented in Section 4.6 and Appendix C for a variant of MoE-Mamba25M with 32 experts (not the 42-expert configuration from the main experiments). Figure 7 shows that MoE-Mamba25M maintains lower log perplexity than Transformer-MoE25M throughout training, yet Transformer-MoE consistently achieves higher accuracy (fraction of tokens predicted correctly).

The authors hypothesize that this discrepancy arises because "attention-based models are able to copy tokens verbatim, unlike SSM-based models, whose similar abilities might be hindered by the compression of the history into a finite hidden state" (Section 4.6). This connects to mechanistic interpretability work (Elhage et al., 2021; Olsson et al., 2022) showing that Transformers develop "induction heads" capable of exact token copying across context, a capability that Mamba's fixed-size state may not support as effectively. The implication is that perplexity and accuracy can diverge as evaluation metrics — models may achieve low perplexity by being well-calibrated on frequent patterns while still failing on tasks requiring precise recall of specific tokens.

This finding is important but is presented only for a single model variant and is not explored systematically across scales, numbers of experts, or training durations. It is noted as an observation rather than a rigorously tested result.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Number of experts (Table 4, Figure 4):** MoE-Mamba performance improves monotonically from 1 to 32 experts, with the crossover from harmful to beneficial at 4 experts. The 32-expert configuration achieves the best speedup (1.23× over vanilla Mamba at 1B tokens). This is a robustness check confirming that the main results (using 42 experts) are not sensitive to the exact number of experts — any number above ~8 yields clear benefits.

**Mamba-to-MoE active parameter ratio (Figure 5, Table 2):** Performance is relatively robust across a range of ratios from 2:4 to 5:1, with the 3:3 ratio chosen as the practical optimum. The 1:5 ratio (MoE-dominant) is clearly suboptimal. This ablation shows that the main results (which use 3:3) are near but not exactly at the theoretical optimum — slightly more Mamba-heavy ratios (4:2, 5:1) might yield marginally better perplexity but are impractical.

**Sequential vs. Parallel integration (Figure 6, Table 7):** Sequential integration outperforms parallel across all expert counts, with the gap roughly constant at ~0.03 log perplexity. This robustness check confirms that the sequential design choice is not sensitive to the number of experts.

**Internal MoE placement (Table 3):** All 14 configurations of MoE within the Mamba block underperform the proposed sequential interleaving, with three designs achieving marginal improvements over vanilla Mamba (3.70–3.71 vs. 3.72) but none approaching MoE-Mamba (3.67). This is a comprehensive negative result that validates the design decision to place MoE layers outside the Mamba block.

**Scaling to 100M parameters (Table 1, Figure 1):** The speedup of MoE-Mamba over Mamba increases from 1.76× at the 25M scale to 2.35× at the 100M scale, and the gap over Transformer-MoE widens from 0.04 to 0.07 log perplexity. This is a limited but positive scaling trend — the benefits do not diminish at larger scale, and may increase. However, only two scales are tested, and the 100M scale is still small relative to production models.

**Training duration (Appendix D, Figure 8):** The speedup grows over training time, from ~1.6× early in training to 2.35× at the end. This suggests that the reported speedup is a lower bound for what would be observed with longer training.

**Test set evaluation (Appendix G, Figure 9):** The ordering of models on test log perplexity matches the training set ordering, confirming that the results are not an artifact of overfitting. The test curves are noisier due to "a limited number of sequences in each evaluation step" (Appendix G).

**Learning rate stability:** Transformer-MoE100M required halving the learning rate "due to repeated instabilities," while MoE-Mamba did not require such adjustment. While not a formal ablation, this indicates that MoE-Mamba exhibits more stable training dynamics than Transformer-MoE, which is a practical robustness advantage.

### Critical Assessment

#### The 2.35× Speedup Claim: Demonstrated, but on a Single Run with a Specific Model Scale

The headline claim — "MoE-Mamba reaches the same performance as Mamba in 2.35× fewer training steps" — is the paper's central quantitative result. The experiments do demonstrate this for the specific configuration tested (MoE-Mamba100M vs. Mamba100M on C4, 30B tokens, single training run). Figure 1 and Table 1 clearly show MoE-Mamba's curve consistently below Mamba's, crossing any given log perplexity threshold earlier.

However, several qualifications apply that narrow what has actually been demonstrated:

**The speedup is measured from single training runs** with no error bars, confidence intervals, or multiple seeds. The reported 2.35× could vary under different random initializations or data orderings, and the paper provides no way to assess this. In the deep learning reproducibility literature, training curve variance is known to be non-trivial, especially for MoE models where routing noise can affect early training dynamics. A single run per configuration is insufficient to establish a reliable speedup estimate — at minimum, 3–5 runs with standard deviation would be needed.

**The speedup refers specifically to the *end of training*** and is not constant. Appendix D (Figure 8) shows the speedup rising from lower values throughout training. If a practitioner needed to stop training at an earlier checkpoint (e.g., due to compute budget), the realized speedup would be smaller than 2.35×. The paper's reported number is essentially the maximum speedup observed over the entire training run, not a representative average.

**The speedup is measured in training steps, not wall-clock time or FLOPs.** While MoE-Mamba uses fewer training steps, each step processes the same number of tokens (~1M for the 100M models) and has comparable active parameters per token (117M vs. 121M). However, the MoE layers introduce additional overhead from routing computation, all-to-all communication in distributed training, and load balancing loss computation that are not present in vanilla Mamba. The paper does not report per-step wall-clock time. If MoE-Mamba's training steps are, say, 20% slower than Mamba's (due to routing overhead), the wall-clock speedup would be 2.35 / 1.20 ≈ 1.96× — still substantial but notably less than claimed. Without wall-clock measurements, the "speedup" should be understood as a step-count reduction, not necessarily an end-to-end training time reduction.

**The speedup was measured at the 100M scale with 30B tokens.** Extrapolation to larger scales or longer training is not supported by data. The paper shows speedup increasing from 25M to 100M (1.76× → 2.35×) and increasing over training time (Figure 8), both of which are encouraging trends, but two data points do not establish a scaling law. At billion-parameter scales, factors like expert capacity saturation, routing collapse, or communication bottlenecks could emerge that change the speedup characteristics.

**The "same performance" is measured by log perplexity, which Appendix C shows can diverge from accuracy** — MoE-Mamba can have lower perplexity but lower accuracy than Transformer-MoE. If the downstream metric of interest is accuracy rather than perplexity, the speedup relative to Mamba (which likely also shows the perplexity-accuracy discrepancy) is unclear.

In summary, the 2.35× speedup claim is **supported as an empirical observation for this specific configuration**, but it should not be interpreted as a general property of MoE-Mamba architectures or as a guarantee that similar speedups would be observed at larger scales, on different datasets, or in wall-clock time.

#### The Claim That MoE-Mamba Outperforms Transformer-MoE: Supported but with a Hyperparameter Confound

In Table 1, MoE-Mamba100M achieves log perplexity 2.81 versus Transformer-MoE100M's 2.88 — a 0.07 advantage. This supports the paper's claim that combining MoE with Mamba is more effective than combining MoE with Transformers.

However, there is a significant confound: **Transformer-MoE100M required a lowered learning rate due to training instabilities** (learning rate halved to `2.5 × 10^{-4}`, while MoE-Mamba100M used `5 × 10^{-4}` — Table 6). This means the comparison is not between architectures at their respective optimal hyperparameters, but rather between MoE-Mamba at its tuned learning rate and Transformer-MoE at a learning rate that was *adjusted down in response to instability*. If Transformer-MoE simply requires more careful learning rate tuning (e.g., a different schedule, warmup, or optimizer settings), the performance gap might be narrower than reported. The fact that Transformer-MoE could not be trained stably at the same learning rate as MoE-Mamba is itself meaningful — it suggests MoE-Mamba has more benign optimization dynamics — but it also means the perplexity gap partly reflects training stability rather than architectural superiority.

Additionally, the Transformer-MoE baseline uses a *fixed* allocation of parameters between attention and FFN layers (inherited from the standard Transformer design), while MoE-Mamba uses a *tuned* 3:3 ratio of Mamba to MoE active parameters. The paper tunes the Mamba-to-MoE ratio (Section 4.3, Figure 5) but does not tune the corresponding attention-to-FFN ratio for Transformer-MoE. If the optimal ratio differs for attention-based models, Transformer-MoE might benefit from a similar ratio sweep.

Finally, the comparison is at a single scale (100M active parameters) and a single training duration (30B tokens). The paper shows that MoE-Mamba's advantage over Transformer-MoE widens from the 25M scale (0.04 perplexity gap) to the 100M scale (0.07 perplexity gap) — a trend that, if it continues, would make the advantage even larger at bigger scales. But this is extrapolation from two points.

#### The Claim That Benefits Are "Robust to Design Choices and Number of Experts": Partially Supported

The paper claims via comprehensive studies that "the improvement achieved by MoE-Mamba is robust to varying model sizes, design choices, and the number of experts."

**Number of experts:** Robustness here is well-supported. The monotonic improvement from 4 to 32 experts (Table 4, Figure 4) shows that the method works across a wide range of expert counts, and the crossover at 4 experts establishes a clear operational threshold. The main experiments at 42 experts are consistent with this trend.

**Model sizes:** Only two sizes are tested (25M and 100M active parameters). The speedup increases from 1.76× to 2.35×, which is encouraging but does not establish robustness — it could be that the speedup peaks at some intermediate scale and declines thereafter, or that it continues to grow. Two points are insufficient to characterize the scaling trend.

**Design choices:** The paper ablated two major alternatives (Parallel integration and Inner MoE) and found both to underperform. This shows that the sequential interleaving is the best among the tested options, but the design space of possible SSM+MoE combinations is vastly larger than what was explored (different routing strategies beyond Switch, different expert architectures, different interleaving patterns, different placement of MoE relative to Mamba's internal projections, etc.). The claim of robustness to design choices should be understood as: *within the set of designs tested, sequential interleaving is consistently best*. It does not mean that MoE-Mamba is robust to arbitrary design perturbations.

#### Missing Experiments That Would Strengthen the Paper

**Multiple training runs.** The absence of any variance estimate is the single largest methodological gap. At minimum, reporting standard deviations across 3–5 seeds for the main perplexity and speedup results would allow readers to assess whether the observed gaps (e.g., 2.81 vs. 2.88 for MoE-Mamba vs. Transformer-MoE) are statistically meaningful or within the noise of training variance.

**Wall-clock time measurement.** Reporting both step-count speedup and wall-clock speedup would address the critical practical question of whether MoE-Mamba actually trains faster in real time or merely requires fewer optimizer steps (with each step potentially being more expensive). The MoE routing, expert dispatch, and load balancing computation introduce overhead that is not captured by active parameter counts. Even a single measurement of per-step time for each model would be informative.

**Ablation of the load balancing loss coefficient.** The paper uses `α = 0.01` for the auxiliary load balancing loss, following Fedus et al. (2022). This hyperparameter controls the tradeoff between routing quality and load balance. If it were set differently (lower α allows more imbalanced routing, potentially improving performance on easy tokens but causing more token dropping; higher α forces uniform routing, potentially reducing specialization), the results might change. This is especially relevant because Mamba's token representations may have different routing characteristics than Transformer representations, so the optimal α might differ.

**Ablation of capacity factor.** The paper uses a capacity factor of 1, meaning tokens are dropped if an expert is overloaded. Higher capacity factors (e.g., 1.25 or 1.5) reduce token dropping at the cost of more computation. The extent to which token dropping affects MoE-Mamba's training dynamics is unknown and could interact with Mamba's sequential processing (dropped tokens might create "gaps" in the sequence representation that the SSM must handle).

**Evaluation on standard language modeling benchmarks.** The paper reports only perplexity on C4. Performance on downstream benchmarks (e.g., LAMBADA, HellaSwag, PIQA, or MMLU) would contextualize whether the perplexity improvements translate to practically meaningful capability gains, and whether the accuracy-perplexity discrepancy noted in Appendix C affects benchmark performance.

**Long-context evaluation.** The paper motivates MoE-Mamba partly by Mamba's linear-time inference, which is most advantageous for long contexts, but all experiments use a context length of 1024 tokens — which is short by modern standards and well within the regime where Transformers are efficient. Experiments at longer context lengths (e.g., 8K, 32K, or beyond) would test whether MoE-Mamba's claimed inference advantages materialize in practice and whether the MoE layers interact differently with long-context Mamba representations.

**Inference speed benchmarking.** The paper claims MoE-Mamba "preserves the inference performance gains of Mamba against Transformer" but provides no inference timing data. Measuring tokens-per-second at various batch sizes and sequence lengths for MoE-Mamba vs. Mamba vs. Transformer-MoE would substantiate this claim.

#### Where the Claims Hold Conditionally

- **The 2.35× speedup holds for the specific MoE-Mamba100M vs. Mamba100M comparison** on C4 with 30B tokens of training, using a single training run, measured in training steps (not wall-clock time), and referenced to the end of training (speedup is lower earlier). Whether it generalizes to other scales, datasets, or training durations is not tested.

- **The advantage over Transformer-MoE holds at the tested scales** but may partially reflect training stability differences (Transformer-MoE required a lowered learning rate) rather than purely architectural superiority. The gap widens from 25M to 100M, suggesting the advantage grows with scale, but two data points are insufficient to confirm a trend.

- **The optimal 3:3 Mamba-to-MoE parameter ratio holds at the 25M scale with 542M total parameters** and the specific hyperparameter configuration used. Whether this ratio is scale-invariant (i.e., would also be optimal at 1B+ parameters) is not tested.

- **The sequential > parallel design preference holds across the tested expert counts (1–32)** but may not hold at larger scales where hardware considerations (communication overhead, kernel fusion opportunities) change the practical tradeoff between the two designs.

- **The robustness of MoE-Mamba to design choices is demonstrated only for the specific alternatives tested** (Parallel, Inner MoE variants). The design space is much larger, and the paper does not test variations in routing strategy, expert architecture, interleaving frequency, or placement granularity.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### Difficulty Estimation Cost Is Not Accounted For
- **The assumption or constraint:** MoE-Mamba's primary efficiency metric — 2.35× training step reduction — is measured in terms of optimizer steps or processed tokens, comparing models with comparable active parameters per token. However, this metric does not account for the overhead of MoE-specific operations: the routing computation, the all-to-all communication for dispatching tokens to experts in distributed training, the load balancing loss evaluation, and token dropping. The paper acknowledges that it reports active parameters rather than FLOPs because "the number of FLOPs is both harder to calculate and less relevant for hardware-aware architecture like Mamba with its optimizations, especially during inference" (Appendix B), but this choice means the "speedup" is a step-count ratio, not a wall-clock time or total compute ratio.

- **The consequence:** If each MoE-Mamba training step is slower than a vanilla Mamba step — due to routing overhead, expert dispatch communication across GPUs, or load balancing computation — the realized wall-clock speedup is strictly less than the reported 2.35×. In the worst case, if per-step latency were, for instance, 40% higher for MoE-Mamba than Mamba, the wall-clock speedup would be approximately 2.35 / 1.40 ≈ 1.68× — still beneficial but substantially below the headline claim. The paper provides no per-step timing data, no FLOPs accounting that includes routing, and no analysis of distributed training communication patterns. This makes it impossible for a practitioner to estimate the actual training throughput improvement they would observe when deploying MoE-Mamba at scale.

- **What evidence exists in the paper:** The paper explicitly does not measure wall-clock time. Appendix B justifies avoiding FLOPs comparisons by citing hardware-aware optimizations in Mamba, but this argument applies to Mamba's internal computations (kernel fusion, activation recomputation), not to the MoE layers added on top. The MoE layers use standard matrix multiplications without Mamba's specialized CUDA kernels. Table 1 reports parameters and step-count speedup only. No timing experiments are reported anywhere in the paper or appendices. The parallel MoE-Mamba experiments (Section 4.4, Table 7) are motivated partly by potential hardware efficiency from fused matrix multiplications (Chowdhery et al., 2023), but this remains speculative — no timing data supports the claim that parallel integration would improve throughput.

- **Mitigation status:** Not addressed. The paper does not report any wall-clock measurements, does not estimate the FLOPs overhead of routing and expert dispatch, and does not discuss how these overheads scale with model size or expert count. Future work is not explicitly called out for this gap — the discussion of "efficient hardware utilization" in Section 4.3 (regarding the 3:3 ratio choice) and Section 4.4 (regarding parallel MoE-Mamba) gestures at the practical concern but does not propose measurement or mitigation strategies.

### Single Benchmark, Single Model Family, Single Training Run
- **The assumption or constraint:** All experiments use the C4 dataset for perplexity-based language modeling evaluation with PaLM-architecture-inspired Mamba models. The paper acknowledges training on "C4 dataset (Raffel et al., 2020) on the next token prediction task" (Section 4.1), with the GPT2 tokenizer. No downstream benchmarks (e.g., LAMBADA, HellaSwag, MMLU, or any task requiring reasoning, factual recall, or few-shot learning) are evaluated. The models are based on a single SSM architecture (Mamba) with a single MoE design (Switch Transformer). Furthermore, each configuration is trained exactly once — there are no multiple random seeds, no cross-validation, no error bars on any reported metric.

- **The consequence:** The reported speedups and perplexity improvements are uncalibrated for variance. A practitioner cannot assess whether the 2.35× speedup is reliably achievable or whether it could be, say, 1.8× or 2.8× under different random initializations. More importantly, the accuracy-perplexity discrepancy documented in Appendix C — where MoE-Mamba achieves lower perplexity but lower accuracy than Transformer-MoE — suggests that perplexity improvements may not translate to downstream task performance. If MoE-Mamba's lower perplexity reflects better calibration on frequent patterns rather than genuine language understanding improvements (as the authors hypothesize when discussing Mamba's limited verbatim copying ability), then the headline speedup may overstate practical gains for applications where accuracy matters. The absence of downstream benchmarks means there is no evidence that the perplexity improvements produce models that are more useful for any specific task.

- **What evidence exists in the paper:** Section 4.6 and Appendix C present the accuracy-perplexity discrepancy for one model variant (MoE-Mamba25M with 32 experts vs. Transformer-MoE25M, Figure 7), showing that despite lower log perplexity, MoE-Mamba achieves lower next-token prediction accuracy. The authors hypothesize this is due to SSM-based models having limited ability to "copy tokens verbatim" because of "compression of the history into a finite hidden state." This observation is not systematically explored — it is presented for a single variant, at a single scale, with no investigation of whether the gap widens or narrows with scale, training duration, or number of experts. The paper does not evaluate any standard language model benchmarks. Additionally, all results in Table 1, Figures 4–6, and Tables 3–4, 7 are from single training runs. No confidence intervals, standard deviations, or multiple-seed averages are reported anywhere.

- **Mitigation status:** Partially acknowledged. The accuracy-perplexity discrepancy is discussed in Section 4.6 and Appendix C, with the authors noting that "this discrepancy hints at a potential failure mode of Mamba and other SSMs." However, no experiments are run to quantify the scope of the problem (e.g., whether it affects all SSM-based architectures, whether it diminishes at larger scales, whether specific token types are differentially affected). The single-run limitation is not acknowledged at all — the paper reports exact numbers (2.35×, 3.19, 2.81) without any discussion of variance or reproducibility. The future work section (Section 5) mentions "developing scaling laws" and studying "synergies of Mamba and MoE" but does not call for replication with multiple seeds or broader benchmark evaluation.

### Training Scale Is Limited to 100M Active Parameters and 30B Tokens
- **The assumption or constraint:** The largest model trained has approximately 121M active parameters (MoE-Mamba100M) and processes approximately 30B tokens. The paper states: "In this work, we perform experiments on models with the number of active parameters per token smaller than 1B, with total parameters up to 2.4B" (Section 5). This is small relative to modern production language models, which typically operate at billions of active parameters and trillions of training tokens. The paper explicitly frames this as a limitation: "Scaling... we will be excited to see the impact of scaling on the approaches proposed in our work."

- **The consequence:** Several phenomena observed at small scale may not extrapolate to larger scales, and new phenomena may emerge. Specifically: **(1) The optimal 3:3 Mamba-to-MoE parameter ratio** (Section 4.3, Figure 5) might shift at larger scales — as models grow, the relative importance of unconditional sequence processing vs. conditional per-token computation could change. **(2) The crossover point where MoE becomes beneficial** (4 experts at 25M scale, Table 4) might differ at larger scales — the minimum viable sparsity for the model might increase or decrease as total capacity grows. **(3) The increasing speedup with training duration** (Appendix D, Figure 8) might plateau or reverse — if MoE-Mamba saturates its expert capacity earlier than Mamba saturates its state space capacity, the speedup could stop growing or even shrink at very long training horizons. **(4) Training stability** — Transformer-MoE100M required learning rate reduction due to instabilities (Table 6); at larger scales, MoE-Mamba might encounter its own stability challenges not visible at 100M parameters.

- **What evidence exists in the paper:** The paper shows two scale points: ~25M and ~100M active parameters. Between these, the speedup increases (1.76× → 2.35×) and the gap over Transformer-MoE widens (0.04 → 0.07 log perplexity). These trends are encouraging but insufficient for establishing scaling laws — two data points cannot distinguish between linear, sublinear, or superlinear scaling of the speedup. The paper also shows speedup increasing with training duration at fixed scale (Appendix D, Figure 8) — again encouraging, but only for the tested 30B-token horizon. The paper does not attempt to fit parametric scaling laws or predict behavior at larger scales, unlike the approach in Kaplan et al. (2020) or Hoffmann et al. (2022) for Transformers.

- **Mitigation status:** Explicitly acknowledged as a limitation in Section 5: "Scaling... we will be excited to see the impact of scaling on the approaches proposed in our work. Developing scaling laws would be instrumental in this endeavor." The authors do not attempt to address it experimentally. Follow-up work by the community (training MoE-Mamba at billion-parameter scales on hundreds of billions of tokens) would be needed to determine which of the paper's findings are scale-invariant and which are artifacts of the small-scale regime. The paper notes that concurrent work (Anthony et al., 2024, BlackMamba) explores MoE-SSM combinations on downstream tasks, providing some validation at larger scale, but the architecture and design choices differ.

### Inference Efficiency Claims Are Not Empirically Validated
- **The assumption or constraint:** The paper repeatedly states that MoE-Mamba "preserves the inference performance gains of Mamba against Transformer" (Abstract, Section 1) and references Mamba's linear-time inference as a key motivation. Specifically, the advantages of Mamba over attention are described as "especially prominent during inference, as not only is the computational complexity lowered, but also the memory usage is not dependent on the context length" (Section 3.1). The paper also motivates MoE partly by its inference benefits: "MoE has enabled Transformers to be scaled to unprecedented sizes" and Mixtral "fares comparably to Llama 2 70B while requiring only around 1/6 of its inference computational budget" (Section 2). However, **no inference speed, latency, or memory usage measurements are reported anywhere in the paper.**

- **The consequence:** Adding MoE layers to Mamba introduces several inference-time considerations that are uncharacterized: **(1)** The MoE layers require loading expert parameters from memory; with many experts (42 in the main experiments, up to 96 in ratio experiments), the total parameter count is massive (2.4B for the 100M model) even though only one expert per layer is active per token. This means the model's memory footprint is dominated by expert parameters, which must either fit in GPU memory or be streamed from host memory — potentially negating Mamba's memory efficiency advantages. **(2)** The routing computation (linear projection + softmax + argmax) adds per-token overhead that scales with the number of experts. **(3)** In batched inference, load imbalance across experts can cause straggler effects where some experts process many tokens while others are idle. **(4)** The interaction between Mamba's recurrent state (which must be maintained per sequence in the batch) and the MoE expert dispatch (which routes tokens across experts) adds complexity to the inference implementation that is not present in either vanilla Mamba or vanilla Transformer-MoE. Without inference benchmarking, a practitioner cannot assess whether MoE-Mamba actually delivers faster tokens-per-second than a comparably-sized Transformer, or whether the memory footprint is practical for deployment hardware.

- **What evidence exists in the paper:** There is no inference timing data, no memory profiling, and no throughput measurements at any sequence length or batch size. The paper's claim that MoE-Mamba preserves Mamba's inference advantages is purely architectural reasoning — the Mamba layers still use linear-time SSM computation, and the MoE layers are feed-forward and thus also linear in sequence length — but this says nothing about constant-factor overhead, memory bandwidth constraints, or practical throughput. The paper does not compare inference FLOPs, tokens-per-second, or peak memory usage between MoE-Mamba, Mamba, and Transformer-MoE at any scale.

- **Mitigation status:** Not addressed experimentally. The future work section (Section 5) mentions "synergies" where "Mamba alleviates computation and memory throughput issues stemming from larger context sizes, while MoE alleviates those same issues stemming from increasing number of parameters and knowledge stored in the model," and speculates that "this synergy may allow for unprecedented scaling of language models both in the number of parameters and length of the input/output." This is a forward-looking claim about what *might* be possible, not a demonstrated capability. The paper does not call for future inference benchmarking work explicitly.

### The Revision-Search Hybrid and Long-Context Synergies Are Hypothesized but Not Tested
- **The assumption or constraint:** The paper positions MoE-Mamba as enabling two synergies: combining SSM efficiency (linear-time sequence processing) with MoE efficiency (sparse parameter activation) to address orthogonal bottlenecks (Section 1, Section 3.2, Section 5). However, the experiments test these mechanisms only in isolation and only at a short context length of 1024 tokens. The long-context regime — where Mamba's linear-time property matters most relative to Transformers' quadratic attention — is never evaluated. The interaction between MoE routing and long-context Mamba representations is unexplored.

- **The consequence:** The claimed "synergy" remains a hypothesis rather than an empirically demonstrated property. If Mamba's long-context representations are qualitatively different from its short-context representations (e.g., compressing more information into the same fixed-size state), the MoE routing might need different capacity, different numbers of experts, or different load balancing to work effectively. If the MoE layers introduce sequence-position-dependent computation patterns (certain positions routing to certain experts), this could interact with Mamba's selective state updates in ways that either amplify or degrade long-context processing. At context length 1024, the quadratic cost of attention is modest — Transformers are still efficient at this scale — so the experiments do not test the regime where Mamba's architectural advantages are most pronounced relative to Transformers. The paper's claim that MoE-Mamba "preserves the inference performance gains of Mamba against Transformer" therefore applies only to the tested short-context regime; whether the gains persist, amplify, or shrink at longer contexts is unknown.

- **What evidence exists in the paper:** All experiments use context length 1024 (Tables 5 and 6). This is a standard length for medium-scale language model training but is far from the long-context regime (8K–128K+ tokens) where Mamba's linear-time property provides the largest relative advantage over Transformers. The paper mentions long-context processing as a motivation (Section 1: "strong performance in tasks requiring long-context processing, such as those illustrated by the Long Range Arena"; Section 5: "Mamba can process very long inputs but might struggle with tasks requiring detailed knowledge of the past input") but conducts no experiments at extended context lengths. The Mamba-to-MoE ratio experiments (Section 4.3), sequential vs. parallel experiments (Section 4.4), and expert count experiments (Section 4.5) are all at context length 1024.

- **Mitigation status:** Partially acknowledged. Section 5 ("Future Work and Limitations") includes a subsection on "Long Context Utilization" that notes: "Mamba and other SSMs are praised for their ability to process long context. However, the extent to which they can utilize it effectively and techniques for improving the utilization have not yet been studied in depth." This acknowledges the gap but frames it as a general SSM limitation rather than a specific MoE-Mamba evaluation shortcoming. The paper does not commit to running long-context MoE-Mamba experiments in future work. The "Synergies" subsection speculates about "efficiency gains growing with the context length" but offers no empirical support.
