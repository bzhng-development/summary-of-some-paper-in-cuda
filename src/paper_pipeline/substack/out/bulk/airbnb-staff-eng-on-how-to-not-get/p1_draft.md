Here are the takeaways from this Laurent Charignon interview (went from IC to manager and back, ultimately Staff engineer at Airbnb, Instagram, and Stripe, with deep coaching and calibration expertise):

**Joining Airbnb and the test-infrastructure early days**

Laurent joined Airbnb in 2016 specifically for developer productivity work, after stints at Apple and Meta. He picked it partly for practical reasons (walking distance from home; no more 1.5-hour bus commute to Facebook) and partly because they were “very early on” in dev-prod. He joined as one of only two engineers on test infrastructure—the moment when that function was just starting to become multiple teams. The team grew to six, seven, eight people within a year, and the manager who oversaw it was stretched across three or four teams with more than 15 reports.

**Pitching his own transition to manager**

Rather than waiting to be asked, Laurent walked into his manager’s office and explicitly pitched becoming the team’s dedicated manager. His argument was utilitarian: the people on the team were mostly new to the industry and needed more support than the overextended manager could give; Laurent was already doing that work as tech lead, and formalizing the role would let him scale it. His manager agreed, starting him with two reports before the whole team rolled under him. The move from peer to manager presented immediate power-dynamic challenges. One engineer came to their first one-on-one with a litmus test: “you open your laptop, you have an email from recruiting saying your employee missed a scheduled interview—what do you do?” Laurent’s response (asking for the facts, listening for both sides) was the right answer; the wrong answer would have been “you’re grounded,” because “there’s always two sides to a story.” The test crystallized a principle—rigid, one-size-fits-all management styles fail. Micromanagement might work for new grads but will alienate experienced engineers; flexibility and adapting to the individual is what matters.

**Boredom as a signal and the manager-to-IC boomerang**

Laurent tracks boredom carefully: “if you feel bored in a job, something’s not right and that means you’ve got to do something about it.” He had initially switched to management out of a desire to make the team maximally effective on a big delivery he was leading as tech lead. The first phase was intense—learning “all the secrets” of leveling, promotions, performance, calibrations. The second phase was people management: building rapport, planning careers, representing interests. Then came a third phase where he started optimizing everything. He built a tool that defragmented his reports’ calendars against his own so they could maximize focus time. Then he aimed for the highest team-health score in Airbnb’s infrastructure group, sending an exhaustive 20-25 minute Google Form about every team process (standups, planning, code review, etc.). The results shocked him: “everybody thought the standup was useful for the other people,” so nobody actually needed it. They killed the expensive standup in favor of a lightweight form; team engagement and effectiveness climbed while time spent working decreased. After his wedding—three weeks completely away—he came back to find the team had planned the entire next quarter themselves using the algorithm he’d helped set up. “I was like, what am I doing here? I’m getting bored.” His manager offered him an M1→M2 path, but Laurent declined: he missed coding, shipping, watching graphs move. So he switched back to IC.

**The surprise factor and managing career alignment**

As a manager, Laurent instituted what he calls the **surprise factor**: before delivering a performance review or rating, he and the report would each write their expectation on a piece of paper and reveal them simultaneously. If they matched, it was a pass; if not, it was a failure of the manager. “You want to minimize the surprise. If you’re a good manager, you don’t want people to have surprise at performance review time.” Positive surprises are bad too—if a manager puts someone up for promotion without ever signaling it, they missed an opportunity to coach the person into taking more risks and accelerating earlier. Most of his own promos had an element of surprise, which he regards as a sign that he didn’t have a good read on the system. The worst instance: as an IC4 (senior level) leading the test-infra team, he was expecting a staff promotion. Instead he got a meets-expectations rating because he’d changed roles (to M0) two months before the end of the cycle—an automatic-meets rule he never knew existed because he hadn’t had explicit enough conversations with his manager about timing. “I should have said, what happens at the end of the year if I change role two months before? Can you check?” That experience gave him conviction that career conversations must be ongoing, not passive; otherwise “it’s like life is very short” and you’re just hoping the system delivers what you want.

**Untold rules of calibrations and the manager’s value alignment**

Every organization develops local, unwritten calibration rules: a new grad cannot be promoted in fewer than X months; anyone in a new role for less than ~2.5 months gets an automatic meets; and so on. Laurent led a transparent session for all ICs in his org at Airbnb walking through “what really happens in calibration,” with a bar that “if you teleport anybody in that room at any point in time and they see what’s happening, they would be proud of the work we’re doing.” When he encountered a rule he thought was demotivating or outdated, he disputed it—but “don’t do it in the room.” Public disagreement makes people defensive because their reputation feels at stake; instead, gather evidence, make your case one-on-one, and take the time to understand *why* the rule exists. Sometimes the backstory persuades you; sometimes it turns out to be “a wrong precedent that was set a long time ago that’s no longer valid.” Laurent also names a hard truth of the manager role: “you represent the interest of the company. You have to put the company first before your report.” Doing this job well requires strong value alignment with the company—if you don’t believe the decisions you’re defending, “it’s going to make you quite sad.”

**Disputing bad metrics with better ones**

When Stripe leadership considered using lines of code or PR count as productivity metrics—something nearly everyone disliked—Laurent didn’t just oppose it; he reframed the question entirely. Instead of measuring output, he instrumented the journey of a code change: thinking, writing, CI, code review, back-and-forth. That surfaced richer signals, like “in this organization it takes twice as long to make a code change as in another.” That gave him a lever to investigate *why*—awful codebase? inefficient processes?—without reducing anyone to a single number. “The manager assesses all the work that someone’s doing, regardless of if it’s code or not.”

**Airbnb’s design-led culture and the firefighter hire**

Airbnb’s design-led DNA made it harder to justify low-level infrastructure investments, but it also produced outside-the-box thinking. When the company struggled with incident and reliability problems, someone asked: who’s good at this? Firefighters, emergency responders—people trained in crisis management. They hired from that background to overhaul incident measurement, and it “brought a lot of rigor and interesting practices” that Laurent carried to every subsequent job.

**Boomerang to Meta (Instagram) and onboarding Facebook’s reliability tools**

Four years in, the stock cliff hit and Laurent’s earning potential was dropping. He thought he wanted to be an “infra generalist” (he was wrong, he admits), and that job search landed him on-call for Instagram over July 4th—pager going off while he was trying to meet new neighbors. He decided to do “everything I can to make Instagram as reliable as possible.” From his Facebook tenure, he knew the mothership had already solved many incident patterns with mature tools that Instagram wasn’t using. He pitched his manager’s manager on a partnership to onboard them. The company paired him with an IC on the Facebook side named **Ariane**, whom Laurent calls “one of the most remarkable tech leads I’ve seen.” She bridged the orgs and brought the right people into the room. Laurent was so impressed that he explicitly asked her to mentor him—a pattern of his: “when I met them, I’m like, this person’s able to be thoughtful, to challenge me… they would just work well with me.” His litmus test for a great coach: “are you able to change your mind as a result of coaching, or do you just do the same thing you were doing before?”

**Uber TL at Stripe: credibility through questions, not dictates**

Laurent joined Stripe as tech lead for build, test, and tooling—a 35-person group covering Bazel, remote builds, internal GitHub, code review, testing infrastructure, testing data. His first move: embed. He rotated through the teams, working on a specific project for a month at a time, just to absorb rhythm and build face-to-face trust. He set a rule for himself that he maintained throughout his Stripe tenure: except in urgent incident situations, he would *never* tell people what to do or reject a design outright. All his influence came through asking questions, getting engineers to change their own minds. “If you use your past experience all the time to justify what to do, you’re like, ‘oh, it worked at Facebook, so it must work here’—it belittles the people and their experience.” After a year, he knew everyone and had been embedded in every part of the group, but recognized he now knew *less* than every individual about their own domain. That’s “what leadership is.” His frame: the engineers are experts; his job is to guide, ask good questions, and let his years of dev-prod intuition steer through inquiry rather than proclamation.

As the org swelled from ~35 to 140 people, his operating model shifted again. He’d deploy on a problem for two to eight weeks, bootstrapping something, getting execution in motion, then making it sustainable so he could leave without being the bottleneck. “I always think about sustainability first. What happens if I want to go on vacation next week? Will they have to call me?… I never want to be that guy who knows all the secrets about the code base and then you’re supposed to ask.”

**Scaling yourself across a 70+ person org**

When leading 70+ engineers, you cannot know what everyone is working on. In a leadership meeting when someone asks for a project update, you have to be comfortable saying “I don’t know—I’ll check and get back to you by that date.” In software engineering, “if you claim with confidence something that is wrong, you’re going to lose all the respect from your coworkers who are not going to be able to rely on your word moving forward.” To stay connected despite not having bandwidth for individual relationships, Laurent used **office hours**—a bookable block where people could grab time with him within the week. He also shifted his workday to 5:30am starts to overlap with European teammates, since half the group was in Europe.

**The coaching mistake nearly everyone makes and the situational-leadership fix**

Early in his career (Facebook, 2014), Laurent was onboarding buddy to four bootcampers. Three were thriving; one showed no progress. Scott Renfro pulled him aside and told him to look up **situational leadership**. The model maps skill-learning to two axes: mastery and motivation. Initially someone is high-motivation, low-skill; then their motivation dips as they realize how much they don’t know; eventually both climb. The coaching approach must shift through four phases: **directing** (explicit step-by-step), **selling** (encouraging while framing decisions), **supporting** (empowering them to make decisions), and **delegating** (getting out of the way). Laurent’s mistake had been assuming every beginner starts in the first bucket. His struggling bootcamper had already devoured the docs, realized how much he didn’t know, and was in a low-motivation, higher-knowledge phase. Directing him interrupted his learning; when Laurent switched to supporting—“giving way less advice about what to do”—the bootcamper took off. It “felt like magic.” The framework applies to everything: an engineer choosing a technology, a kid learning a game controller, a parent with an iPhone at 4am. “People make mistakes with coaching. They think they know the way; they find the technique that works and they pick one of those four and they forget the rest.”

**Why people get stuck at senior engineer**

The senior-to-staff promo demands a “big, big mindset shift” from working on problems other people have identified to autonomously running the full cycle: finding the problem, pitching it, solving it end-to-end, documenting the result. Staff to senior-staff is “the same thing, but with higher scope”; principal is just higher scope still. But getting from senior to staff breaks people because they’re used to being handed well-defined problems and asked to design a solution. Laurent’s advice for the stuck senior: **channel your inner frustration**. That means using whatever product or tool you’re working on from first principles and noticing every friction point as if you were exhausted and prone to fail. At Stripe this was institutionalized as the **friction log**—photograph every step, analyze every loading screen, every unnecessary window, every extra keystroke. From those 50-60 pieces of friction, you identify families of problems, rank them, and then go solve them. That cycle—finding, pitching, solving bigger problems than the ones you already know about—is “the staff engineer mindset,” and practicing it deliberately is the unlock.

**Radical Candor and the speed of the industry**

The most career-impacting book was Kim Scott’s *Radical Candor*, which gave Laurent a framework for the difficult, trust-building manager conversations he needed when the role was new. To his younger self, he’d say: “it’s moving even faster than you think.” When he entered the industry, interviews asked how many golf balls fit in a 747; in 2025, your screen and camera are monitored for AI use. Staying on top of everything requires constant reading—he’s read the top Hacker News posts every week for his whole career via hn.algolia.dev. “It is not the same to be a software engineer now than it was 10 years ago or 20 years ago. And I know that in five years it will be very, very different.”
