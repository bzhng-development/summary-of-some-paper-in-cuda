Here are the takeaways from this Ryan Peterman article comparing Junior (L3) and Mid-level (L4) engineers at FAANG-like companies:

**Scope & Impact**

The primary distinction between L3 and L4 engineers lies in the size of the scope they can handle independently. Ryan offers a clear rule of thumb: an L3 "can handle individual tasks (<2 weeks of work) with minimal guidance," while an L4 "can handle medium-to-large features (<2 months of work) with minimal guidance." At both levels, engineers are not expected to originate these projects—senior engineers (L5) typically set the initial direction and delegate downward. Because L4s are driving full features, they are expected to perform project management for them: breaking the project into tasks, setting reasonable timelines, and keeping stakeholders updated. The phrase "with minimal guidance" does not mean they cannot ask for help; it means they unblock themselves and make consistent progress. Ryan notes that "asking great questions is one of the most effective ways to unblock yourself."

**Engineering Craft**

Both levels are expected to ship solid, well-tested code, but L4s must take initiative where L3s act when directed. The stronger technical skills L4s possess are what enable them to handle more scope with less oversight. Specifically, an L4 is expected to pursue three areas without guidance: improving the codebase (leaving code better than they found it, initiating refactoring and code cleanups, giving thoughtful code reviews), contributing to production excellence (participating in oncall and debugging production breakages), and owning the health of what they build (adding test coverage, logging, and dashboards to monitor correctness). L3 engineers may do some of these things, but only when directed to.

**The L3 to L4 Promotion**

Ryan positions L3 and L4 as "the most similar on the IC ladder," with differences flowing from L4s' stronger technical skills. For an L3 looking to grow to L4, the path is direct: take on medium-to-large features and deliver them with the expected level of engineering craft. If you do that, Ryan says, "you'll get promoted quickly (~6-12 months)." He references his own rapid trajectory—having gone "from L3 to L6 in 3 years"—and teases a potential "speedrunner's guide" to the L3-to-L4 promotion as fast as possible, noting his unique perspective on accelerating through these early levels.

---

Ryan's **"rule of thumb" for scope sizing**—individual tasks vs. medium-to-large features—comes directly from his **"speedrunner's"** own compressed career path, where mastering the L4 bar of "with minimal guidance" proved to be the gateway to the senior delegation dynamics that followed. His framing of L3 and L4 as the most similar rungs on the ladder underscores that the jump is less about a dramatic role shift and more about demonstrating the **engineering craft initiative** (codebase improvements, production excellence, health ownership) that signals readiness for independent feature ownership.
