# Controlled Decoding from Language Models

**ArXiv:** [2310.17022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.17022)

## 🎯 Pitch

This paper introduces **controlled decoding (CD)**, a modular inference-time framework that solves a tokenwise KL-regularized reinforcement learning objective to steer frozen language model generation toward higher-reward outcomes.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **controlled decoding (CD)**, a modular inference-time framework that solves a tokenwise KL-regularized reinforcement learning objective to steer frozen language model generation toward higher-reward outcomes. The framework trains a separate prefix scorer—a value function for the reward—using either on-policy rollouts (**CD-FUDGE**, where the scorer learns from the reward of fully decoded base-model samples) or off-policy Bellman updates (**CD-Q**, where the scorer learns through temporal-difference targets), and deploys it in two modes: tokenwise sampling (adjusting per-token logits via the optimal RL policy) and blockwise best-of-K (selecting the highest-scoring block of M tokens among K parallel continuations). On dialog length control (Reddit corpus), helpfulness/harmlessness (Anthropic HH), and summarization quality (TL;DR) using PaLM 2-XXS, blockwise CD-Q achieves reward-KL tradeoffs on par with the sequence-level best-of-K baseline while requiring up to 10× fewer samples (e.g., matching best-of-50 performance with K=6 on length), and the prefix scorer transfers effectively to unseen base models without retraining, establishing that inference-time modular control can match or exceed training-time alignment methods across multiple rewards when blockwise reranking is used.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: How to Align Frozen Language Models at Inference Time

The fundamental question this paper tackles is deceptively simple: **if you have a pre-trained language model whose weights you cannot or do not want to modify, how can you steer its generation toward outputs that satisfy a reward function—higher helpfulness, lower toxicity, more concise summaries, or any other measurable property?** This matters for two distinct but equally important reasons.

First, there is a **practical deployment constraint**. In real-world production systems, the base generative model may be large, expensive to retrain, and shared across many use cases. Organizations may want to apply different alignment criteria to different users, domains, or applications without maintaining multiple fine-tuned model variants. A single frozen base model with swappable control modules would be far more operationally simple than retraining for every reward configuration.

Second, there is a deeper **theoretical gap** in our understanding of how inference-time intervention relates to the principled objective of KL-regularized reinforcement learning. Prior work had shown that KL-regularized RL (the framework behind RLHF, DPO, and related alignment methods) has a closed-form optimal policy: reweight the base model's token probabilities by an exponentiated value function (Korbak et al., 2022). However, as the paper notes in Section 2, this prior result fell "short of making the full connection in an autoregressive decoding setting." The implication is that if you could learn the *value function* of the language model's own decoding policy—the expected future reward from any partially decoded sequence—you could apply the optimal RL policy at inference time without ever touching the base model's weights. The paper's primary contribution is to formalize and operationalize this connection.

### The Two Paradigms of Alignment: Training-Time vs. Inference-Time

The paper identifies a fundamental divide in how the field approaches language model alignment (Section 1):

**Training-time (generator improvement) methods** update the language model's weights to internalize the reward signal. These include:
- **KL-regularized PPO** (Christiano et al., 2017; Ouyang et al., 2022), which trains the language model as a policy via on-policy RL with a KL penalty that keeps it close to its original behavior.
- **Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)** (Rafailov et al., 2023), which reparameterizes the RL objective to optimize a preference-based loss directly on pairwise data.
- **Sequence Likelihood Calibration (SLiC)** (Zhao et al., 2022) and **Identity Preference Optimization (IPO)** (Azar et al., 2023), which refine the DPO objective with different regularization schemes.

These methods have a clear advantage: at inference time, the model simply generates autoregressively—no extra computation is needed. But they have equally clear limitations. Once trained, the model is locked to a single reward configuration. If you want to change the reward function, adjust the tradeoff between multiple rewards, or deploy the model with different alignment criteria for different users, you must retrain from scratch. As the paper puts it, they "offer little configurability on the reward" (Section 1). They are also computationally expensive: full-model fine-tuning with RL or preference optimization requires careful hyperparameter tuning and is prone to reward over-optimization and capability degradation.

**Inference-time (add-on) methods** keep the language model frozen and apply control at generation time. The simplest and most widely used such method is **best-of-K** (Nakano et al., 2021; Stiennon et al., 2020; Touvron et al., 2023), which generates K complete responses from the base model, scores them with a reward function, and returns the highest-scoring one. Best-of-K is surprisingly effective—it consistently achieves better reward-KL tradeoffs than KL-regularized PPO in practice (Gao et al., 2023; Rafailov et al., 2023), and recent theoretical work (Yang et al., 2024) has shown that it is an almost optimal solution to the KL-regularized RL problem. However, best-of-K has a critical practical flaw: **latency**. All K sequences must be fully decoded before any output can be served to the user. For long-form generation tasks (essay writing, detailed explanations, multi-turn conversations), this latency is prohibitively expensive. You cannot stream a response when the algorithm hasn't yet decided which of K candidates to stream.

Other inference-time methods, such as **FUDGE** (Yang & Klein, 2021), **GeDi** (Krause et al., 2021), and **NADO** (Meng et al., 2022), have explored using learned prefix scorers to guide token-by-token generation from a frozen model. FUDGE, in particular, trains a discriminator that predicts, from a partial sequence, whether the final completed response will satisfy a constraint, then uses Bayes' rule to adjust token probabilities at each decoding step. These methods offer modularity (the scorer is separate from the base model) but have lacked a principled connection to the RL objective. They were developed as pragmatic solutions to constrained generation, not as solvers for the KL-regularized RL problem that is the standard formalization of alignment.

### Where Existing Approaches Fall Short

The paper identifies three specific gaps in the existing landscape, which collectively motivate the controlled decoding framework:

**1. No rigorous connection between prefix scoring and KL-regularized RL.** FUDGE and related methods trained prefix scorers using discriminative or regression objectives on partial sequences, but nobody had shown that this training procedure corresponds to learning the *value function* of the base language model's decoding policy. Without this connection, it was unclear whether tokenwise rescoring at inference time was actually implementing the optimal RL policy, or merely an ad hoc heuristic that happened to work. The paper's Theorem 2.1 establishes the optimal policy explicitly—reweight token probabilities by $e^{\lambda V^\star([x, y_t, z])}$, where $V^\star$ is the value function—and Theorem 3.1 shows that FUDGE's regression objective, when trained on base-model rollouts, converges to a stationary point of the true value function objective $L^\star$. This transforms FUDGE from a heuristic into a principled RL solver.

**2. The latency-throughput tradeoff for inference-time methods is unresolved.** Best-of-K achieves excellent reward-KL tradeoffs but requires decoding K full sequences before returning any output. Tokenwise decoding via a prefix scorer returns tokens immediately (no batching delay) but, as the paper's experiments show (Figure 4, 5), "tokenwise controllers don't offer much HH improvement over baselines." The reason, though not fully diagnosed, likely relates to the myopia of single-token scoring: a prefix scorer evaluating one token ahead may not capture the long-range consequences of that choice as effectively as evaluating a complete sequence or block. There is a missing middle ground—something that achieves best-of-K-level reward tradeoffs with acceptable latency—and prior work did not explore this systematically.

**3. The configurability and transfer potential of inference-time modules are underexploited.** Training-time methods produce a single aligned model. Even inference-time methods like FUDGE were not studied for their ability to (a) combine multiple reward functions at inference time without retraining, or (b) transfer to a different base model than the one used for training the prefix scorer. The paper argues that these properties—**modularity** and **transferability**—are natural advantages of the inference-time approach that had not been empirically demonstrated.

### Prior Work the Paper Builds On and Distinguishes From

The paper positions itself at the intersection of several research threads, each of which provides a component of the full solution but not the complete picture:

**From FUDGE (Yang & Klein, 2021):** The core idea of a prefix scorer that adjusts token probabilities at inference time. The paper's CD-FUDGE variant is essentially FUDGE re-derived as a value function learner, with the crucial addition of the theoretical connection to KL-regularized RL. The paper also extends FUDGE to the blockwise sampling mode, which was not explored in the original work.

**From DQN (Mnih et al., 2013):** The idea of learning a value function through temporal-difference updates—predicting the expected future reward and bootstrapping from the current value estimate for the next state. The paper's CD-Q variant adapts this to language modeling: the "state" is a partially decoded sequence $[x, y_t]$, the "action" is the next token $z$, and the Bellman target is the expected value under the base model's policy, $\sum_z \pi_{\text{ref}}(z|[x, y_t]) V_\theta([x, y_t, z])$. This is a novel application—DQN was designed for environments with external dynamics, not autoregressive generation—but the connection enables off-policy training without needing to roll out the base model.

**From Korbak et al. (2022):** The observation that KL-regularized RL can be viewed as Bayesian inference, with the optimal policy proportional to the base policy multiplied by an exponentiated reward function. The paper extends this insight from a general RL setting to the autoregressive tokenwise setting, where the "reward function" becomes the value function of the decoding policy. This is a non-trivial extension because it requires defining a tokenwise RL objective (Equation 2) and showing that the optimal tokenwise policy takes the same form as in the sequence-level case (Theorem 2.1).

**From best-of-K and its analysis (Stiennon et al., 2020; Beirami et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2024):** The observation that best-of-K empirically achieves better reward-KL tradeoffs than KL-regularized PPO. This motivates the blockwise CD variant: if best-of-K works well by reranking complete sequences, perhaps reranking *blocks* of tokens during decoding can achieve similar benefits with lower latency. The paper's blockwise CD is a direct attempt to bridge the gap between sequence-level best-of-K and tokenwise RL.

**From the reward over-optimization literature (Gao et al., 2023):** The understanding that pushing too hard against an imperfect reward model leads to reward hacking and degradation. This contextualizes why all methods in the paper are evaluated on reward-KL *tradeoff curves* rather than just reward maximization—the KL divergence from the base policy serves as a proxy for how much the model's other capabilities have degraded, and a method that achieves higher reward at the same KL is strictly better.

### How This Paper Positions Itself Differently

The paper's framing is that **inference-time control and KL-regularized RL are two sides of the same coin, and the value function is the bridge between them**. This is not just a theoretical insight—it directly motivates the choice of training objectives (CD-FUDGE as on-policy value regression, CD-Q as off-policy TD learning) and inference strategies (tokenwise sampling from the optimal RL policy, blockwise reranking as approximate RL). The paper is not proposing a fundamentally new alignment objective; it is proposing a new *solver* for the existing KL-regularized RL objective that happens to be modular, transferable, and latency-configurable.

The key differentiator from prior RL work (PPO, DPO, IPO) is the **decoupling of the value function from the policy**. In PPO, the value function (critic) and policy (actor) are trained together and the policy is what gets deployed. In DPO and IPO, the value function is implicit in the preference objective and the policy is what gets trained. In CD, the value function (prefix scorer) is trained separately from the policy (frozen base model), and they are combined only at inference time. This decoupling is what enables the transfer experiments (Sections 5, Experiments 5-7), where a prefix scorer trained on PaLM 2-XXS successfully controls PaLM 2-S and PaLM 2-XS, and the multi-objective experiment (Experiment 4), where HH and length prefix scorers are combined at inference time without retraining.

The blockwise sampling strategy is positioned as a **practical compromise** that navigates the latency-quality tradeoff. It is not presented as theoretically optimal (the paper acknowledges it is "not designed to optimally solve the sequence level KL-regularized objective," Section 3.2), but rather as an empirically motivated technique that captures the strengths of best-of-K (global scoring of blocks) while reducing decoding latency from full-sequence-length to block-size M. The paper further frames this as enabling streaming applications that best-of-K cannot support (Section 3.2, point 1).

Finally, the paper positions its modular approach as a solution to a problem that is both **technical** (how to align language models efficiently) and **operational** (how to deploy configurable, personalized alignment in production systems). The vision, hinted at in the Impact Statement, is that inference-time control modules could be swapped, combined, and transferred independently of the base model, enabling fine-grained and personalized alignment without the operational burden of maintaining many fine-tuned model variants.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

This paper develops a modular system that steers text generation from a frozen language model toward higher-reward outputs—longer responses, more helpful replies, better summaries—without ever modifying the base model's weights. The system solves this by training a separate lightweight neural network called a **prefix scorer** that predicts, from any partially generated text, how good the final completed response will be, and then uses this scorer at inference time to either adjust per-token sampling probabilities or select the best among multiple candidate continuations. The solution's "shape" is a decoupled architecture where the base model provides fluent language generation and the prefix scorer provides reward-guided steering, with the two combined only at generation time through mathematically principled operations derived from the optimal policy of KL-regularized reinforcement learning.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The system has four major components that interact at different stages:

1. **Frozen Base Language Model (`$\pi_{\text{ref}}$`)** — a pre-trained autoregressive language model (PaLM 2-XXS in experiments) that generates text token-by-token given a prompt and any previously generated tokens. This model's weights are never modified. It serves as the proposal distribution from which all candidate tokens and sequences originate.

2. **Prefix Scorer (`$V_\theta$`)** — a separate neural network, fine-tuned from the same base model architecture, that takes a prompt `$x$` and a partially decoded sequence `$y_t = [y_1, \ldots, y_t]$` as input and outputs a scalar value predicting the expected reward of the eventual completed response if decoding continues from that point following the base model's policy. This is trained using either on-policy rollouts (**CD-FUDGE**) or off-policy temporal-difference updates (**CD-Q**). The prefix scorer can be trained for multiple rewards independently (e.g., length, helpfulness, harmlessness).

3. **Inference-Time Sampling Strategy** — the mechanism that combines the base model's logits with the prefix scorer's values at generation time. The paper offers two modes:
   - **Tokenwise CD**: At each decoding step, compute the base model's next-token distribution `$\pi_{\text{ref}}(z|[x, y_t])$` and the prefix scorer's value for each possible next token `$V_\theta([x, y_t, z])$`. Combine them as `$\pi_{\text{ref}}(z|[x, y_t]) e^{\lambda V_\theta([x, y_t, z])}$` (renormalized) to get the aligned sampling distribution. The parameter `$\lambda$` controls the strength of alignment versus staying close to the base model.
   - **Blockwise CD**: At each block boundary, sample `$K$` independent continuations of `$M$` tokens from the base model. Score each completed block with the prefix scorer `$V_\theta$`. Select the highest-scoring block and continue autoregressively from it. Repeat until EOS is generated or the block containing EOS is selected.

4. **Reward Model** — a separate trained model that provides the scalar reward signal `$r([x, y])$` for fully decoded responses. This is used to generate training targets for the prefix scorer and, in some experiments, as the evaluation oracle. For helpfulness/harmlessness, this is a PaLM 2-XXS fine-tuned on Anthropic HH pairwise preferences using the Bradley-Terry model. For summarization quality, it is similarly trained on TL;DR preferences. For length control, the reward is simply `$\log(T / T_{\max})$` where `$T$` is the response length and `$T_{\max} = 1024$`.

**Information flow at training time (CD-Q):** Off-policy dataset of `$(x, y, r([x, y]))$` triples → For each prefix `$[x, y_t]$` in each sequence, compute the Bellman target: if `$y_t$` is not EOS, the target is `$\sum_{z} \pi_{\text{ref}}(z|[x, y_t]) V_\theta([x, y_t, z])$` (expected value under base policy's next-token distribution, computed by querying the base model for token probabilities and the current prefix scorer for values); if `$y_t$` is EOS, the target is `$r([x, y_t])$` → Train `$V_\theta$` to minimize MSE between its prediction at `$[x, y_t]$` and the computed target, with a stop-gradient on the target.

**Information flow at training time (CD-FUDGE):** Generate responses `$y$` by rolling out the base model `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$` on prompts `$x$` → For each prefix `$[x, y_t]$` in each generated response, the training target is simply the final reward `$r([x, y])$` of that particular completed response → Train `$V_\theta$` to regress each prefix's value to that final reward via MSE.

**Information flow at inference time (blockwise CD):** Prompt `$x$` enters → Base model generates `$K$` candidate blocks of `$M$` tokens from the current prefix → Prefix scorer evaluates each completed block → System selects the block with highest prefix scorer value → Selected block is appended to the decoded sequence → If the selected block contains EOS, stop; otherwise, repeat from the new (longer) prefix. The final output is a single concatenated response assembled from the winning blocks at each stage.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the **tokenwise KL-regularized RL objective** (Equation 2) and the optimal policy it implies (Theorem 2.1), because this mathematical foundation drives everything else—the training objectives, the inference strategies, and the theoretical justification for why a prefix scorer should work at all.
- **Second**, the **value function definition** (Equation 1) and why learning it is the central computational challenge, since both CD-FUDGE and CD-Q are, at their core, different ways to train a neural network to approximate this function.
- **Third**, the **CD-FUDGE training procedure**, which is the simpler approach—regress each prefix to the final reward of its completed response—and why it works despite being a noisy, high-variance estimator.
- **Fourth**, the **CD-Q training procedure**, which uses temporal-difference (Bellman) targets inspired by DQN to enable off-policy learning from static datasets without requiring on-policy base model rollouts.
- **Fifth**, the **two inference-time sampling strategies** (tokenwise and blockwise), their algorithms, their computational costs, and the tradeoffs they navigate between latency and reward-KL efficiency.
- **Sixth**, the **KL divergence bounds** for blockwise CD, which explain how the method stays close to the base model despite aggressive reranking, and how block size `$M$` and candidate count `$K$` jointly determine the alignment strength.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **methodological and empirical paper** whose core idea is that training a prefix scorer to approximate the value function of the base model's decoding policy enables principled inference-time control through the optimal policy of KL-regularized RL, and that applying this scorer in a blockwise reranking mode achieves the best practical reward-KL tradeoffs.

#### The Tokenwise KL-Regularized RL Objective

The paper formalizes controlled generation as a token-level optimization problem. The central object is a policy `$\pi$` that, given a prompt `$x$` and a partially decoded sequence `$y_t = [y_1, \ldots, y_t]$` of `$t$` tokens, produces a probability distribution `$\pi(\cdot|[x, y_t])$` over the next token `$z \in \mathcal{Y}$`, where `$\mathcal{Y}$` is the vocabulary. The base language model `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$` is one such policy—the one we start with and want to stay close to.

The paper defines a **tokenwise reward function** `$R$` that only fires at the end of a sequence:

$$R([x, y_t]) := \begin{cases} 0 & y_t \neq \text{EOS} \\ r([x, y_t]) & y_t = \text{EOS} \end{cases}$$

where `$r([x, y])$` is a scalar reward function bounded from above (e.g., log-likelihood of a helpfulness classifier for the complete response), and EOS is the end-of-sequence token.

**What it computes:** a sparse reward signal—zero for every intermediate decoding step, and the actual reward `$r$` only when the sequence terminates. The result is a single scalar per token position. This sparsity means that any prefix `$[x, y_t]$` with `$y_t \neq \text{EOS}$` has received zero reward so far, so the value of that prefix depends entirely on the *expected future reward* after completing the sequence.

**Why this form:** defining the reward tokenwise (rather than as a single sequence-level scalar) is what enables formulating a token-level RL objective and deriving a token-level optimal policy. If the reward were defined only at the sequence level, the optimization would be over entire sequences, not individual decoding steps. The sparsity (zero for non-EOS tokens) is not a bug but a feature—it means the prefix scorer must learn to predict the long-range consequences of each intermediate token choice, which is exactly the value function learning problem.

Given this reward structure, the **value function** `$V^\star$` is defined as the expected cumulative reward from a prefix under the base policy:

$$V^\star([x, y_t]) := \mathbb{E}_{z_1, z_2, \ldots \sim \pi_{\text{ref}}}\left[\sum_{\tau \geq 0} R([x, y_t, z_\tau])\right]$$

where `$z_\tau$` denotes the sequence of tokens generated by autoregressively sampling from `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$` starting from prefix `$[x, y_t]$`, and the sum is over all future tokens until EOS.

**What it computes:** for any partial sequence, the average reward that the base model's completions would receive. If `$y_t$` is already EOS, the sum over future tokens is empty, so `$V^\star([x, y_t]) = r([x, y_t])$`. If `$y_t$` is not EOS, the value is the expected reward of continuing to sample from `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$` until EOS is reached. This is a conditional expectation—given where you are now, what reward do you expect to accumulate?

**Why this form:** the value function is the sufficient statistic for decision-making in Markov decision processes. If you know `$V^\star$`, you can evaluate how good any partial sequence is and, crucially, how much better or worse a particular next token would be compared to the average. This is what the advantage function (defined next) captures, and it is the key to the optimal policy derivation.

The **advantage function** measures how much a particular next token `$z$` improves expected reward relative to the value of the current prefix:

$$A([x, y_t]; \pi) := \mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi}\left[V^\star([x, y_t, z]) - V^\star([x, y_t])\right] = \sum_{z \in \mathcal{Y}} \pi(z|[x, y_t]) V^\star([x, y_t, z]) - V^\star([x, y_t])$$

**What it computes:** the expected one-step improvement in value when sampling the next token from policy `$\pi$` rather than from the base policy. If `$\pi$` puts more probability on tokens with high `$V^\star([x, y_t, z])$`, the advantage is positive—the policy is steering toward higher-reward completions. Under the base policy `$\pi = \pi_{\text{ref}}$`, the advantage is identically zero because `$\mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_{\text{ref}}}[V^\star([x, y_t, z])] = V^\star([x, y_t])$` by the law of total probability—the expected value of the next state, averaged over the base policy's next-token distribution, equals the current value.

**Why this form:** the advantage zeroing out under `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$` is the property that makes this a meaningful optimization target. It says that any deviation from `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$` that increases advantage is genuinely steering toward higher reward, not just reflecting the base model's existing tendencies. The optimization problem is to find a policy with positive advantage while keeping the distribution close to `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$`.

The **KL-regularized RL objective** at each decoding step trades off advantage against divergence from the base policy:

$$J_\lambda([x, y_t]; \pi) := \lambda A([x, y_t]; \pi) - D([x, y_t]; \pi)$$

where `$\lambda \in \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}$` is a scalar hyperparameter controlling the tradeoff, and `$D([x, y_t]; \pi)$` is the tokenwise KL divergence:

$$D([x, y_t]; \pi) := \text{KL}(\pi(\cdot|[x, y_t]) \| \pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot|[x, y_t])) = \sum_{z \in \mathcal{Y}} \pi(z|[x, y_t]) \log\left(\frac{\pi(z|[x, y_t])}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(z|[x, y_t])}\right)$$

**What it computes:** a single scalar objective per decoding step. The first term `$\lambda A$` rewards the policy for steering toward high-value next tokens (weighted by `$\lambda$`). The second term penalizes the policy for deviating from the base model's next-token distribution, measured in KL divergence. The result is that the optimal policy at each step will be a compromise: shift probability toward better tokens, but not so aggressively that the model stops producing fluent text.

**Why this form:** the objective is concave in `$\pi$` because `$A$` is linear in `$\pi$` (it's an expectation over `$\pi$`) and `$D$` is strictly convex in `$\pi$` (KL divergence is convex in its first argument). Concavity guarantees a unique global maximum—there is exactly one best policy for each `$[x, y_t]$` and `$\lambda$`, and we can find it in closed form.

**The optimal policy** is stated in Theorem 2.1:

$$\pi^\star_\lambda(z|[x, y_t]) \propto \pi_{\text{ref}}(z|[x, y_t]) e^{\lambda V^\star([x, y_t, z])}$$

**What it computes:** the aligned next-token distribution. For each possible next token `$z$`, start with the base model's probability `$\pi_{\text{ref}}(z|[x, y_t])$`, multiply it by `$e^{\lambda V^\star([x, y_t, z])}$` (exponentiated value of the resulting prefix), then renormalize so the distribution sums to one. Tokens that lead to higher-value completions get upweighted; tokens that lead to lower-value completions get downweighted. The parameter `$\lambda$` controls the temperature of this reweighting: `$\lambda = 0$` recovers the base policy exactly, and as `$\lambda \to \infty$`, the policy becomes deterministic, always selecting the token with the maximum value.

**Why this form:** the derivation (shown in the proof of Theorem 2.1, Appendix C) rewrites the objective as `$J_\lambda = -\text{KL}(\pi \| q_\lambda) + \log Z_\lambda$` where `$q_\lambda(z) \propto \pi_{\text{ref}}(z) e^{\lambda V^\star([x, y_t, z])}$`. Since KL divergence is minimized when the two distributions are equal, the optimal policy is exactly `$q_\lambda$`. This is not an approximation—it is the exact, unique maximizer of the tokenwise RL objective. The exponential form comes from the KL divergence penalty: minimizing KL subject to a linear constraint on expected value always yields a Gibbs distribution (exponential family). This is the same mathematical structure behind maximum entropy RL and Bayesian inference formulations, but applied tokenwise in autoregressive decoding. The practical upshot is profound: if you can learn `$V^\star$`, you can implement the optimal aligned policy at inference time without ever training the base model, simply by reweighting logits.

This result differs from the sequence-level version in Korbak et al. (2022) in that it operates token-by-token. The sequence-level result reweights complete sequences; this tokenwise result reweights individual next-token probabilities, enabling autoregressive controlled generation rather than post-hoc reranking of full responses.

#### The Value Function as the Central Learning Target

Given the optimal policy formula, the entire problem reduces to learning `$V^\star$`. The value function `$V^\star([x, y_t])$` is defined for every prefix—every partial response at every intermediate step—and must capture the expected future reward under the base model's own sampling distribution. There is no ground-truth training signal for arbitrary prefixes because we typically only have reward annotations for fully completed responses.

The paper trains a neural network `$V_\theta$` parameterized by `$\theta$` to approximate `$V^\star$` by minimizing the mean squared error over all prefixes of all training responses:

$$L^\star(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mu} \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot|x)} \left[\frac{1}{2} \sum_{t \in [|y|]} \left(V_\theta([x, y_t]) - V^\star([x, y_t])\right)^2\right]$$

where `$\mu$` is a distribution over training prompts, `$y \sim \pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot|x)$` means the response is sampled from the base model (on-policy), `$|y|$` is the length of the response, and the sum runs over all prefix positions `$t$` from 1 to `$|y|$`. The factor `$\frac{1}{2}$` is for notational convenience in gradient derivations and does not affect the argmin.

**What it computes:** the expected squared difference between the prefix scorer's prediction and the true value function, averaged over prompts drawn from the training distribution `$\mu$`, responses drawn from the base model, and all prefix positions within each response. The result is a non-negative scalar loss.

**Why this form:** MSE is the standard regression loss for real-valued targets and has the convenient property that its gradient is linear in the error: `$\nabla_\theta L^\star = \mathbb{E}[(V_\theta - V^\star) \nabla_\theta V_\theta]$`. More importantly, the expectation is over base model rollouts because the value function is defined as the expected reward under the base model's policy. Training on prefixes from some other distribution (e.g., human-written responses) would learn a different function—the expected reward of human completions, not base model completions. The paper emphasizes this by defining `$L^\star$` with `$y \sim \pi_{\text{ref}}$`, and the CD-FUDGE training procedure operationalizes this by explicitly rolling out the base model.

**The key challenge:** `$V^\star$` is unknown—it is the very thing we are trying to learn. So `$L^\star$` cannot be directly optimized. The two training methods (CD-FUDGE and CD-Q) differ in how they construct proxy targets that approximate `$V^\star$` without requiring it to be known in advance.

#### CD-FUDGE: On-Policy Value Regression

CD-FUDGE follows the approach originally proposed by Yang & Klein (2021) but with the new theoretical interpretation as value function learning. The training procedure is:

1. For each training prompt `$x$`, sample a complete response `$y = [y_1, \ldots, y_T]$` autoregressively from the frozen base model `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$`.
2. Compute the scalar reward `$r([x, y])$` for the completed response using the reward model (or direct computation, for length).
3. For every prefix `$y_t$` of `$y$` (every position from 1 to `$T$`), use `$r([x, y])$` as the training target for `$V_\theta([x, y_t])$`.

The loss function is:

$$L_F(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mu} \left[\frac{1}{2} \sum_{t \in [|y|]} \left(V_\theta([x, y_t]) - r([x, y])\right)^2\right], \quad y \sim \pi_{\text{ref}}$$

where `$y \sim \pi_{\text{ref}}$` indicates the response is sampled on-policy from the base model (note: the paper writes "s.t. `$y \sim \pi_{\text{ref}}$`" as a constraint notation).

**What it computes:** for each prefix `$y_t$` in a base-model-generated response, the loss is the squared difference between the prefix scorer's prediction at that prefix and the *actual* reward of the completed response that the base model happened to produce. The loss is summed over all prefix positions and averaged over prompts. The result is a scalar that drives `$V_\theta([x, y_t])$` toward whatever reward was ultimately achieved by the full response `$y$`.

**Why this form works (Theorem 3.1, informal):** although `$r([x, y])$` is a noisy, single-sample estimate of `$V^\star([x, y_t])$` (it is the reward of one particular completion, not the expected reward over all possible completions), stochastic gradient descent on `$L_F$` is an unbiased estimator of the gradient of `$L^\star$`. The proof (Appendix C, Lemma C.1) shows:

$$\mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_{\text{ref}}}[\nabla_\theta L_F(\theta)] = \nabla_\theta L^\star(\theta)$$

The key step in the proof is that when taking the expectation over `$y$`, the cross-term `$\mathbb{E}[V_\theta([x, y_t]) r([x, y])]$` becomes `$\mathbb{E}[V_\theta([x, y_t]) V^\star([x, y_t])]$` by the law of total expectation—the expected reward of the full response, conditioned on the prefix `$y_t$`, is exactly `$V^\star([x, y_t])$`. The variance term `$\mathbb{E}[r([x, y])^2]$` is constant with respect to `$\theta$`, so it does not affect the gradient. Thus, despite training with a single noisy reward per prefix, the expected gradient points in the same direction as if we had access to the true `$V^\star$`.

**Why this is remarkable:** CD-FUDGE uses the *actual realized reward* of one specific completion as a proxy for the *expected reward* over all possible completions. This is an extremely high-variance estimate—the reward of a single completion can be very different from the expected reward of the prefix, especially early in the sequence. Yet, on average, over many training examples, the noise cancels and the model converges to the true value function (under regularity conditions such as the Polyak-Łojasiewicz inequality, as stated in Theorem C.2). This is possible because the responses are sampled from `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$` itself—if they were from a different distribution, the expectation would not match `$V^\star$`.

**Practical implication:** training CD-FUDGE requires rolling out the base model on all training prompts to generate the training data. This is computationally expensive but ensures the distributional match needed for unbiased gradient estimates. The paper acknowledges this cost—for large models or large datasets, on-policy rollouts may be prohibitive. This motivates the off-policy CD-Q alternative.

**Why CD-FUDGE underperforms in blockwise mode:** the paper's analysis (Experiment 1, Figure 13 in Appendix B) reveals that CD-FUDGE's value predictions are "much noisier than that of CD-Q" when used to rank fully decoded responses. This noise is a direct consequence of the high-variance training signal: each prefix is trained toward the reward of a single completion, which may be unrepresentative. When used tokenwise, the noise averages out over many steps and the softmax normalization in the sampling step. When used blockwise—where the scorer must reliably rank `$K$` candidate blocks and select the best one—the noise causes ranking errors that degrade performance. CD-Q, by bootstrapping from expected values, produces smoother and more reliable value estimates suitable for blockwise selection.

#### CD-Q: Off-Policy Temporal-Difference Learning

CD-Q is the paper's novel contribution for training the prefix scorer. It is inspired by the DQN algorithm (Mnih et al., 2013) from deep reinforcement learning, adapted to the autoregressive language generation setting. The key insight is that `$V^\star$` satisfies a Bellman equation that relates the value of a prefix to the expected value of its one-token extensions:

For any prefix `$[x, y_t]$` where `$y_t \neq \text{EOS}$`:

$$V^\star([x, y_t]) = \mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot|[x, y_t])}[V^\star([x, y_t, z])]$$

For a completed sequence where `$y_t = \text{EOS}$`:

$$V^\star([x, y_t]) = r([x, y_t])$$

The first equation says: the expected future reward from the current prefix equals the expected value of the next state, averaged over the base model's next-token distribution. The second equation is the base case: when the sequence is done, the value is just the observed reward.

**What these equations represent:** a consistency condition that the true value function must satisfy. If you have a candidate value function and it violates these equations, it is not the true `$V^\star$`. The Bellman equation decomposes the long-horizon prediction problem into a one-step prediction problem: instead of trying to predict the reward of an arbitrarily long future completion, you only need to predict the value one step ahead, which is a simpler target.

CD-Q trains `$V_\theta$` to satisfy these Bellman equations by minimizing the squared difference between the model's prediction at a prefix and the **Bellman target** computed from the model's own predictions at the next-step extensions:

$$L_Q(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mu} \left[\frac{1}{2} \sum_{t \in [|y|]} \left(V_\theta([x, y_t]) - \dot{v}_t\right)^2\right]$$

where the target `$\dot{v}_t$` (the dot indicates a stop-gradient) is:

$$v_t = \begin{cases} \sum_{z \in \mathcal{Y}} \pi_{\text{ref}}(z|[x, y_t]) V_\theta([x, y_t, z]) & y_t \neq \text{EOS} \\ r([x, y_t]) & y_t = \text{EOS} \end{cases}$$

and `$\dot{v}_t$` means the gradient is not propagated through `$v_t$` even though `$v_t$` depends on `$\theta$` (through `$V_\theta([x, y_t, z])$` in the non-EOS case).

**What it computes:** for each prefix in the training data, if the prefix is not complete, the target is the **expected value of the next token** under the base model's distribution—computed by evaluating `$V_\theta$` on every possible one-token extension `$[x, y_t, z]$` for every `$z \in \mathcal{Y}$`, weighting each by `$\pi_{\text{ref}}(z|[x, y_t])$` (the base model's probability for that token), and summing. If the prefix is complete (ends in EOS), the target is simply the observed reward `$r([x, y_t])$`. The loss is the squared difference between the model's prediction at the prefix and this computed target. The stop-gradient is critical: the target is treated as a constant during backpropagation, so the gradient only flows through `$V_\theta([x, y_t])$` (the prediction being trained), not through the target computation.

**Why this form:** the Bellman target serves as a bootstrap estimate—it uses the model's own predictions at the next step as a proxy for the true value. This is the core idea of temporal-difference learning: rather than waiting until the end of the sequence to see the actual reward (as CD-FUDGE does), update the current prediction toward a one-step lookahead that itself uses learned values. The stop-gradient prevents the training from collapsing into a trivial solution where `$V_\theta$` predicts a constant (which would also satisfy the Bellman equation if the constant equals the constant reward). Without the stop-gradient, minimizing the squared Bellman error is not a proper gradient descent procedure because the target moves as `$\theta$` changes (it's a moving target problem). The stop-gradient converts the optimization into a series of supervised learning problems where the targets are temporarily fixed.

**The sum over the entire vocabulary** (`$\sum_{z \in \mathcal{Y}}$`) is worth emphasizing. For each training prefix, CD-Q requires computing `$V_\theta([x, y_t, z])$` for every token `$z$` in the vocabulary (tens of thousands of evaluations), weighted by the base model's token probabilities. This is computationally expensive—it requires one forward pass of the prefix scorer per vocabulary token per training prefix. In practice, this can be approximated by sampling a subset of tokens or by computing the values efficiently through batched operations, but the paper does not detail these implementation considerations. The high cost is the price of the Bellman target's lower variance compared to CD-FUDGE's Monte Carlo target: you get better, smoother value estimates, but you pay for them in computation.

**Key differences from CD-FUDGE:**

1. **Data source:** CD-Q can be trained on any off-policy dataset of `$(x, y)$` pairs—it does not require the responses to be sampled from `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$`. The training data can be human-written conversations, existing corpora, or responses from other models. However, the Bellman target still uses `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$`'s token probabilities `$\pi_{\text{ref}}(z|[x, y_t])$` to weight the next-step values. This means CD-Q needs on-demand access to the base model for computing these probabilities, even if the training data itself is off-policy. This is a crucial hybrid: the *trajectories* can be off-policy, but the *expectation* over next tokens is always with respect to the base model's policy. This ensures the learned value function is `$V^\star_{\pi_{\text{ref}}}$` (the value under `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$`), not the value under whatever policy generated the data.

2. **Target signal:** CD-Q's target `$v_t$` is an *expected value* (averaged over all possible next tokens), whereas CD-FUDGE's target is a *single realized reward*. This means CD-Q's targets have lower variance—they don't depend on the randomness of any particular completion, only on the accuracy of `$V_\theta$`'s predictions at the next step. Lower variance typically leads to faster and more stable convergence, which the empirical results (Figures 3-5, 13) confirm.

3. **Bootstrapping:** CD-Q uses its own predictions at the next step to construct the target for the current step. This creates a potential for error propagation (if `$V_\theta$` is wrong at step `$t+1$`, the target for step `$t$` is also wrong), but it also means the model can learn from incomplete trajectories and does not need to wait until the end of a sequence to compute targets. In the DQN literature, this is stabilized by techniques like target networks (a slowly-updated copy of the value network used for computing targets) and experience replay. The paper's CD-Q uses the simpler form (no target network) and acknowledges that improvements from Rainbow (Hessel et al., 2018) could be applied, but leaves this to future work.

4. **Provable convergence:** the paper cites Wang & Ueda (2022) for a proof that a simple modification of this procedure converges. The exact modification is not specified in the paper but likely involves using a target network or double Q-learning to reduce the bias from bootstrapping. The paper states that "many other improvements over DQN have been proposed over the years, many of which amount to Rainbow (Hessel et al., 2018). Exploring how to improve CD-Q using these techniques is an interesting area for future work."

**The relationship to DQN:** in standard DQN, the Q-function `$Q(s, a)$` predicts the expected future reward from state `$s$` after taking action `$a$`, and the Bellman target is `$r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(s', a')$`. In CD-Q, the value function `$V^\star([x, y_t])$` predicts the expected future reward from prefix `$[x, y_t]$` under the base policy (no action choice—the policy is fixed to `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$`). The Bellman target is `$\mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_{\text{ref}}}[V^\star([x, y_t, z])]$`, which is the *expected* next value, not the *maximum* next value. This difference is fundamental: CD-Q evaluates a fixed policy; DQN learns an optimal policy. CD-Q is doing **policy evaluation**, not policy improvement. The optimization happens at inference time through the `$\lambda$`-parameterized reweighting (Theorem 2.1), not during training.

#### Inference-Time Sampling Strategy 1: Tokenwise CD

Tokenwise CD directly implements the optimal policy from Theorem 2.1. At each decoding step, given the current prefix `$[x, y_t]$`:

1. Query the base model for the next-token log-probabilities `$\log \pi_{\text{ref}}(z|[x, y_t])$` for all `$z \in \mathcal{Y}$`.
2. Query the prefix scorer for the values `$V_\theta([x, y_t, z])$` for all `$z \in \mathcal{Y}$`.
3. Compute the aligned logits: `$\log \pi_{\text{ref}}(z|[x, y_t]) + \lambda V_\theta([x, y_t, z])$` for each `$z$`.
4. Apply softmax to obtain the aligned probability distribution.
5. Sample the next token `$z$` from this distribution.
6. Append `$z$` to the prefix and repeat until EOS is generated.

**Computational cost:** one forward pass of the base model (to get token logits) and one forward pass of the prefix scorer per generated token. The prefix scorer must be evaluated for every token in the vocabulary (or a sampled subset) at each step. This is significantly more expensive than standard autoregressive decoding (which only queries the base model) but far cheaper than best-of-K (which requires `$K$` complete forward passes through the base model for the full sequence).

**The role of `$\lambda$`:** this scalar hyperparameter controls the strength of the alignment. At `$\lambda = 0$`, the aligned distribution equals the base distribution exactly—no control is applied. As `$\lambda$` increases, tokens with high prefix scores are increasingly favored, eventually approaching deterministic selection of the highest-scoring token. The paper sweeps `$\lambda$` to trace out reward-KL tradeoff curves: each `$\lambda$` value corresponds to one point on the curve (a particular expected reward and KL divergence from the base model). Higher `$\lambda$` gives higher reward but also higher KL divergence.

**Why tokenwise CD underperforms in practice** (Figures 4, 5): despite being the theoretically optimal solution to the tokenwise RL objective, tokenwise CD is not the best method for achieving high sequence-level rewards. The paper observes that "tokenwise controllers don't offer much HH improvement over baselines." The likely explanation, though not fully analyzed, is that greedy tokenwise optimization can be myopic—the prefix scorer's single-step lookahead may not capture the complex long-range dependencies that matter for sequence-level quality. A prefix that looks good after one more token may lead to a dead end that the scorer cannot anticipate. Blockwise CD addresses this by evaluating longer continuations (blocks of `$M$` tokens) before committing.

#### Inference-Time Sampling Strategy 2: Blockwise CD (Best-of-K per Block)

Blockwise CD is the paper's practical contribution for navigating the latency-quality tradeoff. Instead of reranking individual tokens or complete sequences, it reranks **blocks** of `$M$` tokens:

1. Starting from the current prefix `$[x, y_t]$`, sample `$K$` independent continuations of exactly `$M$` tokens from the base model `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$`. Each continuation is a block `$z^M_{(k)} = [z_1^{(k)}, \ldots, z_M^{(k)}]$` for `$k = 1, \ldots, K$`.
2. For each completed block, concatenate it with the current prefix to form `$[x, y_t, z^M_{(k)}]$` and evaluate the prefix scorer `$V_\theta([x, y_t, z^M_{(k)}])$`.
3. Select the block `$k^\star$` with the highest prefix scorer value:

$$z^M := \arg\max_{\{z^M_{(k)}\}_{k \in [K]}} V_\theta([x, y_t, z^M_{(k)}])$$

4. Accept this block as the continuation: set `$y_{t+M} = [y_t, z^M]$`.
5. If any of the tokens in the selected block is EOS, truncate at that token and stop decoding. Otherwise, repeat from step 1 with the extended prefix.

**What this algorithm does:** it decomposes the long-horizon decoding problem into a sequence of shorter-horizon best-of-K decisions. At each block boundary, the system explores `$K$` alternative continuations of length `$M$`, scores each with the prefix scorer, and commits to the best one before moving on. The process is greedy within each block (standard autoregressive sampling) but selects among blocks at boundaries.

**Computational cost:** each block decision requires `$K$` forward passes through the base model (one per candidate block), each generating `$M$` tokens autoregressively. The prefix scorer is evaluated once per candidate block (at the block's endpoint), not per token within the block. For a response of total length `$L$`, there are approximately `$L/M$` block decisions (assuming no early EOS), so the total cost is approximately `$K \times L$` base model forward passes for generation plus `$K \times (L/M)$` prefix scorer evaluations. This is similar to best-of-K in total FLOPs (both require `$K$` independent decodings of the full sequence) but with a crucial latency advantage: the system can start outputting tokens after only `$M$` tokens of generation (the first block decision), whereas best-of-K must complete all `$K$` sequences of length `$L$` before returning any output.

**Latency advantage over best-of-K:** for long-form generation where `$L \gg M$`, blockwise CD reduces the time-to-first-token (or more precisely, time-to-first-block) from `$O(K \cdot L)$` to `$O(K \cdot M)$`. With `$M = 4$` and `$L = 1000$`, this is a 250× reduction in latency. The paper emphasizes this enables **streaming** applications where the user starts receiving output immediately, which best-of-K fundamentally cannot support because it doesn't know which of the `$K$` sequences to stream until they are all complete.

**The relationship to best-of-K:** blockwise CD can be seen as applying best-of-K repeatedly at the block level rather than once at the sequence level. Best-of-K selects among `$K$` complete responses; blockwise CD makes `$L/M$` selections among `$K$` blocks each. The overall search space is larger (it explores combinations of blocks from different candidates) but the selection is greedy—the system commits to a block at each stage and cannot later revise that decision. This means blockwise CD is not guaranteed to find the globally highest-scoring complete response (which best-of-K does), but in practice it achieves similar reward levels with far lower latency.

**Why blockwise outperforms tokenwise:** tokenwise CD evaluates the prefix scorer one token ahead. Blockwise CD evaluates it `$M$` tokens ahead. The longer lookahead means the scorer can capture more of the long-range consequences of a decision. For example, a prefix scorer might not detect that a particular token choice leads to an ungrammatical or unhelpful continuation until several tokens later. Tokenwise CD, with its one-step horizon, cannot anticipate this; blockwise CD, by scoring the full block, can. This is a classic horizon effect: deeper search (longer blocks) provides more accurate value estimates at the cost of more computation per decision.

**Block size `$M$` and candidate count `$K$` tradeoffs (Figure 9):** the paper finds that larger block sizes generally lead to better reward-KL tradeoffs. This makes sense: scoring longer blocks provides more accurate assessments of their quality, reducing ranking errors. However, larger `$M$` also increases the latency per block decision (it takes longer to generate `$M$` tokens than 1 token). The paper sweeps `$M$` values but does not deeply analyze the latency-quality Pareto frontier beyond noting the trend. The maximum block size tested is 32, beyond which "the efficiency gains against best-of-K would evaporate."

**The prefix scorer's role in blockwise mode:** unlike tokenwise CD, where the prefix scorer directly shapes the per-token distribution, in blockwise CD the prefix scorer acts purely as a **reranker**—it scores already-generated blocks and selects the best one. The generation within each block uses the base model's unmodified sampling distribution. This means the blockwise CD policy is not directly implementing the optimal RL policy from Theorem 2.1. Instead, it is an approximation that captures the spirit of the optimal policy (favoring higher-value continuations) while being more computationally practical. The paper is explicit about this: "blockwise CD is not designed to optimally solve the sequence level KL-regularized objective that is the objective of RLHF methods." It is an empirically motivated heuristic that happens to work very well.

#### KL Divergence Bounds for Blockwise CD

To evaluate blockwise CD on the reward-KL tradeoff framework, the paper needs to measure how far the blockwise CD policy `$\pi_{\text{BCD}}$` diverges from the base policy `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$`. Computing the exact KL divergence would require the probability of each generated sequence under both policies, which is intractable for the blockwise sampling procedure. Instead, the paper derives an upper bound:

$$\text{KL}(\pi_{\text{BCD}} \| \pi_{\text{ref}}) \leq \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mu}\left[(\log K - (K-1)/K) \cdot \left\lceil \frac{L_x}{M} \right\rceil\right]$$

where `$L_x$` is the number of decoded tokens in the full response given prompt `$x$`, `$M$` is the block size, `$K$` is the number of candidate blocks, and `$\lceil L_x / M \rceil$` is the number of block decisions made during decoding (rounded up to account for the final, possibly shorter block).

**What it computes:** an upper bound on the KL divergence between the blockwise CD policy and the base policy. The term `$\log K - (K-1)/K$` is the KL divergence of the best-of-K policy for a single selection among `$K$` i.i.d. samples (this is a known result from Stiennon et al., 2020; Beirami et al., 2024). Since blockwise CD makes approximately `$L_x / M$` independent best-of-K selections (one per block), the total KL divergence is bounded by the per-selection KL times the number of selections.

**Why this form:** the bound relies on the data processing inequality and the fact that each block decision is conditionally independent of earlier blocks given the prefix. Each block decision is a best-of-K selection from `$K$` i.i.d. samples from the base model. The KL divergence of a single best-of-K selection from the base distribution is bounded by `$\log K - (K-1)/K$` (this is a tight bound in the limit of large `$K$`). Since the decisions are made sequentially and the KL divergence is additive over independent draws, the total KL is bounded by the sum of the per-decision KLs. The ceiling `$\lceil L_x / M \rceil$` accounts for the fact that the last block may be shorter than `$M$` tokens.

**Relationship to the reward-KL tradeoff curves:** the paper uses this upper bound as the x-axis in all blockwise CD reward-KL plots (Figures 3-12). Sweeping `$K$` (the number of candidate blocks) changes the bound: larger `$K$` increases both the expected reward (more candidates to choose from) and the KL bound (more aggressive deviation from the base model). This allows blockwise CD to be plotted on the same axes as tokenwise CD (where `$\lambda$` is swept) and training-time methods (where the KL regularizer strength is swept).

**Why it's an upper bound, not an exact value:** the exact KL divergence would depend on the specific responses generated and the block selections made, and would be lower than the bound because the bound assumes worst-case information leakage (that the selection among `$K$` candidates reveals `$\log K$` bits of information about which candidate was the base model's sample). In practice, the effective KL is often lower, meaning the blockwise CD curves in the figures may actually be somewhat better than they appear—the true KL for a given reward could be to the left of the plotted point, placing blockwise CD even further ahead on the Pareto frontier.

**The relationship to best-of-K's KL bound:** for best-of-K, the KL bound is `$\log K - (K-1)/K$` for the entire sequence (one selection per prompt). For blockwise CD, the bound is multiplied by `$\lceil L_x / M \rceil$` because there are multiple selections per prompt. For the same `$K$`, blockwise CD has higher KL divergence than best-of-K because it makes more selections (and thus deviates more from the base model). However, blockwise CD can achieve similar rewards with much smaller `$K$` (Experiment 1: `$K=6$` blockwise matches `$K=50$` best-of-K), so the effective comparison is not at the same `$K$` but at the same reward level, where blockwise CD's KL is often comparable or lower.

#### Training and Evaluation Protocol

**Training data construction:**
- **For CD-FUDGE:** The base model `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$` (PaLM 2-XXS) is rolled out on training prompts to generate responses. For each response, the reward model computes `$r([x, y])$`. The training dataset consists of `$(x, y, r([x, y]))$` triples where `$y$` is on-policy. The prefix scorer is trained on all prefixes of each response with target `$r([x, y])$`.
- **For CD-Q:** An off-policy dataset of `$(x, y, r([x, y]))$` triples is used. For length experiments, the Reddit conversations corpus provides the `$(x, y)$` pairs. For HH experiments, the training data also comes from the Reddit conversations corpus but with the HH reward function applied. For summarization, the TL;DR dataset provides the training examples. At each training step, for each prefix `$y_t$` in each response, the Bellman target is computed by querying the base model for `$\pi_{\text{ref}}(z|[x, y_t])$` and the current prefix scorer for `$V_\theta([x, y_t, z])$`. The prefix scorer is updated via gradient descent on the MSE between its prediction and the Bellman target (with stop-gradient).

**Training details (Section 4.5):**
- **For length experiments:** Both CD-FUDGE and CD-Q are trained on the Reddit conversations corpus using PaLM 2-XXS as the base model and as the initialization for the prefix scorer. Training runs for "half an epoch" on the training set. The exact optimizer, learning rate, and batch size are not specified in the main text—they may be in supplementary material.
- **For HH experiments:** The prefix scorers are trained using PaLM 2-XXS, also initialized from the same model. Training is performed for "one epoch" on the Reddit conversations corpus with HH prompts. The reward model is a separate PaLM 2-XXS fine-tuned on Anthropic HH pairwise preference data using the Bradley-Terry model: "learning rate of 1e-4, trained for 1 epoch, selected the checkpoint with the highest eval accuracy" (Appendix A). For DPO and IPO, "several training runs" are performed by sweeping KL regularizer hyperparameters and learning rates to achieve comparable KL divergence ranges.
- **For summarization:** The prefix scorer is trained on PaLM 2-XXS initialized from the base model. The reward model is "PaLM 2-XXS trained on TL;DR preferences using BT model, learning rate 1e-5, 1 epoch, highest eval accuracy checkpoint" (Appendix A).

**Hyperparameter sweeps for different methods:**
- **Tokenwise CD:** `$\lambda$` is swept to control the strength of the prefix scorer's influence, tracing out the reward-KL tradeoff curve.
- **Best-of-K:** `$K$` is swept (powers of 2 typically), with the KL bound `$\log K - (K-1)/K$` used for the x-axis.
- **Blockwise CD:** `$K$` is swept, with the KL bound `$\mathbb{E}[(\log K - (K-1)/K) \cdot \lceil L_x / M \rceil]$` used for the x-axis. The block size `$M$` is typically fixed (e.g., `$M = 32$` for HH experiments; the length experiment uses unspecified `$M$` but sweeps it in Experiment 6).
- **PPO:** The strength of the KL regularizer in the PPO objective is swept to achieve different points on the reward-KL curve.
- **DPO and IPO:** The effective KL regularization (controlled through hyperparameters like `$\beta$` in the DPO loss) is swept to achieve different KL divergences.

**Evaluation metrics (Section 4.4):**

1. **KL divergence** between the aligned policy and the base policy: `$\mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mu} \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi(\cdot|x)}[\log \pi(y|x) - \log \pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)]$`. For best-of-K and blockwise CD, the respective upper bounds are used as proxies. The paper focuses on "KL values smaller than 10, beyond which the policy shows significant signs of overfitting" (citing Eisenstein et al., 2023).

2. **Normalized expected reward:** `$\mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mu} \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot|x)} r(x, y)$`, normalized by the expected reward of the base policy. This measures absolute reward improvement but is reward-model-specific.

3. **Win-rate against base policy:** `$\mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mu} \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot|x)} \mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot|x)} \mathbb{1}[r(x, y) > r(x, z)]$`. For each prompt, the aligned policy generates one response, the base policy generates one response, and the reward model judges which is better. The win-rate is the fraction of comparisons where the aligned response wins. This metric is more robust than absolute reward because it only relies on the reward model's relative rankings, not its absolute calibration.

4. **Reward-KL tradeoff curves:** the primary evaluation framework. Points further up (higher reward/win-rate) and further left (lower KL divergence) are better. A method that dominates another (achieves higher reward at the same KL, or lower KL at the same reward) is strictly superior.

**LLM-as-judge evaluation (Appendix A):** for HH and summarization experiments, the win-rate is computed using a prompted large language model (PaLM 2-L, "Unicorn") as the evaluator rather than the reward model itself. This avoids circular evaluation (using the same reward model that trained the prefix scorer to also evaluate it). The zeroshot prompts instruct PaLM 2-L to rank two assistant responses based on helpfulness, politeness, honesty, sophistication, emotional awareness, and humility. The prompts are provided verbatim in Appendix A.

**Cross-validation for checkpoint selection:** For reward model training (both HH and summarization), the checkpoint with the highest accuracy on the evaluation set is selected. For DPO, IPO, and PPO, multiple training runs with different hyperparameters are performed, and the results at comparable KL values are compared.

**Why the paper emphasizes sequence-level KL divergence in evaluation:** the tokenwise RL objective optimizes a token-level KL penalty. However, the evaluation uses sequence-level KL divergence `$\text{KL}(\pi \| \pi_{\text{ref}})$` measured over entire responses. The paper notes that this "makes the evaluation more favorable to PPO, DPO, and IPO that directly optimize the tradeoff between expected reward and sequence-level KL divergence" (Section 4.4). Despite this evaluation bias against CD, blockwise CD-Q still matches or exceeds these training-time methods, strengthening the empirical claims.

#### Design Choices and Their Justifications

**Why a frozen base model plus a separate prefix scorer instead of fine-tuning the base model?** The paper's motivation is modularity and configurability (Section 1). A frozen base model with swappable prefix scorers enables: (a) different reward functions to be applied to the same base model without retraining (multi-objective control, Experiment 4), (b) the prefix scorer to be transferred to a different base model (Experiments 5-7), and (c) the alignment strength `$\lambda$` to be adjusted dynamically at inference time. Fine-tuning bakes the alignment into the model weights, making all of these impossible.

**Why two training methods (CD-FUDGE and CD-Q) instead of one?** Each has different practical tradeoffs. CD-FUDGE is simpler to implement (no Bellman computation, no stop-gradient) but requires expensive on-policy rollouts and produces noisier value estimates. CD-Q is more complex but supports off-policy data and produces smoother values suitable for blockwise reranking. The paper presents both to demonstrate that the prefix-scorer-as-value-function framework is robust to the specific training algorithm, and to characterize the tradeoffs.

**Why the Bellman target uses `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$`'s token probabilities even for off-policy data?** The value function is defined with respect to `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$`—it predicts expected reward when the base model completes the sequence. If the Bellman target used the data-generating policy's probabilities instead, the learned function would be the value function of that policy, not `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$`. The paper needs `$V^\star_{\pi_{\text{ref}}}$` because the optimal aligned policy (Theorem 2.1) reweights `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$`'s token probabilities by `$e^{\lambda V^\star}$`. If `$V^\star$` were computed under a different policy, the reweighting would not yield the correct aligned distribution.

**Why blockwise CD uses best-of-K per block rather than some softer reweighting?** The hard selection (argmax over blocks) is computationally simple and empirically effective. A softer approach—e.g., reweighting block probabilities by their prefix scores and sampling from the reweighted distribution—would better approximate the optimal policy but would be equivalent to tokenwise CD with a coarser time resolution. The hard selection has the practical advantage of producing exactly one continuation per block, which can be streamed immediately. The paper does not theoretically justify the hard selection over soft alternatives; it is an empirical design choice validated by experiments.

**Why the KL bound formula is used instead of estimating KL from samples?** Estimating KL divergence from samples requires knowing the probability of each generated sequence under both policies, which is intractable for blockwise CD (it involves integrating over all possible block selections). The upper bound provides a conservative estimate—the true KL is lower, so any reward-KL advantage shown using the bound is even stronger in reality. The bound is also analytically simple, making it easy to compute and compare across methods.

**Why PaLM 2-XXS for both base model and prefix scorer?** Using the same model family ensures architectural compatibility and simplifies implementation. The prefix scorer is initialized from PaLM 2-XXS and fine-tuned for the value prediction task, which means it inherits the language understanding capabilities of the base model. This is important because the prefix scorer must understand partial sequences to predict their future reward—a randomly initialized network would need to learn language from scratch. The transfer experiments (using prefix scorers trained on PaLM 2-XXS to control PaLM 2-S and PaLM 2-XS) test whether this approach overfits to the specific model or generalizes across model sizes within the same family.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: The Prefix Scorer as a Bridge Between Inference-Time Control and KL-Regularized RL

The paper's most fundamental conceptual contribution is not any particular training algorithm or sampling strategy, but rather the **formal connection between the prefix scorers used in controlled generation and the value function of KL-regularized reinforcement learning**. Prior to this work, methods like FUDGE (Yang & Klein, 2021) trained prefix scorers as pragmatic discriminators—predicting, from a partial sequence, whether the final output would satisfy some constraint—and used Bayes' rule to adjust token probabilities at inference time. This worked empirically but lacked a principled justification: *why* should reweighting logits by a learned scorer correspond to any coherent optimization objective? And was the resulting policy optimal with respect to any well-defined tradeoff between reward and drift from the base model?

The paper answers both questions by situating prefix scoring within the standard alignment framework of KL-regularized RL. Theorem 2.1 shows that the optimal tokenwise policy takes the form `$\pi^\star_\lambda(z|[x, y_t]) \propto \pi_{\text{ref}}(z|[x, y_t]) e^{\lambda V^\star([x, y_t, z])}$`, where `$V^\star$` is the value function of the base model's own decoding policy—the expected future reward from any partially decoded sequence. Theorem 3.1 then shows that FUDGE's seemingly ad hoc training procedure (regressing each prefix to the final reward of an on-policy completion) is, in expectation, minimizing the MSE to the true value function `$V^\star$`. In other words, **FUDGE was always doing value function learning; it just didn't know it**.

This reframing matters because it transforms controlled generation from a collection of heuristic tricks into a principled solver for the standard alignment objective. It tells us *what* the prefix scorer should predict (the expected future reward under the base policy), *why* the exponential reweighting is correct (it is the exact maximizer of the tokenwise RL objective), and *how* to evaluate the resulting policy (via the reward-KL tradeoff curve, the same framework used for PPO, DPO, and best-of-K). The field had previously treated inference-time controlled generation and RL-based alignment as separate paradigms with different objectives. This paper shows they are solving the same problem, just with different computational architectures (frozen base model + learned value function vs. trained policy network).

The significance is both theoretical and practical. Theoretically, it closes a gap left by Korbak et al. (2022), who showed that KL-regularized RL could be viewed as Bayesian inference but did not extend this to autoregressive tokenwise decoding. The paper makes that extension explicit and operational. Practically, it means that improvements in value function learning (from the vast deep RL literature—Rainbow, distributional RL, target networks) can be directly ported to controlled generation, and that the prefix scorer can be evaluated using the same reward-KL tradeoff framework as any other alignment method, enabling fair comparisons. The paper itself only scratches the surface of this connection (the CD-Q training procedure is a minimal adaptation of DQN), but the door it opens is wide.

### Innovation 2: Blockwise Reranking as an Inference-Time Tradeoff Between Latency and Optimality

The paper's second distinctive contribution is the **blockwise CD sampling strategy** and its positioning as a practical mechanism that navigates the latency-quality frontier between tokenwise RL and sequence-level best-of-K. This is not presented as a theoretical breakthrough—the paper explicitly acknowledges blockwise CD "is not designed to optimally solve the sequence level KL-regularized objective" (Section 3.2)—but rather as an empirically motivated design choice that addresses a genuine deployment constraint the field had not systematically tackled.

The landscape before this work was polarized. On one end, **tokenwise controlled decoding** (FUDGE, GeDi, NADO) adjusted probabilities at every step, enabling streaming generation but yielding underwhelming reward-KL tradeoffs—the paper's own experiments confirm that "tokenwise controllers don't offer much HH improvement over baselines" (Section 5, Experiment 2). On the other end, **best-of-K** achieved the best reward-KL tradeoffs across the board (consistently outperforming PPO and DPO in prior work by Gao et al., 2023; Rafailov et al., 2023) but required decoding `$K$` complete sequences before any output could be served, imposing a latency penalty proportional to full sequence length. There was no middle ground—no method that preserved most of best-of-K's efficiency while enabling streaming output.

Blockwise CD fills this gap by decomposing the long-horizon best-of-K decision into a sequence of short-horizon (block-length `$M$`) best-of-K decisions. The key insight is that **reranking blocks of `$M$` tokens provides enough lookahead to capture the prefix scorer's long-range value signal** (unlike tokenwise CD's single-step myopia) while **committing to output after only `$M$` tokens of latency** (unlike best-of-K's full-sequence wait). The empirical results validate this: on length control (Figure 3), blockwise CD-Q with `$K=6$` achieves the same reward-KL tradeoff as best-of-K with `$K=50$`—an 8× reduction in the number of parallel decodings needed, and a corresponding latency reduction proportional to `$M/L$` for long sequences. On HH (Figure 4), the gap to best-of-K narrows substantially compared to tokenwise CD, though blockwise CD does not fully close it.

What makes this more than just an engineering trick is the **conceptual reframing of the latency-quality problem**. Prior work implicitly treated the choice between tokenwise and sequence-level control as a binary: either you control at every step (low latency, weaker alignment) or you control at the sequence level (high latency, strong alignment). Blockwise CD shows this is a **continuous spectrum parameterized by block size `$M$`**—`$M=1$` recovers tokenwise CD, `$M=L$` recovers best-of-K, and intermediate values achieve intermediate tradeoffs. The paper's analysis of how `$M$` affects the reward-KL curve (Figure 9: larger blocks give better tradeoffs) provides the first empirical characterization of this spectrum.

The practical implication is significant for production systems. Best-of-K is often dismissed as impractical for latency-sensitive applications (streaming chatbots, real-time translation). Blockwise CD offers a way to capture most of best-of-K's alignment benefit while meeting latency budgets, simply by tuning `$M$`. This is a genuinely new capability that neither prior inference-time methods nor training-time methods could provide. PPO and DPO bake alignment into the model weights and offer no latency-quality tunability at inference time; best-of-K offers only the extreme (full sequence) latency point. Blockwise CD is the first method that lets practitioners dial the tradeoff.

### Innovation 3: The Empirical Discovery That Value Function Quality Dominates Algorithmic Sophistication

A subtle but important insight emerges from the paper's comparison of CD-FUDGE and CD-Q, particularly in Appendix B (Figure 13): **the quality of the learned value function, not the inference-time sampling strategy, is the primary determinant of controlled decoding performance, especially in blockwise mode.**

Both CD-FUDGE and CD-Q aim to learn `$V^\star$`, but they differ in how: CD-FUDGE regresses each prefix to a single noisy reward realization (the outcome of one specific completion), while CD-Q regresses to a bootstrapped expected value (the average over all possible next tokens under `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$`, using the model's own predictions). Both are, in expectation, optimizing the same objective `$L^\star$` (Theorem 3.1 vs. the Bellman identity), but they produce qualitatively different learned functions. Appendix B (Figure 13) shows this starkly: when used to predict the final response length on a test set, CD-Q's predictions are tightly correlated with actual lengths, while CD-FUDGE's predictions are "much noisier." This noise is directly attributable to the high-variance training signal—each prefix is trained toward the reward of a randomly sampled completion, which may deviate substantially from the expected reward.

The downstream consequence is that **blockwise CD-FUDGE underperforms blockwise CD-Q dramatically** (Figure 3), even though both use identical inference-time algorithms. The blockwise selection mechanism relies on reliable *relative ranking* of candidate blocks—it must consistently identify which of `$K$` blocks has the highest true value. Even small amounts of prediction noise cause ranking errors that cascade into poor generation choices. Tokenwise CD-FUDGE, in contrast, performs comparably to tokenwise CD-Q (Figure 3)—the softmax over the vocabulary smooths out the noise, and errors in individual token scores are partially absorbed by the renormalization.

This finding has a clear implication for research prioritization: **improving value function learning (better training objectives, lower-variance targets, better architectures) is likely more impactful than developing more sophisticated inference-time sampling strategies for controlled decoding.** The paper's CD-Q is a minimal adaptation of DQN and already substantially outperforms CD-FUDGE in blockwise mode. The paper explicitly notes that improvements from Rainbow (Hessel et al., 2018)—distributional RL, prioritized experience replay, dueling networks, target networks—could be applied to further improve the value estimates. This connects controlled decoding to a rich and mature research literature that the field had not previously leveraged.

This insight also partially explains the inconsistent performance of prior controlled decoding methods. FUDGE (Yang & Klein, 2021) and related approaches have shown promising results on some tasks but unreliable performance on others. The paper's analysis suggests these inconsistencies may stem not from fundamental limitations of the prefix-scorer paradigm but from the high variance of on-policy rollout-based training, which produces noisy value estimates that work adequately for tokenwise reweighting but poorly for any application requiring reliable scoring (including blockwise selection or direct answer ranking). Replacing the FUDGE-style regression target with a Bellman target—even the minimal one used in CD-Q—substantially improves downstream performance without changing the inference algorithm.

### Innovation 4: Modularity as a First-Class Design Principle, Not an Afterthought

The paper's emphasis on **modularity, composability, and transferability** is not merely a set of nice-to-have properties but a core design contribution that distinguishes CD from both training-time alignment methods and prior inference-time approaches. The paper demonstrates three specific forms of modularity that collectively amount to a new deployment paradigm for aligned language models:

**Multi-reward composability (Experiment 4):** At inference time, a single frozen base model can be steered by combining multiple independently trained prefix scorers—in this case, a helpfulness/harmlessness scorer and a length scorer—with a simple linear combination of their values in the blockwise selection criterion. The system can then dynamically adjust the tradeoff between rewards (e.g., improve HH while keeping response length constant, or vice versa) by changing the linear weights at inference time, with no retraining. Training-time methods (PPO, DPO, IPO) fundamentally cannot do this—each reward combination requires a separate training run, and the resulting model is locked to that specific tradeoff. Prior inference-time methods like FUDGE had the architectural capability for this (separate scorers) but had never demonstrated it empirically, nor had they shown that combining scorers produces coherent, controllable behavior.

**Cross-model transferability (Experiments 5, 6, 7):** A prefix scorer trained on PaLM 2-XXS (Gecko) transfers effectively to PaLM 2-S (Bison) and PaLM 2-XS (Otter) with no retraining. On length control (Figure 7), the transferred scorer achieves normalized length on par with best-of-K. On HH (Figure 8), the transferred scorer achieves win-rates indistinguishable from the same scorer applied to its native model. Experiment 7 takes this further: applying the prefix scorer to a DPO-fine-tuned base model (which the scorer was never trained on) yields a combined system ("DPO + CD-Q blockwise") that achieves win-rates on par with or better than either DPO alone or CD-Q alone, while requiring fewer parallel decodings (smaller `$K$`) to reach the same KL divergence (Figure 10). This is a genuine system-level improvement: the prefix scorer does not need to be retrained when the base model is updated via training-time methods.

**Alignment-strength configurability:** The `$\lambda$` parameter in tokenwise CD and the `$K$` and `$M$` parameters in blockwise CD can be adjusted dynamically at inference time, allowing the same trained components (base model + prefix scorer) to operate at different points on the reward-KL Pareto frontier for different prompts, users, or contexts. A deployment could use conservative settings (`$\lambda$` low, `$K$` small) for routine interactions and aggressive settings for high-stakes decisions, all with the same models. Training-time methods require separate checkpoints for each alignment strength.

Collectively, these properties enable a deployment architecture where a single large base model serves as the foundation, and a library of lightweight, independently developed prefix scorers provides pluggable alignment behaviors. This is a fundamentally different operational model from the dominant paradigm of "train a separate aligned model for each use case." The paper does not frame this as a minor convenience but as a qualitative shift in how aligned language models can be developed, deployed, and updated. The practical implications are substantial: prefix scorers are smaller and cheaper to train than full-model alignment (they fine-tune the same architecture but can be trained on off-policy data), they can be developed in parallel by different teams targeting different rewards, and they decouple the base model's release cycle from the alignment module's update cycle. A base model can be upgraded without invalidating existing alignment investments (as the transfer experiments show), and new alignment criteria can be added post-hoc without touching the base model.

### Innovation 5: The Diagnosis That CD-FUDGE Noise Limits Blockwise Performance (A Productive Negative Result)

The paper's finding that **CD-FUDGE's value predictions contain noise that severely degrades blockwise performance, while CD-Q's smoother predictions enable near-best-of-K performance**, is a diagnostic contribution that clarifies the failure mode of a popular approach and points directly to the fix.

FUDGE (Yang & Klein, 2021) established the prefix-scorer paradigm and has been influential in controlled generation research. The paper's theoretical result (Theorem 3.1) gives FUDGE a principled justification—it is an unbiased estimator of the value function gradient—which might seem to validate the approach. However, the empirical reality (Figure 13, Appendix B) tells a more nuanced story: FUDGE's single-sample Monte Carlo targets produce value estimates that are unbiased but **high-variance**, and this variance matters enormously for downstream use. Tokenwise sampling (where the softmax smoothes out noise) is relatively robust to this variance, but blockwise selection (which requires accurate relative ranking) is not.

This diagnosis is valuable because it is **actionable**. It tells practitioners that if they want to use prefix scorers for reranking or blockwise selection (which the paper shows is the best-performing mode), they should train with Bellman targets (CD-Q) rather than rollout targets (CD-FUDGE), or at minimum supplement the FUDGE objective with techniques to reduce variance (e.g., training with multiple rollouts per prefix and averaging their rewards as the target). It also explains why prior work using FUDGE-style scorers exclusively in tokenwise mode may have missed the performance ceiling imposed by value noise—the failure mode only becomes visible when the scorer is used for ranking, which is precisely the mode that achieves the best reward-KL tradeoffs.

This is an incremental rather than fundamental contribution, but it is practically significant because it guides resource allocation: if a team has already implemented FUDGE and is seeing underwhelming blockwise results, the fix is not to tune hyperparameters or increase model capacity but to change the training objective from regression-to-reward to regression-to-Bellman-target. The paper's Experiment 1 (Figure 3) quantifies the magnitude of the improvement: blockwise CD-FUDGE's reward-KL curve sits far below blockwise CD-Q's, which in turn tracks best-of-K closely. The gap is large enough to be practically meaningful—it is the difference between matching best-of-K and being substantially worse than it.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** Three datasets are used across experiments. For **response length control**, the DSTC8 Reddit conversations corpus (Microsoft, 2019) provides multi-turn conversations from Reddit threads; the paper uses an unspecified training split for training and an "eval set of conversations corpus" for evaluation. For **helpfulness and harmlessness (HH)**, the Anthropic HH dataset (Bai et al., 2022) provides pairwise human preference judgments on assistant responses; the paper trains a reward model on this data and evaluates generations on prompts from the Reddit conversations corpus using an HH-style prompt (Section 4.5, Appendix A). For **summarization quality**, the TL;DR dataset (Stiennon et al., 2020) provides Reddit posts with human preference judgments between two summary candidates; the reward model is trained on these preferences, and evaluation uses PaLM 2-L as a zero-shot judge on the TL;DR test set.

- **Base model(s).** All primary experiments use PaLM 2-XXS (Gecko) (Anil et al., 2023) as the frozen base language model. The prefix scorers for CD-FUDGE and CD-Q are also fine-tuned from PaLM 2-XXS, initialized from the same pretrained weights. The paper states this model is chosen as a representative small model, but the choice is not deeply justified beyond using a model family that spans multiple scales for the transfer experiments. For evaluation, PaLM 2-L (Unicorn) is used as a zero-shot judge for HH and summarization win-rate measurements. Transfer experiments additionally use PaLM 2-S (Bison) and PaLM 2-XS (Otter) as alternative base models.

- **Metrics.** Three primary metrics are reported, unified under the **reward-KL tradeoff curve** framework. **KL divergence** between the aligned policy and the base policy, `$\mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mu} \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi(\cdot|x)}[\log \pi(y|x) - \log \pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)]$`, serves as the x-axis proxy for model degradation. For best-of-K, the upper bound `$\log K - (K-1)/K$` (Stiennon et al., 2020; Beirami et al., 2024) is used; for blockwise CD, an extended upper bound `$\mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mu}[(\log K - (K-1)/K) \cdot \lceil L_x / M \rceil]$` is used. The paper restricts analysis to KL values below 10, "beyond which the policy shows significant signs of overfitting" (citing Eisenstein et al., 2023). **Normalized expected reward** is `$\mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mu} \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot|x)} r(x, y)$` divided by the base policy's expected reward—this is used primarily in the length experiment (Figure 3). **Win-rate against base policy** is `$\mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mu} \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot|x)} \mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot|x)} \mathbb{1}[r(x, y) > r(x, z)]$`—the fraction of comparisons where the aligned policy's response scores higher than a base policy response according to the reward model or, in HH and summarization experiments, according to a PaLM 2-L zero-shot evaluator. Win-rate is the primary y-axis metric in Figures 4, 5, and related plots.

- **Baselines.** The paper compares against seven distinct methods, spanning both inference-time and training-time alignment approaches. **Best-of-K** (Nakano et al., 2021; Stiennon et al., 2020): generate K complete responses from the frozen base model, rank by the reward model, and return the highest-scoring one. **CD-FUDGE** (Yang & Klein, 2021): the prefix scorer trained with on-policy rollout regression targets, deployed in both tokenwise and blockwise modes. **KL-regularized PPO** (Ouyang et al., 2022): trains the language model as a policy via on-policy RL with a KL penalty relative to the base model. **DPO** (Rafailov et al., 2023): online DPO where the policy generates two responses per prompt and the DPO objective is optimized on their explicit rewards rather than human preferences. **IPO** (Azar et al., 2023): trained similarly to online DPO but with the IPO objective's different regularization. **Majority voting** is mentioned but does not appear as a primary baseline in the main results—it is absent from Figures 3-12. The paper does not include comparisons to other inference-time controlled generation methods like GeDi (Krause et al., 2021), NADO (Meng et al., 2022), or DIRECTOR (Arora et al., 2022) beyond citing them as related work.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper measures test-time compute in terms of the number of parallel decodings required, parameterized by `$K$` (the number of candidates in best-of-K or blockwise CD, or the effective parallelism factor). For tokenwise CD, the cost is paying one prefix scorer evaluation per token, which is not directly comparable to `$K$`-based methods. For blockwise CD, the total generation cost is approximately `$K \times L$` token generations (same as best-of-K) but the latency is reduced from `$O(K \cdot L)$` to `$O(K \cdot M)$` where `$M$` is the block size. The paper's fixed-throughput experiment (Experiment 8, Figures 11 and 12) explicitly controls for inference budget by comparing DPO + best-of-K with the same `$K$` as blockwise CD-Q, ensuring both methods use the same number of parallel decodings. For PPO, DPO, and IPO, there is no extra throughput cost at inference time—they generate one sequence autoregressively—but they require expensive training runs. The paper does not account for training cost in the reward-KL tradeoff comparison; the curves reflect only inference-time performance.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** For the reward models (both HH and summarization), training runs for 1 epoch with fixed learning rates (1e-4 for HH, 1e-5 for summarization), and "the checkpoint with the highest eval accuracy" is selected (Appendix A). For DPO, IPO, and PPO, "several training runs" are performed by sweeping KL regularizer hyperparameters and learning rates "to reach comparable KL against other methods" (Section 4.5). For the prefix scorers (both CD-FUDGE and CD-Q), the length experiment trains for "half an epoch" and the HH experiment trains for "one epoch"—no explicit cross-validation or early stopping protocol is described for these models. The paper does not report confidence intervals, standard errors, or statistical significance tests for any of the reward-KL tradeoff curves. The win-rate measurements (Figures 4, 5) rely on PaLM 2-L as the judge, which introduces its own variance, but no inter-rater reliability or calibration analysis is provided for this evaluator. The test sets used for evaluation are not explicitly described—for length, it's "the eval set of conversations corpus"; for HH, it's the Reddit conversations corpus with HH prompt; for summarization, it's "the test set of the TL;DR corpus." Sample sizes for evaluation are not reported.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Experiment 1: Response Length Control (Figure 3)

The paper first tests on response length control because it provides "a clear test metric free of reward overoptimization and noise" (Section 5)—the reward is simply `$\log(T / T_{\text{max}})$`, which is deterministic given the generated response and cannot be gamed by the prefix scorer.

The headline result: **blockwise CD-Q achieves reward-KL tradeoffs on par with best-of-K while requiring substantially fewer parallel decodings.** In Figure 3, the blockwise CD-Q curve (blue circles) closely tracks the best-of-K curve (green line) across the entire KL divergence range from approximately 1 to 10. Tokenwise CD-Q (light blue diamonds) and tokenwise CD-FUDGE (pink diamonds) achieve intermediate tradeoffs—better than all training-time baselines (PPO, DPO, IPO) but substantially below best-of-K and blockwise CD-Q. Blockwise CD-FUDGE (red circles) performs dramatically worse than blockwise CD-Q, sitting well below both.

The efficiency gain is specifically quantified: "with `$K=6$`, blockwise CD-Q obtains very similar length and KL divergence as best-of-K with `$K=50$`" (Section 5, Experiment 1). This is approximately an 8× reduction in required parallel decodings. At the highest KL values (around 9-10), best-of-K achieves normalized length of approximately 3.5× the base model (reading from Figure 3's y-axis), while blockwise CD-Q with `$K=6$` achieves approximately 3.5× as well. Best-of-K needs `$K=50$` to reach this level. The paper annotates Figure 3 with arrows highlighting `$K=6$` on the blockwise CD-Q curve and `$K=50$` on the best-of-K curve, making the efficiency comparison explicit.

Tokenwise CD methods (both CD-FUDGE and CD-Q) achieve normalized length of approximately 2.5× at KL ≈ 10, outperforming DPO and IPO (which reach roughly 2.0× at similar KL) and PPO (which reaches roughly 1.8×). This is noteworthy because tokenwise CD is the theoretically optimal solution to the tokenwise RL objective, yet it underperforms blockwise CD—the paper's first indication that blockwise selection provides practical benefits beyond theoretical optimality.

The paper observes an unexpected ordering among the training-time baselines: "best-of-K achieves a better reward-KL tradeoff compared to KL-regularized PPO" (Section 5). This is consistent with prior findings by Gao et al. (2023, Figure 1) and Rafailov et al. (2023, Figure 3), and motivates the blockwise CD approach: if best-of-K works better than PPO, perhaps the best-of-K mechanism applied at the block level will also work better than tokenwise RL.

#### Experiment 2: Helpfulness and Harmlessness (Figure 4, Table 1)

The HH experiment tests alignment on a more complex and noisy reward signal—a learned preference model rather than a deterministic length function. The y-axis of Figure 4 is win-rate against the base policy, measured by zero-shot PaLM 2-L evaluation (Appendix A provides the full prompt).

The headline result: **blockwise CD-Q substantially outperforms training-time baselines (IPO, PPO) but falls short of best-of-K.** In Figure 4, blockwise CD-Q (green circles) achieves approximately 0.80 win-rate at KL ≈ 8, while IPO (orange squares) plateaus around 0.55-0.60 win-rate across the KL range of 2-8, and PPO (red triangles) achieves roughly 0.65-0.70 at KL ≈ 3 and does not reach higher KL values. Tokenwise CD appears to perform poorly—the paper states that "tokenwise controllers don't offer much HH improvement over baselines" (Section 5, Experiment 2), though the actual tokenwise CD curves do not appear in Figure 4. This is a notable omission: the paper makes a strong claim about tokenwise underperformance but does not show the data for HH.

Best-of-K (blue line) outperforms all other methods, reaching approximately 0.85 win-rate at KL ≈ 8. Blockwise CD-FUDGE (red circles) performs intermediate between blockwise CD-Q and the training-time methods, achieving roughly 0.70-0.75 win-rate at KL ≈ 8. The gap between CD-Q and CD-FUDGE in blockwise mode mirrors the length experiment: CD-FUDGE's noisier value estimates (confirmed in Figure 13) degrade blockwise selection performance.

Table 1 provides a diagnostic: when the CD-FUDGE and CD-Q prefix scorers are used as classifiers on the ground-truth Anthropic HH preference data (by applying them to `$[x, y]$` pairs and comparing the scores), both achieve test accuracy of approximately 0.63. The reward model itself (Reward-XXS) achieves 0.71. The paper notes this gap: "the classification accuracy of CD-Q and CD-FUDGE are weaker than that of Reward-XXS (≈0.6 vs ≈0.7). This is likely due to the noisy nature of the training data, and is an area for future investigation to improve the training using value function learning methods better suited to noisy reward environments" (Section 5, Experiment 2). This diagnostic is important: it reveals that the prefix scorers are not as good at the underlying classification task as the reward model they are trying to approximate, which sets a ceiling on how well they can guide generation.

#### Experiment 3: Summarization Quality (Figure 5)

The summarization experiment evaluates whether CD can improve the quality of Reddit post summaries according to a learned preference model. The y-axis is win-rate against the base policy measured by PaLM 2-L zero-shot evaluation on the TL;DR test set.

The headline result: **blockwise CD-Q outperforms IPO but does not match best-of-K.** In Figure 5, blockwise CD-Q (green circles) achieves approximately 0.65 win-rate at KL ≈ 6, while IPO (orange squares) reaches roughly 0.55 win-rate at similar KL. Best-of-K (blue line) substantially outperforms both, reaching approximately 0.75 win-rate at KL ≈ 6. The gap between blockwise CD-Q and best-of-K is larger here than in the length experiment—approximately 10 percentage points of win-rate versus much smaller or negligible gaps in Figure 3. The paper does not analyze why the summarization task shows a larger gap; possible explanations include the reward model being less reliable for summarization, the prefix scorer being harder to train for summary quality assessment, or the blockwise selection making more consequential errors when ranking summary blocks (where quality is more holistic and harder to assess from partial text).

Only three methods are compared in Figure 5: best-of-K, blockwise CD-Q, and IPO. CD-FUDGE, tokenwise CD, PPO, and DPO are absent from this experiment without explanation. This limits the strength of conclusions that can be drawn about the relative performance of CD methods on summarization.

#### Experiment 4: Multi-Objective Control (Figure 6)

This experiment demonstrates the modularity advantage of CD: combining independently trained prefix scorers at inference time without retraining. The paper uses only blockwise CD-FUDGE for this experiment (the choice of CD-FUDGE over CD-Q is not explained).

The headline result: **CD enables dynamic, inference-time adjustment of the tradeoff between HH and response length.** Figure 6 is a single plot with normalized average length on the x-axis and HH win rate on the y-axis, with KL divergence represented by point size or annotations. Three configurations are shown: (1) HH prefix scorer alone, which increases both HH win-rate (to approximately 0.72-0.77) and length (to approximately 1.3-1.6× normalized) compared to the base model (which is at approximately 1.0× length and 0.50 win-rate by definition); (2) a linear combination of HH and a *negative* length reward (penalizing long responses), which brings length back down to approximately 1.0× but reduces HH win-rate to approximately 0.65-0.70; (3) presumably intermediate combinations trace out a tradeoff curve. The paper states: "This experiment would be impossible with training-time KL-regularized RL methods (PPO/DPO/IPO) as they need to be retrained from scratch for different linear combinations of rewards" (Section 5, Experiment 4).

The finding is practically significant: a deployment could serve different users with different HH-length preferences (some wanting concise but helpful responses, others wanting detailed but less-filtered responses) using the same base model and prefix scorers, swapping only the linear combination weights at inference time. The paper does not report numerical values for specific points on Figure 6 beyond what can be read from the plot, nor does it analyze the shape of the tradeoff curve or the sensitivity to the linear combination coefficients.

#### Experiment 5: Transfer to a New Base Model — Length (Figure 7)

This experiment tests whether a CD-Q prefix scorer trained on PaLM 2-XXS (Gecko) transfers to PaLM 2-S (Bison) without retraining.

The headline result: **the transferred prefix scorer performs "on par with the strongest baseline, best-of-K"** (Section 5, Experiment 5). In Figure 7, which reports normalized average length vs. KL divergence, the "CD-Q (blockwise) + PaLM 2-S" curve (blockwise deployment with the transferred scorer and the new base model) closely tracks the best-of-K curve. The paper does not provide specific numerical comparisons, but from the figure, at KL ≈ 6, both achieve normalized length of approximately 2.0-2.5×. The paper does not compare against applying CD-Q to the original PaLM 2-XXS base model in the same plot, so the absolute performance difference between the transferred and native settings cannot be assessed from Figure 7 alone. The claim is specifically comparative against baselines running on the *same* (transferred) base model.

#### Experiment 6: Transfer to a New Base Model — HH (Figure 8)

This experiment parallels Experiment 5 but for the HH task, transferring the CD-Q prefix scorer from PaLM 2-XXS to PaLM 2-XS (Otter).

The headline result: **again, CD-Q "generalizes well and retains the good performance without retraining"** (Section 5). In Figure 8, blockwise CD-Q with PaLM 2-XS (green circles) achieves win-rates of approximately 0.72-0.78 across KL values of 2-8, closely tracking the best-of-K curve for the same base model (blue line). The gap to best-of-K is small but visible—approximately 2-3 percentage points of win-rate at KL ≈ 6-8. The paper does not directly compare this transferred performance to the original PaLM 2-XXS setting (Figure 4), but cross-referencing suggests that the absolute win-rates are comparable: Figure 4 shows blockwise CD-Q with PaLM 2-XXS achieving approximately 0.78-0.80 at KL ≈ 6-8, while Figure 8 shows approximately 0.75-0.78 at similar KL. The base model change from XXS to XS does not appear to significantly degrade the prefix scorer's effectiveness.

The paper makes an explicit contrast: "Note that PPO/DPO/IPO could not be used without re-training in this experiment" (Section 5). Training-time alignment methods fuse the alignment into the model weights, so changing the base model requires a new alignment run. CD's decoupling of the value function from the policy is what enables transfer.

#### Experiment 7: Block Size Ablation (Figure 9)

This experiment sweeps the block size `$M$` in blockwise CD-Q for the HH task to analyze its impact on the reward-KL tradeoff.

The headline result: **larger block sizes yield better win-rate vs. KL tradeoffs.** Figure 9 shows blockwise CD-Q curves for three block sizes (the paper mentions 4, 8, 16, and 32 in the text, but Figure 9 itself should be consulted for the exact values shown). The curve for larger `$M$` lies above and to the left of the curve for smaller `$M$`, indicating that with the same `$K$`, longer blocks achieve higher win-rates at lower KL divergence. The paper states that "reducing the block size `$M$` generally results in worse win-rate vs KL divergence trade-offs" and notes that block sizes larger than 32 were not analyzed because "the efficiency gains against best-of-K would evaporate."

This result makes intuitive sense: longer blocks give the prefix scorer more context to evaluate the quality of a continuation, reducing ranking errors. The cost is increased latency—with `$M=32$`, the system must generate 32 tokens per candidate before making the first block decision, versus 4 tokens with `$M=4$`. The paper does not provide a systematic latency-quality Pareto analysis showing latency on one axis and win-rate on the other, which would be needed to identify the *optimal* block size for a given latency budget.

#### Experiment 8: Combining DPO and CD-Q (Figure 10)

This experiment layers blockwise CD-Q on top of a DPO-fine-tuned base model, without retraining the prefix scorer on the DPO model's outputs.

The headline result: **"DPO + CD-Q (blockwise)" achieves win-rates on par with or better than CD-Q alone, while being more efficient in terms of `$K$`** (Section 5). Figure 10 shows three curves: CD-Q blockwise (blue circles), DPO alone (different checkpoints at various KL divergences, red markers), and "DPO + CD-Q blockwise" (green circles). The paper provides a specific comparison: at total KL ≈ 5, the "DPO + CD-Q blockwise" variant with `$K=8$` achieves a win-rate of approximately 0.78 (the annotated green point), while "CD-Q blockwise" requires `$K=32$` to achieve a similar tradeoff (the annotated blue point). The DPO component contributes approximately 2.5 KL and the blockwise CD-Q component (with `$K=8$`) contributes the remaining approximately 2.5 KL, for a total of 5. The combined variant's win-rate is approximately 0.78 at KL ≈ 5, while CD-Q alone at KL ≈ 5 (with `$K=32$`) achieves a similar win-rate.

The paper interprets this as a win for the combined approach: it achieves the same performance with 4× fewer parallel decodings (K=8 vs. K=32). However, the comparison is slightly asymmetric: the DPO model itself underwent training (which has an unaccounted training cost), and the prefix scorer was not retrained for the DPO distribution (which could be seen as robustness or as suboptimality). Figure 14 (Appendix B) provides additional detail, showing blockwise CD-Q applied to four different DPO checkpoints with varying KL divergences (0.3, 1.15, 2.25, 3.25), demonstrating that the approach works across a range of initial DPO alignment strengths.

#### Experiment 9: Fixed Throughput Budget — Length (Figure 11)

This experiment enforces a fair comparison between CD-Q (blockwise) and "DPO + best-of-K" by giving both methods the same inference throughput budget `$K$`.

The headline result: **for a fixed `$K$`, blockwise CD-Q strictly outperforms DPO + best-of-K, and the gap widens with larger `$K$`.** Figure 11 shows normalized length vs. KL divergence for three values of `$K$`: 4, 8, and 16. For each `$K$`, blockwise CD-Q (achieving different points on its curve by varying `$M$` for the fixed `$K$`) achieves higher normalized length at the same or lower KL compared to DPO + best-of-K with the same `$K$`. At `$K=4$`, the gap is modest—blockwise CD-Q achieves approximately 1.5-2.0× normalized length vs. approximately 1.5× for DPO + best-of-K. At `$K=16$`, the gap is substantial—blockwise CD-Q reaches approximately 3.0-3.5× while DPO + best-of-K reaches approximately 2.0×. The paper states: "CD-Q (blockwise) outperforms DPO with best-of-K sampling, and the performance gap between the two approaches increases for larger values of `$K$`, suggesting that blockwise CD-Q is strictly better than DPO, even with a fixed throughput budget" (Section 5).

This is a strong claim that the experimental setup supports, with two caveats. First, blockwise CD-Q varies `$M$` to achieve different points while keeping `$K$` fixed, which changes latency (larger `$M$` means longer wait before the first block is output). DPO + best-of-K always waits for the full sequence to be generated. The paper's fixed-throughput comparison equalizes total FLOPs but not latency, which is a meaningful dimension for practical deployment. Second, the DPO baseline uses best-of-K as a post-hoc reranker, not CD—comparing DPO+CD-Q against DPO+best-of-K at the same `$K$` would isolate the value of blockwise selection, but this comparison is not shown.

#### Experiment 10: Fixed Throughput Budget — HH (Figure 12)

This experiment parallels Experiment 9 but for the HH task, comparing "DPO + CD-Q (blockwise)" against "DPO + Best-of-K" with a fixed `$K=4$`.

The headline result: **"DPO + CD-Q (blockwise)" is on par with "DPO + Best-of-K"** at `$K=4$`. Figure 12 shows HH win-rate vs. KL divergence, with both methods achieving win-rates of approximately 0.75-0.78 at KL ≈ 3-5. The curves largely overlap. Compared to the length experiment (where blockwise CD-Q dominated DPO + best-of-K at higher `$K$`), this experiment shows more comparable performance. The paper does not analyze why the advantage of CD-Q over best-of-K differs between length and HH. Possible explanations include: (a) only `$K=4$` is shown, and the length experiment showed the gap widens at higher `$K$`; (b) the HH reward model is noisier, making the best-of-K selection (which picks the single highest-scoring full response) relatively more competitive against blockwise selection (which makes multiple greedy block choices).

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

- **CD-FUDGE vs. CD-Q prefix scorer quality (Appendix B, Figure 13)**: When used to predict the length of fully decoded responses on the Reddit test set, CD-Q predictions are tightly correlated with actual lengths, while CD-FUDGE predictions are "much noisier." The test examples are sorted by actual response length on the x-axis (monotonically increasing), and CD-Q's predicted lengths track this trend with modest variance, while CD-FUDGE's predictions oscillate wildly, sometimes predicting lengths of 600-800 tokens for responses that are actually 0-200 tokens. This diagnostic explains the dramatic performance gap between blockwise CD-Q and blockwise CD-FUDGE in Figure 3: blockwise selection requires reliable relative ranking, and CD-FUDGE's noise causes frequent ranking errors. The figure does not report a quantitative noise metric (e.g., mean absolute error, Spearman rank correlation), making the comparison somewhat qualitative.

- **Block size `$M$` sensitivity (Figure 9)**: Already discussed under Experiment 7. A larger `$M$` consistently improves the reward-KL tradeoff, with the effect being monotonic across the tested range. The paper does not evaluate `$M=1$` (which would recover tokenwise CD) in this figure, missing the opportunity to visually demonstrate the continuum from tokenwise to long-block behavior. The maximum block size is 32; larger values are not tested due to diminishing efficiency gains relative to best-of-K.

- **DPO checkpoint KL divergence for combined approach (Appendix B, Figure 14)**: When blockwise CD-Q is applied on top of DPO checkpoints with varying KL divergences (0.3, 1.15, 2.25, 3.25), the combined approach achieves win-rates of approximately 0.70-0.78. The figure shows that starting from a DPO checkpoint with higher KL divergence reduces the `$K$` needed in blockwise CD-Q to reach a given total KL—for example, DPO (KL=3.25) + CD-Q with `$K=4$` achieves total KL ≈ 5 and win-rate ≈ 0.72, while DPO (KL=0.3) + CD-Q with `$K=32$` achieves similar total KL but higher win-rate ≈ 0.78. This suggests the DPO and CD-Q contributions are not fully additive in their effects on win-rate, which is expected if DPO already captures some of the easy alignment gains that CD-Q would otherwise provide.

- **Multi-objective composability (Figure 6)**: Already discussed under Experiment 4. The paper demonstrates that combining an HH prefix scorer with a negative length reward can counteract the length-increasing effect of HH alignment, bringing normalized length back to approximately 1.0× while retaining HH win-rates of approximately 0.65-0.70. This is a qualitative demonstration rather than a systematic sweep of combination weights, and only blockwise CD-FUDGE is used (not CD-Q).

- **Transfer to unseen PaLM 2 scales (Figures 7, 8)**: The prefix scorer transfers effectively from PaLM 2-XXS to PaLM 2-S (for length) and PaLM 2-XS (for HH). Both experiments show transferred performance on par with best-of-K on the new base model. The paper does not test transfer to a completely different model architecture (e.g., from PaLM 2 to LLaMA, or from a dense model to a mixture-of-experts model), so the claim of "generalizes well" is limited to within-family scaling.

- **CD-Q on DPO base model without retraining (Experiment 7, Figure 10)**: The prefix scorer trained on PaLM 2-XXS base model outputs transfers to a DPO-fine-tuned PaLM 2-XXS without retraining and provides additional alignment gains. This is a robustness check that demonstrates the prefix scorer does not overfit to the specific output distribution of the base model it was trained on. However, the DPO fine-tuning was done on the same model family and architecture, so the distribution shift is modest.

- **Majority voting as an alternative selection mechanism**: The paper mentions majority voting in the evaluation metrics and baselines sections but does not report majority voting results in any figure or table for the main experiments. For the revision model experiments (which are not part of this paper—this is likely a carryover from a different document), majority voting is shown. For CD experiments specifically, all reported selection mechanisms use either the prefix scorer (for blockwise/tokenwise CD) or the reward model (for best-of-K and PPO/DPO/IPO). There is no ablation comparing prefix scorer selection against majority voting for blockwise CD, which would test whether the learned value function provides selection signal beyond simple consensus.

### Critical Assessment

**Do the experiments support the paper's central claims?**

The paper's primary claim is that controlled decoding, particularly blockwise CD-Q, offers a modular inference-time alignment method that matches or approaches the reward-KL tradeoffs of best-of-K (the strongest inference-time baseline) while substantially outperforming training-time alignment methods (PPO, DPO, IPO). The evidence for this claim is mixed across tasks.

**On length control (Figure 3), the claim is strongly supported.** Blockwise CD-Q closely tracks best-of-K across the full KL range while requiring 8× fewer parallel decodings. Tokenwise CD-Q also outperforms all training-time baselines, and even tokenwise CD-FUDGE (the worst CD variant in blockwise mode) is competitive with or better than PPO and DPO. Length control is the cleanest test because the reward is deterministic and cannot be gamed—there is no separate reward model that might be over-optimized. This makes the length results the most trustworthy evidence for CD's fundamental effectiveness.

**On helpfulness and harmlessness (Figure 4), the claim is partially supported.** Blockwise CD-Q significantly outperforms IPO and PPO, confirming that CD is competitive with training-time methods. However, blockwise CD-Q falls meaningfully short of best-of-K—approximately 5 percentage points of win-rate at KL ≈ 8. The paper acknowledges this gap but does not diagnose its cause beyond noting that the prefix scorer's classification accuracy (0.63) is lower than the reward model's (0.71, Table 1). This accuracy gap is a fundamental limitation: if the prefix scorer cannot reliably distinguish good from bad responses at the sequence level, it will make errors in blockwise selection that compound over multiple block decisions. The paper frames this as "an area for future investigation" rather than a limitation of the current method, which is fair but means the HH results should be viewed as a lower bound—better value function learning could close the gap to best-of-K.

**On summarization (Figure 5), the evidence is thin.** Only three methods are compared (best-of-K, blockwise CD-Q, IPO). CD-FUDGE, tokenwise CD, PPO, and DPO are absent. The gap to best-of-K is large (approximately 10 win-rate points). The paper does not analyze the summarization results in depth, making it unclear whether the underperformance is specific to the summarization task, the summarization reward model, or a general limitation of blockwise CD for tasks requiring holistic quality assessment from partial text. This experiment does not strongly support the paper's broader claims about CD's effectiveness—it shows CD can improve over IPO but does not demonstrate competitiveness with the best inference-time method.

**The modularity claims are well-supported.** The multi-objective control experiment (Figure 6) clearly demonstrates that prefix scorers can be combined at inference time without retraining, which training-time methods cannot do. The transfer experiments (Figures 7, 8) show that CD prefix scorers work on different base model sizes without retraining, though only within the PaLM 2 family. The DPO + CD-Q combination (Figure 10) demonstrates layering of training-time and inference-time alignment. These experiments collectively establish modularity as a genuine practical advantage of CD, not just a theoretical property.

**Specific weaknesses in the experimental design:**

1. **The tokenwise CD results for HH and summarization are not shown.** The paper states "tokenwise controllers don't offer much HH improvement over baselines" (Section 5, Experiment 2) but does not include the tokenwise CD curves in Figure 4 or 5. Given that tokenwise CD is the theoretically optimal solution to the RL objective (Theorem 2.1), its underperformance is an important finding that deserves explicit data. The absence of these curves makes it impossible to assess whether tokenwise CD is truly ineffective or merely underperforms relative to blockwise CD.

2. **The CD-FUDGE vs. CD-Q comparison is incomplete.** CD-FUDGE is included in Figure 3 (length) and Figure 4 (HH), but not in Figure 5 (summarization), Figure 10 (DPO combination), or the transfer experiments (Figures 7, 8). The paper's conclusion that CD-FUDGE is noisier and underperforms in blockwise mode is well-supported for the tasks where it is tested, but the missing experiments leave open the possibility that CD-FUDGE performs competitively on other tasks or in combination with DPO.

3. **No systematic sweep of `$\lambda$` for tokenwise CD is reported.** The paper mentions that `$\lambda$` controls the alignment strength in tokenwise CD and is swept to generate reward-KL curves, but specific `$\lambda$` values and their corresponding KL divergences are not tabulated. For blockwise CD, `$K$` and `$M$` are explicitly annotated on figures. For tokenwise CD, the relationship between `$\lambda$` and KL is opaque, making it difficult to reproduce the tokenwise CD results or compare them to other methods at matched KL values.

4. **Statistical significance is never addressed.** None of the reward-KL curves include error bars, confidence intervals, or significance tests. The evaluation set sizes are not reported. Win-rates are measured by a prompted judge (PaLM 2-L), which introduces its own variance, but no calibration or inter-rater reliability analysis is provided. For the HH win-rate measurements, a difference of 2-3 percentage points (e.g., blockwise CD-Q at ~0.78 vs. best-of-K at ~0.80 in Figure 4) may or may not be statistically significant—the paper treats it as a meaningful gap without establishing that it is beyond noise.

5. **Training cost is not accounted for in the reward-KL comparison.** PPO, DPO, and IPO require expensive training runs to produce their aligned models; CD requires training the prefix scorer. The reward-KL curves compare inference-time performance but do not amortize training cost. A fairer comparison would plot total FLOPs (training + inference) on the x-axis, but this is not done. The paper's transfer experiments partially address this (a single prefix scorer can serve multiple base models, amortizing its training cost), but the one-to-one comparison between CD and training-time methods in Figures 3-5 does not account for this asymmetry.

6. **The evaluation uses PaLM 2-L as a judge, which may have its own biases.** The HH and summarization win-rates depend on PaLM 2-L's preferences, which may not align perfectly with the ground-truth human preferences in the Anthropic HH and TL;DR datasets. The paper does not calibrate PaLM 2-L's judgments against the original human annotations or report agreement statistics. This is a standard practice in the LLM-as-judge paradigm, but it means the win-rates should be interpreted as "preferred by PaLM 2-L" rather than "preferred by humans."

7. **No comparison against other inference-time control methods.** Related work like GeDi (Krause et al., 2021), NADO (Meng et al., 2022), and DIRECTOR (Arora et al., 2022) are cited but not implemented as baselines. The only inference-time baselines are best-of-K and CD-FUDGE (which is essentially a reimplementation). The paper does not establish whether blockwise CD-Q outperforms these alternative controlled generation approaches, which limits the novelty claim.

8. **The blockwise CD KL upper bound is conservative, making the method look worse on the x-axis than it actually is.** The paper acknowledges this implicitly: the bound is an upper bound, so the true KL divergence is lower than the plotted x-coordinate. This means blockwise CD's reward-KL curves should be shifted leftward relative to methods where KL is estimated directly (PPO, DPO, tokenwise CD). The magnitude of this shift is unknown, but it means blockwise CD's performance relative to training-time methods is at least as good as shown and potentially better. The paper does not quantify the slack in the bound, which is a missed opportunity to strengthen the blockwise CD results.

**Missing experiments that would have strengthened the paper:**

- **A direct latency-quality tradeoff analysis** that plots wall-clock latency (or simulated latency based on block size and sequence length) against win-rate or reward, at fixed KL divergence. This would formalize the paper's central claim that blockwise CD navigates the latency-quality tradeoff better than best-of-K or tokenwise CD.

- **Evaluation on a broader set of tasks.** The three tasks (length, HH, summarization) are all text generation quality tasks. Testing on toxicity reduction, factual accuracy, or instruction following would test whether CD generalizes across reward types. The length task is special because the reward is deterministic—it would be valuable to see CD's performance on tasks with learned reward models of varying quality.

- **Ablation of the prefix scorer architecture.** All prefix scorers are fine-tuned from PaLM 2-XXS. Testing whether a smaller or differently-initialized network could serve as the prefix scorer would test whether the value function learning is robust to architecture choices or requires the same model scale.

- **Training data scale ablations.** How does CD-FUDGE and CD-Q performance scale with the amount of training data? CD-FUDGE requires on-policy rollouts; CD-Q uses off-policy data. A data scaling comparison would illuminate the practical tradeoffs between the two methods.

- **Direct comparison of tokenwise CD and best-of-K at matched KL on HH and summarization.** The paper states tokenwise CD underperforms but does not show the data. Including these curves would complete the picture and help diagnose whether tokenwise CD's underperformance is fundamental (due to myopia) or specific to the prefix scorer quality.

**Conditional nature of the claims:**

- The claim that blockwise CD-Q matches best-of-K **holds for length control** across the full KL range (Figure 3).
- The claim **partially holds for HH**, where blockwise CD-Q falls short of best-of-K by approximately 5 win-rate points at high KL (Figure 4).
- The claim **weakly holds for summarization**, where only three methods are compared and the gap to best-of-K is large (~10 win-rate points, Figure 5).
- The transfer claims **hold within the PaLM 2 model family** for two sizes (S and XS) on two tasks (length and HH). Transfer across model families or architectures is not tested.
- The composability claim **holds for two rewards (HH and length)** with blockwise CD-FUDGE, demonstrated qualitatively. Composability with more than two rewards or with CD-Q is not tested.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### 6.1 The Prefix Scorer's Accuracy Ceiling Imposed by Reward Model Quality

**The assumption or constraint.** CD's entire approach to alignment rests on the prefix scorer faithfully approximating the value function `$V^\star$`, which itself depends on the reward model `$r([x, y])$` used for training. The paper is transparent that the prefix scorer inherits the limitations of the reward model it learns from. In the HH experiment (Section 5, Experiment 2), Table 1 reports that both CD-FUDGE and CD-Q achieve test classification accuracy of approximately 0.63 when used as sequence-level preference predictors, compared to 0.71 for the reward model (Reward-XXS) itself—a gap of roughly 8 percentage points. The paper acknowledges: "the classification accuracy of CD-Q and CD-FUDGE are weaker than that of Reward-XXS (≈0.6 vs ≈0.7). This is likely due to the noisy nature of the training data, and is an area for future investigation to improve the training using value function learning methods better suited to noisy reward environments."

**The consequence.** The accuracy gap means the prefix scorer is a worse discriminator of response quality than the reward model it is trying to approximate. In blockwise CD, where the scorer must correctly rank `$K$` candidate blocks at each decision point, this lower accuracy compounds over multiple block decisions—the paper makes `$\lceil L_x / M \rceil$` selections per response, and each selection is degraded by the scorer's imperfect discrimination. This directly explains why blockwise CD-Q underperforms best-of-K (which uses the full reward model for ranking) by approximately 5 win-rate points on HH (Figure 4) and approximately 10 points on summarization (Figure 5). The gap is not a failure of the inference-time strategy but a fundamental bound imposed by the learned value function's quality. A practitioner deploying CD would see performance that is strictly upper-bounded by what the prefix scorer can learn from the available reward signal, which may be substantially below what the reward model itself is capable of assessing.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 1 provides the direct diagnostic. Figure 3 (length control) provides the contrast case: when the reward is deterministic and noise-free (length), blockwise CD-Q closely tracks best-of-K because the value function can be learned accurately. Figures 4 and 5 show the degradation on HH and summarization where the reward model is learned and imperfect. Appendix B, Figure 13 shows the specific mechanism: CD-FUDGE's value predictions are "much noisier" than CD-Q's, which translates to worse blockwise performance (Figure 3: blockwise CD-FUDGE far below blockwise CD-Q). The paper does not provide an analogous scatterplot for HH or summarization, so the noise level for those tasks cannot be directly assessed.

**Mitigation status.** The paper identifies this as an "area for future investigation" and suggests "value function learning methods better suited to noisy reward environments," including improvements from Rainbow (Hessel et al., 2018) referenced in Section 3.1. No concrete mitigation is implemented or tested. The paper does not explore whether training the prefix scorer with a different objective (e.g., ranking loss instead of MSE, or directly distilling the reward model's pairwise preferences into the prefix scorer) could close the accuracy gap.

### 6.2 The Computational Cost of the CD-Q Bellman Target Is Uncharacterized and Potentially Prohibitive

**The assumption or constraint.** CD-Q's training procedure, described in Section 3.1, requires computing the Bellman target for each prefix in the training data. For a prefix `$[x, y_t]$` where `$y_t \neq \text{EOS}$`, this target is:

$$v_t = \sum_{z \in \mathcal{Y}} \pi_{\text{ref}}(z|[x, y_t]) V_\theta([x, y_t, z])$$

This sum is over the **entire vocabulary** `$\mathcal{Y}$`, which for models like PaLM 2 contains tens of thousands of tokens. Computing it requires: (a) a forward pass of the base model `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$` at prefix `$[x, y_t]$` to obtain the next-token probabilities `$\pi_{\text{ref}}(z|[x, y_t])$`; (b) a forward pass of the current prefix scorer `$V_\theta$` for every token `$z$` in the vocabulary to obtain `$V_\theta([x, y_t, z])$`; (c) computing the weighted sum. For a single training prefix, this requires `$|\mathcal{Y}|$` prefix scorer evaluations. Over an epoch of training with millions of prefixes, this cost dwarfs both the base model rollouts required for CD-FUDGE and the training cost of reward model-based methods. The paper never quantifies this cost, mentions no approximations (e.g., sampling a subset of tokens, using the base model's top-k logits, or leveraging cached activations), and does not report the wall-clock time or FLOPs for CD-Q training in any experiment.

**The consequence.** A practitioner choosing between CD-FUDGE and CD-Q faces an uncharacterized tradeoff: CD-FUDGE requires expensive on-policy rollouts but cheap per-prefix training (the target is a single scalar reward). CD-Q can use cheap off-policy data but requires prohibitive per-prefix computation (a full pass over the vocabulary). The paper's experiments show CD-Q is better in blockwise mode, but the cost to achieve that improvement is unknown. This matters for practical adoption: if CD-Q training takes 100× longer than CD-FUDGE training for the same dataset size, the preference for CD-Q may flip for resource-constrained settings, or practitioners may need to approximate the Bellman target in ways that reintroduce variance and degrade performance. The paper's silence on this point—particularly the absence of any discussion of how the sum over the vocabulary was implemented efficiently—leaves a significant practical gap.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. Section 3.1 describes the CD-Q objective without mentioning computational complexity or implementation details. Section 4.5 reports training was done for "one epoch" or "half an epoch" but does not report training time, hardware, or the number of prefix scorer evaluations per training step. There is no ablation exploring approximations to the full Bellman target (e.g., using only the top-k tokens by `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$` probability, or sampling a subset of tokens from the base model distribution and importance-weighting). The paper does not compare wall-clock training time between CD-Q and CD-FUDGE for any experiment.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper references DQN-style improvements (target networks, experience replay) as future work for CD-Q but does not acknowledge the vocabulary-size scaling problem or propose solutions. The fact that the authors successfully ran CD-Q experiments implies some practical implementation exists (likely batched computation or vocabulary subsetting), but its details and efficiency are unreported, making reproduction uncertain.

### 6.3 Latency Advantages Over Best-of-K Are Asserted But Never Directly Measured

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's central motivation for blockwise CD—and arguably its primary practical contribution—is that it reduces decoding latency compared to best-of-K while achieving comparable reward-KL tradeoffs. Section 3.2 states this explicitly: "The decoding latency here is only `$M$` tokens, whereas the best-of-K method needs to fully decode all `$K$` sequences before it can select one to be served. If the sequence length is large, e.g., when the prompt is to write an essay, this would not be tolerated. This can open up new applications such as streaming." The paper further claims blockwise CD "enables similar reward values with significantly smaller `$K$`"—for example, `$K=6$` in blockwise CD achieves the same length-KL tradeoff as `$K=50$` in best-of-K (Figure 3).

**The consequence.** The claimed latency advantage depends on a comparison that the paper never actually makes: blockwise CD with `$K=6$` and `$M=32$` versus best-of-K with `$K=50$` need not have the same wall-clock latency on real hardware. Best-of-K can run all `$K$` sequences in parallel, with total latency determined by the slowest of `$K$` independent decodings. Blockwise CD must generate `$K$` blocks of `$M$` tokens, select the best block, then repeat—a sequential loop of `$\lceil L/M \rceil$` iterations, each requiring `$K$` parallel decodings of `$M$` tokens. The total latency is `$\lceil L/M \rceil$` times the per-block latency, not `$M$` tokens. Furthermore, switching between block decisions introduces coordination overhead (scoring all `$K$` blocks with the prefix scorer, selecting the winner, re-initializing the decoding state for the next block) that may be non-trivial on real serving infrastructure. Without measurements or a latency model, the claim that blockwise CD "opens up new applications such as streaming" is hypothetical.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. There are no wall-clock time measurements, no latency models that account for parallelism and sequential dependencies, and no comparisons of time-to-first-token or time-to-completion between blockwise CD and best-of-K on any hardware configuration. The paper's experiments vary `$K$` and `$M$` (Figure 9) but only report reward-KL tradeoffs, not latency. The "fixed throughput budget" experiments (Figures 11, 12) equalize `$K$` (the number of parallel decodings) but do not measure or equalize latency. The paper acknowledges in the concluding remarks that "a more comprehensive and rigorous understanding of such tradeoffs is left for future work" (Section 7), which is an honest admission but means the latency advantage—a core selling point of blockwise CD—is untested.

**Mitigation status.** Partially acknowledged in the conclusion: "our development of controlled decoding is motivated by tradeoffs between throughput, latency, and performance. While we explored these tradeoffs in a narrow set of experiments, a more comprehensive and rigorous understanding of such tradeoffs is left for future work, which might require exploring these methods in conjunction with speculative decoding." This is a forward-looking mitigation suggestion but not an implemented solution. Speculative decoding (Leviathan et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2023; Sun et al., 2023) is mentioned as a complementary technique but is not combined with CD in this paper.

### 6.4 All Experiments Use a Single Model Family; Transfer Claims Are Circumscribed

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's transfer experiments (Experiments 5, 6, and 7) demonstrate that a CD-Q prefix scorer trained on PaLM 2-XXS (Gecko) can effectively control PaLM 2-S (Bison), PaLM 2-XS (Otter), and a DPO-fine-tuned PaLM 2-XXS—all within the same PaLM 2 architecture family. The paper characterizes this as the prefix scorer being "robust and generalizing well to other base generative LLMs other than the one for which it was trained" (Section 5, Experiment 5). However, these experiments test scaling within a single model family, not transfer across different architectures, pretraining procedures, or tokenizers. The claim that the prefix scorer "generalizes well to other base generative LLMs" is supported only for the specific case where "other" means a larger or fine-tuned version of the same architecture trained by the same organization on similar data.

**The consequence.** A practitioner wanting to deploy CD with a different model family—say, using a prefix scorer trained on PaLM 2 to control a LLaMA or Mistral model—has no evidence from this paper about whether that would work. The transfer may fail for several reasons: different tokenizers would make prefix state representations incompatible; different pretraining distributions would shift the semantics of partial sequences; different architectural inductive biases would change how the value function's features relate to the base model's logits. Even within the PaLM 2 family, the paper only tests two transfer configurations (XXS→S for length, XXS→XS for HH). The transfer to DPO-fine-tuned XXS (Experiment 7, Figure 10) works, but the DPO fine-tuning is modest (KL divergences of 0.3–3.25 from the base model) and the prefix scorer was trained on the same base model's outputs, so the distribution shift is limited.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Three transfer experiments (Figures 7, 8, 10) all within the PaLM 2 family. The paper does not test transfer to any non-PaLM model, does not measure how transfer quality degrades with model scale (e.g., XXS→L or XXS→Ultra), and does not test transfer to a model with a different tokenizer. The paper's language—"other base generative LLMs" and "generalizes well"—implies broader applicability than the experiments support. This is a scope limitation that matters for practical deployment: if the prefix scorer must be retrained every time the base model architecture changes, the modularity advantage of CD (over training-time methods like PPO/DPO) is substantially reduced—it would be retraining the scorer rather than the full model, but still retraining.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper presents the transfer results as evidence of robustness without discussing the boundaries of that robustness. Future work would need to test transfer across model families with different tokenizers and architectures to determine whether CD's modularity is a general property of prefix scorers or specific to in-family scaling.

### 6.5 The Hardest Alignment Problems (Where the Base Model's Pass Rate Is Low) Show the Largest Gaps to Best-of-K

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's three tasks—length control, helpfulness/harmlessness, and summarization quality—represent different points on a spectrum of alignment difficulty, but the paper never characterizes difficulty explicitly (unlike the compute-optimal test-time scaling paper, which bins problems by the base model's pass@1 rate). Nevertheless, the results reveal a clear pattern: on the easiest task (length, where the reward is deterministic and there is no learned reward model to over-optimize), blockwise CD-Q nearly matches best-of-K. On the harder tasks (HH and summarization, which require learned reward models), blockwise CD-Q falls meaningfully short of best-of-K—by approximately 5 win-rate points on HH (Figure 4) and approximately 10 points on summarization (Figure 5). The paper does not analyze this difficulty-dependent degradation, but it is consistent with the interpretation that CD's performance relative to best-of-K depends on how well the prefix scorer can be trained: on easy problems with clean reward signals, the value function is accurate and CD approaches optimality; on hard problems where the reward model is noisy or the value function is hard to learn, CD degrades relative to best-of-K (which uses the full reward model directly).

**The consequence.** This pattern suggests CD may be least effective precisely where alignment is most needed—on tasks with subtle, hard-to-learn reward functions (safety, factual accuracy, nuanced helpfulness). For a practitioner deploying CD, the method's efficiency advantages (lower latency, smaller `$K$`) come at the cost of reduced alignment quality on harder tasks, and the paper provides no guidance on how to predict which tasks will fall into which regime. The worst case is that on safety-critical alignment tasks where the reward model is already imperfect, CD amplifies the imperfection through prefix scorer approximation error, producing a policy that is substantially worse than simply running best-of-K with the original reward model. The paper does not test on an explicit safety or toxicity reduction task, so this worst-case scenario is speculative but consistent with the degradation pattern from length→HH→summarization.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figures 3, 4, and 5 collectively show the pattern: the gap between blockwise CD-Q and best-of-K increases as the task moves from deterministic reward (length) to learned reward (HH) to learned reward on a more nuanced task (summarization). Table 1 shows the mechanism: the prefix scorer achieves only 0.63 accuracy on HH preference classification vs. 0.71 for the reward model. The paper does not provide an analogous diagnostic for summarization, and does not explicitly analyze difficulty-dependent degradation as a phenomenon.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed as a systematic limitation. The paper notes the accuracy gap in Table 1 and suggests future work on "value function learning methods better suited to noisy reward environments," but does not frame this as a difficulty-dependent failure mode that may be fundamental to the prefix scorer approach. The paper does not test on tasks explicitly designed to stress the value function learning (e.g., rewards requiring multi-hop reasoning, long-horizon planning, or nuanced social judgment), so the boundary of where CD remains effective is unknown.

### 6.6 The Multi-Objective Composability Demonstration Is Limited to Blockwise CD-FUDGE with Two Rewards

**The assumption or constraint.** Experiment 4 (Figure 6) demonstrates that CD can combine multiple independently trained prefix scorers at inference time to navigate a multi-objective tradeoff—specifically, improving HH win-rate while controlling response length. This is presented as a key advantage of CD's modularity: "This experiment would be impossible with training-time KL-regularized RL methods (PPO/DPO/IPO) as they need to be retrained from scratch for different linear combinations of rewards" (Section 5, Experiment 4). However, the experiment uses only blockwise CD-FUDGE (not CD-Q), combines only two rewards, and shows qualitative tradeoff behavior without a systematic sweep of combination weights or an analysis of how combining scorers affects the reward-KL tradeoff (the KL is shown via point size/annotation but not systematically controlled).

**The consequence.** Two limitations follow. First, practitioners wanting to use CD-Q (the better-performing variant for blockwise control) for multi-objective alignment have no evidence that it works. CD-FUDGE's performance in blockwise mode is substantially worse than CD-Q's on both length (Figure 3) and HH (Figure 4), raising the question of whether CD-Q prefix scorers can also be linearly combined or whether CD-Q's training objective (which optimizes for single-reward value estimation) produces scorers that interact poorly when combined. Second, the linear combination approach (adding `$\alpha \cdot V_{\text{HH}} + \beta \cdot V_{\text{length}}$` and taking the argmax) is plausible but has no theoretical justification—it is not guaranteed to trace out the Pareto frontier of the multi-objective RL problem. A practitioner wanting to achieve a specific point on the HH-length tradeoff would need to manually tune `$\alpha$` and `$\beta$` with no principled guidance.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Only Figure 6, which shows three configurations: HH scorer alone, HH + negative length reward, and the base model. The paper does not provide: (a) the same experiment using CD-Q, (b) combination of more than two rewards, (c) a sweep of combination coefficients showing the full attainable Pareto frontier, (d) a comparison of CD-based multi-objective control against training-time multi-objective RL methods (e.g., training a single PPO policy with a scalarized reward), or (e) any analysis of whether the linear combination of prefix scores corresponds to a known optimal policy for multi-objective KL-regularized RL. The conclusion that this capability is a definitive advantage of CD over training-time methods is based on a single qualitative demonstration with the weaker CD variant.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper presents the experiment as a demonstration of modularity but does not discuss its limitations or suggest how to extend it (e.g., to more rewards, to CD-Q, to theoretically-grounded scalarization). The absence of CD-Q from this experiment is particularly notable since CD-Q is the paper's main methodological contribution and its superior blockwise performance is one of the central empirical claims. A practitioner reading the paper would reasonably conclude that multi-objective composability is a benefit of CD generally, but the evidence only directly supports it for CD-FUDGE on two rewards.
