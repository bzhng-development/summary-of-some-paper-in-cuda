## 2. Context and Motivation

### 2.1 The Core Problem

**If an LLM is allowed to use a fixed but non-trivial amount of inference-time compute, how much can it improve its performance on a challenging prompt?** The field lacks a systematic understanding of *how* to spend a given test-time compute budget — which test-time strategy to use, when, and why — even though answering this question would inform a broad set of concrete deployment and design decisions:

- **On-device deployment.** If test-time compute can substitute for model size, a modest on-device model could replace a datacenter-scale LLM for many queries, provided sufficient inference-time processing is available.
- **Self-improving agents.** A general method that uses extra computation to steadily improve open-ended natural language outputs could run in an automated loop, reducing dependence on human supervision.
- **Pretraining–inference resource allocation.** Organizations choosing how to divide their total compute budget between pretraining larger models and running more inference-time compute need principled guidance, yet today’s allocation largely defaults to “train the largest model you can afford, then sample greedily.”

These practical needs remain unmet because the scaling behavior of test-time compute has not been characterized with the same rigor as pretraining scaling laws `(Hoffmann et al., 2022)`. Most prior work either applies a single, fixed inference strategy or reports conflated results that obscure the true drivers of improvement, leaving a critical gap between the *possibility* of using extra test-time compute and an *optimal* way to allocate it.

### 2.2 What Prior Approaches Existed and Where They Fall Short

The existing literature can be grouped into several lines of work, each of which leaves important questions open:

**Best-of-N sampling with outcome-based verifiers is the dominant paradigm, but it treats all tokens of compute identically.** The most studied approach to scaling test-time compute is to sample *N* complete solutions from an LLM and select the one that scores highest under a learned verifier `(Cobbe et al., 2021)`. While straightforward, best-of-N is a crude uniform-apply strategy: it never adapts its resource investment to the nature of the prompt, and it only makes use of a final-answer score, ignoring intermediate steps that could guide more efficient search.

**Process reward models (PRMs) were introduced to enable richer search, but their test-time optimization has not been rigorously analyzed.** PRMs that assign correctness estimates to each reasoning step — rather than just the final answer — were proposed by Lightman et al. `(2023)` and Wang et al. `(2023)`. Yet these works largely focus on *training* the verifier, not on *how* to search against it at test time. When the paper attempted to use the public PRM800k human-labeled dataset, it found that “this data to be largely ineffective for us” due to distribution shift between the GPT‑4 generated solutions and the PaLM 2‑S* models, and it notes that a PRM “was easy to exploit … via even naïve strategies such as best-of-N sampling.” No prior study systematically compares beam search, lookahead search, and best-of-N against a PRM across varying compute budgets and question difficulties.

**Self-correction via prompting the base model proved ineffective for complex reasoning, forcing the community to build specialized revision models.** Several early efforts showed that instructing an LLM to “critique and revise” can improve outputs on simpler tasks `(Madaan et al., 2023; Bai et al., 2022; Saunders et al., 2022; Du et al., 2023)`. But on reasoning tasks, these gains largely vanish. Huang et al. `(2023)` famously concluded that **“large language models cannot self-correct reasoning yet,”** a finding echoed by Stechly et al. `(2023)` and Valmeekam et al. `(2023)`. The paper directly acknowledges this limitation: “Simply prompting existing LLMs to correct their own mistakes tends to be largely ineffective for obtaining performance improvements on reasoning problems.” To obtain any meaningful improvement, fine-tuning a model specifically to produce revisions — following Qu et al. `(2024)` — becomes necessary, and even then, the inference-time *allocation* of revisions versus fresh samples has not been studied.

**RL-based fine-tuning for self-improvement (STaR, ReSTᵉᴹ) improves the proposal distribution but does not address adaptive allocation at test time.** Techniques such as STaR `(Zelikman et al., 2022)` and ReSTᵉᴹ `(Singh et al., 2024)` finetune an LLM on its own correct completions to raise its pass@1 rate. While these methods modify the underlying proposal distribution, they do not prescribe how an inference budget should be split between new parallel samples, sequential revisions, or search at deployment time. In fact, the paper’s own ReSTᵉᴹ-trained revision model **degraded substantially** with additional sequential revisions (Figure 16), underscoring the sensitivity of the approach to the inference-time budget configuration.

**The training–inference compute tradeoff was explored only with access to ground-truth answers, limiting its practical applicability.** Jones `(2021)` studied the tradeoff for board games with MCTS, and Villalobos and Atkinson `(2023)` surveyed it across domains. But the language-model analyses in those works assumed access to a ground-truth correctness oracle during inference. The present paper is among the first to perform a FLOPs-matched comparison without assuming oracle access, using only learned verifiers and revision models.

**Collectively, no prior framework adaptively allocates test-time compute per prompt based on difficulty.** Despite the multitude of methods — best-of-N, PRM search, self-revision, RL refinement — each was studied in isolation with a one-size-fits-all hyperparameter setting. There was no mechanism to ask: for this specific problem, given my current compute budget, should I run beam search, generate revisions, or sample in parallel? This missing adaptive layer is the central gap the paper addresses.

### 2.3 Reconciling Conflicting Prior Evidence

The literature contains a genuine contradiction. On one side, results show that LLMs *can* use test-time compute to improve their outputs, whether through self-critique `(Bai et al., 2022; Saunders et al., 2022)`, debate `(Du et al., 2023)`, self-refinement `(Madaan et al., 2023)`, or tree-of-thought style search `(Yao et al., 2023)`. On the other side, multiple rigorous studies conclude that LLMs fail to correct their own reasoning, even after multiple attempts, and that iterative prompting does not reliably raise accuracy on tasks like GSM8K or MATH `(Huang et al., 2023; Stechly et al., 2023; Valmeekam et al., 2023)`. These opposing findings are not merely an artifact of model scale; they also appear in strong proprietary models.

The paper reconciles this contradiction with an observation that the entire experimental program rests upon: **the efficacy of any given test-time compute strategy depends critically on the difficulty of the prompt, and prior contradictory conclusions arose from evaluating methods on implicitly different difficulty distributions.** Concretely, self-revision helps most on easy problems where the model’s initial answer is “on the right track but may just need further refinement,” whereas broad parallel sampling or tree search is essential for harder problems requiring exploration of disparate high-level strategies. Sections 5 and 6 validate this difficulty-dependent pattern empirically, demonstrating that a method can appear to “fail” or “succeed” depending entirely on which difficulty bins are aggregated in the analysis.

### 2.4 How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper unifies the landscape of test-time compute methods through a proposer–verifier abstraction (Section 2). Any method can be understood as modifying either (a) the **proposal distribution** — the set of responses the model generates — or (b) the **verifier** — the scoring or selection mechanism applied to those responses. Modifying the proposal includes generating sequential revisions (Qu et al., 2024) or using RL-based fine-tuning (STaR/ReSTᵉᴹ). Optimizing the verifier includes training a PRM and searching against it via beam search, lookahead search, or the usual best-of-N. These two axes are independent and complementary, much like a Markov chain Monte Carlo approach that combines a proposal and a score function to sample from a complex target.

Within this framework, the paper’s specific contribution is not a new search algorithm or revision model, but rather a **systematic analysis of how to adaptively allocate a fixed inference budget across these two axes, conditioned on a prompt’s estimated difficulty**, to maximize final accuracy (Equation 1). It introduces the notion of a “compute-optimal” test-time scaling strategy — a policy that selects the best combination of search method, beam width, sequential-to-parallel ratio, and revision depth per difficulty bin — and demonstrates that this adaptive policy yields >4× compute efficiency improvements over a uniform best-of-N baseline.

The entire study is anchored to a concrete setting: the MATH benchmark `(Hendrycks et al., 2021)` using PaLM 2‑S* as the base model, deliberately chosen because it is a non-saturated model on a task where true reasoning (multi-step inference from known concepts) rather than factuality is the primary challenge. The authors assume that if test-time compute can be made to work for symbolic mathematical reasoning, the same principles may carry over to other complex inference tasks.
