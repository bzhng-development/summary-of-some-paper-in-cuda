Here are the takeaways from this Ryan Peterman episode (Part 1 of a series on writing tactics learned through trial and error):

**Writing is the job for software engineers**

Ryan opens with a blunt assertion: "Writing is the job for software engineers." Almost everything we do relies on writing, yet we're taught almost nothing about how to be good at it. Most engineers learn as they go, hoping whatever they picked up in school is enough. The problem is that writing for work and writing for an English class are radically different pursuits. In school, we're often rewarded for difficult-to-understand prose—dense sentences, elaborate vocabulary, complexity that signals effort. At work, that approach backfires entirely. If you want to be effective, "your writing can't be that way." This sets up the entire post as a corrective: practical tactics he discovered only through repeated failure and iteration, not formal training.

**Tactic 1: Make the first line interesting**

The first line determines whether anyone reads the rest. If you want someone to engage with what you've written, "you have to show them why they should care immediately." This demands knowing your audience deeply—what keeps them up at night, what metrics they're accountable for, what problems they already feel. Ryan gives three concrete opening-line strategies mapped to different contexts. For sharing launch results: open with the specific metric movements that audience cares about, not background or methodology. For asking for collaboration: lead with the expected outcomes most important to that reader, not the request itself. For raising awareness of a problem: state the severity in terms the audience instinctively understands. He frames this as a **"hook," but much less sensational in the work context**—the principle isn't clickbait; it's respect for the reader's attention.

**Tactic 2: Make your ask clear**

If you want your audience to take action, "don't bury your ask somewhere in the middle of your message." Ryan offers two acceptable placements: share the ask first thing, or place it at the end once the reader has the relevant context to understand it. The longer your writing is, the more critical this rule becomes—"your reader is more likely to miss your ask" buried in a wall of text. The subtext is that most workplace writing contains an implicit or explicit request (approval, feedback, awareness, collaboration), and failing to surface that request clearly wastes everyone's time. Ryan is essentially giving engineers permission to be direct—you don't need to build a dramatic arc toward your conclusion; you need your reader to know what you want from them.

**Tactic 3: Write simply**

"The goal of writing is to be understood by the reader." The simpler your writing, the more your ideas will actually reach your audience. Ryan reveals a specific standard: he aims to write at a **6th-grade level** (as measured by the free web app Hemingway), and notes that this very post meets that bar. His editing discipline is ruthless: "If I can remove a word and preserve meaning, I do. If I can replace a complex word or phrase with a simple one, I do." He recommends running your own writing through Hemingway to check readability. This tactic pushes directly against the academic conditioning most engineers carry—the instinct to sound sophisticated rather than clear. Ryan argues the sophisticated move is actually the opposite: stripping away everything that doesn't help the reader grasp your point.

**Looking ahead to longer-form writing**

Ryan closes by distinguishing the short-form tactics covered here from a larger set of techniques he's developed for longer-form workplace documents: engineering proposals and designs, strategy and direction docs, launch or learning posts, workstream and team updates. He had "too many tactics to share for one post" and will continue the series next week, directly addressing readers who feel their teammates don't read their longer writing. The implication is that the quick wins above—interesting first line, clear ask, simple prose—are foundational, but sustained documents operating at scale require additional scaffolding. The post ends with a small meta-demonstration of its own advice: a clear, simple closing that names exactly what comes next and invites the reader to act ("Next week's post should fix that").

---

Ryan's **"hook, but much less sensational in the work context"** framing echoes across all three tactics—the entire philosophy is about respecting the reader's attention rather than manipulating it. His **6th-grade level** standard operationalizes the "write simply" principle into something measurable, giving engineers a concrete target instead of vague advice. And his stance on the ask—surface it first or last, never bury it—treats clarity as a form of professional generosity: doing the thinking upfront so your audience doesn't have to hunt for what you need. The through-line is a rejection of the academic conditioning that rewards opacity, replaced by a pragmatic, audience-first discipline he clearly honed the hard way.
