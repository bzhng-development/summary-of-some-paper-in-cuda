Here are the takeaways from this Ryan Peterman article on finding next-level scope for engineering career growth:

**Two basic ways to land bigger projects**

Ryan opens with a simple, two-bucket model. The first is **receiving scope**: someone hands you an opportunity. People often assume that only junior levels rely on getting projects this way, but the problems sitting on an engineering director or CTO’s plate are themselves massive in scope, and being handed one of those can drive senior-level promotions just as readily. The second is **creating scope for yourself**: your team’s systems are far from perfect, and identifying improvements that fit expectations at the next level will get you promoted fast. The rest of the article unpacks both.

**Receiving scope: the straightforward and the less-obvious paths**

The most direct way to receive scope is simply asking your management chain or tech lead. Engineering managers know their teams’ top problems, and tech leads often have problems they don’t have time for that would make excellent growth opportunities. The less obvious path is increasing the probability that someone comes to you by building a track record and becoming known in a specific technical area. This played a large role in Ryan’s promotions to Staff. He walks through three concrete examples from his own career: at the **promotion to Mid-Level (L4)**, after finishing several features faster than expected, his tech lead asked him to take on a larger project, which became the main reason for the promotion. At **promotion to Senior (L5)**, after demonstrating strong ownership over his team’s backend, his manager positioned him as the lead on a new initiative—this workstream “gave me invaluable leadership experience and helped me build an L5 track record.” At **promotion to Staff (L6)**, Ryan was known in his org for getting things done to the point where his Director knew his work. The Director handed him two major initiatives to lead, and while they weren’t the main reason for the promotion, they “helped strengthen my packet.”

**Creating scope: domain expertise and discovering significant problems**

For growth to more senior levels, creating scope becomes a baseline expectation. Aside from that, finding projects this way carries a major structural advantage: no one needs to trust you with the projects because you create them yourself, which “drives much faster growth trajectories since building trust takes time.” Ryan offers two high-level approaches. The first is **leveraging domain expertise**, where ideas for improvement flow naturally from a strong end-to-end understanding of a system. His own experience in video processing taught him how such pipelines should be built. When he dug into the ads video pipeline, he realized “there were a ton of opportunities to improve it,” and that understanding turned into a large collaboration with the ads team that got him promoted to Senior (L5). The second approach is **discovering significant problems** in less mature areas, where major problems surface just by digging around. The project that drove his Staff promotion came from noticing, in the relatively immature space of video compute efficiency, that the company was “running out of compute.” Chasing that problem down turned into a massive initiative yielding savings so large that “even Zuck wrote about it,” and it propelled his fast promotion to Staff (L6).

**Keeping options warm and strategizing with mentors**

Done well, this approach means you’ll always have a few candidate projects to choose from. Ryan’s practice is to then ask a manager or trusted mentors for their perspective on where to spend time—a discipline of **strategizing with mentors** that he credits as significantly helpful in his growth to Staff. The underlying framework feeds into his published level-by-level expectations guides for L4 through L7, which serve as the target for the scope you aim to find or create.

---

Ryan’s **receiving scope** and **creating scope** distinction isn’t a one-time choice but two muscles that alternate across a career: early on, receiving scope builds the track record that later makes someone hand you director-sized problems, while the **leveraging domain expertise** path turns deep system knowledge into self-generated Staff-level initiatives. His own arc—from being handed a larger feature at L4, to being positioned as a lead at L5, to chasing down a compute-shortage problem that the CEO wrote about at L6—shows how deliberate scope-finding and regular **strategizing with mentors** compound into faster promotions without waiting for trust to be granted.
