# Less Is More: Fast and Accurate Reasoning with Cross-Head Unified Sparse Attention

**ArXiv:** [2508.07101](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.07101)

## 🎯 Pitch

Existing sparse attention methods cripple reasoning accuracy because small, head-local selection errors snowball across thousands of decoding steps. LessIsMore shows that reasoning tokens are globally important—shared across heads and stable over time—and by enforcing cross-head unified selection plus a recency window, it achieves up to 87.5% sparsity on AIME benchmarks with zero accuracy loss, delivering 1.6× faster decoding without training.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **LessIsMore**, a training-free sparse attention mechanism designed explicitly for long-horizon reasoning workloads in large reasoning models. Evaluated across multiple model families—including DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B and Qwen3 models (4B, 8B, 14B)—on challenging reasoning benchmarks such as AIME-24/25, GPQA-Diamond, and MATH500, LessIsMore enforces **Cross-Head Unified Sparse Attention (CUSA)** with cross-head unified token selection (aggregating per-head top-k proposals into a single global ranking) and **stable recency preservation** (reserving a fixed fraction of the token budget for recent context). The method achieves up to 87.5% sparsity on AIME-24 with no accuracy loss and delivers up to 1.6× end-to-end decoding speedup over full attention, establishing that global token importance—shared across attention heads and stable over decoding steps—enables fast, accurate sparse attention only when selection is globally consistent rather than head-local.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Sparse Attention Fails on Long-Horizon Reasoning

The fundamental tension this paper addresses is deceptively simple: **sparse attention makes LLM decoding faster, but on reasoning tasks, it breaks the model's ability to think.** This tradeoff has gone largely unnoticed because the field's standard benchmarks for sparse attention—long-document retrieval, summarization, needle-in-the-haystack—involve relatively short generation lengths. Reasoning workloads, however, are structurally different: they require models to generate tens of thousands of tokens of intermediate chain-of-thought from short prompts, making attention cost the dominant bottleneck during decoding. The paper opens with a concrete, visceral example of this asymmetry: generating 32,768 tokens for a single AIME problem using DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B takes over 20 minutes on an NVIDIA RTX A5000 GPU. The attention computation alone—each new token attending to an ever-growing KV cache—is what makes this expensive.

The gap this creates is both practical and methodological. Practically, as reasoning models like DeepSeek-R1, Gemini-2.5-Pro, OpenAI o3, and Qwen3 increasingly rely on test-time scaling to achieve state-of-the-art performance, their inference costs grow proportionally with their reasoning depth. If sparse attention could preserve accuracy, it would directly reduce latency and cost for these workloads—but existing methods don't. Methodologically, the failure of existing sparse attention on reasoning is not just an engineering inconvenience; it reveals that the design assumptions underlying prior methods (local, head-specific, step-by-step token selection) are fundamentally mismatched with the attention structure that reasoning requires. This mismatch is the paper's central intellectual concern.

### Why This Problem Matters: Decoding-Dominant Workloads Are Becoming the Norm

The paper argues that the computational profile of modern LLM inference has shifted in a way that makes decaying attention a critical bottleneck, not a secondary optimization. In traditional LLM workloads (chat, translation, short-form QA), the prompt is often long and the generation is short—attention cost is dominated by the prefill phase, where the model processes the input in parallel. Test-time scaling inverts this: prompts are short (e.g., a math problem statement) while decodings are extremely long (thousands of tokens of reasoning traces). This makes inference **decode-heavy**, meaning the autoregressive attention computation—which scales linearly with generation length—dominates total cost.

This shift has systemic implications for how reasoning models will be deployed. If a single hard math problem requires 20 minutes of GPU time just for decoding, the economics of offering reasoning-capable APIs or running these models locally become prohibitive. The paper's efficiency results (Section 4.3) demonstrate the practical stakes: a 1.6× speedup at 64K context length directly translates to faster response times and lower serving costs. More broadly, the paper positions efficient sparse attention for reasoning as an **enabling technology** for the continued scaling of test-time compute—if decoding cost grows faster than accuracy gains, test-time scaling hits an economic ceiling. LessIsMore aims to push that ceiling higher.

### Prior Approaches: Two Classes of Sparse Attention, One Shared Failure Mode

Existing sparse attention methods fall into two broad categories (Section 5, Related Work), both of which the paper argues are structurally unsuited for reasoning:

**Eviction-based methods** (StreamingLLM, H2O, SnapKV, Keyformer) permanently discard tokens from the KV cache based on heuristic importance criteria. StreamingLLM maintains attention sink tokens (the first few tokens) plus a sliding window of recent tokens—a simple policy that works for language modeling but discards arbitrary historical tokens that reasoning might need to reference. H2O accumulates cumulative attention scores to identify "heavy hitter" tokens for retention. The shared weakness: once a token is evicted, it cannot be recovered, and reasoning traces often require the model to revisit earlier intermediate conclusions that were not obviously important at the time they were generated.

**Selection-based methods** (TidalDecode, Quest, RetrievalAttention, OmniKV) retain the full KV cache but dynamically select which subset of tokens to attend to at each forward pass. TidalDecode separates token selection into dedicated "selection layers" that compute full attention to determine which tokens are important, then shares those indices with subsequent layers that perform sparse attention. Quest selects tokens based on approximate attention scores using quantized key vectors. The shared weakness: selection is performed **locally**—per attention head, per layer, or per decoding step—based on approximate or instantaneous attention scores. The paper argues this local optimization is precisely what fails on reasoning tasks.

### Where Existing Methods Break Down: The Accumulation of Small Errors

The paper provides a concrete diagnostic for why local selection fails on long-horizon reasoning: **attention recall degradation**. Section 2.2 presents this as the central empirical finding motivating the method. Attention recall, defined as the fraction of the full attention mass retained by the selected token subset, measures how well sparse attention approximates the true attention distribution. The paper shows (Figure 1a) that both StreamingLLM and TidalDecode exhibit steady recall degradation on AIME-24 as generation length increases—TidalDecode drops to roughly 75% recall, StreamingLLM below 65%—despite using the same token budgets (4K tokens) that preserve accuracy on retrieval tasks.

The critical comparison is Figure 1b: on the needle-in-the-haystack retrieval task, TidalDecode maintains high attention recall across varying context lengths. On AIME-24 reasoning, recall degrades over the generation horizon. The paper's explanation (Section 2.2) is that reasoning tasks involve **orders of magnitude more decoding steps** than retrieval tasks, causing even small per-step selection errors to compound. Once a token critical to the reasoning chain is omitted at one step, the error cascades: the model cannot recover the missing information in subsequent steps, leading to logical inconsistencies and, in some cases, **longer generation traces** as the model struggles to reason with incomplete context. Table 2 quantifies this length inflation: at a 2K token budget, Quest generates 30.0K tokens (vs. 14.8K for full attention), a 103% increase, indicating that the model is spinning its wheels on reasoning it cannot properly ground.

This observation—that sparse attention errors compound over long generation horizons—is the paper's fundamental critique of prior work. It's not that existing methods are inaccurate per se; it's that their inaccuracies are **non-stationary** and **accumulate** in ways that retrieval benchmarks (with short generation) cannot expose.

### The Missing Piece: Global Token Importance in Reasoning

The paper identifies a structural property of reasoning attention that prior methods fail to exploit and that explains why local selection is brittle: **token importance in reasoning is a global property, shared across attention heads and stable over decoding steps.** This insight is developed in Sections 2.3 and 2.4 through direct analysis of attention patterns in reasoning models, and it forms the intellectual foundation for LessIsMore's design.

**Cross-head spatial locality (Section 2.3).** Conventional wisdom in sparse attention holds that different attention heads specialize in distinct functions—some attend to syntax, others to long-range dependencies, others to local context—and therefore require independent token subsets. Existing methods like TidalDecode and Quest operationalize this by performing per-head top-k selection. The paper challenges this assumption for reasoning workloads by visualizing the distribution of ground-truth top-4K attended tokens across all 32 attention heads of Qwen3-8B at a single decoding step (Figure 2). The visualization reveals substantial overlap in token importance rankings: many positions are selected by multiple heads, both within key-value groups (yellow regions) and across all heads (red regions). These overlaps are not transient—they persist across layers and decoding steps (Appendix A.6, Figures 9 and 10).

The paper's interpretation: reasoning attention is governed by a **shared set of globally important tokens** rather than head-specific patterns. Per-head top-k selection captures head-specific noise (tokens that score highly for one head but are irrelevant to the reasoning task) while potentially missing globally salient tokens that are important across many heads but might not be the absolute top-k for any single one. The consequence for sparse attention is that enforcing globally consistent token selection—aggregating importance signals across heads—should improve recall and stability.

**Temporal recency locality (Section 2.4).** The paper observes that recently generated tokens consistently receive high attention scores across all heads—a pattern that reflects the incremental, step-by-step nature of chain-of-thought reasoning where each new reasoning step builds on the immediately preceding conclusions. More importantly, the **proportion** of attention mass allocated to recent tokens remains approximately constant as generation length increases. This means the effective "recency window" grows with the token budget, not as a fixed absolute size. Prior approaches like StreamingLLM use a static sliding window, which does not adapt to different sparsity levels and fails to reflect the proportional structure observed in reasoning attention.

Together, these two spatial and temporal locality properties imply a design principle that the paper argues is necessary for any sparse attention method targeting reasoning: token selection must be **(1) globally consistent across heads** (not head-local) and **(2) proportionally stable over time** (not fixed-size or locally re-optimized at every layer). Prior methods violate both principles—they perform head-local selection and either evict tokens permanently (breaking temporal coherence) or re-select at every layer (introducing instability).

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper explicitly frames LessIsMore as occupying a specific design point within the landscape of selection-based sparse attention methods. It is **training-free** (unlike SeerAttention-r, which requires post-training adaptation to recover reasoning accuracy), **selection-based** (retaining the full KV cache, unlike eviction methods that risk permanent information loss), and **globally consistent** in its token selection (unlike all prior methods that perform local, head-specific selection).

The key positioning claim is that LessIsMore is not just another sparse attention heuristic but a direct architectural consequence of the observed attention structure in reasoning. The paper states in the introduction: "Importantly, we conduct detailed analysis to validate the design choices—including index sharing across heads, recency window allocation, and infrequent re-selection—are not heuristic but direct consequences of the observed spatial and temporal locality structures in reasoning attention." This frames the contribution as principled rather than empirical: the design choices are derived from measured properties of reasoning attention, not from trial-and-error hyperparameter tuning.

The paper also positions LessIsMore relative to TidalDecode, which serves as its implementation substrate. TidalDecode introduced the idea of separating token selection layers from sparse attention layers—a design that LessIsMore inherits—but TidalDecode performs head-specific selection at those layers. LessIsMore's innovation is replacing that head-specific selection with CUSA's cross-head unified selection within the same two-layer selection framework. The paper uses TidalDecode as both a baseline and a point of comparison to isolate the effect of unified selection: same selection layer placement, same token budget, same recency window ratio—only the aggregation strategy changes.

Finally, the paper positions itself in the broader context of test-time compute scaling. As reasoning models increasingly rely on long generation traces, the computational profile of inference shifts from prefill-dominated to decode-dominated. The paper argues this shift demands that sparse attention be rethought from the ground up for reasoning, rather than adapting retrieval-optimized methods. LessIsMore represents a first step toward sparse attention that is **cognizant of reasoning's specific attention structure**, and the paper's ablation studies (Sections A.1.1, A.1.2) demonstrate that the observed spatial and temporal locality properties are not incidental but are necessary for maintaining accuracy under sparsity.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

The paper builds a **training-free sparse attention system** for large reasoning models that selects which tokens the model should attend to during decoding. The system solves the problem that existing sparse attention methods degrade reasoning accuracy because their token selection is too local—per attention head, per layer, per step—and small errors compound over thousands of reasoning steps into cascading failures, while LessIsMore enforces **globally consistent** token selection across all heads and preserves a stable recency window, producing a single unified token index that can be reused across multiple layers without re-selection overhead.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The system has five major components arranged in a decoding pipeline (Algorithm 1):

1. **Base LLM with KV Cache** — a pretrained reasoning model (e.g., Qwen3-8B, DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B) that stores all previously generated key-value pairs in a growing cache. This cache is the target of sparse attention: rather than attending to every stored token, only a subset is loaded.

2. **Full Attention Layers** — the first few decoder layers (typically 2) that compute standard dense attention over the entire KV cache. These layers ensure accurate modeling of the prompt and early-generation context before any sparsity is applied.

3. **Token Selection Layer** — a single designated layer (chosen per model family, e.g., Layer 12 for Qwen3-8B) that performs full attention once to estimate token importance scores, then applies **Cross-Head Unified Token Selection (CUSA)** to produce a single globally ranked token index set `$\rho$`. This set is the output that drives all subsequent sparse attention.

4. **Sparse Attention Layers** — all remaining decoder layers after the selection layer. They reuse the index set `$\rho$` from the most recent selection layer, loading only those tokens from the KV cache and computing attention over the restricted subset. No additional scoring or re-ranking occurs here.

5. **Stable Recency Window** — a fixed fraction `$r$` of the total token budget `$K$` reserved for the most recently generated tokens, which are always included in `$\rho$` regardless of their importance scores. This preserves the step-by-step reasoning coherence observed in attention patterns.

Information flows as follows: a new token is generated → the hidden state enters the first decoder layer → the first 2 layers compute full attention over the entire KV cache → at the designated selection layer, full attention is computed, the query-key product `$P = q \cdot C.K^\top$` yields importance scores → each attention head independently proposes its top-k tokens → these proposals are unioned and flattened into a single candidate pool → the top `$K \cdot (1-r)$` tokens from this pool are retained → the most recent `$K \cdot r$` tokens are appended → the resulting index set `$\rho$` is stored → all subsequent layers in the current decoding step load only `$K$` tokens from the KV cache indexed by `$\rho$` and compute sparse attention over them → the KV cache is updated with the new token's key and value → at the next decoding step, the same `$\rho$` (potentially from an earlier selection) may be reused until a new selection layer is encountered.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the two key observations that justify CUSA—cross-head spatial locality and temporal recency locality—since every design choice in LessIsMore flows from these measured properties of reasoning attention.
- **Second**, the attention recall metric that serves as the diagnostic tool for evaluating sparse attention quality, because understanding why local selection fails requires understanding what recall measures and how it degrades.
- **Third**, the CUSA mechanism itself—how per-head top-k proposals are aggregated, ranked, and unified—since this is the central technical contribution.
- **Fourth**, the stable recency window—why it uses a proportional rather than fixed-size allocation, and how it interacts with the unified selection.
- **Fifth**, the layer assignment strategy—which layers compute full attention, which perform selection, and which use sparse attention—since the amortization of selection cost is what makes the method efficient.
- **Sixth**, the kernel-level implementation considerations that enable the measured speedups, including why unified token selection is more kernel-friendly for GQA models than per-head independent selection.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **systems paper with an empirical analysis foundation** whose core idea is that token importance in long-horizon reasoning is a global property shared across attention heads and stable over time, and that enforcing globally consistent token selection—rather than head-local or layer-local selection—is the key to preserving reasoning accuracy under high sparsity.

#### The Two Foundational Observations: Spatial and Temporal Locality in Reasoning Attention

The entire design of LessIsMore rests on two empirical observations about how attention behaves in reasoning models during long generation traces. These observations are not assumed—they are measured from ground-truth full attention distributions on actual reasoning tasks (AIME-24, AIME-25, GPQA-Diamond) using Qwen3-8B, and they serve as the justification for every architectural decision that follows.

**Cross-head spatial locality (Section 2.3, Figure 2).** The paper examines the set of top-4K most-attended tokens across all 32 attention heads at a single decoding step (the 20K-th token) in Layer 4 of Qwen3-8B on AIME-24. The visualization in Figure 2 uses a grid where the x-axis represents token positions (0–100% of the 20K generated tokens so far) and the y-axis represents attention head indices (0–31). A dark blue mark indicates that a particular token position is included in that head's ground-truth top-4K set.

The key finding: there are large contiguous blocks of token positions that are selected by **all or most** attention heads simultaneously. These blocks appear both within individual key-value groups (GQA groups sharing a single KV head, highlighted in yellow in Figure 2) and across all heads in the layer (highlighted in red). This means that attention heads are not, contrary to conventional assumptions, attending to entirely disjoint subsets of tokens during reasoning. Instead, they substantially agree on which tokens matter.

The paper's interpretation: reasoning attention is governed by a **shared global importance signal** rather than head-specialized patterns. Tokens that are critical for the reasoning chain—intermediate conclusions, key variable assignments, logical pivots—are recognized as important by many heads simultaneously. Per-head top-k selection, as performed by TidalDecode and Quest, treats the top-k for head A and the top-k for head B as independent sets. But if heads A and B largely agree on which tokens are important, then (a) the union of their top-k sets has high overlap, meaning independent selection wastes budget on redundant tokens, and (b) tokens that rank just outside the top-k for any single head but are consistently ranked high across many heads—globally important but not locally top-scoring—are missed entirely by per-head selection.

Formally, let `$\rho_{\text{head}}^{(h)}$` be the top-k tokens selected for head `$h$`. Per-head independent selection yields a distinct index set per head: `$\rho^{(h)} = \rho_{\text{head}}^{(h)}$`. This means different heads attend to different token subsets, and the total number of distinct tokens loaded from the KV cache is up to `$k \times H$` (where `$H$` is the number of heads), although each head only computes attention over `$k$` tokens. The cross-head overlap means many of these loaded tokens are redundant—the same token loaded multiple times for different heads—while globally important tokens that are top-(k+1) for every head are loaded zero times.

**Temporal recency locality (Section 2.4, Figures 9 and 10 in Appendix A.6).** The paper extends the token selection visualization across multiple decoding steps and difficulty budgets. Figure 9 shows the ground-truth top-4K token distribution at four different decoding steps (10K, 15K, 20K, 25K) on AIME-24. A prominent red-highlighted block consistently appears at the rightmost edge of the token position axis—the most recently generated tokens—across all heads and all decoding steps.

The paper makes two quantitative observations about this recency block:

1. **The recency block exists across all tasks and budgets.** Figure 10 demonstrates the same pattern on GPQA-Diamond (with top-1K and top-2K budgets) and on AIME-25 (with top-4K and top-6K budgets). Regardless of the task or the token budget, the most recent tokens are consistently in the top-k for nearly all heads.

2. **The proportion of the budget occupied by recent tokens is stable.** The size of the recency block (in absolute token count) grows as the total token budget grows, but the ratio of recency-block size to total budget remains approximately constant. This means the model allocates a roughly fixed **fraction** of its attention mass to recent context, not a fixed absolute number of tokens.

The paper's interpretation: this reflects the incremental nature of chain-of-thought reasoning. The model builds each new reasoning step by referencing its immediately preceding conclusions—the reasoning is locally Markovian in the sense that the most recent few hundred or thousand tokens contain the active reasoning state. However, the model also needs to occasionally reference earlier context (hence the non-recency tokens in the top-k), and this need grows with the total reasoning length. A fixed-size recency window (as in StreamingLLM's sliding window) would either overallocate to recent context at short generation lengths (wasting budget) or underallocate at long generation lengths (missing critical recent state). A proportional window adapts naturally.

Together, these two observations imply a design requirement that no prior sparse attention method satisfies: **token selection must be (a) aggregated across heads to capture global importance and (b) include a proportional allocation to recent context that adapts to both the token budget and the generation length.** LessIsMore is the direct implementation of these requirements.

#### Attention Recall as the Diagnostic Metric

Before presenting the method, the paper formalizes the metric used to evaluate sparse attention quality: **attention recall** (Section 2.1). This metric quantifies how much of the full attention distribution is preserved when only a subset of tokens is attended to, and it serves as the paper's primary diagnostic for understanding why prior methods fail on reasoning.

The standard attention computation for a single head `$i$` is:

$$W_i = \frac{Q_i K_i^\top}{\sqrt{d}}, \quad O_i = \text{softmax}(W_i) V_i$$

where `$Q_i$` is the query tensor for head `$i$`, `$K_i$` is the key tensor, `$V_i$` is the value tensor, and `$d$` is the head dimension. `$W_i$` is a vector of unnormalized attention scores (one per token in the KV cache), `$\text{softmax}(W_i)$` converts these to a probability distribution, and `$O_i$` is the weighted sum of value vectors.

**What this computes:** for each query token, the model computes a similarity score against every key in the cache (the dot product, scaled by `$\sqrt{d}$` to control variance), normalizes these scores into a probability distribution via softmax, and produces the output as a weighted combination of the corresponding value vectors. The softmax ensures attention weights sum to 1, so the output is a convex combination of all value vectors.

Sparse attention approximates this by selecting a subset `$\rho$` of token indices (with `$|\rho| = k$`, where `$k$` is the token budget) and computing attention only over those tokens. The attention recall for head `$i$` is defined as:

$$R_i = \frac{\sum(\text{softmax}(W_i)[\rho])}{\sum(\text{softmax}(W_i))}$$

where `$\text{softmax}(W_i)[\rho]$` extracts the softmax entries corresponding to the selected indices `$\rho$`, and the denominator sums to approximately 1 (exactly 1 if the full softmax is computed, though in practice approximate methods may normalize differently). `$R_i \in [0, 1]$` is the fraction of the total attention probability mass covered by the selected tokens.

**What it computes:** the numerator sums the softmax probabilities of only the tokens in `$\rho$`; the denominator sums the probabilities of all tokens. Since the denominator is approximately 1, `$R_i$` is effectively just the total attention mass allocated to the selected tokens. If `$\rho$` contains all the tokens that would have received high attention under full attention, `$R_i$` is close to 1. If `$\rho$` misses important tokens, `$R_i$` is lower.

**Why this form:** attention recall directly measures the quality of the sparse approximation. A recall of 0.9 means 90% of the attention mass is preserved; the remaining 10% is lost to unselected tokens. In retrieval tasks, losing 10% of attention mass might not matter because the answer typically depends on a few key passages. In reasoning, losing 10% at each step compounds: if the model makes 20,000 decoding steps and each step loses some fraction of the attention context, the cumulative information loss can cause the model to lose track of its reasoning chain entirely. This is why the paper emphasizes **cumulative recall** over the generation horizon (Figure 1a): it's not just per-step recall that matters, but how recall evolves as generation length increases.

The paper uses attention recall as a lightweight proxy for accuracy. Rather than running full benchmark evaluations for every design variant (which would be computationally prohibitive given the number of ablations), the paper measures recall on representative reasoning traces to compare selection strategies. Higher recall indicates better preservation of the full attention distribution, which is a necessary (though not sufficient) condition for preserving accuracy.

#### Cross-Head Unified Token Selection (CUSA)

CUSA is the central mechanism of LessIsMore. It replaces per-head independent top-k selection with a two-stage process: (1) each head proposes candidates, and (2) these candidates are aggregated across heads into a single globally ranked set. This is implemented at the designated token selection layer (Algorithm 1, lines 9–13).

**Step 1: Per-head proposal generation.** At the selection layer, the model first computes full attention to obtain the query-key product:

$$P = q \cdot C.K^\top$$

where `$q$` is the query tensor for the current token (shape: `$H \times d$`, where `$H$` is the number of attention heads and `$d$` is the head dimension), `$C.K$` is the key tensor for all tokens in the KV cache (shape: `$\text{seq_len} \times H_{\text{kv}} \times d$`, where `$H_{\text{kv}}$` is the number of key-value heads, which is smaller than `$H$` in GQA models), and `$P$` is the resulting attention score matrix (shape: `$H \times \text{seq_len}$`). Each entry `$P[h, t]$` represents how strongly query head `$h$` attends to cached token `$t$`.

Each attention head `$h$` independently selects its top-k candidates from the historical tokens (excluding the recency window), where `$k = K \cdot (1 - r)$`, `$K$` is the total token budget, and `$r$` is the recency window ratio:

$$\rho_{\text{head}}^{(h)} = \text{TopKIndices}\left(P[h, :-\!(K \cdot r)], \; k = K \cdot (1 - r)\right)$$

where `$P[h, :-\!(K \cdot r)]$` extracts the attention scores for head `$h$` over all tokens except the most recent `$K \cdot r$` positions (which are reserved for the recency window and therefore excluded from this competition), and `$\text{TopKIndices}$` returns the indices of the `$k$` highest-scoring positions.

**What this computes:** for each attention head independently, find the `$K \cdot (1 - r)$` historical tokens that receive the highest attention scores from that head's query. This is the same computation that TidalDecode performs, except TidalDecode would stop here and use these per-head sets directly. LessIsMore treats these as **proposals** to be aggregated.

**Step 2: Cross-head aggregation and unification.** The per-head proposals are combined into a single candidate pool:

$$\rho_{\text{candidates}} = \text{UnionFlatten}\left(\{\rho_{\text{head}}^{(h)}\}_{h=1}^H\right)$$

`$\text{UnionFlatten}$` takes the collection of `$H$` index sets (one per head), takes their union (removing duplicates), and flattens the result into a single list of unique token indices. The size of `$\rho_{\text{candidates}}$` is at most `$H \times k$` (if all heads propose disjoint sets) and at minimum `$k$` (if all heads propose identical sets). In practice, because of the observed cross-head overlap (Section 2.3), the union is much smaller than `$H \times k$`.

The candidates are then globally ranked. The paper does not specify the exact ranking criterion in the main text, but the natural choice (and what the recall results imply) is to rank by the number of heads that proposed each token—tokens proposed by many heads rank higher than tokens proposed by few heads. This implements the intuition that globally important tokens are those that multiple heads independently recognize as important. The top `$K \cdot (1 - r)$` tokens from this ranking are retained:

$$\rho_{\text{unified}} = \text{GlobalRank}(\rho_{\text{candidates}})[:\!K \cdot (1 - r)]$$

**Step 3: Combining with the recency window.** The final token index set is the concatenation of the globally selected historical tokens and the recency window:

$$\rho = \rho_{\text{unified}} \cup \rho_{\text{recent}}$$

where `$\rho_{\text{recent}} = \text{Recent}(K \cdot r)$` simply takes the indices of the `$K \cdot r$` most recently generated tokens. The union operator appends these indices; since the recency window tokens were excluded from the candidate pool in Step 1, there is no duplication.

**Why this three-step process over per-head independent selection:**

1. **Redundancy elimination.** In GQA models, multiple query heads share the same KV head. With per-head independent selection, if `$g$` query heads share one KV head, the KV cache must load up to `$g \times k$` distinct tokens for that KV group. In CUSA, all heads in the group share the same `$\rho$`, so only `$k$` tokens are loaded per KV group. This directly reduces memory bandwidth consumption and is the source of the kernel-level speedup (1.3×–1.72× over TidalDecode in Figure 6b).

2. **Global importance capture.** A token that ranks 17th for every head in an 8-head group (just outside the top-16 for each) would be excluded by per-head top-16 selection but receives 8 "votes" in the union—likely enough to place it in the global top-k. This token is globally important (all heads agree it matters) but locally invisible to per-head selection. CUSA captures it.

3. **Selection variance reduction.** Per-head selection amplifies noise: a token that briefly spikes in attention score for one head (perhaps due to a spurious correlation in the dot product) gets included in that head's set, consuming budget. In CUSA, such single-head spikes are diluted by the aggregation across all heads—only tokens with broad support across many heads survive the global ranking.

4. **KV cache access simplification.** When all heads share the same `$\rho$`, the KV cache loading logic is simpler: load one contiguous (or gather-scattered) set of tokens and broadcast to all heads. When heads have different `$\rho$` sets, each head (or KV group) requires a separate gather operation, increasing kernel launch overhead and memory transaction count. This is explicitly quantified in Table 4: LessIsMore transfers 1.04 MB from global to shared memory versus 2.34 MB for TidalDecode/Quest under the same FLOP count.

**The CUSA design is not an assumption that all heads are identical.** The paper is careful to note that CUSA does not force heads to attend to the same tokens because they would individually; rather, it exploits the empirically observed overlap to identify tokens that are **robustly useful** across heads. The per-head proposal stage preserves head-specific preferences—a token that head 3 cares about but head 7 ignores enters the candidate pool via head 3's proposal. The global ranking then selects the tokens with the broadest consensus, which the paper's recall measurements show is a better proxy for true importance than any single head's ranking.

#### Stable Recency Preservation

The recency window component of LessIsMore is conceptually simpler than CUSA but is carefully justified and integrated. The design choices are: (1) the recency window is **proportional** (a fixed fraction `$r$` of the budget `$K$`), not fixed-size; (2) the recency tokens are **unconditionally included** (they bypass the CUSA ranking); and (3) the recency tokens are **excluded from the CUSA candidate pool** (so they don't compete with historical tokens for the `$K \cdot (1 - r)$` non-recency slots).

**Why proportional over fixed-size.** A fixed-size recency window (e.g., always keep the most recent 1024 tokens, as in StreamingLLM) has two failure modes. First, at small token budgets, the fixed window might consume the entire budget (e.g., a 1024-token window with a 1024-token budget leaves zero budget for historical tokens), starving the model of long-range context. Second, at large token budgets, the fixed window might be too small relative to the total attention mass the model allocates to recent context—the model wants to attend to, say, the most recent 25% of tokens, but only 10% are preserved, degrading step-by-step coherence. A proportional window `$r \cdot K$` adapts automatically: at `$K = 2\text{K}$` with `$r = 0.25$`, the window is 512 tokens; at `$K = 8\text{K}$`, the window is 2048 tokens. The paper's ablation (Figure 8) empirically validates `$r = 0.25$` by testing `$r \in \{0\%, 25\%, 50\%, 75\%, 100\%\}$` and finding that 25% achieves the highest cumulative attention recall on AIME-24.

**Why unconditionally included.** The recency window bypasses the CUSA ranking because the temporal recency locality observation (Section 2.4) shows that recent tokens are **always** in the top-k for nearly all heads—the model will attend to them regardless. Including them in the CUSA candidate pool would just cause them to occupy slots that would otherwise go to historical tokens, and since they would nearly always win those slots anyway, the net effect is the same. Explicitly reserving the recency budget ensures that historical token selection is optimized over the non-recency portion of the cache without interference.

**Why excluded from the CUSA candidate pool.** The candidate pool in Step 1 of CUSA is constructed from `$P[:, :-\!(K \cdot r)]$`—the attention scores over all tokens **except** the most recent `$K \cdot r$`. This is a practical optimization: if recent tokens were included in the candidate pool, they would dominate the top-k selection (since they consistently receive high scores) and crowd out historical tokens. By excluding them, the CUSA ranking focuses on identifying which **historical** tokens are globally important, and the recency tokens are added back separately via the union.

**The stable recency window ratio `$r = 0.25$`** is used across all experiments and models without tuning per benchmark or model size. The paper's ablation (Appendix A.1.2, Figure 8) shows that `$r = 0.25$` (the LessIsMore configuration, labeled with a star) achieves the highest attention recall on AIME-24 compared to `$r = 0$` (CUSA with no recency, which improves recall over TidalDecode but still fails to reach the correct answer), `$r = 0.50$` and `$r = 0.75$` (which allocate too much budget to recent tokens at the expense of historical context), and `$r = 1.0$` (only recent tokens, which yields the lowest recall by discarding long-range context entirely). The 25% value emerges as the sweet spot: enough budget for recent context to maintain step-by-step coherence, but not so much that long-range reasoning dependencies are starved.

#### Layer Assignment Strategy: Full, Selection, and Sparse Layers

LessIsMore partitions the decoder layers into three types, following the layer-separation design introduced by TidalDecode but with a different selection mechanism at the designated layer (Algorithm 1, lines 4–17):

**Full attention layers (lines 7–8).** The first two decoder layers always compute standard dense attention over the entire KV cache. The paper states this is "to ensure accurate early-context modeling." The rationale (consistent with TidalDecode and other selection-based methods) is that early layers transform the raw token embeddings into initial contextual representations, and errors in these early representations would propagate through all subsequent layers. Using full attention in the first two layers is a small cost (since these layers process the prompt and early generation tokens, when the KV cache is still relatively small) that provides a stable foundation for the sparse attention that follows.

**Token selection layer (lines 9–15).** A single designated layer performs the CUSA computation described above. The choice of **which** layer serves as the selection layer is model-specific and determined by a preliminary needle-in-the-haystack test (Appendix A.5): for each model, the paper sweeps potential selection layers and chooses the one that maximizes retrieval accuracy with a 256-token budget at 5K context length. The chosen layers are: Layer 12 for Qwen3-8B, Qwen3-14B, and DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B; Layer 20 for Qwen3-4B; Layer 13 for Llama-3-8B and Llama-3.1-8B; Layer 7 for LongChat-7B-v1.5-32k. Table 10 summarizes these assignments.

**Why this layer selection procedure works.** The needle-in-the-haystack test identifies layers where the attention patterns have stabilized enough that a single snapshot of token importance remains valid for subsequent layers. Earlier layers have more volatile attention patterns (still forming representations); later layers have more specialized patterns that may not generalize. Layer 12 in the Qwen3-8B model (a roughly middle layer in the 32-layer stack) balances these considerations. The paper validates this choice empirically in Table 11: on AIME-24 with Qwen3-8B, LessIsMore with selection at Layer 12 achieves 73.00% accuracy at 2K budget versus 58.02% at Layer 5, 71.67% at Layer 18, and 63.23% at Layer 30, confirming that the needle-in-the-haystack heuristic correctly identifies the optimal selection layer for reasoning tasks as well.

**Sparse attention layers (line 16).** All layers after the selection layer (until the next selection layer, which in the typical configuration is none, since selection is performed only once per decoding step) compute attention only over the tokens indexed by `$\rho$`. The `SparseAttention` operation is:

$$O = \text{softmax}\left(\frac{q \cdot C.K[\rho]^\top}{\sqrt{d}}\right) \cdot C.V[\rho]$$

where `$C.K[\rho]$` and `$C.V[\rho]$` are the key and value tensors restricted to the indices in `$\rho$`. This reduces the attention computation from `$O(\text{seq_len})$` to `$O(K)$` per head, and the KV cache access from loading `$\text{seq_len}$` vectors to loading `$K$` vectors.

**Infrequent re-selection (Section 3.4).** A critical design choice that distinguishes LessIsMore from methods that re-select at every layer is that selection is performed **only once per decoding step** at the designated selection layer. The same `$\rho$` is reused across all subsequent sparse attention layers in the same decoding step. This is possible because CUSA's cross-head aggregation produces token indices that are globally consistent—valid across multiple layers—whereas per-head selection produces indices tuned to a specific layer's attention patterns and degrades when reused elsewhere.

The paper validates this empirically in Figure 4 (which in the paper's formatting appears as a recall comparison figure in Section 3.4). Three selection strategies are compared: (1) "LessIsMore (L2)" applies CUSA only at Layer 2 and reuses the indices for all subsequent layers; (2) "LessIsMore (All)" applies CUSA at every layer (re-selecting at each layer); (3) "Head-to-Head (L2)" applies per-head independent selection only at Layer 2 and reuses those head-specific indices; (4) "Randomized Top-k (L2)" randomly assigns one head's top-k to the entire KV group only at Layer 2. The results show that LessIsMore (L2) maintains recall comparable to LessIsMore (All)—the globally consistent indices remain valid across layers—while Head-to-Head (L2) and Randomized Top-k (L2) degrade significantly, with recall dropping from near-1.0 to below 0.7 over the generation horizon. This confirms that **global consistency is what enables infrequent re-selection**; head-local selection is too brittle to reuse.

The practical benefit of infrequent re-selection is amortization of selection cost. Computing full attention at the selection layer costs `$O(\text{seq_len} \cdot d)$` operations, which is expensive at long context lengths. By performing this computation only once (or a small number of times) per decoding step and reusing the result across many layers, the amortized cost per layer is negligible. The paper explicitly notes that this design is inherited from TidalDecode—what LessIsMore adds is the global consistency that makes the reused indices more robust.

#### Integration with GQA and Kernel-Level Optimizations

While CUSA is architecture-agnostic, the paper designs kernel-level optimizations specifically for GQA (Grouped Query Attention) models, which all evaluated reasoning models use. GQA models have `$H$` query heads but only `$H_{\text{kv}} < H$` key-value heads, with groups of `$g = H / H_{\text{kv}}$` query heads sharing each KV head.

**The kernel bottleneck in per-head selection.** In TidalDecode and Quest, each query head (or each KV group, depending on the implementation) selects its own token subset. When loading the KV cache for attention computation, if different query heads within a KV group have selected different token subsets, the kernel must either (a) load the union of all subsets for that KV group (paying the memory cost of the union while each head only uses its subset) or (b) perform separate gather operations for each head (paying multiple kernel launches or sequential memory transactions). Both options increase global-to-shared memory transfer compared to a scenario where all heads in a KV group share the same token subset.

**CUSA's kernel advantage.** Because CUSA produces a single `$\rho$` shared by all heads, every KV group loads exactly the same set of tokens: `$\rho$` restricted to the positions relevant to that group's KV head. This means a single gather operation suffices per KV group, and the loaded tokens are broadcast to all query heads in the group. The memory transfer is `$K \times d \times H_{\text{kv}}$` rather than up to `$K \times d \times H$` (if heads in a group select disjoint subsets, the union size grows with `$g$`).

Table 4 quantifies this advantage: at 2K token budget and 16K context length on DeepSeek-R1-Distilled-LLaMA-8B, LessIsMore transfers 1.04 MB from global to shared memory per attention kernel versus 2.34 MB for TidalDecode and Quest—a 2.25× reduction in memory traffic—despite identical FLOP counts (1.05M). This directly translates to the 1.3×–1.72× kernel-level speedup in Figure 6b.

**End-to-end speedup.** Figure 6a shows the end-to-end time-between-token (TBT) speedup of LessIsMore over full attention on DeepSeek-R1-Distilled-LLaMA-8B with one A100 GPU. The speedups are 1.09× at 16K context (4K budget), 1.27× at 32K context, and 1.58× at 64K context. At 2K budget, the speedups are slightly higher: 1.10×, 1.27×, and 1.55× respectively. The speedup increases with context length because the relative cost of full attention grows linearly with sequence length while sparse attention cost remains constant at the token budget `$K$`.

**SGLang integration latency.** Table 1 reports end-to-end per-token decoding latency when integrating LessIsMore into the SGLang serving stack with FlashInfer kernels on a single A5000 GPU. At 64K context with a 2K budget, LessIsMore achieves 24.1 ms/token versus 34.4 ms/token for full attention (1.43× speedup), 25.4 ms for TidalDecode, and 24.8 ms for Quest. The gap over TidalDecode and Quest is modest at these latency levels because the kernel-level advantage (reduced memory transfer) is partially offset by the cost of the CUSA aggregation logic in the selection layer. However, the critical advantage is that LessIsMore achieves these latencies while **preserving reasoning accuracy**, whereas TidalDecode and Quest degrade significantly (Figure 5, Section 4.2).

#### Attention Sinks

LessIsMore reserves four tokens as **attention sinks** in all experiments. Attention sinks are a phenomenon documented in prior work (StreamingLLM, Xiao et al., 2023) where the first few tokens of a sequence receive disproportionately high attention scores regardless of their semantic content—the model uses them as a "dump" for excess attention mass. StreamingLLM showed that preserving these initial tokens is critical for maintaining model stability under sparse attention. LessIsMore inherits this practice: four initial tokens are always included in `$\rho$` in addition to the unified and recency selections, consuming a negligible fraction of the total budget (4 tokens out of 2K–8K, or 0.05%–0.2%).

#### Algorithm Summary: End-to-End Decoding Step

Bringing all components together, a single decoding step with LessIsMore proceeds as follows (Algorithm 1):

1. **Input:** the hidden state `$h$` from the previous layer (or the embedding for the first layer), the current KV cache `$C$`, the token budget `$K$`, and the recency ratio `$r$`.

2. **For each decoder layer `$i$`:**
   - Project `$h$` to query, key, and value: `$q, k, v = f(W_{qkv}, h)$`.
   - Append the new key and value to the KV cache: `$C.\text{append}(k, v)$`.
   - **If layer `$i$` is a full attention layer** (first 2 layers): compute full attention `$o = \text{FullAttention}(q, C[:])$` over the entire KV cache.
   - **Else if layer `$i$` is the token selection layer** (e.g., Layer 12): compute full attention to obtain the query-key product `$P = q \cdot C.K^\top$`. Each head proposes its top `$K \cdot (1 - r)$` tokens from the non-recency portion of the cache: `$\rho_{\text{head}}^{(h)} = \text{TopKIndices}(P[h, :-(K \cdot r)], k = K \cdot (1 - r))$`. The proposals are unioned and flattened: `$\rho_{\text{unified}}, \rho_{\text{recent}} = \text{UnionFlatten}(\{\rho_{\text{head}}^{(h)}\}), \text{Recent}(K \cdot r)$`. The final index set is assembled: `$\rho = \rho_{\text{unified}}[:K \cdot (1 - r)] \cup \rho_{\text{recent}}$`.
   - **Else** (sparse attention layer): compute sparse attention `$o = \text{SparseAttention}(q, C[\rho])$` using the index set `$\rho$` from the most recent selection layer.
   - Apply the feed-forward network: `$h = \text{FFN}(o)$`.

3. **Output:** the logits `$\text{lm_head}(h)$` after the final layer, from which the next token is sampled.

#### Design Choices: Why These, and Not Alternatives

**Why training-free over training-required.** SeerAttention-r (Gao et al., 2025) requires post-training adaptation (continued pretraining or fine-tuning with sparse attention masks) to recover reasoning accuracy that is lost when naively applying sparse attention to reasoning models. This is expensive (requires retraining for each model and sparsity level), brittle (the adapted model may not generalize to different sparsity levels or tasks), and requires access to training data and compute that practitioners may not have. LessIsMore is plug-and-play: it works on any pretrained reasoning model without modification. The paper's results show that LessIsMore at 2K–4K budgets matches or exceeds the accuracy of full-attention models across benchmarks (Tables 5 and 6), while SeerAttention-r at similar budgets shows accuracy degradation (Figure 5, AIME-24: SeerAttention-r at 2K achieves approximately 58% vs. LessIsMore at 73% and Full Attention at 74%).

**Why selection-based over eviction-based.** Eviction methods (StreamingLLM, H2O, SnapKV) permanently delete tokens from the KV cache. In reasoning, the model may need to reference an intermediate conclusion generated 10,000 tokens ago that seemed unimportant at the time. Eviction makes this impossible. Selection-based methods retain the full KV cache and dynamically choose what to attend to—the same token can be selected at one step and not at another step, adapting to the model's changing information needs. The paper's Figure 1a shows that StreamingLLM (an eviction method) achieves the lowest attention recall (<65%) on AIME-24, consistent with its inability to recover evicted tokens.

**Why cross-head unified over per-head independent.** This is the paper's central design claim, validated by the recall comparisons in Figure 4 (Section 3.4) and the ablation in Figure 7 (Appendix A.1.1). Per-head independent selection: (a) loads redundant tokens when heads agree (increasing memory traffic), (b) misses globally important tokens that are top-(k+1) for every head, (c) produces indices that are layer-specific and cannot be reused across layers without degradation, and (d) amplifies selection variance from head-specific noise. CUSA addresses all four issues by aggregating across heads before finalizing the selection, producing a single globally consistent `$\rho$` that is more robust to reuse, more memory-efficient, and better at capturing true token importance.

**Why proportional recency window over fixed-size.** A fixed-size window fails at both extremes of the token budget: it starves historical context at small budgets and starves recency context at large budgets. The observation that the proportion of attention mass on recent tokens is stable (Section 2.4) directly implies that a proportional allocation is correct. The ablation in Figure 8 empirically confirms that `$r = 0.25$` outperforms all alternatives.

**Why a single selection layer over selection at every layer.** The recall stability demonstrated in Figure 4 (where "LessIsMore (L2)" matches "LessIsMore (All)") justifies this choice: if the globally consistent indices remain valid across layers, re-selection at every layer is wasted computation. The practical benefit is that the expensive full-attention computation at the selection layer is amortized over many sparse attention layers. For a 32-layer model with selection at Layer 12, the selection cost is paid once per decoding step, and 20 subsequent layers (Layers 13–32) benefit from the sparse indices without any additional scoring cost.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Global Token Importance as a Measured Property of Reasoning Attention, Not a Design Assumption

The paper's most fundamental conceptual move is its approach to designing sparse attention for reasoning: rather than proposing a new selection heuristic and then measuring whether it preserves accuracy, the paper first **measures what reasoning attention actually looks like** and derives its design from those measurements. This flips the standard methodology in sparse attention research, where methods are typically designed based on assumptions about attention structure (e.g., "recent tokens matter most," "different heads specialize") and then validated on benchmarks. LessIsMore's design choices—cross-head unified selection, proportional recency window, infrequent re-selection—are presented not as heuristics that happen to work well, but as **direct consequences of empirically observed, stable properties of reasoning attention patterns.**

What makes this distinctive as an idea—rather than just good engineering—is that the paper identifies two structural properties of reasoning attention that contradict the assumptions underlying all prior sparse attention methods:

**Cross-head spatial locality contradicts the head specialization assumption.** The dominant assumption in sparse attention design, encoded in methods like TidalDecode and Quest, is that attention heads serve distinct roles and require independent token subsets. This assumption is natural: it aligns with the extensive interpretability literature showing that attention heads specialize in syntactic patterns, semantic relations, positional offsets, etc. TidalDecode explicitly performs per-head top-k selection based on this assumption, and Quest extends it with head-specific quantization. The paper's Figure 2 visualization directly challenges this: at a given decoding step on AIME-24, the ground-truth top-4K tokens across all 32 attention heads in Qwen3-8B show **substantial overlap**—large blocks of token positions are selected by all or most heads simultaneously. This is not a transient artifact; Appendix Figures 9 and 10 demonstrate the same pattern persists across decoding steps, across tasks (AIME-25, GPQA-Diamond), and across token budgets (1K, 2K, 4K, 6K). The implication is that during reasoning, attention heads are not operating independently—they converge on a shared set of globally important tokens. Per-head independent selection therefore optimizes a local objective (finding the best tokens for each head individually) that is misaligned with the global structure of reasoning attention (finding the tokens that are collectively important across heads).

**Temporal recency locality as a stable proportion, not a fixed size.** Prior methods that preserve recency context—most notably StreamingLLM's sliding window—use a fixed-size window (e.g., always keep the most recent 1024 tokens). This reflects an implicit assumption that the model's need for recent context is constant regardless of generation length or total attention budget. The paper's analysis shows this assumption is wrong for reasoning: as generation length grows from 10K to 25K tokens, the **proportion** of attention mass allocated to recent tokens remains approximately stable, not the absolute count. This means a fixed-size window allocates too much budget to recent context at short generation lengths (wasting slots that could capture important historical tokens) and too little at long generation lengths (failing to preserve the full recency state the model needs). The proportional window in LessIsMore (25% of the total budget) adapts automatically: at 2K total budget, 512 tokens are recent; at 8K budget, 2048 tokens are recent.

What elevates these observations from descriptive analysis to an innovation is that the paper **uses them to explain why prior methods fail** on reasoning. The diagnostic framework—attention recall measured cumulatively over the generation horizon (Figure 1a)—shows that TidalDecode and StreamingLLM maintain high recall on short-generation retrieval tasks (Figure 1b) but progressively degrade on reasoning. The degradation is not random; it is a direct consequence of the mismatch between their local, head-specific, fixed-size design assumptions and the global, shared, proportional structure of reasoning attention. This transforms the paper's contribution from "we built a better sparse attention method" to "we characterized a failure mode of existing methods and derived a principled solution from that characterization."

A subtle but important aspect of this innovation: the paper's design is **falsifiable** in a way that heuristic methods are not. If reasoning attention did not exhibit cross-head overlap, CUSA would reduce to per-head selection (the union of per-head sets would be disjoint, and the global ranking would simply reproduce the per-head rankings). If the recency proportion were not stable, the fixed 25% ratio would be suboptimal for some generation lengths or budgets. The paper validates both properties empirically before committing to the design, making the architecture a consequence of measurement rather than trial-and-error hyperparameter search.

### Innovation 2: Infrequent, Globally Consistent Token Selection as an Enabler of Both Accuracy and Efficiency

A second distinctive contribution is the insight that **global consistency in token selection enables infrequent re-selection without recall degradation**, and that this dual property—accuracy through consistency, efficiency through amortization—is uniquely achievable when token importance is treated as a global property. This insight challenges a implicit assumption in selection-based sparse attention: that accurate token selection requires frequent re-optimization (at every layer, or at least at every few layers) because attention patterns shift across the depth of the model.

The paper's counter-demonstration is Figure 4 (Section 3.4), which compares attention recall when selection is performed only at Layer 2 (infrequent) versus at every layer (frequent). Three selection strategies are tested: LessIsMore (CUSA with cross-head unification), Head-to-Head (per-head independent top-k), and Randomized Top-k (assigning one random head's top-k to the entire KV group). The result is striking: LessIsMore maintains **nearly identical recall** whether selection is performed only at Layer 2 or at every layer—the unified indices remain valid across the entire layer stack. In contrast, Head-to-Head and Randomized Top-k show severe recall degradation under infrequent selection, dropping from near-1.0 to below 0.7 over the generation horizon when selection is performed only at Layer 2.

What makes this more than a convenient engineering property is the conceptual implication: **global consistency is what makes tokens' importance stable across layers, not layer-specific optimization.** Per-head selection produces indices that are tuned to a specific layer's attention distribution; when reused at a different layer with different attention patterns, the mismatch causes recall loss. CUSA's cross-head aggregation produces indices reflecting a consensus across heads—tokens that are broadly important, not just locally top-scoring—and this consensus signal generalizes across layers because it captures something structural about the token's role in the reasoning chain rather than a layer-specific attention artifact.

This has a direct practical consequence that the paper exploits: the expensive full-attention computation at the selection layer (which costs O(seq_len × d) operations) is performed only once per decoding step, and its output is reused across all subsequent layers. For a 32-layer model with selection at Layer 12, this means 20 layers of attention computation benefit from the selection without paying any scoring cost. Methods that re-select at every layer would pay this cost 32 times per decoding step, making them substantially slower even if their per-layer sparse attention is equally fast. The paper's efficiency results (Figure 6a, Table 3) show that this amortization, combined with CUSA's kernel-friendly unified index structure, delivers 1.1×–1.6× end-to-end speedups over full attention, with the gap widening at longer context lengths.

This innovation is best understood as a redefinition of what "optimal" token selection means in the context of sparse attention for reasoning. Prior methods optimize for **instantaneous recall**—the best possible token subset for the current layer's current attention distribution. LessIsMore optimizes for **sustained recall**—a token subset that remains valid across layers and decoding steps, trading a small amount of per-layer precision for substantial gains in stability and amortized efficiency. The empirical finding that this tradeoff is strongly favorable (LessIsMore matches or exceeds full attention accuracy while attending to far fewer tokens; Tables 5 and 6) validates the redefinition: sustained recall is what matters for long-horizon reasoning, and global consistency is how to achieve it.

### Innovation 3: The Diagnostic Framework of Cumulative Attention Recall as a Lightweight Proxy for Reasoning Accuracy

The paper introduces and validates **cumulative attention recall over the generation horizon** as a diagnostic metric that predicts sparse attention's impact on reasoning accuracy without requiring full benchmark evaluations. This is a methodological innovation—not a new model architecture or training procedure, but a measurement framework that makes sparse attention design for reasoning tractably testable.

The problem this solves is practical but profound: evaluating a sparse attention method's effect on reasoning accuracy requires running full benchmark evaluations with multiple sampled solutions per problem, which is computationally expensive (64 traces per problem for AIME-24/25, 8 for MATH500, 16 for GPQA-Diamond). Ablation studies—varying the recency ratio, the selection layer, the aggregation strategy—would be combinatorially infeasible if every configuration required a full accuracy evaluation. Attention recall provides a cheap, continuous, per-step signal that correlates with downstream accuracy: if recall degrades over the generation horizon, accuracy will eventually degrade; if recall remains stable, accuracy is likely preserved.

What makes this an innovation rather than an obvious metric is the paper's demonstration that **the relationship between recall and accuracy is fundamentally different for reasoning versus retrieval**. Figure 1b shows TidalDecode maintaining high recall across varying context lengths on needle-in-the-haystack retrieval, yet Figure 1a shows the same method with the same token budget suffering progressive recall degradation on AIME-24 reasoning. The difference is generation length: retrieval tasks involve short generations (typically a single decoding step or a few steps to output the needle), so per-step recall errors don't have time to compound. Reasoning tasks involve thousands of consecutive decoding steps, each building on the context established by previous steps. A small recall gap at step 100 becomes a moderate gap by step 1000 and a severe gap by step 10,000 as the model repeatedly operates on degraded context.

This insight reframes the evaluation of sparse attention methods: a method that achieves 95% per-step recall on a retrieval benchmark might be genuinely adequate for that task, but the same 95% recall compounded over 20,000 reasoning steps could mean the model has effectively lost access to most of its context by the end of the trace. The paper's diagnostic framework captures this compounding effect by measuring **cumulative** rather than **average** recall over the generation trajectory (Figure 1a), showing how recall evolves as a function of generation length.

The practical impact of this innovation is visible throughout the paper's ablation studies. Figure 8 uses attention recall to compare different recency window ratios on a single AIME-24 problem, avoiding the need to run a full 64-trace benchmark for each ratio. Figures 4 and 7 similarly use recall to evaluate selection strategies and aggregation methods. This enables a much more thorough empirical analysis than would be feasible with accuracy alone, and it provides the paper with a principled basis for its design choices: CUSA with 25% recency window is selected not because it happened to win a hyperparameter sweep, but because it maximizes cumulative attention recall, which the paper has established as a necessary condition for preserving reasoning accuracy.

### Innovation 4: The Finding That Sparse Attention Induces Generation Length Inflation, and That Global Selection Prevents It

A finding that runs through the paper's experimental results but is never elevated to a named contribution is the observation that **inaccurate sparse attention causes reasoning models to generate longer traces**—sometimes dramatically longer—and that LessIsMore's globally consistent selection prevents this inflation. This is a significant empirical finding with implications for how the field evaluates sparse attention methods: efficiency comparisons based solely on per-token speedup can be misleading if the method being compared causes the model to generate more tokens.

Table 2 quantifies this effect on AIME-24 with Qwen3-8B. Under a restrictive 2K token budget, Quest generates 30.0K tokens on average versus 14.8K for full attention—a 103% increase. SeerAttention-r generates 19.8K (34% increase). TidalDecode generates 17.4K (18% increase). LessIsMore generates 15.8K—only 7% longer than full attention—while achieving higher accuracy (73.8% vs. 74.5%) than any other sparse method at that budget. At a 4K budget, the pattern is even clearer: Quest generates 22.9K (55% increase) with 46.7% accuracy; TidalDecode generates 16.9K (14% increase) with 70.0% accuracy; LessIsMore generates 14.8K (identical to full attention) with 75.8% accuracy—actually exceeding full attention's 74.5%.

The mechanism behind this inflation is suggested by the paper's analysis of attention recall: when sparse attention discards tokens that the model needs for coherent reasoning, the model does not simply produce wrong answers in the same number of steps. Instead, it generates additional reasoning steps—re-deriving information it can no longer access, circling back to earlier conclusions that have been lost from context, or producing redundant verification steps because it is uncertain about its own reasoning state. This inflation partially offsets the per-token speedup of sparse attention: a method that achieves 1.5× per-token speedup but generates 50% more tokens yields only a 1.0× net speedup (no improvement) at potentially lower accuracy.

LessIsMore avoids this inflation because its globally consistent selection preserves the tokens the model needs to maintain a coherent reasoning chain. The recency window ensures the model always has access to its immediately preceding conclusions. The CUSA selection ensures that historically important tokens—intermediate results, variable definitions, key logical pivots—remain accessible across layers and decoding steps. The result is that the model can follow its natural reasoning trajectory without the detours and repetitions that inaccurate sparse attention forces.

This finding has implications beyond LessIsMore. It suggests that **evaluating sparse attention methods on reasoning tasks requires measuring generation length**, not just per-step latency or accuracy. A method that appears faster in a kernel-level microbenchmark but causes length inflation may be net slower in end-to-end wall-clock time. The paper's end-to-end speedup measurements (Figure 6a, Table 3) account for this implicitly by measuring time-between-token on actual generation traces, but the explicit analysis in Table 2 makes the mechanism visible. It also suggests a diagnostic signal: if a sparse attention method causes generation length to increase relative to full attention, it is likely discarding information the model needs—even if per-step accuracy on some metric looks acceptable in early generation steps.

### Innovation 5: Reconciling the Tension Between Accuracy Preservation and Training-Free Operation for Reasoning

A practical contribution that carries conceptual weight is LessIsMore's demonstration that **training-free sparse attention can preserve or even improve reasoning accuracy at high sparsity levels**, contradicting the apparent necessity of training-required adaptation suggested by prior work. SeerAttention-r (Gao et al., 2025) showed that naive application of sparse attention to reasoning models caused substantial accuracy degradation, and proposed post-training adaptation (continued training with sparse attention masks) as the solution. This created an implicit narrative: reasoning attention is too complex and sensitive for heuristic selection; recovering accuracy under sparsity requires learning the sparsity pattern.

LessIsMore challenges this narrative. Across four model families (Qwen3-4B, -8B, -14B, and DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B) and four reasoning benchmarks (AIME-24, AIME-25, MATH500, GPQA-Diamond), LessIsMore matches or exceeds full attention accuracy while attending to only 2K–8K tokens (representing 75–87.5% sparsity at typical generation lengths of 15K–32K tokens). Tables 5 and 6 show that LessIsMore at a 2K budget achieves 73.00% on AIME-24 with Qwen3-8B versus 74.48% for full attention—a gap of 1.48 percentage points, well within sampling variance. At 4K, it achieves 75.56%, actually exceeding full attention. On MATH500 at 1K budget, LessIsMore achieves 93.35% versus 94.43% for full attention. These are lossless or near-lossless results achieved with a training-free method.

What makes this significant as an innovation is that it reframes the challenge from "can we learn to attend sparsely during reasoning?" to "can we identify the structural properties of reasoning attention that make sparse attention possible?" SeerAttention-r's approach—training the model to adapt to sparsity—is effective but expensive, model-specific, and potentially brittle to distribution shift. LessIsMore's approach—measuring reasoning attention patterns and enforcing consistency with those patterns—is cheap, architecture-agnostic, and principled. The fact that the latter approach substantially outperforms the former at similar sparsity levels (Figure 5: LessIsMore at 2K achieves ~73% on AIME-24 vs. SeerAttention-r at 2K achieving ~58% on Qwen3-8B) suggests that the structural approach captures something fundamental about reasoning attention that learned sparsity masks do not.

The paper does not claim that training-free is universally superior—the Appendix K experiment with ReST^{EM}-trained revision models (not in this paper, but referenced) shows that some training procedures can improve revision quality. But the finding that training-free global selection matches or exceeds training-required local selection at the same sparsity levels is a meaningful data point for the broader debate about whether sparse attention should be learned or engineered. LessIsMore's results suggest that for reasoning workloads specifically, the attention structure is regular enough that principled engineering can match learned adaptation, at least at the sparsity levels tested (2K–8K tokens).

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The paper evaluates on four reasoning benchmarks: AIME-24 and AIME-25 (competition math problems from the American Invitational Mathematics Examination; AoPS, 2025), MATH500 (500 problems from the MATH benchmark; Hendrycks et al., 2021, as used in Lightman et al., 2022), and GPQA-Diamond (graduate-level STEM proof problems; Rein et al., 2023). For long-context generalization, the paper also evaluates on LongBench (Bai et al., 2023) and needle-in-the-haystack retrieval using the PG-19-mini dataset (Rae et al., 2019).

- **Base model(s).** The primary reasoning experiments use four models from two families: Qwen3-4B, Qwen3-8B, and Qwen3-14B (Team, 2025) and DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B (DeepSeek-AI, 2025). All four use Grouped Query Attention (GQA). For long-context generalization beyond reasoning, the paper also evaluates on Llama-3-8B-Instruct-Gradient-1048k (AI, 2024a), Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct (AI, 2024b), and the MHA-based LongChat-7B-v1.5-32k (Li et al., 2023). The paper states these models are "specifically trained for reasoning tasks and perform the effective thinking process by generating extensive tokens" (Section 4.1), arguing they are representative of modern LRMs whose decode-heavy computational profile motivates efficient sparse attention.

- **Metrics.** The paper uses two classes of metrics. For accuracy evaluation on reasoning benchmarks, the primary metric is **Pass@1 accuracy (%)** — the fraction of problems for which the model's selected answer matches ground truth — computed over multiple independent traces per problem to reduce variance (Section 4.1: 64 traces for AIME-24/25, 8 for MATH500, 16 for GPQA-Diamond). For efficiency evaluation, the metrics are **time-between-token (TBT)** in milliseconds for end-to-end decoding latency (Figures 6a, Table 1, Table 3), **kernel-level latency** in microseconds for the attention computation alone (Figure 6b, Table 4), and **generation length** in tokens (Table 2) to measure length inflation. For diagnostic analysis, the paper uses **attention recall** (Equation 3, Section 2.1), defined as the fraction of full attention probability mass retained by the selected token subset, measured cumulatively over the generation horizon (Figures 1a, 4, 7, 8).

- **Baselines.** The paper compares against five baselines spanning both selection-based and eviction-based sparse attention, as well as training-required approaches:
  - **TidalDecode** (Yang et al., 2024): a selection-based method that separates token selection layers from sparse attention layers, performing per-head top-k selection at designated layers. LessIsMore inherits TidalDecode's layer-separation framework but replaces per-head selection with CUSA at the same selection layers.
  - **Quest** (Tang et al., 2024): a selection-based method that selects tokens based on approximate attention scores using quantized key vectors, with hybrid attention layers and block sizes of 16 or 32 depending on the model.
  - **SeerAttention-r** (Gao et al., 2025): a training-required method specifically designed for reasoning, using a block size of 64 with sparse attention applied at every layer after post-training adaptation.
  - **StreamingLLM** (Xiao et al., 2023): an eviction-based method that retains only attention sink tokens (initial tokens) plus a fixed-size sliding window of recent tokens, permanently discarding all others.
  - **Full Attention**: standard dense attention over the entire KV cache, serving as the accuracy upper bound.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The token budget `K` is the primary unit of compute: it specifies the maximum number of tokens from the KV cache that sparse attention loads and computes attention over at each decoding step. For reasoning benchmarks, `K` is swept across {1K, 2K, 4K, 6K, 8K} for AIME and GPQA-Diamond, and {1K, 2K, 4K, 6K} for MATH500 (Figure 5, Tables 5 and 6). At the evaluated generation lengths (typically 15K–32K tokens for AIME, 8K–18K for GPQA, 5K–16K for MATH500 under sparse methods), these budgets represent sparsity ratios of 75–93.75%. For efficiency evaluation, the budget is fixed at 2K or 4K tokens (Figures 6a, 6b, Tables 1 and 3). All methods use the same token budget for fair comparison; the paper explicitly notes that generation budgets do not account for the cost of difficulty estimation (unlike the reference example paper, LessIsMore has no difficulty estimation phase).

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not use cross-validation in the standard sense (there is no training step for LessIsMore itself). To reduce evaluation variance on reasoning benchmarks, the paper generates multiple independent traces per problem (64 for AIME, 8 for MATH500, 16 for GPQA) and reports Pass@1 accuracy aggregated over all traces. The selection layer choice for each model is determined by a preliminary needle-in-the-haystack test with a 256-token budget at 5K context length on PG-19-mini (Appendix A.5, Table 10)—a lightweight calibration step that does not use reasoning benchmark data. All models use the same prompts and a maximum generation length of 32K tokens (Section 4.1).

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Accuracy on Reasoning Benchmarks

**Headline result (Figure 5, Tables 5 and 6).** LessIsMore consistently matches or exceeds full attention accuracy across all four reasoning benchmarks and all four model families while attending to substantially fewer tokens. At the smallest token budgets where all baselines degrade significantly, LessIsMore maintains near-lossless accuracy.

**AIME-24 (Table 5, Figure 5).** On Qwen3-8B at a 2K token budget, LessIsMore achieves 73.00% accuracy versus 74.48% for Full Attention—a gap of only 1.48 percentage points. At 4K, LessIsMore achieves 75.56%, exceeding Full Attention by 1.08 points. In comparison, at 2K budget: Quest achieves 18.2%, TidalDecode achieves 53.3%, and SeerAttention-r achieves 58.2%—all substantially below both LessIsMore and Full Attention. The gap widens at higher budgets: at 6K, LessIsMore reaches 76.45% versus TidalDecode at 71.3% and Quest at 49.6%.

On Qwen3-4B at 2K, LessIsMore achieves 71.48% versus 71.25% for Full Attention (exceeding by 0.23 points). On Qwen3-14B at 2K, LessIsMore achieves 78.58% versus 79.79% for Full Attention (gap of 1.21 points). On DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B at 2K, LessIsMore achieves 43.22% versus 44.16% for Full Attention (gap of 0.94 points).

**AIME-25 (Table 5, Figure 5).** The pattern is consistent. On Qwen3-8B at 2K, LessIsMore achieves 65.24% versus 67.86% for Full Attention (gap of 2.62 points). At 4K, LessIsMore achieves 70.31%, exceeding Full Attention by 2.45 points. On Qwen3-14B at 4K, LessIsMore achieves 70.59% versus 70.52% for Full Attention—essentially matching.

**MATH500 (Table 6, Figure 5).** This benchmark, with shorter generation lengths (Full Attention averages ~5K tokens), shows LessIsMore achieving high accuracy even at very small budgets. On Qwen3-8B at 1K token budget, LessIsMore achieves 93.35% versus 94.43% for Full Attention. On DeepSeek-R1-8B at 1K, LessIsMore achieves 86.90% versus 85.45% for Full Attention—actually exceeding the baseline by 1.45 points. At 2K, LessIsMore achieves 88.15% versus 85.45%.

**GPQA-Diamond (Table 6, Figure 5).** On Qwen3-8B at 1K, LessIsMore achieves 58.62% versus 60.54% for Full Attention (gap of 1.92 points). At 2K, LessIsMore achieves 60.65% versus 60.54%—slightly exceeding Full Attention. On Qwen3-14B at 4K, LessIsMore achieves 64.85% versus 64.02% for Full Attention.

**Cross-model consistency.** Across all 16 model-benchmark pairs in Tables 5 and 6, LessIsMore at a 2K–4K budget matches or exceeds Full Attention accuracy in 12 of 16 cases. The four cases where LessIsMore is below Full Attention all show gaps of less than 3 percentage points, well within the expected variance from 64/8/16 traces. There is no case where LessIsMore accuracy collapses relative to Full Attention while maintaining the same budget, in contrast to all baselines which show substantial degradation (e.g., Quest at 2K on AIME-24: 18.2% vs. 74.5% for Full Attention).

#### Generation Length Inflation

**Headline result (Table 2, Appendix A.1.3).** Inaccurate sparse attention methods cause reasoning models to generate substantially longer traces than full attention, partially or fully negating their per-token speedup advantages. LessIsMore avoids this inflation, maintaining generation lengths nearly identical to Full Attention.

On AIME-24 with Qwen3-8B at a 2K token budget, average generation lengths are: Full Attention 14.8K tokens, LessIsMore 15.8K (+7%), TidalDecode 17.4K (+18%), SeerAttention-r 19.8K (+34%), Quest 30.0K (+103%). At 4K budget: Full Attention 14.8K, LessIsMore 14.8K (+0%), TidalDecode 16.9K (+14%), SeerAttention-r 16.3K (+10%), Quest 22.9K (+55%). The paper notes that "combining with the average decoding latency in Figure 6a, LessIsMore achieves a 1.16× end-to-end speedup compared to TidalDecode" (Appendix A.1.3), accounting for both per-token speed and length differences.

The length inflation is most severe at the smallest budgets and diminishes as the budget increases: at 6K, TidalDecode generates 15.9K (+7%), Quest generates 19.6K (+32%), and LessIsMore generates 15.1K (+2%). This is consistent with the attention recall analysis: as the token budget approaches the generation length, selection errors decrease, and the model no longer needs to compensate with additional reasoning steps.

#### Efficiency Evaluation

**Headline result (Figure 6, Tables 1, 3, and 4).** LessIsMore delivers up to 1.6× end-to-end decoding speedup over Full Attention at 64K context length, with kernel-level sparse attention 1.3×–1.72× faster than TidalDecode due to reduced memory transfer from unified token selection.

**End-to-end speedup (Figure 6a, Table 3).** On DeepSeek-R1-Distilled-LLaMA-8B with one 80GB NVIDIA A100 GPU, LessIsMore at a 4K token budget achieves 1.09× speedup at 16K context, 1.24× at 32K, and 1.55× at 64K relative to Full Attention. At a 2K budget, speedups are 1.10×, 1.27×, and 1.58× respectively. The speedup increases with context length because Full Attention cost scales linearly with sequence length (O(seq_len)) while sparse attention cost is constant at O(K). The paper projects that this advantage will scale further as reasoning models increasingly support generation lengths beyond 100K tokens.

**Kernel-level speedup (Figure 6b, Table 4).** Comparing sparse attention kernel latency between LessIsMore and TidalDecode at identical token budgets (using FlashInfer as the backend on DeepSeek-R1-Distilled-LLaMA-8B): at 512-token budget, LessIsMore achieves ~9.5 µs versus ~12.3 µs for TidalDecode (1.30× faster); at 1024, ~13.7 µs versus ~20.6 µs (1.50×); at 1536, ~16.2 µs versus ~27.8 µs (1.72×); at 2048, ~20.1 µs versus ~32.1 µs (1.60×). The speedup increases with budget size because the memory transfer savings (loading unified indices vs. per-head indices) scale with the number of tokens loaded.

Table 4 breaks down the kernel-level metrics at 2K budget and 16K context: LessIsMore transfers 1.04 MB from global to shared memory versus 2.34 MB for TidalDecode/Quest—a 2.25× reduction—while both methods have identical FLOP counts (1.05M) and on-device memory consumption (8.38 MB). StreamingLLM matches LessIsMore's memory transfer (1.04 MB) but "performs poorly on reasoning accuracy due to its static attention structure." Full Attention requires 8.40M FLOPs and 8.38 MB transfer, resulting in 76.4 µs latency—3.8× slower than LessIsMore's 20.1 µs.

**SGLang integration latency (Table 1).** When integrated into the SGLang serving stack with FlashInfer on a single NVIDIA A5000 GPU, LessIsMore at a 2K token budget achieves per-token decoding latencies of 23.0 ms (16K context), 23.4 ms (32K), and 24.1 ms (64K). TidalDecode achieves 24.3 ms, 24.7 ms, and 25.4 ms respectively. Quest achieves 24.2 ms, 24.4 ms, and 24.8 ms. Full Attention achieves 25.3 ms, 28.4 ms, and 34.4 ms. At 64K context, LessIsMore is 1.43× faster than Full Attention and modestly faster than TidalDecode (1.05×) and Quest (1.03×). The modest gap over other sparse methods at this serving stack level reflects that end-to-end latency includes non-attention components (FFN, embedding, sampling) that are identical across sparse attention methods.

#### Long-Context Generalization

**Headline result (Tables 7, 8, and 9; Appendix A.4).** LessIsMore's CUSA mechanism generalizes beyond reasoning to standard long-context retrieval and comprehension tasks, matching or exceeding baselines on both GQA and MHA model architectures.

**Needle-in-the-haystack (Tables 7 and 8).** On Llama-3-8B-Instruct-Gradient-1048k with 100K context, LessIsMore achieves 100% accuracy at a 128-token budget (0.13% of the input) and 98% at 32 tokens. In comparison, Quest at 128 tokens achieves only 65%, and TidalDecode reaches 100% at 256 tokens (Table 7). On the MHA-based LongChat-7B-v1.5-32k with 10K context, LessIsMore achieves 100% accuracy at 256 tokens, matching TidalDecode and Quest, and substantially outperforming eviction-based methods (H2O: 1%, StreamingLLM: 3%, TOVA: 3% at 256 tokens; Table 8). At extreme sparsity (32-token budget), LessIsMore achieves 92% accuracy versus 73% for TidalDecode and 65% for Quest.

**LongBench (Table 9).** On five LongBench datasets (MultiFieldQA, Qasper, HotpotQA, TriviaQA, PassageRetrieval) testing long-context retrieval, multi-hop QA, and multi-document comprehension, LessIsMore at a 4K token budget achieves an average F1/accuracy of 44.78, exceeding Full Attention's 44.08, TidalDecode's 44.56, and Quest's 42.62. At a 1K budget, LessIsMore achieves 43.82, closely tracking Full Attention while substantially outperforming both TidalDecode (40.89) and Quest (39.29). The paper notes this demonstrates that LessIsMore "not only preserves accuracy in complicated reasoning tasks but also exhibits potential on solving long-context tasks" (Appendix A.4.2).

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Recency window ratio (Appendix A.1.2, Figure 8).** The paper ablates the recency window ratio `r` on AIME-24 with Qwen3-8B at a 4K token budget, comparing `r = 0%` (CUSA with no recency), `r = 25%` (the LessIsMore default), `r = 50%`, `r = 75%`, `r = 100%` (only recent tokens), and TidalDecode (which uses per-head selection with its own recency mechanism). The `r = 25%` configuration achieves the highest cumulative attention recall across the 32K-token generation horizon. `r = 100%` yields the lowest recall by discarding all historical context. `r = 0%` and TidalDecode improve recall over the `r = 100%` baseline but "still fails to reach the correct answer"—only configurations combining CUSA with a non-zero recency window (25%, 50%, 75%) successfully solve the task. Within these successful configurations, `r = 25%` maximizes recall, validating the proportional recency allocation.

**Cross-head aggregation strategy for GQA models (Appendix A.1.1, Figure 7).** The paper compares three aggregation strategies on Qwen3-8B: LessIsMore (CUSA with cross-head unified selection), Randomized Top-k (randomly assigning one query head's top-k to the entire KV group), and Head-to-Head (per-head independent top-k selection as in TidalDecode). Two regimes are tested: selection applied at all decoding layers (All) and selection applied only at Layer 2 (L2, the infrequent re-selection regime). Under the All regime, all three strategies achieve high recall, with Head-to-Head appearing competitive. Under the L2 regime, Head-to-Head and Randomized Top-k degrade substantially (recall drops from near-1.0 to below ~0.7 over 25K tokens), while LessIsMore maintains recall comparable to the All regime. The paper interprets this as evidence that "a globally consistent cross-head selection strategy is significantly more robust than layer-specific or head-specific methods" (Appendix A.1.1).

**Selection layer placement (Appendix A.5, Table 11).** The paper ablates the choice of re-selection layer on AIME-24 with Qwen3-8B at token budgets of 2K, 4K, 6K, and 8K. Configurations tested: no re-selection (selection only at the initial layers, never updated), re-selection at Layer 5, Layer 12 (the default), Layer 18, and Layer 30. Layer 12 consistently achieves the highest accuracy across all budgets: 73.00% at 2K versus 58.02% (no re-selection), 53.33% (L5), 71.67% (L18), and 63.23% (L30). The paper notes that "earlier or later layers consistently underperform compared to L12, indicating that re-selection must occur at a layer that balances sufficient semantic abstraction with stable attention patterns." The needle-in-the-haystack search used to identify Layer 12 for TidalDecode reliably predicts the optimal selection layer for LessIsMore as well.

**Effectiveness on MHA architectures (Appendix A.4.3, Table 8).** Although all primary reasoning models use GQA, the paper validates that CUSA extends to standard MHA models. On LongChat-7B-v1.5-32k (an MHA model) with 10K-context needle-in-the-haystack, LessIsMore achieves 100% accuracy at 256 tokens, matching TidalDecode and Quest, and 92% at 32 tokens—substantially above TidalDecode (73%) and Quest (65%). Eviction-based methods (H2O, TOVA, StreamingLLM) achieve only 1–3% at 256 tokens. This demonstrates that CUSA's cross-head unification is not dependent on the KV head sharing structure of GQA; it captures global token importance patterns that are effective under full MHA as well.

**Model scale robustness (Tables 5 and 6).** LessIsMore is evaluated across three scales of the Qwen3 family (4B, 8B, 14B) plus DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B. The accuracy preservation pattern is consistent across scales: at small budgets, LessIsMore closely tracks Full Attention across all model sizes. There is no evidence that the method's effectiveness degrades with model scale—on Qwen3-14B at 2K, LessIsMore achieves 78.58% on AIME-24 versus 79.79% for Full Attention, a gap of 1.21 points, comparable to the 1.48-point gap on Qwen3-8B.

**Benchmark diversity (Tables 5 and 6).** The method is evaluated on four benchmarks spanning competition mathematics (AIME-24/25), standard mathematics (MATH500), and graduate-level STEM reasoning (GPQA-Diamond). The accuracy preservation pattern holds across all four, with no benchmark showing systematic degradation relative to Full Attention. The consistency across different reasoning domains (pure math vs. interdisciplinary STEM) suggests the global attention structure CUSA exploits is a general property of chain-of-thought reasoning, not specific to mathematical derivations.

**Generation length analysis (Appendix A.1.3, Table 2).** The paper directly quantifies the length inflation caused by inaccurate sparse attention and demonstrates that LessIsMore avoids it. At 2K budget, Quest generates 103% more tokens than Full Attention, SeerAttention-r 34% more, and TidalDecode 18% more, while LessIsMore generates only 7% more. At 4K budget, LessIsMore generates exactly the same number of tokens as Full Attention (14.8K). The paper frames this as an implicit validation of attention recall: methods with lower recall (Figure 1a) exhibit greater length inflation, suggesting the model compensates for missing context by generating additional reasoning steps.

**Kernel-level FLOPs, memory transfer, and latency (Appendix A.2.1, Table 4).** The paper profiles the attention kernel at 2K budget and 16K context on DeepSeek-R1-Distilled-LLaMA-8B. All sparse attention methods have identical FLOPs (1.05M vs. 8.40M for Full Attention) and on-device memory consumption (8.38 MB vs. 8.38 MB). The differentiating factor is global-to-shared memory transfer: LessIsMore transfers 1.04 MB versus 2.34 MB for TidalDecode/Quest—a 2.25× reduction—because unified token selection means each KV group loads a single shared token set rather than separate sets for each query head. This directly translates to the 1.3×–1.72× kernel-level speedup in Figure 6b.

### Critical Assessment

**Claim 1: LessIsMore matches or exceeds full attention accuracy at high sparsity levels across multiple model families and reasoning benchmarks.**

This claim is well-supported by the experimental results in Figure 5 and Tables 5 and 6. Across 16 model-benchmark pairs and budget levels from 1K to 8K, LessIsMore consistently achieves accuracy within a few percentage points of Full Attention, and in several cases exceeds it (e.g., Qwen3-8B on AIME-24 at 4K: 75.56% vs. Full Attention 74.48%). The evaluation protocol is thorough—64 traces per problem for AIME, multiple budget levels, four model sizes—and the consistency across benchmarks and model scales supports the generality claim.

However, there are boundary conditions that the paper does not explicitly test. The maximum sparsity demonstrated is approximately 87.5% (2K budget at 16K generation length). It is unknown whether accuracy would be preserved at even higher sparsity (e.g., 512 tokens at 32K generation, or 93.75% sparsity). The paper's recall curves (Figure 1a) show LessIsMore maintaining recall near 1.0 at 4K budget on a ~32K trace, but this is a single trace, not a systematic sweep. The claim of "lossless accuracy" should be understood as "lossless at the tested sparsity levels," not "arbitrarily lossless."

Additionally, the evaluation uses a maximum generation length of 32K tokens (Section 4.1). Reasoning traces from state-of-the-art models can be substantially longer—the paper itself notes that "modern reasoning models—both open- and closed-source—already support generation lengths well beyond 100K tokens" (Section 4.3). Whether CUSA's globally consistent selection remains sufficient at 100K+ generation lengths is untested. The stable recency window (25% of budget) and cross-head unification are motivated by attention patterns observed at up to 32K tokens (Figures 2, 9, 10), but attention structure could shift at extreme lengths.

**Claim 2: Cross-head unified token selection (CUSA) is superior to per-head independent selection for reasoning, and this superiority derives from measured cross-head spatial locality in reasoning attention.**

This claim is supported by two types of evidence: (a) the visualization of cross-head overlap in Figure 2, showing substantial token selection overlap across attention heads, and (b) the recall comparison in Figures 4 and 7, showing that CUSA maintains higher recall than per-head selection, especially under infrequent re-selection. The ablation in Figure 7 is the strongest direct test: it isolates the CUSA aggregation strategy from other factors (recency window, layer placement) and shows that CUSA substantially outperforms per-head selection when selection is infrequent (L2 only).

However, the causal chain—"cross-head overlap is measured → CUSA exploits this overlap → CUSA outperforms per-head selection"—has a gap. The paper demonstrates that overlap exists and that CUSA works, but it does not directly measure how much of CUSA's advantage is attributable specifically to overlap exploitation versus other properties of the aggregation (e.g., the union operation reduces variance regardless of overlap). A useful ablation would be: what if the heads were artificially decorrelated (e.g., adding noise to the per-head scores before selection)? Would CUSA's advantage disappear? The paper does not run this experiment.

Additionally, the cross-head overlap analysis is conducted on a single model (Qwen3-8B) at a single layer (Layer 4) and primarily on AIME-24 (with supplementary figures for GPQA and AIME-25 in Figures 9 and 10). The claim that this property is general requires more evidence: different model families, different layers, and a quantitative measure of overlap (e.g., Jaccard similarity between head top-k sets) rather than qualitative visualization. The current evidence is suggestive rather than definitive.

**Claim 3: The stable recency window (proportional to budget, fixed fraction r = 0.25) is necessary for preserving reasoning coherence, and this design is justified by measured temporal recency locality.**

The ablation in Figure 8 provides strong evidence that `r = 0.25` is the optimal ratio among the tested values (`r ∈ {0%, 25%, 50%, 75%, 100%}`). The finding that `r = 0%` (no recency window) fails to reach the correct answer while `r = 25%` succeeds is compelling—it establishes that the recency window is not just helpful but necessary.

However, the evidence for the proportional (rather than fixed-size) design is less direct. The paper observes (Section 2.4, Figures 9 and 10) that the recency block grows with the token budget, but it does not systematically measure how the optimal recency ratio changes with generation length or budget. The ablation in Figure 8 tests different ratios at a single budget (4K) and generation length (~32K). Would `r = 0.25` still be optimal at a 1K budget? At an 8K budget? The paper does not vary the budget in the ablation.

The choice of `r = 0.25` is used uniformly across all experiments without per-model or per-benchmark tuning (Section 4.1, Appendix A.1.2), which is a strength for demonstrating robustness. But it also means the paper does not explore whether different tasks or model families might benefit from different ratios. The fixed 25% allocation could be suboptimal for some configurations, and the paper provides no evidence either way.

**Claim 4: LessIsMore achieves up to 1.6× end-to-end decoding speedup over Full Attention, with the speedup increasing at longer context lengths.**

This claim is well-supported by Figure 6a and Table 3. The speedup measurements are conducted on realistic hardware (A100 GPU for kernel-level, A5000 for SGLang integration), using production-grade attention kernel libraries (FlashInfer), and at context lengths (16K–64K) that match the generation lengths observed in the reasoning benchmarks. The increasing speedup with context length (1.09× at 16K, 1.55× at 64K) is consistent with the theoretical expectation that sparse attention cost is O(K) while full attention cost is O(seq_len).

However, the speedup numbers should be interpreted with the following caveats:

- **The measurements are on a single model (DeepSeek-R1-Distilled-LLaMA-8B).** Speedups may differ on other model architectures (different layer counts, hidden dimensions, GQA group sizes). The paper does not provide efficiency measurements for Qwen3 models, which constitute three of the four reasoning models evaluated.

- **The speedup is measured at a single GPU scale.** In multi-GPU inference (tensor parallelism, pipeline parallelism), attention computation is distributed differently, and the speedup from sparsity may not scale linearly with the single-GPU case. The paper does not evaluate distributed inference.

- **The 1.6× is the maximum observed speedup (64K context, 4K budget).** At shorter context lengths (16K), the speedup is only 1.1×—a more modest improvement. For workloads where reasoning traces average 15K–20K tokens (as reported for AIME-24 with Full Attention on Qwen3-8B), the practical speedup may be closer to 1.1×–1.2× rather than 1.6×. The 1.6× figure is best understood as a projection for very long generation traces (64K+), not the typical-case improvement.

- **The SGLang integration latencies (Table 1)** show LessIsMore achieving 1.43× speedup at 64K context over Full Attention, but only modest improvements over TidalDecode (1.05×) and Quest (1.03×) at the same budget. This means the primary efficiency advantage over other sparse methods is in accuracy preservation, not raw latency—LessIsMore achieves comparable or better latency while being the only sparse method to maintain reasoning accuracy.

**Claim 5: LessIsMore is training-free and generalizes across model architectures (GQA and MHA).**

The training-free claim is unambiguous: LessIsMore requires no fine-tuning, continued pretraining, or adaptation of the base model. This is validated by the fact that all experiments use off-the-shelf pretrained models.

The generalization claim is supported by the MHA experiment on LongChat-7B-v1.5-32k (Table 8), which shows LessIsMore achieving accuracy comparable to or better than selection-based baselines on needle-in-the-haystack. However, this experiment is limited to a single MHA model on a single task (retrieval, not reasoning). The paper does not evaluate LessIsMore on an MHA reasoning model, so the claim that CUSA generalizes to "MHA reasoning" is untested. The structural properties that motivate CUSA—cross-head spatial locality, temporal recency locality—were measured on GQA reasoning models. The paper does not demonstrate that these properties hold in MHA reasoning models, nor that CUSA would preserve reasoning accuracy under MHA.

**Missing experiments and ablations that would strengthen the paper:**

1. **Extreme sparsity limits.** At what token budget does LessIsMore's accuracy begin to degrade significantly? The paper tests down to 1K budget (which still represents, at typical generation lengths, only 3–6% of tokens). A sweep to lower budgets (512, 256, 128 tokens) would establish the sparsity frontier.

2. **Combined with other efficiency techniques.** LessIsMore targets attention computation exclusively. How does it interact with KV cache quantization, weight quantization, or speculative decoding? Combinations could yield multiplicative speedups but might also introduce new failure modes.

3. **Non-reasoning generation tasks.** The paper demonstrates generalization to long-context retrieval (Tables 7, 8, 9), but these tasks have short generation lengths (typically single-token or short answers). What about standard long-form generation (e.g., summarization, story generation) where generation length is long but the task is not step-by-step reasoning? LessIsMore's design is motivated by reasoning-specific attention patterns, so it may not generalize to non-reasoning long-form generation.

4. **Continuous or learned recency ratio.** The 25% recency window is fixed and universal. A learned or dynamic recency ratio (e.g., varying by layer, by generation step, or by estimated task difficulty) might improve recall further, analogous to the compute-optimal allocation in the reference example paper. The paper does not explore this.

5. **Attention recall as a predictor of accuracy.** The paper uses recall extensively for diagnostics and ablation (Figures 4, 7, 8) but never quantitatively correlates recall with downstream accuracy across configurations. A scatter plot or rank correlation showing that higher recall predicts higher accuracy would validate recall as a reliable proxy.

**Overall assessment.** The experiments provide strong evidence that LessIsMore preserves reasoning accuracy at high sparsity levels where all existing training-free and training-required baselines degrade significantly. The evaluation is thorough in breadth (multiple models, benchmarks, budget levels) and includes careful diagnostics (attention recall, generation length, kernel-level profiling) that explain *why* the method works. The primary limitations are: (a) the sparsity frontier is not fully explored (how far can LessIsMore go before degrading?), (b) the MHA generalization evidence is limited to retrieval, not reasoning, (c) efficiency measurements are on a single model family, and (d) the causal link between measured attention properties and CUSA's performance—while plausible and supported by ablations—has not been definitively isolated through counterfactual experiments (e.g., what if cross-head overlap were artificially reduced?).

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### Assumption: Token Selection Stability Persists Unboundedly as Generation Length Grows Beyond Evaluated Horizons

The paper's central design principle—that globally consistent token indices remain valid across many layers without re-selection—is validated empirically at generation lengths up to approximately 32K tokens (the maximum generation length used in all experiments, Section 4.1). The cumulative attention recall curves (Figures 1a, 4, 7, 8) show LessIsMore maintaining recall near 1.0 over these horizons, and the accuracy results (Tables 5 and 6) confirm that reasoning performance is preserved. However, the paper does not evaluate generation lengths beyond 32K, even as it explicitly notes that "modern reasoning models—both open- and closed-source—already support generation lengths well beyond 100K tokens" (Section 4.3).

**The consequence.** The claim that CUSA's globally consistent indices remain stable "across layers and decoding steps" (Section 3.4) is supported only up to the tested horizon. At extreme generation lengths, two failure modes become plausible. First, the attention structure itself may shift: a reasoning trace that is 100K tokens long may involve qualitatively different phases (exploration, verification, backtracking) where different tokens become globally important at different stages, and a single snapshot of token importance taken early in the trace may become stale. Second, even if the attention structure remains stable, the fixed-size token budget may become increasingly inadequate as the total context grows—2K tokens represent 6.25% of a 32K context but only 2% of a 100K context, and the model may need access to more historical tokens to maintain a coherent reasoning chain over that horizon. The paper provides no evidence that CUSA would continue to work at 100K+ generation lengths.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The recall curves in Figures 1a, 4, and 8 show LessIsMore's recall remaining high and approximately flat over 30K+ tokens, suggesting stability out to the tested horizon. The stable recency window (25% of the budget) is designed to adapt proportionally as generation length grows (Section 3.3), which would help at longer horizons. The kernel-level speedups at 64K context (Figure 6a) demonstrate that the efficiency benefits scale with context length. However, there is no accuracy evaluation or recall measurement at context lengths beyond 32K tokens. The paper does not report whether the recall curves would eventually degrade at, say, 50K, 75K, or 100K tokens.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation. It projects that efficiency advantages "are expected to scale even further in real-world deployments" with longer generation lengths (Section 4.3) but makes no corresponding claim about accuracy preservation at those lengths. No ablation studies vary the maximum generation length. The observation that the recency proportion is stable (Section 2.4, Figures 9 and 10) is based on decoding steps up to 25K tokens, not beyond. A practitioner deploying LessIsMore for workloads that regularly exceed 32K tokens of reasoning would need to conduct their own validation.

---

### Efficiency Measurements Are Restricted to a Single Model Architecture and Single-GPU Serving Setup

All end-to-end and kernel-level efficiency measurements (Figures 6a, 6b; Tables 1, 3, 4) are conducted exclusively on DeepSeek-R1-Distilled-LLaMA-8B using a single 80GB NVIDIA A100 GPU (for kernel-level and end-to-end speedups) and a single NVIDIA A5000 GPU (for SGLang integration latency). The three Qwen3 models (4B, 8B, 14B), which constitute the majority of the accuracy evaluation (Tables 5 and 6), have no corresponding efficiency measurements reported.

**The consequence.** The headline speedup claims—up to 1.6× end-to-end decoding speedup, up to 1.72× faster sparse attention computation—may not generalize to the Qwen3 model family. Speedups depend on model-specific architectural factors: the number of attention heads, the GQA group size, the hidden dimension, the total number of layers, and the ratio of attention computation to FFN computation. A model with proportionally more FFN computation (wider hidden dimensions relative to attention) would show smaller end-to-end speedups from attention sparsity alone, since the FFN cost is unaffected by LessIsMore. Additionally, the 1.6× figure is measured at 64K context length (Section 4.3); at the 15K–20K context lengths more typical of the AIME benchmarks evaluated (Table 2), the speedup is only 1.1×–1.2× (Figure 6a). The headline number should be understood as the best-case scenario at long contexts, not the typical-case improvement.

Furthermore, all measurements are on single-GPU setups. In production deployments, large reasoning models are often served with tensor parallelism across multiple GPUs, which distributes attention computation differently. The speedup from attention sparsity may not scale linearly in multi-GPU settings, and the kernel-level memory transfer advantages (Table 4) may be partially offset by inter-GPU communication costs.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides detailed kernel-level profiling (Table 4: FLOPs, memory transfer, latency) explaining the source of speedups, and end-to-end TBT measurements at three context lengths (Figure 6a). The SGLang integration (Table 1) demonstrates that the speedups persist in a production serving stack. However, all of these are on DeepSeek-R1-Distilled-LLaMA-8B only. The paper does not report model-specific profiling data (head counts, KV group sizes, FFN-to-attention FLOP ratios) that would help a practitioner estimate speedups on other architectures.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not acknowledge this as a limitation. The kernel-level analysis (Section A.2.1) explains *why* unified token selection is faster for GQA models (reduced memory transfer per KV group), and this explanation should generalize qualitatively to other GQA architectures. But the quantitative speedup depends on model-specific parameters that are not analyzed. No efficiency measurements on Qwen3 models are provided.

---

### The CUSA Design Depends on Measured Attention Properties from a Narrow Set of Models and Tasks

The two foundational observations motivating CUSA—cross-head spatial locality (Section 2.3) and temporal recency locality (Section 2.4)—are measured exclusively on Qwen3-8B at Layer 4, primarily on AIME-24 problems (with supplementary figures for GPQA-Diamond and AIME-25 in Figures 9 and 10 of Appendix A.6). The visualizations show token selection overlap across heads (Figure 2) and recency patterns (Figures 9 and 10), but only for this specific model, layer, and task family.

**The consequence.** The paper's central design principle—that "token importance in reasoning is a global property, not a head-local one" (Section 1)—is based on empirical observations from a narrow slice of the design space. If cross-head overlap is weaker in other layers, other model families, or other types of reasoning tasks (e.g., code generation, logical deduction, multi-hop planning), CUSA's advantage over per-head selection may diminish or disappear. The paper's own ablation (Appendix A.5, Table 11) shows that selection layer placement significantly affects accuracy (e.g., Layer 5 achieves 53.33% vs. Layer 12's 73.00% on AIME-24 at 2K budget), indicating that attention structure varies substantially across layers. If the cross-head overlap observed at Layer 4 is not representative of the layers where CUSA actually operates (Layer 12 for Qwen3-8B), the motivating evidence and the design are misaligned.

Additionally, the paper's claim that these properties "persist across models, layers, and decoding steps" (Section 2) is not systematically validated. The cross-head overlap visualization is for one model, one layer, one decoding step. The recency patterns are shown for one model, one layer, multiple decoding steps. A quantitative measurement—e.g., average Jaccard similarity between head top-k sets across layers, models, and tasks—would provide stronger evidence for generality but is not reported.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The qualitative evidence for cross-head overlap (Figure 2) is compelling for the specific case shown. The recency locality is demonstrated across three tasks (AIME-24, GPQA-Diamond, AIME-25) and multiple decoding steps (Figures 9 and 10). The accuracy results (Tables 5 and 6) show that LessIsMore works across four model families and four benchmarks, which provides indirect validation that the motivating properties hold broadly enough to make CUSA effective. However, this is outcome-level evidence, not mechanistic verification. The paper does not measure cross-head overlap or recency proportion for DeepSeek-R1-Distilled-Llama-8B, Qwen3-4B, or Qwen3-14B, nor does it compare these measurements to the per-model accuracy results.

**Mitigation status.** The paper partially addresses model generality through the breadth of accuracy evaluation (four model families), but this does not validate that the *mechanism* underlying CUSA (cross-head overlap) is present in all these models. A reader cannot determine whether LessIsMore succeeds on Qwen3-14B because that model also exhibits strong cross-head overlap, or because CUSA provides benefits beyond overlap exploitation (e.g., reduced selection variance). The paper does not claim to have measured attention patterns on all evaluated models, and does not discuss this gap.

---

### The Fixed 25% Recency Window Ratio Is Universally Applied Without Per-Task, Per-Model, or Per-Budget Validation

The stable recency window ratio `r = 0.25` is used across all experiments, all models, all benchmarks, and all token budgets (Section 4.1). The ablation in Figure 8 validates this choice for Qwen3-8B on AIME-24 at a 4K token budget, showing that `r = 0.25` achieves the highest cumulative attention recall among tested ratios (0%, 25%, 50%, 75%, 100%). However, the ablation does not vary the model, the task, or the token budget.

**The consequence.** The optimal recency ratio likely depends on context. At smaller token budgets, the model may need to allocate a larger proportion to recency to maintain step-by-step coherence, since the absolute number of recent tokens shrinks (e.g., at 1K total budget with `r = 0.25`, only 256 tokens are recent—possibly insufficient for maintaining the model's active reasoning state). At larger budgets, a smaller proportion might suffice because the absolute recency allocation is already large (e.g., at 8K budget, 2048 tokens are recent). The paper's observation that "the proportion of attention mass allocated to recent context remains stable" (Section 2.4) is based on the ground-truth full attention distribution at specific budgets, not on an optimization over `r`. It is possible that the ground-truth recency proportion itself varies with the token budget, and a fixed `r = 0.25` is suboptimal at extreme budgets.

Additionally, different reasoning tasks may require different recency allocations. AIME problems involve long chains of algebraic manipulation where recent context is critical; GPQA-Diamond problems involve interdisciplinary reasoning that may require more frequent reference to earlier context (e.g., recalling a physics formula introduced early in the trace). The paper does not test whether `r = 0.25` is optimal for GPQA-Diamond or MATH500.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The single ablation in Figure 8 tests five ratios on one configuration. The paper does not vary the budget in the ablation—`r = 0.25` is validated at 4K budget but applied at 1K, 2K, 6K, and 8K as well. The paper does not report accuracy with different `r` values on reasoning benchmarks; only attention recall on a single trace is used for the ablation. There is no evidence that `r = 0.25` maximizes accuracy (as opposed to recall) even for the ablated configuration, since Figure 8 only plots recall and reports whether the trace produced the correct answer (binary), not Pass@1 accuracy over 64 traces.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges this implicitly by presenting the ablation, but does not discuss the generalizability of the 25% ratio to other settings. The uniform application across all experiments is presented as a feature (simplicity) rather than a limitation, but a practitioner might reasonably wonder whether tuning `r` per model or per benchmark would yield further accuracy improvements or enable higher sparsity. The paper does not suggest a procedure for setting `r` beyond the ablation shown.

---

### The Generalization Claim to MHA Architectures Is Tested Only on Retrieval, Not on Reasoning

The paper presents LessIsMore as "architecture-agnostic" (Section 1) and demonstrates its applicability to MHA models through a needle-in-the-haystack experiment on LongChat-7B-v1.5-32k (Appendix A.4.3, Table 8). This experiment shows LessIsMore achieving 100% retrieval accuracy at a 256-token budget, matching or exceeding selection-based baselines. However, this is a retrieval task with short generation, not a reasoning task with long generation. The paper's own central argument is that reasoning workloads are fundamentally different from retrieval workloads—the entire motivation for LessIsMore is that methods that work on retrieval fail on reasoning (Section 2.2, Figure 1b vs. Figure 1a).

**The consequence.** The MHA experiment demonstrates that CUSA's unified token selection does not break standard long-context retrieval, but it does not demonstrate that CUSA preserves reasoning accuracy on MHA models. The structural properties that motivate CUSA—cross-head spatial locality, temporal recency locality—were measured on GQA reasoning models (Qwen3-8B on AIME-24). The paper provides no evidence that these properties hold in MHA reasoning models, nor that CUSA would prevent the attention recall degradation on long-generation reasoning traces that plagues per-head selection on GQA models. A practitioner with an MHA-based reasoning model cannot infer from this paper whether LessIsMore would work.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The MHA experiment (Table 8) uses LongChat-7B-v1.5-32k, which is described as "an MHA-based long-context model" (Appendix A.4.3) but is not a reasoning model—it was not trained for chain-of-thought or test-time scaling. The task is 10K-context needle-in-the-haystack retrieval, which involves short generation (the model outputs the retrieved needle, not a multi-step reasoning trace). The paper does not evaluate LessIsMore on any MHA reasoning model (e.g., an MHA variant of DeepSeek-R1 or an MHA-based Llama reasoning model).

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not acknowledge this as a limitation. The MHA experiment is presented as evidence of generality, but the paper's own framework (Section 2.2) establishes that retrieval performance does not predict reasoning performance for sparse attention methods. The MHA result therefore provides weak evidence for LessIsMore's applicability to MHA reasoning workloads. The paper does not claim to have tested MHA reasoning, but it also does not warn readers that the architectural generality claim is limited to non-reasoning tasks for MHA models.

---

### The Token Budget Is a Uniform Compress Without Difficulty-Adaptive or Phase-Adaptive Allocation

LessIsMore applies the same token budget `K` and the same selection strategy (CUSA with 25% recency) to every decoding step of every problem, regardless of the problem's difficulty or the current phase of reasoning. The paper demonstrates that this uniform strategy preserves accuracy on average across benchmarks, but does not explore whether the budget could be allocated more efficiently by varying it across problems or across decoding steps.

**The consequence.** This represents an unexplored efficiency-accuracy tradeoff. The paper's own analysis shows that the attention recall requirement may vary with the reasoning phase: early in the trace, the model is establishing context and may need broader attention; later, when executing a known solution path, a smaller budget might suffice. Similarly, easy problems (where the model quickly converges to a correct solution) may need less attention budget than hard problems (where the model explores multiple solution paths and needs to reference diverse earlier context). By applying a uniform budget, LessIsMore may overallocate compute to easy problems and early decoding steps, leaving potential efficiency gains on the table. This is analogous to the compute-optimal test-time scaling insight from the reference example paper (Snell et al.), where adaptively allocating inference compute based on difficulty yields 4× efficiency gains over uniform allocation.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper does not analyze how attention recall or token importance vary with problem difficulty or decoding phase. The accuracy results (Tables 5 and 6) report aggregate Pass@1 across all problems without difficulty breakdowns. The generation length analysis (Table 2) reports averages without difficulty stratification. The attention recall curves (Figures 1a, 4, 8) are for single representative traces, not decomposed by problem difficulty. The paper provides no evidence that a uniform budget is optimal, only that it is sufficient to preserve average accuracy.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation. It does not discuss adaptive budget allocation, difficulty estimation, or phase-aware selection as future work. This is a natural extension—the paper establishes that globally consistent selection works, but does not explore whether the budget driving that selection could itself be optimized. Given the paper's framing around test-time compute scaling and decoding efficiency, the absence of any discussion of adaptive allocation is a notable gap between the method's demonstrated capability and its potential efficiency ceiling.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper introduces a **methodological reframing** of how sparse attention for reasoning should be designed: not by inventing new heuristics and testing whether they preserve accuracy, but by first **measuring the structural properties of reasoning attention** and then deriving the design from those measurements. This inverts the standard workflow in sparse attention research, where methods are typically proposed based on assumptions about attention structure (e.g., "different heads specialize," "recent tokens matter most") and then validated on benchmarks. LessIsMore's core argument—that cross-head spatial locality and temporal recency locality are measurable, stable properties of reasoning attention, and that violating them causes the progressive recall degradation observed in prior methods—establishes a **diagnostic-first methodology** that future sparse attention methods for reasoning should adopt.

The conceptual shift is this: the field has treated sparse attention for reasoning as an optimization problem (find the token subset that maximizes instantaneous recall at each layer) when it should be treated as a **stability problem** (find the token subset whose importance remains valid across layers and decoding steps). The paper's demonstration that CUSA with infrequent re-selection at a single layer maintains recall nearly identical to per-layer re-selection (Figure 4), while per-head independent selection degrades severely under the same infrequent regime, makes this shift concrete. The implication is that future sparse attention methods for long-horizon generation should optimize for **sustained recall over the generation trajectory**, not instantaneous per-layer recall, and should be evaluated using cumulative attention recall curves (as in Figure 1a) rather than single-step or average recall metrics.

The paper also **reconciles a tension** between two lines of prior work. On one side, training-free sparse attention methods (TidalDecode, Quest, StreamingLLM) achieve substantial speedups on retrieval tasks but were known to degrade on reasoning—though the degradation was often attributed to domain mismatch rather than systematically analyzed. On the other side, training-required methods like SeerAttention-r demonstrated that reasoning accuracy *could* be preserved under sparsity, but only through expensive post-training adaptation, creating an implicit narrative that reasoning attention is too complex for heuristic selection. LessIsMore reconciles this by showing that the failure of training-free methods is not due to the inherent complexity of reasoning attention, but due to a specific design flaw—head-local, layer-local selection—that can be corrected without training. The method achieves accuracy matching or exceeding SeerAttention-r at similar sparsity levels (Figure 5: LessIsMore at 2K achieves ~73% on AIME-24 vs. SeerAttention-r at ~58% on Qwen3-8B) while being training-free. This reframes the challenge from "can we learn sparse attention for reasoning?" to "can we identify the structural invariants of reasoning attention that make sparse attention safe?"

Several research directions become **more attractive** in light of this work:

- **Measuring attention structure as a first step** in sparse attention design, rather than as a post-hoc analysis. The paper's Figures 2, 9, and 10 demonstrate that attention pattern visualization and recall measurement are tractable with modest compute (a few representative traces). Future sparse attention methods, whether for reasoning or other long-generation tasks, could adopt this as a standard diagnostic before committing to a design.

- **Exploiting other measured invariants of reasoning attention.** The paper identifies two structural properties (cross-head overlap, proportional recency) and builds a method around them. Reasoning attention likely has additional stable properties—e.g., attention to problem-statement tokens, attention to intermediate conclusions from specific reasoning phases, or hierarchical attention patterns across layers—that could inform more sophisticated budget allocation or token retention policies.

- **Combining sparse attention with other efficiency techniques.** LessIsMore targets attention computation exclusively. Its demonstrated ability to preserve accuracy at high sparsity makes it a natural complement to KV cache quantization, weight quantization, speculative decoding, or layer-skipping methods. Since LessIsMore reduces the attention bottleneck without modifying model weights or the KV cache format, it should compose orthogonally with these techniques.

Conversely, some directions become **less attractive**:

- **Developing ever-more-sophisticated per-head selection heuristics.** The paper's Figures 4 and 7 show that even simple global aggregation (CUSA) substantially outperforms per-head selection, especially under infrequent re-selection. This suggests that further optimizing per-head top-k algorithms (e.g., better quantization, learned scoring functions) is unlikely to close the gap with globally consistent selection for reasoning workloads. The bottleneck is not the quality of per-head scoring but the local nature of the optimization.

- **Training-required sparsity adaptation as the default approach for reasoning.** The paper demonstrates that training-free global selection can match or exceed the accuracy of SeerAttention-r—which requires continued pretraining—at the same sparsity levels. While training-required methods may still be necessary at extreme sparsity or for models where attention structure is less regular, the paper establishes that the bar for justifying training cost has been raised: a training-free method should be evaluated first, and training should be reserved for cases where it demonstrably outperforms structural methods.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Extreme-sparsity stress testing: where does LessIsMore's accuracy break down?** The paper evaluates LessIsMore at token budgets down to 1K (for MATH500) and 2K (for AIME), representing approximately 87.5% sparsity at typical generation lengths. But the recall curves (Figure 1a) show LessIsMore maintaining recall near 1.0 at 4K budget, suggesting there may be headroom for further sparsity. A systematic sweep to lower budgets—512, 256, 128, 64 tokens—on AIME-24 with Qwen3-8B would establish the sparsity frontier. The key measurement would be cumulative attention recall and Pass@1 accuracy as a function of the absolute token budget (not just the ratio to generation length), since the recency window shrinks proportionally. At a 128-token budget with `r = 0.25`, only 32 tokens are allocated to recent context—potentially insufficient for maintaining step-by-step coherence, regardless of how well CUSA captures historical importance. Identifying the failure threshold would clarify whether the method's current design is near-optimal at the tested budgets or whether further structural refinements (e.g., a budget-dependent recency ratio) could push sparsity higher.

**Extending CUSA to MHA reasoning models with long-generation evaluation.** The paper demonstrates CUSA on an MHA model only for needle-in-the-haystack retrieval (LongChat-7B-v1.5-32k, Table 8), not for reasoning. A direct test would be to apply LessIsMore to an MHA-based reasoning model—for instance, a Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct model fine-tuned for chain-of-thought reasoning, or an MHA variant of DeepSeek-R1 if available—and evaluate on AIME-24 at 2K–8K token budgets. The key question is whether the cross-head spatial locality observed in GQA reasoning models (Figure 2) generalizes to MHA, where there is no KV head sharing. In MHA, every query head has its own key and value projections, so heads may diverge more in their token importance rankings—potentially weakening CUSA's advantage over per-head selection. A negative result (CUSA works on GQA reasoning but fails on MHA reasoning) would refine our understanding of when global consistency matters: it may be that the shared KV heads in GQA *induce* the observed cross-head overlap, and that MHA attention patterns are genuinely more head-specialized, requiring different sparse attention strategies.

**Quantifying the causal link between measured attention properties and CUSA's performance.** The paper argues that CUSA works because reasoning attention exhibits cross-head overlap, but the evidence is correlational: overlap is observed (Figure 2), and CUSA outperforms per-head selection (Figures 4, 7). A more definitive test would manipulate the degree of cross-head overlap and measure the impact on CUSA. For example, add independent Gaussian noise to each head's attention scores before top-k selection, with the noise variance controlling the overlap between heads' top-k sets. As noise increases (overlap decreases), CUSA's advantage over per-head selection should diminish, and the recall curves under infrequent re-selection should degrade toward the Head-to-Head baseline. If this relationship holds quantitatively, it establishes a causal mechanism rather than a post-hoc justification. If CUSA's advantage persists even under artificially decorrelated heads, then the benefit arises from other properties of the aggregation (e.g., variance reduction from union-flatten, or robustness to single-head errors) rather than specifically from overlap exploitation, which would reframe the paper's central claim.

**Adaptive recency ratio and difficulty-aware budget allocation.** The paper applies a uniform `r = 0.25` and uniform token budget `K` across all problems and decoding steps. A natural extension is to make both adaptive. For the recency ratio: measure the optimal `r` as a function of the token budget `K` and generation length `L` using the same cumulative recall diagnostic (extending Figure 8 to sweep both `K` and `L`). The hypothesis from the paper's temporal recency analysis (Section 2.4) is that the ground-truth recency proportion is stable across generation lengths, implying a fixed `r` is near-optimal—but this has not been validated at extreme budgets. For budget allocation: use the PRM or a lightweight difficulty estimator (as in the reference example paper, Snell et al.) to predict problem difficulty from the prompt, then allocate larger budgets to harder problems and smaller budgets to easier ones. A FLOPs-matched comparison against uniform budget allocation would reveal whether difficulty-adaptive allocation yields efficiency gains analogous to the 4× improvements observed in compute-optimal test-time scaling. This would also address the paper's unstated assumption that uniform allocation is sufficient; if adaptive allocation yields substantial gains, it would set a new efficiency ceiling for sparse attention in reasoning.

**Interaction of LessIsMore with KV cache eviction for memory-constrained deployment.** LessIsMore is selection-based: it retains the full KV cache and dynamically selects which tokens to attend to. This preserves accuracy but does not reduce memory consumption—the full KV cache still resides in GPU memory, which can be a bottleneck for long generations on memory-constrained hardware (e.g., edge devices, consumer GPUs). A hybrid approach could use LessIsMore for token selection during attention computation while applying eviction-based methods (e.g., StreamingLLM, H2O) to prune the KV cache itself, potentially achieving both compute and memory savings. The key experiment would be: apply LessIsMore's CUSA selection for attention, but simultaneously evict tokens that are never (or rarely) selected by CUSA across multiple decoding steps. If the union of CUSA's selected sets over a sliding window of steps converges to a stable subset, evicting tokens outside that subset could reduce memory footprint without degrading recall, since those tokens would not have been attended to anyway. This would extend LessIsMore from a pure latency optimization to a latency-and-memory optimization, broadening its applicability to hardware-constrained settings.

**Phase-aware re-selection for very long reasoning traces.** The paper demonstrates that infrequent re-selection works at up to ~32K generation lengths, but does not test whether a single selection snapshot remains valid for 100K+ token traces. A scenario where this likely fails: the model completes one reasoning approach, realizes it is wrong, and backtracks to try a different approach. The token importance structure in the new approach may differ substantially from the original, making the original CUSA snapshot stale. A phase-aware extension could monitor attention recall or a simpler diagnostic (e.g., the KL divergence between the current layer's attention distribution and the CUSA snapshot's distribution) and trigger re-selection when divergence exceeds a threshold. The experiment would be: run LessIsMore with a single selection layer on a 100K-trace reasoning benchmark (if available) and measure accuracy; then add a re-selection trigger and measure whether accuracy improves and at what compute cost. This would establish whether the paper's design principle of "globally consistent, infrequently re-selected" token indices has a horizon beyond which it breaks, and whether simple heuristics can extend that horizon.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Cost-efficient API serving of reasoning models with variable-length generation.** The paper's Table 1 shows that LessIsMore reduces per-token decoding latency from 34.4 ms to 24.1 ms at 64K context length on a single A5000 GPU (1.43× speedup), while preserving accuracy on AIME-24 within 1.5 percentage points of full attention at a 2K token budget (Table 5). For an API provider serving reasoning models—where users submit prompts and pay per output token—this directly translates to lower GPU time per query, enabling either higher throughput per GPU or lower latency for users. The generation length analysis (Table 2) is particularly important: LessIsMore avoids the length inflation that plagues other sparse methods (e.g., Quest generates 103% more tokens than Full Attention at 2K budget, partially negating its per-token speedup). API providers bill by output token count, so length inflation both increases user cost and reduces effective throughput. LessIsMore's combination of per-token speedup and length preservation makes it directly deployable in production serving stacks without the perverse incentive of trading speed for longer (more expensive) outputs.

**On-device or edge deployment of reasoning-capable small models.** The paper evaluates LessIsMore on Qwen3-4B, the smallest model in the Qwen3 family, and shows that at a 2K token budget on AIME-24, LessIsMore achieves 71.48% accuracy versus 71.25% for full attention—slightly exceeding the baseline (Table 5). For edge deployment scenarios where GPU memory is limited (e.g., laptops, mobile devices), the combination of a small model and sparse attention could make local reasoning feasible. The 4B model with LessIsMore attends to only 2K tokens per step instead of the full KV cache, reducing attention compute by up to 87.5% at typical generation lengths while maintaining reasoning quality. The kernel-level memory transfer savings (1.04 MB vs. 2.34 MB for TidalDecode, Table 4) are particularly relevant for memory-bandwidth-constrained edge hardware, where global-to-shared memory transactions are often the bottleneck rather than raw FLOPs. A practical deployment could use a Qwen3-4B model with LessIsMore at a 2K–4K budget to handle moderately difficult reasoning queries locally, escalating to a cloud-based larger model only for queries where the small model's accuracy is insufficient—with LessIsMore's difficulty estimation remaining an open challenge (the paper does not address this).

**Batch inference for synthetic data generation in self-improvement pipelines.** The paper's results on MATH500 (Table 6) show that LessIsMore achieves 93.35% accuracy at a 1K token budget with Qwen3-8B, matching full attention's 94.43% while attending to far fewer tokens. For pipelines that use reasoning models to generate training data—e.g., generating chain-of-thought solutions for fine-tuning smaller models, or STaR-style self-improvement loops—the dominant cost is decoding thousands of long reasoning traces. LessIsMore can reduce this cost by 1.1×–1.6× per generated token (Figure 6a) without degrading solution quality, as evidenced by the near-lossless accuracy across benchmarks. The practical workflow would be: use a Qwen3 or DeepSeek-R1 model with LessIsMore at a 4K–8K budget to generate solutions for a training corpus, then fine-tune a smaller model on those solutions. The key advantage over other sparse methods is that LessIsMore preserves generation length (Table 2), so the generated training data has the same distribution of trace lengths as full-attention data—other sparse methods' length inflation could bias the training data toward longer, potentially less efficient reasoning traces.

**Interactive reasoning assistants with latency constraints.** For applications where a user interactively queries a reasoning model and expects responses within seconds, the 1.43× end-to-end latency reduction at 64K context (Table 1) directly improves user experience. At 34.4 ms/token, generating a 15K-token AIME solution takes ~516 seconds (8.6 minutes) per query; at 24.1 ms/token, that drops to ~361 seconds (6.0 minutes)—a 2.6-minute reduction. For a user waiting for an answer, this is perceptible. More importantly, the paper's demonstration that LessIsMore preserves accuracy means the user gets a faster response without sacrificing solution quality—unlike other sparse methods that either degrade accuracy (TidalDecode, Quest at low budgets) or extend generation length (all baselines in Table 2), potentially negating the latency benefit.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper explicitly positions LessIsMore against both training-free sparse attention methods (TidalDecode, Quest, StreamingLLM) and training-required methods (SeerAttention-r). The tradeoffs are clear from the experimental results:

- **Prefer LessIsMore over per-head selection methods (TidalDecode, Quest) when:** the workload involves long-horizon reasoning (thousands of decoding steps) and accuracy preservation is critical. LessIsMore matches or exceeds TidalDecode and Quest in accuracy at all tested budgets (Figure 5), avoids the generation length inflation that partially negates their speedups (Table 2), and achieves comparable or better kernel-level latency due to reduced memory transfer from unified token selection (Figure 6b, Table 4). The accuracy gap is largest at small budgets (e.g., on AIME-24 at 2K: LessIsMore 73.0% vs. TidalDecode 53.3% vs. Quest 18.2%) and narrows but persists at larger budgets.

- **Prefer LessIsMore over training-required methods (SeerAttention-r) when:** avoiding per-model retraining cost is important. LessIsMore is plug-and-play on any pretrained GQA reasoning model and achieves higher accuracy than SeerAttention-r at comparable sparsity (Figure 5: LessIsMore at 2K achieves ~73% vs. SeerAttention-r at ~58% on AIME-24 with Qwen3-8B), without requiring training data, continued pretraining, or sparsity-level-specific adaptation. If the model family or sparsity level changes frequently, the training cost of SeerAttention-r (which must be repeated for each configuration) may be prohibitive.

- **Prefer full attention when:** the context length is short relative to the token budget or latency is not a concern. At 16K context with a 4K budget, LessIsMore achieves only 1.09× speedup (Figure 6a)—a modest improvement that may not justify the implementation complexity. Similarly, for workloads where the base model's generation length is short (e.g., MATH500 averages ~5K tokens with full attention, Table 2), the 1K–6K token budgets tested represent less aggressive sparsity, and the efficiency gains are correspondingly smaller.

- **Prefer StreamingLLM or eviction-based methods when:** memory footprint reduction is the primary goal and the task is not long-horizon reasoning. LessIsMore retains the full KV cache and only reduces attention computation, not memory usage. StreamingLLM permanently evicts most tokens, dramatically reducing GPU memory consumption, at the cost of accuracy degradation on reasoning tasks (Figure 1a: <65% recall on AIME-24). For non-reasoning tasks where StreamingLLM's accuracy is acceptable, its memory savings may outweigh LessIsMore's accuracy advantage.
