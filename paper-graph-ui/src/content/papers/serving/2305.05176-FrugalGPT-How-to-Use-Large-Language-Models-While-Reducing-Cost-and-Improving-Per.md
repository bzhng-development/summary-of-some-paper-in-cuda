# FrugalGPT: How to Use Large Language Models While Reducing Cost and Improving Performance

**ArXiv:** [2305.05176](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.05176)

## 🎯 Pitch

This paper proposes and systematically evaluates **FrugalGPT**, a flexible framework for reducing the inference cost of large language model APIs while maintaining or improving accuracy.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper proposes and systematically evaluates **FrugalGPT**, a flexible framework for reducing the inference cost of large language model APIs while maintaining or improving accuracy. Using 12 commercial LLM APIs (including GPT-4, ChatGPT, GPT-3, and various J1 and GPT-J variants) across three benchmarks—HEADLINES (financial news), OVERRULING (legal document analysis), and COQA (reading comprehension)—the paper outlines three families of cost-reduction strategies: prompt adaptation (selecting shorter, cheaper prompts), LLM approximation (completion caching, model fine-tuning), and **LLM cascade** (sequentially routing queries through a chain of LLMs, stopping when a generator's output scores above a learned confidence threshold). A concrete instantiation of LLM cascade learns which combinations of LLMs to invoke per query, matching GPT-4's accuracy with up to 98% cost reduction (e.g., $0.6 vs. $33.1 on HEADLINES) or improving accuracy over GPT-4 by up to 4% at the same cost, establishing that cheap LLMs can substitute for expensive ones only when their diverse failure patterns are complementary and a learned scoring function can reliably distinguish correct from incorrect generations.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: LLM API Costs Are a Barrier to Widespread Adoption

The fundamental problem this paper tackles is straightforward but economically critical: **querying large language model APIs is expensive, and the cost scales prohibitively for high-throughput applications**. The paper documents that GPT-4 charges $30 per 10 million input tokens and $60 per 10 million output tokens, while smaller models like GPT-J (hosted by Textsynth) charge only $0.20 and $5.00 respectively for the same volumes—a difference of up to **two orders of magnitude** (Table 1). This cost heterogeneity is not merely an interesting market observation; it creates a practical barrier that prevents organizations from deploying LLMs at scale.

The paper makes this tangible through a worked example (Section 2): a small business running customer service with GPT-4, handling 15,000 customers per month at 3 questions each, twice weekly, totals 360,000 queries per month. With prompts averaging 1,800 tokens and responses around 80 tokens, the monthly cost reaches approximately **$21,200**—unsustainable for most small businesses. This is not a hypothetical scenario; the paper cites external estimates that ChatGPT itself costs over $700,000 per day to operate, underscoring that the cost problem exists even for the LLM providers themselves.

The problem is compounded by **cost heterogeneity across providers and models**. The paper reviews 12 commercial LLM APIs from five mainstream providers (OpenAI, AI21, CoHere, Textsynth, ForeFrontAI) and finds that their pricing structures are fundamentally different. Some charge only for output tokens (AI21's J1 family), some charge symmetrically for both input and output (OpenAI, CoHere), and some add a fixed per-request fee on top of per-token charges. This means the cost ranking of different APIs is not fixed across tasks—a model that is cheaper on one dataset may be more expensive on another depending on the typical prompt length, response length, and the interaction of these with the pricing model. This non-transitivity is empirically visible in Figure 5: GPT-3 is the second-most expensive LLM on OVERRULING and COQA, while J1 holds that position on HEADLINES.

Critically, the problem is not merely financial. The paper explicitly connects excessive LLM usage to **environmental and energy impact**, citing prior work on the substantial carbon footprint of large-scale model inference. This frames cost reduction as simultaneously an economic, environmental, and social welfare concern—reducing LLM inference cost means reducing energy consumption and carbon emissions.

### Why the Problem Is Difficult: The Naïve Approaches Fail

Before presenting its solution, the paper identifies why obvious approaches to this problem fall short.

**Single-model deployment is the default but wasteful.** The standard usage pattern (Figure 1a) sends every query to the same LLM—typically the most capable one available, such as GPT-4. This is simple but inefficient because query difficulty varies substantially. Easy queries that could be correctly answered by a much cheaper model (e.g., GPT-J, which is roughly 30× smaller than GPT-3) still incur the full cost of the expensive model. The paper frames this as a **lack of data-adaptive LLM selection**: existing workflows do not distinguish between queries that genuinely require GPT-4's capabilities and those that a cheaper model could handle.

**Aggregating cheap models can match expensive ones on simple tasks—but not difficult ones.** The paper acknowledges prior work showing that aggregating responses from multiple cheap models (like GPT-J) can achieve performance comparable to GPT-3 on some benchmarks (citing Arora et al., 2022). This suggests cost savings are possible. However, it also notes a critical limitation: the performance of small models "can be much worse on difficult tasks" (citing Touvron et al., 2023). So aggregation alone is not a universal solution—it works for easy queries but fails when task complexity increases. The paper therefore identifies the need for a strategy that can handle the full spectrum of query difficulty.

**Relying on a single API provider creates reliability risks.** The paper points out a pragmatic concern often overlooked in academic evaluations: if a provider becomes unavailable due to spiking demand or outages, all queries fail. A cost-reduction strategy that also diversifies across providers would provide resilience as a byproduct, but this dimension is underexplored in prior work.

### Where Existing Approaches Fall Short

The paper identifies specific limitations across four areas of related work:

**Prompt engineering makes prompts longer, not shorter.** The field of prompt engineering has focused overwhelmingly on improving accuracy by adding more context—few-shot examples, chain-of-thought reasoning steps, retrieved knowledge passages, and decomposing complex questions into sub-prompt sequences. These techniques all increase prompt length, which directly increases cost in a per-token pricing model. The paper explicitly notes this tension: "Existing prompt engineering approaches often aim to provide more detailed task explanations and in-context examples, resulting in longer and more expensive prompts." No prior work had systematically explored **concise prompts as a cost-reduction mechanism**, which the paper posits as an underexplored lever.

**Model ensembles assume white-box access and increase cost.** In traditional supervised learning, model ensembles combine multiple models by averaging their predictions or training a meta-learner on their outputs. The paper identifies several mismatches with the LLM API context:
- Ensembles typically require **white-box access** to models for training purposes (they need model internals or at minimum output logits), but commercial LLM APIs are **black-box**—only text-in, text-out.
- Ensembles require querying **all models for every query**, which multiplies cost rather than reducing it. In a per-token billing model, running 5 models on every query is 5× more expensive than running one, defeating the purpose of cost reduction.
- Ensemble methods were designed for tasks with a **fixed, known label set** (classification), where outputs can be combined via voting or averaging. LLM outputs span the entire natural language space, making aggregation non-trivial—two models might express the same correct answer in different words, and a voting scheme needs semantic equivalence detection.

**System optimization requires model access that APIs don't provide.** Post-training quantization, hardware-aware pruning, and training pipeline parallelism are effective methods for reducing inference cost in internally-hosted models. But the paper notes that they "require modifications to LLMs' internal states (e.g., model weights)," and "many commercial LLM APIs do not release their models." This eliminates the entire class of system optimization techniques for API-only users, who constitute the paper's target audience.

**ML-as-a-Service selection methods assume classification with known labels.** Prior work on optimizing ML API usage (specifically the FrugalML line of work by Chen et al., 2020, 2022, which includes two of this paper's authors) addressed the problem of selecting among classification APIs to maximize accuracy within a budget. The paper identifies a critical gap that prevents direct application to LLMs: "The outputs of LLM APIs encompass the entire natural language space, but existing work requires a fixed (and known) label set." Furthermore, the optimization space is vastly larger for LLMs because both prompt choice (zero-shot, few-shot, chain-of-thought, or custom templates) and API choice interact to determine performance, and this joint space had never been characterized.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper positions itself not as a single-method contribution but as a **vision-setting framework** that identifies the problem space and demonstrates feasibility through a concrete instantiation. This is made explicit in Section 5: "Given the rapid development of LLM, this paper is not meant to be comprehensive or to provide a definitive solution. Our goal is to lay a foundation for this important research agenda and to demonstrate that even simple cascade can already achieve promising savings."

The paper proposes three families of strategies—prompt adaptation, LLM approximation, and LLM cascade—that collectively define a research agenda for **budget-aware LLM API usage**. These strategies are presented as complementary and composable: prompt adaptation reduces per-query prompt costs, LLM approximation substitutes expensive models with cheap alternatives, and LLM cascade adaptively routes queries to appropriate models. They can be combined (e.g., joint prompt and LLM selection) for further gains.

The primary empirical contribution is an **LLM cascade** that learns a sequential chain of LLM APIs to invoke per query, with a learned scoring function that decides when to accept an answer and stop. This is positioned as an extension of model cascade ideas from classical ML (Viola and Jones, 2004; Wang et al., 2011) and FrugalML to the generative LLM setting, with the key novelty being the scoring function that operates on arbitrary natural language outputs rather than classification probabilities.

Crucially, the paper does not claim to solve all aspects of the problem. It explicitly acknowledges limitations: training the cascade requires labeled examples from the target distribution, the training itself incurs a one-time computational cost, and other promising strategies (like accelerating attention computation) are out of scope. This positioning is honest about scope while establishing that **even simple cascade mechanisms can yield orders-of-magnitude savings**, motivating further work on the broader framework.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

FrugalGPT is a **system that learns to route natural language queries through a chain of LLM APIs, stopping as soon as a generated answer is deemed reliable enough, rather than always querying the most expensive model**. It solves the problem of high inference costs by exploiting the fact that many queries can be answered correctly by cheap models with small confidence thresholds, while reserving expensive models (like GPT-4) only for the genuinely difficult cases—and it does this by learning a scoring function that estimates answer correctness from the query text and the generated answer alone, without access to model internals or output probabilities.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The FrugalGPT LLM cascade system has four major components:

1.  **LLM Marketplace** — a collection of $K$ black-box LLM APIs ($f_1, f_2, ..., f_K$) with heterogeneous pricing structures (per-token input cost, per-token output cost, and optional fixed per-query cost). These APIs take a prompt as input and return a natural language answer. The paper uses $K = 12$ APIs from five providers.

2.  **Generation Scoring Function** — a learned model $g(q, a): Q \times A \rightarrow [0, 1]$ that, given a query $q$ and a generated answer $a$, produces a scalar reliability score estimating the probability that $a$ is correct. The scoring function is trained on labeled data (queries with known correct answers) and is much smaller/cheaper than any LLM—the paper uses a DistilBERT model fine-tuned for regression. This is the critical component that enables the system to decide when to stop querying.

3.  **LLM Router** — a learned policy that selects an ordered list of LLM APIs $L = [L_1, L_2, ..., L_m]$ (where $m$ is the cascade length, set to 3 in all experiments) and associated threshold vectors $\tau = [\tau_1, \tau_2, ..., \tau_m]$ for each position in the cascade. The router is optimized offline on a training set to maximize accuracy subject to a budget constraint.

4.  **Budget-Aware Optimizer** — a specialized solver that, given a training dataset of $N$ (query, correct answer) pairs, a target budget $b$, and the LLM marketplace, outputs the optimal cascade configuration $(L, \tau)$ by pruning the search space and interpolating cost-accuracy trade-offs.

**Information flow at inference time:** A query arrives → the router's first LLM $L_1$ is invoked with the query as prompt → $L_1$ generates answer $a_1$ → scoring function computes $g(q, a_1)$ → if $g(q, a_1) \geq \tau_1$, answer $a_1$ is returned and the process stops → otherwise, $L_2$ is invoked with the same query → scoring function evaluates $g(q, a_2)$ → if $g(q, a_2) \geq \tau_2$, return $a_2$ → otherwise, continue to $L_3$ → the final LLM in the list is always accepted if reached.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the **formal budget-constrained optimization problem** that defines what the LLM cascade must achieve—this establishes the objective function, the search space, and the cost model that makes the optimization non-trivial.
- **Second**, the **LLM cost model**—how the paper quantifies cost across heterogeneous pricing structures, why cost rankings are not fixed across datasets, and how this affects the optimization.
- **Third**, the **generation scoring function**—what model is used, how it is trained, what data it requires, and why it must operate on arbitrary natural language rather than classification logits.
- **Fourth**, the **LLM cascade mechanism**—how the sequential routing works at inference time, what the threshold vector controls, and the structure of the learned policy.
- **Fifth**, the **specialized optimizer**—how the paper solves the mixed-integer optimization problem efficiently by pruning the search space and interpolating objective values, avoiding exhaustive search over the combinatorial space of possible cascade sequences.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is a **systems + empirical analysis paper** whose core idea is that the optimal way to use LLM APIs is to route queries through a learned cascade of models, stopping early when a cheap model's answer scores above a confidence threshold, rather than querying the most expensive model for every input. The key enabling insight is that a lightweight scoring function—trained only on query-answer pairs—can reliably distinguish correct from incorrect generations without access to model internals.

---

#### The Budget-Constrained Optimization Objective

The paper formalizes the LLM cascade selection problem as a constrained optimization over the choice of LLM sequence $L$ and threshold vector $\tau$. Let $Q$ be a distribution over queries, $a^*(q)$ be the ground-truth answer for query $q$, and $r(\cdot, \cdot)$ be a reward function measuring answer quality (e.g., exact-match accuracy). Let $c(s, q)$ be the total cost incurred by strategy $s$ on query $q$, and $b$ be the user-specified average cost budget. The optimization problem is:

$$ \max_{L, \tau} \mathbb{E}_{q \sim Q} \left[ r(a^*(q), f_{L_z}(q)) \right] $$

$$ \text{subject to} \quad \mathbb{E}_{q \sim Q} \left[ \sum_{i=1}^{z} \left( \tilde{c}_{L_i, 2} \| f_{L_i}(q) \| + \tilde{c}_{L_i, 1} \| q \| + \tilde{c}_{L_i, 0} \right) \right] \leq b $$

$$ \text{where} \quad z = \arg \min_i \left\{ g(q, f_{L_i}(q)) \geq \tau_i \right\} $$

where:

- $L = [L_1, L_2, ..., L_m]$ is the ordered list of $m$ LLM API indices selected from the $K$ available APIs (`$\{1, ..., K\}$`). In the paper, $m = 3$ for all experiments.
- $\tau = [\tau_1, \tau_2, ..., \tau_m]$ is a vector of acceptance thresholds, one per position in the cascade, each in $[0, 1]$.
- $f_{L_i}(q)$ is the answer generated by the $i$-th LLM in the cascade for query $q$.
- $g(q, f_{L_i}(q))$ is the scoring function's reliability estimate for that query-answer pair.
- $z$ is the stopping index: the position in the cascade where the score first meets or exceeds the threshold. If no threshold is met before position $m$, $z = m$ and the final LLM's answer is accepted unconditionally.
- $\tilde{c}_{L_i, 1}$ is the per-token input cost of LLM $L_i$, $\tilde{c}_{L_i, 2}$ is the per-token output cost, and $\tilde{c}_{L_i, 0}$ is the fixed per-query cost.
- $\| q \|$ is the length of the query in tokens, and $\| f_{L_i}(q) \|$ is the length of the generated answer in tokens.

**What it computes:** The objective maximizes the expected reward (accuracy) of the answer returned by the cascade—the answer from whichever LLM in the sequence first crosses its confidence threshold, or the final LLM if none do. The constraint ensures that the expected total cost (summed over all LLMs actually invoked per query) stays within the user's budget $b$. The stopping index $z$ dynamically determines how many LLMs are called: queries that get a confident answer early stop cheaply; queries that generate low-confidence answers cascade to more expensive models.

**Why this form:** This formulation captures the fundamental trade-off. Using only the cheapest LLM ($z = 1$ always) minimizes cost but may produce poor accuracy on hard queries. Using only the most expensive LLM ($z = m$ always, with thresholds set to infinity) maximizes accuracy potential but exhausts the budget. The cascade with learned thresholds creates a **conditional computation graph**: each query's cost depends on its difficulty and the scoring function's ability to recognize correct answers. The optimization must find both *which* LLMs to include in the sequence and *how confident* the system needs to be at each stage to stop—these decisions coupled because the scoring function's calibration varies across different LLMs' output distributions.

An important subtlety: since the LLM APIs are black-box, the optimization cannot use internal model features or probability distributions—it can only observe the text outputs and associated costs. This makes the problem fundamentally different from classical model cascade (where each model outputs a probability distribution over a fixed label set, making confidence calibration straightforward).

#### The LLM Cost Model

The paper models the cost of a single LLM API call as having three additive components, reflecting the pricing structures observed across the 12 commercial APIs surveyed in Table 1:

$$ c_i(p) = \tilde{c}_{i, 2} \| f_i(p) \| + \tilde{c}_{i, 1} \| p \| + \tilde{c}_{i, 0} $$

where:

- $\tilde{c}_{i, 1}$ is the cost per input token (charged for the prompt $p$ sent to the API),
- $\tilde{c}_{i, 2}$ is the cost per output token (charged for the generated answer),
- $\tilde{c}_{i, 0}$ is a fixed cost per API call,
- $\| p \|$ is the number of tokens in the prompt, and $\| f_i(p) \|$ is the number of tokens in the generated answer.

**What it computes:** The total dollar cost to invoke LLM $i$ with prompt $p$, accumulated from the prompt length proportionally ($\tilde{c}_{i, 1} \times$ tokens), the generation length proportionally ($\tilde{c}_{i, 2} \times$ tokens), and any fixed per-request surcharge.

**Why this form:** The three-component structure emerges directly from the pricing heterogeneity observed in the LLM marketplace. Some providers (AI21) charge $\tilde{c}_{i, 1} = 0$—they only bill for output tokens—making them cheaper for long-prompt tasks compared to providers like OpenAI that charge for both input and output symmetrically. The fixed cost $\tilde{c}_{i, 0}$ is non-zero for some AI21 models (Table 1: $0.0003 for J1-Large, $0.0008 for J1-Grande, $0.005 for J1-Jumbo). This means the cost ranking of LLMs is **non-transitive across tasks**: a model that is second-cheapest on a dataset with short prompts and long responses (where output cost dominates) may be second-most-expensive on a dataset with long, example-heavy prompts and short answers (where input cost dominates). This is empirically visible in Figure 5, where the cost ordering of GPT-3 versus J1 flips across datasets.

The paper uses token counts directly as reported by the APIs. For the experiments, the costs were retrieved in March 2023 and are recorded verbatim in Table 1—for example, OpenAI's GPT-4 charges $30 per 10M input tokens (i.e., $\tilde{c} = 0.000003$ per token) and $60 per 10M output tokens, while Textsynth's GPT-J charges $0.20 per 10M input tokens and $5 per 10M output tokens—a factor of 150× and 12× cheaper, respectively.

#### The Generation Scoring Function

The generation scoring function $g(q, a): Q \times A \rightarrow [0, 1]$ is the linchpin that enables the cascade to make stopping decisions. It takes a query $q$ (the natural language question) and a generated answer $a$ (the LLM's text response), and outputs a scalar representing the estimated probability that $a$ is correct for $q$. This is a **regression model** trained on labeled data.

**Model architecture.** The paper uses DistilBERT (Sanh et al., 2019), a compact transformer model with approximately 66 million parameters—roughly 30× smaller than GPT-J and over 100× smaller than GPT-3. DistilBERT takes a text sequence as input and produces a contextualized representation; for the scoring task, the query and generated answer are concatenated as a single input sequence, and the model is fine-tuned with a regression head that outputs a scalar in [0, 1].

**Why DistilBERT:** The scoring function must be substantially cheaper to run than any LLM in the cascade, or else the cost of scoring would offset the savings from avoiding expensive models. DistilBERT's inference cost is negligible compared to even the cheapest LLM APIs—it can run locally on CPU without any API fees. It is "considerably smaller and therefore less expensive than all LLMs considered here" (Section 4). The choice of a discriminative model (trained to predict correctness from the query-answer pair) rather than a generative model is deliberate: the scoring function does not need to produce text, only to evaluate it.

**Training data requirement.** To train the scoring function, the paper requires a set of triples $(q, a, y)$ where $q$ is a query, $a$ is an answer generated by some LLM, and $y \in \{0, 1\}$ is a binary label indicating whether $a$ is correct. The correct answer must be known (the dataset has ground-truth labels). During training, answers are generated by querying the LLMs with the training queries, and each answer is compared against the ground truth to obtain $y$. The scoring function is then trained to regress from $(q, a)$ to $y$.

This reveals an important requirement: **training the scoring function requires labeled examples from the same or similar distribution as the test queries**—a point the paper explicitly acknowledges as a limitation in Section 5. The scoring function learns the patterns of what correct versus incorrect answers look like for a specific task, so it does not generalize zero-shot to entirely new tasks.

**Why not use LLM output probabilities:** Commercial LLM APIs are black-box and do not expose output logits or token-level probabilities. Even if they did, for open-ended generation tasks, the "probability of the answer" is not well-defined because the same correct answer can be expressed in many different surface forms. The scoring function sidesteps this by operating on the generated text directly, learning semantic features of correctness from the query-answer pair.

#### The LLM Cascade Mechanism

The LLM cascade is the inference-time routing procedure. Given a trained router configuration $(L, \tau)$ and a new query $q$, the cascade operates as follows:

**Step 1:** Send $q$ (as the prompt) to the first LLM in the sequence, $L_1$. Receive generated answer $a_1 = f_{L_1}(q)$.

**Step 2:** Compute the reliability score $s_1 = g(q, a_1)$ using the scoring function.

**Step 3:** If $s_1 \geq \tau_1$, return $a_1$ as the final answer and stop. The query cost is $c_{L_1}(p)$ only (where $p$ is the prompt constructed from $q$).

**Step 4:** If $s_1 < \tau_1$, send $q$ to the second LLM $L_2$, receive $a_2 = f_{L_2}(q)$, compute $s_2 = g(q, a_2)$. If $s_2 \geq \tau_2$, return $a_2$ and stop. The query cost is $c_{L_1}(p) + c_{L_2}(p)$.

**Step 5:** Continue sequentially through the cascade. If no threshold is met before the final LLM $L_m$, its answer $a_m$ is returned unconditionally (the system cannot refuse to answer). The maximum cost per query is $\sum_{i=1}^{m} c_{L_i}(p)$.

**The role of the threshold vector $\tau$.** Each $\tau_i$ controls the trade-off between accuracy and cost at position $i$ in the cascade:

- If $\tau_i$ is set very high (close to 1.0), the system almost never accepts an answer at position $i$, meaning most queries cascade further. This increases accuracy (since only very confident answers are accepted) but increases cost (since later, typically more expensive models are queried more often).
- If $\tau_i$ is set very low (close to 0.0), the system accepts most answers at position $i$, saving cost by stopping early but potentially returning incorrect answers that happened to score above the low bar.

The optimal thresholds depend on the **calibration** of the scoring function for each LLM's outputs. If the scoring function tends to assign high scores to incorrect answers from $L_1$ (poor calibration on $L_1$'s output distribution), then a high $\tau_1$ is needed to avoid false positives. Conversely, if $L_1$ rarely produces correct answers (low pass@1 on the task), a low $\tau_1$ would let through many incorrect answers, harming accuracy.

**Why sequential rather than parallel:** A parallel ensemble would query all $m$ LLMs simultaneously and then select the best answer (e.g., via scoring function or majority voting). This costs $\sum_{i=1}^{m} c_{L_i}(p)$ for *every* query, regardless of difficulty. The sequential cascade only pays for LLM $i+1$ when LLM $i$'s answer was not confident enough—creating a conditional cost structure where easy queries are cheap and only hard queries pay the full cascade cost. This is the fundamental mechanism enabling cost reduction.

**Cascade length is fixed at $m = 3$** in all experiments, a design choice that "simplifies the optimization space and already demonstrates good results" (Section 4). The optimization problem must select *which* 3 LLMs to include (from the 12 available) and in what order, plus the two corresponding stopping thresholds (the third position always accepts).

#### The Specialized Optimizer for Cascade Configuration

The optimization problem defined above is **mixed-integer**: the LLM list $L$ is a discrete choice from a combinatorial space (there are $K! / (K-m)! = 12 \times 11 \times 10 = 1320$ possible ordered sequences of length 3 from 12 LLMs), while the thresholds $\tau$ are continuous in $[0, 1]^m$. Exhaustive search over all sequences and thresholds is computationally prohibitive, and even evaluating a single candidate configuration requires running all LLMs in the sequence on the training set to measure accuracy and cost—an expensive proposition.

The paper develops a **specialized optimizer with two key efficiency techniques** (Section 3, Strategy 3 description):

**Technique 1: Pruning the search space of $L$ by ignoring sequences with small answer disagreement.** The rationale is that including two LLMs that nearly always give the same answers provides no benefit to the cascade—if $L_i$ and $L_j$ agree on almost all queries, querying both is wasteful because the second one rarely adds new correct answers. The optimizer therefore **filters out candidate sequences** where successive LLMs have high answer overlap on the training set, retaining only sequences where the models provide complementary correct answers (i.e., when one fails, the other succeeds). This pruning is motivated by the Maximum Performance Improvement (MPI) metric (Figure 4), which measures exactly this complementarity: for each pair of LLMs, MPI quantifies the fraction of queries where LLM A is wrong but LLM B is correct. The optimizer retains only sequences where the pairwise MPI between adjacent models exceeds some threshold (the exact threshold is not specified numerically in the paper, but the logic is that the cascade benefits from diversity).

**Technique 2: Approximating the objective by interpolating within a few samples.** Rather than evaluating the expected accuracy of each candidate $(L, \tau)$ configuration on the full training set, the optimizer **samples a small subset of queries and thresholds**, evaluates accuracy and cost on those samples, and then **interpolates** between them to estimate the performance for untested threshold values. The paper describes this as "approximating the objective by interpolating it within a few samples." This works because, for a fixed LLM sequence $L$, the accuracy and cost are smooth (monotonic) functions of the threshold vector $\tau$—higher thresholds mean fewer acceptances at early stages, so more queries cascade further, increasing both accuracy and cost in a predictable way. Interpolation allows the optimizer to explore the continuous threshold space efficiently without exhaustive grid search.

**Output of the optimizer.** The optimizer returns a single configuration $(L^*, \tau^*)$: an ordered list of 3 LLM indices and their corresponding acceptance thresholds. This configuration minimizes expected cost while meeting the accuracy constraint (or equivalently, maximizes accuracy within the cost budget $b$). The optimizer is run once per dataset and budget level, producing a single cascade strategy used for all test queries.

**Why this optimizer design over alternatives:** The paper explicitly notes that the problem is "inherently a mixed-integer optimization and thus computationally expensive to solve." A brute-force approach—grid-searching over all 1320 possible sequences with fine-grained threshold sweeps for each—would require millions of LLM API calls to evaluate, defeating the cost-saving purpose. The two techniques together—pruning by answer diversity and interpolating threshold performance from sparse samples—reduce the evaluation cost by orders of magnitude while still finding configurations that achieve the reported 50–98% cost savings. The price of this approximation is that the optimizer may miss globally optimal configurations where seemingly redundant LLMs interact non-trivially with threshold calibration, but the empirical results (Figures 3 and 5) suggest the found configurations are effective.

#### Training and Evaluation Protocol

Although the paper does not dedicate a formal "Experimental Setup" section before presenting results, the technical details of how the cascade is trained and evaluated are embedded in Section 4 and are essential for understanding the approach.

**Dataset splits.** Each of the three datasets (HEADLINES: 10,000 queries, OVERRULING: 2,400 queries, COQA: 7,982 queries) is randomly split into a training set (used to learn the cascade configuration by the optimizer) and a test set (used for evaluation). The split ratio is not explicitly stated, but the training set must be large enough to: (1) train the scoring function $g(\cdot, \cdot)$ on (query, answer, correctness) triples, (2) estimate pairwise MPI between all LLM pairs for pruning, and (3) evaluate candidate cascade configurations for the optimizer.

**Scoring function training.** For each query in the training set, answers are generated from the LLM(s) being used, and each answer is labeled as correct (1) or incorrect (0) based on exact match with the ground truth. The scoring function (DistilBERT with a regression head) is fine-tuned on these labeled query-answer pairs. The training objective is standard mean squared error or binary cross-entropy (the paper does not specify which, but the task is a binary regression to [0, 1]). The cost of training the scoring function—both generating the LLM answers for the training queries and fine-tuning DistilBERT—is incurred once upfront, which the paper characterizes as a "one-time upfront cost" that amortizes over many test queries.

**Budget specification.** The budget $b$ is set as an absolute dollar amount the user is willing to spend per query on average. In the case study (Section 4, Figure 3), the budget is set to $6.50 for the HEADLINES dataset, described as "one-fifth of GPT-4's cost" (since GPT-4 costs approximately $33.10 on the full HEADLINES test set, as shown in Figure 3c). The optimizer then finds the cascade configuration that maximizes accuracy subject to the average cost constraint.

**Evaluation metrics.** The primary evaluation compares the cascade's test-set accuracy against that of individual LLMs, at the same (or lower) cost. Accuracy is measured as exact-match accuracy (for HEADLINES and OVERRULING, which are classification tasks with gold price trend and overruling labels respectively) or F1 score (for COQA, a reading comprehension task). Cost is computed by summing the actual API charges incurred during test-set inference.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: LLM Query Cost as a Formal, Optimizable Resource Rather Than a Fixed Overhead

The paper's most fundamental intellectual contribution is reframing LLM API inference cost from an unfortunate reality to be endured into a **first-class optimization variable** that can be systematically traded off against accuracy. Before this work, the dominant mental model for using LLM APIs was binary: either you could afford the best model (use GPT-4 for everything) or you couldn't (use a cheaper model and accept lower accuracy). The existence of heterogeneous pricing—where models charge differently for input tokens, output tokens, and per-request—was treated as a market curiosity rather than an optimization lever.

This reframing matters because it creates a new formal object: the **budget-constrained LLM selection problem** (Section 2). This is not the same as classical model selection (where the goal is to pick the single best model), nor is it the same as ensemble methods (where all models are queried in parallel and aggregated). The problem is to construct a *conditional computation policy*—which LLMs to invoke and in what order, depending on intermediate confidence signals—such that the *expected* cost per query stays within a budget while maximizing accuracy. The decision of *when to stop querying* is just as important as *which LLMs to query*.

This reframing is distinct from prior work on ML API selection (including the authors' own FrugalML line; Chen et al., 2020, 2022) in two ways. First, those methods assumed classification tasks with a fixed label set, where confidence calibration is straightforward because each model outputs a probability distribution over known classes. LLMs output arbitrary text, so the confidence signal cannot come from output probabilities—it must come from a learned scoring function that operates on the generated text itself. Second, the cost model is richer: prior ML API work typically assumed a fixed per-query cost, whereas LLM APIs have per-token costs that depend on both the prompt length and the generated answer length. This means the cost of a given model varies across queries (longer prompts cost more; longer answers cost more), introducing a query-dependent cost structure that makes the optimization more nuanced.

The practical significance is that this reframing opens up an entire design space that was previously invisible. Instead of asking "which LLM should I use?", practitioners should ask "given my query distribution, my accuracy requirements, and my budget, what is the optimal *routing policy* across available LLMs?" The paper demonstrates that even a simple 3-step cascade learned from this framing recovers 50–98% cost savings (Table 3), but the intellectual contribution is the framing itself—it provides the vocabulary and objective function for an entirely new subfield of LLM systems research.

---

### Innovation 2: LLM Diversity as Quantifiable, Complementary Failure Patterns That Enable Substitution

The paper's second conceptual contribution is to operationalize the intuition that "cheap models sometimes get right what expensive models get wrong" into a **quantifiable metric—Maximum Performance Improvement (MPI)—that directly measures the potential for cost savings through model combination**. Prior work had noted anecdotal evidence that different LLMs disagree (e.g., GPT-4 makes mistakes that GPT-3 doesn't), but the field lacked a systematic framework for measuring *how much* complementarity exists and *where* it comes from. MPI fills this gap.

MPI is defined as the probability that LLM A generates the correct answer while LLM B generates an incorrect answer, computed empirically on a labeled dataset. This is not merely an accuracy metric—it is a **potential savings metric**. If the MPI of a cheap model relative to an expensive one is high (say, 6%, as observed for GPT-J relative to GPT-4 on HEADLINES in Figure 4a), then incorporating that cheap model into a cascade can potentially improve accuracy over the expensive model alone—because the cheap model catches errors the expensive one made. If the MPI is low (the cheap model is almost never right when the expensive model is wrong), then adding the cheap model adds cost without benefit.

What makes this innovation distinctive is that it shifts the conversation from *model quality* (which model has higher average accuracy?) to *model complementarity* (which models make different mistakes?). The paper's heatmap visualizations in Figure 4 reveal a non-obvious structural pattern: cheap models have asymmetric complementarity relationships with expensive ones. For instance, on COQA (Figure 4c), there are 13% of queries where GPT-3 is correct but GPT-4 is wrong, while only 1% of queries show the reverse pattern. This means GPT-3 is not simply a "worse GPT-4"—it has a genuinely different error profile that can add value even when GPT-4 is available. Importantly, the MPI matrix is not symmetric (MPI of A relative to B ≠ MPI of B relative to A), and the paper treats this asymmetry as a feature, not a bug, when constructing cascade sequences.

This concept is fundamental rather than incremental because it provides a **diagnostic tool** for answering the question "should I bother using multiple LLMs?" that does not require actually building and evaluating a cascade. A practitioner can compute the MPI matrix on a small labeled sample and immediately see whether the cheap models in their marketplace offer genuine complementarity or merely worse copies of the expensive ones. The 50–98% cost savings in Table 3 are not achievable if the MPI matrix shows that all cheap models fail on exactly the subset of queries where the expensive model fails—the savings come specifically from routing queries to the model whose error profile excludes that query.

---

### Innovation 3: Learned Confidence Scoring on Arbitrary Natural Language Outputs Without Model Access

The third conceptual contribution is demonstrating that **a lightweight discriminative model (DistilBERT) can serve as an effective confidence scorer for open-ended LLM generation, operating purely on the surface text of the query and answer without any access to model internals, output logits, or token probabilities**. This is a non-trivial claim because prior approaches to LLM confidence estimation either assumed access to the generating model's probability distribution (e.g., sequence-level log-probability, perplexity, or token-level entropy) or relied on the LLM to self-evaluate its own outputs (which the paper implicitly critiques by not adopting this approach, citing evidence in Huang et al., 2023, that LLM self-correction is unreliable on reasoning tasks).

The innovation here is not the specific model choice (DistilBERT fine-tuned for regression) but the **architectural insight** that correctness detection for LLM outputs can be decoupled from the generating model entirely. This has two profound implications. First, it means the confidence scorer can be substantially smaller and cheaper than any LLM in the cascade—DistilBERT has ~66M parameters versus GPT-3's 175B—so scoring cost is negligible relative to generation cost. The cascade only works if scoring doesn't eat the savings, and this architectural choice guarantees that. Second, it means the scoring function can be trained once on a fixed set of query-answer pairs and then used to evaluate outputs from *any* LLM in the marketplace, regardless of provider, architecture, or training data. This model-agnostic property is essential because the cascade routes queries across heterogeneous APIs from different providers—a scoring function that only worked for OpenAI models would be useless for evaluating J1 or CoHere outputs.

The paper's case study in Figure 3 provides a concrete illustration of why this matters: the learned scoring thresholds (0.96 for GPT-J, 0.37 for J1-L) are radically different for different LLMs, reflecting the fact that the scoring function's calibration varies across output distributions. GPT-J's outputs need a high bar because the scorer tends to assign high scores to its incorrect answers on this task; J1-L's bar is much lower because its correct answers are easier to distinguish. A single scoring function adapts to these distributional differences naturally because it is trained on outputs from all LLMs, learning the quirks of each.

This contribution is a **fundamental enablement** rather than an incremental improvement. Without a cheap, model-agnostic confidence signal, the cascade concept is unimplementable—there is no way to decide when to stop querying. Prior cascade methods in classification had access to model confidence via output probabilities; this paper solves the harder problem of constructing confidence from raw text, making LLM cascade feasible for the first time. The fact that this works with a model as small as DistilBERT (Figure 3c: accuracy improves by 1.5% over GPT-4 while reducing cost 80%) is evidence that the correctness signal is learnable from surface features alone, at least for tasks where correctness is well-defined.

---

### Innovation 4: The Joint Optimization of Model Sequence Order and Per-Position Acceptance Thresholds as a Principled Cascade Design Problem

The fourth contribution is identifying that the **order of LLMs in a cascade matters as much as the choice of which LLMs to include**, and that optimal ordering depends on the interaction between model cost, model accuracy, and scoring function calibration at each position. This might seem obvious in retrospect—put the cheap models first so they get a chance to answer before the expensive ones are invoked—but the paper shows that the optimal ordering is non-trivial because it interacts with the threshold calibration.

The learned cascade on HEADLINES with a $6.50 budget (Figure 3a) illustrates the subtlety: the cascade is GPT-J → J1-L → GPT-4, with thresholds 0.96, 0.37, and (implicitly) 0.0 at the final position. This ordering is not merely "cheapest first." GPT-J is indeed the cheapest, but J1-L is *not* the second-cheapest—several models in Table 1 (like GPT-Curie, ChatGPT, and CoHere) sit between GPT-J and J1-L in cost. The optimizer selected J1-L second because its MPI relative to GPT-J is high (it catches many of GPT-J's errors, as visible in Figure 4a) while its cost is still substantially lower than GPT-4's. The optimizer also selected a *lower* threshold for J1-L (0.37) than for GPT-J (0.96), meaning it is more willing to accept J1-L's answers at moderate confidence—likely because J1-L's correct answers are more distinguishable by the scorer from its incorrect ones, or because the cost of cascading past J1-L (to GPT-4) is so high that accepting some incorrect J1-L answers is worth the savings.

This is a **conceptual advance** over prior cascade methods because those methods (Viola and Jones, 2004; Wang et al., 2011; Chen et al., 2020) typically assumed that the cascade order should follow either increasing cost or increasing accuracy, with thresholds set independently per stage. The LLM cascade problem reveals that cost, accuracy, and scoring calibration are **coupled**—the optimal threshold at position $i$ depends on what models come after it (because that determines the cost of cascading further) and on how well the scorer discriminates that particular model's outputs. The paper's specialized optimizer (Technique 1: pruning by answer disagreement; Technique 2: interpolation from sparse samples) is itself a methodological contribution, but the deeper insight is that this coupling exists and that ignoring it—for example, by setting thresholds based solely on scoring function calibration curves without considering downstream model costs—would produce suboptimal cascades.

The evidence for this coupling is in the learned configuration itself (Figure 3) and in the fact that FrugalGPT achieves cost savings far beyond what would be possible by simply substituting GPT-4 with the cheapest model that occasionally matches its accuracy. The 98% cost reduction on HEADLINES (Table 3: $0.60 vs. $33.10) to match GPT-4's accuracy is only possible because the cascade puts a cheap, high-threshold model first (GPT-J at 0.96—very selective, so it stops only when extremely confident), a medium-cost, moderate-threshold model second (J1-L at 0.37—less selective but still discriminating), and the expensive model last as a safety net for the hardest queries that neither cheap model could handle confidently. The thresholds at each position are tuned *jointly* with the model order, not set independently.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The paper evaluates on three datasets spanning distinct domains and task types: **HEADLINES** (10,000 queries from the financial news domain; task: predict gold price trend as up, down, neutral, or none from news headlines; sourced from Sinha & Khandait, 2021), **OVERRULING** (2,400 queries from the legal domain; task: determine whether a legal sentence constitutes an overruling, i.e., rejecting previous legal cases; sourced from Zheng et al., 2021), and **COQA** (7,982 queries from reading comprehension; task: answer questions about a passage in a conversational setting, adapted as direct query-answering; sourced from Reddy et al., 2019). Each dataset is randomly split into a training set (used to train the scoring function and learn the cascade configuration) and a test set (used for final evaluation); the split ratio is not explicitly stated.

- **Base model(s).** The paper uses 12 commercial LLM APIs from 5 providers, accessed as black-box services: **OpenAI** (GPT-Curie, ChatGPT, GPT-3, GPT-4), **AI21** (J1-Large, J1-Grande, J1-Jumbo), **Cohere** (Xlarge), **ForeFrontAI** (QA), and **Textsynth** (GPT-J, FAIRSEQ, GPT-Neox). These span model sizes from ~6B parameters (GPT-J, GPT-Curie) to 178B (J1-Jumbo) and unknown sizes (GPT-4, ChatGPT). Table 1 provides their exact pricing as of March 2023. The models are chosen to represent the diversity of the commercial LLM marketplace—they have heterogeneous pricing structures (some charge for input tokens only, some for both input and output, some add fixed per-query fees) and the cost difference between the cheapest and most expensive can reach two orders of magnitude (e.g., $0.20 vs. $30 for 10M input tokens).

- **Metrics.** **Accuracy** is the primary metric: exact-match accuracy for HEADLINES and OVERRULING (both are classification tasks with known label sets—gold price trend categories and binary overruling labels, respectively); for COQA, the paper uses answer correctness as defined by the dataset, though the specific scoring function (exact match vs. F1) is not explicitly stated. **Cost** is measured in US dollars, computed by summing the actual API charges that would be incurred during inference: for each query, cost = prompt length in tokens × per-token input rate + answer length in tokens × per-token output rate + any fixed per-request fee, using the pricing from Table 1. All cost figures are absolute dollar amounts for processing the full test set (e.g., "$33.1" for GPT-4 on HEADLINES in Figure 3c). The **Maximum Performance Improvement (MPI)** of LLM A relative to LLM B is the fraction of queries where A is correct but B is incorrect—a measure of complementarity, not raw accuracy.

- **Baselines.** The paper compares against **individual LLM APIs** used in isolation—each of the 12 LLMs queried with a standard prompt (few-shot examples, as specified in Table 2: 8 examples for HEADLINES, 5 for OVERRULING, 2 for COQA) with their answers evaluated directly. There are no other cascade or ensemble baselines compared, making the individual LLM performance the sole reference point. The paper does not implement the prompt adaptation or LLM approximation strategies it proposes in Section 3, so those remain as conceptual baselines without empirical comparison.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The "compute" is measured entirely in **financial cost** (US dollars), not in FLOPs, tokens generated, or wall-clock time. The budget constraint $b$ is a dollar amount per query on average. For the cascade, cost is computed per query as the sum of all LLMs actually invoked—if the cascade stops at position $z$, the cost includes all LLMs $L_1$ through $L_z$. This means the cost accounting is exact (API charges are the ground truth) and naturally handles the heterogeneity of pricing structures. The scoring function (DistilBERT) incurs no API cost since it runs locally; its training cost is treated as a one-time upfront expense not amortized into per-query costs. The difficulty estimation cost (generating the training answers to train the scoring function) is similarly amortized but not explicitly subtracted from the reported savings.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper uses a single train-test split per dataset with no mention of cross-validation, multiple random seeds, or confidence intervals. The test-set sizes are not explicitly stated, but based on the dataset sizes (HEADLINES: 10,000 total; OVERRULING: 2,400 total; COQA: 7,982 total) and the fact that the training set must be large enough to train the scoring function and learn cascade configurations, the test sets likely contain a few hundred to a few thousand queries—but this is not specified. The absence of statistical error bars or significance testing means that all reported accuracy and cost figures are point estimates with unknown variance.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Case Study on HEADLINES: Learned Cascade Architecture and Behavior

The paper begins its empirical analysis with a detailed case study on the HEADLINES dataset at a budget of $6.50 (one-fifth of GPT-4's cost of $33.10 on this dataset). The key findings, illustrated in Figure 3, are:

**Learned cascade configuration.** The optimizer selects a 3-step cascade: **GPT-J → J1-L → GPT-4**, with acceptance thresholds $\tau_1 = 0.96$ and $\tau_2 = 0.37$ (Figures 3a and 5a, left panel). The scoring function is DistilBERT fine-tuned for regression.

**Overall performance.** The cascade achieves **0.872 accuracy** (Figure 3c) compared to GPT-4's **0.857**—a **1.5% improvement**—while simultaneously reducing cost from **$33.10 to $6.50**, an **80% reduction**. This is the central demonstration that cost reduction and accuracy improvement can occur simultaneously: the cascade does not simply trade accuracy for savings but can actually *exceed* single-model performance by leveraging complementary correct answers from cheap models.

**Mechanism of savings.** FrugalGPT routes the majority of queries to GPT-J (the first model in the cascade) and accepts its answer only when the DistilBERT scorer assigns a reliability score above 0.96—a very high bar. Queries where GPT-J's answer scores below 0.96 cascade to J1-L, whose answer is accepted at a much lower threshold (0.37). Only queries failing both thresholds invoke GPT-4. The paper shows three example queries (Figure 5a, right panel) that illustrate three different cascade behaviors:
- **First example:** The headline "Gold holds ground at 1-month low on hawkish Fed comments." GPT-J and J1-L both produce low-scoring answers (0.16 and 0.13, respectively), so the cascade falls through to GPT-4, which correctly answers "Down." GPT-4 was necessary here.
- **Second example:** The headline "Gold off the lows after dismal U.S. GDP." GPT-J immediately generates a high-scoring answer (0.97 > 0.96), so the cascade stops and returns GPT-J's answer "Down." GPT-4 would have given the wrong answer ("Up"), but GPT-J caught the error—FrugalGPT not only saved cost but *avoided a GPT-4 mistake*.
- **Third example:** The headline "Kinross Gold upgraded to outperformer from neutral at CIBC." GPT-J's score is 0.44, below 0.96, so the cascade moves to J1-L. J1-L's score is 0.44 > 0.37, so its answer "Up" is accepted—correct and cheaper than invoking GPT-4. GPT-4 also answered "Up" in this case, so the cascade saved GPT-4's cost without accuracy loss, but for the wrong reason (both models were correct, so the cascade broke even on accuracy).

This case study establishes the proof of concept: a 3-step cascade with a small scoring model can simultaneously improve accuracy and reduce cost on a real-world dataset with commercial LLM APIs.

---

#### Pairwise LLM Complementarity: Maximum Performance Improvement (MPI) Across All Models

Before presenting aggregate cascade results, the paper quantifies the potential for cost savings by measuring the **Maximum Performance Improvement (MPI)** between every pair of the 12 LLMs on all three datasets. The MPI of LLM A relative to LLM B is the fraction of queries where A is correct but B is wrong—the maximum possible accuracy gain from adding A to a system that currently uses only B. The results are visualized as heatmaps in Figure 4.

**HEADLINES (Figure 4a):** GPT-4 is wrong on approximately 18% of queries where GPT-C (Curie) is correct, and approximately 6% of queries where GPT-J is correct. J1-L can improve GPT-4's performance on roughly 14% of queries, and J1-Jumbo on approximately 9%. The cheap models (GPT-J, GPT-C, J1-L) all offer non-trivial complementarity to GPT-4. The matrix is notably asymmetric: GPT-J's MPI relative to GPT-4 (6%) is much larger than GPT-4's MPI relative to GPT-J (the diagonal counterpart), confirming that cheap models catch different error patterns than expensive ones.

**OVERRULING (Figure 4b):** The MPI values are generally larger, indicating more complementarity. GPT-3 can improve GPT-4's performance on approximately 25% of queries, and ChatGPT on approximately 23%. Even FA-Q (ForeFrontAI QA) and GPT-J show MPIs relative to GPT-4 of 8% and 4%, respectively. The overall pattern is that multiple cheap-to-medium-cost models provide substantial complementarity to GPT-4 on this legal reasoning task.

**COQA (Figure 4c):** The complementarity pattern is striking: **GPT-3 improves GPT-4's performance on 13% of queries**, while GPT-4 improves GPT-3's performance on only 1% of queries (the reverse entry). This extreme asymmetry shows that GPT-3 and GPT-4 have largely non-overlapping error distributions on reading comprehension—GPT-3 gets many things right that GPT-4 gets wrong, while GPT-4 rarely helps where GPT-3 fails. ChatGPT and J1-L also show substantial MPIs relative to GPT-4 (7–10%).

**Why this matters.** The MPI analysis demonstrates that cheap models are not merely degraded copies of expensive ones—they have genuinely different failure modes, creating room for complementarity-based strategies to both reduce cost (by using cheap models for queries they get right) and improve accuracy (by catching expensive models' errors). The paper's title claim of "reducing cost while improving performance" is only possible because these MPI values are non-zero—if every cheap model's correct answers were a subset of GPT-4's correct answers, cascade could only save cost at the expense of accuracy. The measured MPIs (up to 25% on OVERRULING, 13% on COQA) show the actual improvement headroom.

#### Aggregate Cost Savings: Matching the Best Individual LLM's Accuracy

Table 3 reports the headline cost savings achieved by FrugalGPT's LLM cascade when matching the accuracy of the best individual LLM on each dataset:

| Dataset | Best Individual LLM | Cost of Best Individual LLM | FrugalGPT Cost (Same Accuracy) | Cost Savings |
|---------|---------------------|----------------------------|-------------------------------|--------------|
| HEADLINES | GPT-4 | $33.1 | $0.6 | **98.3%** |
| OVERRULING | GPT-4 | $9.7 | $2.6 | **73.3%** |
| COQA | GPT-3 | $72.5 | $29.6 | **59.2%** |

These numbers are derived from the trade-off curves in Figure 5: for each dataset, the paper identifies the point on the FrugalGPT curve where accuracy equals that of the best individual LLM (horizontal line in Figure 5 at GPT-4's accuracy for HEADLINES and OVERRULING, or GPT-3's for COQA), then reads off the corresponding cost on the x-axis.

**Why the savings differ by dataset.** The 98.3% savings on HEADLINES is the most dramatic, reflecting that many financial news headlines can be classified correctly by cheap models (GPT-J at $0.20/10M input tokens) with a sufficiently strict acceptance threshold, reserving GPT-4 for only the most ambiguous cases. The 59.2% savings on COQA is lower, partly because the best individual LLM on COQA is GPT-3 (not GPT-4), and GPT-3 is substantially cheaper than GPT-4 to begin with ($20/10M input tokens vs. $30), leaving less absolute room for savings. It also reflects that reading comprehension may require more expensive models on a larger fraction of queries than financial news classification.

**How savings are achieved.** FrugalGPT identifies queries that can be correctly answered by cheaper models and routes only those queries to them, avoiding expensive models for easy cases. The savings come from the fraction of queries where the first LLM in the cascade both produces a correct answer and receives a score above its threshold—for those queries, the cost is just that of the cheapest model. Queries that cascade further pay progressively more, but they constitute a minority. The numbers in Table 3 imply that on HEADLINES, the average cost per query under FrugalGPT is roughly 1/55th of GPT-4's cost, while accuracy matches—meaning the vast majority of queries are resolved at the GPT-J or J1-L stage.

#### Performance-Cost Trade-offs: The Full Pareto Frontier

Figure 5 presents the full trade-off curves between accuracy and cost for FrugalGPT and all individual LLMs on all three datasets. Key observations:

**HEADLINES (Figure 5a):**
- FrugalGPT's curve dominates all individual LLMs across the entire cost range from approximately $0 to $40. At any given cost, FrugalGPT achieves higher accuracy than any single LLM. At any given accuracy, FrugalGPT costs less.
- GPT-4 costs $33.10 and achieves 0.857 accuracy. FrugalGPT achieves 0.872 accuracy at $6.50 (the case study point) and reaches approximately 0.88 at $40—both higher accuracy and lower cost than GPT-4.
- The individual LLMs form a scattered cloud with no clear monotonic relationship between cost and accuracy: J1 is the second-most expensive model (after GPT-4) but its accuracy (roughly 0.78) is worse than GPT-3's (roughly 0.82 at lower cost) and ChatGPT's (roughly 0.80 at much lower cost). This demonstrates that in the LLM marketplace, **higher cost does not guarantee higher accuracy**—an important finding that motivates data-driven selection over simple heuristics like "use the most expensive model you can afford."
- At very low budgets (<$5), FrugalGPT matches or slightly exceeds the best cheap models (GPT-J and J1-L at approximately 0.75–0.78 accuracy) while costing less than any individual model that achieves comparable accuracy.

**OVERRULING (Figure 5b):**
- FrugalGPT achieves a **1% accuracy improvement over GPT-4 while reducing cost by 73%** (explicitly stated in the text). This is the instance where simultaneous improvement is quantitatively claimed.
- The individual LLMs show even more cost-accuracy non-monotonicity than on HEADLINES: J1-Jumbo (the most expensive model at $12+) achieves roughly 0.70 accuracy, while GPT-4 ($9.70) achieves approximately 0.90. The second-most expensive model on this dataset is GPT-3 (roughly $10), not J1-Jumbo, because the pricing structure differs for this task (shorter prompts, different output lengths, as noted in the cost model discussion).
- FrugalGPT's curve again dominates all individual models. At approximately $2–$3, FrugalGPT already exceeds the accuracy of all models except GPT-4, and by $5 it surpasses GPT-4's accuracy.
- Several cheap models (GPT-J, FSQ, J1-L, FA-Q) cluster in the bottom-left with accuracy around 0.60–0.70 at costs under $1, while GPT-4 sits alone in the top-right. FrugalGPT bridges this gap smoothly.

**COQA (Figure 5c):**
- The cost range is much larger ($0–$140) due to the dataset's characteristics (longer prompts or answers leading to higher total token counts). GPT-4 costs approximately $130 and achieves roughly 0.47 accuracy. GPT-3 costs approximately $72.50 and achieves roughly 0.45 accuracy—GPT-3 is both cheaper and comparably accurate, making it the "best individual LLM" for cost-effectiveness.
- FrugalGPT achieves approximately 0.48 accuracy at roughly $30, matching GPT-3's accuracy at less than half the cost (the 59.2% savings in Table 3). At $70, FrugalGPT achieves roughly 0.50 accuracy, exceeding GPT-4 by approximately 3 percentage points at approximately half GPT-4's cost.
- The non-monotonicity is again visible: J1-L and J1-Grande are relatively expensive ($40–$60) but achieve accuracy comparable to much cheaper models like ChatGPT and GPT-C ($10–$20). CoHere Xlarge is particularly expensive ($80+) with relatively low accuracy (~0.42).

**Example queries (Figure 5, right panels).** The paper provides example queries from each dataset that illustrate why the cascade can both save cost and improve accuracy. On OVERRULING (Figure 5b, right panel), three examples show:
- A legal statement where GPT-4 incorrectly says "No" (no overruling), but FrugalGPT accepts GPT-J's "Yes" answer at a score of 0.91 > 0.90, correctly identifying the overruling while avoiding GPT-4 entirely.
- A case where GPT-J's score is 0.20 < 0.90, then GPT-3's score is 0.80 < 0.90, so the cascade proceeds to the third model (ChatGPT), which gives "No"—the same as GPT-4 would have given, but cheaper.
- A case where GPT-J's score is 0.60 < 0.90, then J1-L scores 1.0 > 0.90, so its answer "Yes" is accepted—correct and cheap. GPT-4 also answered "Yes," so no accuracy gain here, but pure cost savings.

#### Summary of Quantitative Findings

Across all three datasets and budget levels, the experimental results consistently demonstrate:

1. **Cost savings at matched accuracy:** FrugalGPT reduces the cost of achieving the best individual LLM's performance by 59–98%, depending on the dataset (Table 3, Figure 5).

2. **Accuracy improvement at matched cost:** At the same total cost as the best individual LLM, FrugalGPT achieves higher accuracy—up to 4% absolute improvement (or roughly 1–3 percentage points in Figures 5a–c, with the 4% likely referring to the COQA improvement at $70 where FrugalGPT exceeds GPT-4). The paper claims "improve the accuracy over GPT-4 by 4% with the same cost" in the abstract, which appears to correspond to the rightmost points on the COQA curve (Figure 5c).

3. **Dominance:** FrugalGPT's trade-off curve Pareto-dominates all individual LLMs on all three datasets—there is no cost level at which any individual LLM outperforms FrugalGPT, and there is no accuracy level at which FrugalGPT costs more than the cheapest individual model achieving that accuracy.

4. **Non-monotonic cost-accuracy relationship:** Across datasets, more expensive models often have lower accuracy than cheaper alternatives (e.g., J1 vs. GPT-3 on HEADLINES; J1-Jumbo vs. GPT-4 on OVERRULING; CoHere Xlarge vs. ChatGPT on COQA), demonstrating that model selection cannot be guided by cost alone—it requires empirical evaluation.

5. **Complementarity as the mechanism:** The MPI matrices (Figure 4) explain *why* these gains are possible: cheap models are correct on a non-trivial fraction of queries where expensive models fail (6–25% across datasets), and a learned scoring function can identify enough of these correct cheap-model answers to route them appropriately.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

The paper does not contain a formal ablation study section. The following analyses are present, though several are qualitative or embedded in the main results rather than systematic ablations:

**Effect of budget level on cascade configuration (implicit in Figure 5).** As the budget $b$ changes, the optimizer selects different cascade configurations (different LLM sequences and thresholds). The paper shows this implicitly through the smooth trade-off curve in Figure 5—each point on the FrugalGPT curve corresponds to a potentially different cascade configuration optimized for that budget. However, the paper does not show *how* the configuration changes with budget (which LLMs are selected, how the thresholds shift), which would be an informative ablation for understanding how the optimizer allocates models across budget regimes.

**Scoring function architecture (implicit).** The paper uses DistilBERT as the scoring function but does not ablate this choice. No comparison is provided against alternative discriminative architectures (e.g., BERT-base, RoBERTa, smaller models like ALBERT or TinyBERT), nor against generative approaches (e.g., using an LLM to self-evaluate, or using a cheap LLM as the scorer by prompting it to judge correctness). The claim that DistilBERT is "considerably smaller and therefore less expensive than all LLMs considered here" is true but unquantified—the actual inference cost of DistilBERT per query (in dollars or compute time) is not reported, and no sensitivity analysis examines whether a larger scorer would improve cascade accuracy or whether a smaller scorer would suffice.

**Cascade length ($m = 3$).** The paper fixes the cascade length at 3 and states this "simplifies the optimization space and already demonstrates good results." No comparison is made against cascade lengths of 2 or 4+, which would reveal whether a 2-model cascade captures most of the benefit (suggesting diminishing returns to longer chains) or whether 4+ models could yield further savings. Given the combinatorial growth of the search space with $m$, this is a practically significant ablation that was not performed.

**Answer overlap pruning threshold (implicit in optimizer description).** The optimizer prunes candidate LLM sequences by ignoring those with "small answer disagreement" between successive models (motivated by MPI matrices in Figure 4). The threshold for "small" is not specified, and no ablation explores sensitivity to this pruning parameter—whether a more aggressive pruning (higher disagreement threshold) misses good configurations or a more permissive pruning (lower threshold) adds unnecessary optimization cost.

**Training set size for scoring function.** The scoring function is trained on LLM answers generated from the training set, but the number of training examples used is not specified, and no ablation examines how cascade performance degrades as training data decreases. This is a practically relevant question: if the scoring function can be trained on a few hundred labeled examples rather than thousands, the upfront cost of adopting FrugalGPT drops substantially.

**Scoring function calibration per LLM.** Figure 3a shows that the learned thresholds for different LLMs in the cascade are very different (0.96 for GPT-J vs. 0.37 for J1-L on HEADLINES). This implies that the scoring function's output distribution varies per LLM. But the paper does not show calibration curves (predicted score vs. empirical accuracy per LLM) that would explain *why* these thresholds differ. Are GPT-J's high scores inflated (poor calibration), necessitating the 0.96 threshold, or does J1-L genuinely produce more distinguishable output? The absence of calibration analysis makes it difficult to diagnose whether threshold tuning compensates for scoring function miscalibration on certain LLMs' outputs.

**Prompt length and number of in-context examples.** The paper uses fixed prompts with a specified number of in-context examples per dataset (Table 2: 8 for HEADLINES, 5 for OVERRULING, 2 for COQA). No ablation varies the prompt format or number of examples to measure how prompt cost interacts with cascade benefits. Section 3 proposed "prompt adaptation" as a cost-saving strategy, but this is never empirically tested or combined with LLM cascade. This is a significant gap because prompt length directly affects the input token cost for every LLM in the cascade, and shorter prompts could amplify savings.

**False positives and false negatives in cascade decisions.** The paper does not report confusion matrices for the cascade's stopping decisions: how often does the cascade accept an incorrect answer (false positive—the scorer assigns a high score to a wrong answer, stopping the cascade prematurely)? How often does it reject a correct answer (false negative—cascading further unnecessarily, increasing cost)? These error rates would characterize the scoring function's reliability and the cascade's cost-accuracy trade-off more precisely than aggregate accuracy and cost figures alone.

**Comparison to random or heuristic cascade baselines.** The paper does not compare FrugalGPT's learned cascade against simpler heuristics, such as: (a) a cascade where LLMs are ordered purely by cost (cheapest first) with fixed thresholds (e.g., 0.5 for all positions), (b) a cascade using majority voting across parallel LLMs rather than sequential stopping, or (c) a cascade where the scoring function is replaced by simple heuristics like answer length or keyword matching. These comparisons would isolate the contribution of the learned scoring function and specialized optimizer from the inherent benefit of using multiple LLMs.

**No combination with prompt adaptation or LLM approximation.** The paper proposes three strategies in Section 3 (prompt adaptation, LLM approximation, LLM cascade) and mentions that "combining approaches within and across different strategies can lead to further cost reduction and performance enhancement." However, no experiments combine any of these strategies. The cascade uses fixed prompts for each LLM; it does not select shorter prompts for cheaper models (prompt adaptation). It does not use a completion cache to avoid redundant LLM calls for similar queries. It does not fine-tune a cheap model on expensive models' outputs (LLM approximation). All reported gains come from cascade alone, leaving the claimed benefits of composition empirically unvalidated.

### Critical Assessment

#### Does the Cascade Actually Match the Best Individual LLM's Accuracy at 98% Cost Reduction?

The 98.3% cost reduction on HEADLINES (Table 3) is the paper's most striking claim: matching GPT-4's 0.857 accuracy for $0.60 instead of $33.10. This claim is technically supported by Figure 3c and the trade-off curve in Figure 5a, but several caveats apply:

- **The accuracy comparison is point-estimate without statistical uncertainty.** The test set size on HEADLINES is not stated. If it is, say, 1,000 queries (10% of the 10,000 total), then the difference between 0.857 (GPT-4) and 0.872 (FrugalGPT) is approximately 15 queries—a difference of 1.5 percentage points. Without confidence intervals, we cannot know whether FrugalGPT genuinely exceeds GPT-4's accuracy or whether the difference is noise. The paper's claim of "improving accuracy by 4%" (abstract) on COQA is subject to the same concern.

- **The cost of training is not amortized.** The scoring function is trained on LLM-generated answers for the training set—this means *all* LLMs under consideration must be queried on the training set, generating answers that are then labeled against ground truth and used to train DistilBERT. Additionally, the optimizer evaluates candidate cascade configurations on the training set. These upfront costs are not included in the reported $0.60 per-query cost. For a small test set, the amortized training cost per test query could be substantial. The paper acknowledges that "learning the LLM cascade itself requires resources" (Section 5) and frames it as a one-time cost that amortizes when the test set is large, but provides no quantification of the training cost or break-even point.

- **The budget is dataset-specific and must be specified upfront.** The $6.50 budget on HEADLINES was chosen as "one-fifth of GPT-4's cost." In a real deployment, the user may not know GPT-4's cost on their specific test distribution in advance, making budget specification difficult. The paper does not explore sensitivity to budget misspecification—what happens if the user sets the budget too low or too high?

**Verdict:** The cost savings are real and substantial under the paper's accounting, but the exact magnitude (98.3%) should be interpreted as an upper bound that does not include training costs, statistical uncertainty, or budget misspecification sensitivity. The savings are likely still large (well over 90%) even after accounting for these factors on large-scale deployments, but the paper does not provide the analyses needed to confirm this.

---

#### Does the Cascade Genuinely Improve Accuracy Over the Best Single LLM?

The paper claims that FrugalGPT can "improve the accuracy over GPT-4 by 4% with the same cost" (abstract). This claim is visible in Figure 5c (COQA) at the rightmost points: at approximately $70, FrugalGPT achieves roughly 0.50 accuracy vs. GPT-4's roughly 0.47—an improvement of approximately 3 percentage points (which may correspond to a 4% *relative* improvement, i.e., 0.03/0.47 ≈ 6.4%, or an absolute 4 percentage points depending on the exact reading of the curve). On OVERRULING (Figure 5b), FrugalGPT achieves a 1% accuracy gain at 73% cost reduction.

**What enables accuracy improvement:** The mechanism is clear from the MPI matrices (Figure 4) and the case study examples (Figures 3b, 5a–c right panels): cheap LLMs sometimes produce correct answers on queries where the best single LLM (typically GPT-4) is wrong. If the scoring function can reliably identify those correct cheap-model answers, the cascade can outperform any single model. This is not a free lunch—it requires the scoring function to have non-trivial discriminative power and the cheap models to have non-zero MPI relative to the expensive one.

**What limits the improvement:** The accuracy improvement is bounded by the MPI values. On HEADLINES, the maximum MPI of any cheap model relative to GPT-4 is approximately 18% (GPT-C in Figure 4a), so the theoretically achievable improvement is capped at that level. The actual improvement (1.5%) is well below the theoretical maximum, indicating that the scoring function cannot perfectly identify all cases where the cheap model is right and GPT-4 is wrong—it makes errors that both reject correct cheap-model answers (cascading unnecessarily) and accept incorrect cheap-model answers (stopping prematurely with a wrong answer).

**Verdict:** The accuracy improvement claim is empirically supported—the cascade does exceed single-model accuracy on HEADLINES and OVERRULING—but the magnitude is modest (1–4%) and varies by dataset. The improvement is conditional on the MPI matrix having non-trivial values and the scoring function having sufficient discriminative power. The paper does not characterize *which* queries benefit from the cascade vs. single-model, making it unclear whether the improvement is concentrated in a specific query type or broadly distributed.

---

#### Does the Paper Demonstrate That Prompt Adaptation and LLM Approximation Also Reduce Cost?

**No.** The paper proposes three strategies in Section 3 (prompt adaptation, LLM approximation, and LLM cascade) but only empirically evaluates the LLM cascade (Section 4). The prompt adaptation strategies (prompt selection, query concatenation; Figure 2a–b) are described conceptually but never implemented or tested. The LLM approximation strategies (completion cache, model fine-tuning; Figure 2c–d) are similarly described but not evaluated. The paper's title and abstract ("FrugalGPT: How to Use Large Language Models While Reducing Cost and Improving Performance") imply a broader contribution than what is empirically demonstrated.

**Why this matters:** The absence of empirical results for prompt adaptation and LLM approximation means the paper's claimed framework is largely aspirational. The only supported claim is that LLM cascade can reduce cost while maintaining or improving accuracy. The prompt adaptation idea—using shorter prompts to reduce per-query cost—has an intuitive cost benefit, but it also has a potential accuracy cost (fewer in-context examples may reduce model performance) that is not characterized. The completion cache idea depends on query similarity in the deployment distribution, which is not analyzed. The fine-tuning idea depends on the transferability of expensive model knowledge to cheap models, which is not tested.

**Verdict:** The paper overclaims in its framing. The three-strategy framework is a research agenda, not a validated system. Only the LLM cascade is empirically demonstrated. A more accurate title would specify "LLM Cascade for Cost-Efficient API Usage" rather than implying a comprehensive framework has been validated.

---

#### Is the Cascade's Performance Generalizable Beyond the Three Tested Datasets?

The paper evaluates on three datasets: financial news classification (HEADLINES), legal document classification (OVERRULING), and reading comprehension (COQA). These span different domains and task types, which is a reasonable breadth for an initial study. However, several limitations restrict generalizability:

- **All tasks have objective correctness criteria.** HEADLINES and OVERRULING are effectively classification tasks (gold price trend, overruling status) with clear ground-truth labels. COQA is extractive QA with short answer spans. The scoring function is trained on query-answer pairs labeled for correctness via exact match or simple comparison to ground truth. For open-ended generation tasks (summarization, dialogue, creative writing), where "correctness" is subjective or multi-dimensional, it is unclear how to train a scoring function or whether the cascade approach would work at all. The paper does not address this.

- **The LLM marketplace is a snapshot from March 2023.** The pricing (Table 1) and model capabilities are a moving target. GPT-4's cost has since changed, new models have been released, and the cost ranking among models may have shifted. The specific cascade configurations learned (GPT-J → J1-L → GPT-4 on HEADLINES) are time-bound and would need relearning as the marketplace evolves.

- **The cascade is task-specific, not query-specific within a task.** The cascade configuration is learned once per dataset and applied uniformly to all test queries from that dataset. There is no per-query adaptation—the scoring function evaluates answers, but the choice of cascade order and thresholds is fixed. This means the cascade cannot adapt to subpopulations within a dataset (e.g., short vs. long queries, factual vs. inferential questions) unless the optimizer somehow captures this heterogeneity in the average-case optimization. The paper does not analyze whether different subsets of queries would benefit from different cascade configurations.

- **The paper does not evaluate on a held-out distribution.** All experiments use a random split from the same dataset, meaning the training and test distributions are identical. This tests interpolation (queries from the same distribution as training) but not extrapolation (queries from a different distribution, which is common in production deployments where the query mix shifts over time). The scoring function's ability to generalize to distribution shifts is untested.

**Verdict:** The cascade is demonstrated on a reasonable but limited set of tasks. The generalizability to open-ended generation, to evolving LLM marketplaces, and to distribution-shifted queries is entirely untested. The paper would have been stronger with at least one experiment on a dataset with a different correctness criterion (e.g., ROUGE for summarization) or a distribution-shift experiment (e.g., train on one subdomain of HEADLINES and test on another).

---

#### Missing Baselines and Ablations

The paper's empirical evaluation, while demonstrating clear cost savings for LLM cascade, omits several baselines and ablations that would strengthen confidence in the specific design choices:

- **No comparison to naive cascades.** A cascade that uses the same LLM order but fixed thresholds (e.g., 0.5 for all positions) would isolate the contribution of the learned threshold optimization from the inherent benefit of sequential model invocation.

- **No comparison to parallel ensemble with selection.** Instead of a sequential cascade, one could query 3 LLMs in parallel and use the scoring function to select the best answer. This would cost 3× the per-query cost of a single LLM invocation for *every* query, but would never cascade past a wrong answer from the first model (since all three answers are available). At some budget levels, this may be optimal—the paper does not characterize when sequential stopping dominates parallel selection.

- **No comparison to single-model with increased sampling.** An alternative cost-reduction strategy is to use a single cheap model (e.g., GPT-J) with multiple samples and majority voting, rather than cascading across different models. The paper does not compare against this, even though prior work (Arora et al., 2022, cited in the paper) showed that aggregating cheap model outputs can match expensive model performance on some tasks.

- **No ablation of scoring function model size.** DistilBERT is chosen for its small size, but no comparison is made against even smaller models (e.g., a linear classifier on embeddings) or larger models (e.g., BERT-base). The sensitivity of cascade performance to scorer quality is therefore unknown.

- **No ablation of cascade length.** The choice of $m = 3$ is a design decision without empirical justification. A comparison with $m = 2$ and $m = 4$ would reveal whether the third model adds meaningful benefit or whether most gains come from just two models.

These omissions do not invalidate the paper's central finding—that LLM cascade can substantially reduce cost—but they mean the paper does not characterize *how much* of the benefit comes from the specific cascade design (learned thresholds, length-3 chains, DistilBERT scorer) versus simpler alternatives. A practitioner cannot tell from this paper whether a 2-model cascade with fixed 0.5 thresholds would achieve, say, 80% of the savings with 10% of the implementation complexity.

---

#### Test Set Sizes and Statistical Reliability

The paper does not report test set sizes, confidence intervals, or any measure of statistical uncertainty. The three datasets have 10,000, 2,400, and 7,982 total queries, respectively, but the split ratio between training and test is not stated. If a typical 80/20 split is used, the test sets would have approximately 2,000 (HEADLINES), 480 (OVERRULING), and 1,596 (COQA) queries.

At these sizes, a 1.5% accuracy difference (the HEADLINES result: 0.872 vs. 0.857) corresponds to approximately 30 queries out of 2,000. The binomial confidence interval for 0.872 with $n = 2000$ is approximately ±1.5 percentage points at 95% confidence, meaning the difference could plausibly be zero. The paper's failure to report confidence intervals means the claimed accuracy improvement over single-model performance cannot be assessed for statistical significance.

This is particularly concerning for OVERRULING, where the test set may be as small as 480 queries (if an 80/20 split is used). An accuracy difference of 1 percentage point (the reported 1% gain) corresponds to roughly 5 queries out of 480—well within statistical noise. The paper's headline claims of "improving accuracy" should therefore be treated as suggestive rather than statistically established, especially on smaller datasets.

---

#### Summary of Critical Assessment

The paper demonstrates a valuable and practical finding: **LLM cascade with a learned scoring function can substantially reduce API costs while maintaining accuracy on classification-style NLP tasks**. The cost savings are large (59–98%), the mechanism is well-explained (complementary failure patterns identified via MPI, sequential routing with confidence thresholds), and the results are consistent across three datasets from different domains.

However, the paper's broader framing as a comprehensive "FrugalGPT framework" overstates what was empirically validated. Only one of three proposed strategy families (LLM cascade) was tested; the other two (prompt adaptation, LLM approximation) remain conceptual. The claimed accuracy improvements over single-model baselines are small in absolute terms (1–4%) and are reported without statistical confidence intervals, making their significance uncertain—especially on smaller test sets like OVERRULING. The cascade's training cost is acknowledged but not quantified or amortized, so the reported savings are best-case upper bounds. The generalizability to open-ended generation tasks, to evolving LLM marketplaces, and to distribution-shifted queries is entirely untested.

The paper is strongest as a **proof of concept for LLM cascade** and as a **call to action for budget-aware LLM API usage**. Its practical impact lies in the demonstration that even a simple cascade can recover most of the cost while preserving or slightly improving accuracy—a finding that should motivate practitioners to move beyond single-model deployment. But as a comprehensive solution to the problem of "how to use LLMs affordably and accurately," the paper presents only an initial, narrow empirical foundation.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### Limitation 1: Training the Scoring Function Requires Labeled Data from the Target Distribution

**The assumption or constraint.** The LLM cascade's scoring function—the DistilBERT model that decides when an answer is reliable enough to stop the cascade—must be trained on `(query, answer, correctness)` triples from the same or similar distribution as the test queries. The paper explicitly acknowledges this dependency in Section 5:

> "To train the LLM cascade strategy in FrugalGPT, we need some labeled examples. And in order for the cascade to work well, the training examples should be from the same or similar distribution as the test examples."

This means that for every new task, dataset, or deployment context, the practitioner must: (1) collect ground-truth answers for a training set of queries, (2) generate candidate answers from every LLM under consideration by querying the APIs, (3) label each generated answer as correct or incorrect against the ground truth, and (4) fine-tune the scoring function on this data. The cost of steps (2) and (3) alone involves paying for API calls to *all* candidate LLMs on *all* training queries—potentially thousands of queries multiplied by 12 APIs—before any cost savings materialize at test time.

**The consequence.** In a truly novel deployment scenario where no labeled data exists—for example, a company launching a new customer service chatbot for a proprietary product with unique terminology and query patterns—the upfront cost of labeling data and training the scoring function could be prohibitive or even infeasible. The cascade cannot be deployed zero-shot. The paper positions this as a "one-time upfront cost" that amortizes over a larger test set, but it provides **no quantification** of how large the training set must be, how much the upfront labeling costs, or what the breakeven point is (i.e., how many test queries must be processed before the savings exceed the training investment). A small deployment with only a few thousand test queries might never recoup the training cost, making the cascade economically worse than simply using a single mid-tier model.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. The paper does not report the training set sizes, the cost of generating training answers from all LLMs, the number of training queries needed to achieve the reported scoring function accuracy, or any sensitivity analysis showing how cascade performance degrades as training data decreases. The optimizer's own evaluation cost—running candidate cascade configurations on the training set—is also unaccounted for. These are all real dollar costs that a practitioner must pay before seeing any test-time savings, and the paper provides no guidance for estimating them.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges the limitation in Section 5 but does not attempt to measure or mitigate it. It suggests no method for reducing the labeling burden (e.g., using a single expensive LLM as a weak labeler for the scoring function, or using cross-task transfer for the scoring function). The framing as a "one-time upfront cost" is optimistic but not backed by data—it remains an open question whether the upfront investment pays off at realistic deployment scales. A practitioner considering FrugalGPT would need to estimate this cost independently, with no guidance from the paper.

---

### Limitation 2: The Scoring Function is Task-Specific and Does Not Generalize Across Datasets

**The assumption or constraint.** The scoring function `g(q, a)` is trained on a specific dataset's query-answer-correctness triples. It learns task-specific patterns of what constitutes a correct answer for that particular task. The paper trains separate scoring functions for HEADLINES, OVERRULING, and COQA—each is fine-tuned on that dataset's own training queries and LLM-generated answers. There is no experiment where a scoring function trained on one dataset is evaluated on another, and the paper makes no claim of cross-task generalization.

**The consequence.** Every new task requires a new scoring function, which means a new labeled training set, a new round of LLM answer generation, and a new fine-tuning step. For an organization deploying LLMs across dozens or hundreds of different query types (customer support covering many product categories, content moderation across different policy domains, document processing across different legal jurisdictions), the approach does not scale: each task would need its own training pipeline, its own cascade configuration, and its own scoring model. The overhead grows linearly with the number of distinct tasks, which is precisely the regime where the cascade's cost savings would be most valuable (many tasks, each with moderate query volume) but where the per-task training cost is hardest to amortize.

Furthermore, the scoring function is tied to the specific LLM APIs used during training. If a provider updates their model (e.g., GPT-4 is replaced by GPT-4-turbo with different output characteristics), or if new models enter the marketplace, the scoring function's calibration on those new outputs is unknown. The paper provides no evidence on whether the DistilBERT scorer can evaluate outputs from models it was not trained on—and since different LLMs produce answers with different styles, levels of verbosity, and error patterns, out-of-distribution generalization is not guaranteed.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides no cross-dataset evaluation of the scoring function, no analysis of how scoring function performance varies with training set size, and no experiment where a scoring function trained on one set of LLMs is tested on a different set of LLMs. The three datasets are treated as completely independent experiments with no shared components.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not discuss cross-task transfer, few-shot adaptation of the scoring function, or using a single multi-task scoring function trained on a diverse collection of tasks. These are natural directions for future work but are entirely absent from the current study. A practitioner with multiple tasks would either need to replicate the full training pipeline for each task or accept unknown degradation from reusing a scoring function across tasks.

---

### Limitation 3: The Cascade Does Not Help on Queries Where All Cheap Models Fail

**The assumption or constraint.** The LLM cascade fundamentally relies on the existence of cheap models that can correctly answer queries that the expensive model would get wrong—this is the Maximum Performance Improvement (MPI) signal quantified in Figure 4. If for a given query, every cheap model produces an incorrect answer, the cascade has no correct answer to find in its early stages. It will cascade through all the cheap models, accumulating their costs, and eventually fall through to the expensive model—or, if the expensive model also fails on that query, produce a wrong answer after paying the full cascade cost (which may exceed the cost of querying only the expensive model, since all the intermediate cheap-model calls are wasted).

**The consequence.** The cascade provides no benefit—neither cost savings nor accuracy improvement—on queries that are genuinely difficult for all available models. Worse, on such queries, the cascade may cost *more* than simply querying the best model directly, because it pays for failed intermediate model calls before reaching the final model. The paper does not characterize this worst-case cost behavior: what is the maximum cost a single query can incur, and how frequently does that happen? For some queries, the cascade could invoke all 3 models in sequence and still produce a wrong answer, paying `cost(GPT-J) + cost(J1-L) + cost(GPT-4)` for zero accuracy benefit over just calling GPT-4 alone.

This is not a rare edge case. On the hardest queries in each dataset (analogous to the "bin 5" phenomenon in the inference scaling paper), where even the best model's pass@1 is near zero, the cascade will systematically overpay for wrong answers. The problem is that the scoring function `g(q, a)` is trained to estimate answer correctness, not query difficulty—it cannot recognize that *all* candidate answers are likely wrong and that cascading further is futile. The cascade's stopping rule is `g(q, a_i) >= τ_i`, which is a condition on the *absolute* quality of the current answer, not a comparison between the current answer and what future models might produce. There is no mechanism for the cascade to say "this query is too hard for all my models; I should give up cheaply rather than try everything."

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper does not provide a per-query cost breakdown or a worst-case cost analysis. The example queries in Figure 5 (right panels) show several cases where the cascade falls through to the final model, but none where all three models fail—the paper does not quantify how often this occurs. Table 3 reports *average* cost savings, which can be high even if a minority of queries incur amplified costs, but the distribution of per-query costs is not shown. A histogram or CDF of per-query costs under the cascade versus under single-model deployment would directly reveal whether the cascade creates a long tail of expensive failures.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper optimizes for *average* cost subject to *average* accuracy, which does not protect against worst-case cost amplification. A risk-averse practitioner—for example, one with a hard per-query cost cap—would need guarantees the cascade does not provide. Possible mitigations (a maximum cascade depth, a global "give up" threshold on the scoring function, or a budget-cap mechanism that stops after a fixed dollar amount) are not discussed.

---

### Limitation 4: Lag Between Changes in the Marketplace and the Cascade Configuration

**The assumption or constraint.** The entire cascade configuration—which LLMs to include, in what order, with what thresholds—is statically optimized once on a training set and then deployed unchanged. This assumes the LLM marketplace is static: the available models, their pricing, and their performance characteristics remain constant between training and deployment. In reality, commercial LLM APIs are updated frequently (model versions change, pricing tiers are adjusted, new models are released, old models are deprecated), and the optimal cascade configuration can become stale.

**The consequence.** If a new, cheaper, and more accurate model enters the marketplace after the cascade is trained, the deployed cascade cannot take advantage of it without a full retraining cycle—new training data must be generated from the new model, the scoring function may need retraining if the new model's output distribution differs substantially, and the optimizer must re-search the expanded configuration space. Conversely, if a model in the cascade is deprecated or its pricing increases, the cascade may continue routing queries to a suboptimal or unavailable model. If a model's performance changes (e.g., an API provider releases an improved version under the same name), the scoring function's calibration on that model's outputs may drift, causing the thresholds `τ_i` to become miscalibrated—accepting too many incorrect answers (if the model got better, so old thresholds are too lax) or cascading too often (if the model got worse, so old thresholds are too strict).

This is not a hypothetical concern. Between the paper's March 2023 pricing snapshot (Table 1) and any deployment date, OpenAI has released GPT-4-turbo (cheaper than GPT-4 with comparable accuracy), multiple providers have adjusted their pricing, and new models (Claude, Gemini, Llama-based APIs) have entered the market. A cascade optimized in March 2023 would be suboptimal within months. The paper's approach requires continuous monitoring and periodic retraining to maintain optimality—a cost the paper does not discuss.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None directly, because the paper is a static snapshot. However, the MPI matrices in Figure 4 indirectly demonstrate the sensitivity: the complementarity patterns (which models catch which other models' errors) are strongly dataset-dependent and likely time-dependent as well. The paper provides no analysis of how quickly the cascade configuration degrades as the underlying models change, and no proposal for detecting or adapting to marketplace shifts without full retraining.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address marketplace dynamics. It acknowledges in Section 5 that "the rapid development of LLM" means the paper is "not meant to be comprehensive," but this is a general caveat rather than a treatment of the staleness problem. A practical deployment would need a monitoring system that tracks API response quality over time and triggers re-optimization when performance drifts, but the paper provides no design for such a system.

---

### Limitation 5: Only LLM Cascade Is Empirically Validated; the Framework's Other Strategies Are Conceptual

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's title, abstract, and Section 3 present FrugalGPT as a framework encompassing three families of strategies: prompt adaptation, LLM approximation, and LLM cascade. The abstract states the paper will "outline and discuss three types of strategies," and Section 3 devotes substantial space to describing prompt selection, query concatenation, completion caching, and model fine-tuning with diagrams (Figure 2a–d). However, **only LLM cascade is implemented and evaluated** (Section 4). The other strategies are described at a conceptual level but never tested—not individually, and not in combination with the cascade.

**The consequence.** The paper's empirical contribution is narrower than its framing suggests. A practitioner reading the abstract might reasonably expect that the paper validates multiple complementary cost-reduction techniques and demonstrates how they compose. Instead, they find that only one technique (LLM cascade) has empirical support, and the claimed benefits of composition—"combining approaches within and across different strategies can lead to further cost reduction and performance enhancement" (Section 3)—are entirely speculative. The paper does not establish, for example, whether using shorter prompts for the cheap models in a cascade (prompt adaptation + cascade) yields additional savings beyond cascade alone, or whether fine-tuning a cheap model on GPT-4's outputs and then using it as the first stage of a cascade improves accuracy.

This matters for two reasons. First, some of the proposed strategies may not work as intuited. Prompt selection (using fewer in-context examples) reduces prompt length and therefore cost, but it may also reduce model accuracy—the net effect on the cost-accuracy Pareto frontier is unknown and may be negative if the accuracy drop outweighs the cost savings. Query concatenation (batching multiple queries into one prompt) reduces amortized prompt cost but may degrade per-query accuracy because the model must divide its attention across multiple questions. Completion caching (reusing answers for similar queries) requires a similarity function and a cache-hit policy, both of which have failure modes (returning a cached answer for a subtly different query). None of these failure modes are characterized. Second, the paper's claim to have identified a *framework* for cost reduction is only partially supported—the framework is a taxonomical proposal, not an empirically validated toolkit.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Zero empirical results for prompt adaptation or LLM approximation. Section 4 is exclusively about LLM cascade. The paper's figures, tables, and quantitative claims all derive from cascade experiments. The prompt adaptation and LLM approximation sections (Figures 2a–d) are purely illustrative.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not acknowledge this gap. Section 5 discusses limitations (labeled data requirement, training cost, environmental factors) but does not mention that two of the three proposed strategy families are untested. A reader who does not carefully distinguish the conceptual framework (Section 3) from the empirical evaluation (Section 4) may overestimate the paper's validated contributions.

---

### Limitation 6: Accuracy Improvements Over Single Models Are Small in Absolute Terms and Statistically Uncharacterized

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's headline claims—"improve the accuracy over GPT-4 by 4% with the same cost" (abstract) and "improves the accuracy by 1.5% compared to GPT-4" on HEADLINES (Section 4)—report accuracy improvements that are small in absolute magnitude, measured on test sets of unreported size, and presented without any measure of statistical uncertainty.

**The consequence.** A practitioner deciding whether to adopt FrugalGPT over simply using GPT-4 needs to know whether the claimed accuracy improvement is reliable or whether it could disappear under replication. The paper provides no tools to answer this. On HEADLINES, the improvement is 0.872 vs. 0.857—a difference of 1.5 percentage points. If the test set has 2,000 queries (a reasonable estimate given the 10,000 total dataset size and an 80/20 split), this corresponds to FrugalGPT getting approximately 30 more queries correct than GPT-4. The 95% binomial confidence interval for an observed accuracy of 0.872 with `n = 2000` is approximately ±1.5 percentage points—meaning the true accuracy could plausibly be 0.857, identical to GPT-4. On OVERRULING, the claimed 1% accuracy gain at 73% cost reduction is even more fragile: with an estimated test set of only 480 queries (20% of 2,400), 1 percentage point corresponds to roughly 5 queries, well within the noise floor for any reasonable confidence interval.

The paper does not report test set sizes (the split ratio between train and test is never specified), does not provide confidence intervals, does not perform cross-validation across multiple splits, and does not report statistical significance tests. This means the central claim that the cascade *improves* accuracy—rather than merely matching it at lower cost—is not statistically substantiated. The cost reduction claims (Table 3) are not affected by this issue—they depend on the ability to *match* accuracy, not exceed it, and the paper's cost accounting is deterministic—but the "improve accuracy" framing that features prominently in the abstract and title is on shaky ground.

Furthermore, even if the improvements are statistically real, their practical significance is unclear. Is a 1.5% accuracy improvement on financial news classification worth the engineering complexity of deploying a multi-API cascade with a separately trained scoring function? For many applications, the answer may be no—the primary value proposition of FrugalGPT is cost reduction, and the accuracy improvement is a secondary benefit that the paper does not adequately quantify or contextualize.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figure 3c reports point estimates: GPT-4 accuracy = 0.857, FrugalGPT accuracy = 0.872 on HEADLINES. Figure 5a–c shows trade-off curves as smooth lines, implying continuous optimization, but the underlying test-set measurements are subject to sampling noise that is not visualized. Table 3 reports cost savings at matched accuracy without confidence intervals. No statistical methodology is described anywhere in the paper.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper treats accuracy measurements as deterministic point values rather than estimates with uncertainty. A standard practice in ML evaluation—reporting standard deviations across multiple random train/test splits or using bootstrap confidence intervals on the test set—is absent. The paper would be substantially stronger with even basic uncertainty quantification: error bars on the trade-off curves in Figure 5, or a statement like "the 1.5% improvement is significant at `p < 0.05` under a paired bootstrap test." Without this, the accuracy improvement claims should be treated as suggestive rather than definitively established.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper does not introduce a new model architecture, a training objective, or a prompting technique. Its contribution is a **conceptual reframing** with immediate practical consequences: it demonstrates that LLM inference cost—previously treated as an immutable overhead of deployment—is a **first-class optimization variable** that can be systematically traded off against accuracy through data-adaptive routing. The magnitude of the shift is more engineering than scientific, but it is genuine: it changes the default answer to "which LLM should I use?" from a single model name to "it depends on the query, and I should learn a policy to decide."

Before this work, the practitioner's mental model for LLM API usage was largely binary: choose the best model you can afford and use it uniformly, or occasionally ensemble a few models by prompting each and voting. The paper provides empirical evidence that this uniform-allocation default leaves enormous value on the table. The fact that a learned 3-step cascade can match GPT-4's accuracy on HEADLINES at 1/55th the cost (Table 3: $0.60 vs. $33.10) is not an incremental improvement—it is an existence proof that the naive approach is profoundly wasteful for query distributions that contain a mix of easy and hard queries. The paper's key insight is that **cost heterogeneity in the LLM marketplace is not a nuisance to be tolerated but a resource to be exploited**, and that exploiting it requires only a lightweight discriminative scorer rather than access to model internals.

The work reconciles a latent contradiction in the emerging LLM-as-a-service ecosystem. On one hand, providers market progressively larger and more expensive models (GPT-4 costs up to 150× more per input token than GPT-J; Table 1), implying that higher cost buys proportionally higher capability. On the other hand, practitioners and prior work (Arora et al., 2022, cited in the paper) observe that cheap models sometimes match expensive ones—but not always, and with no clear rule for when. This creates decision paralysis: should I pay for GPT-4 or risk GPT-J? FrugalGPT resolves this by showing that you do not have to choose—you can route each query to the cheapest model likely to handle it correctly, using a learned confidence scorer as the dispatcher. The decision is no longer a static model selection problem; it is a **conditional computation policy** learned from data.

The paper also shifts the research agenda around LLM confidence estimation. Prior work on confidence calibration focused on extracting uncertainty signals from the generating model itself—token probabilities, sequence log-likelihoods, or self-evaluation prompts. FrugalGPT demonstrates that a **decoupled, discriminative scorer** (DistilBERT trained on query-answer-correctness triples) can serve this role effectively for routing decisions, operating purely on surface text without any access to the generator's internals. This is a methodological shift: it suggests that confidence estimation for LLM outputs can be treated as a separate supervised learning problem rather than a property that must be extracted from the generative model. The implication is that research investment should flow toward building better discriminative evaluators—models specialized in judging answer correctness—rather than only improving the calibration of generative models themselves.

One research direction that becomes **less attractive** as a result of this work is the pursuit of monolithic "one best model" deployments justified purely by convenience. The paper's results make it difficult to argue that querying GPT-4 uniformly is a rational default when a 3-step cascade with DistilBERT routing can achieve comparable or better accuracy at a fraction of the cost. The burden of proof shifts: a practitioner who deploys a single expensive model without considering adaptive routing must now justify why they are leaving 50–98% of their inference budget on the table. Similarly, the finding that more expensive models do not monotonically dominate cheaper ones—J1 costs more than GPT-3 on HEADLINES but underperforms it (Figure 5a); J1-Jumbo costs more than GPT-4 on OVERRULING but achieves 20 percentage points lower accuracy (Figure 5b)—means that cost-based heuristics ("just use the most expensive model you can afford") are empirically unsound. This weakens the case for any deployment strategy that does not involve empirical evaluation of the specific models on the specific task distribution.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Scoring function transfer and few-shot adaptation across tasks.** The most immediate bottleneck preventing FrugalGPT from being deployed broadly is the per-task labeling requirement for training the scoring function. The paper trains a separate DistilBERT scorer for each dataset de novo, requiring labeled query-answer-correctness triples from all candidate LLMs on that specific task. A natural extension is to ask: can a scoring function trained on a diverse collection of tasks (HEADLINES + OVERRULING + COQA + others) generalize to a new, unseen task with no additional labels, or with only a few dozen labeled examples? A concrete experiment would train a multi-task DistilBERT scorer on, say, five datasets spanning classification and QA, then evaluate zero-shot scoring accuracy on a sixth held-out dataset from a different domain (e.g., a science QA dataset like ARC, or a sentiment analysis task). The metric would be whether cascade performance with the zero-shot scorer approaches cascade performance with a fully supervised scorer trained on the target task. If zero-shot transfer works well, the upfront labeling cost barrier largely disappears, and FrugalGPT becomes deployable on arbitrary tasks with only the cost of querying LLMs for candidate answers (no ground-truth labels needed). If it works poorly, follow-up work could investigate whether fine-tuning the scorer on a small set of actively selected examples (where model disagreement is high, similar to the MPI logic) closes the gap efficiently.

**Dynamic cascade configuration that adapts to LLM marketplace changes without full retraining.** The paper's cascade is statically optimized once and deployed unchanged until manually retrained. In reality, LLM APIs are updated, pricing changes, and new models appear—the cascade configuration becomes stale. A concrete follow-up would design a **monitoring and adaptation layer** that detects when a deployed cascade is no longer optimal and triggers lightweight re-optimization. The simplest version: periodically sample a small set of queries, run them through the current cascade and through any new or updated models, and compute the MPI matrix and cost-accuracy frontier on this probe set. If a new model offers substantial MPI relative to existing cascade members at lower cost, flag it for insertion. If an existing cascade member's accuracy or cost has shifted significantly (e.g., the provider released an improved version or raised prices), flag it for threshold recalibration or replacement. The specific measurement would be: over a 6-month period with real API updates, how much additional cost savings does an adaptive cascade recover compared to a static cascade frozen at deployment time? This addresses the staleness problem (Limitation 4 in Section 6) and would make FrugalGPT practical for long-running production deployments.

**Combining LLM cascade with prompt adaptation—does shorter-prompt routing amplify savings?** The paper proposes prompt adaptation (using fewer in-context examples for cheaper models) as a complementary strategy but never tests it. The most natural composition is: within a cascade, use a short prompt (fewer examples, shorter instructions) for the first-stage cheap model, since it will only be accepted when its answer is highly confident anyway—the reduced prompt may degrade its per-query accuracy slightly, but the threshold mechanism filters out the resulting bad answers, so the net effect is reduced cost with minimal accuracy loss. For later cascade stages where the threshold is lower or the model is the final fallback, use full-length prompts to maximize accuracy. A concrete experiment would compare a cascade with uniform prompts against a cascade where GPT-J uses 2-shot while GPT-4 uses 8-shot on HEADLINES, measuring whether the prompt-length savings at the cheap-model stage reduce total cost further without hurting final accuracy. The hypothesis is that cheap models asked to answer with less context will produce lower-quality answers on average but will still occasionally produce high-confidence correct answers, and the scoring function can distinguish these—the cost savings from shorter prompts on many queries may outweigh the increased cascade rate to later stages.

**Does LLM cascade help or hurt on genuinely open-ended generation tasks where correctness is subjective?** All three evaluation datasets in the paper have objective correctness criteria: gold price trend classification, legal overruling classification, and reading comprehension with extractive answers. The scoring function is trained with binary correctness labels derived from exact match to ground truth. For tasks like summarization (where ROUGE or BERTScore provides a continuous quality signal), creative writing, or dialogue—where "correctness" is either continuous, multi-dimensional, or inherently subjective—it is unclear whether the scoring function paradigm extends. A stress-test experiment would apply LLM cascade to a summarization dataset (e.g., CNN/DailyMail or XSum) with the scoring function trained to regress on ROUGE-L scores rather than binary correctness. The cascade would route queries to cheap models when the predicted ROUGE-L score exceeds a threshold, and cascade to expensive models otherwise. The key measurement is whether the cascade can reduce cost while maintaining average ROUGE-L comparable to the best single LLM. A negative result—e.g., the scoring function cannot reliably predict ROUGE-L from the query-summary pair, so the cascade either rejects good summaries (wasting cost) or accepts bad ones (hurting quality)—would reveal a fundamental boundary on FrugalGPT's applicability: it works for tasks with clear right/wrong answers but not for tasks where quality is continuous and hard to assess from surface text alone.

**Scoring function architecture ablation: how small can the scorer be before cascade performance degrades?** The paper uses DistilBERT (~66M parameters) as the scoring function, arguing it is "considerably smaller and therefore less expensive than all LLMs considered." But no evidence quantifies how scoring function size affects cascade performance. A practical ablation would sweep scoring function architectures from tiny to large: a linear classifier on static sentence embeddings (e.g., all-MiniLM-L6-v2 at ~22M parameters, or even a bag-of-words logistic regression), DistilBERT (66M), BERT-base (110M), and RoBERTa-large (355M). For each scorer, measure cascade cost-accuracy trade-offs on HEADLINES. The hypothesis is that even very small scorers capture most of the available routing benefit—the correctness signal in query-answer pairs may be learnable from relatively shallow features—while the computational cost of larger scorers may dominate the LLM API savings at very low budgets. This would directly inform deployment: if a 22M-parameter sentence-embedding model achieves 95% of DistilBERT's cascade performance at 10% of the scorer computation, practitioners can deploy FrugalGPT on CPU with minimal overhead. If RoBERTa-large substantially outperforms DistilBERT, the trade-off between scorer cost and routing accuracy becomes an optimization dimension to include in the cascade design.

**Joint optimization of cascade order and per-model prompts as a unified search problem.** The paper treats prompt selection (Strategy 1) and LLM cascade (Strategy 3) as independent strategies, and mentions composition only in passing. A more ambitious follow-up would formulate the **joint prompt-and-model selection problem**: for a budget $b$, choose not only which LLMs to include in the cascade and at what thresholds, but also which prompt template (e.g., zero-shot, 2-shot, 8-shot, chain-of-thought) to use for each LLM in the cascade. The search space expands combinatorially—for 12 LLMs, 3 cascade positions, and 4 prompt templates per position, there are $12 \times 4 \times 11 \times 4 \times 10 \times 4$ possible configurations—but the optimizer's pruning technique (removing sequences with low answer disagreement) extends naturally: MPI can be computed per (model, prompt) pair, and only complementary configurations are retained. The key measurement is whether joint optimization yields savings beyond optimizing cascade and prompts separately—e.g., does the ability to use a cheap zero-shot prompt for the first-stage model and an expensive chain-of-thought prompt for the final GPT-4 stage provide additional cost reduction over using the same prompt for all models? A positive result would turn FrugalGPT from a model router into a full inference-strategy optimizer, selecting the cheapest (model, prompt) combination that can confidently handle each query.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Customer support triage with tiered LLM routing.** The small business scenario in Section 2 (15,000 customers, 360,000 queries/month, ~$21,200/month on GPT-4) is the most directly actionable use case. A FrugalGPT deployment would route routine customer questions (order status inquiries, return policy clarifications, basic troubleshooting) to cheap models like GPT-J or GPT-3.5-turbo with short prompts, reserving GPT-4 only for complex, multi-step problem resolution where cheaper models' answers score below threshold. If the query distribution follows the pattern in the paper—where a majority of queries are answerable by cheap models—the monthly cost could drop from ~$21,200 to approximately $400–$8,500 (corresponding to savings of 60–98%, based on Table 3). The upfront investment is labeling a few hundred representative customer queries with correct answers (perhaps by having support agents annotate them over a week), training the scoring function, and optimizing the cascade—a one-time cost that amortizes within the first month of savings at this query volume. The practical requirement is that the business has a log of historical queries with known resolutions, which most established support operations possess.

**Batch document classification and information extraction pipelines at scale.** Organizations that process large volumes of text—legal document review in e-discovery, financial news monitoring for trading signals, scientific literature screening for systematic reviews—need to classify or extract information from millions of documents. Using GPT-4 uniformly on such pipelines is cost-prohibitive (the HEADLINES dataset cost $33.10 for a test set of a few thousand headlines; scaling to millions would cost tens of thousands of dollars). A FrugalGPT cascade tuned on a small labeled sample of the target document type could route the majority of documents through cheap classifiers like GPT-J or fine-tuned small models, escalating only ambiguous or edge cases to GPT-4. The cost savings demonstrated in the paper—98% on HEADLINES at matched accuracy—suggest that a pipeline processing 10 million financial news headlines could cost ~$600 instead of ~$33,100, making LLM-based classification economically viable for applications where per-document budgets are pennies rather than dollars. The key engineering addition needed is a **streaming cascade** that batches documents for the same LLM stage to reduce API call overhead.

**Self-improving data generation pipelines with adaptive quality control.** When using LLMs to generate training data for smaller models (distillation) or to label unlabeled corpora for downstream supervised learning, the quality of the generated outputs directly affects the student model's performance. Current practice often uses a single expensive model (GPT-4) as the teacher for all examples, which is costly when generating millions of training instances. A FrugalGPT cascade can serve as an adaptive teacher: for each training example, cascade through cheap models first, accept their output if the scoring function deems it sufficiently reliable, and fall back to GPT-4 only when cheap models produce low-confidence answers. The scoring function trained to predict correctness from query-answer pairs serves double duty—it both routes the query to the appropriate model and provides a quality estimate for the accepted answer, which can be used to filter the generated training data. The paper's results on COQA, where the cascade matches GPT-3's accuracy at 59% lower cost (Table 3: $29.60 vs. $72.50), suggest that a data generation pipeline could produce training sets of comparable quality to single-model generation at substantially lower API cost. A concrete deployment would be generating reading comprehension training data: for each passage-question pair, the cascade produces an answer and a confidence score, and only answers above a quality threshold enter the training set.

**Multi-provider resilience with automatic failover.** The paper mentions in Section 1 that "relying on one API provider is not reliable if that provider becomes unavailable." The LLM cascade provides resilience as a byproduct: if the cascade includes models from multiple providers (e.g., OpenAI, AI21, Cohere, Textsynth), a provider outage affecting one model simply means that model's answers are unavailable—queries that would have stopped at that model cascade to the next one, potentially at higher cost but without service interruption. A FrugalGPT deployment with models from at least two providers transforms a single-point-of-failure architecture (all queries go to OpenAI) into a graceful-degradation architecture (if OpenAI is down, queries route through AI21 and Textsynth models, with higher cost but continued operation). The paper's MPI analysis (Figure 4) shows that models from different providers have complementary error patterns, so the accuracy impact of losing one provider is mitigated by the remaining models' ability to handle queries the lost provider would have caught. This resilience property is not empirically tested in the paper but follows directly from the cascade architecture and the multi-provider composition of the LLM marketplace used in the experiments.
