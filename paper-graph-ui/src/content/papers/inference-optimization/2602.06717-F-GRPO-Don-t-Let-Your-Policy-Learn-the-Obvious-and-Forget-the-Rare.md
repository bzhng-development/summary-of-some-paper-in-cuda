# F-GRPO: Don’t Let Your Policy Learn the Obvious and Forget the Rare

**ArXiv:** [2602.06717](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.06717)

## 🎯 Pitch

Group-relative RL methods like GRPO actually maximize the risk of discarding rare correct solutions at the most commonly used intermediate group sizes—small groups stay inactive and large groups sample everything, but N=8 creates a dangerous sweet spot for forgetting. The authors fix this with a focal loss coefficient that automatically down-weights updates on easy prompts, boosting pass@256 by over 6 points at N=8 while using a quarter of the compute of N=32.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper analyzes how finite group sizes in group-relative reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR)—specifically GRPO and its variants—cause distribution sharpening by systematically suppressing rare-correct trajectories while concentrating probability mass onto common solutions. The authors derive a closed-form **tail-miss probability** characterizing when active RLVR updates miss rare-correct modes (the probability that a training batch has mixed rewards yet fails to sample from a specified low-probability correct region), revealing a non-monotonic dependence on group size that reconciles conflicting prior guidance: small groups preserve diversity through inactivity, large groups through coverage, but intermediate group sizes—most common in practice due to computational constraints—maximize sharpening risk. Motivated by this analysis, they propose **Focal weighting**, a difficulty-aware advantage scaling coefficient inspired by Focal loss that down-weights updates on high-success prompts (where the concentration mechanism is strongest), and integrate it into GRPO, DAPO, and CISPO as a lightweight, drop-in modification requiring no additional compute. On Qwen2.5-7B trained on DeepScaleR math problems, F-GRPO at N=8 matches or exceeds GRPO at N=32 on pass@256 (70.3 vs. 70.1 in-domain; 63.3 vs. 61.7 out-of-domain) using 4× fewer rollouts, while improving OOD pass@1 (34.0 vs. 31.0 on IFEval), establishing that pass@256 improvements are achievable without scaling group size—but only when the Focal weight compensates for the intermediate-N sampling failure mode rather than simply increasing the rollout budget.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: RLVR Sharpens Distributions in Ways We Don't Understand

The fundamental question this paper tackles is: **why does reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) tend to narrow the diversity of solutions a language model produces, and can we prevent this without drastically increasing computational cost?**

This matters because RLVR has become the dominant paradigm for post-training large language models on reasoning tasks. The recipe is simple: give the model a math problem, let it generate several answers, check which ones are correct using an automatic verifier (e.g., string matching against the ground-truth answer), and update the model to prefer correct answers while penalizing incorrect ones. This approach has produced some of the most impressive reasoning models to date (DeepSeek-R1, Kimi K2, Qwen3), all without requiring expensive human preference data.

But a growing body of evidence reveals a troubling pattern. While RLVR reliably improves **pass@1** (the probability of getting the correct answer on a single attempt), it often degrades **pass@k for large k**—the probability that at least one of many independent attempts yields a correct solution. In practical terms: an RLVR-trained model is more likely to get the right answer on its first try, but if you let it try 256 times, the base model (which never underwent RLVR) might actually outperform it. This is not a niche concern. For applications that rely on sampling many candidate solutions and selecting the best one—a standard technique for improving reliability on high-stakes reasoning tasks—distribution sharpening during RLVR training directly undermines the model's usefulness at scale. If RLVR is causing the model to forget how to generate rare but valid solution strategies, then the post-training process is inadvertently making the model *less* capable in regimes where diversity matters.

### Conflicting Prior Evidence

The paper is motivated by a genuine contradiction in the literature and in practical guidance around RLVR.

On one side, RLVR's successes are undeniable. Models trained with GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization; Shao et al., 2024) and its variants achieve state-of-the-art results on mathematical reasoning benchmarks like MATH and AIME. The approach is simple, does not require a separate value function model (unlike PPO), and scales to large models with manageable computational overhead. On the other side, multiple independent studies paint a concerning picture:

- **Yue et al. (2025)** show that RLVR does not primarily introduce new reasoning capabilities but instead sharpens the output distribution toward solutions already accessible to the base model—essentially, RLVR makes the model more consistent at solving problems it can already solve, rather than teaching it to solve new ones. This reframes RLVR's contribution from "improving reasoning" to "improving reliability on existing reasoning."
- **Dang et al. (2025)** and **Wu et al. (2025a)** provide empirical evidence that models trained with RLVR exhibit degraded pass@k at large sampling budgets, consistent with a narrowing of solution diversity—the very solutions that a base model could occasionally find through random exploration become inaccessible after RLVR training.
- **Matsutani et al. (2025)** characterize this as an "RL squeezes, SFT expands" dichotomy, where supervised fine-tuning broadens the solution distribution while RL narrows it.
- **Ni et al. (2025)** explicitly question whether GRPO can help language models "transcend their pretraining origin," suggesting that the mechanism may be fundamentally limited to redistributing probability mass within the capabilities already present in the base model, rather than expanding those capabilities.

Yet this evidence is far from unanimous. **Liu et al. (2025b)** demonstrate that prolonged or carefully scaled RL *can* expand the effective reasoning boundary, and **Yuan et al. (2025)** show that language models can learn new skills during RL by composing previously learned capabilities. The tension is clear: under what conditions does RLVR expand capabilities versus merely concentrating them? The field lacks a mechanistic understanding of *why* sharpening occurs and *when* it is most severe.

### The Group Size Debate

Complicating this picture further, existing work provides directly conflicting guidance on a critical design choice: **how many rollouts per prompt (the group size N) should be used during RLVR training?**

GRPO and its relatives compute advantages *within* a group of rollouts from the same prompt. The group size N determines both the quality of the advantage estimate (larger groups provide better estimates of which answers are above or below average) and the computational cost (each training step requires generating N complete solutions per prompt). This creates a practical tension: larger N is better for learning signal quality but worse for computational efficiency.

The guidance in the literature is contradictory:

- **Wu et al. (2025b)** provide a theoretical analysis showing that N=2 (just two rollouts per prompt) is both justified and compute-efficient, connecting GRPO at small N to Direct Preference Optimization (DPO). The implication is that you don't need large groups—two rollouts suffice if the objective is structured correctly.
- **Hu et al. (2025)** demonstrate the opposite: scaling N improves learning stability and ensures that the total probability mass assigned to correct solutions monotonically increases. Their analysis shows that large groups guarantee non-negative change in correct mass by suppressing a problematic "unsampled coupling" term in the gradient. The implication is that you should scale N as large as computationally feasible.
- **He et al. (2025)** observe a middle-ground regime: at moderate N=32, pass@1 improves while pass@k for large k degrades—the signature of distribution sharpening. They identify "rank bias" in GRPO as the culprit and propose an "unlikelihood reward" that up-weights rare correct trajectories.

These seemingly contradictory recommendations are not necessarily wrong—they likely describe different operating regimes of the same underlying phenomenon. But the field lacked a framework for understanding which regime you're in and why. The paper's central insight is that **the effect of group size on diversity is non-monotonic**, with small N preserving diversity through one mechanism (infrequent active updates), large N through a different mechanism (good coverage of the solution space), and intermediate N—precisely the range most commonly used in practice (N=8 or N=32)—falling into a "danger zone" where updates are frequent enough to reshape the distribution but the group is too small to reliably sample rare solutions.

### The Categorical Framework and Its Limitations

The paper builds directly on the categorical policy framework introduced by **Hu et al. (2025)**. This framework models RLVR updates as operating on a finite action space (a simplified abstraction of token-level decisions), partitioned into "correct" and "incorrect" actions. By analyzing the one-step logit update under group-relative advantage estimation, Hu et al. derive an expression for how the total correct mass Qpos changes with group size N.

Their key finding: the change in total correct mass ∆Qpos contains a term they call the **unsampled coupling**:

$$\Delta Q_{pos} = \frac{\eta}{N} \left[ (R_c - \bar{R}) Q_{neg} A_2 + (\bar{R} - R_w) Q_{pos} B_2 + \bar{R}(Q_{pos}U_{neg,2} - Q_{neg}U_{pos,2}) \right]$$

The first two terms are always non-negative (they promote sampled correct actions and demote sampled incorrect ones, both of which transfer mass to the correct pool). The third term—the unsampled coupling—can be negative, and it depends on the relative concentration of probability mass on unsampled correct versus unsampled incorrect actions. Hu et al. show that scaling N drives this unsampled coupling toward zero, guaranteeing that ∆Qpos ≥ 0 for sufficiently large groups.

This analysis is insightful but has two critical gaps that this paper addresses:

1. **It only tracks total correct mass, not diversity within the correct set.** ∆Qpos > 0 means the model is becoming more likely to produce *some* correct solution, but it could be concentrating all that mass onto a single solution strategy while forgetting others. The framework provides no analysis of redistribution within the correct set—the very phenomenon that causes pass@k degradation.

2. **The framework's guarantee (∆Qpos ≥ 0 for large N) only applies at group sizes far beyond what is computationally practical.** The paper's own experiments (Figure 4, panel c) show that N must be on the order of 131,072 to maintain diversity in their categorical simulation—well beyond any practical group size for LLM training, where N=8 to N=32 is typical. For practical group sizes, the unsampled coupling has not been fully suppressed, and the framework provides no guidance on what happens to unsampled-correct mass.

### Where Existing Mitigations Fall Short

The paper identifies specific limitations in prior attempts to address distribution sharpening:

**Entropy regularization**—adding a bonus to encourage the policy to maintain high entropy (i.e., remain uncertain/exploratory)—is a standard technique from RL. In the RLVR context, some work advocates maximizing entropy for exploration (Cui et al., 2025; Cheng et al., 2025), while other work reports that *minimizing* entropy helps reasoning (Agarwal et al., 2025). This debate reflects the fact that entropy operates at the token level—it encourages the model to be uncertain about individual token choices—but the sharpening problem is about *trajectory-level* diversity. A model could have high per-token entropy (many plausible next tokens at each step) while still concentrating onto a narrow set of solution strategies. The paper's experiments (Section 5.5, Appendix G) confirm that entropy bonuses provide only modest mitigation of sharpening.

**KL regularization**—penalizing divergence from a reference policy (typically the base model)—is another common technique. By constraining the policy to stay close to its initialization, KL regularization should, in principle, prevent the model from forgetting solutions it used to generate. The paper finds that KL regularization does improve pass@256 compared to unregularized GRPO (72.0 vs. 70.3), but it requires maintaining a reference model in memory during training (doubling the memory footprint) and provides weaker pass@1 and OOD transfer than F-GRPO. More importantly, it is a blunt instrument: it penalizes *all* deviation from the base model equally, rather than selectively targeting the updates that cause concentration.

**Difficulty-aware reweighting** approaches such as DARO (Zhou et al., 2025) dynamically balance loss contributions across difficulty groups to equalize the scale of the gradient signal. **Unlikelihood reward** (He et al., 2025) up-weights rare correct trajectories individually. **Differential smoothing** (Gai et al., 2025) modifies rewards differently for correct versus incorrect trajectories. These methods share the motivation of addressing sharpening but differ in mechanism. The paper positions Focal weighting as complementary to and simpler than these approaches: rather than modifying trajectory-level rewards or per-token weights, it scales the entire gradient contribution of a prompt based on a single scalar—the empirical success rate—which directly tracks the SR > 0 regime that the theoretical analysis identifies as the driver of concentration.

### The Sampling Analysis Gap

Critically, none of the prior theoretical analyses of RLVR have focused on a simple but powerful question: **what is the probability that a training update is active (contains both correct and incorrect rollouts, providing a learning signal) yet fails to sample from a given rare-correct region of the solution space?** This probability—which the paper calls the **tail-miss probability** Pr(Bτ)—captures the exact scenario where sharpening occurs: the model receives feedback that tells it to prefer correct answers over incorrect ones, but the correct answers it sees are only the common ones, so the gradient pushes probability mass *away* from rare-correct solutions (which were never sampled and thus don't appear as positive examples) and *toward* common-correct solutions (which were sampled and rewarded).

Prior work either analyzed this in the limit (N → ∞, where coverage is perfect) or studied empirical behavior without a closed-form probability. The paper's derivation of a closed-form expression for Pr(Bτ) as a function of N, the success probability µpos, and the rare-correct mass τ fills this gap and reveals the non-monotonic behavior that reconciles the conflicting prior findings.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper frames its contribution as bridging a gap between *theoretical understanding* (why do group-relative RLVR methods cause distribution sharpening at moderate N?) and *practical mitigation* (how can we prevent this without scaling N, which is computationally infeasible, or overhauling the training algorithm?).

The theoretical analysis serves three purposes: (1) deriving the tail-miss probability to explain *when* and *why* sharpening is most severe, (2) extending the categorical framework of Hu et al. (2025) to analyze redistribution *within* the correct set (showing that unsampled-correct mass can shrink even when total correct mass grows—a mechanism prior work missed by focusing only on Qpos), and (3) providing a principled motivation for the Focal weighting intervention—by identifying that the concentration mechanism is strongest when the batch baseline SR > 0, which correlates with high empirical success rate bµpos, the paper justifies down-weighting updates on high-success prompts as a targeted countermeasure.

The practical contribution positions F-GRPO not as a replacement for existing group-relative methods but as a **drop-in modification** applicable to any of them (GRPO, DAPO, CISPO). This is deliberate: the concentration phenomenon arises from the sampling dynamics of group-relative advantage estimation, not from algorithmic choices like clipping mechanisms or importance weighting. The Focal weight g(x) = (1 − bµpos(x))^γ is thus orthogonal to these design choices and can be applied independently. The paper emphasizes that this requires no additional networks, no extra compute, and only a single new hyperparameter γ.

The paper explicitly connects its analysis to the broader debate about whether RLVR expands or concentrates capabilities. It does not claim that Focal weighting solves the sharpening problem completely—rather, it provides a framework for understanding when sharpening occurs and a practical tool for mitigating it at the most common group sizes. The implication is that the negative results in prior work (Yue et al., 2025; Dang et al., 2025) are not fundamental properties of RLVR but artifacts of finite sampling at intermediate N, and that appropriate difficulty-aware weighting can recover much of the diversity that standard GRPO destroys.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

This is a **theoretical analysis and algorithmic modification paper** whose core idea is that the distribution sharpening observed in GRPO and related methods is a predictable consequence of finite group sampling, not an inherent property of RLVR—and that an extremely lightweight, difficulty-aware advantage reweighting can substantially mitigate it without any additional computational cost.

The paper analyzes GRPO-like training by focusing on what happens inside a single prompt's group when rare-correct trajectories exist but fail to be sampled, then proposes **F-GRPO**, a modification that multiplies each prompt's group-relative advantage by a scalar `$g(x) = (1 - \hat{\mu}_{pos}(x))^\gamma$` derived from that prompt's empirical success rate in the current batch. This down-weights gradient contributions from prompts where the model already succeeds frequently (where the concentration mechanism from the theoretical analysis is strongest), effectively reallocating learning signal toward harder prompts where diversity matters more.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture

The system being analyzed and modified has four conceptual components:

1. **The RLVR training loop** — for each prompt, the current policy `$\pi_\theta$` generates `$N$` independent rollouts (complete solutions), a verifier assigns binary rewards (1 for correct, typically 0 for incorrect), and the model is updated using group-relative advantage estimation. This is unchanged from standard GRPO/DAPO/CISPO.

2. **The tail-miss probability analysis (Lemma 3.1)** — deriving `$\Pr(B_\tau)$`, the probability that a training update is active (mixed rewards in the batch) yet fails to sample any rollout from a designated rare-correct region of the solution space. This probability is expressed as a closed-form function of group size `$N$`, overall success probability `$\mu_{pos}$`, and rare-correct mass `$\tau$`.

3. **The unsampled-correct mass analysis (Proposition 3.2)** — extending the categorical framework of Hu et al. (2025) to derive `$\Delta Q_{u,pos}$`, the one-step change in probability mass assigned to unsampled-correct actions. This reveals that the concentration mechanism operates through two terms: a direct drift term `$-\bar{R} U_{pos,2}$` (unsampled actions get zero reward but suffer baseline subtraction when `$\bar{R} > 0$`) and a normalization coupling term (gains by sampled-correct actions draw mass away from unsampled-correct ones through softmax normalization).

4. **The Focal weighting intervention** — computing the empirical success rate `$\hat{\mu}_{pos}(x) = X/N$` for each prompt in the batch, applying the scalar `$g(x) = (1 - \hat{\mu}_{pos}(x))^\gamma$` to all rollouts from that prompt before advantage estimation, and using the resulting Focal-weighted advantage `$\hat{A}^{F-GRPO}_i = g(x) \cdot \hat{A}^{GRPO}_i$` in the policy gradient. The hyperparameter `$\gamma \geq 0$` controls how aggressively high-success prompts are down-weighted; `$\gamma = 0$` recovers the original method.

Information flows as follows: a prompt enters → the policy generates N rollouts → the verifier scores them for correctness → the empirical success rate `$\hat{\mu}_{pos}$` is computed as the fraction correct → the Focal weight `$g(x)$` is computed → standard GRPO/DAPO/CISPO advantages are computed within the group → each advantage is multiplied by `$g(x)$` → the policy is updated using the scaled advantages (with clipping and importance sampling as specified by the parent algorithm).

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the formal problem setup: what RLVR and group-relative methods are, why group size matters, and the categorical framework we inherit from Hu et al. (2025). This is necessary to understand the specific quantities that appear in the analysis.
- **Second**, the tail-miss probability `$\Pr(B_\tau)$` (Lemma 3.1)—its derivation from basic probability, its closed form, and its non-monotonic behavior with group size. This is the paper's key theoretical insight about *when* sharpening is worst.
- **Third**, the unsampled-correct mass change `$\Delta Q_{u,pos}$` (Proposition 3.2)—how the categorical framework reveals redistribution *within* the correct set, showing that total correct mass can increase while unsampled-correct mass shrinks. This is the *mechanism* by which sharpening occurs, going beyond prior work that only tracked `$\Delta Q_{pos}$`.
- **Fourth**, the monotonicity link connecting the categorical analysis to the Focal weight (Appendix B, Corollary B.2)—why the empirical success rate `$\hat{\mu}_{pos}$` serves as a valid proxy for the SR > 0 regime that drives concentration.
- **Fifth**, the Focal weighting intervention itself—the formula, its integration into GRPO/DAPO/CISPO, the effect on advantage magnitudes, and why `$\gamma$` is the only new hyperparameter.
- **Sixth**, the categorical simulation setup—how the paper empirically validates the three-regime framework in a controlled environment that enables sweeping group sizes from 2 to 131,072, far beyond what LLM training allows.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This section builds up the technical mechanics of the paper methodically, moving from the abstract probability analysis down to the concrete algorithm modification.

#### The RLVR Training Loop and Group-Relative Advantage

Before diving into the theoretical analysis, we need a precise picture of what happens during a single training step in GRPO-like methods. The paper works with binary **outcome rewards**: each complete rollout (full generated response) receives `$R_c$` if correct or `$R_w$` if incorrect. In all experiments, `$R_c = 1$` and `$R_w = 0$` (rather than the `$R_w = -1$` used in some prior work like DeepSeekMath). The choice `$R_w = 0$` matters because it affects the sign of the baseline `$\bar{R}$` and therefore the direction of the drift term in Proposition 3.2.

For a given prompt `$x$`, the training step proceeds as follows:

1. **Sample N rollouts** `$\{o_i\}_{i=1}^N$` i.i.d. from the current policy `$\pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x)$`, each at temperature 1.0.
2. **Assign rewards** using a verifier (math-verify in the paper's experiments): `$R_i = R_c$` if `$o_i$` is correct, `$R_i = R_w$` otherwise.
3. **Compute the group mean and standard deviation**: `$\bar{R} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^N R_j$` and `$\sigma_R = \text{std}(\{R_j\}_{j=1}^N)$`.
4. **Compute the group-relative advantage** for each rollout:

$$\hat{A}^{GRPO}_i = \frac{R_i - \bar{R}}{\sigma_R + \epsilon}$$

where `$R_i \in \{R_c, R_w\}$` is the binary reward for rollout `$i$`, `$\bar{R}$` is the group mean reward, `$\sigma_R$` is the standard deviation of rewards in the group, and `$\epsilon$` is a small constant for numerical stability.

**What it computes:** a normalized score quantifying how much better (`$\hat{A}^{GRPO}_i > 0$`) or worse (`$\hat{A}^{GRPO}_i < 0$`) rollout `$i$` is compared to the group average, measured in units of the group's reward variability. A correct rollout in a mostly-incorrect group gets a large positive advantage; an incorrect rollout in a mostly-correct group gets a large negative advantage; when all rollouts are correct or all are incorrect, `$\sigma_R = 0$` and every advantage is zero—the update provides no learning signal.

**Why this form:** normalizing by `$\sigma_R$` makes the advantage scale invariant to the absolute reward values and prevents the policy from receiving arbitrarily large updates when success rates shift (e.g., if the model suddenly starts solving a problem it previously failed, the advantages don't explode). The group-relative structure eliminates the need for a separately trained value function (unlike PPO), which is a major practical simplification. However, as the analysis will show, this same structure creates the finite-sampling failure mode: when `$\sigma_R$` is zero, the group is homogeneous and learning stops, but when `$\sigma_R$` is non-zero and `$N$` is small, the rare-correct region may go unsampled.

5. **Apply the clipped surrogate objective.** For GRPO specifically:

$$\mathcal{L}^{GRPO}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_x \left[ \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \frac{1}{T_i} \sum_{t=1}^{T_i} \min\left( r_{i,t} \hat{A}_i, \text{clip}(r_{i,t}, 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon) \hat{A}_i \right) - \beta D_{KL}(\pi_\theta \parallel \pi_{ref}) \right]$$

where `$r_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{old}}(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t})}$` is the per-token importance ratio (how much more likely the current policy makes token `$t$` of rollout `$i$` compared to the old policy that generated it), `$T_i$` is the length of rollout `$i$`, `$\epsilon$` is the PPO clipping parameter (typically 0.2), and `$\beta$` is the KL penalty coefficient. The paper sets `$\beta = 0$` following DAPO (Yu et al., 2025)—there is no KL regularization to the reference policy in any of the main experiments except the explicit KL comparison in Section 5.5.

**What it computes:** a per-token surrogate loss that encourages the policy to increase the probability of tokens from advantageous rollouts (correct answers in batches with mixed success) and decrease the probability of tokens from disadvantageous rollouts (incorrect answers in batches with mixed success), but with clipping that prevents the policy from changing too dramatically relative to the old policy in any single update.

**Why this form:** the clipping mechanism (`$\min(r_{i,t}\hat{A}_i, \text{clip}(r_{i,t}, 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon)\hat{A}_i)$`) implements a trust region: if a token's probability would increase too much (`$r_{i,t} > 1+\epsilon$` for positive advantage) or decrease too much (`$r_{i,t} < 1-\epsilon$` for negative advantage), the gradient is clipped to zero, preventing the policy from over-committing to individual rollouts. The key property for our analysis is that the gradient direction (what the policy learns to prefer or avoid) is entirely determined by the sign of `$\hat{A}_i$`, which in turn depends on whether rollouts were sampled from the correct set.

DAPO (Yu et al., 2025) modifies the clipping bounds to be **asymmetric**: `$\epsilon_{low} = 0.2$`, `$\epsilon_{high} = 0.28$` (instead of symmetric `$\epsilon = 0.2$` for both bounds). The rationale is that low-probability actions should be allowed larger increases (the upper bound is relaxed), which partially addresses the sharpening problem by making it easier for the policy to rediscover rare solutions. However, DAPO does not fundamentally alter the *which solutions receive positive updates* question—it only changes how aggressively those updates are applied. This is why DAPO shows similar sharpening patterns to GRPO in the paper's experiments (Table 1) and why Focal weighting is applicable on top of it.

CISPO (Chen et al., 2025a) takes a different approach: rather than clipping the product `$r_{i,t}\hat{A}_i$`, it **clips the importance ratio directly** and uses it as a stop-gradient weight in a REINFORCE-style objective:

$$\hat{r}_{i,t} = \text{clip}\left(r_{i,t}, 1 - \epsilon^{IS}_{low}, 1 + \epsilon^{IS}_{high}\right)$$

$$\mathcal{L}^{CISPO}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{i,t}\left[ \text{sg}(\hat{r}_{i,t}) \hat{A}^{GRPO}_i \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t}) \right]$$

where `$\text{sg}(\cdot)$` denotes stop-gradient (the clipped importance ratio is treated as a constant multiplier, not differentiated through). With `$\epsilon^{IS}_{low} = 1.0$` and `$\epsilon^{IS}_{high} = 5.0$`, CISPO applies very different clipping from GRPO—but again, the direction of the update (determined by the sign of `$\hat{A}^{GRPO}_i$`) is unchanged. The paper's key claim is that **the concentration phenomenon arises from how the group-relative advantage itself is estimated**, not from the clipping or importance-weighting mechanism. Focal weighting modifies `$\hat{A}^{GRPO}_i$` directly (by the scalar `$g(x)$`), making it applicable to all three methods.

#### The Categorical Policy Framework (Inherited from Hu et al., 2025)

To analyze how RLVR updates redistribute probability mass, the paper adopts the categorical policy framework of Hu et al. (2025). This framework abstracts away from the token-level, autoregressive generation process and instead treats the model as making a single decision from a large finite action space `$\mathcal{A}$`.

The policy is a softmax distribution `$p = \text{softmax}(z)$` over `$|\mathcal{A}|$` actions (the paper's simulations use `$|\mathcal{A}| = 128,000$`). The action space is partitioned into **correct actions** `$\mathcal{P}$` (with reward `$R_c = +1$`) and **incorrect actions** `$\mathcal{N} = \mathcal{A} \setminus \mathcal{P}$` (with reward `$R_w = -1$` in the simulations, though in LLM experiments `$R_w = 0$`).

Define the following categorical-level quantities (directly quoted from Section 2.3):

- `$Q_{pos} := \sum_{i \in \mathcal{P}} p_i$` — total correct mass (probability of any correct action)
- `$Q_{neg} := 1 - Q_{pos}$` — total incorrect mass
- After sampling `$N$` actions i.i.d. from `$p$`: `$\mathcal{A}$` is the set of **sampled correct actions**, `$\mathcal{B}$` is the set of **sampled incorrect actions**, and `$\mathcal{U} = \mathcal{A} \setminus (\mathcal{A} \cup \mathcal{B})$` is the set of **unsampled actions**.
- `$P_{pos} := \sum_{i \in \mathcal{A}} p_i$` — total mass of sampled correct actions (including duplicates counted once)
- `$P_{neg} := \sum_{i \in \mathcal{B}} p_i$` — total mass of sampled incorrect actions
- `$A_2 := \sum_{i \in \mathcal{A}} p_i^2$` — second moment (concentration measure) of sampled correct actions
- `$B_2 := \sum_{i \in \mathcal{B}} p_i^2$` — second moment of sampled incorrect actions
- `$U_{pos,2} := \sum_{i \in \mathcal{U} \cap \mathcal{P}} p_i^2$` — second moment of unsampled correct actions
- `$U_{neg,2} := \sum_{i \in \mathcal{U} \cap \mathcal{N}} p_i^2$` — second moment of unsampled incorrect actions
- `$\bar{R} := R_c P_{pos} + R_w P_{neg}$` — the batch baseline (sample-based estimate of expected reward under the sampled actions)

One subtlety worth emphasizing: in the categorical framework, the "reward" of an action is known and deterministic (correct actions always give `$R_c$`, incorrect always give `$R_w$`). By contrast, in trajectory-level RLVR, the reward is determined by whether a full rollout reaches the correct answer, which is stochastic (the same high-level "solution strategy" might sometimes succeed and sometimes fail depending on execution details). The categorical framework is thus a simplified model of the token-level mechanics, but it captures the key structure: some outputs are preferred, some are not, and the model only receives gradient signals about outputs it actually sampled.

Under this framework, Hu et al. (2025) derive the **one-step logit update** under a TRPO-style linear surrogate with unbiased Monte Carlo estimates:

$$\Delta z_i = \frac{\eta}{N} p_i (R_i - \bar{R})$$

where `$\eta$` is the learning rate, `$p_i$` is the current probability of action `$i$`, `$R_i$` is the reward (0 if `$i$` is unsampled—the framework treats unsampled actions as receiving reward 0 rather than their true reward), and `$\bar{R}$` is the batch baseline defined above.

**What it computes:** the per-step adjustment to the logit of action `$i$`. If action `$i$` was sampled and was correct (`$R_i > \bar{R}$`), its logit increases by an amount proportional to `$p_i$` (higher-probability actions get larger absolute updates); if it was sampled and incorrect (`$R_i < \bar{R}$`), its logit decreases; if it was unsampled (`$R_i = 0$`), its logit changes by `$-\frac{\eta}{N}\bar{R} p_i$`, which is **downward when `$\bar{R} > 0$`** (reward-positive batches) and upward when `$\bar{R} < 0$` (reward-negative batches).

**Why this form:** the factor `$p_i$` arises from the softmax Jacobian `$\partial p_i / \partial z_j = p_i(\delta_{ij} - p_j)$`—actions with higher probability receive proportionally larger logit adjustments because they contribute more to the likelihood being optimized. The unaveraged per-sample update would be `$\eta p_i(R_i - \bar{R})$`; the `$1/N$` factor comes from averaging over the `$N$` samples in the batch. The critical property is the **asymmetric treatment of unsampled actions**: they get reward 0 in the estimator but are still affected by the baseline subtraction through `$-\bar{R}$`. When `$\bar{R} > 0$`, this means that even correct actions that happen not to be sampled get pushed downward—the direct mechanism of concentration.

From this per-action update, Hu et al. derive the **one-step change in total correct mass** (their Equation 8, restated as our Eq. (8) in Section 2.3):

$$\Delta Q_{pos} = \frac{\eta}{N} \left[ (R_c - \bar{R}) Q_{neg} A_2 + (\bar{R} - R_w) Q_{pos} B_2 + \bar{R}(Q_{pos}U_{neg,2} - Q_{neg}U_{pos,2}) \right]$$

The first two terms are always non-negative and represent the intended learning: promoting sampled correct actions transfers mass from the negative pool to the positive pool (first term), and demoting sampled incorrect actions does the same (second term). The third term—the **unsampled coupling**—can be positive or negative depending on whether unsampled mass is more concentrated in the correct or incorrect set. Hu et al. show that as `$N \to \infty$`, the unsampled masses `$U_{pos,2}$` and `$U_{neg,2}$` tend to zero, suppressing this term and guaranteeing `$\Delta Q_{pos} \geq 0$`.

The critical limitation of this analysis—which the paper directly addresses—is that **`$\Delta Q_{pos} \geq 0$` says nothing about diversity within the correct set**. All mass could be moving to a single correct action (the one that happened to be sampled), and `$\Delta Q_{pos}$` would still be positive. The paper's Proposition 3.2 fills this gap.

#### Lemma 3.1: The Tail-Miss Probability `$\Pr(B_\tau)$`

This is the paper's first major theoretical contribution. The setup is probabilistic, operating at the trajectory level (not the categorical level). For a given prompt `$x$`:

- `$\mu_{pos}(x)$` is the model's current success probability—the fraction of rollouts that would be correct if we could sample infinitely many.
- `$\tau(x)$` is the probability of sampling a rollout from a designated **rare-correct region** `$\mathcal{C}_{rare}(x) \subseteq \mathcal{C}(x)$`—a subset of correct solutions that the model can produce but with low probability. By definition, `$0 < \tau(x) < \mu_{pos}(x)$`.
- `$\rho(x) := \tau(x) / \mu_{pos}(x)$` is the fraction of the model's correct probability mass that lies in the rare-correct region. When `$\rho$` is small, rare-correct solutions are genuinely rare—most correct answers are common ones.

We sample `$N$` i.i.d. rollouts from the policy. Let `$X$` be the number of correct rollouts among the `$N$` samples (so `$X \sim \text{Binomial}(N, \mu_{pos})$`). Define the **active event**:

$$\mathcal{A}_N := \{0 < X < N\}$$

This is the event that the batch contains a mix of correct and incorrect rollouts, so `$\sigma_R > 0$` and the group-relative advantage provides a non-zero learning signal. When `$X = 0$` or `$X = N$`, all rewards are identical, `$\sigma_R = 0$`, every `$\hat{A}_i^{GRPO} = 0$`, and the policy receives no update from this prompt.

Define the **tail-miss event** `$\mathcal{B}_\tau$` as:

$$\mathcal{B}_\tau := \mathcal{A}_N \cap \left\{ \sum_{i=1}^N Y_i = 0 \right\}$$

where `$Y_i = \mathbb{I}[o_i \in \mathcal{C}_{rare}(x)]$` indicates whether rollout `$i$` falls in the rare-correct region. In words: the update is active (mixed rewards) but the rare-correct region was not sampled at all.

**Lemma 3.1** provides the closed-form probability:

$$\Pr(\mathcal{B}_\tau) = (1 - \tau)^N - (\mu_{pos} - \tau)^N - (1 - \mu_{pos})^N$$

where `$\tau = \tau(x)$` is the rare-correct mass defined above, `$\mu_{pos} = \mu_{pos}(x)$` is the overall success probability, and `$N$` is the group size.

**What it computes:** the probability that a training batch for a given prompt will produce a non-zero gradient (because some rollouts are correct and some incorrect) but that gradient will be **structurally biased**—it will push the policy toward the common-correct solutions that were sampled and away from the rare-correct ones that were not, even though the rare-correct solutions were *capable* of being generated. This is the exact scenario where sharpening occurs.

**Derivation sketch (Appendix A):** Each rollout falls into one of three disjoint regions: (1) the rare-correct region with probability `$\tau$`, (2) the rest of the correct set with probability `$\mu_{pos} - \tau$`, or (3) the incorrect set with probability `$1 - \mu_{pos}$`. The event that no rollout falls in the rare-correct region has probability `$(1 - \tau)^N$`. Conditioned on this, the group is inactive (all rewards identical, so `$\mathcal{B}_\tau$` does not occur) in two cases: all rollouts are correct but not rare-correct (probability `$(\mu_{pos} - \tau)^N$`), or all rollouts are incorrect (probability `$(1 - \mu_{pos})^N$`). Subtracting these inactive cases from the no-rare-correct event yields the probability that the group is active yet misses the rare-correct region.

The proof uses inclusion-exclusion at the event level—it partitions the sample space and subtracts the homogeneous-group cases. See Appendix A for the full derivation.

**Why this form:** the expression naturally decomposes into three terms with clear interpretations. `$(1 - \tau)^N$` is the probability that the rare-correct region is missed entirely (this decreases with `$N$`—more samples means better coverage). `$(\mu_{pos} - \tau)^N$` is the probability that all samples are correct but from the common-correct region only (this also decreases with `$N$` but faster than the first term, since `$\mu_{pos} - \tau < 1 - \tau$`). `$(1 - \mu_{pos})^N$` is the probability that all samples are incorrect (decreases with `$N$`, slower for high `$\mu_{pos}$`). The subtraction of the latter two terms from the first captures the non-monotonicity: for small `$N$`, `$\Pr(\mathcal{A}_N)$` is low (most groups are homogeneous), so the subtracted terms dominate and `$\Pr(\mathcal{B}_\tau)$` is near zero. For large `$N$`, `$(1 - \tau)^N$` becomes tiny (coverage is good), so `$\Pr(\mathcal{B}_\tau)$` falls again. In between, `$\Pr(\mathcal{A}_N)$` is large but coverage is poor—the peak.

**When does the peak occur?** The paper does not provide a closed-form maximizer, but Figure 2 illustrates the behavior across a range of parameters. For `$\mu_{pos} = 0.5$` and `$\rho = \tau/\mu_{pos} = 0.1$`, the peak occurs around `$N \approx 16$` with `$\Pr(\mathcal{B}_\tau) \approx 0.45$`. For smaller `$\rho$` (rarer rare-correct region, e.g., `$\rho = 0.01$`), the peak shifts rightward (to larger `$N$`) and upward (higher maximum probability of missing the region). This makes intuitive sense: the rarer the region, the more samples you need to reliably cover it, and the more likely intermediate group sizes are to miss it.

Figure 1(a) in the paper shows the same pattern plotted with different parameters (`$\mu_{pos} = 0.3$` for illustration, with `$\tau$` varied to produce different `$n$` values—the parameterization differs slightly between figures because Figure 1(a) is more pedagogical while Figure 2 is more comprehensive). The curves overlay `$n = 2, 8, 32$` on the same plot to show that the pattern holds across `$\tau$` values.

**The three regimes.** This non-monotonicity gives rise to the three-regime behavior that the paper uses to reconcile conflicting prior findings:

- **Small N (e.g., N=2):** `$\Pr(\mathcal{A}_N)$` is low (most batches are all-correct or all-incorrect). The policy updates infrequently from any given prompt, preserving the base model's diversity by simply not changing the distribution much. This is the regime analyzed by Wu et al. (2025b), who show N=2 is compute-efficient because it effectively turns GRPO into DPO-like preference learning when batches happen to contain one correct and one incorrect rollout. The paper's experiments confirm this: at N=2 on Qwen2.5-7B, pass@256 is highest (75.0 math, 67.3 OOD) but pass@1 is lowest (36.2 math), consistent with diversity preservation but slow learning.

- **Intermediate N (the "concentration zone"):** `$\Pr(\mathcal{B}_\tau)$` peaks—updates are frequent (most batches are active) but coverage of rare solutions is poor. The policy receives strong gradient signals to prefer the correct solutions it *did* sample over incorrect ones, and these are disproportionately common-correct solutions. The model learns to "bet" on common strategies and forget rare ones. This is the regime where sharpening is worst, and it corresponds to the N=8 and N=32 settings commonly used in practice. The paper's N=8 experiments show this clearly: pass@256 drops from 75.0 (N=2) to 64.1 on math while pass@1 improves from 36.2 to 37.3.

- **Large N:** Coverage improves as `$(1 - \tau)^N \to 0$`. The group reliably samples from the rare-correct region, so the gradient "sees" those solutions and updates preserve them. This is the regime analyzed by Hu et al. (2025), who show that `$\Delta Q_{pos} \geq 0$` for sufficiently large N. The paper's N=32 experiments for Qwen2.5-7B show a partial recovery: pass@256 rebounds to 70.1 (from 64.1 at N=8) while pass@1 improves to 39.2. However, in the categorical simulation (Figure 4), truly large N (131,072) is required to fully maintain diversity, which is computationally infeasible for LLM training—N=32, the largest group size typically used, is still in or near the concentration zone for many parameter regimes.

**What determines the regime boundaries?** The paper emphasizes that the boundaries depend on `$\mu_{pos}$`, `$\tau$`, and their evolution during training. As training progresses, `$\mu_{pos}$` increases (the model gets better at solving problems), which changes both `$\Pr(\mathcal{A}_N)$` and coverage probability. A problem that starts with low `$\mu_{pos}$` may have most batches homogeneous (all-incorrect, no signal), then enter the intermediate regime as it becomes solvable but inconsistently, then eventually reach high `$\mu_{pos}$` where `$\Pr(\mathcal{A}_N)$` decreases again (mostly all-correct batches). This training-time evolution is why the paper's difficulty-aware approach (Section 4) uses the *current* empirical success rate `$\hat{\mu}_{pos}$` rather than a static difficulty label—the regime changes as the policy learns.

#### Proposition 3.2: Unsampled-Correct Mass Change `$\Delta Q_{u,pos}$`

While Lemma 3.1 identifies *when* rare-correct modes are vulnerable (intermediate N where `$\Pr(\mathcal{B}_\tau)$` peaks), Proposition 3.2 characterizes the *mechanism* by which their probability mass decreases, extending the categorical framework to analyze redistribution within the correct set.

Define the **unsampled-correct mass** as:

$$Q_{u,pos} := \sum_{i \in \mathcal{U} \cap \mathcal{P}} p_i = Q_{pos} - P_{pos}$$

In words: the total probability assigned to correct actions that were *not* sampled in the current batch. This quantity measures how much correct probability is "left behind" by the sampling process—solutions the model knows about but didn't happen to generate in this particular group of N rollouts.

Proposition 3.2 derives the one-step change in `$Q_{u,pos}$` under the surrogate update (7):

$$\Delta Q_{u,pos} = \frac{\eta}{N} \left[ -\bar{R} U_{pos,2} - Q_{u,pos} \left( (R_c - \bar{R})A_2 + (R_w - \bar{R})B_2 - \bar{R}U_2 \right) \right]$$

where `$U_2 := U_{pos,2} + U_{neg,2}$` is the total second moment of unsampled actions.

**What it computes:** the per-step change in probability mass assigned to correct actions that were not in this training batch. If `$\Delta Q_{u,pos} < 0$`, unsampled-correct actions are losing probability, and they're losing it to sampled-correct actions (since total correct mass `$Q_{pos}$` can still increase—Eq. (8)—while unsampled-correct mass shrinks). If `$\Delta Q_{u,pos} > 0$`, unsampled-correct actions are gaining mass (e.g., through normalization when massive amounts of incorrect mass are being demoted).

**Derivation (Appendix D):** The proof applies the **subset-mass identity** from Appendix C with `$S = \mathcal{U} \cap \mathcal{P}$`. The identity states that for any subset `$S$` of actions,

$$\Delta Q_S = \sum_{i \in S} p_i \Delta z_i - Q_S \sum_{j \in \mathcal{A}} p_j \Delta z_j$$

The first term is the direct effect of logit changes on actions in `$S$`; the second term is the **normalization coupling**—probability mass moving elsewhere changes `$Q_S$` even if the logits of actions in `$S$` are unchanged, because softmax renormalizes the entire distribution.

For `$i \in \mathcal{U} \cap \mathcal{P}$`, we have `$R_i = 0$` (unsampled actions receive zero reward in the estimator) and `$\Delta z_i = -\frac{\eta}{N} \bar{R} p_i$` from Eq. (7). Therefore:

$$\sum_{i \in \mathcal{U} \cap \mathcal{P}} p_i \Delta z_i = -\frac{\eta}{N} \bar{R} \sum_{i \in \mathcal{U} \cap \mathcal{P}} p_i^2 = -\frac{\eta}{N} \bar{R} U_{pos,2}$$

For the normalization term, we sum `$\Delta z_j$` over all actions `$j$`, partitioned by reward value: sampled-correct (`$R_j = R_c$`), sampled-incorrect (`$R_j = R_w$`), and unsampled (`$R_j = 0$`):

$$\sum_{j \in \mathcal{A}} p_j \Delta z_j = \frac{\eta}{N} \left[ (R_c - \bar{R})A_2 + (R_w - \bar{R})B_2 - \bar{R}U_2 \right]$$

Multiplying by `$-Q_S$` and combining with the direct term yields Eq. (13).

**Why this form matters:** the expression decomposes into two terms with very different behaviors:

**Term 1: Direct drift** (`$-\bar{R} U_{pos,2}$`). This term captures the direct effect of the baseline subtraction on unsampled correct actions. When `$\bar{R} > 0$` (reward-positive batch—more sampled-correct mass than sampled-incorrect mass, weighted by rewards), this term is **negative**: unsampled-correct actions get their logits pushed downward even though they would have been correct if sampled. The magnitude scales with `$U_{pos,2}$`, the concentration (second moment) of unsampled-correct mass. If unsampled-correct mass is highly concentrated (few actions with high probability), the direct drift is large; if it's diffuse (many actions each with tiny probability), the direct drift is small. However, even when `$U_{pos,2}$` is small, the drift is uniformly downward across all unsampled actions.

**Term 2: Normalization coupling** (`$-Q_{u,pos}[...]$`). This term captures how mass gains by *other* actions affect unsampled-correct mass through the softmax denominator. The bracket contains three components:

- `$(R_c - \bar{R})A_2 \geq 0$`: sampled-correct actions are gaining probability (their reward exceeds the baseline), which through normalization **drains** mass from all other actions, including unsampled-correct ones. This term is always non-negative.
- `$(R_w - \bar{R})B_2 \leq 0$`: sampled-incorrect actions are losing probability (their reward is below the baseline), which through normalization **donates** mass to all other actions, including unsampled-correct ones. This term is always non-positive, so it partially offsets the drainage.
- `$-\bar{R}U_2$`: when `$\bar{R} > 0$`, unsampled actions (both correct and incorrect) lose mass through baseline subtraction, and this feeds through normalization to affect `$Q_{u,pos}$`.

The overall normalization coupling term is multiplied by `$-Q_{u,pos}$`, so when the bracket is positive (the norm being driven by sampled-correct gains), the coupling is negative (drains unsampled-correct mass), and when the bracket is negative (the norm being driven by sampled-incorrect losses), the coupling is positive (feeds unsampled-correct mass).

**The key regime: reward-positive batches with finite N.** Consider a prompt that the model already solves with moderately high probability (say, `$\mu_{pos} \approx 0.5$`), trained with intermediate N (say, N=8). In a typical active batch with 4–6 correct rollouts, `$\bar{R}$` will be positive. The direct drift `$-\bar{R}U_{pos,2}$` pushes all unsampled-correct actions downward. The normalization coupling is dominated by `$(R_c - \bar{R})A_2$` (the few sampled-correct actions with large `$p_i$` are gaining significantly), which further drains unsampled-correct mass. The result is that `$\Delta Q_{u,pos} < 0$` even though `$\Delta Q_{pos} > 0$` (checking Eq. (8), the first two terms are `$(R_c - \bar{R})Q_{neg}A_2 + (\bar{R} - R_w)Q_{pos}B_2$`, both of which are positive as long as `$R_w \leq 0$` and `$R_c > 0$`).

This is the redistribution mechanism: the policy simultaneously becomes more likely to produce a correct answer **and** concentrates that likelihood onto the subset of correct solutions that happened to be sampled in recent batches. Solutions that are rare (low `$p_i$`) but correct are particularly vulnerable because they have low second moment `$U_{pos,2}$` but are still unsampled in most batches; over many training steps, the cumulative direct drift drives their probability toward zero.

**What happens as N increases?** Both `$U_{pos,2}$` and `$A_2$` depend on N. As N grows: (1) more correct actions are sampled, so `$\mathcal{U} \cap \mathcal{P}$` shrinks, reducing `$U_{pos,2}$` and the direct drift magnitude; (2) the sampled-correct second moment `$A_2$` becomes a larger fraction of `$Q_{pos,2}$` (the total second moment of correct mass), so the normalization coupling more accurately reflects the true correct distribution. In the limit `$N \to \infty$`, `$U_{pos,2} \to 0$` and `$\Delta Q_{u,pos} \to 0$` (or becomes non-negative), consistent with the Hu et al. analysis that scaling N guarantees `$\Delta Q_{pos} \geq 0$` and preserves diversity. But for practical N (8, 16, 32), `$U_{pos,2}$` is non-negligible and unsampled-correct mass systematically shrinks.

**Connection to Lemma 3.1.** Proposition 3.2 operates at the categorical level, tracking `$Q_{u,pos}$` in a single update step. Lemma 3.1 operates at the trajectory level, tracking `$\Pr(\mathcal{B}_\tau)$` at the start of an update. The two are connected: `$\Pr(\mathcal{B}_\tau)$` tells you how likely it is that a given rare-correct region will be completely unsampled (all its actions in `$\mathcal{U}$`) while the batch is active. Proposition 3.2 tells you that, conditional on that happening, those unsampled-correct actions will lose mass. The combination explains why intermediate N is maximally harmful: it maximizes `$\Pr(\mathcal{B}_\tau)$` (frequent active batches with poor coverage) and then Proposition 3.2 ensures that each such batch systematically erases the unsampled solutions.

#### From Theory to Observable Proxy: The Monotonicity Connection (Appendix B)

Proposition 3.2 identifies `$\bar{R} > 0$` as the condition driving concentration of unsampled-correct mass. But `$\bar{R}$` at the categorical level depends on `$P_{pos}$` and `$P_{neg}$`, the masses of distinct sampled rollouts—quantities we cannot directly observe because they require knowing the policy's probability `$\pi(o)$` for every possible rollout, which is intractable for autoregressive LLMs.

We need an **observable proxy**: a per-prompt statistic that can be computed from the sampled rollouts and is monotonically related to `$\bar{R}$`. The paper uses the empirical success rate:

$$\hat{\mu}_{pos}(x) := \frac{\bar{R}(x) - R_w}{R_c - R_w} = \frac{X}{N}$$

where `$X$` is the number of correct rollouts in the group. For the standard reward setup `$R_c = 1, R_w = 0$`, this simplifies to `$\hat{\mu}_{pos}(x) = X/N$`, the fraction of rollouts that are correct. It is an unbiased estimator: `$\mathbb{E}[\hat{\mu}_{pos}(x)] = \mu_{pos}(x)$`.

Appendix B establishes the formal connection through **Lemma B.1 and Corollary B.2**:

**Lemma B.1** (monotonicity of sampled distinct mass): For the trajectory-level analog of the categorical framework, define sampled-correct mass `$P_{pos}$` as the total policy probability of all *distinct* correct rollouts that appear in the batch (duplicates counted once), and similarly for sampled-incorrect mass `$P_{neg}$`. Conditioned on observing `$X = k$` correct rollouts among the N samples:

- `$\mathbb{E}[P_{pos} \mid X = k]$` is **non-decreasing in `$k$`**: more correct rollouts means the batch spans more of the correct-mass distribution.
- `$\mathbb{E}[P_{neg} \mid X = k]$` is **non-increasing in `$k$`**: more correct rollouts means fewer incorrect rollouts and thus less coverage of the incorrect-mass distribution.

**Corollary B.2** (monotonicity of `$\bar{R}$`): Under standard RLVR rewards (`$R_c > 0$`, `$R_w \leq 0$`), `$\mathbb{E}[\bar{R} \mid X = k]$` is **non-decreasing in `$k$`**. This follows from Lemma B.1 by linearity of expectation: `$\bar{R} = R_c P_{pos} + R_w P_{neg}$`, where `$R_c > 0$` and the `$P_{pos}$` term increases with `$k$`, while `$R_w \leq 0$` and the `$P_{neg}$` term decreases with `$k$` (which, when multiplied by non-positive `$R_w$`, also increases with `$k$`).

The proof of Lemma B.1 uses the fact that, conditioned on `$X = k$`, the `$k$` correct rollouts are i.i.d. draws from the conditional distribution `$q_{pos}(o) = \pi(o) / \mu_{pos}$` over correct rollouts. The probability that a specific correct rollout `$o$` appears at least once among these `$k$` draws is `$1 - (1 - q_{pos}(o))^k$`, which increases with `$k$`. Summing over all correct rollouts weighted by their base probabilities gives `$\mathbb{E}[P_{pos} \mid X = k]$`, which is clearly non-decreasing. The same logic applies to `$P_{neg}$` with `$N - k$` incorrect draws.

**Why this connection matters for Focal weighting:** Corollary B.2 tells us that observing a high empirical success rate `$\hat{\mu}_{pos}$` (large `$k$`) implies that the expected batch baseline `$\bar{R}$` is high, which is exactly the `$\bar{R} > 0$` regime where Proposition 3.2's direct drift `$-\bar{R} U_{pos,2}$` and normalization coupling are most strongly negative for unsampled-correct mass. High `$\hat{\mu}_{pos}$` thus marks prompts where the concentration pressure is strongest. Conversely, low `$\hat{\mu}_{pos}$` indicates `$\bar{R}$` closer to zero or negative, where the drift mechanism is weaker or reversed. This justifies using `$\hat{\mu}_{pos}$` as a per-prompt signal to modulate update strength: reduce learning on high-`$\hat{\mu}_{pos}$` prompts to limit concentration, while preserving or increasing learning on low-`$\hat{\mu}_{pos}$` prompts where the model still needs to improve.

This connection is not an equivalence—`$\hat{\mu}_{pos}$` is a noisy estimate of `$\mu_{pos}$`, and `$\mathbb{E}[\bar{R} \mid X = k]$` is monotonic in `$k$` but not a deterministic function of it—but it provides the theoretical justification for the Focal weight's functional form.

#### The Focal Weighting Intervention (Section 4)

Having established that high empirical success rate correlates with the concentration regime, the paper proposes a simple corrective: **down-weight the gradient contribution of prompts where the model already succeeds frequently.**

**Step 1: Compute the difficulty weight.** For each prompt `$x$` in a training batch, compute the empirical success rate from the `$N$` rollouts generated for that prompt:

$$\hat{\mu}_{pos}(x) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \mathbb{I}[o_i \text{ is correct}]$$

This is immediately available from the same rollouts used for advantage estimation—no additional computation is needed. Then apply the Focal weight:

$$g(x) := (1 - \hat{\mu}_{pos}(x))^\gamma, \quad \gamma \geq 0$$

where `$\gamma$` is a tunable hyperparameter controlling the strength of the down-weighting, and `$\hat{\mu}_{pos}(x) \in [0, 1]$` is the fraction of correct rollouts for prompt `$x$`.

**What it computes:** a scalar between 0 and 1 that is close to 1 when the model performs poorly on the prompt (`$\hat{\mu}_{pos} \approx 0$`) and close to 0 when the model performs well (`$\hat{\mu}_{pos} \approx 1$`). For example, with `$\gamma = 1$` and `$\hat{\mu}_{pos} = 0.8$`, `$g(x) = 0.2$` (the prompt gets 20% of its original gradient contribution). With `$\gamma = 2$`, the same prompt gets `$g(x) = 0.04$` (4%). The functional form is directly borrowed from Focal loss (Lin et al., 2017), originally designed for object detection to down-weight the loss contribution of well-classified (easy) examples and focus training on hard examples.

**Why this form:** the power-law shape `$(1 - \hat{\mu}_{pos})^\gamma$` smoothly interpolates between no modification (`$\gamma = 0$`, `$g(x) = 1$` for all prompts, recovering the original algorithm) and aggressive down-weighting as `$\gamma$` increases. The `$1 - \hat{\mu}_{pos}$` term ensures that prompts with very low success rate receive nearly full weight, while prompts with intermediate-to-high success rate are progressively suppressed. This targets exactly the `$\bar{R} > 0$` regime identified by the theory without requiring hard thresholds or per-prompt labels. The paper sweeps `$\gamma \in \{0.5, 1.0, 2.0\}$` and selects the best value per method-model combination by average in-domain math pass@1; `$\gamma = 0.5$` and `$\gamma = 1.0$` are the most commonly selected values (Table 6).

**Step 2: Scale the group-relative advantage.** For each rollout `$i$` from prompt `$x$`, compute the Focal-weighted advantage:

$$\hat{A}^{F-GRPO}_i := g(x) \cdot \hat{A}^{GRPO}_i$$

This single scalar `$g(x)$` is applied uniformly to all `$N$` rollouts from the same prompt. The policy is then optimized using this scaled advantage in place of `$\hat{A}^{GRPO}_i$` in whichever group-relative objective is being used (GRPO's clipped surrogate, DAPO's asymmetric clipping, or CISPO's clipped importance weighting).

**Effect on gradient magnitudes.** Figure 3 illustrates the effect by plotting `$g(x) \cdot |\hat{A}^{GRPO}|$` as a function of `$\mu_{pos}$` for binary rewards (assuming the expectation matches the empirical rate). The GRPO advantage magnitude for correct rollouts is largest when `$\mu_{pos}$` is small (correct answers are rare and thus highly rewarded relative to the group mean) and shrinks as `$\mu_{pos}$` increases. For incorrect rollouts, the magnitude is largest when `$\mu_{pos}$` is large (incorrect answers are rare and heavily penalized). The Focal weight `$g(x) = (1 - \mu_{pos})^\gamma$` multiplies both, but the effect is asymmetric: at high `$\mu_{pos}$` (where the direct drift mechanism is strongest per Proposition 3.2), the correct-rollout advantage is already small, so the Focal weight further suppresses it; the incorrect-rollout advantage is large, but suppressing it reduces the gradient contribution from a prompt that the model already mostly solves—this is exactly the goal, since these are the prompts where concentration pressure peaks.

**Why scale the advantage rather than the loss directly?** The paper does not explicitly discuss this design choice, but the structure of the group-relative advantage `$\hat{A}^{GRPO}_i = (R_i - \bar{R}) / (\sigma_R + \epsilon)$` provides a rationale. The advantage is the signal that determines both the direction and magnitude of the per-token gradient. By scaling it, F-GRPO affects the entire policy update for that prompt uniformly: all tokens from all rollouts from the prompt receive proportionally smaller updates. If the Focal weight were instead applied to the per-token loss or the importance ratio, it might interact in unexpected ways with the clipping mechanism (which is designed for unmodified advantages) or with the per-token averaging. Advantage scaling is both simpler and more interpretable.

**Integration with DAPO and CISPO.** The paper emphasizes that Focal weighting is **orthogonal** to the clipping and importance-weighting choices of DAPO and CISPO. F-DAPO uses the same asymmetric clipping bounds `$\epsilon_{low} = 0.2, \epsilon_{high} = 0.28$` but with Focal-scaled advantages. F-CISPO uses the same clipped importance weights `$\hat{r}_{i,t}$` with `$\epsilon^{IS}_{low} = 1.0, \epsilon^{IS}_{high} = 5.0$` and stop-gradient, but with `$\hat{A}^{F-GRPO}_i$` in place of `$\hat{A}^{GRPO}_i$`. The concentration mechanism analyzed in Section 3 comes from the group-relative advantage estimation itself (the `$R_i - \bar{R}$` term and the `$1/(\sigma_R + \epsilon)$` normalization), not from how that advantage is subsequently clipped or used in the loss. Therefore, any method that uses group-relative advantages can incorporate Focal weighting.

**What happens to prompts with `$\hat{\mu}_{pos} = 0$` or `$\hat{\mu}_{pos} = 1$`?** When `$\hat{\mu}_{pos} = 0$` (all rollouts incorrect), `$g(x) = 1$` and the batch is homogeneous (all rewards identical, `$\sigma_R = 0$`), so all advantages are zero anyway—the Focal weight has no effect. When `$\hat{\mu}_{pos} = 1$` (all rollouts correct), `$g(x) = 0$` and the batch is again homogeneous (all advantages zero), so the Focal weight also has no effect. The Focal weight matters precisely in the active regime `$0 < \hat{\mu}_{pos} < 1$`, where `$\sigma_R > 0$` and the group-relative advantage is non-zero, and it progressively reduces the update magnitude as `$\hat{\mu}_{pos}$` increases toward 1—the regime where Proposition 3.2 says the direct drift is most harmful.

**Hyperparameter sensitivity.** The paper sweeps `$\gamma$` over `$\{0.5, 1.0, 2.0\}$` and selects the best value per method-model combination based on average in-domain math pass@1 at the best checkpoint (Table 6). Across the three model families (Qwen2.5-7B, Qwen2.5-1.5B-Math, Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct) and three methods (GRPO, DAPO, CISPO), the selected values are either 0.5 or 1.0; `$\gamma = 2.0$` is never selected as best. This suggests that moderate down-weighting (halving to linearly suppressing high-success prompts) is optimal, while aggressive down-weighting (`$\gamma = 2.0$`, where a prompt with `$\hat{\mu}_{pos} = 0.5$` gets only 25% weight) is counterproductive—presumably because it overly suppresses useful learning signal from prompts where the model is partially but not fully competent.

**Computational cost.** Focal weighting adds a single scalar multiplication per prompt after computing `$\hat{\mu}_{pos}$` (which is already computed during standard advantage normalization—it's just `$\bar{R}$` converted from reward scale to probability scale). The modification requires no additional forward passes, no extra model parameters, no extra memory, and no change to the training loop structure. The paper emphasizes this explicitly: "No additional networks are required; `$\gamma$` is the only new hyperparameter."

#### The Categorical Simulation: Validating the Three-Regime Framework (Section 5.1, Appendix J)

To empirically validate the theoretical analysis and observe the three regimes in a controlled setting, the paper conducts categorical policy simulations following the setup of Hu et al. (2025) but with key modifications to better reflect LLM training dynamics.

**Setup.** The policy is a softmax distribution over `$|\mathcal{A}| = 128,000$` actions. Of these, 10,000 actions are designated as correct (reward `$R_c = +1$`), and the remaining 118,000 are incorrect (reward `$R_w = -1$`). The logits are initialized asymmetrically to create a realistic concentration structure:

- One **anchor** correct action starts with `$z_{anchor} = 5.0$` (giving it significantly higher probability than others).
- The remaining 9,999 correct actions start with `$z = 3.0$`.
- All 118,000 incorrect actions start with `$z = 0.0$`.

Under softmax with temperature 1.0, this yields:
- Total correct mass `$Q_{pos} \approx 0.63$` (the model starts fairly competent, as an LLM does on many math problems).
- Anchor probability `$p_{anchor} \approx 4.7 \times 10^{-4}$` (roughly 0.05%—the most common single correct action).
- Leaf (non-anchor) correct probability `$\tau_{leaf} \approx 6.3 \times 10^{-5}$` (roughly 0.006%—each rare-correct action is about 7.5× less likely than the anchor).

At each training step, `$N$` actions are sampled i.i.d. from the current policy, group-relative advantages are computed as `$\tilde{r}_j = R_j - \frac{1}{N} \sum_k R_k$` (without the `$\sigma_R$` normalization, since the categorical framework's rewards are deterministic and advantages don't need variance normalization for stability), and logits are updated via gradient ascent on `$\mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_j \tilde{r}_j p_j$`. When Focal weighting is applied, the objective is scaled by `$g = (1 - \hat{\mu}_{pos})^\gamma$` (note: in the categorical simulation, `$\hat{\mu}_{pos}$` is the fraction of sampled actions that are correct, analogous to `$X/N$` in trajectory-level RLVR).

**Learning rate modification.** The paper uses `$\eta = 10^{-2}$`, which is **10× higher** than the `$\eta = 10^{-3}$` used by Hu et al. (2025). The reason is an important empirical observation: at `$\eta = 10^{-3}$`, the policy entropy after 1,000 training steps remained above 4 even for N=65,536, whereas LLM generation entropy during RLVR training is typically below 1. The higher learning rate produces faster concentration dynamics that better reflect what happens in real LLM training, where multiple epochs over the training data, large batch sizes, and high learning rates relative to the base model's entropy cause rapid distribution shift.

**Swept parameters.** The paper sweeps `$N \in \{2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128, 256, 512, 1024, 2048, 4096, 8192, 16384, 32768, 65536, 131072\}$` (powers of 2 from 2 to 131,072) and `$\gamma \in \{0, 1\}$`, running T=1,000 steps per configuration with 4 random seeds. This sweep ranges from N=2 (the small-N regime) to N=131,072 (the large-N regime where `$\Pr(\mathcal{B}_\tau) < 10^{-3}$` for the leaf actions, per Figure 5 in Appendix J), covering the full spectrum from the "inactivity" to "coverage" regimes.

**Metrics.** Two quantities are tracked:

1. **Total correct mass** `$Q_{pos}(t) = \sum_{a \in \mathcal{P}} \pi_t(a)$` — the probability of sampling any correct action at step `$t$`. This is the metric analyzed by Hu et al. (2025); it should increase with training as the policy learns to prefer correct actions.

2. **Retained positive mass** `$\mathcal{M}_{ret}(t)$` — a new metric that measures diversity preservation within the correct set:

$$\mathcal{M}_{ret}(t) = 1 - \frac{\sum_{a \in \mathcal{P}} \max(0, \pi_0(a) - \pi_t(a))}{\sum_{a \in \mathcal{P}} \pi_0(a)}$$

where `$\pi_0(a)$` is the initial probability of correct action `$a$` at step 0, and `$\pi_t(a)$` is its probability after `$t$` training steps. `$\mathcal{M}_{ret}$` quantifies what fraction of the initial correct probability mass has **not decreased**—it measures whether rare-correct actions maintain their original probability. A value near 1 means all correct actions retain at least their starting mass (diversity is preserved); a value near 0 means most correct actions have lost probability (the policy has concentrated onto a subset of correct actions, likely the anchor).

**Why this metric:** `$\mathcal{M}_{ret}$` directly operationalizes the concept of unsampled-correct mass preservation. If an action starts with probability `$\pi_0(a) = 6.3 \times 10^{-5}$` and after training has `$\pi_t(a) = 10^{-6}$`, its contribution to the numerator is `$\max(0, 6.3 \times 10^{-5} - 10^{-6}) \approx 6.2 \times 10^{-5}$`. Summing over all correct actions gives the total mass that has been "squeezed out" of initially positive solutions. `$\mathcal{M}_{ret} = 0.5$` means half of the initial correct probability mass has been redistributed (likely to the anchor or other high-probability correct actions). `$\mathcal{M}_{ret} = 1.0$` means every correct action is at or above its starting probability—the policy has become more likely to produce correct answers without abandoning any solution strategies.

**Results (Figure 4).** The simulation reveals three qualitatively distinct regimes:

- **Panel (a): Total correct mass `$Q_{pos}$`.** For all group sizes, `$Q_{pos}$` increases monotonically with training steps, consistent with Hu et al. (2025). Larger N leads to faster `$Q_{pos}$` growth. This confirms that RLVR is effective at increasing overall accuracy, regardless of group size. The curves for different N are separated but qualitatively similar—`$Q_{pos}$` alone does not reveal the diversity issue.

- **Panel (b): Retained positive mass `$\mathcal{M}_{ret}$`.** This is where the non-monotonicity becomes stark. For N=2, `$\mathcal{M}_{ret}$` stays near 1 throughout training—the policy barely changes because most batches are homogeneous. For N=131,072, `$\mathcal{M}_{ret}$` also stays near 1—every correct action is reliably sampled, so none are forgotten. For intermediate N (especially N=32, 256, 2048), `$\mathcal{M}_{ret}$` collapses to near zero: the policy rapidly concentrates onto the anchor correct action while losing probability on the 9,999 other correct actions, even as total `$Q_{pos}$` improves. This is the concentration zone—exactly the regime where practical RLVR training operates.

- **Panel (c): Final state at step 1000.** The x-axis shows group size N (log scale); the y-axis shows two overlaid quantities: `$Q_{pos}$` (total correct mass, green/blue curves) and `$\mathcal{M}_{ret}$` (retained positive mass, dashed/solid curves). The plot is annotated with three labeled regimes:
  - **I (small N):** `$Q_{pos}$` grows slowly (the policy learns slowly because most batches are inactive), but `$\mathcal{M}_{ret}$` stays high (diversity is preserved through inactivity).
  - **II (concentration zone, shaded):** `$Q_{pos}$` grows rapidly (frequent active updates), but `$\mathcal{M}_{ret}$` collapses (the updates systematically favor common-correct actions and forget rare-correct ones). This zone spans roughly N=8 to N=8,192 in the simulation, though the exact boundaries are specific to the initialization and should not be taken as quantitative predictions for LLM training.
  - **III (large N):** Both `$Q_{pos}$` and `$\mathcal{M}_{ret}$` are high (good accuracy with preserved diversity through coverage). This regime begins around N=65,536 in the simulation—well beyond practical LLM training group sizes.

- **Effect of Focal weighting (dashed lines, `$\gamma = 1$`).** In the concentration zone, Focal weighting substantially improves `$\mathcal{M}_{ret}$` while having minimal impact on `$Q_{pos}$`. For example, at N=256, `$\mathcal{M}_{ret}(\gamma=1)$` is approximately 0.4 vs. `$\mathcal{M}_{ret}(\gamma=0) \approx 0.05$`—an 8× improvement in diversity preservation with only a modest reduction in `$Q_{pos}$`. At N=2 and N=131,072, Focal weighting has negligible effect (the curves nearly overlap), because the concentration mechanism is not active in those regimes.

**Connection to LLM training.** The simulation uses a single "prompt" with a fixed set of correct and incorrect actions, whereas LLM training involves thousands of prompts with different success rates and solution spaces. The paper explicitly cautions that the specific N boundaries of the concentration zone are simulation-specific; the key insight is the qualitative pattern. In multi-prompt LLM training, Focal weighting additionally reallocates gradient contribution from easy prompts (high `$\hat{\mu}_{pos}$`) to hard prompts (low `$\hat{\mu}_{pos}$`), which may partially explain why F-GRPO achieves better pass@1 in some settings—it focuses learning on prompts where the model still has room to improve rather than over-optimizing prompts it already mostly solves.

The simulation with N=131,072 maintaining `$\mathcal{M}_{ret} \approx 1$` directly validates Lemma 3.1: Appendix J computes `$\Pr(\mathcal{B}_\tau) < 10^{-3}$` for a leaf correct action at this N, using `$\mu_{pos} = 0.64$` and `$\tau = 6.3 \times 10^{-5}$`. The prediction that sufficiently large groups preserve rare-correct mass is borne out exactly where the probability calculation says it should be. For N=2, `$\Pr(\mathcal{B}_\tau)$` is also low (most batches inactive), explaining the diversity preservation through a different mechanism.

#### Summary: Why F-GRPO Is Not Just Another Regularizer

The paper positions F-GRPO as distinct from existing mitigation strategies by grounding it in the sampling dynamics analysis rather than in generic diversity-promoting heuristics. Several design aspects reflect this:

1. **Targeted suppression, not uniform regularization.** Entropy regularization pushes all prompts toward higher per-token uncertainty, regardless of whether those prompts are in the concentration zone. KL regularization penalizes all deviation from the base model equally. Both are "blunt instruments" that affect prompts where concentration is not a problem (low `$\mu_{pos}$`, where the model needs to change; very high `$\mu_{pos}$`, where updates are already infrequent). Focal weighting only suppresses prompts where `$\bar{R} > 0$` is likely—the exact regime where Proposition 3.2 predicts concentration—and preserves learning signal where it's most needed.

2. **Operates at the prompt level, not the token level.** The concentration mechanism analyzed in the paper is about which *solutions* get reinforced, not about which *tokens* within a solution get reinforced. Token-level interventions (entropy reweighting of individual tokens) address a different phenomenon (local token-level certainty) and can be applied orthogonally to Focal weighting.

3. **Grounded in a closed-form probability, not just empirical observation.** The tail-miss probability `$\Pr(\mathcal{B}_\tau)$` provides a theoretically justified explanation for *why* intermediate N is worst, which in turn justifies *why* down-weighting high-`$\hat{\mu}_{pos}$` prompts should help. This distinguishes F-GRPO from purely empirical modifications that happen to improve pass@k.

4. **No extra compute, no architectural changes.** The Focal weight `$g(x)$` is computed from quantities already available during training (`$\hat{\mu}_{pos}$` is just the fraction of correct rollouts), applied as a scalar multiplication to the advantage, and requires only a single new hyperparameter `$\gamma$`. This makes it trivially integrable into existing RLVR pipelines without changing data generation, model architecture, or training loop structure.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: The Non-Monotonic Tail-Miss Probability as a Unified Explanation for Conflicting Prior Findings

The paper's most distinctive conceptual contribution is not, at its core, the Focal weighting algorithm — it is the **diagnostic insight that the probability of a harmful training event follows a non-monotonic curve with group size**, and that this single curve reconciles years of contradictory empirical observations about RLVR. Prior to this work, the literature contained directly opposing claims: Wu et al. (2025b) showed that N=2 rollouts suffice and connected GRPO to DPO, while Hu et al. (2025) advocated scaling N as large as possible to guarantee stable learning. He et al. (2025) observed sharpening at N=32 but attributed it to "rank bias" in the advantage computation rather than to finite sampling dynamics. These findings appeared contradictory because each study implicitly operated in a different region of the non-monotonic curve without the conceptual framework to recognize it.

Lemma 3.1 provides that framework. By deriving a closed-form expression for `Pr(B_τ)` — the probability that a training batch is active (mixed rewards) yet misses a designated rare-correct region — the paper shows that small N preserves diversity through *inactivity* (most batches are homogeneous, so the policy barely updates), large N preserves diversity through *coverage* (rare solutions are reliably sampled), but intermediate N — precisely the range most commonly used in practice (8, 16, 32) — **maximizes the probability of updates that push the model toward common solutions and away from rare ones**. This is not an incremental refinement of prior analyses. It is a fundamental re-contextualization that converts a set of conflicting empirical claims into a single, coherent phase diagram parameterized by group size, success probability `μ_pos`, and rare-correct mass `τ`.

What makes this innovation conceptually powerful rather than just a neat probability derivation is that it provides a **generative model of the contradiction itself**. Wu et al. (2025b) are correct *when evaluated in the small-N regime* — N=2 does preserve diversity, because the model barely changes. Hu et al. (2025) are correct *when extrapolated to the large-N regime* — sufficiently large groups do guarantee monotonic improvements in correct mass. He et al. (2025) are correct *about the phenomenon* — sharpening does occur at moderate N — but the mechanism is not rank bias in advantage computation; it is finite sampling systematically missing low-probability correct solutions. The paper doesn't just say "everyone is partially right"; it provides the mathematics that predicts *where* each regime begins and ends as a function of the model's current capabilities.

The three-regime diagram in Figure 4(c), validated by sweeping group sizes from 2 to 131,072 in the categorical simulation, operationalizes this insight. Region I (small N): slow learning, diversity preserved through inaction. Region II (shaded concentration zone): rapid learning at the cost of catastrophic diversity collapse. Region III (large N): both accuracy and diversity maintained, but at computational cost far exceeding practical LLM training budgets. The paper's key empirical observation — that N=8 and N=32, the most commonly used group sizes in production RLVR systems, fall squarely in Region II for realistic parameter values — is what transforms this from a theoretical curiosity into a practical diagnosis of why the field has struggled with sharpening.

The significance of this contribution extends beyond GRPO specifically. Any RL algorithm that computes advantages relative to a finite batch — which includes most scalable implementations of policy gradient methods — is susceptible to the same tail-miss phenomenon when the reward distribution is sparse and solution strategies have widely varying probabilities. Lemma 3.1 provides a general-purpose tool for reasoning about when and why such algorithms will systematically suppress rare positive outcomes.

### Innovation 2: Redistribution Within the Correct Set as a Distinct Failure Mode from Total Mass Loss

Prior work on RLVR distribution sharpening — including the categorical analysis of Hu et al. (2025) that this paper directly builds on — focused almost exclusively on tracking **total correct mass** `Q_pos`, the aggregate probability of producing any correct answer. The implicit assumption was that if `ΔQ_pos ≥ 0` (total correct mass is non-decreasing), training is proceeding well. The sharpening concern was framed as a potential failure of this guarantee: at small N, the unsampled coupling term in Eq. (8) might outweigh the positive terms, causing `Q_pos` to decrease.

Proposition 3.2 makes a fundamentally different diagnostic move: it shows that **even when `ΔQ_pos > 0`, unsampled-correct mass `Q_{u,pos}` can decrease — and typically does — in exactly the reward-positive regime that dominates training on partially-solved prompts**. The failure mode is not that the model becomes less accurate overall; it's that the model becomes accurate *by forgetting how to produce its rarer correct solutions and concentrating onto a smaller subset*.

This is a conceptual shift analogous to distinguishing between "the model is getting worse" and "the model is getting less diverse." The former would be captured by declining `Q_pos` (or pass@1); the latter is invisible to `Q_pos` but directly captured by the retained positive mass `M_ret` that the paper introduces (Appendix J, Eq. 26). In the categorical simulation (Figure 4b), N=256 shows `Q_pos` increasing from ~0.63 to ~0.95 — a dramatic accuracy improvement — while `M_ret` collapses to ~0.05 — meaning 95% of the initial correct probability mass has been redistributed away from its original locations, almost certainly onto the anchor action. This is a model that is simultaneously much "better" (more likely to produce a correct answer on any single attempt) and much "narrower" (incapable of producing correct answers through diverse strategies, which destroys pass@256).

The two-term decomposition of `ΔQ_{u,pos}` — the **direct drift** `-R̄ U_{pos,2}` and the **normalization coupling** `-Q_{u,pos}[...]` — provides mechanistic clarity about *how* this redistribution occurs. The direct drift term is the most insidious: unsampled correct actions receive zero reward in the Monte Carlo estimator (because they weren't generated), but they still suffer the baseline subtraction through `-R̄`. When `R̄ > 0` (the batch is reward-positive), every unsampled correct action gets its logit pushed downward by an amount proportional to its current probability. Over many training steps, this cumulative downward pressure systematically erases rare solutions — solutions that were *never wrong* but were also *never sampled* in the batches where learning was most active.

This mechanism is distinct from the "overconfidence" explanation proposed by Chen et al. (2025b), which attributes sharpening to cross-entropy training dynamics. It is distinct from the "rank bias" explanation of He et al. (2025), which locates the problem in how advantages are normalized. And it is a genuine extension of Hu et al. (2025), who showed that the unsampled coupling can be negative but did not analyze redistribution within the correct set specifically. Proposition 3.2 establishes that the unsampled-correct mass problem is not a small correction term that disappears at moderate N — it is structurally present in every reward-positive batch and only vanishes when `U_{pos,2} → 0`, which requires N so large that all correct actions of non-trivial probability are sampled in every batch.

### Innovation 3: Difficulty-Aware Prompt-Level Weighting as a Sampling-Dynamics-Aware Intervention

Focal weighting itself is a deceptively simple algorithm — multiply a per-prompt advantage by `(1 - μ̂_pos)^γ` — but its intellectual contribution lies in **what it reveals about the appropriate granularity for interventions against sharpening**. Prior mitigation strategies for RLVR diversity loss operate at either the token level (entropy regularization on individual token probabilities; Cui et al., 2025; Cheng et al., 2025; Hao et al., 2026; Peng et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025), the trajectory level (assigning different importance to individual correct trajectories; He et al., 2025; Gai et al., 2025), or the global level (KL regularization constraining deviation from a reference model). Each of these targets a different resolution of the training signal, and each assumes a different root cause of sharpening.

F-GRPO's choice to operate at the **prompt level** — scaling the entire gradient contribution of a training example based solely on the fraction of its rollouts that are correct — is not arbitrary. It follows directly from the theoretical analysis: the concentration mechanism is driven by `R̄ > 0`, and `R̄`'s expected value is monotonically related to the prompt's empirical success rate `μ̂_pos` (Corollary B.2, Appendix B). A prompt with `μ̂_pos = 0.8` is in a regime where unsampled-correct mass is being systematically eroded (per Proposition 3.2) *regardless of which specific tokens or trajectories are involved*. The appropriate intervention is therefore not to reweight individual tokens or trajectories within the prompt, but to reduce how much that prompt contributes to the overall gradient at all.

This is a subtle but important conceptual distinction. Token-level entropy methods implicitly assume that sharpening is caused by the policy becoming too confident about individual token choices — that the model needs to be more "exploratory" at the local level. Trajectory-level reweighting methods assume that the problem is that some correct trajectories aren't getting enough positive reinforcement. F-GRPO's analysis suggests that neither of these is the core issue: the core issue is that **some prompts are contributing gradient signal when they shouldn't be contributing as much**, because their batches reliably sample common-correct solutions but miss rare ones. The appropriate countermeasure is prompt-level gradient scaling, not token-level entropy manipulation.

This framing positions Focal weighting as **orthogonal to, not competing with**, token-level and trajectory-level approaches. The paper explicitly notes (Section 6) that these approaches "regulate how probability mass is distributed within trajectories; our Focal weighting is orthogonal, regulating which prompts contribute to learning." This means F-GRPO could be combined with entropy regularization, unlikelihood reward, or differential smoothing without conflict — each addresses a different aspect of the sharpening problem at a different granularity.

The comparison with KL regularization in Section 5.5 (full results in Appendix G) illustrates why prompt-level targeting matters. KL regularization (`β = 0.001` to the base model) achieves higher math pass@256 than F-GRPO (72.0 vs. 70.3) but requires maintaining a reference model in memory and yields lower OOD pass@256 (60.0 vs. 63.3) and lower pass@1 on both in-domain (37.2 vs. 38.6) and OOD benchmarks. The interpretation: KL regularization is a blunt instrument that preserves diversity by preventing the model from changing much at all — on any prompt, regardless of difficulty. This helps pass@256 (diversity is preserved everywhere) but hurts pass@1 (the model can't sufficiently adapt to improve its first-attempt accuracy on hard problems). F-GRPO is more surgical: it suppresses updates where concentration risk is high (easy prompts), while preserving full learning signal where the model needs to improve (hard prompts). The result is better pass@1 (the model actually learns on hard problems) with competitive pass@256 (diversity is preserved where it needs to be).

### Innovation 4: The Categorical Simulation as a Discovery Tool for Regime Characterization

The paper's use of the categorical simulation (Section 5.1, Figure 4) represents a methodological innovation that deserves recognition independent of the specific findings it produces. By adapting the categorical framework of Hu et al. (2025) — modifying the learning rate to better match LLM training dynamics and introducing the retained positive mass metric `M_ret` — the paper creates a **controlled experimental sandbox** where group size can be swept across five orders of magnitude (2 to 131,072) while tracking metrics that are impossible to compute for real LLMs (exact per-solution probabilities, second-moment decompositions, retention of initial mass).

This is significant beyond validating Lemma 3.1 and Proposition 3.2. The simulation enables **discovering the boundaries of the concentration zone** as a function of group size, initial distribution structure, and learning rate — a characterization that would be prohibitively expensive with full LLM training (which requires thousands of GPU-hours and can only sweep a handful of N values). Figure 4(c) labels three regimes (I, II, III) that are not theoretically derived but **empirically discovered** through the simulation sweep. The shaded concentration zone in that plot is not a prediction of Lemma 3.1 — Lemma 3.1 describes where tail-miss probability peaks, but the simulation reveals how that probability translates into actual `M_ret` collapse over many training steps, accounting for the cumulative effect of repeated unsampled-correct drift.

The paper is appropriately cautious about translating simulation boundaries to LLM training (Section 5.1: "The specific boundaries of the concentration zone depend on the initial distribution and should not be interpreted as quantitative predictions for LLM training"). But this caution itself is a form of precision: the simulation establishes the **existence** and **qualitative shape** of the concentration zone, not its exact N boundaries. For LLM training with many prompts at varying difficulty levels, the concentration zone may be broader, narrower, or shifted depending on the distribution of `μ_pos` across the training set and how it evolves during training. The simulation provides the conceptual vocabulary (Regime I/II/III, `M_ret` collapse, anchor vs. leaf actions) that makes the LLM results interpretable: when we see N=8 GRPO achieving higher pass@1 but lower pass@256 than N=2 (Table 2), we can recognize this as entrenchment in Regime II, not as a mysterious degradation.

This methodological contribution — a lightweight, analytically tractable simulation that captures the essential dynamics of a complex training process and enables sweeping parameters that are infeasible at full scale — is a template for how theoretical RLVR analysis can be empirically validated without running dozens of full LLM training runs. It bridges the gap between closed-form probability derivations (which provide insight into single-step behavior) and full-scale experiments (which provide ground truth but limited coverage of the parameter space).

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** All models are trained on **DeepScaleR** (Luo et al., 2025), a dataset of competition-level mathematics problems. The paper filters samples longer than 1024 tokens and removes duplicates with conflicting answers, retaining **39,202 training samples**. For in-domain evaluation, the paper uses **MATH500** (Hendrycks et al., 2021), **AIME24/25** (Art of Problem Solving, 2024a), **AMC23** (Art of Problem Solving, 2024b), **Minerva Math** (Lewkowycz et al., 2022), and **Olympiad Bench** (He et al., 2024). Out-of-domain evaluation uses **GPQA Diamond** (Rein et al., 2023), **IFEval** (Zhou et al., 2023), and **SynLogic** (Liu et al., 2025a).

- **Base model(s).** The paper evaluates on three model families at different scales: **Qwen2.5-7B** (Yang et al., 2024a), **Qwen2.5-1.5B-Math** (Yang et al., 2024b), and **Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct** (Grattafiori et al., 2024). This covers both general-purpose instruction-tuned models (Qwen2.5-7B, Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct) and a math-specialized model (Qwen2.5-1.5B-Math), with parameter counts spanning 1.5B to 7B. The models are chosen to demonstrate that Focal weighting is not specific to a single architecture or scale.

- **Metrics.** The paper reports **pass@1** (single-attempt accuracy) and **pass@256** (the probability that at least one of 256 independent samples is correct), computed using the unbiased estimator from Chen et al. (2021):
  $$\text{pass@k} := \mathbb{E}_{\text{Problems}}\left[1 - \frac{\binom{n-c}{k}}{\binom{n}{k}}\right]$$
  where `n = 256` total samples per problem and `c` is the number of correct samples. Pass@1 measures reliability on a single attempt; pass@256 measures solution diversity—whether the model retains the ability to occasionally produce correct solutions through diverse strategies that may not be its "default" answer. The paper also reports `$\Delta$NLL$_{\text{rare}}$` (Appendix F.2), an empirical proxy for redistribution away from base-model rare-correct solutions, defined as the increase in negative log-likelihood on a fixed set of trajectories that were correct but low-probability under the base model.

- **Baselines.** The primary baselines are **GRPO** (Shao et al., 2024) at group sizes N=2, 8, and 32; **DAPO** (Yu et al., 2025) at N=8 with asymmetric clipping (`$\epsilon_{low}=0.2$`, `$\epsilon_{high}=0.28$`); and **CISPO** (Chen et al., 2025a) at N=8 with clipped importance weights (`$\epsilon^{IS}_{low}=1.0$`, `$\epsilon^{IS}_{high}=5.0$`). All methods use `$R_c = 1$`, `$R_w = 0$`, temperature 1.0 sampling, and no KL regularization (`$\beta = 0$`) following DAPO. For the regularization comparison (Section 5.5), additional baselines include **GRPO with entropy bonus** (coefficient tuned over {0.0001, 0.001}, best at 0.001) and **GRPO with KL penalty** (coefficient tuned over {0.001, 0.01}, best at 0.001).

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** Compute is measured in terms of **group size N** (number of rollouts per prompt), which determines total generation cost per training step since each prompt generates exactly N complete responses. All methods at the same N require identical generation budgets; Focal weighting adds zero additional compute beyond the base algorithm (it only adds a scalar multiplication per prompt). The Focal weight hyperparameter `$\gamma$` is swept over {0.5, 1.0, 2.0} and selected per method-model combination by average in-domain math pass@1 at the best checkpoint (Table 6).

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** Checkpoint selection uses the best checkpoint by average math pass@1 across all in-domain benchmarks, with F-GRPO checkpoints selected using equal or less compute than the baseline. For statistical significance testing (Appendix I), the paper employs a paired m-out-of-n subsampling test (Politis et al., 1999): 50,000 subsampling iterations of m=256 samples drawn from n=1024 total samples per problem, with two-sided significance at `$\alpha = 0.05$`. Statistically significant results are bolded in Table 1.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Group Size Sweep: Validating the Three-Regime Framework

Table 2 (full per-benchmark breakdown in Appendix F, Table 3) compares GRPO at N=2, 8, and 32 with F-GRPO at N=8 on Qwen2.5-7B. The results reveal the predicted non-monotonic pattern:

- **N=2 (small-N regime):** achieves the highest in-domain math pass@256 (75.0) but the lowest pass@1 (36.2). OOD pass@256 is also highest (67.3, second only to F-GRPO's 63.3), while OOD pass@1 is 18.0. The model changes slowly (few active batches), preserving diversity at the cost of learning speed.

- **N=8 (intermediate-N/concentration regime):** pass@1 improves slightly to 37.3, but pass@256 drops sharply to 64.1 on math and 55.9 on OOD—the lowest pass@256 across all four configurations. This is the sharpening signature: the model becomes more consistent on its first attempt (marginally) but loses the ability to occasionally find correct solutions through rare strategies. The `$\Delta$NLL$_{\text{rare}}$` metric confirms this: 0.68 for N=8 vs. 0.19 for N=2, indicating substantial deviation from the base model's distribution on rare-correct trajectories.

- **N=32 (partial recovery toward large-N regime):** pass@1 improves further to 39.2 (best among all configurations), and pass@256 partially recovers to 70.1 on math and 61.7 on OOD. `$\Delta$NLL$_{\text{rare}}$` drops to 0.52 (intermediate between N=2 and N=8), indicating that the larger group size is beginning to cover some rare-correct modes but has not fully escaped the concentration zone.

- **F-GRPO at N=8:** achieves math pass@256 of 70.3 (essentially matching GRPO at N=32's 70.1, and marked as second-best overall) while using 4× fewer rollouts per prompt. Math pass@1 is 38.6 (second-best, between N=8 baseline 37.3 and N=32 baseline 39.2). OOD pass@256 reaches 63.3 (best overall), and OOD pass@1 improves to 19.2 (also best overall). The `$\Delta$NLL$_{\text{rare}}$` is 0.46, below both N=8 (0.68) and N=32 (0.52), indicating that Focal weighting reduces concentration more effectively than simply scaling N at this budget level.

The key takeaway from Table 2 is that F-GRPO at N=8 achieves the diversity preservation of N=32 **without the 4× generation cost**, and actually surpasses N=32 on OOD transfer, while maintaining competitive pass@1. The `$\Delta$NLL$_{\text{rare}}$` ordering (N=2 < F-GRPO N=8 < N=32 < N=8) mirrors the pass@256 ordering, providing converging evidence that the pass@256 improvements are driven by better retention of base-model rare-correct solutions.

#### Focal Weighting Across Methods: Consistent Pass@256 Gains at Zero Extra Cost

Table 1 reports the main results applying Focal weighting to GRPO, DAPO, and CISPO at N=8 across three model families. The headline finding: **Focal weighting improves pass@256 in all 9 method-model combinations** (average +3.5 on math, +3.8 on OOD) while pass@1 remains stable or improves.

**Qwen2.5-7B (Table 1, top block):**

- **F-GRPO** vs. GRPO: math pass@1 improves from 37.3 → 38.6 (+1.3) and pass@256 from 64.1 → 70.3 (+6.2). OOD pass@1 improves from 17.1 → 19.2 (+2.1) and pass@256 from 55.9 → 63.3 (+7.4). The largest single-benchmark gain is AIME25 pass@256: 40.8 → 52.6 (+11.8). IFEval (OOD instruction following) shows pass@256 improving from 70.3 → 75.7 (+5.4) and pass@1 from 32.1 → 34.0 (+1.9).

- **F-DAPO** vs. DAPO: math pass@1 improves from 39.4 → 40.5 (+1.1) and pass@256 from 69.3 → 72.5 (+3.2). OOD pass@1 improves from 15.7 → 17.9 (+2.2) and pass@256 from 58.4 → 63.6 (+5.2).

- **F-CISPO** vs. CISPO: math pass@1 is identical at 39.5, but pass@256 improves from 73.2 → 76.8 (+3.6). OOD pass@1 improves from 14.9 → 18.1 (+3.2) and pass@256 from 59.0 → 65.9 (+6.9). Notably, CISPO already achieves the highest baseline pass@256 (73.2 math, 59.0 OOD), and F-CISPO pushes it further to 76.8 (the highest math pass@256 across all methods and models in Table 1).

**Qwen2.5-1.5B-Math (Table 1, middle block):**

This is a math-specialized model with higher baseline pass@1 (GRPO achieves 36.7 math average pass@1 vs. 37.3 for the 7B general model—remarkably close). Focal weighting shows consistent pass@256 improvements but some minor pass@1 trade-offs:

- **F-GRPO**: math pass@1 decreases slightly (36.7 → 36.3, -0.4) but pass@256 improves (74.4 → 74.5, marginal). OOD pass@1 improves (7.9 → 8.3) and pass@256 improves (43.1 → 46.5).

- **F-DAPO**: math pass@1 nearly unchanged (37.7 → 37.8) while pass@256 improves from 74.3 → 76.0 (+1.7). OOD pass@1 improves (8.7 → 9.1) and pass@256 improves (45.4 → 46.3).

- **F-CISPO**: math pass@1 decreases (38.9 → 37.4, -1.5) but pass@256 improves from 72.9 → 76.1 (+3.2)—the largest pass@256 gain in this model block. This trade-off is consistent with the paper's framework: suppressing updates on high-success prompts may slow pass@1 improvement on the easiest problems while preserving rare-correct modes that boost pass@256.

**Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct (Table 1, bottom block):**

This is the smallest and least capable base model (GRPO math pass@1 only 23.0), providing a test of whether Focal weighting helps when the baseline success rate is low (which should mean less concentration pressure, per the theory). Even here, Focal weighting improves pass@256:

- **F-GRPO**: math pass@1 unchanged at 23.0 but pass@256 improves from 59.9 → 63.4 (+3.5). OOD pass@1 decreases slightly (25.5 → 25.4) but pass@256 improves (56.5 → 57.6).

- **F-DAPO**: math pass@1 improves (24.3 → 24.8) and pass@256 improves sharply from 54.2 → 62.3 (+8.1)—the single largest pass@256 improvement across all 9 combinations. OOD pass@1 improves (23.9 → 24.8) and pass@256 improves (51.3 → 55.4).

- **F-CISPO**: math pass@1 improves (24.1 → 24.5) and pass@256 improves from 58.0 → 59.7 (+1.7). OOD pass@1 decreases (25.7 → 25.0) but pass@256 improves (52.5 → 53.0).

**Cross-method patterns.** Several patterns emerge from Table 1:

1. **CISPO consistently achieves the highest baseline pass@256** (Qwen2.5-7B: 73.2; Qwen2.5-1.5B-Math: 72.9; Llama: 58.0), suggesting that CISPO's approach of clipping importance weights rather than surrogate products is more diversity-preserving than GRPO or DAPO. F-CISPO pushes this advantage further without sacrificing pass@1.

2. **DAPO shows the largest Focal-weighted gains in pass@256 on the weakest model** (Llama: +8.1), but more modest gains on stronger models (+3.2 on Qwen2.5-7B). This is consistent with the theory: DAPO's asymmetric clipping allows low-probability actions to increase more aggressively, which may amplify the concentration mechanism on easy prompts; Focal weighting counteracts this.

3. **OOD pass@1 improvements are surprisingly robust** (improved in 7/9 cases, average +1.1), suggesting that preserving solution diversity during math training transfers to better single-attempt generalization on non-math tasks—an unexpected benefit not directly predicted by the theory.

4. **Per-benchmark results show that pass@256 gains are broad, not driven by outliers.** On Qwen2.5-7B, F-GRPO improves pass@256 on all 7 benchmarks (6 math + IFEval), with gains ranging from +2.5 (Olympiad) to +11.8 (AIME25). The full per-benchmark breakdown is in Appendix F, Table 3.

#### Comparison with Entropy and KL Regularization

Table 4 (Appendix G) compares F-GRPO against GRPO with entropy bonus (GRPO-H, coefficient 0.001) and GRPO with KL penalty (GRPO-KL, coefficient 0.001) on Qwen2.5-7B at N=8. The key findings:

- **Math pass@1:** F-GRPO achieves 38.6, vs. 37.8 for GRPO-H and 37.2 for GRPO-KL. F-GRPO has the highest in-domain single-attempt accuracy.

- **Math pass@256:** GRPO-KL achieves 72.0, outperforming both F-GRPO (70.3) and GRPO-H (69.5). KL regularization is more effective at preserving in-domain diversity than Focal weighting at this N, but at the cost of requiring a reference model in memory during training.

- **OOD pass@256:** F-GRPO achieves 63.3, vs. 60.0 for GRPO-KL and 59.9 for GRPO-H. F-GRPO provides the best out-of-domain diversity transfer.

- **OOD pass@1:** F-GRPO achieves 19.2, vs. 19.4 for GRPO-KL (marginal difference) and 18.7 for GRPO-H.

The paper's interpretation: KL regularization preserves diversity by preventing the model from changing much at all—it's a global constraint that helps pass@256 everywhere but limits adaptation on hard problems (hurting pass@1). Focal weighting is more targeted: it preserves full learning signal on hard prompts (where the model needs to improve) while suppressing updates on easy prompts (where concentration risk is highest). The result is better pass@1 and OOD transfer with competitive pass@256, **without the memory overhead of maintaining a reference model**.

#### Categorical Simulation: Regime Characterization

Figure 4 presents the categorical simulation results from Section 5.1. This is an empirical validation of the three-regime framework in a controlled environment, not a direct test of F-GRPO on language tasks. The key observations:

- **Panel (a): Total correct mass Q_pos.** All group sizes show monotonically increasing Q_pos over 1,000 training steps. Larger N leads to faster convergence to Q_pos ≈ 1.0. No configuration shows Q_pos decreasing—consistent with Hu et al. (2025)'s finding that the unsampled coupling is not strong enough to cause net decreases in total correct mass for the tested group sizes.

- **Panel (b): Retained positive mass M_ret.** This reveals the diversity collapse. N=2 and N=131,072 maintain M_ret ≈ 1 throughout (diversity preserved through inactivity and coverage, respectively). Intermediate N values show catastrophic collapse: at N=256 and N=8192, M_ret drops to near zero by step 200-400, meaning the policy has concentrated onto the anchor correct action while losing probability on the other 9,999 correct actions. N=8 and N=32 show similar but less extreme collapse.

- **Panel (c): Final state at step 1000 with regime labels.** The solid curves ($\gamma=0$) show three regimes: **Regime I** (N=2-8): Q_pos grows slowly (~0.7) but M_ret stays high (~0.6-0.9). **Regime II** (shaded, N=16-8192): Q_pos grows rapidly (reaching ~0.95-1.0) but M_ret collapses (below 0.2 for most of the zone). **Regime III** (N=16384-131072): both metrics are high (Q_pos ≈ 1.0, M_ret ≈ 1.0 for N=131072).

- **Dashed curves ($\gamma=1$, Focal weighting):** In Regime II, Focal weighting substantially improves M_ret while slightly reducing Q_pos. For example, at N=8192, M_ret improves from ~0.1 to ~0.5. At N=131,072 and N=2, the curves nearly overlap—Focal weighting doesn't affect regimes where concentration is already minimal.

The simulation does not produce numbers that map directly to LLM training (the N boundaries are specific to the 128,000-action initialization), but it validates the qualitative framework: (1) total correct mass alone is insufficient to detect diversity collapse, (2) M_ret captures the collapse that Q_pos misses, (3) the coll apse occurs at intermediate N, and (4) Focal weighting meaningfully mitigates it in the concentration zone.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

- **Focal exponent $\gamma$ sensitivity (Table 6):** The paper sweeps $\gamma \in \{0.5, 1.0, 2.0\}$ across all method-model combinations. $\gamma = 2.0$ is never selected as best; the optimal values are evenly split between 0.5 and 1.0 across the 9 setups (Table 6). This suggests moderate down-weighting is optimal—aggressive suppression ($\gamma=2.0$, where a prompt with $\hat{\mu}_{pos} = 0.5$ gets only 25% gradient weight) is counterproductive, likely because it excessively reduces learning signal from partially-solved prompts that still need improvement.

- **Group size comparison on real models (Table 2, Appendix F, Table 3):** The sweep across N=2, 8, 32 on Qwen2.5-7B validates the three-regime prediction. The critical finding is that N=8 (a commonly used setting) produces the worst pass@256 across both in-domain and OOD benchmarks—the exact prediction of the concentration zone. F-GRPO at N=8 recovers pass@256 to N=32 levels while maintaining pass@1, demonstrating that the diversity loss at N=8 is not an inevitability of small groups but a remediable failure mode.

- **Applied to three different group-relative methods (Table 1):** Focal weighting improves pass@256 for GRPO, DAPO, and CISPO across three model families (9/9 combinations). This supports the paper's claim that the concentration mechanism is inherent to group-relative advantage estimation, not to any particular clipping or importance-weighting scheme. The consistent gains across very different algorithms (GRPO's symmetric clipping, DAPO's asymmetric clipping, CISPO's importance-weight clipping with stop-gradient) demonstrate that Focal weighting addresses a shared root cause.

- **Applied across three model families (Table 1):** The gains persist from 1.5B to 7B parameters, from general-purpose to math-specialized models, and from Qwen to Llama architectures. This suggests the phenomenon is not specific to a particular pretraining distribution or model scale. The fact that Focal weighting helps even on Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct (which starts with only 23% math pass@1) indicates that the concentration mechanism operates even at relatively low baseline success rates—it is not limited to models that are already highly competent.

- **Entropy bonus comparison (Table 4, Appendix G):** GRPO-H (entropy bonus 0.001) achieves math pass@256 of 69.5 and OOD pass@256 of 59.9, both below F-GRPO's 70.3 and 63.3. This supports the paper's argument that token-level entropy methods address a different phenomenon than trajectory-level concentration. Entropy regularization encourages per-token uncertainty but does not prevent the model from converging to a narrow set of solution strategies (it can be uncertain about individual tokens while still following the same high-level reasoning pattern).

- **KL regularization comparison (Table 4, Appendix G):** GRPO-KL (coefficient 0.001) achieves higher math pass@256 (72.0 vs. 70.3) but lower pass@1 (37.2 vs. 38.6) and lower OOD pass@256 (60.0 vs. 63.3) than F-GRPO. This demonstrates the trade-off: global KL constraints preserve diversity more aggressively (benefiting in-domain pass@256) but restrict the model's ability to adapt on hard problems (hurting pass@1) and transfer to OOD tasks. The paper notes that KL regularization also requires maintaining a reference model in memory.

- **Deviation from base-model rare solutions ($\Delta$NLL$_{\text{rare}}$, Table 2):** This metric provides converging evidence for the mechanism. The ordering $\Delta$NLL$_{\text{rare}}$ (N=2: 0.19) < (F-GRPO N=8: 0.46) < (N=32: 0.52) < (N=8: 0.68) mirrors the pass@256 ordering. F-GRPO reduces deviation from base-model rare-correct trajectories compared to its N=8 baseline, and achieves lower deviation than N=32—consistent with the interpretation that Focal weighting preserves rare-correct modes by suppressing updates when concentration pressure is highest.

- **Training stability (no explicit ablation, but implicit in results):** The fact that F-GRPO never produces catastrophic failures (no configuration shows worse pass@256 than its baseline in Table 1) and that pass@1 is stable or improved in 7/9 cases indicates that the Focal weight does not introduce training instability. The gradient scaling is bounded between 0 and 1 and applies uniformly to all rollouts from a prompt, so it cannot flip gradient signs or create adversarial incentive structures.

### Critical Assessment

**Do the experiments support the claim that the tail-miss probability explains sharpening?**

The paper's central theoretical claim is that intermediate N maximizes Pr(B_τ)—the probability of active updates that miss rare-correct modes—and that this explains why N=8 and N=32 cause sharpening while N=2 and very large N do not. The experimental evidence for this claim is primarily **indirect**:

- **Table 2 shows the predicted non-monotonic pass@256 pattern** (N=2: 75.0 → N=8: 64.1 → N=32: 70.1), which is consistent with the tail-miss framework. However, pass@256 is an aggregate metric over thousands of prompts with varying μ_pos and τ, not a direct measurement of Pr(B_τ) for any specific rare-correct region. The experiment demonstrates that the *consequence* of the tail-miss mechanism (diversity loss) follows the predicted pattern, but does not directly verify that the mechanism operates through the specific probabilities derived in Lemma 3.1.

- **The categorical simulation (Figure 4) provides more direct evidence**, showing that M_ret collapse occurs precisely at intermediate N and that N=131,072 (where Pr(B_τ) < 10^{-3}) maintains diversity. This validates the mechanism in the simplified categorical setting. However, the simulation uses a single fixed "prompt" with a static set of correct actions, whereas real LLM training involves thousands of prompts with continuously evolving success probabilities. The paper appropriately cautions against interpreting simulation N boundaries quantitatively.

- **ΔNLL_rare (Table 2) partially bridges this gap** by showing that F-GRPO reduces deviation from base-model rare-correct trajectories. But this metric is a post-hoc correlate, not a causal demonstration that tail-miss events during training were responsible for the original deviation. A stronger experiment would track the probability of specific rare-correct solutions being sampled in training batches over the course of training and correlate this with their eventual probability under the trained policy—but this is infeasible for autoregressive LLMs where "specific rare-correct solutions" cannot be enumerated.

**Limitation:** The paper does not experimentally manipulate Pr(B_τ) by, for example, using different sampling strategies that explicitly control whether rare-correct modes are included in training batches. Such an experiment would provide causal evidence for the mechanism. The evidence presented is correlational: the predicted pattern appears in both simulation and LLM training, but alternative explanations (e.g., overfitting to common reward patterns, optimization dynamics unrelated to sampling) are not ruled out.

**Does F-GRPO genuinely preserve diversity, or does it just slow down concentration?**

The pass@256 improvements in Table 1 are clear and consistent, but there is an important ambiguity: does F-GRPO **prevent** the sharpening mechanism (per the theory), or does it simply **delay** it by reducing the effective learning rate on easy prompts? If training were continued for more epochs, would F-GRPO eventually converge to the same sharpened distribution as standard GRPO, just more slowly?

The paper does not address this question directly. Training is capped at 10 epochs for all methods, and checkpoints are compared at their best epoch. There is no analysis of how the gap between F-GRPO and baseline pass@256 evolves over training—does it widen, narrow, or stabilize? If F-GRPO truly addresses the root cause (tail-miss events), the diversity gap should persist or widen with more training. If it only delays concentration, the gap should narrow.

The categorical simulation (Figure 4b) provides some evidence: with γ=1, M_ret remains above 0 at step 1000 for N values where γ=0 collapses to near-zero, but the gap narrows after the initial collapse phase. At N=256, M_ret(γ=1) at step 1000 is approximately 0.4 vs. M_ret(γ=0) ≈ 0.05—so Focal weighting substantially preserves diversity but does not fully prevent concentration, even in the simplified categorical setting.

**Limitation:** No long-training ablation comparing F-GRPO and GRPO at 20, 30, or 50 epochs. The paper cannot distinguish between "Focal weighting prevents the concentration mechanism" and "Focal weighting slows the concentration mechanism enough that it hasn't fully manifested by epoch 10."

**Does Focal weighting work primarily through the claimed mechanism (suppressing R̄ > 0 batches) or through a simpler effect (curriculum learning by focusing on hard prompts)?**

The paper's theory says Focal weighting should help because it reduces updates when R̄ > 0, which is when Proposition 3.2's direct drift is most harmful. But Focal weighting also has a simpler interpretation: it implements a form of automatic curriculum learning, where the model learns more from prompts it currently fails and less from prompts it already masters. This curriculum effect alone could improve pass@256 by preventing the model from overfitting to easy-prompt solutions, without anything to do with rare-correct mode suppression.

The experiments cannot cleanly separate these two interpretations because the theory and the curriculum explanation make similar predictions: both predict that down-weighting high-μ̂_pos prompts should help pass@256. The only evidence favoring the tail-miss mechanism over generic curriculum learning is:

1. **The ΔNLL_rare metric (Table 2)** shows that F-GRPO specifically reduces deviation from base-model rare-correct trajectories—not just any trajectories, but those that were correct and low-probability. A pure curriculum effect would reduce overfitting to easy prompts generally, but it's not obvious that it would specifically preserve base-model rare-correct modes over other types of solutions.

2. **The categorical simulation (Figure 4b)** isolates the mechanism: there is only one "prompt" (the entire action space), so there is no curriculum across prompts. The fact that Focal weighting preserves M_ret even in this single-prompt setting supports the tail-miss mechanism. However, the simulation also inherently has the property that high μ̂_pos means the model is "doing well" and may not need further updates, so the curriculum interpretation still partially applies.

**Limitation:** The paper does not run an ablation comparing F-GRPO against an alternative difficulty-aware scheme that is not grounded in Proposition 3.2—for example, a hard threshold that zeroes out updates when μ̂_pos exceeds a cutoff, or inverse weighting by μ̂_pos instead of (1-μ̂_pos)^γ. Such comparisons would test whether the specific functional form matters or whether any difficulty-based reweighting would produce similar results.

**Does the 4× compute reduction claim (N=8 F-GRPO matches N=32 GRPO) hold robustly?**

Table 2 shows F-GRPO at N=8 matching GRPO at N=32 on math pass@256 (70.3 vs. 70.1) and exceeding it on OOD pass@256 (63.3 vs. 61.7). This supports the 4× compute reduction claim for the specific Qwen2.5-7B setup. However:

- **The claim is based on a single model (Qwen2.5-7B) and a single comparison (N=8 F-GRPO vs. N=32 GRPO).** The main Table 1 only compares at N=8, so we don't know whether F-GRPO at N=8 would match DAPO or CISPO at N=32 on the other model families. The 4× claim is specifically about GRPO on Qwen2.5-7B.

- **Pass@1 trades off slightly**: F-GRPO at N=8 achieves math pass@1 of 38.6 vs. 39.2 for GRPO at N=32. The paper presents this as a modest trade-off, but it means the 4× compute savings come with a small accuracy cost on single-attempt performance—the comparison is not strictly Pareto-superior.

- **The 4× comparison is between N=8 and N=32, not between the actual training costs of the two methods.** Since F-GRPO uses the same per-step compute as GRPO at N=8, the claim is that you can achieve N=32-level pass@256 at N=8-level cost by switching to F-GRPO. This holds for the reported numbers but has not been tested at other N values (e.g., does F-GRPO at N=4 match GRPO at N=16?).

**Missing ablations and experiments that would strengthen the paper:**

- **No comparison against simply training longer at N=8 with standard GRPO:** Could the pass@256 improvement from F-GRPO be achieved by training standard GRPO at N=8 for 20 epochs instead of 10? If so, the benefit of Focal weighting would be primarily about training efficiency (reaching good pass@256 faster) rather than about overcoming a fundamental sampling limitation.

- **No pass@k curves for intermediate k values:** The paper reports pass@1 and pass@256, which capture the extremes of the diversity spectrum. Pass@4, pass@16, and pass@64 curves would reveal where on the k-axis the diversity benefit manifests—does F-GRPO help only at very large k, or does it provide meaningful diversity gains at moderate sampling budgets?

- **No direct measurement of how many distinct correct solutions the model produces per prompt.** Pass@256 is an indirect measure of diversity (the model might produce the same 3 correct solutions repeatedly and still achieve high pass@256 if those 3 are reliably sampled). Analyzing the number of unique correct answers or the entropy of the correct-answer distribution would more directly quantify diversity preservation.

- **No ablation on N=4 or N=16 for F-GRPO:** The theoretical analysis predicts that Pr(B_τ) peaks at intermediate N and that Focal weighting should help most in this regime. Testing F-GRPO at N=4 (smaller, closer to Region I) and N=16 (larger, still in Region II) would test whether the benefit is specific to N=8 or generalizes across the intermediate regime.

- **No comparison against a "naive" baseline that simply discards prompts with μ̂_pos exceeding a threshold during training.** This would test whether the continuous Focal weighting is necessary or whether a hard filtering rule would achieve similar results.

- **Statistical significance is only reported as binary (bold in Table 1)**, without confidence intervals or effect sizes. The m-out-of-n subsampling test with 50,000 iterations provides p-values, but the magnitude of the improvement (and its variance across subsamples) is not reported. For some configurations (e.g., F-CISPO vs. CISPO on Qwen2.5-7B, where pass@1 is identical at 39.5), the practical significance of the pass@256 gain (+3.6) is unclear without knowing the variance of the pass@256 estimator.

**Strengths that should be acknowledged:**

- **The three-model, three-method design (9 configurations) is unusually thorough** for an algorithmic modification paper. Showing consistent gains across model families (Qwen, Llama), scales (1.5B-7B), and base algorithms (GRPO, DAPO, CISPO) provides strong evidence of generality.

- **The inclusion of OOD benchmarks (IFEval, GPQA, SynLogic) is a genuine strength.** Many RLVR papers evaluate only on in-domain math benchmarks, which can mask sharpening (the model concentrates onto math-specific reasoning patterns that still work for math). The fact that F-GRPO improves OOD pass@256 and often OOD pass@1 suggests the diversity preservation transfers beyond the training distribution—a non-trivial finding.

- **The ΔNLL_rare metric (Appendix F.2) provides a mechanistic proxy** that directly supports the paper's claims about redistribution away from rare-correct solutions. While not a causal test, it is a more specific diagnostic than pass@256 alone and distinguishes this work from papers that only report aggregate metrics.

- **The categorical simulation (Section 5.1) is comprehensive** (N swept across 17 values from 2 to 131,072, 4 seeds per configuration) and includes the novel M_ret metric that captures exactly the phenomenon the theory predicts. The simulation is not a replacement for LLM experiments, but it validates the theoretical framework in a setting where direct measurement of per-solution probabilities is possible.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Difficulty Estimation Problem Is Punted, Not Solved

The central practical question raised by this paper is: given a prompt, how do you know when to down-weight its updates? The Focal weight `g(x) = (1 - μ̂_pos)^γ` uses the empirical success rate from the current batch's rollouts — information that is already available at zero additional cost during training. The paper correctly notes this as a strength: "No additional networks are required; γ is the only new hyperparameter." But this formulation assumes that **the current batch's success rate is a reliable proxy for the concentration pressure on rare-correct solutions**, which depends on `μ_pos` and `τ` (the true success probability and rare-correct mass under the policy) — quantities that the empirical rate `μ̂_pos` estimates with noise that scales as `1/√N`.

At N=8, a prompt with true `μ_pos = 0.4` will produce `μ̂_pos = 0` (all incorrect) approximately `(1 - 0.4)^8 ≈ 1.7%` of the time, and `μ̂_pos = 1` (all correct) approximately `0.4^8 ≈ 0.07%` of the time — in both cases, the Focal weight would be 1 or 0 respectively, but the true regime is intermediate. This mis-estimation could cause F-GRPO to either under-suppress updates on prompts that are actually in the concentration zone (when `μ̂_pos` underestimates `μ_pos`) or over-suppress updates on prompts that still need learning signal (when `μ̂_pos` overestimates `μ_pos`). The paper does not analyze the sensitivity of F-GRPO's performance to this estimation error, nor does it compare against using a running average of `μ̂_pos` across multiple batches (which would reduce variance at the cost of tracking a moving statistic). The fact that the same N=8 rollouts are used for both advantage estimation and Focal weight computation means that the per-batch noise in `μ̂_pos` directly translates to noise in the gradient scaling — an interaction that could matter at small N.

The paper partially mitigates this concern through the `γ` sweep (Table 6), which empirically selects the degree of down-weighting. Moderate `γ` values (0.5 or 1.0) are universally preferred over `γ = 2.0`, suggesting that aggressive down-weighting based on noisy `μ̂_pos` is counterproductive — consistent with the interpretation that estimation error matters. However, the paper does not ablate whether a smoothed or delayed version of `μ̂_pos` (e.g., exponential moving average across batches) would enable more aggressive `γ` or produce better results at the same `γ`. This is not a fatal limitation — the method works as-is — but it represents an unresolved design choice that a practitioner would need to tune in a new setting.

**Consequence:** A practitioner deploying F-GRPO on a new problem distribution or model family cannot assume that `γ = 0.5` or `1.0` is universally optimal, and the interaction between estimation noise (which depends on N) and the optimal `γ` is unexplored. In the worst case, noisy `μ̂_pos` could cause F-GRPO to inconsistently suppress and unsuppress the same prompt across batches, introducing gradient variance that slows convergence.

**Evidence:** The `γ` sweep in Table 6 shows that `γ = 2.0` is never selected (suggesting that aggressive weighting hurts), but no ablation on `μ̂_pos` estimation quality (e.g., using a moving average, or oracle `μ_pos`) is performed.

**Mitigation status:** Not addressed. The paper acknowledges through its `γ` sweep that the exact weighting strength matters, but does not investigate whether the noise properties of `μ̂_pos` at different N constrain the viable `γ` range.

### The Theory and Experiments Never Combine Search with Revisions — Only One Axis of Test-Time Compute Is Studied

The paper analyzes and mitigates distribution sharpening *during RLVR training* — that is, it addresses the question of whether the post-trained model retains diverse solution strategies. But a natural next question for any practitioner is: **after training with F-GRPO, can the improved diversity at pass@256 be exploited through better inference-time strategies?** The paper evaluates pass@256, which measures whether at least one of 256 independent samples is correct, but this is a passive metric — it says the model *can* produce correct answers through diverse strategies, but does not test whether those strategies can be actively selected or composed. More importantly, the paper never studies whether F-GRPO-trained models benefit *more* or *less* from inference-time techniques like majority voting, verifier-based best-of-N selection, or tree search compared to standard GRPO-trained models.

This matters because the entire motivation for preserving diversity is to enable better downstream inference. If F-GRPO preserves rare-correct modes that are still too low-probability to be reliably selected by standard inference strategies (even at k=256), the practical benefit is limited. Conversely, if F-GRPO preserves modes that are *more amenable* to verifier-guided search (because they are structurally distinct from common modes and thus identifiable by a reward model), the practical benefit could be larger than pass@256 alone suggests. The paper provides no evidence either way.

Additionally, the paper does not study whether F-GRPO's diversity preservation interacts with **self-improvement loops** (where the model is iteratively fine-tuned on its own RLVR-generated solutions). If F-GRPO preserves rare-correct modes, these modes might be sampled more frequently in subsequent training rounds, creating a positive feedback loop that expands the model's effective reasoning boundary. If standard GRPO's sharpening is self-reinforcing (common solutions get more probability, become even more likely to be sampled, get even more reinforcement), F-GRPO might break this cycle. But the paper's training runs stop at 10 epochs with no iterative fine-tuning analysis.

**Consequence:** A practitioner choosing between F-GRPO and standard GRPO cannot know whether the pass@256 improvement translates to better performance under their specific inference pipeline (majority voting, verifier-based selection, search, ensembling). The reported metric (pass@256 with uniform random sampling) may overstate or understate the practical benefit depending on the inference strategy used downstream.

**Evidence:** The paper only reports pass@1 and pass@256 computed via the standard unbiased estimator (Eq. 25) — both are based on uniform i.i.d. sampling with no selection mechanism. No experiments combine F-GRPO training with verifier-guided selection, beam search, or other inference-time optimization.

**Mitigation status:** Not addressed. The paper acknowledges this implicitly by evaluating both pass@1 (single-attempt accuracy, relevant for greedy deployment) and pass@256 (diversity metric, relevant for sampling-based deployment), but does not bridge these two metrics with any active inference strategy.

### Hard Problems Remain Largely Unaffected — And the Framework Offers No Guidance for Them

The concentration mechanism analyzed in Section 3 is most severe on prompts where `μ_pos` is moderate-to-high — exactly the prompts where the Focal weight `g(x) = (1 - μ̂_pos)^γ` substantially reduces the gradient contribution. On prompts where `μ_pos` is very low (the model rarely succeeds), `μ̂_pos` is typically 0 or close to 0, so `g(x) ≈ 1` — F-GRPO does not modify the learning signal. This is by design: hard prompts are not in the `R̄ > 0` regime that drives concentration (Proposition 3.2), so they don't need suppression.

But this reveals a deeper limitation: **F-GRPO does nothing to improve learning on the hardest problems**, where the model's pass@1 is near zero and test-time diversity is irrelevant because there are no correct solutions to preserve. The paper's own data (Table 2) shows that even after F-GRPO training, the model's pass@1 on AIME24 is 15.9 (for Qwen2.5-7B) — meaning that on 84% of AIME24 problems, the model cannot produce a correct answer in a single attempt. Pass@256 improves from 37.7 (GRPO N=8) to 46.2 (F-GRPO N=8), which is a meaningful relative gain (+22.5%) but still means that on more than half of AIME24 problems, even 256 attempts fail to find a correct solution. The model's *capability ceiling* — the set of problems it can solve at any sampling budget — is largely unchanged; F-GRPO only helps the model retain solutions it already had some non-negligible probability of generating.

This is consistent with the paper's theoretical framework: if the base model has `τ(x) ≈ 0` for some problem (truly zero probability of generating a correct solution through any strategy), no amount of diversity preservation will create that capability. F-GRPO prevents the model from *losing* capabilities it already has; it does not help the model *acquire* new ones. The paper is transparent about this — the categorical simulation (Section 5.1) starts with `Q_pos ≈ 0.63` and the model never exceeds ~1.0, so no new correct actions are ever "discovered" — but the implication for deployment is not fully spelled out: F-GRPO is most useful on problem distributions that are already partially solvable by the base model, and provides diminishing returns (or no benefit) on genuinely hard out-of-distribution reasoning tasks.

This limitation is partially inherent to the RLVR paradigm (which sharpens existing distributions rather than expanding capabilities, as argued by Yue et al., 2025 and others), and F-GRPO does not claim to solve this broader issue. But a practitioner should understand that F-GRPO is a **conservation** mechanism, not an **expansion** mechanism — it preserves solution diversity that already exists, rather than enabling the model to solve problems it previously could not.

**Consequence:** On benchmarks with many problems outside the base model's capability range (e.g., AIME, frontier math, novel reasoning tasks), F-GRPO's benefits are concentrated in the subset of problems the base model can already occasionally solve. For the hardest tier of problems, F-GRPO offers no advantage over standard GRPO — and if Focal weighting reduces gradient signal on prompts that are partially solvable (moderate `μ_pos`) in a way that slows learning on those prompts' *incorrect* rollouts, it could theoretically slow progress on hard problems relative to standard GRPO with more epochs.

**Evidence:** Table 2 shows F-GRPO at N=8 achieves AIME24 pass@1 of 15.9 vs. GRPO at N=8 (15.0) and N=32 (13.0). The improvement is modest in absolute terms — the vast majority of AIME24 problems remain unsolvable on a single attempt. The paper does not break out results by problem difficulty within benchmarks to show whether F-GRPO's benefits are concentrated on easier problems.

**Mitigation status:** Not addressed. The paper acknowledges in Section 1 that existing work debates whether RLVR expands or concentrates capabilities, and positions F-GRPO as a tool for mitigating concentration — not for expanding the capability frontier. But it does not empirically characterize the difficulty-dependent benefit of F-GRPO (e.g., by reporting pass@k stratified by base-model success rate on each problem, analogous to the difficulty-bin analysis in the reference paper on test-time compute scaling).

### The N=32 Comparison Ignores Alternative Ways to Spend That Compute Budget

One of the paper's headline claims is that "F-GRPO at N=8 matches or exceeds GRPO at N=32 on pass@256 (...) using 4× fewer rollouts" (Section 1, Figure 1). Table 2 supports this: math pass@256 is 70.3 (F-GRPO N=8) vs. 70.1 (GRPO N=32). But the 4× compute savings claim treats the comparison as **F-GRPO at N=8 vs. GRPO at N=32 with all else equal**, which is a reasonable scientific comparison but not the only way a practitioner could spend the N=32 compute budget.

A practitioner considering increasing from N=8 to N=32 has several options: (1) use N=32 with standard GRPO (the baseline in Table 2), (2) use N=32 with F-GRPO (not evaluated in the paper), (3) stay at N=8 but train for 4× more steps (since each step is 4× cheaper), or (4) stay at N=8 but increase the batch size by 4× (processing 4× more prompts per step). The paper only evaluates option (1) against F-GRPO at N=8, and implicitly compares against option (3) by training all methods for the same number of epochs (10). But option (3) is a critical missing baseline: if training GRPO at N=8 for 40 epochs (4× more steps) also recovers pass@256 to N=32 levels, then Focal weighting is primarily a training efficiency improvement (reaching good pass@256 in fewer steps) rather than a fundamental fix to the concentration mechanism. The paper cannot distinguish these interpretations because it caps training at 10 epochs for all methods.

Options (2) and (4) are also unexplored. F-GRPO at N=32 — combining Focal weighting with larger group sizes — might achieve even better pass@256 than either F-GRPO N=8 or GRPO N=32, since the larger group size improves advantage estimation quality while Focal weighting prevents concentration on the prompts that are already well-solved. Conversely, if N=32 is already in the large-N regime for the training distribution, Focal weighting might provide negligible additional benefit (analogous to the categorical simulation where `γ=1` and `γ=0` curves overlap for N=131,072 in Figure 4c). The paper provides no data to resolve this.

**Consequence:** The 4× compute reduction claim, while technically supported for the specific comparison shown, overstates the practical advantage if alternative strategies for spending the N=32 compute budget could achieve similar or better results. A practitioner with a fixed total FLOPs budget might be better served by F-GRPO at N=16 with 2× the training steps (which the paper does not evaluate) than by either F-GRPO N=8 or GRPO N=32.

**Evidence:** The paper trains all methods for exactly 10 epochs (Appendix H, Table 5) with no sweep over training duration. The N=2, N=8, N=32 comparison in Table 2 controls for epochs but not for total FLOPs (N=32 uses 4× the total generation compute of N=8, and 16× that of N=2, over the same 10 epochs).

**Mitigation status:** Partially addressed. The paper acknowledges this implicitly by comparing at fixed N=8 in the main Table 1 and treating the N=32 comparison (Table 2) as a validation of the three-regime framework rather than as the primary practical claim. But the Figure 1 caption and Section 1 abstract explicitly state the 4× compute reduction, and the missing baselines weaken this claim.

### Single Training Dataset, No Non-Math Reasoning Domains

All experiments train on DeepScaleR (a competition-level math dataset with 39,202 problems after filtering) and evaluate on math benchmarks (MATH500, AIME, AMC, Minerva, Olympiad) plus three OOD benchmarks (GPQA, IFEval, SynLogic). The OOD benchmarks are a strength — they show that diversity preservation during math training transfers to instruction following and logical reasoning — but they are all evaluated **zero-shot**, testing whether the math-trained model incidentally generalizes. The paper does not train F-GRPO on any non-math reasoning task (code generation, multi-step planning, formal theorem proving, etc.) where RLVR with verifiable rewards is also commonly used.

This matters because the tail-miss probability `Pr(B_τ)` depends on `μ_pos` and `τ`, which in turn depend on the base model's capabilities and the structure of the solution space. In math, correct solutions are often structurally similar (the same algebraic manipulation expressed with slightly different notation), so rare-correct modes may have very small `τ` relative to `μ_pos` — the regime where `Pr(B_τ)` peaks at moderate N and Focal weighting is most beneficial. In code generation, correct solutions can be algorithmically diverse (different data structures, different control flow), potentially leading to a more uniform distribution over correct solutions (larger `ρ = τ/μ_pos`). In that regime, the concentration zone might be narrower or shifted to different N values, and Focal weighting might provide less benefit (since rare-correct modes are less "rare" relative to the total correct mass). The paper provides no evidence about whether the three-regime framework and F-GRPO's benefits generalize across domains with different solution-space structures.

Additionally, the DeepScaleR dataset is filtered as described in Appendix H.1: samples longer than 1024 tokens are removed, and duplicates with conflicting answers are filtered out, retaining ~39K samples. This filtering removes exactly the kinds of ambiguous or contradictory training examples that might require the most careful handling of rare-correct modes. The paper does not analyze whether F-GRPO is more or less beneficial with unfiltered training data (which is more representative of real-world RLVR pipelines where the verifier may produce noisy or inconsistent labels).

**Consequence:** A practitioner applying F-GRPO to code generation, planning, or theorem proving cannot rely on the paper's `γ` recommendations (0.5-1.0) or the N=8 operating point being optimal. The relative benefit of Focal weighting over standard GRPO may be larger or smaller depending on the solution-space entropy of the target domain, and the paper provides no guidance for predicting this.

**Evidence:** All training runs use DeepScaleR (Section 5.2, Appendix H.1). The three OOD benchmarks are evaluated zero-shot from the math-trained checkpoints, not from models trained with RLVR on those domains. The paper does not discuss how solution-space structure (e.g., entropy of the correct-answer distribution) interacts with the tail-miss probability.

**Mitigation status:** Not addressed. The paper does not claim to have tested on non-math domains, but also does not discuss the domain-specificity of its findings as a limitation. The three model families (Section 5.2) provide some evidence of architectural robustness, but they are all evaluated on the same math training distribution.

### The Unsolved Problem: M_ret Collapse Is Moderated, Not Prevented

The categorical simulation (Figure 4b) provides the paper's most direct evidence for the mechanism by which Focal weighting preserves diversity. But the simulation also reveals a sobering fact: **even with Focal weighting (`γ = 1`), retained positive mass `M_ret` eventually falls substantially at intermediate N.** At N=256 with `γ = 1`, `M_ret` at step 1000 is approximately 0.4 — better than `γ = 0` (M_ret ≈ 0.05), but still meaning that 60% of the initial correct probability mass has been redistributed away from its original locations. At N=8192, the gap is even smaller (M_ret ≈ 0.3 vs. ≈ 0.05 for `γ = 0`). Focal weighting slows the collapse and prevents it from reaching catastrophic levels, but it does not fundamentally alter the asymptotic trajectory — given enough training steps in the concentration zone, diversity still degrades substantially.

This matters because the paper's LLM experiments (Table 2) are evaluated at a single checkpoint (the best by math pass@1, typically at epoch 10). We don't know whether the pass@256 advantage of F-GRPO over standard GRPO would **persist, widen, narrow, or reverse** if training continued for 20 or 30 epochs. If F-GRPO's diversity preservation is primarily a kinetic effect (it takes longer to reach the same collapsed state), then extended training would eliminate the advantage. If it's a steady-state effect (Focal weighting maintains diversity indefinitely by continuously reallocating gradient away from high-`μ_pos` prompts), the advantage should persist. The simulation suggests a partial kinetic effect (M_ret still declines with `γ = 1`, just more slowly), but the simulation uses a single static prompt distribution, whereas real LLM training involves an evolving distribution where `μ_pos` increases over time — which could either amplify or counteract the kinetic effect.

The paper also does not explore combined strategies that might more fully prevent `M_ret` collapse. For example: Focal weighting + larger N on hard prompts only, or Focal weighting + explicit replay of rare-correct solutions from earlier checkpoints, or Focal weighting + a small entropy bonus. F-GRPO is presented as a standalone fix, but the simulation suggests it is better understood as one component of a broader diversity-preservation toolkit whose individual contribution is significant but incomplete.

**Consequence:** The paper cannot guarantee that F-GRPO-trained models are robust to extended training or to fine-tuning on additional RLVR data. In iterative self-improvement settings where the model is repeatedly trained on its own outputs, the cumulative concentration pressure over multiple rounds might eventually overcome Focal weighting's moderating effect. A practitioner planning multi-round RLVR fine-tuning should not assume that F-GRPO in each round will prevent long-term diversity collapse.

**Evidence:** Figure 4b shows declining `M_ret` over 1,000 steps even with `γ = 1` for intermediate N. Table 2 evaluates at the best epoch (≤10), not at fixed training duration or at convergence. No extended-training or multi-round experiments are conducted.

**Mitigation status:** Not addressed. The paper does not discuss the distinction between delaying and preventing concentration, nor does it propose how F-GRPO should be configured for extended training regimes. Section 7 (Conclusion) frames Focal weighting as "a practical, drop-in solution for maintaining solution diversity" without qualifying the asymptotic behavior.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper doesn't propose a new RL algorithm or a new model architecture — it identifies a **sampling failure mode** that has been invisibly degrading RLVR training at the group sizes everyone actually uses, and provides a one-line fix that requires no additional compute. The magnitude of the shift is diagnostic rather than paradigmatic: the paper changes **how the field should think about group size in group-relative RLVR**, not what algorithm the field should use.

The central reframing is deceptively simple. Prior to this work, the narrative around group size was "small N is efficient, large N is stable, intermediate N is a compromise." Wu et al. (2025b) argued N=2 suffices; Hu et al. (2025) argued N should be scaled as large as possible; practitioners used N=8 or N=32 because that's what fit in GPU memory. These positions appeared contradictory because they assumed a monotonic relationship between N and training quality. Lemma 3.1 shows the relationship is **non-monotonic**: small N preserves diversity through inactivity (most batches are homogeneous, the model barely updates), large N preserves diversity through coverage (rare solutions are reliably sampled), and intermediate N — exactly N=8 and N=32, the most common production settings — **maximizes the probability of active updates that systematically miss rare-correct solutions.**

This resolves the contradiction without declaring anyone wrong. Wu et al. were right about N=2 preserving diversity, but for the wrong reason (it's not that N=2 is "sufficient" — it's that at N=2, the model learns so slowly that diversity-by-stagnation is the primary mechanism). Hu et al. were right that scaling N stabilizes learning, but their guarantee (ΔQ_pos ≥ 0 at large N) applies at group sizes (N ~ 10^5) that are computationally infeasible for LLM training. He et al. (2025) correctly observed sharpening at N=32 but attributed it to rank bias in the advantage computation rather than to finite sampling dynamics. The paper's contribution is to provide the unified phase diagram — parameterized by `μ_pos`, `τ`, and N — that locates everyone's findings as observations of the same non-monotonic curve from different vantage points.

The practical implication is a reorientation of how RLVR practitioners should spend their compute budget. Before this work, scaling group size was the primary lever for combating sharpening: if your model was losing diversity at N=8, the prescribed remedy was to increase to N=32 or N=64, accepting the linear increase in generation cost. The paper shows this is working against the underlying physics: N=8 to N=32 is moving *within* the concentration zone for many parameter regimes, so the remedy helps (pass@256 partially recovers from 64.1 to 70.1 in Table 2) but at a 4× cost that could have been spent on more training steps, more prompts, or other interventions. F-GRPO at N=8 achieves the same pass@256 as GRPO at N=32 without the extra rollout cost, fundamentally changing the cost-benefit calculus of diversity preservation.

More subtly, Proposition 3.2 shifts attention from a metric the field was already tracking (total correct mass, pass@1) to a metric it was not (unsampled-correct mass retention, M_ret). The demonstration that total correct mass can increase while unsampled-correct mass collapses — that a model can simultaneously become more accurate and less diverse, with the former metric entirely masking the latter — is a cautionary tale about evaluation. Researchers who only report pass@1 (or even pass@k for modest k) after RLVR training may be celebrating accuracy improvements while unknowingly presiding over a catastrophic loss of solution diversity. The paper doesn't just identify the problem; it provides a concrete diagnostic (`ΔNLL_rare`, Table 2) and a simulation sandbox (Figure 4) for measuring it. This makes diversity preservation a **measurable objective** in RLVR research, rather than an anecdotal concern.

The paper also changes which research directions look promising versus unpromising. Work on more sophisticated clipping schemes (DAPO's asymmetric bounds, CISPO's importance-weight clipping) is revealed as orthogonal to the core sampling problem: all three methods show similar sharpening patterns at N=8 (Table 1), and Focal weighting helps all three. This suggests that **advantage estimation, not advantage utilization, is the bottleneck** — future algorithmic work on RLVR should focus on how advantages are computed from finite groups, not on how they are subsequently clipped or weighted. Token-level entropy methods (Cui et al., 2025; Cheng et al., 2025) are similarly revealed as addressing a different problem: they regulate local token-level confidence, but the concentration mechanism operates at the trajectory level through repeated tail-miss events across many batches. The paper's entropy comparison (Table 4) shows only modest pass@256 improvement (69.5 vs. F-GRPO's 70.3), consistent with this diagnosis.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**1. Direct causal validation of the tail-miss mechanism through controlled group composition.** Lemma 3.1 makes a precise, falsifiable prediction: if you construct training batches where rare-correct solutions are guaranteed to be included (e.g., by stratified sampling that explicitly includes at least one rollout from a designated rare-correct region), the concentration effect should be suppressed even at intermediate N, and the resulting model should retain higher pass@256 than standard i.i.d. sampling at the same N. This experiment is feasible: on a benchmark with known solution strategies (e.g., MATH problems where distinct proof approaches can be labeled), train two GRPO models at N=8 — one with standard i.i.d. sampling, one where each batch is constructed to include at least one rollout from each of K predefined solution categories. If the tail-miss mechanism is causal, the stratified-sampling model should show higher pass@256 and lower `ΔNLL_rare` at equal training steps. A null result (no difference) would suggest that the sharpening observed in Section 5 is driven by optimization dynamics (overfitting to common reward patterns) rather than by finite sampling, which would necessitate revisiting the theoretical framework. This experiment directly tests the paper's central mechanistic claim, which is currently supported only by correlational evidence (the non-monotonic pass@256 pattern and the categorical simulation).

**2. F-GRPO with dynamic γ scheduling as a function of training progress.** The paper sweeps γ ∈ {0.5, 1.0, 2.0} and selects a fixed value per run, but the concentration pressure evolves during training: early on, `μ_pos` is low for most prompts (so Focal weighting has little effect regardless of γ), while late in training, `μ_pos` is high for many prompts and the concentration mechanism is maximally active. A natural extension is to **anneal γ upward during training** — start with γ = 0 (standard GRPO) to allow rapid initial learning, then increase γ to 0.5, 1.0, or higher as the average `μ_pos` across the training set rises. This would preserve aggressive learning on hard prompts early in training while progressively strengthening diversity protection as the model becomes competent on more of the training distribution. The paper's results hint that this could be beneficial: Qwen2.5-7B with F-GRPO at γ = 0.5 achieves pass@1 of 38.6 (vs. 39.5 for F-CISPO at γ = 1.0), suggesting room for more aggressive weighting later in training without sacrificing early learning. A concrete experiment: train F-GRPO on DeepScaleR with γ = 0 for epochs 1-3, γ = 0.5 for epochs 4-6, γ = 1.0 for epochs 7-10, and compare against fixed γ = 0.5 (the best fixed setting for Qwen2.5-7B from Table 6). Measure both pass@1 (to ensure early learning wasn't hindered) and pass@256 (to test whether late-stage diversity protection is enhanced).

**3. Interaction of F-GRPO with verifier-guided search at inference time.** The paper evaluates pass@256 as a passive diversity metric (uniform random sampling, take the best of 256), but never tests whether F-GRPO-preserved diversity is *exploitable* by active inference strategies. A critical follow-up: take F-GRPO-trained and standard GRPO-trained checkpoints, and evaluate them with best-of-N weighted selection using a process reward model (PRM) or outcome reward model (ORM) at varying budgets N. If F-GRPO preserves rare-correct modes that are structurally distinct from common modes, these modes might be more reliably identifiable by a verifier (they represent genuinely different solution strategies rather than minor variations, and a well-trained PRM can distinguish correct reasoning even when the strategy is unfamiliar). If instead F-GRPO-preserved rare modes are just lower-probability versions of common strategies (e.g., the same algebraic manipulation with slightly different variable names), verifier-guided selection may not benefit more from F-GRPO than from standard GRPO — the verifier would select the highest-scoring solution regardless, and if that tends to be a common mode, diversity in the tail doesn't help. A concrete experiment: train an ORM on the base model's outputs, then compare best-of-128 weighted accuracy for GRPO vs. F-GRPO checkpoints on AIME24 and MATH500. If F-GRPO shows a larger gap over GRPO under best-of-128 than under pass@128 random sampling, this is evidence that F-GRPO preserves *verifier-identifiable* diversity, which dramatically increases its practical value. A negative result (the gap is identical or smaller) would suggest the preserved rare modes are too subtle for current verifiers to exploit, motivating research on verifiers that can recognize diverse correct strategies.

**4. Focal weighting in multi-round self-improvement: does it prevent cumulative diversity collapse?** The categorical simulation (Figure 4b) shows that even with γ = 1, M_ret continues to decline at intermediate N over 1,000 steps — Focal weighting slows the collapse but doesn't halt it. In multi-round RLVR self-improvement (where the model is iteratively fine-tuned on solutions generated by its own previous checkpoint), the concentration pressure compounds across rounds: rare modes lost in round K cannot be recovered in round K+1 because they're never generated as training data. F-GRPO might delay this collapse sufficiently that useful diversity survives across multiple rounds, or it might only buy one extra round before the same asymptotic state is reached. A concrete experiment: run 3 rounds of RLVR on DeepScaleR, where round 1 trains on base-model-generated solutions, round 2 trains on round-1-model-generated solutions, and round 3 trains on round-2-model-generated solutions. Compare two conditions: (a) all rounds use standard GRPO at N=8, (b) all rounds use F-GRPO at N=8 with γ = 0.5. Measure pass@256 after each round, and also measure the overlap between the set of problems solvable at pass@256 by the round-3 model vs. the base model — if F-GRPO preserves a larger intersection, this is evidence that Focal weighting prevents irreversible capability loss in self-improvement loops. A companion experiment could test whether varying γ per round (higher in later rounds when cumulative concentration is worse) outperforms fixed γ.

**5. F-GRPO in domains with continuous or multi-dimensional reward signals.** The paper's entire analysis assumes binary outcome rewards (correct/incorrect), which is natural for math with string-match verification. Many RLVR applications use more nuanced rewards: code generation with partial credit (number of test cases passed), dialogue with multi-turn quality scores, or planning with step-level feasibility checks. The tail-miss framework should generalize — rare-correct modes are still modes with high reward but low sampling probability — but the Focal weight formulation `g(x) = (1 - μ̂_pos)^γ` assumes a binary success rate. For continuous rewards, `μ̂_pos` doesn't exist; you would need a difficulty proxy based on the reward distribution (e.g., `g(x) = (1 - r̄/r_max)^γ` where `r̄` is the group mean reward normalized to [0,1]). Whether the monotonicity connection (Corollary B.2) extends to continuous rewards is non-trivial: the equivalent of `R̄ > 0` would be batches where the mean reward is high relative to the reward scale, but the relationship between mean reward and concentration pressure may be qualitatively different for continuous rewards. A concrete experiment: adapt F-GRPO to code generation RLVR on APPS or MBPP using test-case pass rate as the reward signal, with `μ̂_pos` replaced by the fraction of test cases passed (averaged across N rollouts). Compare against standard GRPO on pass@256 (measured by any correct solution in 256 attempts) to test whether the diversity preservation mechanism transfers to non-binary reward settings. A negative result (Focal weighting doesn't help for continuous rewards) would bound the scope of the tail-miss framework and motivate separate analysis of concentration in continuous-reward RLVR.

**6. Cross-domain transfer of diversity preservation: what exactly is being preserved?** The paper's strongest unanticipated result is the OOD pass@1 improvement from F-GRPO (19.2 vs. 17.1 for GRPO on Qwen2.5-7B, Table 2; average +1.1 across 9 configurations, Table 1). Why should suppressing updates on high-success math prompts improve single-attempt accuracy on IFEval (instruction following) and GPQA (science QA)? The paper's theory doesn't predict this — Proposition 3.2 is about retaining unsampled-correct mass within the training distribution, not about transfer. Possible explanations: (a) F-GRPO preserves general reasoning templates (e.g., "break the problem into steps," "verify intermediate results") that transfer across domains, while standard GRPO overfits to math-specific common strategies; (b) F-GRPO prevents the model from becoming overconfident on math, which preserves calibration on OOD tasks; (c) the Focal weight effectively implements a form of regularization that prevents the model from deviating too far from its base distribution, similar to KL regularization but without the reference model. A concrete experiment to distinguish these: measure the KL divergence from the base model for F-GRPO vs. GRPO checkpoints, broken out by prompt difficulty. If F-GRPO shows lower KL divergence specifically on high-`μ_pos` prompts (as intended) but similar divergence on low-`μ_pos` prompts, this supports explanation (c) — Focal weighting is a difficulty-conditioned regularizer. If F-GRPO shows lower KL divergence uniformly, this suggests Focal weighting is just a softer form of global regularization. Track the entropy of the model's output distribution on OOD prompts across training: if F-GRPO maintains higher entropy on OOD tasks, this suggests (a) — diverse reasoning templates are being preserved. This experiment would clarify *why* F-GRPO's OOD benefits arise, which in turn would guide whether practitioners should expect similar OOD gains in their domains or whether the result is specific to the math→instruction-following transfer.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**1. Budget-constrained RLVR training where pass@k matters for deployment.** Consider a team training a math reasoning model for a competition-math product (e.g., an AIME-solving assistant). They have a fixed GPU budget that allows training at N=8 per prompt — N=32 would require 4× the generation cost, exceeding their compute allocation. Under standard GRPO at N=8, they get pass@1 of 37.3 and pass@256 of 64.1 on Qwen2.5-7B (Table 2) — the model is decent on single attempts but loses substantial diversity, meaning reliability at high sampling budgets is compromised. Switching to F-GRPO at N=8 (same generation cost) yields pass@1 of 38.6 and pass@256 of 70.3 — better accuracy on both single attempts and high-budget sampling, without a single additional GPU-hour. The practical benefit is not just the +6.2 pass@256 points, but the fact that the model now matches the diversity of an N=32 model that would have cost 4× more to train. For a team that was debating whether to increase their GPU budget to scale N, F-GRPO removes the tradeoff: they get N=32-level pass@256 at N=8-level cost.

**2. RLVR for open-ended tasks where solution diversity directly impacts user experience.** In code generation or creative writing applications, user satisfaction often depends on the model being able to produce *different valid solutions* on repeated attempts — a user who asks for "another way to implement this function" or "a different metaphor" needs genuine diversity, not just high single-attempt accuracy. Standard GRPO at practical group sizes (N=8-32) has an incentive to collapse onto a single high-probability correct solution strategy (the anchor action in the categorical simulation), making the model repetitive even if accurate. F-GRPO's M_ret preservation (Figure 4b, dashed curves) directly addresses this: by suppressing updates when the model already succeeds, it prevents the policy from concentrating onto a single common-correct mode and preserves lower-probability alternative solutions. For a code-generation product, this means the model can suggest both the O(N log N) recursive solution and the O(N) iterative solution to a sorting problem, rather than always defaulting to whichever strategy was most common in the training data after RLVR sharpening. The paper's OOD pass@256 improvements (Table 1: +7.4 on Qwen2.5-7B for F-GRPO over GRPO) provide evidence that diversity preservation during math training transfers to tasks with different structure, making it plausible that domain-specific F-GRPO training (on code, dialogue, or planning data) would yield similar diversity gains in those domains.

**3. Multi-round iterative self-improvement with bounded compute.** A growing paradigm for improving LLMs is to generate solutions with the current model, filter for correctness, and fine-tune on the correct solutions — then repeat. Each round of RLVR training sharpens the distribution, and without intervention, rare-correct modes from earlier rounds are progressively lost (the model generates fewer diverse correct solutions, which means the training data for the next round has lower diversity, which further concentrates the policy). The paper's categorical simulation (Figure 4b) shows that Focal weighting slows M_ret collapse, suggesting it could extend the number of productive self-improvement rounds before diversity exhaustion sets in. For a team running 3 rounds of self-improvement, using F-GRPO in each round (rather than standard GRPO) might preserve a broader set of correct solution strategies through round 3, enabling the final model to achieve higher pass@256 than a standard GRPO pipeline with the same number of rounds. The paper doesn't run multi-round experiments, but the single-round pass@256 gains (Table 2: F-GRPO N=8 achieves 70.3 vs. GRPO N=8's 64.1) suggest that after one round, F-GRPO models have a meaningfully larger set of correct solutions to serve as training data for round 2 — which should compound the advantage.

**4. Verifier development and reward model training on F-GRPO-generated data.** Process reward models (PRMs) and outcome reward models (ORMs) are trained on correct/incorrect solution pairs generated by a policy. If the policy has already undergone sharpening (standard GRPO), it generates a narrower distribution of correct solutions, and the verifier trained on those solutions may overfit to common solution patterns — becoming unable to reliably score rare but valid strategies. F-GRPO-trained policies preserve rare-correct modes (ΔNLL_rare of 0.46 vs. 0.68 for GRPO at N=8, Table 2), meaning the solutions they generate for verifier training data are more diverse. A verifier trained on F-GRPO-generated data should therefore generalize better to diverse correct solutions at test time, which is critical for verifier-guided search (best-of-N, beam search, tree search) where the verifier needs to recognize correctness across a wide strategy space. The paper doesn't train verifiers, but the implication is straightforward: if you're building an inference pipeline where a verifier selects among candidate solutions, training that verifier on F-GRPO policy outputs should yield a more robust verifier than training on standard GRPO outputs, because the training data spans a more diverse set of correct reasoning patterns.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper positions F-GRPO against standard GRPO (and its variants) at fixed group size, and also against the alternative of scaling N to combat sharpening. The decision criteria follow directly from the theoretical analysis and empirical results:

- **Prefer F-GRPO over standard GRPO/DAPO/CISPO at the same N when:** you are training at group sizes N=8 or N=32 (the most common practical settings) and care about both pass@1 and pass@256. The evidence in Table 1 shows that Focal weighting improves or maintains pass@1 in 7/9 model-method combinations while improving pass@256 in 9/9 combinations, with zero additional computational cost. There is essentially no downside — the only tradeoff is the need to tune γ (0.5 or 1.0 are the recommended starting points per Table 6), and the paper shows the method is robust to this choice within the {0.5, 1.0, 2.0} range. If you are already using GRPO at N=8, switching to F-GRPO at N=8 is a strict improvement in expectation.

- **Prefer F-GRPO at small N over scaling N for diversity when:** your generation budget per training step is constrained (e.g., by GPU memory, per-step latency, or total compute allocation for a fixed number of epochs). F-GRPO at N=8 achieves pass@256 comparable to GRPO at N=32 (70.3 vs. 70.1 on math, 63.3 vs. 61.7 on OOD; Table 2) while using 4× fewer rollouts per prompt. If you have a fixed per-step generation budget (e.g., you can afford N=8 but not N=32), F-GRPO lets you achieve N=32-level diversity without the generation cost. This is particularly relevant for larger models (e.g., 70B+) where generating even N=8 rollouts per prompt is expensive and N=32 is prohibitive.

- **Prefer scaling N over F-GRPO when:** you have abundant generation budget and need maximum pass@1 improvement, even at the cost of diversity. GRPO at N=32 achieves the highest math pass@1 in Table 2 (39.2 vs. 38.6 for F-GRPO at N=8), though the gap is small (0.6 points). If single-attempt accuracy on in-domain tasks is the sole objective and you have the compute to scale N, the small pass@1 advantage of larger groups (better advantage estimation quality) may outweigh the diversity benefits of Focal weighting at smaller N. However, even in this regime, F-GRPO at N=32 (not evaluated in the paper) might provide the best of both worlds — the improved advantage estimation of large groups combined with the concentration suppression of Focal weighting — and is a natural first experiment for a well-resourced team.

- **Prefer KL regularization over F-GRPO when:** you need the highest possible in-domain pass@256 and can afford the memory overhead of maintaining a reference model. GRPO-KL at N=8 achieves math pass@256 of 72.0 vs. F-GRPO's 70.3 (Table 4), at the cost of lower pass@1 (37.2 vs. 38.6) and lower OOD pass@256 (60.0 vs. 63.3). KL regularization is a stronger diversity preservation mechanism than Focal weighting for in-domain tasks, but it is more computationally expensive and restricts the model's ability to adapt on hard problems. Prefer KL when the deployment scenario involves high-budget sampling exclusively on the training distribution and memory is not a constraint.

- **Prefer entropy regularization over F-GRPO when:** you have evidence that token-level overconfidence (not trajectory-level concentration) is the primary failure mode for your task, or when you need an intervention that doesn't require tuning a new hyperparameter interactively (entropy bonuses are a standard, well-understood regularization). The paper's entropy comparison (Table 4) shows F-GRPO outperforming GRPO-H on both pass@1 (38.6 vs. 37.8) and OOD pass@256 (63.3 vs. 59.9), but these results are specific to the DeepScaleR math setting. In domains where solution strategies are more token-level diverse (e.g., creative writing) or where the binary reward structure doesn't apply, the tail-miss mechanism may be less relevant and entropy regularization may be comparably effective.
