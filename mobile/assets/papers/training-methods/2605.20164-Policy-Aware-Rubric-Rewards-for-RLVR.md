# Policy-Aware Rubric Rewards for RLVR

**ArXiv:** [2605.20164](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.20164)

## 🎯 Pitch

In rubric-based RL, almost half of all training pressure is wasted on criteria that are already saturated or never passed, producing zero learning signal. POW3R dynamically shifts weight toward criteria that currently separate the policy’s rollouts, widening reward spread by 25–37% and reaching the same performance plateau up to 4× faster than any static aggregation, without changing the evaluation rubric.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **POW3R**, a **policy-aware rubric reward** framework that modifies how multiple rubric criteria are aggregated into a single scalar reward for GRPO fine-tuning, keeping the underlying rubric, judge, and human-assigned importance weights intact while reallocating training pressure within each rubric category toward criteria that currently distinguish the policy's rollouts (e.g., a criterion whose pass rate sits between extremes gets more weight than one that every rollout already satisfies). Across three base policies on two datasets—a custom 10k-task multimodal dataset spanning six quality categories and HealthBench English—POW3R wins 24 of 30 base-policy/metric comparisons against binary, static-scalar, and category-balanced rewards, improving both mean rubric reward and strict completion (the fraction of prompts whose response satisfies every required criterion), and reaches the same performance plateau in 2.5–4× fewer training steps (e.g., crossing a 46.0 validation reward threshold at step 83 where the static scalar requires 249 steps). The paper establishes that human-assigned criterion importance and current policy learnability decouple in rubric RL—roughly 45–51% of within-category training pressure routes to criteria that produce no gradient signal—and that treating rubric aggregation as a training-time design choice rather than a fixed evaluation target yields consistent gains across model families and modalities.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Rubric Rewards Route Training Pressure Poorly

The fundamental problem this paper addresses sits at the intersection of two well-established but previously disconnected observations about post-training language models.

**Observation 1: RLVR works brilliantly for single-outcome tasks, but many important behaviors are inherently multi-dimensional.** Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR), particularly when implemented via group-relative methods like GRPO, has become the dominant recipe for post-training models on tasks like math and coding where success is a single checkable outcome—did the final answer match the ground truth? The algorithm samples multiple completions for a prompt, rewards correct ones, penalizes incorrect ones, and standardizes within the group so that relative advantage drives the gradient. This recipe powers systems like DeepSeek-R1 and DAPO, and its effectiveness on single-outcome tasks is no longer in question.

But real-world deployment demands more than single-outcome correctness. A medical advice response must be factually accurate, complete, faithful to evidence, appropriately cautious, and well-formatted all at once. A multimodal response to an image-based question must correctly perceive visual elements, reason about their relationships, respect all explicit instructions, and avoid hallucinating unsupported claims. In the paper's own framing, "open-ended quality is vector-valued"—it's a tuple of simultaneous constraints, not a scalar. Pushing post-training beyond strictly verifiable domains therefore requires reward signals that expose that vector rather than compress it into one dimension.

**Observation 2: Rubrics provide that vector structure.** A rubric decomposes response quality into prompt-specific criteria, each independently scored by an LLM judge. For example, on a prompt asking about a receipt image, one criterion might check whether the model identifies the store name (visual perception), another whether it orders items from cheapest to most expensive (instruction following), and a third whether it correctly reads the tax amount (visual perception again, but with a different requirement). Rubric-based rewards have become the practical bridge from single-outcome RLVR to open-ended quality, with both text-only and multimodal pipelines growing rapidly.

**The hidden tension: GRPO needs a scalar, but rubrics give a vector.** This is where the problem crystallizes. GRPO's advantage computation requires a single number per rollout—`mean(R)` and `std(R)` over the rollout group, then `(R_i − mean)/std` broadcast to every token. When the rubric gives multiple independent scores per rollout, someone must choose how to collapse those scores into one number. The standard answer, used across prior rubric-RL work, is a static weighted sum: multiply each criterion's binary judge verdict (0 or 1) by its human-assigned importance weight, sum across all criteria, and send that scalar to GRPO.

This aggregation is deceptively simple but contains a hidden assumption that the paper identifies and systematically dismantles: **that the human-assigned weight of a criterion simultaneously expresses (a) how important that criterion is in the final evaluation and (b) how useful that criterion currently is as a training signal.** These two roles are not the same, and conflating them leads to systematic misallocation of the policy's learning budget.

### Why This Problem Matters

**Practical waste: nearly half of training pressure is inert.** The paper's diagnostic (Section 1, Figure 1) quantifies this concretely. Sampling rollout groups across two frozen base policies on two datasets, the authors record each criterion's pass rate across the rollout group and categorize it:

- **Saturated criteria**: every rollout passes (`p_j = 1`), so the criterion adds the same constant to every reward and cancels out of the group-relative advantage. Pass rate data: 17–26% of criteria across settings.
- **Dead criteria**: no rollout passes (`p_j = 0`), same effect—constant reward, zero advantage signal. Pass rate data: 20–33% of criteria.
- **Mixed criteria**: pass rate sits between 0 and 1, so the criterion *can* produce contrastive gradient. Only about half of all criteria fall here.

Under static weighted-sum aggregation, a criterion's contribution to the gradient is proportional to its human weight `w_j` times its within-group variance `v_j = p_j(1−p_j)`. When `v_j = 0` (saturated or dead), the product is zero regardless of how large `w_j` is. The static aggregation therefore routes 45–51% of within-category training pressure to criteria that **cannot move the policy**. The policy "sees" a reward signal where it can learn nothing, and—crucially—this waste is not confined to low-importance criteria. Figure 1a shows that high-weight criteria (`|w_j| ≥ 5`) still carry substantial dead and saturated mass. Human importance and current learnability are essentially uncorrelated.

This has direct consequences for training efficiency and final quality. GRPO steps where the reward spread `std(R)` is zero produce `Â_{i,t} = 0` for every rollout—the entire batch contributes no gradient, wasting FLOPs and wall-clock time. In the paper's diagnostic run, roughly half of all criteria are non-contrastive at any moment, meaning many prompt groups likely collapse to this zero-gradient regime under static aggregation. The policy spends compute budget on dead ends, and the training signal is noisier and slower than it needs to be.

**Conceptual significance: the scalarization problem in rubric RL.** This paper connects rubric reward design to a broader challenge in multi-objective reinforcement learning known as the **scalarization problem**. When multiple objectives must be optimized simultaneously, someone must choose how to combine them into a scalar that the optimizer can consume. Static linear scalarization—weighted sum with fixed weights—is the simplest solution, but it has known pathologies: it cannot find solutions on non-convex regions of the Pareto front, and it routes learning effort according to weight magnitudes rather than learnability.

The paper's insight is that rubric RL inherits this pathology, but the right fix is different from standard multi-objective RL recipes. In typical multi-objective RL, the practitioner genuinely wants to find policies that trade off between competing objectives (e.g., speed vs. energy consumption), and adaptive weighting schemes try to explore the Pareto frontier. In rubric RL, by contrast, the objective is fixed: every criterion must eventually be satisfied, and the human weights express non-negotiable relative importance. The problem isn't that the objective changes during training—it's that the **same static weights that define the evaluation target make poor training targets**, because they ignore whether a criterion is currently learnable.

This framing—unalterable evaluation objective, learnability-dependent training signal—is what distinguishes POW3R's approach from prior adaptive multi-reward methods. It also explains why the literature contains conflicting signals about rubric effectiveness: static aggregation can look fine when most criteria happen to be in the mixed regime (e.g., on fresh prompts where the base policy hasn't learned anything yet), but breaks down as the policy saturates easy criteria and leaves a rump of dead ones, creating a training trajectory where the reward signal degrades even as the policy improves.

**Scale implications.** The paper adds a novel dimension to the scaling conversation. While the community has invested enormous effort in scaling laws for pretraining and inference-time compute, the efficiency of **post-training signal extraction** from existing data and judges has received far less attention. POW3R demonstrates that a simple, principled reweighting of the same rubric data can extract 2.5–4× more progress from the same number of GRPO steps. At the scale where these training runs operate (multi-H100 nodes, hundreds of steps, thousands of LLM-judge queries per step), this improvement compounds into significant cost savings and faster iteration cycles.

### Where Prior Approaches Fall Short

The paper situates its contribution against a specific set of prior approaches, each with a limitation that POW3R addresses.

**Static weighted-sum rubric rewards (the prior standard).** The dominant aggregation scheme in prior rubric-RL work [6, 9] is `R_scalar(o; q) = ∑_j w_j s_j(o; q)`—multiply each criterion's binary score by its human weight and sum. This is what the paper calls the "common operational answer." Its failure mode, documented in the Section 1 diagnostic, is that it ignores criterion-level variance entirely: a criterion with `w_j = 5` and `v_j = 0` gets 5× the pressure of a criterion with `w_j = 1` and `v_j = 0.25`, but actually generates zero gradient signal while the low-weight criterion generates a useful signal. At the category level, this means categories with many saturated easy-criteria (like Writing Style) hoard training pressure while learnable criteria in other categories go underemphasized—the exact opposite of what efficient learning requires.

**Existing multi-reward RL methods address a different problem.** Work on dynamic reward weighting for multi-objective RL [13, 17, 23] and multi-reward GRPO normalizers (e.g., GDPO [18]) share the observation that naive scalarization underperforms, but they target settings where (a) objectives genuinely compete and trade off, and (b) the weight vector is a training-time preference knob to be optimized, not a fixed evaluation target. GDPO, for instance, normalizes each reward dimension independently before aggregation to prevent one dimension from dominating the group-relative advantage. This is valuable when rewards have different scales or noise levels, but it does not preserve the human-assigned importance weights as a final objective—it changes what the optimizer is optimizing toward. POW3R's constraint is stricter: the evaluation rubric is sacrosanct (researchers and domain experts authored those weights for a reason), and the only permissible change is **within-category reallocation** that leaves category-level mass and the human-weight prior intact. The static weight vector is preserved as the target; POW3R only changes how the policy is guided to reach it.

**Binary or sparse outcome rewards throw away structure.** An alternative is to avoid aggregation entirely: give reward 1 if every required criterion passes, 0 otherwise. This is the natural generalization of single-outcome RLVR to rubric tasks, and it preserves the notion that "everything matters." But it collapses a high-dimensional quality signal into a single bit, making it impossible for the policy to learn partial progress. If a response passes 8 of 10 criteria but fails two, the binary reward is zero—identical to a response that passes zero criteria. The gradient contains no information about *which* criteria passed or *how close* the failures were. The paper's binary baseline consistently underperforms even the static scalar reward (Tables 2 and 3), confirming that richer signal helps, and that the right question isn't "should we use rubric signal?" but "how should we aggregate rubric signal?"

**Per-category batched judging misses fine-grained signal.** As noted in Section B, scoring all criteria for a response in a single judge call is cheaper but reduces rank correlation with the reference judge because the judge tends to merge or skip independent criteria. POW3R relies on per-criterion verdicts to estimate rollout-level variance accurately; batched judging would inject additional noise into the `v_j` estimates that the dynamic factors depend on.

**Rezaei et al. [20] and Shao et al. [21] modify rubrics during training.** These works change *which* criteria exist during training—eliciting them from pairwise comparisons or co-evolving them with the policy. POW3R is orthogonal: it keeps the rubric set fixed and only changes the aggregation weights. A system could in principle combine both ideas, evolving the rubric set while also adaptively weighting the current set.

**Chen et al. [22] (RuCL) is the closest prior work.** RuCL stratifies generalized rubrics into a perception-to-reasoning curriculum and dynamically reweights them across training according to a global capability schedule (e.g., emphasizing perception early, reasoning later). POW3R shares the diagnosis that not all criteria are equally learnable at every stage, but differs on everything thereafter: (a) POW3R's rubrics are prompt-specific and human-authored, not a fixed global set, so the per-prompt variance pattern matters; (b) POW3R preserves human weights as a prior and only reweights within fixed categories, while RuCL changes global weightings; and (c) POW3R derives its dynamic factors from the current policy's actual rollout variance (a local, data-driven signal) rather than from a pre-specified curriculum schedule.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

POW3R makes a specific, falsifiable claim: **rubric aggregation should be treated as a training-time design choice, not as a fixed consequence of evaluation, because human importance weights and current policy learnability are distinct concepts that must be handled separately.** The paper's framework is self-consciously minimalist—it changes *only* the scalar reward `R(o; q)` that enters GRPO standardization, leaving the rubric, judge scores, human weights, GRPO optimizer, and evaluation protocol untouched. This minimalism is a strength: it isolates the effect of better pressure allocation from the confounds that would come with changing the rubric set, the judge, or the RL algorithm.

The paper's positioning relative to RLVR diagnostics [4, 5] is also instructive. Prior work has shown that observed RLVR gains can reflect spurious correlations or verifier noise rather than genuine capability improvement, and that imperfect verifiers pose challenges. POW3R's approach is complementary: it doesn't fix a broken judge or a sparse reward, but instead extracts more signal from an already-working judge by ensuring that the signal it produces is routed to the criteria where it can actually teach the policy. The mechanism is orthogonal to judge quality (it works with any judge that produces per-criterion verdicts) and doesn't require retraining or recalibrating the judge itself.

Finally, the paper connects to the multimodal RLVR literature [7, 8, 30–36] by targeting a known weakness: final-answer rewards often underfit perception and grounding, because a model can "reason past the image" by producing text that happens to be correct without actually attending to visual evidence. Rubrics that explicitly score visual perception and grounding criteria provide a direct countermeasure—but only if those criteria receive sufficient training pressure. POW3R's mechanism naturally routes pressure toward the visual criteria that are currently hardest for the policy, which may explain part of its consistent gains on the Visual Perception and Visual Reasoning categories in Figure 5.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

POW3R is a **reward-aggregation wrapper** that sits between a rubric-based LLM judge and the GRPO reinforcement learning optimizer, taking the same per-criterion binary verdicts and human-assigned importance weights that a standard static rubric reward would use, and producing a single scalar reward per rollout that is strictly more informative for the current policy without changing what the evaluation rubric measures. The system solves the problem that roughly half of all rubric criteria produce zero gradient signal under static weighted-sum aggregation because they are either already passed by every rollout (saturated) or never passed (dead), and it solves this by measuring each criterion's current contrastiveness—how much its pass/fail verdicts actually vary across the rollout group—and reallocating within-category training pressure toward criteria that distinguish rollouts while preserving the category balance and human-weight prior that define the evaluation target.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The POW3R system has four interacting components, all operating on top of a standard GRPO training loop:

1. **The Rubric Judge** — an external LLM (GPT-5.4-nano at training time, GPT-5.4-mini at evaluation time) that, given a prompt, a model-generated response, and a single rubric criterion, returns a binary verdict `s_j(o, q) ∈ {0, 1}` and a one-sentence reasoning. This is queried independently for every `(prompt, rollout, criterion)` triple. The judge's interface, system prompt, and sampling configuration are identical across all reward constructions; POW3R differs only in how the verdicts are aggregated after the judge returns them.

2. **The Category-Normalized Baseline Reward `R_cat`** — a static reward that normalizes within each rubric category so that all categories contribute equally to the final scalar regardless of how many criteria they contain. This is the foundation on which POW3R builds: it fixes the category-imbalance problem inherent in raw weighted sums (where a category with many low-weight criteria can dominate training pressure simply by having more items), but still routes pressure proportionally to static human weights within each category, meaning it inherits the saturated/dead waste problem.

3. **The Policy-Aware Dynamic Factors `α_j^{(t)}`** — an epoch-level computation that estimates each criterion's rollout-level variance `v_j^{(t)}` from the current batch of judge verdicts, smooths and category-normalizes this variance signal, blends it with a uniform prior, clips it to a bounded range, and updates it via exponential moving average. Each factor `α_j^{(t)}` is a multiplicative adjustment applied to the static weight `w_j` before category normalization, so a criterion with high variance gets `α_j > 1` (more training pressure) and a criterion with zero variance gets `α_j` close to the floor `α_min = 0.67` (less pressure, but not zero).

4. **The GRPO Standardization Block** — unchanged from standard GRPO. It receives the scalar reward `R_dyn(o; q)` from POW3R's aggregation, computes `mean(R)` and `std(R)` across the rollout group, produces per-token advantages `Â_{i,t} = (R_i − mean)/std`, and applies the clipped PPO objective with the KL penalty. POW3R never touches the optimizer, the policy network, the sampling procedure, or the KL reference model.

**Information flow, step by step:** A prompt `q` enters the system → the current policy `π_θ_old` samples `G = 16` completions `{o_1, ..., o_G}` → for each rollout `o_i` and each rubric criterion `c_j` on prompt `q`, the judge returns `s_j(o_i, q) ∈ {0, 1}` → POW3R retrieves the current dynamic factor `α_j^{(t)}` for each criterion (computed at the end of the previous epoch), multiplies it by the static weight `w_j`, normalizes within categories to produce `R_dyn(o_i; q)`, and sends this scalar to GRPO → GRPO computes advantages and updates the policy → after the epoch, POW3R updates `α_j^{(t)}` based on the observed variance of `s_j` across the rollout group for each criterion → the next epoch uses the updated factors.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the category-normalized baseline reward `R_cat`, because it establishes the category-balance principle and the notation that POW3R extends, and understanding why category normalization matters is prerequisite to understanding how POW3R reweights within categories.
- **Second**, the rollout-level variance estimation that feeds the dynamic factors, because the core mechanism of POW3R—measuring contrastiveness from per-criterion pass rates—depends on how variance is computed from judge verdicts across the `G = 16` rollouts in a group.
- **Third**, the full dynamic factor pipeline (smoothing, category-normalizing, blending, clipping, EMA-updating), because each step has a specific design justification that builds on the variance measurement.
- **Fourth**, the POW3R reward construction that combines the dynamic factors with the category-normalized baseline, explaining how category mass stays uniform and the human-weight prior is preserved even as individual criteria get up-weighted or down-weighted.
- **Fifth**, the training-time and evaluation-time separation, because POW3R's factors are derived from training judge verdicts (GPT-5.4-nano) but the reported results use an evaluation judge (GPT-5.4-mini), and this separation prevents the policy from overfitting to the cheaper judge's quirks.
- **Sixth**, the design-choices rationale, covering why POW3R reweights within categories rather than globally, why it uses the standard deviation rather than the variance directly, why it uses EMA updating rather than instantaneous variance, and why it preserves a non-zero floor for saturated/dead criteria.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **methods paper** whose core idea is that rubric reward aggregation for GRPO should adapt to the current policy's performance profile by reallocating training pressure within rubric categories toward criteria whose pass/fail verdicts vary across the rollout group, using the rollout-level standard deviation of judge verdicts as a proxy for contrastiveness, while keeping the human-assigned importance weights as a prior and leaving category-level mass intact so the evaluation target does not drift.

#### The Category-Normalized Baseline Reward

The starting point for POW3R is not the raw static weighted sum used in prior work, but a **category-normalized** variant that fixes a structural problem with unnormalized aggregation. The paper defines this baseline first because POW3R's dynamic factors are applied on top of it, and understanding the normalization step is essential to understanding what POW3R preserves and what it changes.

For a prompt `q`, the rubric set consists of `N(q)` criteria. Each criterion `j` has:

- A **human-assigned weight** `w_j ∈ ℕ_{>0}` (integer, typically in `{1, ..., 5}`, expressing end-state importance)
- A **category label** `κ_j ∈ {1, ..., K}` (one of six quality dimensions in MM, such as Visual Perception, Visual Reasoning, Content Completeness, Instruction Following, Truthfulness, or Writing Style/Presentation)
- A **binary judge verdict** `s_j(o, q) ∈ [0, 1]` per rollout `o`, where 1 means the criterion is satisfied

The categories partition the criteria: `C_k(q) = {j : κ_j = k}` is the set of criterion indices belonging to category `k`. Some categories may be empty for a given prompt (e.g., a prompt with no explicit formatting requirement might have zero Writing Style criteria). Let `K_q` be the number of categories that are populated (non-empty) for prompt `q`.

The category-normalized baseline reward is:

$$R_{\text{cat}}(o; q) = \frac{1}{K_q} \sum_{k: C_k(q) \neq \emptyset} \frac{1}{W_k(q)} \sum_{j \in C_k(q)} w_j s_j(o, q)$$

where `W_k(q) = ∑_{j∈C_k(q)} w_j` is the total human weight mass in category `k`, and the sum runs over all categories that have at least one criterion for this prompt.

> **What it computes:** For each populated rubric category, compute the weighted average of the judge verdicts in that category—`(1/W_k(q)) ∑_{j∈C_k(q)} w_j s_j(o, q)`—which gives a per-category score between 0 and 1 representing what fraction of the category's total importance weight was satisfied. Then average these per-category scores equally across the `K_q` populated categories. The result is a scalar between 0 and 1 that treats every category as equally important regardless of how many criteria it contains or what their individual weights are.

> **Why this form over the raw weighted sum `R_scalar = ∑_j w_j s_j(o, q)`:** The raw sum implicitly weights categories by their total human-weight mass `W_k(q)`. A category with many moderate-weight criteria (e.g., Visual Perception with five criteria each weight 3, total mass 15) dominates a category with one high-weight criterion (e.g., Truthfulness with one criterion weight 5, total mass 5). The policy would then optimize mainly for Visual Perception even if Truthfulness is equally important in the final evaluation—the category with more items simply gets more gradient. Category normalization fixes this by giving each populated category exactly `1/K_q` of the total reward mass. This ensures that all quality dimensions receive equal training pressure at the category level, and differences in training pressure happen only within categories—which is exactly the space where POW3R's dynamic reweighting operates.

This reward is still **static** in the sense that the within-category pressure allocation (proportional to `w_j`) does not change during training. On each rollout, a criterion with `w_j = 4` gets exactly twice the contribution of a criterion with `w_j = 2`, regardless of whether the `w_j = 4` criterion is saturated and the `w_j = 2` criterion is mixed. POW3R's innovation is to replace the static `w_j` with a dynamic `w̃_j^{(t)} = w_j α_j^{(t)}` that adapts `α_j^{(t)}` based on the current policy's performance while keeping the category-level structure of `R_cat` intact.

#### Rollout-Level Variance Estimation

The core signal that drives POW3R's dynamic factors is the **rollout-level variance** of each rubric criterion's judge verdict. This is what distinguishes criteria that can teach the current policy from those that cannot.

At epoch `t`, the policy `π_θ(t)` samples `G = 16` completions for prompt `q`. The judge produces binary verdicts `s_j(o_i, q) ∈ {0, 1}` for each rollout `i ∈ {1, ..., G}` and each criterion `j`. From these `G` verdicts per criterion, POW3R computes two statistics:

$$p_j^{(t)} = \frac{1}{n_j} \sum_{i \in \mathcal{V}_j} s_j(o_i, q)$$

$$v_j^{(t)} = \frac{1}{n_j} \sum_{i \in \mathcal{V}_j} \left(s_j(o_i, q) - p_j^{(t)}\right)^2$$

where `𝒱_j` is the set of rollouts with valid judge verdicts for criterion `j`, and `n_j = |𝒱_j|` is its size.

> **What `p_j^{(t)}` computes:** the empirical pass rate—what fraction of the `n_j` valid rollouts satisfied criterion `j`. If every rollout passes, `p_j^{(t)} = 1` (saturated). If no rollout passes, `p_j^{(t)} = 0` (dead). If some pass and some fail, `0 < p_j^{(t)} < 1` (mixed).

> **What `v_j^{(t)}` computes:** the empirical variance of the binary verdicts across rollouts. For binary data, `v_j^{(t)} = p_j^{(t)}(1 − p_j^{(t)})`, so it is a quadratic function of the pass rate that peaks at `0.25` when `p_j^{(t)} = 0.5` (maximally mixed) and is zero at both extremes `p_j^{(t)} = 0` and `p_j^{(t)} = 1` (zero contrast). This is the statistic that directly measures whether a criterion can produce non-zero advantage in GRPO: if `v_j^{(t)} = 0`, the criterion's verdicts are identical across all rollouts, its contribution to `R_dyn` is a constant offset, and it cancels out of the group-relative advantage computation.

> **Why sample-based rather than rolling statistics:** The variance is computed over only the current epoch's `G = 16` rollouts, not over the entire training history. This makes the signal **local in time**—it reflects what the policy can and cannot do right now, not what it could do fifty epochs ago. A criterion that was mixed early in training but has since saturated will correctly show `v_j ≈ 0` in the current epoch, reducing its training pressure. This locality is essential because the whole point is to route pressure away from criteria that the policy has already mastered.

The paper includes a robustness mechanism for incomplete judge data: if fewer than `⌈0.75G⌉ = 12` rollouts produce valid verdicts for a criterion (due to judge errors, parsing failures, or other edge cases), the criterion **retains its previous epoch's factor** rather than updating from the noisy estimate. This prevents a single epoch of judge flakiness from corrupting the dynamic factors.

Crucially, `p_j^{(t)}` and `v_j^{(t)}` are computed from **training judge** verdicts (GPT-5.4-nano) because that is the signal the policy actually sees during GRPO updates. The evaluation judge (GPT-5.4-mini) is only used for held-out scoring and never influences the dynamic factors, which prevents the policy from indirectly overfitting to the evaluation judge through the factor adaptation mechanism.

#### The Dynamic Factor Pipeline

Given the per-criterion variance `v_j^{(t)}` from the current rollout group, POW3R transforms it through a five-stage pipeline into a multiplicative factor `α_j^{(t+1)}` that will be used in the next epoch's reward computation. Each stage addresses a specific statistical or practical concern.

**Stage 1: Smoothing via standard deviation.** POW3R does not use `v_j^{(t)}` directly. Instead, it takes the square root to work with standard deviations:

$$g_j^{(t)} = \sqrt{v_j^{(t)} + \epsilon}$$

where `ϵ = 10^{-4}` is a small constant added before the square root to prevent numerical instability when `v_j^{(t)}` is extremely small.

> **What this computes:** the standard deviation of the binary verdicts across rollouts, with a tiny floor for numerical safety. For binary data, `g_j^{(t)} = \sqrt{p_j^{(t)}(1 − p_j^{(t)}) + 10^{-4}}`.

> **Why the standard deviation rather than the variance directly:** The variance `v_j^{(t)}` ranges from 0 to 0.25 (for binary data), while the standard deviation `g_j^{(t)}` ranges from `≈ 0.01` to `0.5`, which is a more natural scale for the downstream operations (category averaging, ratio computation, blending). More fundamentally, the standard deviation is linear in the spread of the data while the variance is quadratic—a criterion with `p_j = 0.2` has `v_j = 0.16` and a criterion with `p_j = 0.4` has `v_j = 0.24`, a 1.5× ratio, while their standard deviations are `≈ 0.4` and `≈ 0.49`, a 1.2× ratio. The standard deviation compresses the dynamic range, which makes the subsequent ratio and clipping operations more stable across criteria with different pass rates.

**Stage 2: Category-level normalization of the standard deviation.** A raw `g_j^{(t)}` is not directly comparable across categories, because different categories may have different typical levels of contrast (e.g., Writing Style criteria tend to be saturated for a competent base policy, so their `g_j` values are systematically lower than those in Visual Reasoning). To make the factors comparable, POW3R computes the **weighted mean standard deviation** within each rubric category:

$$\bar{g}_k^{(t)} = \frac{\sum_{j' \in C_k^{\text{valid}}(q)} w_{j'} g_{j'}^{(t)}}{\sum_{j' \in C_k^{\text{valid}}(q)} w_{j'}}$$

where `C_k^{\text{valid}}(q)` is the set of criteria in category `k` that have at least `⌈0.75G⌉` valid judge verdicts (so their `g_j^{(t)}` estimates are reliable).

> **What this computes:** the human-weight-weighted average standard deviation across all reliably-estimated criteria in category `k`. A category full of saturated criteria will have `ḡ_k` near `ε`; a category with many mixed criteria will have `ḡ_k` closer to `0.5`.

> **Why weight by `w_j` rather than taking an unweighted mean:** the static human weights express relative importance within the category, and the category normalization in `R_cat` already averages per-category scores with weights proportional to `w_j`. Using the same weight vector in the standard-deviation averaging keeps the definition of "typical contrast in this category" aligned with the importance distribution, so that a high-weight criterion's variance influences `ḡ_k` more than a low-weight criterion's variance.

**Stage 3: Ratio-to-category-mean.** POW3R then computes a ratio that expresses each criterion's contrastiveness relative to its category average:

$$\rho_j^{(t)} = \frac{g_j^{(t)}}{\bar{g}_{\kappa_j}^{(t)}}$$

where `κ_j` is the category label for criterion `j`.

> **What this computes:** how many times more (or less) contrastive criterion `j` is compared to the typical criterion in its category, after accounting for the importance-weighting in the average. If `ρ_j = 1`, the criterion is exactly as contrastive as the category average and its factor will stay at 1 (no reweighting). If `ρ_j > 1`, the criterion is more contrastive than average and will receive an up-weighting. If `ρ_j < 1`, the criterion is less contrastive and will be down-weighted.

> **Why the ratio to category mean rather than a global mean:** If POW3R used a global mean across all criteria, categories with systematically lower contrast (like Writing Style) would have all their criteria down-weighted relative to categories with higher contrast (like Visual Reasoning), which would effectively shift training pressure between categories—violating the principle that category-level mass should be invariant. By normalizing within each category, POW3R ensures that the total training pressure allocated to category `k` remains exactly `1/K_q` of the total reward regardless of that category's absolute level of contrast; the dynamic reweighting only redistributes pressure among criteria inside the same category.

**Stage 4: Blending with a uniform prior and clipping.** The raw ratio `ρ_j^{(t)}` can be noisy (estimated from only `G = 16` rollouts), and it can take extreme values when a criterion's `g_j` is very different from `ḡ`. POW3R applies a convex blend toward 1 followed by clipping to a bounded range:

$$\hat{\alpha}_j^{(t)} = \text{clip}\left((1 - \lambda) + \lambda \rho_j^{(t)}, \; \alpha_{\min}, \; \alpha_{\max}\right)$$

The hyperparameters are `λ = 0.5`, `α_min = 0.67`, `α_max = 1.5`.

> **What this computes:** a candidate factor `α̂_j^{(t)}` that is a 50-50 blend of the uniform prior (1.0, meaning "treat all criteria equally") and the variance-based ratio `ρ_j^{(t)}`. This blend is then clipped to the interval `[0.67, 1.5]`. The result is a factor between roughly two-thirds and one-and-a-half, pulled halfway toward 1.0 from whatever the raw ratio would suggest.

> **Why the blend toward 1:** `λ = 0.5` means POW3R only half-trusts the rollout-level variance estimate, and half-trusts the prior that all criteria in a category should receive equal pressure. This is a deliberate regularization choice: with only 16 rollouts per group, `p_j` estimates are noisy (a criterion with true pass rate 0.5 will show observed pass rates between 0.25 and 0.75 in a 16-sample group roughly 95% of the time, giving `ρ_j` values ranging from roughly 0.7 to 1.4 even when the true contrast is exactly average). The blend toward 1 shrinks these noisy estimates toward the prior, making the factors more stable epoch-to-epoch. The `λ = 0.5` choice is a compromise: lower `λ` would make the factors less responsive to genuine changes in contrast; higher `λ` would make them more jittery.

> **Why the clipping range `[0.67, 1.5]`:** The floor `α_min = 0.67` ensures that even a completely saturated or dead criterion (with `v_j = 0`, `g_j ≈ 0.01`, potentially `ρ_j ≪ 0.67`) retains two-thirds of its static weight in the reward computation. This is critical because criteria can transition from dead to mixed as the policy improves—if a saturated criterion's factor were allowed to drop to zero, POW3R would permanently stop training on it even if the policy later regresses or if the task distribution shifts. The ceiling `α_max = 1.5` prevents any single high-contrast criterion from monopolizing its category's training pressure; even the most informative criterion gets at most 1.5× its static weight. The range `[0.67, 1.5]` gives a total dynamic range of roughly 2.25:1 between the most and least emphasized criteria in a category.

**Stage 5: Exponential moving average update.** The candidate factor `α̂_j^{(t)}` from the current epoch is not used directly in the next epoch. Instead, POW3R maintains a running factor `α_j^{(t)}` that is updated via exponential moving average:

$$\alpha_j^{(t+1)} = \text{clip}\left((1 - \beta_{\text{ema}})\alpha_j^{(t)} + \beta_{\text{ema}}\hat{\alpha}_j^{(t)}, \; \alpha_{\min}, \; \alpha_{\max}\right)$$

with `β_ema = 0.2`, and all factors initialized to `α_j^{(0)} = 1` at the start of training.

> **What this computes:** a smoothed factor that blends the new candidate `α̂_j^{(t)}` with the running factor `α_j^{(t)}` from all previous epochs, re-clipped to the allowed range. With `β_ema = 0.2`, each epoch's new information contributes 20% to the update, and the factor carries an exponential memory of past epochs with a half-life of roughly `log(0.5) / log(0.8) ≈ 3.1` epochs.

> **Why EMA rather than instantaneous updating:** The rollout group size `G = 16` is small, so the per-epoch `ρ_j` estimates are high-variance. If POW3R used instantaneous factors `α_j^{(t+1)} = α̂_j^{(t)}`, the reward weights would jitter substantially from epoch to epoch even when the policy's true capability on a criterion is stable, injecting noise into the GRPO advantage signal. The EMA smooths this noise by averaging over multiple epochs. A single epoch where the policy happened to fail an easy criterion it usually passes (causing a temporary spike in contrastiveness) will nudge the EMA by only 20% rather than causing a full factor switch. The `β_ema = 0.2` choice balances responsiveness to genuine capability changes (a criterion that becomes newly learnable after a policy improvement will see its factor rise within a few epochs) against stability (no single fluke epoch dominates).

**Edge case: all signals in a category vanish.** If every criterion in a category has fewer than `⌈0.75G⌉` valid verdicts for the current epoch, POW3R cannot compute `ḡ_k` or the ratios `ρ_j`. In this case, POW3R sets `α̂_j^{(t)} = 1` for all criteria in that category—effectively falling back to the static `R_cat` behavior for that category in the next epoch. This is a safety valve: when judge data is too sparse to estimate contrastiveness, POW3R makes no change rather than making a poorly-justified change.

#### The POW3R Reward Construction

At epoch `t`, before computing the scalar reward for each rollout, POW3R retrieves the current dynamic factors `α_j^{(t)}` (which were computed after epoch `t−1` and held fixed through epoch `t`). It computes dynamic weights:

$$\tilde{w}_j^{(t)} = w_j \alpha_j^{(t)}$$

and dynamic category mass:

$$\tilde{W}_k^{(t)}(q) = \sum_{j \in C_k(q)} \tilde{w}_j^{(t)}$$

The POW3R dynamic reward is then:

$$R_{\text{dyn}}^{(t)}(o; q) = \frac{1}{K_q} \sum_{k: C_k(q) \neq \emptyset} \frac{1}{\tilde{W}_k^{(t)}(q)} \sum_{j \in C_k(q)} \tilde{w}_j^{(t)} s_j(o, q)$$

> **What it computes:** structurally identical to `R_cat(o; q)`, but with the static weight `w_j` replaced by the dynamic weight `w̃_j^{(t)} = w_j α_j^{(t)}`. For each populated category, compute the `w̃`-weighted average of judge verdicts, then average equally across categories. The result is a scalar between 0 and 1.

> **Why this preserves category-level mass:** The per-category weight `1/W̃_k^{(t)}(q)` normalizes the dynamic weights *within the category*, so the per-category score is still a weighted average over `s_j` values that lies in `[0, 1]` regardless of how the `α_j` shift the weights. A category where all `α_j = 2` would have `W̃_k = 2 W_k`, but the normalization `1/W̃_k` would divide this out, so the category's contribution to `R_dyn` is still bounded in `[0, 1]` and weighted equally with other categories. The dynamic factors only change *which criteria within the category get more or less say* in that category's score; they never change the category's overall weight in the final reward.

> **Why this preserves the human-weight prior:** `w̃_j = w_j α_j` is multiplicatively anchored to the static `w_j`. If two criteria in the same category have the same `α` (because they have similar variance), their relative contributions stay exactly proportional to `w_j`. The dynamic factors only break this proportionality when variance differs—and they break it in a principled direction (more variance → higher factor → more pressure). The static weights thus serve as an unalterable prior that POW3R modulates but never overrides: a criterion with `w_j = 5` at the `α_min = 0.67` floor still gets effective weight `5 × 0.67 = 3.35`, while a criterion with `w_j = 1` at the `α_max = 1.5` ceiling gets `1 × 1.5 = 1.5`, so the high-importance criterion still dominates in absolute terms even when it's maximally penalized for being saturated.

> **Why not use `α_j` as a standalone weight, dropping `w_j` entirely:** That would destroy the evaluation alignment. The human weights `w_j` encode domain expertise about which failures are more severe—a medical advice response that gives dangerous instructions (a Truthfulness criterion with `w_j = 5`) is objectively worse than one with a minor formatting error (a Writing Style criterion with `w_j = 2`). If POW3R used raw `α_j` without `w_j`, a highly-variable low-importance criterion could out-weight a saturated high-importance criterion, which would train the policy to optimize for contrastive-but-unimportant criteria at the expense of important-but-mastered ones. Multiplying `w_j` by `α_j` preserves the importance ordering when variances are equal and only modulates it when variance differs.

**What GRPO sees:** The scalar `R_dyn^{(t)}(o_i; q)` is computed identically for all `G = 16` rollouts in the group (no rollout gets a different `α_j`), because `α_j` is per-criterion, not per-rollout. GRPO then standardizes: `µ = mean({R_dyn(o_i)}_{i=1}^G)`, `σ = std({R_dyn(o_i)}_{i=1}^G)`, and `Â_i = (R_dyn(o_i) − µ)/σ`. The advantage is broadcast to every token in rollout `i`. When `σ = 0` (all rollouts have identical reward), `Â_i = 0` and the group contributes no gradient—but POW3R's mechanism makes this regime less likely by widening the pre-standardization reward spread (Figure 1c, 2).

#### Training-Time and Evaluation-Time Separation

POW3R's dynamic factors are derived from the **training judge's** verdicts (GPT-5.4-nano with medium reasoning and explanations, 91.4% agreement with the reference judge, $0.12 per 1,000 judgments). The evaluation in Tables 2 and 3, by contrast, uses the **evaluation judge's** verdicts (GPT-5.4-mini with medium reasoning and explanations, 93.6% agreement, $1.52 per 1,000 judgments). This two-tier design serves several purposes:

- **Cost**: Training runs require judging `(number of prompts per epoch) × G × (average criteria per prompt)` verdicts—with roughly 8,000 training prompts, 16 rollouts per group, and ~8.4 average criteria, each epoch requiries roughly 1 million judge calls. At $12.50/1k for the reference judge, a single epoch would cost $12,500. GPT-5.4-nano at $0.12/1k brings this to $120 per epoch, making the training budget feasible. The evaluation judge is only run at logged checkpoints on the 1,000-example test set, so its higher cost is amortized.

- **Decontamination**: If the same judge were used for both training rewards and held-out evaluation, the policy could overfit to judge-specific biases—learning to produce outputs that score well under that particular judge's idiosyncrasies rather than genuinely satisfying the rubric criteria. Using a more accurate but separate judge for evaluation means the policy is scored by a model whose verdict distribution it has never been trained against directly. This reduces judge-training entanglement and makes the reported gains more likely to represent real capability improvements rather than judge-gaming.

- **Factor fidelity**: The dynamic factors `α_j` are computed from training-judge verdicts because those are the verdicts that GRPO sees. Using evaluation-judge verdicts for the factors would create a mismatch—the factors would reflect variance in the evaluation judge's scoring while GRPO optimizes against the training judge's scoring. Since the two judges agree ~91% of the time but differ on specific verdicts, using the training judge for both the reward and the factor computation keeps the signal internally consistent.

The evaluation rubric metric is always the **static weighted aggregation** `R_scalar` normalized to 0–100% (for MM) or the official HealthBench scoring script (for HB). This is independent of POW3R's dynamic factors—the evaluation target is the same regardless of which reward construction was used during training. This means POW3R cannot win by changing the evaluation metric; it must genuinely improve the policy's performance under the static rubric that defines success.

#### Design Choices Rationale

**Why reweight within categories rather than globally?** A global reweighting (e.g., `α_j` applied across all criteria without category normalization) would let a category with many contrastive criteria (e.g., Visual Reasoning) absorb training pressure from a category with mostly saturated criteria (e.g., Writing Style). This would train the policy to optimize primarily for the dimension that happens to be hardest right now, potentially neglecting dimensions that are equally important in the final evaluation. Category normalization is the mechanism that guarantees every quality dimension receives equal attention at the category level regardless of its internal contrast. The paper's category definitions are semantically meaningful (crafted by domain experts during rubric authoring), so preserving their balance is an explicit design requirement.

**Why the standard deviation rather than a learned importance weight?** One could imagine training a small network to predict `α_j` from the prompt, criterion text, and rollout statistics—or using the policy's loss gradient itself to infer which criteria are learnable. POW3R explicitly avoids this complexity. The standard deviation of binary verdicts is a non-parametric, calibration-free, computationally trivial statistic that directly measures the quantity of interest (does this criterion produce contrastive gradient?). It requires no additional model, no hyperparameter tuning beyond the pipeline constants, and no risk of the meta-learner itself overfitting or introducing bias. The paper argues that this simplicity is a feature: the mechanism is inspectable, the factors are directly interpretable (`α_j = 1.2` literally means "this criterion gets 20% more within-category pressure than its static weight would give it"), and the computation adds negligible overhead to the training loop (a few array operations per epoch on `G × N` matrices that are already in memory from the judge verdicts).

**Why `α_min = 0.67` rather than 0?** If saturated and dead criteria had `α_j = 0`, they would receive zero weight in the reward, effectively removing them from the training objective. This creates two problems. First, criteria can transition from dead to mixed as the policy improves—a criterion the policy never passes in epoch 10 might become passable in epoch 50 after the policy has improved on related criteria. If its factor had been driven to zero, POW3R would never notice this transition because the criterion's verdicts would never enter the reward computation. The floor `0.67` ensures it retains enough weight that when the policy starts occasionally satisfying it, the observed variance `v_j` becomes non-zero, `ρ_j` rises, and the EMA can raise `α_j` back toward 1.0. Second, even a saturated criterion serves as a regularizer—it penalizes responses that regress on previously-mastered behaviors. Eliminating it from the reward would allow the policy to forget easy criteria with no penalty. The floor keeps a learning signal alive for maintaining already-acquired capabilities.

**Why `α_max = 1.5` rather than a higher ceiling?** If a single criterion could get `α_j → ∞`, it would dominate its category's reward contribution, and the policy would optimize almost exclusively for that one criterion while neglecting other criteria in the same category. The ceiling bounds the distortion so that even the most contrastive criterion gets at most `w_j × 1.5` effective weight in its category. Combined with the category normalization, this means a category with one very-contrastive criterion and several saturated ones will shift pressure toward the contrastive one, but not to the exclusion of the saturated ones. The specific value `1.5` is an empirical choice—the paper does not provide an ablation over `α_max`, but the value sits in the regime where the dynamic range (max 2.25:1 between most and least emphasized criteria in a category) is large enough to matter for training efficiency but small enough to avoid over-optimization on any single criterion.

**Why `β_ema = 0.2` rather than 1.0 (instantaneous) or 0.01 (very slow)?** Instantaneous updating (`β_ema = 1.0`) would make the factors track the current batch's noise, causing the reward construction to change substantially between epochs even when the policy's underlying capability is stable. This would add noise to the GRPO advantage signal and potentially destabilize training. Very slow updating (`β_ema = 0.01`) would make the factors respond too slowly to genuine capability changes—by the time the factor adjusts to reflect that a previously-dead criterion has become mixable, the policy might have already moved past the regime where that signal is most useful. `β_ema = 0.2` gives a half-life of roughly 3 epochs: after ~3 epochs, the factor has moved about halfway from its old value to the new steady-state implied by current variance. This is fast enough to track learning progress (a criterion that saturates over 10-15 epochs will see its factor decline smoothly over that window) but slow enough that single-epoch flukes average out.

**Why `λ = 0.5` for the blend toward the uniform prior?** This is directly connected to the noisiness of variance estimates from `G = 16` samples. A criterion with true pass rate `0.5` (maximal variance `0.25`) has a binomial sampling distribution over 16 trials: its observed `v_j` will have mean `0.25 × (15/16) ≈ 0.234` and standard deviation roughly 0.06. Across many criteria in a category, the ratios `ρ_j` will therefore have a spread driven partly by true contrast differences and partly by sampling noise. `λ = 0.5` shrinks all `ρ_j` estimates halfway toward 1, which is the right Bayesian-inspired regularization under a prior that all criteria are equally contrastive. The choice is not ablativly justified in the paper, but it reflects a standard bias-variance tradeoff: more regularization (higher `1 − λ`) gives more stable factors but less responsiveness to true contrast differences.

**Why the `⌈0.75G⌉` validity threshold?** This is a conservative filter: if more than 25% of rollouts produce invalid or missing judge verdicts for a criterion, the variance estimate from the remaining samples may be biased (e.g., if the judge fails only on long or complex responses, and those responses also tend to fail the criterion). The threshold ensures that factors are only updated when the judge data is mostly complete. The paper does not report how often this threshold is triggered in practice, but it is a standard robustness pattern in systems that depend on external API calls.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Decomposing Rubric Importance into Evaluation Target vs. Training Signal — And Showing They Decouple

The paper's most fundamental intellectual move is not a new algorithm but a **diagnostic reframing** that changes what the field considers the "reward design problem" in rubric RL. Prior to this work, the dominant mental model treated a rubric's human-assigned weights as the natural answer to two questions simultaneously: *what should the final model care about?* and *what should the policy optimize toward during training?* The static weighted sum—multiply each criterion's binary verdict by its importance weight and sum—was the obvious operationalization because it encoded both answers in one vector. If a criterion was important for evaluation, it seemed self-evident that it should also receive proportionally large training pressure.

POW3R's Section 1 diagnostic demonstrates that this conflation is not just theoretically imprecise but **empirically wasteful at scale**. Across two frozen base policies and two datasets, 45–51% of within-category training pressure routes to criteria whose pass rate is either 0 (dead) or 1 (saturated)—criteria that add a constant to every rollout's reward and therefore **cancel out of the group-relative advantage** that GRPO uses to produce gradients. The diagnostic further shows that this waste is uncorrelated with human-assigned weight: roughly half of the highest-weight criteria carry zero variance, meaning the criteria that domain experts judged most important are precisely the ones the optimizer cannot learn from in the current training regime. Figure 1a visualizes this directly—high-weight criteria in the `|w_j| ≥ 5` tier still contain substantial saturated and dead mass—and Figure 1b quantifies the consequence: under static aggregation, 8–12 percentage points of within-category training pressure are wasted on zero-signal criteria.

This finding is significant beyond the specific numbers because it **reconceptualizes rubric design as a two-stage problem**. The first stage is *evaluation definition*: a fixed rubric expressing what a good answer looks like, with human weights encoding non-negotiable relative importance. The second stage is *training signal design*: how to guide the current policy toward that fixed evaluation target, where the optimal pressure allocation depends on what the policy can currently learn, not just what matters in the final answer. The two stages have different objectives and different optimal solutions, but prior work collapsed them into one static aggregation. By separating them conceptually—and showing empirically that this separation matters—POW3R opens a design space that did not previously exist in the rubric-RL literature.

This reframing connects to a broader challenge in reinforcement learning that the field has addressed in other contexts but not in rubric-based post-training: the **scalarization problem**. In multi-objective RL, it is well-known that fixed linear scalarization routes learning effort poorly when objectives have different difficulties or when their learnability changes over time. But rubric RL differs from standard multi-objective RL in a crucial way that makes the standard solutions inapplicable: the objectives (rubric criteria) are not in competition—every criterion must eventually be satisfied—and the weight vector is not a preference knob to be optimized over but a fixed evaluation target authored by domain experts. POW3R's framing—unalterable evaluation objective, learnability-dependent training pressure, within-category reallocation only—is therefore a genuinely new problem formulation that does not map cleanly onto existing multi-reward RL taxonomies. The paper's contribution is identifying that this formulation is both necessary (because the static aggregation demonstrably wastes gradient budget) and tractable (because rollout-level variance provides a simple, calibration-free signal for which criteria are currently teachable).

The diagnostic itself is a **portable tool**, not a one-off analysis. The method—sample a rollout group from a frozen policy, judge every criterion on every rollout, compute per-criterion pass rates and variances, and measure what fraction of within-category pressure goes to zero-variance criteria—can be applied to any rubric-RL setup with any base policy and any judge. It requires no training, no modification to the RL pipeline, and answers in a single forward pass whether static aggregation is leaving gradient efficiency on the table for a given model/dataset/judge combination. The paper's Figure 2 shows that the diagnostic signal is consistent prompt-by-prompt, not just in aggregate, making it a practical pre-training check that practitioners can run before committing to a specific reward design.

---

### Innovation 2: Category-Level Invariance as the Constraint That Enables Safe Adaptation

Many adaptive reward-weighting schemes exist in the multi-objective RL and multi-reward RLHF literatures—dynamic scalarization, Pareto-seeking algorithms, per-dimension normalization in GDPO—but they share a common property: they change what the optimizer is optimizing *toward*, not just how it routes learning effort along the way. If a dimension becomes harder, the optimizer may shift attention away from it, implicitly accepting worse performance on that dimension in the final policy. This is appropriate when objectives genuinely trade off (speed vs. accuracy, helpfulness vs. harmlessness) and the practitioner wants to explore the Pareto frontier.

Rubric RL has the opposite property: every criterion is a hard requirement, and the human weights define non-negotiable relative importance. The optimizer **cannot be allowed to abandon a category** just because its criteria are currently hard or saturated. POW3R's key design constraint—the category-normalized baseline `R_cat` and the within-category-only reweighting in `R_dyn`—is what makes adaptive weighting safe in this setting. The paper is explicit that category-level pressure stays uniform: each populated category contributes exactly `1/K_q` to the scalar reward regardless of how POW3R redistributes pressure among its constituent criteria. A category full of saturated easy-criteria (like Writing Style for a competent base policy) still gets equal weight with a category full of mixed hard-criteria (like Visual Reasoning), preventing the policy from optimizing for the hard dimension at the expense of the easy one.

This constraint is not an implementation detail—it is the **intellectual framework that distinguishes POW3R from prior adaptive-weighting work**. RuCL (Chen et al.) dynamically reweights a global fixed rubric set across training according to a capability curriculum, but it changes *which criteria matter most at each stage* globally, which could shift attention between quality dimensions. Multi-reward GRPO normalizers like GDPO decouple reward dimensions so they contribute equally to the advantage, but they do not preserve human-assigned importance weights as a prior—the normalization itself changes the effective weight vector. POW3R's category-level invariance means the human-authored importance hierarchy is preserved as the asymptotic target; the adaptive reweighting only changes the *path* the policy takes to get there, not the *destination*.

The evidence that this constraint works comes from two places. First, the per-category validation reward trajectories in Figure 5 show that POW3R improves on **every** rubric category throughout training, not just the hard ones—the framework does not sacrifice saturated categories to focus on contrastive ones. Second, the external VLM benchmark results in Table 2 show that POW3R preserves or slightly improves scores on six held-out benchmarks (HallusionBench, POPE, MM-IFE, MMVetV2, MathVista, RealWorldQA) despite never training against them directly, confirming that the policy is not overfitting to the rubric judge's idiosyncrasies by chasing only the most contrastive criteria at the expense of general capability.

This insight—that **safe adaptive weighting requires invariant category mass and a static human-weight prior**—has implications beyond POW3R's specific implementation. It provides a design principle for any adaptive reward scheme in settings where the evaluation target is fixed and multi-dimensional: reallocate pressure *within* semantic groups that are themselves held at fixed relative importance, use the static weights as a multiplicative prior so the importance ordering is preserved, and bound the dynamic range so no single criterion can hijack its category. The paper does not claim POW3R's specific variance-to-factor pipeline is the only way to implement this principle, but it establishes the principle itself as the contribution worth adopting.

---

### Innovation 3: Rollout-Level Variance as a Sufficient, Calibration-Free Learnability Signal

A natural question when designing an adaptive reward scheme is: *how should we measure which criteria are currently learnable?* Prior adaptive-weighting work in multi-objective RL has used learned value functions, policy gradient magnitudes, or hand-designed curriculum schedules to infer which objectives need attention. These approaches introduce additional models, hyperparameters, or domain expertise requirements, making them complex to implement and fragile to misconfiguration.

POW3R's third contribution is demonstrating that **the rollout-level variance of binary judge verdicts**—a statistic that requires no additional model, no learning, no calibration, and negligible compute beyond what the GRPO loop already produces—is sufficient to drive meaningful improvements in training efficiency and final policy quality. The variance `v_j = p_j(1 − p_j)` directly measures whether a criterion's verdicts differ across the rollout group, and in GRPO's group-relative advantage formulation, only criteria with non-zero variance can contribute non-zero gradient. The statistic is therefore a perfect match between what POW3R measures and what GRPO needs: high variance means the criterion can teach, low variance means it cannot, and the standard deviation provides a natural continuous scale between these extremes.

What makes this finding intellectually distinctive is not the statistic itself—`p_j(1 − p_j)` is the variance of a Bernoulli, known for centuries—but the **demonstration that this statistic, computed from only `G = 16` rollouts per epoch, noisy, and smoothed by a simple EMA, produces a training signal that is both stable enough to not destabilize GRPO and responsive enough to track the policy's changing capability profile**. The paper could have used more sophisticated signals: a learned meta-critic that predicts criterion learnability from the policy's hidden states, a running estimate of the policy gradient norm per criterion, or a hand-tuned curriculum based on criterion difficulty. Instead, it shows that the simplest possible signal—does this criterion's pass rate vary across the group?—is not just adequate but outperforms the static baseline by 2.5–4× in training efficiency.

The mechanism check in Figure 2 provides the empirical backbone for this claim. Each gray point represents one prompt under one diagnostic run, plotting the relationship between *weight moved off no-signal criteria* and *reward spread gain*. The positive trend holds prompt-by-prompt in every run, confirming that reallocating pressure toward contrastive criteria causes the pre-standardization rollout reward spread to widen. This matters because GRPO's advantage `Â_i = (R_i − mean)/std` is zero for the entire group when `std(R) = 0` (all rollouts have identical reward). By widening the spread, POW3R reduces the frequency of zero-gradient batches, extracting useful updates from prompt groups that the static aggregation would have wasted. The 25–37% wider reward spreads reported in Figure 1c are a direct, measurable mechanism through which the variance-based reweighting improves training, not an indirect correlation.

The portability of this signal is also worth emphasizing. The variance computation requires only the per-criterion judge verdicts that any rubric-RL pipeline already produces—it is a byproduct of reward judging, not an additional cost. It works with any judge model, any rubric format, and any base policy. It requires no ground-truth labels, no human annotation beyond the rubric weights already authored, and no retraining if the judge or the policy changes. This makes POW3R's mechanism closer to a **training-time hyperparameter schedule** than to a learned component—it adapts to the current policy's performance profile automatically, without needing to be trained or tuned per-task, because the variance statistic is a property of the data, not a learned function of the state.

### Innovation 4: Separating Training and Evaluation Judges as a Systematic Decontamination Strategy

The paper's two-tier judge design—GPT-5.4-nano (91.4% reference agreement) for training rewards and dynamic factor computation, GPT-5.4-mini (93.6% reference agreement) for held-out evaluation—is presented as a cost decision in Section B, but it represents a more fundamental methodological move: **using a cheaper, slightly less accurate judge for the optimization signal while reserving a more accurate judge exclusively for evaluation, so the policy never sees the evaluation judge's verdict distribution during training.**

This is not standard practice in the rubric-RL literature. Prior work on rubric-based rewards typically uses a single judge for both training and evaluation, or at most uses a single judge tier with different sampling parameters. The cost motivation is real—Section B reports a ~12× cost difference between mini and nano per 1,000 judgments, and training epochs require millions of judge calls—but the **decontamination** motivation is methodologically deeper. When the same judge model scores both training rollouts and evaluation rollouts, the policy can learn to exploit that specific judge's biases: phrasings that trigger favorable verdicts, patterns that game the judge's reasoning style, or criteria that the judge systematically over- or under-scores. The resulting policy may show high evaluation scores without genuinely satisfying the rubric criteria under a different judge.

By using a different, higher-quality judge for all reported results, POW3R creates a **judge-independent evaluation bar** that the training signal cannot directly target. The policy is optimized against GPT-5.4-nano's verdict distribution; the reported gains are measured against GPT-5.4-mini's verdict distribution. An improvement in reported rubic reward therefore represents a genuine improvement in the policy's outputs that transfers across judge models, not an artifact of overfitting to a particular judge's quirks. This is particularly important for POW3R because the dynamic factors themselves are computed from training-judge verdicts—if the same judge were used for evaluation, the factors might route training pressure toward criteria where the training judge's variance is high but the evaluation judge's is not, creating a spurious alignment that inflates reported gains.

The fact that POW3R shows consistent gains across this judge gap (winning 24 of 30 base-policy/metric comparisons, with the largest gains on the visually hardest categories in Figure 5) strengthens the claim that the improvements are real capability gains rather than judge-gaming. The paper does not ablate the judge separation to quantify its contribution to the reported numbers, but the design choice itself reflects a methodological sophistication that the rubric-RL field would benefit from adopting more broadly. As LLM judges become cheaper and more diverse, the two-tier approach—use the cheapest acceptably-accurate judge for the high-volume training signal, reserve the most accurate affordable judge for the low-volume evaluation signal—is a principled template for cost-quality-decontamination tradeoffs.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The paper uses two datasets. MM is a custom 10k-task multimodal dataset authored by the paper's contributors, pairing images (charts, diagrams, photos, screenshots, natural scenes) with prompts and rubric sets spanning six quality categories; it is split into 8K training, 1K development, and 1K test tasks, with 65% single-turn and 35% multi-turn prompts. HB is HealthBench [6] restricted to English-language prompts, with native physician-authored point-valued criteria; the paper uses HealthBench's 500-task hard subset as the test split and a separate 10% slice of the remaining English training prompts as the dev split. The paper chose these datasets because they expose criterion-level categories and static importance weights rather than only a single outcome label—existing rubric-RL datasets did not simultaneously provide complex images, prompt-specific categories, static weights, and sufficient scale.

- **Base model(s).** Three vision-language policies for MM: Qwen3-VL-4B-Instruct, Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct, and Gemma 3 4B-IT. Three text-only policies for HB: Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507, Qwen3-8B, and Gemma 3 4B-IT. The Section 1 diagnostic additionally uses the larger Gemma 3 12B-IT and Qwen3-VL-4B-Instruct to verify that findings are not specific to a single base model. The models span two families (Qwen and Gemma) and two scales (4B and 8B/12B), chosen to test whether the method's gains generalize across architectures and sizes.

- **Metrics.** Two primary metrics are reported for MM: **mean rubric reward**, the static weighted aggregation `R_scalar` from Section 3.2 normalized to 0–100%, applied uniformly across all five reward constructions so the evaluation target is held fixed; and **strict completion**, the fraction of prompts whose response satisfies every criterion flagged as required in the rubric (no partial credit). For HB, the paper reports **overall score** using HealthBench's official scoring script with its native signed point values, and **strict completion** defined as perfect-score responses. The paper also reports **per-category mean pass rate** for MM's six rubric categories and **external VLM benchmark scores** (HallusionBench, POPE, MM-IFE, MMVetV2, MathVista, RealWorldQA) to check that POW3R does not overfit to the rubric judge. Evaluation metrics are always computed using the held-out evaluation judge (GPT-5.4-mini), never the training judge.

- **Baselines.** Four reward constructions, all using the same rubric set, judge interface, and GRPO recipe, differing only in how rubric criterion verdicts are aggregated into a scalar reward per rollout: (i) **Base model**: the un-trained checkpoint with no RL, serving as the no-RL reference; (ii) **Binary**: `R_binary(o; q) = 1`[every required criterion passes] on MM and `R_binary(o; q) = 1`[HealthBench scorer = 1] on HB, representing the exact-answer-style RLVR baseline; (iii) **Static scalar**: the standard prior-work weighted sum `R_scalar` from Section 3.2; (iv) **Category-balanced**: the static category-balanced reward `R_cat` from Equation (3). (v) **POW3R dynamic**: `R_dyn` from Equation (8). Each reported trained setting averages three completed runs under the same split, decoding, and evaluation protocol.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** All methods run under the same GRPO hyperparameters (G = 16 rollouts per prompt-group, sampling temperature T = 1.0, maximum completion length 3584 tokens, KL coefficient β = 0.1, clip range ε = 0.2, learning rate 3 × 10⁻⁷, constant LR schedule, per-device batch size 1 with 4 gradient-accumulation steps) for up to 664 GRPO steps on one node with 8× H100 GPUs under DeepSpeed ZeRO-3 in BF16 with gradient checkpointing. The training and evaluation judges are queried per rubric criterion (per-prompt, per-rollout, per-criterion calls) at T = 1.0 with up to 2048 completion tokens. Compute fairness is guaranteed by design: every method uses the same number of judge calls, the same number of rollouts per step, the same optimizer hyperparameters, and the same evaluation checkpointing schedule (every 83 steps). The only difference is the arithmetic that combines judge verdicts into a scalar reward per rollout before GRPO standardization.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** No cross-validation is reported for the main results. The development split (1K tasks for MM, 10% slice for HB) is used for validation reward monitoring and checkpoint selection (val-best per method), but the test split evaluations in Tables 2 and 3 appear to be single-pass evaluations at the best validation checkpoint. Each trained setting averages three completed runs, which provides some estimate of training variance, though the paper does not report standard deviations, confidence intervals, or statistical significance tests on any metric. The Section 1 diagnostic does not use the test set; it samples rollout groups from the frozen base policies on the training prompts.

### Main Quantitative Results

The paper organizes results around three axes: (1) comparison of POW3R against baselines on held-out rubric metrics (Tables 2 and 3, Figure 4); (2) training efficiency measured by steps to reach validation reward thresholds (Table 4) and validation reward trajectories (Figures 5 and 6); and (3) a mechanistic analysis showing where POW3R's reweighting concentrates (Figures 1 and 7) and how it affects reward spread (Figures 1c and 2). The first two axes constitute the main quantitative claims; the third is covered in Section 6.4 and connects the mechanism to the observed outcomes.

#### Rubric Reward and Strict Completion

**Multimodal dataset (MM), Table 2.** Across the three vision-language base policies (Qwen3-VL-4B-Instruct, Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct, Gemma 3 4B-IT) and six metrics (rubric reward, strict completion, plus six external VLM benchmarks), POW3R achieves the best score on 18 of 18 rubric-reward and strict-completion comparisons, and 6 of 18 external VLM benchmark comparisons. The remaining 12 external VLM cells are split among the baselines, with no consistent winner.

For **Qwen3-VL-4B-Instruct**: POW3R reaches 48.8% rubric reward (vs. 47.9% for category-balanced, 47.1% for static scalar, 42.8% for binary, 42.6% base) and 20.2% strict completion (vs. 18.7%, 17.9%, 14.6%, 14.7%). The gap between POW3R and category-balanced is +0.9 pp on rubric reward and +1.5 pp on strict completion. On external benchmarks, POW3R scores 62.4% HallusionBench (category-balanced: 62.5%), 89.1% POPE (static scalar: 89.2%), 13.5% MM-IFE (category-balanced: 13.0%), 52.2% MMVetV2 (category-balanced: 51.8%), 73.2% MathVista (category-balanced: 72.8%), and 73.1% RealWorldQA (category-balanced: 72.8%). POW3R does not sweep every external benchmark—category-balanced wins HallusionBench and static scalar wins POPE—but the differences are within 0.1–0.5 pp, well within the noise floor of benchmark evaluation.

For **Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct**: POW3R reaches 51.6% rubric reward and 22.6% strict completion, vs. 50.4%/21.6% for category-balanced, 49.5%/20.7% for static scalar, 47.0%/18.3% for binary, 46.8%/18.4% base. External benchmark pattern is similar: POW3R wins 2 of 6, with the other 4 having different winners by margins ≤0.5 pp.

For **Gemma 3 4B-IT**: POW3R reaches 37.8% rubric reward and 10.3% strict completion, vs. 36.7%/9.4% for category-balanced, 35.8%/8.5% for static scalar, 33.9%/7.6% for binary, 34.1%/7.7% base. External benchmarks: POW3R wins 4 of 6, with margins up to 0.5 pp.

Two patterns are consistent across all three base policies. First, the **ordering** of the four trained reward constructions is `Rdyn > Rcat > Rscalar > Rbinary ≥ base` on both rubric reward and strict completion, with the gaps between adjacent methods typically 0.5–1.5 pp. Second, the external VLM benchmark scores are **largely invariant** to the reward construction—no method consistently outperforms others, and differences are typically within 1 pp of the base model—indicating that POW3R's gains on rubric metrics do not come at the expense of general VLM capability, nor do they derive from overfitting to the rubric judge's idiosyncrasies in a way that corrupts broader vision-language skills.

**HealthBench (HB), Table 3.** Across three text-only base policies (Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507, Qwen3-8B, Gemma 3 4B-IT) and two metrics (overall score and strict completion), POW3R achieves the best score on 6 of 6 overall-score comparisons, and 1 of 3 strict-completion comparisons.

For **Qwen3-4B**: POW3R reaches 32.7% overall score (∆ +4.7 pp over base's 28.0%) and 5.4% strict completion. Category-balanced reaches 30.5% (∆ +2.5 pp) and 4.6% strict; static scalar reaches 29.6% (∆ +1.6 pp) and 3.8% strict; binary reaches 27.8% (∆ −0.2 pp) and 4.0% strict. POW3R's overall-score gain is the largest in the table, and it also achieves the highest strict completion.

For **Qwen3-8B**: POW3R reaches 31.5% overall (∆ +3.7 pp) and 4.6% strict; category-balanced reaches 30.7%/4.8%; static scalar 29.9%/4.5%; binary 27.9%/4.4%. Here POW3R is best on overall score but trails category-balanced on strict completion by 0.2 pp—the smallest possible non-tie margin.

For **Gemma 3-4B**: POW3R reaches 28.1% overall (∆ +3.8 pp) and 2.7% strict; category-balanced 27.1%/2.6%; static scalar 25.4%/2.8%; binary 24.2%/2.2%. POW3R is best on overall score but trails static scalar on strict completion by 0.1 pp.

The overall-score ordering is `Rdyn > Rcat > Rscalar > Rbinary` across all three HB base policies, matching the MM pattern. The strict-completion picture is noisier—POW3R wins on Qwen3-4B (5.4% vs. 4.6% for the next best) but loses by tiny margins on the other two policies—reflecting that strict completion is a sparse metric (only a small fraction of prompts achieve it) and small sample-size effects can dominate. The overall-score gains over base (∆) are +3.7 to +4.7 pp for POW3R, +2.5 to +2.9 pp for category-balanced, and +1.1 to +2.1 pp for static scalar, establishing a clear gradient of improvement from the simplest to the most adaptive aggregation.

**Two-objective dominance, Figure 4a.** The paper places each method in a plane with rubric reward on the vertical axis and strict completion on the horizontal axis for each of the three MM base policies. POW3R is the top-right endpoint of every base-policy line—it Pareto-dominates all other reward constructions on both objectives simultaneously. This means no other method achieves higher rubric reward without lower strict completion, or vice versa. The base and binary methods cluster in the lower-left; static scalar and category-balanced lie on a diagonal toward the upper-right; POW3R extends furthest in both directions. This joint dominance addresses a natural concern about adaptive weighting—that emphasizing contrastive criteria might inflate average rubric scores at the expense of all-criteria success—by showing that the reverse is true: POW3R's pressure reallocation improves both metrics relative to baselines.

**Cross-setting consistency, Figure 4b.** The ∆ vs. base plot shows rubric-reward gain over the corresponding base model for each of the three reward constructions, separately for MM (three vision-language base policies) and HB (three text-only base policies). The ordering `Rdyn > Rcat > Rscalar > 0` holds in all six setting/base-policy combinations. On MM, POW3R gains are +6.2 pp (Qwen3-VL-8B), +4.8 pp (Qwen3-VL-4B), +3.7 pp (Gemma3-4B). On HB, POW3R gains are +4.7 pp (Qwen3-4B), +3.7 pp (Qwen3-8B), +3.8 pp (Gemma3-4B). The smallest POW3R gain across all six combinations is +3.7 pp, and the largest single-baseline gain (category-balanced over static scalar) is +2.5 pp. The consistency across modalities (multimodal and text-only) and model families (Qwen and Gemma) argues against dataset- or model-specific explanations for POW3R's advantage.

#### Training Efficiency

**Steps-to-threshold, Table 4.** The paper reports the first training step at which each reward construction crosses fixed validation reward thresholds on the Qwen3-VL-4B / MM setting. Thresholds are percentages on the validation split's rubric reward (same metric as the test split in Table 2, but evaluated on the 1K-task dev split). The thresholds tested are 45.0, 46.0, 47.0, 48.0, 49.0, and 50.0. Results:

- **Threshold 45.0**: Static scalar reaches at step 249, category-balanced at step 166, POW3R at step 83. Speed-up: 3.0× over static scalar, 2.0× over category-balanced.
- **Threshold 46.0**: Static scalar at 249, category-balanced at 332, POW3R at 83. Speed-up: 3.0× over static scalar, 4.0× over category-balanced.
- **Threshold 47.0**: Static scalar at 415, category-balanced at 415, POW3R at 166. Speed-up: 2.5× over both static baselines.
- **Threshold 48.0**: Static scalar at 581, category-balanced at 581, POW3R at 166. Speed-up: 3.5×.
- **Threshold 49.0**: Neither static scalar nor category-balanced reaches this threshold within the 664-step schedule; POW3R reaches at step 249. No speed-up calculable since baselines do not reach the threshold.
- **Threshold 50.0**: Neither static baseline reaches; POW3R reaches at step 249.

POW3R is the only method to cross a 50.0 validation reward threshold within the training schedule. The speed-ups range from 2.5× to 4.0× over the static baselines, and in three cases (46.0, 48.0, 49.0+), the speed-up is infinite in a practical sense because the baselines either reach much later or never reach the threshold at all. The paper states (without providing the tables) that "the same ordering holds on the other base policies and on HB, with comparable speed-ups," and treats the per-setting numbers in Tables 2 and 3 as the canonical efficiency reference, meaning the step-count analysis is illustrative rather than exhaustive.

**Validation trajectories, Figures 5 and 6.** Figure 6 shows the overall validation reward trajectory for Qwen3-VL-4B / MM from step 0 through step 664 (validation logged every 83 steps, giving checkpoints at steps 83, 166, 249, 332, 415, 498, 581, 664, though the paper reports up to step 501 in the figure). POW3R separates from both static aggregations by the first logged checkpoint (step 83) and remains above them through the end of training. The gap widens in the middle of training (steps 166–415) and narrows slightly at the end as all methods converge toward their respective plateaus. Category-balanced and static scalar track each other closely, with category-balanced consistently above static scalar by a small margin. The binary reward never moves above base and is omitted from the figure.

Figure 5 breaks the validation reward into per-category trajectories for the six MM rubric categories. POW3R leads on every category throughout training, with the gap opening at the first checkpoint (step 83). The largest absolute gaps between POW3R and the static baselines appear on Content, Instruction Following, Truthfulness, Visual Perception, and Visual Reasoning—the categories that the paper's diagnostic shows have the most contrastive signal (see Figure 7). On Writing Style, POW3R still stays above the static baselines, but the three curves stay within roughly a point of each other because most Writing Style criteria are already passed by the base policy and offer little contrastive signal for POW3R to act on. This per-category consistency addresses the concern that POW3R might over-optimize on a single high-contrast category at the expense of others—the evidence shows improvement across all categories, with the magnitude of improvement roughly proportional to how much contrastive signal the category provides.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

The paper's ablation and robustness analysis is distributed across the main text (Figures 1, 2, 5, 7), the appendix (judge calibration in Section B), and the per-category breakdowns. Unlike the prior example paper, this paper does not contain a dedicated "Ablation Studies" section with systematically varied hyperparameters. The following items represent the closest equivalent—checks that isolate components of the method or test the stability of the diagnostic.

- **Reward spread widening as a mechanism check (Figures 1c and 2).** Figure 1c reports prompt-level reward spread (standard deviation of rollout rewards before GRPO standardization) for static vs. POW3R aggregation, measured on the two diagnostic base policies (Qwen3-VL-4B-Instruct and Gemma 3 12B-IT) on both MM and HB. Mean spread widens by 25% (MM-Qwen), 30% (MM-Gemma), 34% (HB-Qwen), and 37% (HB-Gemma) under POW3R. Figure 2 shows the prompt-by-prompt relationship between "weight moved off no-signal criteria" and "reward spread gain" across all four diagnostic runs, with a consistent positive trend. This confirms that POW3R's within-category reallocation genuinely translates into fewer prompt groups with `std(R) = 0` (zero-gradient batches), which is the local mechanism through which more informative rewards should improve GRPO training.

- **Pressure shift from zero-signal to contrastive criteria (Figure 1b).** The paper defines "training pressure" as a criterion's within-category reward-weight share. Averaged over the two diagnostic base policies per setting, POW3R reduces pressure on zero-signal (saturated + dead) criteria by 8 percentage points on MM and 12 pp on HB relative to static aggregation. This reallocated pressure flows to mixed criteria, increasing their weight share by corresponding amounts. The magnitude is substantial—roughly one-sixth to one-quarter of the total pressure wasted under static aggregation is redirected under POW3R—and the direction confirms that the algorithm does what it is designed to do.

- **Per-category signal-to-action alignment (Figure 7).** For the Qwen3-VL-4B POW3R-dynamic run, the paper computes two per-category averages over the full training schedule: the mean rollout variance `v_j` (the contrastive signal POW3R has to work with) and the within-category standard deviation of the dynamic factors `α_j` (how much POW3R differentiates criteria inside that category). The two co-vary: Writing Style sits lowest on both (`v_j ≈ 0.080`, `std(α_j) ≈ 0.060`), the visual and reasoning categories sit at the top of `v_j`, and the ordering of `std(α_j)` tracks `v_j` to within rounding. This confirms that POW3R's reweighting is proportional to the contrastiveness each category exposes, not driven by an arbitrary or pathological feature of the algorithm.

- **Two-tier judge separation as implicit robustness check.** The fact that POW3R's gains are measured by the evaluation judge (GPT-5.4-mini, 93.6% agreement with reference) while training used a different judge (GPT-5.4-nano, 91.4% agreement, ~12× cheaper) serves as an implicit robustness check against judge overfitting. If POW3R's dynamic factors were simply amplifying the training judge's noise or biases, the gains would not transfer to the evaluation judge. The consistent gains across this judge gap (including 4.7–6.2 pp rubric reward improvements and external VLM benchmark preservation) argue that POW3R captures genuine learnability signal that generalizes across judge models.

- **Base model and dataset diversity as robustness check (Tables 2 and 3, Figure 4b).** The paper tests POW3R across six base-policy/dataset combinations (three models × two datasets). The ordering `Rdyn > Rcat > Rscalar > Rbinary` holds in all six, with the smallest POW3R gain over base being +3.7 pp. No single model or dataset drives the aggregate results—the pattern is consistent whether the base policy is Qwen or Gemma, 4B or 8B, multimodal or text-only. This is not a true ablation (it tests external validity rather than internal mechanism), but it serves the function of showing the method does not depend on idiosyncrasies of a particular architecture or data distribution.

- **No hyperparameter sensitivity analysis.** The paper does not ablate `α_min`, `α_max`, `λ`, `β_ema`, or `G`—the key hyperparameters that control POW3R's dynamic range, responsiveness, and regularization. Values are stated in Table 6 but never varied. This is a significant gap: we do not know whether the results are sensitive to these choices, whether simpler configurations (e.g., `λα_max = 1`, `β_ema = 1`, or `α_min = 0`) would perform similarly or better, or whether the specific pipeline is over-engineered relative to the problem's needs.

- **No ablation of POW3R components (EMA vs. instantaneous, blend vs. raw ratio, category normalization vs. global).** The paper reports only the full POW3R pipeline against the static baselines. Intermediate designs—e.g., instantaneous variance factors without EMA, raw `ρ_j` without the `λ` blend toward 1, or global reweighting without category normalization—are not evaluated. This makes it impossible to attribute the gains to specific design choices. For instance, Figure 7 suggests that category-level reweighting is the key to safe adaptation, but the paper never ablates category normalization to show that removing it hurts; it only compares against the static `R_cat` (which has normalization but no adaptation) and `R_scalar` (which has neither).

- **No ablation of the `⌈0.75G⌉` validity threshold or the `ϵ = 10⁻⁴` floor.** These are safety mechanisms to handle incomplete judge data and numerical edge cases. The paper does not report how often the validity threshold is triggered or whether `ϵ` affects results.

- **No comparison to non-variance-based adaptive schemes.** The paper compares against static baselines but not against alternative adaptive weighting methods—e.g., weighting criteria by their gradient norm, using a learned meta-controller, or applying a fixed curriculum. The absence of these comparisons means we cannot assess whether POW3R's specific variance-to-factor pipeline is more effective than simpler alternatives, or whether any adaptive scheme that reallocates pressure away from zero-variance criteria would achieve comparable gains.

- **ReST^EM revision model analog (not applicable).** Unlike the prior example paper (which tested a revision model with ReST^EM training that backfired), this paper does not have a comparable "attempted optimization that degraded performance" ablation in the appendix. The three-run averaging provides some estimate of training stability but not of method robustness under systematic perturbation.

### Critical Assessment

#### Claim 1: POW3R improves both mean rubric reward and strict completion over binary, static-scalar, and category-balanced rewards in rubric-RL GRPO training.

**The evidence supports this claim with appropriate specificity.** Tables 2 and 3 report head-to-head comparisons across 30 base-policy/metric cells (18 rubric-reward + strict-completion cells in Table 2, 12 overall-score + strict-completion cells in Table 3), and POW3R achieves the best score in 24 of 30. The ordering `Rdyn > Rcat > Rscalar > Rbinary` holds in all MM rubric-reward and strict-completion columns, all HB overall-score columns, and 5 of 6 HB strict-completion columns (the exceptions are two 0.1–0.2 pp margins in favor of a static baseline). The two-objective dominance in Figure 4a shows POW3R as the Pareto-optimal point in the rubric-reward/strict-completion plane for all three MM base policies.

**What limits this claim:** The gains, while consistent, are modest in absolute magnitude. On MM, POW3R improves rubric reward by +0.9–1.2 pp over category-balanced and +1.7–2.2 pp over static scalar. On HB, the overall-score gains are +0.8–2.2 pp over category-balanced and +2.1–4.7 pp over static scalar. These are real improvements but they represent single-digit percentage-point differences on metrics that range from ~28% to ~52% across settings. The paper does not report variance across the three completed runs (standard deviations or confidence intervals), so we cannot assess whether these differences are statistically reliable or within the noise floor of training variance. Given the small test sets (1,000 tasks for MM, 500 for HB), it is possible that some of the finer-grained comparisons (e.g., POW3R vs. category-balanced on HB strict completion, where margins are 0.1–0.2 pp) are not statistically distinguishable.

**What would strengthen it:** Reporting standard deviations across runs, computing confidence intervals, and using paired-bootstrap or permutation tests to establish whether observed differences are significant at the available sample sizes. Additionally, testing on larger test sets or multiple random test-set splits would reduce the noise floor and make small-magnitude claims more credible.

#### Claim 2: POW3R reaches the same performance plateau in 2.5–4× fewer training steps.

**The evidence supports a narrower version of this claim.** Table 4 demonstrates speed-ups of 2.5–4.0× for Qwen3-VL-4B on MM when measuring steps to fixed validation reward thresholds (45.0 through 50.0). The paper states without showing data that "the same ordering holds on the other base policies and on HB, with comparable speed-ups." The speed-up is defined as `(step_baseline_reaches_threshold) / (step_POW3R_reaches_threshold)`, which is a meaningful efficiency metric.

**What limits this claim:** The speed-up analysis is reported for only one of the six base-policy/dataset combinations. We cannot verify whether the 2.5–4× range generalizes to the other five settings, or whether the speed-ups are larger or smaller in those cases. Additionally, the speed-up depends on the specific threshold chosen: at threshold 46.0, POW3R shows a 4.0× speed-up over category-balanced (83 steps vs. 332), but at threshold 47.0, the speed-up over the same baseline is only 2.5× (166 steps vs. 415). The 2.5–4× range thus aggregates speed-ups at different thresholds and against different baselines; reporting a single "typical" range obscures this variability.

**What would strengthen it:** Providing speed-up tables for all six base-policy/dataset combinations, ideally at a standardized set of thresholds (e.g., 80%, 90%, 95% of final POW3R performance) rather than absolute reward thresholds that may be differently reachable across settings. Also, clarifying whether "reaches the same performance plateau" means POW3R's final performance equals or exceeds the baselines' final performance—at threshold 49.0 and 50.0, the baselines never reach the plateau within the 664-step schedule, so the comparison is not "POW3R reaches the same level faster" but rather "POW3R reaches a level the baselines cannot reach in the allotted budget," which is a stronger claim about final quality, not just speed.

#### Claim 3: Human-assigned criterion importance and current policy learnability decouple in rubric RL, with 45–51% of training pressure routing to criteria that produce no gradient signal.

**The evidence in Figure 1 supports this claim robustly.** The diagnostic is run on four (model, dataset) combinations (Qwen3-VL-4B and Gemma 3 12B on MM; Qwen3-VL-4B and Gemma 3 12B on HB) and finds consistent patterns: 17–26% saturated, 20–33% dead, totaling 37–59% non-contrastive criteria depending on the combination, with 45–51% as the typical range after accounting for the within-category pressure allocation. Figure 1a visualizes this directly across static-weight tiers, showing that high-weight criteria carry substantial dead and saturated mass. Figure 1b quantifies the pressure shift when moving from static to POW3R aggregation, confirming that 8–12 pp of pressure is reallocated.

**What limits this claim:** The diagnostic is computed on frozen base policies, not on policies mid-training. The paper does not show how the saturated/dead/mixed distribution evolves as training progresses—it is possible that after some GRPO steps under static aggregation, the criterion variance landscape shifts, and the 45–51% figure becomes an overestimate (if the policy saturates more criteria) or an underestimate (if the policy unlocks previously dead criteria). The diagnostic also uses a larger rollout group (the paper samples "rollout groups" for the diagnostic without specifying `G`, though the method description says "we sample rollout groups on 1,300 prompts" in Section 1—this is likely `G = 16` per the training config but is not explicitly stated for the diagnostic). Finally, the claim is about training pressure allocation under static aggregation, but the diagnostic does not actually train: it computes pressure from a single forward pass. A full training run under static aggregation would reveal whether the pressure allocation shifts over time, and whether the waste problem self-corrects (as saturated criteria become mixed if the policy regresses) or compounds (as more criteria saturate with training).

**What would strengthen it:** Tracking the pressure allocation over the course of a full GRPO training run under static aggregation, showing whether the 45–51% waste is a snapshot problem or a persistent one. Also, computing the diagnostic at multiple training checkpoints to show how the landscape evolves.

#### Claim 4: POW3R's mechanism—reallocating within-category pressure toward criteria that distinguish rollouts—wides the pre-standardization reward spread and reduces zero-gradient batches.

**The evidence in Figures 1c and 2 supports the local mechanism.** The 25–37% wider average reward spreads under POW3R in Figure 1c, and the prompt-by-prompt positive correlation between pressure moved and spread gain in Figure 2, confirm that POW3R's reweighting translates into more informative GRPO advantages at the batch level. The mechanism is sound: by removing weight from criteria that add the same constant to every rollout's reward, POW3R ensures that the remaining criteria' variance drives the reward differences, widening `std(R)` and reducing the probability that all rollouts are tied.

**What limits this claim:** The reward spread is measured on the diagnostic runs (frozen base policies, single forward pass), not during actual training. The paper does not report reward spread statistics from the full training runs (e.g., average `std(R)` per epoch, fraction of batches with `std(R) = 0`), so we cannot verify that the spread-widening persists throughout training or that it correlates with the observed speed-ups. It is possible that early in training, the spread-widening is substantial, but as the policy improves and criteria saturate under any reward construction, the spread converges. Additionally, the causal link from wider spread to faster training is assumed but not directly tested—the paper does not ablate the spread-widening separately (e.g., by artificially inflating `std(R)` without POW3R's reweighting) to confirm it is the mechanism rather than a correlated consequence.

**What would strengthen it:** Reporting training-run `std(R)` distributions over time for each reward construction, and ideally running a control experiment where reward spread is artificially inflated (e.g., by rescaling the static reward) to see whether spread alone accounts for the speed-up, or whether POW3R's specific pressure allocation matters beyond spread.

#### Hidden weaknesses and missing experiments

**Small test sets and no statistical reporting.** MM's test set is 1,000 tasks; HB's is 500 tasks. Per-category breakdowns for MM further split these 1,000 tasks across six categories, so category-level comparisons may rest on as few as 50–300 prompts depending on the category (Table 1 reports category shares ranging from 2.8% to 32.4%, i.e., ~28 to ~324 prompts per category in the test set). The paper reports no confidence intervals, no standard deviations across the three training runs per setting, and no significance tests. The binary comparison in HB strict completion—POW3R's 2.7% vs. static scalar's 2.8% on Gemma3-4B—could easily be an artifact of evaluating 500 prompts where strict completion is a rare event (~13–14 prompts expected). The paper claims "wins 24 of 30 comparisons" as if this is a robust finding, but many of those wins are separated by margins that may not be statistically distinguishable given the sample sizes.

**No hyperparameter sensitivity analysis.** As noted in the ablation section, POW3R introduces five key hyperparameters (`α_min`, `α_max`, `λ`, `β_ema`, and the validity threshold `0.75G`) with specific values (0.67, 1.5, 0.5, 0.2, 0.75). The paper never varies any of them. We do not know whether `α_max = 2.0` would improve gains by allowing more dramatic reweighting, whether `α_max = 1.0` would eliminate gains entirely, whether `λ = 0` (no blend toward uniform prior) would make the factors too noisy and destabilize training, or whether `β_ema = 1.0` (instantaneous updating) would still work. The hyperparameter values feel like they were set once and never revisited, which is common in systems papers but limits the scientific understanding of the method's sensitivity.

**No ablation of the category-normalized baseline independently.** The paper compares `R_dyn` against `R_cat` (category-normalized, static) and `R_scalar` (raw weighted sum). The gap between `R_cat` and `R_scalar` is itself substantial in some settings (e.g., +1.6 pp on Gemma3-4B MM, +1.7 pp on Qwen3-4B HB). One could argue that the category normalization is doing most of the work, and the dynamic factors add only a small increment. The paper does not test `R_dyn` without category normalization (i.e., dynamic global reweighting), nor does it test `R_cat` with simpler adaptive schemes (e.g., binary variance gates that zero out saturated/dead criteria rather than the full EMA pipeline). This makes it hard to attribute the gains to the dynamic factors versus the category normalization alone.

**Limited external benchmark coverage.** The paper reports six external VLM benchmarks to test whether POW3R overfits to the rubric judge, but these benchmarks are all vision-language tasks; none tests text-only capability, and none tests the specific quality dimensions that the rubric measures (perception grounding, instruction following, truthfulness). A method that genuinely improves rubric-measured behaviors should show gains on benchmarks that target those same behaviors—e.g., a benchmark that specifically tests instruction-following or visual grounding. The external benchmarks serve more as a "do no harm" check than as a positive validation that rubric improvements transfer to other quality signals. The fact that scores are largely invariant across reward constructions (within ~1 pp of base) could indicate either that POW3R genuinely doesn't harm general capability, or that these benchmarks are insufficiently sensitive to the kinds of improvements the rubric captures.

**No analysis of per-criterion-level pass-rate evolution.** The paper's diagnostic shows the criterion state at a single frozen point; the per-category trajectories in Figure 5 show aggregate category-level validation reward over time; but the paper never shows how individual criteria's pass rates and variance evolve during POW3R training versus static training. Such trajectories would directly test the claimed mechanism—do previously dead criteria become mixed under POW3R, does POW3R prevent criteria from saturating prematurely, do saturated criteria under the static baseline actually stay saturated or does the policy regress on them? This analysis is feasible (the judge verdicts per criterion per rollout are available at every validation checkpoint) and would provide the strongest evidence for the learnability-signal hypothesis.

**No combination with other RLVR improvements.** The paper uses a vanilla GRPO recipe with fixed hyperparameters. Recent RLVR work has proposed various improvements to GRPO—e.g., DAPO's token-level loss, length penalties, or dynamic sampling budgets. The paper does not test whether POW3R's gains are additive with or subsumed by these improvements. Since POW3R only changes the reward aggregation, it should in principle stack with any GRPO variant, but this is untested.

**The health of the framing relative to the magnitude of results.** The paper frames rubric aggregation as a first-class training-time design choice with large efficiency implications. The 2.5–4× speed-up numbers are attention-grabbing, but the absolute performance improvements are modest (+0.8–2.2 pp over category-balanced on held-out metrics). A practitioner might reasonably ask: is the additional complexity of per-criterion variance tracking, EMA updating, and category renormalization worth +1 pp of rubic reward? The speed-up argument answers "yes" for compute-constrained settings, but the final-quality argument alone (without the efficiency framing) might not justify the method's overhead on its own. The paper does not compute the additional computational cost of POW3R's factor updates relative to the static baselines—it is negligible (a few array operations per epoch on data already in memory), but the implementation complexity (tracking per-criterion state, handling edge cases, maintaining the EMA) is non-zero. The paper's strongest case is therefore the combined argument—better final quality AND faster training—but this argument would be stronger with efficiency results on all six settings, not just one.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### Reliance on LLM Judges for Both Training Signal and Difficulty Estimation

POW3R's entire mechanism — the variance estimates that drive dynamic factor computation and the per-criterion verdicts that produce the reward — flows through LLM judges. The paper uses GPT-5.4-nano (91.4% reference agreement) for training and GPT-5.4-mini (93.6% agreement) for evaluation, calibrated against a high-effort reference judge. The authors acknowledge this dependence directly in Section 7:

> "Because both training rewards and reported scores flow through these judges, exact numbers can shift with judge, prompt template or sampling choice and POW3R's policy-aware factors are themselves estimated from judge verdicts, so any consistent verdict bias propagates into where training pressure is redirected."

**Consequence.** Any systematic bias in the training judge's verdicts — a tendency to over-score certain response patterns, under-score specific visual details, or produce noisy verdicts on particular criterion types — propagates into POW3R's dynamic factors. If the training judge systematically scores a criterion as "passed" when a more careful judge would disagree, POW3R will observe high pass rates, classify that criterion as saturated or nearly-saturated, and down-weight it — redirecting pressure away from a criterion the policy has not actually mastered. Conversely, if the judge is noisily inconsistent on a criterion that the policy consistently satisfies, the observed variance will be non-zero, and POW3R will up-weight that criterion, wasting training pressure on judge noise rather than genuine policy failure. The two-tier judge separation partially mitigates this by ensuring the evaluation judge's biases are not directly optimized against, but it does not prevent the training judge's biases from misdirecting learning in the first place.

The cost-quality tradeoff documented in Section B — nano judges are ~12× cheaper than mini judges but lose ~2 percentage points of reference agreement — means practitioners must choose between affordable training (accepting lower-quality verdicts and the associated bias in factor computation) and accurate factor estimation (paying mini-level costs for every training rollout, which may be infeasible at scale). The paper's operating points (nano for training, mini for evaluation) represent one point on this tradeoff curve, but the sensitivity of POW3R's mechanism to judge quality is unexplored. No experiment compares POW3R trained with nano verdicts against POW3R trained with mini verdicts; we do not know whether the ~2 pp agreement gap between the judges changes the dynamic factors' effectiveness, the speed-up magnitude, or the final policy quality.

**Evidence.** The judge calibration table (Section B, Table 5) quantifies agreement across judge configurations but does not measure how agreement differences translate to factor estimation quality. The main results (Tables 2 and 3) use one fixed judge pair. No ablation varies the training judge.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper flags the limitation in Section 7 but performs no sensitivity analysis. The suggestion to test "stronger and more diverse judges" in Section 8 indicates this is future work.

---

### No Hyperparameter Sensitivity Analysis

POW3R introduces five key hyperparameters that control its adaptive behavior: the variance floor `α_min = 0.67`, the ceiling `α_max = 1.5`, the blend coefficient `λ = 0.5` (shrinkage toward the uniform prior), the EMA coefficient `β_ema = 0.2` (smoothing speed), and the minimum valid rollout fraction `0.75G` (robustness to missing judge data). The paper states these values in Table 6 but **never varies any of them** in an ablation study. The entire empirical case for POW3R rests on a single hyperparameter configuration.

**Consequence.** Without ablations, we cannot answer basic questions about the method's robustness and design tradeoffs:

- **Is the dynamic range necessary, or would binary variance gating suffice?** Setting `α_min` close to 0 and `α_max` very large would effectively turn POW3R into a hard on/off switch: near-zero weight for saturated/dead criteria, amplified weight for contrastive ones. The paper's chosen range `[0.67, 1.5]` represents a specific design philosophy — preserve a learning floor for already-mastered criteria, bound the maximum distortion per criterion — but we cannot tell whether this philosophy is essential or just one of many workable choices.

- **How fast should the EMA respond?** `β_ema = 0.2` gives a half-life of ~3 epochs. If `β_ema` were 0.05 (half-life ~14 epochs), the factors would be stabler but respond slowly to capability changes; if `β_ema` were 1.0 (instantaneous), the factors would track the current batch's noise directly. The paper argues conceptually for the EMA's necessity (Section 3.4, "Why EMA rather than instantaneous updating"), but provides no empirical evidence that the smoothing helps, that the specific value 0.2 is near-optimal, or that instantaneous updating actually harms training.

- **Does the blend toward the uniform prior matter?** `λ = 0.5` means POW3R only half-trusts the rollout-level variance estimates. If `λ` were 0 (raw `ρ_j` with no shrinkage), the factors would be fully driven by observed variance; if `λ` were 1 (no adaptation), POW3R collapses to `R_cat`. The paper never tests intermediate values or the extremes.

- **Are the gains sensitive to the validity threshold?** The `0.75G` threshold for retaining previous factors when judge data is incomplete is a safety mechanism whose triggering frequency is never reported. If it rarely triggers, the choice is inconsequential; if it frequently triggers, the choice could substantially affect factor quality.

**Evidence.** None. The paper contains no hyperparameter ablation studies, no sensitivity analysis, and no discussion of how the values were chosen beyond the conceptual rationale in Section 3.4.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper treats the hyperparameter configuration as fixed and given. This is the single largest methodological gap in the experimental validation: a method whose core claims are about adaptive, data-driven pressure reallocation provides no empirical evidence about the sensitivity of its adaptation mechanism to its own tuning parameters.

### Difficulty Estimation Cost Is Not Accounted For in the Speed-Up Claim

POW3R's dynamic factors `α_j^{(t)}` require computing per-criterion pass rates and variances from the current epoch's rollout group — a computation whose inputs (the per-criterion judge verdicts) are already produced by the GRPO loop and whose cost (a few array operations per epoch on `G × N` matrices) is negligible. This is not, in itself, a hidden cost. The hidden cost is in the **difficulty estimation diagnostic** that motivates and validates POW3R's design, not in POW3R itself.

However, the paper's speed-up claim in Table 4 reports that POW3R crosses validation reward thresholds in 2.5–4× fewer training steps than static baselines. A practitioner adopting POW3R would need to verify that the method works on their specific model, dataset, and judge combination. The paper provides no guidance for how to do this verification without running the full diagnostic (sampling rollout groups from a frozen policy, judging every criterion, computing pressure allocation) — which itself requires substantial judge calls before training even begins. The cost of this pre-training verification is not included in any efficiency comparison.

**Consequence.** The 2.5–4× speed-up is measured from the start of GRPO training. If a practitioner spends, say, 25% of a full training run's compute budget on diagnostic verification before committing to POW3R, the effective speed-up over a static baseline (which requires no pre-training verification) is reduced accordingly. The paper does not discuss what fraction of the training budget the diagnostic represents, what the minimum verification cost would be for a new setting, or whether the diagnostic results from one (model, dataset, judge) triple generalize to others.

More subtly, POW3R's effectiveness depends on the base policy having a substantial fraction of non-contrastive criteria — the waste problem that the diagnostic documents. If a practitioner applies POW3R to a setting where most criteria are contrastive from the start (e.g., a very weak base policy on very hard tasks), POW3R's dynamic factors will remain near 1.0, and the method will reduce approximately to `R_cat`. The practitioner would have paid the implementation complexity cost of POW3R for no gain over the simpler category-balanced baseline. The paper provides no guidance on when POW3R's benefits are likely to materialize and when they are not, and the diagnostic is the only tool offered for making this determination — but its cost and sample-size requirements are not analyzed.

**Evidence.** The diagnostic results (Section 1, Figure 1) show that 45–51% of training pressure routes to zero-signal criteria for the tested (model, dataset) combinations. The paper argues this demonstrates the need for adaptive reweighting. But it does not show whether this fraction is typical across RLVR settings in general, or whether it is specific to the combination of base policies at the tested capability level. A much stronger base policy might have 80% saturated criteria (making POW3R more beneficial), while a much weaker one might have 5% (making POW3R unnecessary). The paper does not characterize the relationship between base policy strength and POW3R's marginal benefit.

**Mitigation status.** Partially acknowledged. The paper notes in Section 7 that "broader open availability of rubric datasets with static weights would let the community measure the limits of policy-aware aggregation more directly," implicitly recognizing that the current evidence base is narrow. But the cost-of-verification problem is not discussed.

### Small Sample Sizes and No Statistical Reporting

All experimental results are reported as single-number percentages with no standard deviations, confidence intervals, or significance tests. The test sets are relatively small: 1,000 tasks for MM, 500 for HB HealthBench hard subset. The rubric-reward and strict-completion results in Tables 2 and 3 report differences between methods at the level of 0.1–2.2 percentage points, but the paper provides no way to assess whether these differences are statistically reliable or within the noise floor of finite-sample evaluation.

**Consequence.** Several of the paper's headline comparisons may not be statistically distinguishable given the sample sizes:

- The MM test set has 1,000 tasks. A 0.9 pp difference in rubric reward (POW3R vs. category-balanced on Qwen3-VL-4B: 48.8% vs. 47.9%) corresponds to 9 tasks out of 1,000. With three training runs averaged per method (as the paper states), the standard error of the mean depends on the run-to-run variance, which is not reported. If the standard deviation across runs is, say, 0.5 pp, the 0.9 pp gap is less than two standard errors and may not be significant.

- The HB strict completion comparisons are even more fragile. For Gemma3-4B on HB, POW3R's strict completion is 2.7% vs. static scalar's 2.8% — a difference of 0.1 pp on 500 prompts, meaning 0.5 prompts out of 500. The paper reports this as static scalar "winning" but a single different judge verdict on one prompt in one run would flip the ordering.

- The "24 of 30 comparisons won" framing aggregates across metrics and settings where the margins range from ~0.1 pp to ~6 pp. Counting wins without weighting by margin or statistical confidence overstates the robustness of the finding. A fairer summary might be "POW3R achieves the highest numerical score in 24 of 30 cells, with margins ranging from 0.1 to 6.2 percentage points, and no statistical testing performed."

- The per-category breakdowns shown in Figure 5 split the 1,000 MM test tasks six ways, with category shares from Table 1 ranging from 2.8% (Writing Style) to 32.4% (Visual Perception). The Writing Style category thus represents ~28 test prompts. Category-level comparisons on such small slices are inherently high-variance, and the paper's claim that POW3R "leads on every rubric category throughout training" (Figure 5) rests on aggregate validation reward computed from a few dozen prompts per category.

**Evidence.** Tables 2 and 3 report no error bars. The paper states each trained setting "averages three completed runs" but never reports the standard deviation, range, or any distributional information about run outcomes. Checkpoint selection is "val-best per method" (Table 6), which introduces an optimistic bias — the single best-performing checkpoint out of up to 8 validation checkpoints (logged every 83 steps over 664 steps) is selected for test-set evaluation, and this selection is performed independently per method. Methods with noisier training trajectories (which could include POW3R due to its dynamic factor updates adding variance) get a larger selection advantage than methods with smoother trajectories.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper provides no statistical analysis, no discussion of sample size limitations, and no power analysis for the observed effect sizes.

### Generalization Limited to Two Datasets and Two Model Families

POW3R is tested on exactly two datasets — a custom multimodal dataset (MM) and HealthBench English (HB) — and two model families (Qwen3 and Gemma 3). The paper acknowledges this scope limitation in Section 7:

> "Public rubric-RL datasets that combine prompt-specific criteria, integer human weights, multiple high-level categories, and held-out scale are rare. We therefore authored MM in-house and used HealthBench for the text-only setting. These choices leave POW3R untested on domains such as long-form coding feedback, scientific writing, or multilingual instruction following, which can have very different category structures and saturation patterns."

**Consequence.** POW3R's mechanism depends on rubric structure — specifically, the existence of semantically meaningful categories with multiple criteria per category, integer human-assigned weights, and a base policy whose capability profile produces a mix of saturated, dead, and mixed criteria. The paper provides no evidence about what happens when these structural conditions are violated or different:

- **Different category semantics.** The six MM categories (Visual Perception, Visual Reasoning, Content Completeness, Instruction Following, Truthfulness, Writing Style) have a natural difficulty ordering — visual perception tends to be harder for VLMs than writing style — which helps POW3R because contrastive and saturated criteria tend to cluster in different categories. If the categories were more homogeneous (e.g., all criteria in all categories are roughly equally difficult for the base policy), POW3R's within-category reweighting would have less room to operate — most criteria in a category would have similar variance, `ρ_j` would be near 1.0 for all `j`, and `R_dyn` would approximately equal `R_cat`. The paper does not test on datasets without this difficulty heterogeneity.

- **Different weight scales.** MM uses integer weights in `{1, ..., 5}`; HealthBench uses signed integer point values. The paper's good-behavior conversion for HB (Appendix A.4) maps signed weights to absolute values for training, effectively treating penalty and reward criteria symmetrically at training time while leaving evaluation unmodified. Whether this conversion works well for other signed-weight rubrics, or whether alternative conversions (e.g., scaling weights by sign, using separate penalty categories) would be better, is unexplored.

- **Different numbers of criteria per prompt.** MM has mean 8.4 criteria per task (Table 1). If prompts had far fewer criteria (e.g., 2–3), the within-category variance estimation would be based on very few data points per category, making the `ρ_j` ratios noisier and the dynamic factors less reliable. If prompts had many more criteria (e.g., 20–30), the category structure might become unwieldy, and POW3R's per-prompt category normalization (each populated category gets exactly `1/K_q` weight) might dilute pressure across too many dimensions.

- **Single-turn vs. multi-turn.** MM is 65% single-turn and 35% multi-turn (Table 1). The paper does not break out results by turn count, so we do not know whether POW3R's benefits differ between single-turn and conversational settings. Multi-turn evaluation introduces additional complexities (context accumulation, error propagation across turns) that the paper does not address.

- **Model scale.** The base policies tested range from 4B to 12B parameters. The paper does not test on smaller models (e.g., 1B parameters, where the base capability might be so low that most criteria are dead, giving POW3R little variance to exploit) or on larger models (e.g., 70B+, where the base capability might be so high that most criteria are saturated, also leaving little variance). The relationship between base policy strength and POW3R's marginal benefit is not characterized.

The paper argues that consistency across two model families and two modalities "suggests that POW3R is not exploiting one dataset's rubric convention" (Section 6.1), which is true as far as it goes, but two datasets and two model families do not establish broad generalizability. The rubric-RL literature is nascent, and we have little understanding of how rubric structure, category semantics, and base policy capability interact with adaptive reward schemes.

**Evidence.** The main results (Tables 2 and 3) cover two datasets and two model families. The per-category trajectories (Figure 5) and signal-to-action analysis (Figure 7) are reported for only one setting (Qwen3-VL-4B on MM). The paper does not report per-category results or speed-ups for Gemma3-4B or for HealthBench.

**Mitigation status.** Acknowledged in Section 7 as a limitation, with future work suggested on "domains such as long-form coding feedback, scientific writing, or multilingual instruction following." No mitigation is attempted within the paper.

---

### No Combination with Revisions, Search, or Other Test-Time Compute Strategies

The paper studies rubric reward aggregation for GRPO in isolation — it changes only how per-criterion judge verdicts are combined into a scalar reward before GRPO standardization. But the paper's own intellectual framing positions rubric RL as a natural extension of RLVR into multi-dimensional quality, and the broader RLVR literature demonstrates that test-time compute strategies (search against verifiers, iterative revisions, majority voting) can substantially amplify the gains from RL post-training. POW3R is never tested in combination with any of these strategies.

**Consequence.** The absolute performance levels achieved by POW3R — e.g., 48.8% rubric reward for Qwen3-VL-4B on MM, 32.7% overall for Qwen3-4B on HB — leave substantial room for improvement, and the paper provides no evidence on whether POW3R's gains are additive with, subsumed by, or orthogonal to other post-training improvements:

- **Best-of-N or majority voting at inference time.** All results in Tables 2 and 3 use a single sampled response per evaluation prompt (standard GRPO evaluation). If the evaluation protocol instead sampled multiple responses and used majority voting or best-of-N weighted by the rubric reward, the absolute scores would likely be higher, and the ordering between reward constructions might change. POW3R trains the policy to produce better individual responses; whether those responses are also more complementary (producing better ensemble performance) or more correlated (producing redundant ensemble gains) is unknown.

- **Search against the rubric during training.** The paper's GRPO implementation samples `G = 16` rollouts independently and uses outcome supervision (scalar reward broadcast to all tokens). Recent work on process reward models and step-level search during RL training shows additional gains on reasoning tasks. POW3R's rubric reward could serve as the outcome signal for such search, but the interaction between process-level credit assignment and POW3R's pressure reallocation is unexplored.

- **Iterative revision or self-refinement.** If the policy generates an initial response, receives rubric feedback, and revises its response accordingly (similar to the revision model in the paper's reference example), the rubric reward structure would change: the first attempt might fail certain criteria, the revision might fix them, and the final reward would reflect both attempts. POW3R's per-criterion variance estimation would need to account for this sequential structure — a criterion that every first attempt fails but every revision passes would show high contrast on the first attempt and low contrast on the revision, requiring different factors per revision step.

The paper's rigorous separation of the reward aggregation problem from the RL algorithm is a strength for isolating POW3R's contribution, but the flip side is that the reported numbers represent a **lower bound** on what rubric RL could achieve with both better reward aggregation and better optimization algorithms. A practitioner wanting to maximize rubric performance would combine POW3R with the best available post-training recipe, but the paper provides no guidance on which combinations are likely to be complementary versus redundant.

**Evidence.** The paper uses a single GRPO configuration throughout (Table 6). No experiments combine POW3R with alternative RL algorithms, inference-time strategies, or self-improvement loops. The related work section discusses multi-reward GRPO (GDPO), dynamic reward weighting for multi-objective RL, and multimodal RLVR with perception rewards, but none of these are tested as baselines or combined with POW3R.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not acknowledge this as a limitation, and Section 8's future work suggestions focus on "stronger and more diverse judges, longer training schedules, and adaptive rubric generation" rather than on combining POW3R with complementary RL improvements.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

POW3R introduces a **conceptual reframing**, not a paradigm shift. It does not propose a new RL algorithm, a new judge architecture, or a new class of reward functions. Instead, it identifies a previously invisible design choice—how rubric criteria are aggregated into a scalar for GRPO—and shows that treating this choice as a training-time optimization target rather than a fixed consequence of evaluation yields consistent, measurable improvements. The magnitude is that of a well-executed **methodological correction**: the paper demonstrates that 45–51% of training pressure under standard rubric aggregation is wasted on criteria that produce zero gradient signal, and that reallocating this pressure via rollout-level variance yields 2.5–4× faster training and small but consistent gains in final quality (+0.9–2.2 pp over the next-best static baseline on held-out metrics).

This reframing matters because it addresses a blind spot in how the field has adopted rubric-based rewards. The RLVR community has invested heavily in scaling GRPO to larger models, longer training horizons, and more diverse tasks, but has treated the rubric-to-scalar aggregation step as an implementation detail—multiply by human weights, sum, and move on. POW3R's diagnostic demonstrates that this step is a **first-class design choice with large efficiency implications**, analogous to how the Chinchilla scaling laws revealed that model size and data quantity could not be chosen independently during pretraining. The practical consequence is that any practitioner running GRPO with rubric rewards should, at minimum, run a pressure-tracking diagnostic to check whether their static aggregation is wasting gradient budget—and should strongly consider adopting some form of adaptive within-category reweighting.

**What changes in the research conversation:**

- **Rubric design becomes a two-stage problem.** Prior to POW3R, the community's rubric-design workflow implicitly assumed that a single set of weights served both evaluation and training purposes. POW3R makes explicit what some practitioners likely suspected: the weight vector that defines "what a good answer looks like" is not the same as the weight vector that optimally guides the policy toward that answer. This creates a new design axis—training-time aggregation—that is decoupled from evaluation and can be optimized independently. Future rubric authoring pipelines can now separate the specification of (a) the evaluation rubric (fixed, human-authored, non-negotiable) from (b) the training rubric (adaptive, data-driven, designed to maximize learning efficiency), with POW3R's mechanism as one candidate implementation of the latter.

- **The scalarization problem becomes visible in rubric RL.** The multi-objective RL literature has long known that fixed linear scalarization can route learning effort poorly, but those insights were developed for settings where objectives genuinely compete. POW3R shows that even in the cooperative setting—where every criterion must eventually be satisfied and the weight vector encodes non-negotiable importance rather than a preference trade-off—static aggregation wastes gradient budget because it ignores criterion-level learnability. This connects rubric RL to a broader intellectual tradition while also establishing its distinctiveness: the solution space for cooperative scalarization (preserve the evaluation target, reweight only within semantic categories, bound the dynamic range) is different from the solution space for competitive scalarization (explore the Pareto frontier, adapt to preference changes). POW3R provides a concrete template for the cooperative case that subsequent work can refine.

- **Diagnostic-first methodology gains a foothold.** The paper's Section 1 diagnostic—sample a rollout group, judge every criterion, measure pressure allocation—is a portable, non-invasive check that any rubric-RL practitioner can run before committing to a reward aggregation strategy. This represents a methodological improvement over the current default (pick static weighted sum, train, evaluate, hope) by inserting a cheap information-gathering step that can reveal whether adaptive reweighting is likely to help. The paper's Figure 2 shows that the diagnostic signal is consistent prompt-by-prompt, not just in aggregate, making it a practical pre-training tool. This diagnostic-first approach parallels the "loss-per-token" and "attention-pattern" diagnostics that the pretraining community uses to catch issues before committing to full training runs, and POW3R provides the first such diagnostic specifically for rubric-based RLVR.

**What research directions become more attractive:**

- **Adaptive within-category reweighting as a plug-in component.** POW3R's architecture—a reward-aggregation wrapper that sits between the judge and GRPO, requires no optimizer changes, and adds negligible compute overhead—makes it straightforward to integrate into existing RLVR pipelines. The paper's design choices (within-category only, category-mass invariant, human-weight as multiplicative prior, bounded dynamic range) provide a recipe that other methods can adopt, extend, or challenge. This opens a new sub-area: *training-time rubric aggregation strategies*, where researchers compete to design better variance-to-factor mappings, better category structures, or better prior preservation mechanisms.

- **Criterion-level learning dynamics as a first-class object of study.** POW3R's per-criterion pass rates and variance estimates open a window into what the policy is actually learning at each stage of training. The paper's per-category trajectories (Figure 5) show that different quality dimensions improve at different rates, but the paper does not dive into per-criterion-level dynamics. Future work that tracks individual criteria's pass rates, variance, and contribution to the advantage over the course of training could reveal patterns—do criteria cluster into "easy-first" vs. "hard-first" learning phases? Do certain criterion types (e.g., explicit vs. implicit, Table 1) benefit more from adaptive reweighting? Does POW3R cause the policy to learn criteria in a different order than static aggregation?

**What research directions become less attractive:**

- **Global adaptive weighting for rubric RL.** POW3R's key design constraint—category-level mass remains uniform, reweighting happens only within categories—is motivated by the requirement that the evaluation target not drift. Methods that reweight criteria globally (e.g., by estimated difficulty, by gradient magnitude, or by a learned meta-controller) would violate this constraint and risk the policy optimizing for contrastive categories at the expense of saturated ones. The paper's evidence that category-level invariance preserves performance on all categories (Figures 5 and 7) while global static weighting demonstrably wastes pressure (Figure 1a) suggests that the research effort should focus on within-category mechanisms rather than global schemes. This does not rule out global approaches entirely—a global scheme that preserves human-authored importance ratios across categories could theoretically work—but POW3R sets a high bar by showing that within-category reweighting alone already recovers most of the available efficiency gains.

- **Binary or sparse rubric rewards as the default.** The paper's binary baseline consistently underperforms even the static scalar reward (Tables 2 and 3), confirming that collapsing multi-dimensional rubric quality into a single all-or-nothing bit destroys useful training signal. Combined with the finding that static aggregation wastes nearly half of training pressure, the binary baseline's poor performance argues that the right question is not "should we use rich rubric signal?" but "how should we optimally aggregate rich rubric signal?" Future rubric-RL work should treat binary rewards as a lower bound, not a competitive baseline, and focus on aggregation methods that preserve the criterion-level structure that rubrics provide.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Per-criterion pass-rate evolution during POW3R training vs. static training.** The paper's Section 1 diagnostic captures a single frozen snapshot of criterion states; the per-category validation trajectories in Figure 5 show only aggregate category-level reward. A direct test of POW3R's claimed mechanism would track individual criteria's `p_j`, `v_j`, and `α_j` trajectories over the full training schedule, comparing POW3R-dynamic against category-balanced and static-scalar runs. Specifically: do previously-dead criteria (pass rate ~0) under static aggregation transition to mixed (0 < pass rate < 1) faster under POW3R? Does POW3R prevent premature saturation of criteria that the static policy saturates early but then regresses on? Are POW3R's dynamic factors `α_j` actually tracking the policy's changing capability—do they rise when the policy starts occasionally passing a criterion, and fall when it reliably saturates one? The data for this analysis already exists in the training logs (the judge calls produce per-criterion verdicts at every epoch); it requires only that the experimenter save and analyze them. A strong follow-up would also correlate criterion-level trajectories with criterion metadata (explicit vs. implicit, objective vs. subjective, weight tier, category) to characterize which criterion types benefit most from adaptive reweighting. A null result—POW3R's factors `α_j` are essentially constant at 1.0 throughout training for most criteria—would severely undermine the claimed mechanism and suggest the gains come from a different source (e.g., the category normalization in `R_cat` alone, or improved reward spread from reduced constant-offset criteria).

**Hyperparameter sensitivity analysis for POW3R's dynamic factor pipeline.** The paper introduces five key hyperparameters—`α_min = 0.67`, `α_max = 1.5`, `λ = 0.5`, `β_ema = 0.2`, and the valid-verdict threshold `0.75G`—and never varies any of them. A systematic ablation would test: (a) `α_max ∈ {1.0, 1.5, 2.0, 4.0, ∞}` to determine whether larger dynamic range improves or destabilizes training; (b) `α_min ∈ {0.0, 0.33, 0.5, 0.67, 1.0}` to test whether a learning floor for saturated/dead criteria is necessary, and if so, how large; (c) `λ ∈ {0.0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1.0}` to quantify the value of shrinkage toward the uniform prior, with `λ = 0` (raw `ρ_j`, no regularization) and `λ = 1` (collapse to `R_cat`) as the extremes; (d) `β_ema ∈ {0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.5, 1.0}` to map the stability-responsiveness tradeoff, with `β_ema = 1.0` (instantaneous factors, no smoothing) as the critical test of whether the EMA is necessary; and (e) the validity threshold `∈ {0.5, 0.75, 1.0}` to test robustness to missing judge data. A strong follow-up would run this grid on at least one (model, dataset) combination (Qwen3-VL-4B on MM, the setting where the paper already provides speed-up data) and report both final held-out rubric reward and training speed-up for each configuration. The key negative result would be finding that POW3R's gains are highly sensitive to one or two hyperparameters (e.g., `λ` must be exactly 0.5, or gains vanish), which would cast doubt on the method's robustness and practical deployability. Alternatively, finding that gains are largely invariant across a wide range (e.g., `α_max ∈ [1.2, 4.0]` all work similarly) would strengthen the claim that the variance-to-pressure mechanism, not the specific hyperparameter tuning, drives the improvement.

**Combining POW3R with process-level reward models or step-level search.** The paper uses outcome supervision—a scalar rubric reward per complete rollout, broadcast to every token. Recent RLVR work on math and code reasoning has shown that process reward models (PRMs) providing per-step correctness signals, combined with step-level search (beam search, lookahead search) during training, yield substantial gains over outcome-only supervision. POW3R's rubric reward provides a natural multi-dimensional outcome signal; combining it with a PRM that scores individual response steps on the same rubric criteria would give the policy per-step feedback on which reasoning steps satisfied which criteria and which steps caused criterion failures. This would be particularly interesting for the multimodal setting, where visual perception criteria might fail early in the response (misidentifying an object) and cascade into visual reasoning failures later. A strong follow-up would: (a) train a rubric-based PRM using Monte Carlo rollouts from the base policy, scoring each step against each rubric criterion; (b) use this PRM during GRPO training to provide per-step advantages rather than outcome-only advantages; and (c) measure whether POW3R's dynamic reweighting of rubric criteria at the outcome level also improves the PRM's training signal at the step level. The paper's finding that POW3R improves Visual Perception and Visual Reasoning categories (Figure 5)—exactly the categories where early perception errors cascade into later reasoning errors—suggests that per-step rubric feedback could further amplify POW3R's gains by catching perception mistakes before they propagate.

**Testing POW3R on rubrics without pre-defined categories.** POW3R's within-category reweighting depends on the existence of semantically meaningful rubric categories that partition the criteria into groups. The paper's MM dataset has six categories authored by domain experts; HealthBench has its own native category structure. Many real-world rubric pipelines—especially those generated automatically via LLM-based rubric elicitation or synthetic pipelines—may not have clean category labels. What happens when POW3R is applied to a flat list of criteria with no categories? One option is to treat the entire list as a single category, in which case POW3R reduces to global dynamic reweighting (all criteria compete for pressure, categories are not balanced). The paper's category-invariance principle suggests this would underperform because high-contrast categories (e.g., Visual Reasoning) would absorb pressure from low-contrast categories (e.g., Writing Style), but the empirical severity of this issue is unknown. A strong follow-up would compare: (a) POW3R with expert-authored categories, (b) POW3R with LLM-generated categories (clustering criteria by semantic similarity), (c) POW3R with random categories (to test sensitivity to category quality), and (d) POW3R with a single global category (no category balancing). The results would reveal how much of POW3R's gain comes from the category structure itself versus from the variance-based reweighting, and would provide practical guidance for rubric authors who do not have pre-existing category labels.

**Applying POW3R to code generation, scientific writing, or multilingual instruction following.** The paper tests two datasets—multimodal VQA and English health advice—and acknowledges that "long-form coding feedback, scientific writing, or multilingual instruction following" are untested. These domains have potentially very different rubric structures and saturation patterns. For code generation, rubric criteria might include functional correctness, code style, documentation quality, efficiency, and test coverage—categories where saturation patterns could be very different (e.g., functional correctness might be bimodal: either passes all tests or fails dramatically, with little mixed signal; code style might be mostly saturated for a capable base model). For multilingual instruction following, the category structure would interact with language-specific capability—a model strong in English but weak in Swahili would show saturated criteria in the former and dead criteria in the latter, creating a systematic category imbalance that POW3R's within-category reweighting would not address (since language is not a rubric category, but a property of the prompt). A strong follow-up would test POW3R on at least one of these domains, running both the Section 1 diagnostic (to check whether the 45–51% pressure-waste finding generalizes) and a full GRPO training comparison (to measure whether POW3R's speed-up claims hold). A negative result—POW3R shows no gain over `R_cat` on, say, a code-generation rubric—would establish a boundary condition for the method and suggest that domain-specific rubric structure (not just criterion-level variance) is a prerequisite for POW3R's effectiveness.

**Learning to predict criterion learnability from criterion text and prompt metadata.** POW3R's per-criterion variance `v_j` is computed from actual rollout verdicts, requiring judge calls at every epoch for every criterion. This is operational during training (the judge calls are already being made for the reward computation), but it means POW3R cannot predict *in advance* which criteria will be contrastive for a new prompt or a new base policy. A natural extension is to train a lightweight classifier that takes the criterion text, the prompt, and the base policy's embedding of the prompt as input and predicts whether the criterion is likely to be saturated, dead, or mixed—without requiring any rollout samples. This would enable: (a) pre-allocation of training budgets to prompts where POW3R is likely to help (prompts with many saturated/dead criteria get POW3R; prompts where most criteria are already mixed can use static aggregation), (b) curriculum design where training starts with prompts predicted to have high contrastive-signal density, and (c) zero-shot transfer of POW3R's dynamic factors to new prompts or policies without re-running the diagnostic. The training data for such a classifier would come from POW3R's own training logs: for each prompt and each criterion, the observed `p_j` and `v_j` at various training checkpoints provide labeled examples of "this criterion was saturated/mixed/dead at this training stage." A strong follow-up would compare the classifier-guided POW3R against the original POW3R on a held-out prompt set, measuring whether predicted factors produce comparable training efficiency to measured factors.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Cost-efficient rubric-RL training for organizations with limited compute budgets.** The paper's efficiency results—POW3R reaches the same validation reward thresholds in 2.5–4× fewer GRPO steps than static baselines on Qwen3-VL-4B / MM (Table 4)—translate directly to cost savings. In the paper's setup, each GRPO step requires `G × (average criteria per prompt) × (number of prompts per batch)` judge calls, which at the nano-judge tier costs $0.12 per 1,000 judgments. A full 664-step training run processes roughly 8,000 training prompts × 16 rollouts × 8.4 criteria = ~1.1 million judge calls per epoch, but with batch sampling only a fraction of prompts are used per step. Even with these high-throughput numbers, reducing the required number of steps by 3× means cutting the total judge-call budget by roughly two-thirds and the GPU-hours by a similar fraction. For small research labs or startups post-training models on domain-specific rubric datasets, this efficiency gain can make the difference between a feasible training run and one that exceeds their compute budget. The paper's finding that the speed-up ordering holds across model families (Qwen, Gemma) and modalities (vision, text) suggests this benefit generalizes, though per-setting validation is advised. A pragmatic adoption strategy: (1) run the Section 1 diagnostic on a frozen base policy to confirm that 30%+ of training pressure is wasted under static aggregation; (2) if confirmed, adopt POW3R with the paper's hyperparameter defaults; (3) monitor per-category validation reward trajectories to catch any category where POW3R underperforms `R_cat` (the paper's Figure 5 shows this did not happen in their setting, but monitoring is cheap insurance).

**Improving visual grounding in multimodal RLVR pipelines.** A known weakness of outcome-only RLVR for vision-language models is that models can "reason past the image"—producing text that happens to align with the expected answer format without actually attending to visual evidence. The rubric-based approach addresses this by including explicit visual perception and visual reasoning criteria that require the model to engage with image content. POW3R amplifies this benefit by routing training pressure toward the visual criteria that are currently hardest for the policy. The per-category validation trajectories in Figure 5 show POW3R leading the static baselines on Visual Perception and Visual Reasoning throughout training, with the gap opening at the first logged checkpoint. Table 2's external VLM benchmarks show POW3R's trained policies preserving or slightly improving on HallusionBench (visual hallucination detection), POPE (object hallucination), and MMVetV2 (integrated vision-language capability) relative to static baselines. For practitioners building multimodal systems where visual fidelity is critical—medical image analysis, autonomous driving perception, remote sensing, document understanding—POW3R with a perception-heavy rubric provides a training recipe that demonstrably improves visual grounding without sacrificing other quality dimensions. The key implementation requirement is that the rubric must include visually-grounded criteria with sufficient weight to matter (MM's Visual Perception and Visual Reasoning categories together constitute 58.4% of criteria; Table 1), and the base policy must have a non-trivial gap between its current visual capability and ceiling (so that the criteria are in the mixed or dead regime, not already saturated).

**Adaptive data filtering or prompt selection for targeted post-training.** Organizations often post-train models on curated datasets where some prompts are more valuable training examples than others. POW3R's per-criterion variance signal provides a natural prompt-level quality score: prompts with many high-variance criteria under the current policy are rich training examples (the policy produces diverse, distinguishable responses, so GRPO advantages will be informative), while prompts where all criteria are saturated or dead produce flat rewards and zero gradient. By filtering or weighting the training prompt distribution to favor high-variance prompts, practitioners can further increase training efficiency beyond what POW3R's reward reweighting alone achieves. This is particularly relevant for large rubric datasets where the cost of judging every prompt at every epoch is high—prompts can be pre-screened using a fast pass (a single rollout group from the base policy, judged once, to estimate prompt-level variance) and low-variance prompts can be down-sampled or excluded from the training loop. The paper's Figure 2 shows that prompt-level reward spread varies substantially across prompts (the x-axis in that figure is "weight moved off no-signal criteria," which varies per-prompt), confirming that not all prompts are equally informative. A data curator could use this signal to build training batches that maximize the expected gradient information per judge-call dollar.

**Judge budget allocation between training and evaluation in two-tier judge setups.** The paper's two-tier judge design—cheap nano judge for high-volume training verdicts, more accurate mini judge for low-volume evaluation verdicts—is a cost-quality-decontamination strategy that any rubric-RL practitioner can adopt. Section B's calibration table provides concrete cost and agreement numbers: nano at $0.12/1k judgments with 91.4% reference agreement vs. mini at $1.52/1k with 93.6% agreement. The 12× cost multiplier means a training run that uses nano for every rollout can afford 12× more judge calls than one using mini—or equivalently, can train on 12× more prompts at the same judge budget. The 2 pp agreement gap between the judges is non-trivial but, per the paper's results, does not prevent POW3R's dynamic factors (computed from nano verdicts) from improving training efficiency when evaluation is measured by mini verdicts. This two-tier template extends naturally: practitioners can calibrate their own judge tiers (using their preferred LLM-as-judge models and the protocol in Section B), select the cheapest judge that preserves rank correlation with their reference judge, use it for all training verdicts, and reserve the strongest affordable judge for held-out evaluation and final model selection. The paper's finding that the `Rdyn > Rcat > Rscalar > Rbinary` ordering is stable across the nano/mini judge gap (nano used for training, mini for evaluation) suggests this strategy is robust, though practitioners should verify the ordering on their own data.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper positions POW3R primarily against static rubric aggregations (raw weighted sum and category-balanced) and the binary reward baseline, not against other adaptive weighting schemes. It does not articulate a detailed tradeoff matrix against named alternatives like GDPO, multi-objective RL dynamic scalarization, or RuCL. Rather, the paper's experimental design lets the results speak: POW3R is strictly better than the static baselines on rubric reward and strict completion across all tested settings, with no observed regression. The decision rule the paper implies is:

- **If you are using rubric-based rewards with GRPO and your criteria have category labels with human-assigned importance weights** (as in MM and HealthBench), adopt POW3R with the paper's hyperparameter defaults. The method requires no changes to the judge, the GRPO optimizer, or the evaluation protocol; it adds negligible compute overhead; and it improves both final quality and training speed across all tested (model, dataset) combinations. The paper provides no evidence of a setting where POW3R underperforms `R_cat`, and the 2.5–4× speed-ups (Table 4) make the adoption cost negative in compute-constrained regimes.

- **If you lack category labels**, either author them (the paper's MM rubric authoring process produced six categories covering perception, reasoning, content, instruction-following, truthfulness, and style), or test POW3R with a single global category (which reduces to global dynamic reweighting) and verify that the loss of category-level invariance does not cause regressions on specific quality dimensions. The paper does not test this configuration, so adoption without categories carries unknown risk.

- **If your base policy is very weak or very strong relative to the rubric**, run the Section 1 diagnostic before adopting POW3R. If most criteria are already mixed (weak policy, all criteria are partially passable), POW3R's factors will remain near 1.0 and the method reduces to `R_cat`—you get no benefit over the simpler baseline, and you may prefer `R_cat` for its implementation simplicity. If most criteria are already dead (very weak policy) or saturated (very strong policy), POW3R's reweighting range `[0.67, 1.5]` may not provide enough dynamic range to matter—the remaining mixed criteria may be too few to benefit from reweighting. The diagnostic costs a single forward pass of judge calls on a frozen policy and tells you which regime you are in.

- **If your judge budget is so tight that even the cheapest acceptable judge is expensive**, the paper's two-tier design (nano for training, mini for evaluation) provides a calibrated template, but the cost-quality tradeoff should be recalibrated for your specific judges and rubric domain using the protocol in Section B. The paper's ~12× cost multiplier between tiers is specific to GPT-5.4-nano vs. GPT-5.4-mini and may differ with other judge models or API pricing.
