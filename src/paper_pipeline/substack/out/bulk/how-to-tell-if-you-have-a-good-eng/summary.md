Here are the takeaways from this Ryan Peterman essay (a self-reflection on seven engineering managers, distilling what separated the great ones from the ones who lost their teams):

**Putting the “E” in EM**

Across all seven of his managers, technical fluency was the dividing line. When a manager deeply understands the work, every interaction—roadmapping, status checks, performance reviews—gets smoother. The clearest test is the performance review itself: the better your manager grasps *your* work, the better they can defend it in calibration rooms. This becomes existential when your impact isn’t measured by a well-known, easily-quantified metric; in those cases your manager has to supply the qualitative context—the milestones you actually hit, the quality of the engineering—and they can only do that credibly if they understand it.

Technical fluency also governs trust and retention. Ryan’s current manager was his team’s old tech lead, so the engineers trusted him immediately because “he had been in the trenches.” On the opposite end, he had a manager who regularly said things “that were not technically sound,” and that “eroded trust little by little”—a contributing factor to engineers eventually leaving the team. The pattern is stark: without technical fluency, a manager “is not an EM; they are more like a PM with some people-manager responsibilities.” It’s also the mechanism by which a good EM coaches junior engineers on fundamentals, something Ryan directly benefited from as a new engineer at Instagram when his manager coached him out of his early mistakes.

**Positioning for growth**

The best managers don’t just evaluate performance—they actively strategize a career. Because an EM controls work allocation, a manager who knows your goals can “place you in areas that have scope for you to grow into.” Ryan’s promotion to senior hinged on his ability to lead a small workstream, and since his manager knew he was motivated, he was positioned as the backend lead for a workstream despite never having led one before. That placement *was* the growth opportunity.

Positioning goes beyond project assignment. When Ryan was hunting for Staff-level scope and kept coming up short, his manager diagnosed the gap and assigned him a mentor who excelled at exactly that skill—finding and framing large-scope work. He notes, “since then, finding staff level scope hasn’t been an issue.” A good manager doesn’t just hand you scope; they figure out *why* you’re not finding it yourself and give you the strategic resource (a mentor, a stretch project, air cover) to close the gap.

**Radical transparency**

Transparency shows up most powerfully in uncomfortable conversations. Ryan’s best managers were direct about where he had gaps and clarified the performance-review process itself so there were no surprises at calibration time. Having those awkward conversations early was “worth it since it helped us avoid surprises.”

The trust-building effect went beyond individual performance. During one past reorg, a manager brought the team into the decision-making process and shared the rationale “as soon as possible.” The result was striking: the reorg “was well received and there was no attrition.” The people who were brought into the discussion actually trusted the manager *more* afterward because they could give input and agreed with the reasoning. A reorg that could have been a trust-destroying event became a trust-building one, entirely through early, candid communication.

Ryan closes with a practical litmus test: your manager is “often the single most important person at work,” and you can often judge their quality by looking at turnover rates on their team. If the team isn’t stable or you see persistent gaps in technical fluency, growth positioning, or transparency, “consider switching teams.”

---

Ryan’s framework is essentially a three-part diagnostic for the **“E” in EM**: without technical fluency the role collapses into product management with reports; without intentional **positioning for growth** a manager is just a task-router; and without **radical transparency** even a technically strong manager will bleed trust during hard moments like reorgs. The through-line is that great management isn’t soft—it’s the hard, awkward, strategic work of defending calibrations, assigning stretch leads before someone feels ready, and having the uncomfortable conversation early so there are no surprises.
