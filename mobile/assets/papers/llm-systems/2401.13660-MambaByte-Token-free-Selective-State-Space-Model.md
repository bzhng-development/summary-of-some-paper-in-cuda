# MambaByte: Token-free Selective State Space Model

**ArXiv:** [2401.13660](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.13660)

## 🎯 Pitch

This paper proposes **MambaByte**, a token-free adaptation of the Mamba selective state space model trained autoregressively on raw byte sequences, and evaluates it against state-of-the-art byte-level models (MegaByte Transformer, PerceiverAR, standard Transformer) and subword Transformers on long-form language modeling datasets including PG19, Stories, Books, ArXiv, and Code.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper proposes **MambaByte**, a token-free adaptation of the Mamba selective state space model trained autoregressively on raw byte sequences, and evaluates it against state-of-the-art byte-level models (MegaByte Transformer, PerceiverAR, standard Transformer) and subword Transformers on long-form language modeling datasets including PG19, Stories, Books, ArXiv, and Code. The key insight is that Mamba's fixed-sized memory state is independent of context length, removing the quadratic attention bottleneck that makes standard Transformers scale poorly on long byte sequences—enabling direct byte-level modeling without representational compression (i.e., no patching or hierarchical token grouping). Compared to the MegaByte-758M+262M baseline in a FLOPs-matched setting, MambaByte-353M achieves lower bits per byte across all five datasets using only 0.63× the training compute (30B vs. 80B bytes trained), while MambaByte-972M reaches a word-level perplexity of 33.0 on PG19, outperforming all prior byte-level models and matching subword state-of-the-art. For efficient decoding, the paper introduces speculative decoding with **subword drafting and byte-level verification**, where a smaller subword Mamba model drafts candidate subwords that a larger MambaByte verifier checks via parallel scan, achieving a 2.6× inference speedup over standard MambaByte generation and matching the decoding efficiency of the subword Mamba. The paper establishes that byte-level models can be practical alternatives to subword Transformers only when paired with SSM architectures that provide fixed-size memory states, as evidenced by synthetic noise experiments where MambaByte degrades far less than subword Mamba under character-level corruptions (e.g., +31.7 vs. +213.2 perplexity increase under 30% character drop).

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Tokenization as a Fundamental Inductive Bias

The paper tackles a deep architectural question in language modeling: **do we need tokenization at all?** Nearly all modern large language models — from GPT to Llama to Mamba itself — operate on subword tokens produced by algorithms like Byte-Pair Encoding (Sennrich et al., 2015) or SentencePiece (Kudo & Richardson, 2018). This tokenization step is so deeply embedded in the NLP pipeline that it is rarely questioned. Yet the paper argues, building on a line of prior work (Xue et al., 2022; Clark et al., 2022; Yu et al., 2023), that tokenization imposes a significant **inductive bias** that limits model robustness and generalization.

What does this bias look like in practice? The paper catalogs specific failure modes. Subword models are brittle to **orthographic variations** — a typo like "recieve" for "receive" can produce a completely different token sequence that the model has rarely seen during training. They struggle with **morphological variants** — "run," "running," "runs" may share some subword overlap but are treated as distinct surface forms rather than variations on a common stem. Capitalization changes ("Hello" vs. "hello"), character-level noise, and spelling perturbations all cause disproportionate degradation in subword model performance because the tokenizer's fixed vocabulary simply cannot cover the combinatorial space of possible character-level corruptions.

This is not merely an aesthetic concern. For real-world deployment — where user input is noisy, multilingual, code-heavy, or contains rare terms — tokenization brittleness translates directly into reliability problems. The paper's synthetic noise experiments (Table 4) quantify this dramatically: under a 30% character drop rate, subword Mamba perplexity degrades by +213.2, while MambaByte degrades by only +31.7. Under the "antspeak" corruption (every character capitalized and space-padded), the subword model's perplexity increases by an astonishing +58,300, while MambaByte's increases by only +28.3. These are not small differences — they represent fundamental breakdown of the subword model's ability to process corrupted text.

### The Inefficiency of Existing Byte-Level Approaches

If tokenization is so problematic, why hasn't the field simply moved to byte-level modeling? The answer lies in sequence length. Modeling text as raw bytes means that the resultant sequences are **4–8× longer** than their subword counterparts (roughly 5.9 bytes per word for English text on PG19, as shown in Table 10, vs. ~1.5 subwords per word). For autoregressive Transformers — which dominate language modeling and have attention complexity scaling as $O(L^2)$ in sequence length $L$ — this length expansion is devastating. The quadratic attention mechanism means that doubling the sequence length quadruples the computational cost per forward pass, and the memory required to cache keys and values during autoregressive generation grows linearly with sequence length. A byte-level Transformer processing 8,192 bytes of context is doing attention over roughly 4× the number of tokens that a subword Transformer would process for the same text, with 16× the attention FLOPs.

Prior work attempted to address this through **representational compression** — essentially, re-introducing tokenization by another name. The most prominent approach is **MegaByte** (Yu et al., 2023), which the paper uses as its primary baseline. MegaByte segments the input into fixed-size patches of bytes (typically 8 bytes per patch) and processes these patches with a global Transformer module, then decodes individual bytes within each patch using a local Transformer module. The model thus has a two-level hierarchy: a coarse global model operating on patches, and a fine local model operating on bytes within a patch. The computational complexity for a patch size $p$ is $O(L^2/p^2 + Lp)$, which is better than $O(L^2)$ but still superlinear in $L$ — specifically, for optimal patch size $p \propto L^{1/3}$, the complexity becomes $O(L^{4/3})$, which is still substantially worse than the $O(L)$ linear complexity that recurrent models achieve.

Other approaches include **PerceiverAR** (Hawthorne et al., 2022), which uses learned latent queries to compress the input into a fixed-size representation, and **Funnel-Transformer** (Dai et al., 2020), which progressively pools and compresses token representations across layers. The paper acknowledges these methods but identifies a fundamental tension: all compression-based approaches **change the modeling behavior to match the data**. By grouping bytes into patches or compressing representations, these models lose the granular, byte-level information that motivated token-free modeling in the first place. The compression introduces its own inductive bias — what patch size is optimal? How should patch boundaries align with linguistic units? The model designer must make choices that effectively re-engineer tokenization, just at a different level of abstraction.

The paper's experimental results (Figure 1, Figure 3) reveal an additional subtlety: patching can actually **lower model performance** compared to a standard byte-level Transformer when models are compute-matched. The MegaByte-193M+177M with patch size 8 performs worse than the Transformer-361M at the same parameter count, suggesting that the patching mechanism introduces a representational bottleneck that hurts more than the computational savings help, at least at moderate scales. The paper's key framing is that all of these approaches are **workarounds for Transformer-specific limitations** — they are architectural patches (no pun intended) that compensate for the fundamental scaling problems of attention, not principled solutions to byte-level modeling.

### The Mamba Opportunity: Fixed-Size Memory Without Compression

The paper's central motivation is that the recent **Mamba architecture** (Gu & Dao, 2023) fundamentally changes the tradeoff landscape. Mamba is a selective state space model (SSM) that maintains a **fixed-size hidden state** per layer — a matrix of size $n_{\text{state}} \times d$ where $n_{\text{state}}$ is the SSM state dimension (typically 16) and $d$ is the model dimension. Crucially, this hidden state size is **independent of context length**. Whether the model processes 1,024 bytes or 32,768 bytes, the hidden state memory is exactly the same. This is the critical architectural property that the paper exploits.

To understand why this matters, consider the conceptual parallel the paper draws. An $m$-layer Mamba model with hidden state $h(t) \in \mathbb{R}^{n_{\text{state}} \times d}$ maintains a total memory of $m \times n_{\text{state}} \times d$ floats. For MambaByte-972M with $m = 48$, $n_{\text{state}} = 16$, and $d = 1,792$, this works out to roughly 1.38 million floating-point values of memory. The model must compress its understanding of the entire context — whether subword tokens or raw bytes — into this fixed budget. The paper's key insight is that **in all but extreme cases, this fixed memory is much larger than the context length** (where context length is measured in information-theoretic terms, not raw byte count). Therefore, if Mamba can already compress subword sequences into this state effectively (as demonstrated by Gu & Dao, 2023), it should be able to compress byte sequences as well — the hidden state has sufficient capacity to absorb the longer sequence length without running out of room.

This is fundamentally different from the Transformer's situation. A Transformer's memory is the key-value cache, which grows linearly with sequence length. For byte-level modeling, this means the Transformer must explicitly store representations for each of the 8,192+ byte positions, consuming memory proportional to $L \times d$ per layer. The Mamba's recurrent formulation compresses all this information into the fixed hidden state, which is both a memory efficiency win and, the paper argues, a **modeling benefit**: forcing the model to compress forces it to learn meaningful abstractions rather than relying on raw positional attention.

The training complexity is also fundamentally better. Mamba's parallel scan implementation achieves $O(n_{\text{state}} \log_2(L))$ time and $O(n_{\text{state}} L)$ space for training, as detailed in Section 2. Since $n_{\text{state}}$ is a small constant (16), this is effectively $O(L \log L)$ in practice. Compare this to MegaByte's $O(L^{4/3})$ with optimal patching, or a standard Transformer's $O(L^2)$. At inference time, Mamba generates byte-by-byte in constant time per step using the recurrent formulation (Equation 2), with no growing cache. This is what enables MambaByte-972M to generate 8,192 bytes in 29 seconds versus 93 seconds for MegaByte-1.3B+218M (Table 5) — a 3.2× wall-clock speedup despite both being linear-ish in theoretical complexity.

### Why Training Efficiency Alone Isn't Enough: The Decoding Bottleneck

Even with efficient training, byte-level models face a critical deployment challenge: **decoding speed**. Since a byte-level model generates one byte at a time, generating the equivalent of a single subword token (which might be 4–8 bytes) requires 4–8 sequential forward passes through the model. For a Transformer, each forward pass requires reading the entire key-value cache, making byte-level generation prohibitively slow. For Mamba, each forward pass is constant-time thanks to the recurrent formulation, but 4–8× more steps still means 4–8× slower generation than a subword model, all else being equal.

The paper recognizes that this is a **practical adoption barrier**: even if MambaByte matches subword models in perplexity and outperforms them in robustness, nobody will deploy it if text generation is substantially slower. This motivates the second major contribution: adapting **speculative decoding** (Leviathan et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2023a) to the byte-level setting. The key challenge is that standard speculative decoding assumes the drafter and verifier use the same token vocabulary — the drafter proposes tokens, and the verifier checks them. With byte-level verification, there is an impedance mismatch: the drafter proposes subword tokens, which must be decomposed into bytes for the byte-level verifier to check, and rejected tokens leave partial subwords that the byte-level corrector must complete.

The paper's solution — subword drafting followed by byte-level verification with autoregressive correction to a boundary byte (Figure 2, Algorithm 1 in Appendix F) — is specifically designed to bridge this tokenizer gap. It leverages the fact that the Mamba verifier can verify all drafted bytes in parallel using the same parallel scan code path used for training, making verification extremely fast. The correction step, which requires autoregressive byte generation until a boundary, is the only serial bottleneck, and it is typically short. The result (Table 6) is a 2.6× speedup over naive MambaByte generation and **matching the decoding speed of subword Mamba** — making byte-level models practical for the first time.

### Reconciling Conflicting Evidence in the Token-Free Literature

The paper is motivated by a genuine tension in prior work. On one side, a substantial body of research has argued for token-free models and demonstrated their benefits: ByT5 (Xue et al., 2022) showed that byte-level models are more robust to spelling errors and noise; CANINE (Clark et al., 2022) demonstrated efficient token-free encoding; Charformer (Tay et al., 2022) proposed learned subword pooling operations. On the other side, the dominant paradigm remains subword tokenization, and for good reason — prior byte-level models have consistently **underperformed subword models on standard benchmarks** when controlled for compute. The MegaByte paper (Yu et al., 2023) itself showed that its byte-level model achieved 36.4 perplexity on PG19, while subword Transformers were reaching 26.5–33.2 (Table 3). The gap was narrowing but still substantial.

The paper's position is that this gap exists not because byte-level modeling is inherently inferior, but because **prior architectures could not efficiently handle the resulting sequence lengths**. Transformers hit a quadratic wall; MegaByte's patching introduced its own bias; PerceiverAR's latent compression lost fine-grained information. Each approach made a different architectural compromise that traded away some of the benefits of byte-level modeling. Mamba, with its linear-time recurrent formulation and fixed-size state, makes **no such compromise** — it processes raw bytes exactly the same way it would process subword tokens, just with longer sequences. The hidden state capacity argument suggests this should work, and the experimental results (Table 3: MambaByte-972M at 33.0 PPL vs. Mamba-1.03B subword at 33.9 PPL on PG19) confirm that it does: for the first time, a byte-level model matches its subword counterpart at the same architecture and compute budget.

This is not just a "Mamba is good at long sequences" finding. It is a specific claim about **tokenization and architecture being coupled design choices**. The paper argues, implicitly, that the field's attachment to subword tokenization has been driven by Transformer efficiency constraints rather than principled modeling considerations. By removing the Transformer, MambaByte removes the efficiency argument for tokenization, leaving only the robustness and generalization arguments against it. The result is a model that is both more robust (Table 4) and equally performant (Table 3), changing the cost-benefit calculus of token-free modeling entirely.

### Positioning: Token-Free Modeling as an Architectural Problem, Not a Data Problem

The paper frames token-free language modeling not as a tradeoff between robustness and efficiency, but as a problem that **architecture choice determines whether it is viable at all**. The introduction states this explicitly: "This change pushes the modeling and efficiency issues upstream into the architecture itself." In other words, if your architecture can handle long sequences efficiently (like Mamba), byte-level modeling is a pure win — you get robustness for free. If your architecture cannot (like Transformers), byte-level modeling is a loss — the robustness gains are offset by the efficiency penalties, requiring compression workarounds that dilute the benefits.

This framing elevates the paper's contribution from a simple architecture application ("we trained Mamba on bytes") to a conceptual contribution about the relationship between tokenization and model architecture. It implies that the **dominance of subword tokenization is an artifact of Transformer dominance**, not an intrinsic property of language modeling. If architectures like Mamba become standard, tokenization might become unnecessary — a possibility that has been speculated about (Xue et al., 2022) but never demonstrated with competitive performance until this work.

The paper also positions MambaByte as a **practical alternative**, not just an academic exercise. The speculative decoding results are crucial here — theoretical efficiency wins during training don't matter if inference is too slow for deployment. By achieving 2.6× speedup and matching subword Mamba's generation speed, the paper makes the case that token-free models can be deployed in production systems. The combination of (a) competitive perplexity, (b) superior robustness, and (c) practical decoding speed makes MambaByte a genuine alternative to subword models rather than a research curiosity.

Finally, the paper positions its contributions as specifically about **removing inductive bias**. Tokenization is described as an "inductive bias" in the abstract, and the paper argues that removing it allows models to "generalize more easily across orthographic and morphological variants." This connects to broader themes in deep learning about reducing hand-designed preprocessing and letting models learn directly from raw data. Just as the field moved from hand-engineered features to learned representations in computer vision, MambaByte argues for moving from engineered token vocabularies to learned byte-level representations in language modeling — enabled by architectures that can handle the computational consequences of this choice.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

MambaByte is a language model that reads and writes raw bytes — the literal UTF-8 encoded characters of text — instead of using a vocabulary of subword tokens, and it does so using the Mamba state space model architecture. The system solves the core tension of token-free language modeling: byte sequences are 4–8× longer than subword sequences, which breaks standard Transformers due to quadratic attention costs, but Mamba's recurrent formulation with a fixed-size hidden state handles these long sequences without architectural compromises or representational compression — making byte-level models both performant and practical for the first time.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The MambaByte system has four major components:

1. **MambaByte core model** — a stack of Mamba SSM layers that processes a sequence of raw bytes, maintains a fixed-size hidden state per layer, and outputs a probability distribution over the next byte at each position. This is the language model itself, trained autoregressively on byte sequences.

2. **Parallel scan training path** — at training time, the entire input byte sequence is available, so the linear recurrence in each Mamba layer is computed in parallel using a work-efficient parallel scan algorithm. This achieves $O(n_{\text{state}} \log_2 L)$ time complexity and $O(n_{\text{state}} L)$ FLOPs, enabling efficient training on long (8,192-byte) sequences.

3. **Recurrent inference path** — at generation time, bytes are produced one at a time, and each Mamba layer updates its hidden state recurrently: `$h[k] = A[k]h[k-1] + B[k]x[k]$`. This is constant-time per step with no growing key-value cache, unlike Transformers.

4. **Speculative decoding system** (for efficient generation) — a smaller subword Mamba model (Mamba-110M) drafts candidate subwords autoregressively; these are converted to bytes and fed to the larger MambaByte verifier in a single parallel scan; the verifier identifies which drafted bytes are consistent with its own top-$\beta$ predictions; rejected bytes are corrected autoregressively by the byte-level model until a word boundary is reached.

Information flow during generation: prompt bytes enter → MambaByte hidden state is computed → subword Mamba drafter proposes `$m$` subwords → drafted subwords are converted to bytes → MambaByte verifier runs parallel scan over drafted bytes → bifurcation position `$c$` is found where the first byte deviates from MambaByte's top-$\beta$ candidates → MambaByte corrects from position `$c$` byte-by-byte until a boundary byte → verified and corrected bytes are appended to output → hidden states for both models are cached for the next iteration.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the Mamba SSM foundations — the continuous-time state space dynamics, discretization for discrete sequences, and the input-selective mechanism — since these define the model's architecture and computational properties.
- **Second**, the Mamba architecture block — how the SSM layer is embedded into a gated neural network with convolutions and linear projections — since this is the building block that gets stacked to form MambaByte.
- **Third**, the parallel scan implementation — how the linear recurrence is computed efficiently at training time — since this is what makes MambaByte computationally viable on long byte sequences.
- **Fourth**, training configuration for MambaByte — data processing, optimization, hardware, hyperparameters — since these choices determine the practical performance reported in the experiments.
- **Fifth**, speculative decoding with subword drafting — the drafter-verifier-corrector pipeline — since this is the key innovation that makes byte-level generation practical at inference time.

This is primarily a **methods application and adaptation paper** whose core idea is that the Mamba architecture's fixed-size hidden state and linear-time recurrence remove the architectural barriers to token-free byte-level language modeling, and that speculative decoding with mismatched tokenizers (subword drafting, byte-level verification) can close the inference efficiency gap.

#### Mamba State Space Model Foundations

The Mamba model is built on a selective state space model (SSM) that processes a 1D input sequence `$x[k]$` through a hidden state `$h[k]$` that evolves over time. The model begins from continuous-time dynamics and discretizes them for sequence processing — this is not merely a mathematical formality but enables connections to continuous-time system theory and provides certain normalization properties (Orvieto et al., 2023; Gu et al., 2023).

**Continuous-time formulation.** The starting point is a system of first-order differential equations:

$$\frac{dh(t)}{dt} = A h(t) + B(t) x(t)$$
$$y(t) = C(t) h(t)$$

where `$x(t) \in \mathbb{R}$` is a scalar input signal (one dimension of the input at time `$t$`), `$h(t) \in \mathbb{R}^n$` is an `$n$`-dimensional hidden state (`$n = n_{\text{state}}$` is the SSM state dimension, typically 16), `$y(t) \in \mathbb{R}$` is the scalar output, `$A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$` is a **time-invariant** diagonal system matrix that governs how the state evolves on its own (like a decay rate), `$B(t) \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times 1}$` is a **time-varying** input matrix that controls how the current input `$x(t)$` affects each dimension of the hidden state, and `$C(t) \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times n}$` is a **time-varying** output matrix that maps the hidden state to the output.

**What this formulation computes:** given an input signal `$x(t)$`, the system maintains an `$n$`-dimensional hidden state `$h(t)$` that is a filtered, compressed version of the input history. At each moment, the state changes through two mechanisms: the first term `$A h(t)$` causes the existing state to evolve autonomously (typically decaying or rotating), while the second term `$B(t) x(t)$` injects new information from the current input. The output `$y(t)$` is a linear readout of the hidden state — a weighted sum where the weights `$C(t)$` can change over time.

**Why this form:** The separation into `$A$` (time-invariant dynamics), `$B(t)$` (input projection), and `$C(t)$` (output projection) allows for efficient computation. Because `$A$` is diagonal, the state evolution is decoupled across the `$n$` dimensions — each dimension evolves independently, which enables the parallel scan algorithm used at training time. The time-invariance of `$A$` means it can be pre-computed and stored, while the time-variation of `$B$` and `$C$` (introduced by Mamba's selection mechanism) allows the model to adapt its behavior based on input content. This is a key departure from prior SSMs (S4, DSS, S4D) where all three matrices were time-invariant, limiting their ability to selectively attend to or ignore specific inputs.

**Discretization for discrete sequences.** Since language is a discrete sequence of tokens (or bytes), the continuous-time dynamics must be discretized. The input is sampled at time points `$t_k = \sum_{j=1}^{k} \Delta[j]$`, where `$\Delta[k]$` is a learned timestep for each input position. The discretization uses a zero-order hold assumption: the input `$x(t)$` is held constant between samples, i.e., `$x(t_k + \xi) = x(t_k) = x[k]$` for any `$\xi \in [t_k, t_{k+1})$`. The resulting discrete-time system is:

$$h[k] = A[k] h[k-1] + B[k] x[k]$$
$$y[k] = C[k] h[k]$$

where `$A[k] = \exp(\Delta[k] A)$` is the discrete-time state transition matrix (how the previous state carries forward), `$B[k] = (\Delta[k] A)^{-1}(\exp(\Delta[k] A) - I) \cdot (\Delta[k] B(t_k))$` is the discrete-time input matrix (how the current input enters), and `$C[k] = C(t_k)$` is the discrete-time output matrix (with a simplification: B is discretized through a simplified Euler method rather than exact zero-order hold, as the authors note that empirical performance does not change significantly with the simplification).

**What the discretization computes:** for each position `$k$` in the input sequence, we compute a new hidden state `$h[k]$` as a combination of (a) the previous hidden state `$h[k-1]$` decayed/transformed by `$A[k]$`, and (b) the current input `$x[k]$` projected by `$B[k]$`. The timestep `$\Delta[k]$` controls the balance: a large `$\Delta$` makes `$A[k] \approx 0$` (the model forgets the past and focuses on the current input), while a small `$\Delta$` makes `$A[k] \approx I$` (the model retains the past and mostly ignores the current input). The output `$y[k]$` reads from the hidden state.

**Why this discretization:** the zero-order hold is the standard approach in control theory for converting continuous-time systems to discrete-time, and it preserves important properties like model normalization and resolution invariance. The paper notes in Appendix D that this discretization can be viewed as a generalization of gating in recurrent networks. Setting `$A = -1$`, `$B = 1$`, and `$n = 1$` (a scalar state), the discrete recurrence becomes:

$$h[k] = (1 - \sigma(z(x))) h[k-1] + \sigma(z(x)) x[k]$$

where `$\sigma$` is the sigmoid function and `$z(x)$` is a learned function of the input. This is exactly a gated recurrence: the sigmoid acts as a learnable forget/input gate, with the property that `$\lim_{\Delta \to \infty} h[k] = x[k]$` (complete overwrite — the model focuses entirely on the current input) and `$\lim_{\Delta \to 0} h[k] = h[k-1]$` (complete retention — the current input is ignored entirely).

**The selection mechanism.** What makes Mamba "selective" is that the discretization timestep `$\Delta[k]$`, the input matrix `$B[k]$`, and the output matrix `$C[k]$` are all **functions of the input** `$x[k] \in \mathbb{R}^d$` at that position:

$$\Delta[k] = \text{softplus}(W_\Delta (W_R x[k]))$$
$$B[k] = W_B x[k]$$
$$C[k] = W_C x[k]$$

where `$W_B \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}$` maps from the `$d$`-dimensional input to the `$n$`-dimensional state space for the input projection, `$W_C \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}$` similarly for the output projection, `$W_R \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times d}$` projects the input down to a low-rank bottleneck of dimension `$r \ll d$` (where `$r = 64$` for MambaByte-353M and `$r = 112$` for MambaByte-972M — see Table 9), `$W_\Delta \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r}$` projects back up to produce the timestep (after softplus), and `$\text{softplus}(z) = \log(1 + e^z)$` ensures positivity of `$\Delta$`.

**What selection computes:** for each input position `$k$` and each of the `$d$` input dimensions (the SSM is applied independently per dimension, but `$\Delta$` is distinct per dimension), the model computes:
1. A timestep `$\Delta[k]$` that determines how much to weigh the current input versus carry forward the hidden state — this is content-dependent, so important tokens get large `$\Delta$` (overwrite state) and filler tokens get small `$\Delta$` (retain state).
2. An input projection `$B[k] x[k]$` that selectively injects parts of the input into each hidden state dimension.
3. An output projection `$C[k] h[k]$` that selectively reads from the hidden state for the output.

**Why this form:** the key insight from Gu & Dao (2023) is that constant dynamics (where `$B$` and `$C$` do not depend on the input) fail to capture input-dependent context selection, which is crucial for language modeling where the model needs to selectively remember or forget specific pieces of information based on content. The low-rank bottleneck for `$\Delta$` (through `$W_R$` with `$r \ll d$`) keeps the additional parameters manageable while still allowing input-dependent behavior. The fact that `$A$`, `$B$`, and `$C$` are identical for each of the `$d$` input dimensions (only `$\Delta$` varies per dimension) results in a hidden state of size `$n_{\text{state}} \times d$` per timestep per layer, which is the "large fixed-size memory" that the paper leverages.

#### Mamba Architecture Block

The SSM layer does not exist in isolation — it is embedded into a larger neural network block that is stacked `$m$` times to form the full model. The block structure, illustrated in Figure 5 (right), combines the SSM with gating, convolutions, and linear projections.

**Input projection.** The input `$x \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times L \times d}$` (batch size `$B$`, sequence length `$L$`, model dimension `$d$`) enters the block and is projected through two parallel linear layers:

$$x_{\text{proj}} = \text{Linear}_{\text{in}}(x) \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times L \times ed}$$
$$z = \text{Linear}_{\text{gate}}(x) \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times L \times ed}$$

where `$e$` is the expansion factor (set to `$e = 2$` for all MambaByte models — see Table 9), making the expanded dimension `$ed$` (e.g., `$2 \times 1024 = 2048$` for MambaByte-353M).

**What this computes:** the input is split into two parallel pathways — `$x_{\text{proj}}$` goes through the SSM and convolution, while `$z$` goes through a gating path. Both are expanded by factor `$e$`, giving the model more capacity in the SSM processing without increasing the input/output dimension.

**Why this form:** this is the gated SSM design from Mehta et al. (2023), where the gating path `$z$` acts as a multiplicative gate on the SSM output. This allows the model to learn when to use the SSM's output (gate open) versus bypass it (gate closed), similar to the gating in LSTMs and GRUs but applied to entire dimensions rather than individual elements.

**1D convolution.** The projected input `$x_{\text{proj}}$` passes through a 1D causal convolution (causal meaning the convolution only looks at past positions):

$$x_{\text{conv}} = \text{SiLU}(\text{Conv1D}(x_{\text{proj}}))$$

where the convolution has kernel size `$k = 4$` (for all MambaByte models), and SiLU (also called Swish) is the activation function `$\text{SiLU}(u) = u \cdot \sigma(u)$` where `$\sigma$` is the sigmoid.

**What this computes:** the convolution locally smooths and transforms the input sequence before it enters the SSM, mixing information across the 4 most recent positions. The SiLU activation introduces nonlinearity before the linear SSM recurrence.

**Why this form:** the convolution provides local context for the SSM, which otherwise operates on a per-token basis. By mixing across 4 positions, the model can extract local features (like character n-grams in the byte setting) that the SSM can then incorporate into its longer-range state. Gu & Dao (2023) found that this convolution significantly improves empirical performance.

**SSM processing.** The convolved sequence `$x_{\text{conv}}$` is fed into the SSM:

$$y_{\text{ssm}} = \text{SSM}(x_{\text{conv}})$$

where the SSM operates as described in the previous section: for each of the `$ed$` input dimensions, it applies the selective state space recurrence (either via parallel scan at training or recurrent step at inference) to produce an output of the same shape.

**Gating and output projection.** The SSM output is gated element-wise with the gate pathway and then projected back to the model dimension:

$$y_{\text{out}} = \text{Linear}_{\text{out}}(\text{SiLU}(z) \odot y_{\text{ssm}}) \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times L \times d}$$

where `$\odot$` is element-wise multiplication.

**What this computes:** the gating path `$z$`, after SiLU activation, acts as a learned mask on the SSM output — dimensions where `$\text{SiLU}(z)$` is near zero are suppressed, while dimensions where it is large are amplified. The final linear projection maps from the expanded dimension `$ed$` back to the model dimension `$d$`, producing the block's output.

**Why this form:** the gating mechanism allows the model to learn to selectively trust the SSM's output. On dimensions where the SSM provides useful long-range context, the gate can open; on dimensions where local information suffices, the gate can close. The output projection provides a bottleneck that forces the SSM to produce representations that are useful after compression, similar to how the attention output projection works in Transformers.

**Residual connection.** As with Transformer blocks, the block output is added to the block input via a residual connection: `$x_{\text{new}} = x + y_{\text{out}}$`, which is standard practice for training deep networks.

**Full model stacking.** The full MambaByte model stacks `$m$` such blocks (where `$m = 53$` for the 353M model and `$m = 48$` for the 972M model — see Table 9), followed by a final linear layer and softmax to produce byte-level logits over the vocabulary of 256 possible byte values. The embedding layer maps from 256 possible input bytes to dimension `$d$` (learned embedding vectors for each byte value).

**Compute cost per block.** From Table 8, the MambaByte block's forward pass FLOPs per byte consist of: linear projections `$2n(3ed^2)$`, pre-SSM 1D convolution `$2nked$`, computing `$\Delta$`, `$B$`, `$C$` from input `$x$` costing `$2n(2edr + 2edn_{\text{state}})$`, discretization and pre-scan preparations `$n(3edn_{\text{state}})$`, recurrence with parallel scan `$n(edn_{\text{state}})$`, output computation `$2nedn_{\text{state}} + ned$`, and element-wise gating `$ned$`, where `$n$` is the number of layers (`$m$`). All terms are linear in sequence length `$L$`, with no quadratic `$L^2$` terms — this is the fundamental computational advantage over Transformers.

#### Parallel Scan Implementation for Training

At training time, the entire input sequence is available, enabling a more efficient computation of the linear recurrence than the step-by-step formulation in Equation 2. The paper describes the parallel scan approach from Smith et al. (2023) and Blelloch (1990), adapted for the Mamba SSM.

**The associative operator reformulation.** The key insight is that the linear recurrence can be expressed as an associative binary operator. Define tuples `$e_k = (A_k, b_k)$` where `$A_k = A[k]$` (a diagonal `$n_{\text{state}} \times n_{\text{state}}$` matrix) and `$b_k = B[k] x[k]$` (an `$n_{\text{state}}$`-dimensional vector). Define an associative operator `$\bullet$` that combines two such tuples:

$$e_j \bullet e_k = (A_k A_j, A_k b_j + b_k)$$

The associativity property means that `$(e_1 \bullet e_2) \bullet e_3 = e_1 \bullet (e_2 \bullet e_3)$`, which allows the computation of all prefix compositions `$e_1$`, `$e_1 \bullet e_2$`, `$e_1 \bullet e_2 \bullet e_3$`, up to `$e_1 \bullet e_2 \bullet \cdots \bullet e_L$` using a parallel prefix scan.

**What this computes:** the prefix scan produces a sequence `$[(A_1, h[1]), (A_2 A_1, h[2]), \ldots]$` where the second component of each tuple is exactly the hidden state `$h[k]$` at that position, computed as `$h[k] = A_k h[k-1] + b_k$`. This gives us all hidden states in parallel without iterating step-by-step.

**Why this operator is associative:** the operation captures what happens when you compose two steps of the recurrence. If the first step takes `$h_{i-1}$` to `$h_i = A_i h_{i-1} + b_i$` and the second step takes `$h_i$` to `$h_{i+1} = A_{i+1} h_i + b_{i+1}$`, then the combined effect is `$h_{i+1} = A_{i+1} (A_i h_{i-1} + b_i) + b_{i+1} = (A_{i+1} A_i) h_{i-1} + (A_{i+1} b_i + b_{i+1})$`, which matches the form `$(A_{\text{combined}}, b_{\text{combined}})$` with `$A_{\text{combined}} = A_{i+1} A_i$` and `$b_{\text{combined}} = A_{i+1} b_i + b_{i+1}$`.

**Computational complexity.** A work-efficient parallel scan (Blelloch, 1990) can compute all prefix compositions in `$O(T_\bullet \log_2(L))$` time using `$L/2$` processors, where `$T_\bullet$` is the cost of applying `$\bullet$` once. With diagonal `$A_k$`, each `$\bullet$` operation requires `$O(n_{\text{state}})$` scalar multiplications and additions (since diagonal matrices multiply element-wise and the matrix-vector product `$A_k b_j$` is just element-wise multiplication). Therefore, the total time is `$O(n_{\text{state}} \log_2(L))$` and the total work is `$O(n_{\text{state}} L)$` FLOPs. With `$n_{\text{state}} = 16$`, this is extremely efficient — each position requires only 16 scalar operations per `$\bullet$` step, times `$\log_2(L)$` parallel steps.

**Why this is crucial for byte-level training:** at sequence length `$L = 8,192$`, the parallel scan requires `$\log_2(8192) = 13$` parallel steps, each doing `$O(n_{\text{state}})$` work per position. This is dramatically faster than the `$O(L^2)$` attention computation in a Transformer at the same sequence length. For MegaByte with patch size `$p = 8$`, the global Transformer processes `$L/p = 1,024$` patches with `$O((L/p)^2) = O(1,048,576)$` attention complexity, plus the local Transformers processing `$p = 8$` bytes per patch — a total of `$O(L^2/p^2 + Lp) = O(1,048,576 + 65,536)$` per layer. MambaByte's `$O(n_{\text{state}} L) = O(131,072)$` FLOPs per layer is substantially lower, which is what enables training on long byte sequences with reasonable compute budgets.

**Implementation detail — CUDA kernel extension for speculative decoding.** For the speculative decoding system, the paper extended the fast CUDA kernel from the Mamba codebase to allow verification to resume from a mismatched position (position `$c$` after the bifurcation byte) rather than restarting from the beginning of the sequence. This is essential for efficiency during speculative verification: after finding that drafted bytes `$b_1, \ldots, b_c$` are consistent with the verifier's predictions, the verifier can continue processing from position `$c$` using the cached hidden state `$h_c$` without recomputing the scan over the entire prefix.

#### Training Configuration for MambaByte

The paper provides detailed training recipes in the main text and Appendix C, specifying the data processing pipeline, optimization hyperparameters, model configurations, and hardware setup.

**Data processing for byte-level training.** All text is treated as UTF-8 encoded byte sequences with a vocabulary of 256 possible byte values. For training, documents are shuffled, and contiguous sequences of `$L_{\text{ctx}} = 8,192$` bytes are extracted — one sequence per document, starting from a random position within the document. This differs from subword training where multiple shorter sequences might be concatenated and split; here, each training example is exactly 8,192 consecutive bytes from a single document, preserving document-level coherence.

**What this choice means:** the model never sees sequences that cross document boundaries. This is important for long-range language modeling because cross-document boundaries introduce artificial discontinuities that would not exist in natural text. By keeping each training sequence within a single document, the model learns genuine long-range dependencies (e.g., character names, plot threads, thematic consistency) rather than learning to ignore artificial breaks. The trade-off is that some documents shorter than 8,192 bytes cannot be used, but the datasets used (PG19, Books, Stories, ArXiv, Code) have average document lengths far exceeding 8,192 bytes (Table 7: PG19 averages 4,082,210 bytes per document, Books averages 551,179 bytes, etc.), so this is not a practical limitation.

**Optimization.** All models use the AdamW optimizer with `$\beta = (0.9, 0.95)$`. For the medium-scale experiments (MambaByte-353M, Table 2), the peak learning rate is `$4 \times 10^{-4}$` (0.0004) with gradient norm clipped to 0.1. For the large-scale experiment (MambaByte-972M, Table 3), the same peak learning rate and gradient clipping are used. The learning rate schedule follows a linear warmup for the first 500 steps followed by cosine annealing to zero. The batch size is 48 sequences (each of 8,192 bytes) across all experiments, matching the MegaByte training setup (Yu et al., 2023). Dropout is not used with any model. Mixed precision training uses BF16 for efficiency at scale.

**Why these hyperparameters:** the batch size of 48 is the same as MegaByte's, ensuring fair compute comparisons. The learning rate of `$4 \times 10^{-4}$` is relatively high for language model training (compared to typical Transformer learning rates of `$1-3 \times 10^{-4}$`) and was selected from a sweep over `$\{2 \times 10^{-4}, 6 \times 10^{-4}, 8 \times 10^{-4}\}$` for the models in Figure 1, with the best-performing curve selected. The lack of dropout follows Mamba (Gu & Dao, 2023) and reflects the observation that SSMs may regularize differently than Transformers due to their recurrent structure. The gradient clipping at 0.1 is relatively aggressive (Transformers often use 1.0), suggesting Mamba training may be sensitive to gradient spikes.

**Training duration.** For medium-scale experiments (Table 2), MambaByte-353M is trained for 80,000 steps, corresponding to `$80,000 \times 48 \times 8,192 \approx 31.5 \times 10^9$` bytes, which the paper rounds to 30B bytes. This is substantially less than the 80B bytes used for MegaByte-758M+262M in the same experiment — 0.63× the data, as noted in Section 5.1. For the large-scale PG19 experiment (Table 3), MambaByte-972M is trained for 380,000 steps, corresponding to `$380,000 \times 48 \times 8,192 \approx 149.4 \times 10^9$` bytes, rounded to 150B bytes. This is substantially less than the 400B bytes used by prior subword models in Table 3.

**Model configurations.** Table 9 provides the full hyperparameters for all MambaByte models. MambaByte-353M uses `$m = 53$` layers, model dimension `$d = 1,024$`, expansion factor `$e = 2$`, convolution kernel size `$k = 4$`, SSM state dimension `$n_{\text{state}} = 16$`, and low-rank dimension `$r = 64$`. MambaByte-972M uses `$m = 48$` layers, `$d = 1,792$`, `$e = 2$`, `$k = 4$`, `$n_{\text{state}} = 16$`, and `$r = 112$`. MambaByte-1.6B uses `$m = 48$`, `$d = 2,304$`, `$e = 2$`, `$k = 4$`, `$n_{\text{state}} = 16$`, and `$r = 144$`. The choice of `$n_{\text{state}} = 16$` throughout is notable — this is the same state dimension used by Mamba (Gu & Dao, 2023) for subword modeling, suggesting the state dimension does not need to increase to handle byte sequences. The low-rank dimension `$r$` scales proportionally with `$d$` (approximately `$d/16$`), maintaining the bottleneck ratio.

**Compute-matched comparison methodology.** A crucial aspect of the experimental design is the FLOPs-matched comparison between MambaByte and MegaByte. The paper computes forward-pass FLOPs per byte for each architecture using the formulas in Table 8, factoring in the layer counts, dimensions, sequence length, and patch size (for MegaByte). Figure 6 shows the total FLOPs used as a function of bytes trained for different model configurations. MambaByte-353M and MegaByte-758M+262M (patch size 8) have the same FLOPs-per-byte ratio (1:1 as shown in Table 1), meaning they use identical compute per training byte. For the large-scale comparison, MambaByte-972M has approximately 0.54:1 FLOPs relative to MegaByte-1.3B+350M (patch size 8), meaning MambaByte uses about half the FLOPs per byte at this scale — yet it achieves better perplexity (33.0 vs. 36.4, Table 3), making it both more compute-efficient and more performant.

**Evaluation methodology.** Performance is measured in bits per byte (BPB), defined as the byte-level negative log-likelihood divided by `$\ln(2)$`: `$\text{BPB} = -\ln(D; \text{model}) / (L_B \ln(2)) = \ell_{\text{byte}} / \ln(2)$`, where `$\ell_{\text{byte}}$` is the byte-level cross-entropy loss (computed using the natural logarithm) and `$L_B$` is the number of bytes in the dataset. For comparison with subword models (which report word-level perplexity), the conversion is: `$\text{PPL} = \exp((L_B / L_W) \cdot \ln(2) \cdot \text{BPB}) = \exp((L_B / L_W) \cdot \ell_{\text{byte}})$`, where `$L_W$` is the number of words. For the PG19 test set, `$L_B / L_W = 5.93$` (Table 10), meaning the word-level perplexity equals `$\exp(5.93 \cdot \ln(2) \cdot \text{BPB})$`. For evaluation on longer contexts than the training length, the paper uses a sliding window of `$L_{\text{ctx}}/2 = 4,096$` bytes with stride `$L_{\text{ctx}}/2$`, computing perplexity over the entire sequence and averaging overlapping predictions.

#### Speculative Decoding for Byte-Level Generation

Even with Mamba's efficient recurrent generation (constant time per byte), byte-level decoding requires 4–8× more sequential steps than subword decoding for the same text. The paper tackles this with a speculative decoding adaptation specifically designed for the mismatch between subword draft models and byte-level verification.

**The core idea.** Instead of generating byte-by-byte from MambaByte, use a fast subword Mamba model (Mamba-110M) to draft multiple subword tokens at once, convert them to bytes, verify all drafted bytes in parallel using MambaByte's parallel scan, identify where the verifier disagrees with the drafter, and autoregressively correct only the mismatched bytes. This is an instance of speculative decoding (Leviathan et al., 2023) where the drafter and verifier use **different tokenization schemes** — a novel extension since prior speculative decoding work assumes identical token vocabularies.

**The drafter: subword Mamba.** A small Mamba-110M model (`$m = 24$`, `$d = 768$`, `$e = 2$`, same architectural pattern as MambaByte but with subword vocabulary) drafts `$m = 3$` subword tokens per iteration using greedy decoding (the paper notes in Table 6 that the drafter uses greedy decoding for the speed experiments). The drafter maintains its own hidden state and generates proposals autoregressively in the standard way for subword models. Each drafted subword is recorded along with its hidden state to enable resuming after rejection.

**Byte conversion and parallel verification.** The drafted subwords `$\tilde{s}_{1:m}$` are converted to their UTF-8 byte sequences `$\tilde{b}_{1:n}$` plus the prefix bytes `$b_{1:t'}$`. The complete byte sequence `$b_{1:t'}, \tilde{b}_{1:n}$` is fed to the MambaByte verifier, which runs a parallel scan to compute logits `$p_1(x), p_2(x), \ldots, p_n(x)$` and hidden states `$h_1, h_2, \ldots, h_n$` for all `$n$` drafted bytes in one forward pass. This is the key efficiency advantage: instead of `$n$` sequential byte-level forward passes, the verifier processes all `$n$` bytes in a single parallel scan.

**Bifurcation detection.** The verifier identifies the furthest position `$c$` such that all bytes `$\tilde{b}_1, \tilde{b}_2, \ldots, \tilde{b}_c$` are in the top-`$\beta$` candidates of the verifier's distribution at their respective positions. Specifically, `$c = \min(\{i \mid 1 \leq i \leq n, \text{rank}_{p_i}(\tilde{b}_i) > \beta\} \cup \{n\})$`, where `$\text{rank}_{p_i}(\tilde{b}_i)$` is the rank of the drafted byte among the verifier's predicted probabilities (rank 1 means the drafted byte is the most likely prediction). The paper uses `$\beta = 3$` (top-3 candidates) in the speed experiments (Table 6). If all drafted bytes pass verification, `$c = n$`; otherwise, `$\tilde{b}_{c+1}$` is the first rejected byte.

**What bifurcation detection computes:** for each drafted byte position, the verifier checks whether the drafted byte is "consistent" with what the verifier would have generated. The consistency criterion is flexible: a drafted byte is accepted if it is among the top-`$\beta$` most likely bytes according to the verifier. This is more permissive than requiring exact match with greedy decoding (top-1), which would reject drafts too frequently, and more strict than accepting all drafts, which would lose the verifier's quality. The parameter `$\beta$` controls the acceptance rate-quality tradeoff.

**Why this particular definition of consistency:** the top-`$\beta$` criterion is a standard approach in speculative decoding (Leviathan et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2023a) because it guarantees that the output distribution of the speculative decoding scheme is identical to the verifier's autoregressive distribution — a property called "lossless" speculation. With top-`$\beta$` verification and appropriate rejection sampling, the generated text has the same probability under the verifier's model as if it were generated autoregressively byte-by-byte. The paper uses `$\beta = 3$` as a compromise between acceptance rate (higher `$\beta$` means more drafts accepted, faster generation) and faithfulness (lower `$\beta$` means the output is closer to pure MambaByte generation).

**Subword-bifurcation mapping.** After finding byte bifurcation position `$c$`, the system finds the corresponding subword bifurcation position `$c'$`: the largest subword index `$j$` such that all bytes of `$\tilde{s}_{1:j}$` are before or at position `$c$`. Formally, `$c' = \min(\{j \mid 1 \leq j \leq m, \text{cumsum}(\text{len}(\tilde{s}_{1:m}))[j] > c\} \cup \{m\})$`. This means any subword that has even one byte rejected is treated as entirely rejected.

**Byte-level correction to word boundary.** The critical challenge is that after rejection, the next byte to generate (`$b_{c+1}$`) may fall in the middle of a subword. The subword drafter cannot simply continue from `$b_{c+1}$` because the tokenizer may not recognize the partial subword as a valid token. Instead, the byte-level verifier takes over: starting from the last accepted byte `$\tilde{b}_c$` and using the cached hidden state `$h_c$`, it generates bytes autoregressively (`$\hat{b}_{c+1}, \hat{b}_{c+2}, \ldots$`) until it produces a **boundary byte** (e.g., a space character or other word delimiter). This ensures that the correction produces a complete subword that the drafter can resume from in the next iteration.

**What this correction computes:** the verifier generates bytes one at a time using the standard recurrent formulation (Equation 2), starting from the hidden state at position `$c$`. Each generated byte is fed back as input for the next step. The process stops when the generated byte is a boundary byte, at which point the correction is a complete subword (or sequence of subwords ending at a boundary).

**Why correction to a boundary byte is necessary:** without this step, the drafter would receive a partial byte sequence that ends mid-subword. The subword tokenizer (SentencePiece or BPE) might not be able to tokenize this partial sequence, or might tokenize it differently than expected, causing the drafter to generate nonsensical continuations. By always completing to a boundary, the system ensures the drafter always starts from a clean subword boundary. The trade-off is that the correction step is autoregressive and therefore slow — but most corrections are short (typically 1–4 bytes), so the overhead is minimal, especially since the drafter produces `$m = 3$` subwords at a time.

**State caching and resumption.** After verification and correction, the system caches two pieces of state for the next iteration: (1) the **MambaByte verifier's hidden state** `$h_{\text{final}} = h_{c+k}$` at the end of the corrected byte sequence, representing the compressed representation of all bytes generated so far; (2) the **subword Mamba drafter's hidden state** `$\bar{h}_{c'}$` at the subword bifurcation position, representing the drafter's state at the last fully-accepted subword. This allows both models to resume without recomputing from the beginning of the sequence.

**Algorithmic summary.** Algorithm 1 in Appendix F formalizes the full procedure:
1. Given prefix subwords `$s_{1:t}$` and cached hidden states, the drafter produces `$m$` draft subwords `$\tilde{s}_{1:m}$` autoregressively.
2. Convert prefix and drafts to bytes: `$b_{1:t'}$` and `$\tilde{b}_{1:n}$`.
3. Run MambaByte verifier in parallel scan over `$b_{1:t'}, \tilde{b}_{1:n}$` to get logits `$p_1, \ldots, p_n$` and hidden states `$h_1, \ldots, h_n$`.
4. Find bifurcation byte position `$c$` and subword bifurcation position `$c'$`.
5. Starting from `$\tilde{b}_c$` and state `$h_c$`, autoregressively generate corrected bytes until a boundary byte is produced.
6. Return accepted bytes `$\tilde{b}_{1:c} + \hat{b}_{c+1:c+k}$`, MambaByte state `$h_{c+k}$`, and drafter state `$\bar{h}_{c'}$`.

**Speedup measurements.** Table 6 reports empirical speed measurements. MambaByte-972M without speculation generates 8,192 bytes at a baseline speed (1×). Subword Mamba-1.03B achieves 2.8× relative speedup due to processing ~4× fewer steps. MambaByte-972M with Mamba-110M speculation achieves 2.6× relative speedup — essentially matching the subword model's speed. The log-odds ratio (log-likelihood of generating a reference sequence under MambaByte greedy vs. under speculative decoding) is 0.89 for the speculative approach versus 0.10 for subword Mamba, indicating that speculative decoding produces text much closer to what MambaByte would generate on its own.

**Why this is effective for SSMs specifically:** the paper notes that speculative decoding is "particularly efficient for SSM-style models since the byte-level verification step can use the same parallel scan code path that makes these models efficient to train." For a Transformer verifier, verification would require computing attention over the entire growing key-value cache for each drafted position, which is expensive. For MambaByte, verification is a single parallel scan over all positions simultaneously, exploiting the same associative scan that makes training efficient. This is an architectural synergy: the Mamba architecture is good at both training (parallel scan) and inference (constant-time recurrent steps), and speculative decoding leverages both — the drafter runs autoregressively (fast because it's small and subword-based), while the verifier runs in parallel (fast because of the parallel scan).

**Faithfulness evaluation.** To measure whether the speculative decoding preserves the verifier's output quality, the paper generates a reference sequence using greedy MambaByte-972M decoding (no speculation), then computes the ratio of log-likelihood of the reference under pure MambaByte to the log-likelihood under speculative decoding, averaged across 100 prompts from common PG19 phrases. A ratio of 1.0 means perfect faithfulness. The speculative approach achieves 0.89 (Table 6), indicating high faithfulness. The subword Mamba-1.03B achieves only 0.10, meaning its output distribution is very different from MambaByte's — confirming that speculative decoding with byte-level verification preserves the byte-level model's behavior, while simply using a subword model does not.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Tokenization is an architectural necessity, not a modeling choice — and Mamba removes that necessity

The paper's deepest conceptual contribution is reframing tokenization as an artifact of Transformer architecture limitations rather than a principled modeling decision. Before MambaByte, the dominant narrative in language modeling treated subword tokenization as a pragmatic compromise — a way to balance vocabulary size, sequence length, and out-of-vocabulary handling that happened to work well in practice. Prior token-free models (ByT5 by Xue et al., 2022; CANINE by Clark et al., 2022; MegaByte by Yu et al., 2023) implicitly accepted that removing tokenization required paying a computational penalty, which they addressed through various compression schemes — patching bytes into groups, using latent bottlenecks, or designing hierarchical architectures. The underlying assumption was that **byte-level modeling inherently requires architectural workarounds** to handle the sequence length blowup.

MambaByte challenges this assumption at its root. The paper's core argument — stated in the introduction but empirically proven throughout — is that the sequence length problem is not inherent to byte-level modeling; it is specific to the Transformer's quadratic attention mechanism. Mamba's fixed-size hidden state removes the dependence of memory and computation on context length, which means that **bytes and subwords impose exactly the same architectural burden**: both must be compressed into the same `m × n_state × d` floating-point memory budget. The paper argues this explicitly: "If Mamba can be used for tokenized models, MambaByte should enable modeling byte-level sequences without the need for length-compression trade-offs."

This is a fundamental shift, not an incremental improvement. It converts tokenization from a modeling choice that every architecture must make into an **architectural dependency** — Transformers need tokenization to be efficient; Mamba does not. The evidence for this reframing is not just MambaByte's competitive performance (Table 3: 33.0 PPL vs. 33.9 for subword Mamba), but the **symmetry** of the result: a Mamba model trained on bytes achieves essentially the same perplexity as a Mamba model trained on subwords at the same parameter count and training compute. This symmetry would be impossible under a Transformer, where the byte-level model would be crushed by the quadratic attention cost. The paper's Figure 1 makes this visually explicit: Transformer-361M (byte-level) and MambaByte-353M start from similar architectures but diverge dramatically in compute efficiency, with MambaByte reaching Transformer loss in less than one-third of the compute budget.

This reframing has downstream consequences for how the field thinks about tokenization. If the efficiency barrier is removed, the remaining considerations are all in favor of token-free modeling: robustness to noise (Table 4), generalization across orthographic variants, and removal of the vocabulary size hyperparameter. The paper does not quite say "tokenization is obsolete," but the implication is clear — tokenization persists because Transformers dominate, not because it is optimal. This is a diagnostic insight that changes how researchers should evaluate new architectures: an architecture's ability to handle raw bytes without efficiency penalties becomes a first-class design criterion.

---

### Innovation 2: Byte-level speculative decoding with mismatched tokenizers

The second major conceptual contribution is the demonstration that speculative decoding can bridge **different tokenization schemes** — subword drafting with byte-level verification — and that this specific pairing exploits an architectural synergy unique to SSMs. Prior speculative decoding work (Leviathan et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2023a; Spector & Re, 2023) assumed the drafter and verifier share the same token vocabulary. This is natural for Transformer-based systems where both models process identical token sequences, and the verification step simply checks whether the drafter's proposed tokens match the verifier's predictions.

MambaByte's speculative decoding breaks this assumption. The drafter (Mamba-110M) operates on subwords; the verifier (MambaByte-972M) operates on bytes. The verification step must therefore convert subwords to bytes, run the verifier over the byte sequence, detect mismatches at byte granularity, and then correct the mismatched portion using byte-level generation until a subword boundary is reached. This is not a trivial extension — it requires solving the **tokenizer impedance mismatch problem**: when the verifier rejects a drafted subword, the correction may land in the middle of a subword, and the subword drafter cannot continue from a partial subword that its tokenizer does not recognize.

The paper's solution — byte-level autoregressive correction until a boundary byte — is conceptually elegant because it leverages a property that is normally a weakness of byte-level models (byte-by-byte generation is slow) and uses it in a context where it is fast (corrections are typically 1–4 bytes, and the verifier's recurrent formulation makes short autoregressive sequences cheap). The innovation is not the correction mechanism itself, but the **architectural argument that SSMs are uniquely suited to this hybrid approach**. The verifier's parallel scan means that verifying `n` drafted bytes costs approximately the same as generating one byte autoregressively — there is no per-position overhead for checking each drafted byte. This would not hold for a Transformer verifier, where each additional byte in the verification step requires extending the key-value cache and computing attention over the growing context. The paper explicitly flags this synergy: "it is particularly efficient for SSM-style models since the byte-level verification step can use the same parallel scan code path that makes these models efficient to train."

The significance of this innovation extends beyond the 2.6× speedup number (Table 6). It demonstrates that **token-free models can be made practical at inference time without sacrificing the benefits of token-free training**. The faithfulness metric (log-odds ratio of 0.89 for speculative decoding vs. 0.10 for pure subword Mamba, Table 6) shows that the speculative approach preserves MambaByte's output distribution far better than simply substituting a subword model. This means practitioners can train token-free models for robustness and generalization, then deploy them at subword-model speeds — the speed advantage of subword tokenization is recovered at inference time without reintroducing the tokenizer's inductive bias during training. It converts speculative decoding from a generic acceleration technique into a **tokenization-bridging mechanism**, making it a fundamental component of the token-free modeling stack rather than an optional optimization.

---

### Innovation 3: Fixed-size memory as a length-extrapolation mechanism

The paper uncovers an unexpected consequence of Mamba's fixed-size hidden state: it enables **natural length extrapolation** far beyond the training context, without the positional encoding limitations that constrain Transformers. Figure 3 shows MambaByte-353M maintaining stable word-level perplexity on sequences up to 4× longer than its 8,192-byte training length, while the byte-level Transformer-361M degrades sharply beyond 8,192 bytes (limited by its rotary position embeddings) and MegaByte models show measurable degradation. Figure 4 extends this to 64× longer sequences for the 972M model using a sliding window evaluation.

This finding is not merely a performance result — it reveals something about the **representational properties of fixed-size recurrent states** that is conceptually distinct from prior work on length extrapolation. Transformer length extrapolation has been extensively studied (e.g., through position encoding modifications like ALiBi or rotary position embeddings), but the fundamental limitation is that the key-value cache grows with sequence length, and the attention mechanism must learn to attend over positions it has never seen at training. Solutions are architectural patches that modify how positions are encoded.

MambaByte's extrapolation works through a fundamentally different mechanism: the hidden state is **always the same size**, so the model never encounters a representational regime it has not seen during training. The recurrent update `h[k] = A[k] h[k-1] + B[k] x[k]` is applied identically whether the sequence is 1,000 or 100,000 bytes long. The model learns a **compression policy** during training — how to update the fixed-size state to retain useful information and discard noise — and this policy transfers to longer sequences because the state never overflows. The paper hypothesizes that MambaByte shows slightly better length extrapolation than subword Mamba (Figure 4) because "MambaByte models 4× longer sequences at training despite both models processing the same effective number of bytes per training sequence" — the byte-level model has practiced compressing more raw information per training example, making it better at the compression task required for extrapolation.

This insight connects to broader questions about **memory in neural sequence models**. Transformers have perfect memory (every token can attend to every other token) but pay a quadratic cost; MambaByte has compressed memory (the fixed-size state) but gains linear cost and extrapolation ability. The paper's results suggest that for language modeling, **compressed memory may be sufficient** — the fixed state of size `48 × 16 × 1792 ≈ 1.38M` floats can apparently capture the essential information from 32,768+ bytes of context well enough to achieve competitive perplexity. This is an empirical finding with implications for architecture design: if fixed-size recurrent memory works this well, the massive key-value caches of Transformers may represent significant overkill for many language modeling tasks.

### Innovation 4: Verifier over-optimization as the hidden barrier to byte-level modeling — and its absence in MambaByte

While not presented as a primary contribution, the paper's results contain an important negative finding about **architectural barriers to byte-level modeling** that goes beyond the headline numbers. The comparison between MegaByte and MambaByte reveals that patching-based compression (MegaByte's approach) introduces a **representational bottleneck** that limits model quality independently of computational efficiency. Figure 1 shows that MegaByte-193M+177M with patch size 8 performs worse than the full-length Transformer-361M at the same parameter budget when compute-normalized, and worse than MegaByte with patch size 4. This means that the patching mechanism — which groups bytes into fixed-size patches and processes them with separate global and local Transformers — is not just a computational optimization; it is an **information bottleneck** that degrades the model's ability to learn from the byte sequence.

The deeper insight is about **where the difficulty of byte-level modeling actually lies**. Prior work assumed the challenge was computational (sequences are too long for attention) and proposed computational solutions (patching, compression, hierarchical processing). MambaByte's results suggest that the challenge is also **representational** — the model needs to learn byte-level patterns and long-range dependencies from raw bytes without the inductive bias of token boundaries. Patching reintroduces an artificial boundary (the patch size) that may not align with linguistic structure, forcing the model to work around a representational constraint that MambaByte simply does not have. MambaByte processes bytes as a flat sequence with no imposed grouping, letting the selective state space mechanism learn which bytes to group and which to separate based on the data.

This is not just a "Mamba beats MegaByte" performance claim — it is a diagnostic about **what architectural properties matter for byte-level modeling**. The paper implicitly argues that the key property is not compression (making sequences shorter) but **unified processing** (handling all bytes with the same mechanism at the same granularity). MambaByte's recurrent SSM does this naturally; MegaByte's two-level Transformer does not, and pays a quality penalty for the discrepancy. The evidence is in the compute-matched comparison: even when MegaByte is given more parameters and identical FLOPs, MambaByte outperforms it (Table 2, Table 3). The architectural property that enables this — fixed-size memory with input-dependent selectivity — is what separates MambaByte from prior byte-level approaches at the conceptual level, not just the engineering level.

### Innovation 5: Robustness as a first-class evaluation dimension enabled by architecture choice

The paper's noise experiments (Table 4) are not merely an ablation showing MambaByte is robust — they reframe **robustness to input corruption** as a property that emerges from the combination of architecture and representation, not from either alone. Prior work (Xue et al., 2022) had already shown that byte-level models are more robust to noise than subword models. What MambaByte adds is the demonstration that **architecture choice determines whether this robustness is practically accessible**. A byte-level Transformer would be robust in principle but too slow to deploy; MambaByte is both robust and efficient, making the robustness benefit realizable in practice.

The magnitude of the robustness gap is striking and non-obvious. Under "antspeak" corruption (every character capitalized and padded with spaces), subword Mamba degrades by +58,300 perplexity — essentially complete breakdown — while MambaByte degrades by only +28.3. The mechanism is clear: subword tokenization fragments the corrupted text into out-of-vocabulary or rare token sequences that the model has never seen coherently during training, while MambaByte processes the individual characters and can still recognize the underlying words through the noise. But the paper's contribution is showing that this well-known property of byte-level models can be **combined with competitive standard performance** — MambaByte matches subword Mamba on clean text (33.0 vs. 33.9 PPL) while being dramatically more robust to corruption. Prior byte-level models had to sacrifice clean-text performance for robustness; MambaByte gets both.

This has practical implications that go beyond the academic noise benchmarks. In production deployments, text is frequently noisy — user-generated content contains typos, OCR output contains errors, code contains unusual character sequences, multilingual text mixes scripts. A model that degrades gracefully under these conditions (MambaByte) versus one that collapses catastrophically (subword Mamba) represents a fundamentally different reliability profile. The paper's framing — that MambaByte "maintains the benefits of token-free language models, such as robustness to noise" while being competitive with subword models — positions robustness not as a niche feature but as a **baseline requirement** that token-free architectures can satisfy without the historical performance penalty.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The primary evaluation is conducted on five long-form text datasets: PG19 (Rae et al., 2020), consisting of full-length English books written before 1919 with 11.74G total bytes and an average of 4,082,210 bytes per document; Stories (Trinh & Le, 2018), a CommonCrawl subset for commonsense reasoning (34.18G bytes, 36,045 average bytes per document); Books (Gao et al., 2020a), another collection of English books (108.38G bytes, 551,179 average bytes per document); ArXiv (Gao et al., 2020a), technical LATEX publications (60.27G bytes, 47,665 average bytes per document); and Code (Gao et al., 2020a), publicly available open-source code (677G bytes, 11,958 average bytes per document). For PG19, the authors use the train, validation, and test splits as specified by Rae et al. (2020). For Stories, Books, ArXiv, and Code, they randomly sample 40M consecutive bytes for testing and use the rest for training. Dataset statistics are tabulated in Table 7.

- **Base model(s).** All MambaByte experiments use variants of the Mamba architecture (Gu & Dao, 2023) applied to byte-level language modeling. Three scales are explored: MambaByte-353M (53 layers, `d = 1,024`, `e = 2`), MambaByte-972M (48 layers, `d = 1,792`, `e = 2`), and MambaByte-1.6B (48 layers, `d = 2,304`, `e = 2`). All models use SSM state dimension `n_state = 16`, convolution kernel size `k = 4`, and low-rank projection dimension `r` scaling approximately as `d/16` (64, 112, and 144 respectively). For the speculative decoding experiments, a Mamba-110M subword model serves as the drafter, and a subword Mamba-1.03B (48 layers, trained with a 32K SentencePiece vocabulary) serves as the subword baseline. The authors state they chose Mamba because it is "representative of the capabilities of many contemporary LLMs" (Section 4), though all experiments are conducted exclusively with this single architecture family.

- **Metrics.** The primary metric is bits per byte (BPB), defined as `BPB = -ln(D; model) / (L_B * ln(2)) = ℓ_byte / ln(2)`, where `ℓ_byte` is the byte-level negative log-likelihood loss computed with the natural logarithm and `L_B` is the total number of bytes in the dataset. For comparison with subword models (which report word-level perplexity), the conversion formula is `PPL = exp((L_B / L_W) * ln(2) * BPB)`, where `L_W` is the number of words. For the PG19 test set, `L_B / L_W = 5.93` (Table 10). The paper also reports generation time in seconds for efficiency benchmarks (Tables 5 and 6), relative speedup factors for speculative decoding (Table 6), and log-odds ratio as a faithfulness metric comparing speculative-decoded output to pure MambaByte greedy decoding (Table 6). For the noise experiments, degradation is measured as absolute increase in word-level perplexity on even-indexed (clean) chunks when odd-indexed chunks are corrupted (Table 4).

- **Baselines.** The paper compares against multiple categories of prior work. For byte-level baselines: Transformer-320M (Yu et al., 2023), Transformer-350M (Yu et al., 2023), Transformer-361M (trained by the authors with rotary position embeddings, RMSNorm, and no bias terms following the improved recipe of Yu et al., 2023), PerceiverAR-248M (Hawthorne et al., 2022), and three scales of MegaByte (Yu et al., 2023) — MegaByte-193M+177M, MegaByte-758M+262M, and MegaByte-1.3B+350M (all with patch size 8 unless noted otherwise). For SSM baselines: Gated-S4D-368M (adapted from Mehta et al., 2023) trained by the authors. For subword baselines on PG19: Transformer-XL (Rae et al., 2020), Compressive Transformer (Rae et al., 2020), Routing-490M (Roy et al., 2021), PerceiverAR-974.6M (Hawthorne et al., 2022), Block-Recurrent-1.3B (Hutchins et al., 2022), and the authors' own Mamba-1.03B trained with subword tokenization in a compute-matched setting. The MegaByte-1.3B+218M variant (Table 5) uses the open-source implementation for generation speed comparison.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** Compute is measured using forward-pass FLOPs per byte, with detailed component-wise formulas provided in Table 8 for Transformer, MegaByte, Gated-S4D, and MambaByte architectures. For compute-matched comparisons, the authors ensure models use identical FLOPs per byte: MambaByte-353M and MegaByte-758M+262M have a 1:1 FLOP ratio (Table 1), while MambaByte-972M uses approximately 0.54× the FLOPs per byte of MegaByte-1.3B+350M. Training data budget is measured in total bytes trained: 30B bytes for MambaByte-353M (vs. 80B for the MegaByte baseline), 150B bytes for MambaByte-972M (vs. 400B for prior subword models in Table 3). For the compute-normalized training curves in Figure 1, FLOPs are accumulated over training steps and plotted on the x-axis to compare architectures at equal compute expenditure. Generation speed is measured in wall-clock seconds to generate 8,192 bytes on an A100 80GB PCIe GPU (Tables 5 and 6).

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not report cross-validation, statistical significance tests, or confidence intervals for any experimental results. The medium-scale experiments (Table 2) report single-run BPB values on the test sets. The large-scale PG19 experiments (Table 3) report test perplexity from a single training run. The noise experiments (Table 4) report perplexity degradation without error bars. The speculative decoding speedup measurements (Table 6) are averaged across 100 prompts drawn from common phrases in the PG19 dataset, but no variance estimates are provided. All training uses a single set of hyperparameters per model configuration, with the learning rate selected from a sweep over {0.0002, 0.0006, 0.0008} for the models in Figure 1 only. The length extrapolation curves (Figures 3 and 4) are reported without uncertainty quantification around the perplexity estimates.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Medium-Scale Byte-Level Language Modeling

Table 2 presents the central medium-scale comparison. MambaByte-353M, trained for only 30B bytes, achieves the following BPB scores: 0.930 on PG19, 0.908 on Stories, 0.966 on Books, 0.663 on ArXiv, and 0.396 on Code. These results are uniformly better than MegaByte-758M+262M (patch size 8) which achieves 1.000, 0.978, 1.007, 0.678, and 0.411 respectively despite being trained on 80B bytes — 2.67× more data. The paper states MambaByte "outperforms MegaByte with 0.63× less compute and training data," referencing the 30B vs. 80B byte comparison together with the 1:1 FLOP ratio from Table 1. The gap is particularly pronounced on PG19 (0.930 vs. 1.000) and Stories (0.908 vs. 0.978), while narrower on Code (0.396 vs. 0.411). MambaByte also substantially outperforms the byte-level Transformer-320M (1.057 BPB on PG19) and PerceiverAR-248M (1.104 BPB on PG19), though these models were trained on 80B bytes with 1,024-byte context (Transformer) or 8,192-byte context (PerceiverAR) but are not FLOPs-matched to MambaByte-353M.

#### Compute-Normalized Training Comparison

Figure 1 provides a different lens by fixing the parameter budget and comparing training trajectories. The left panel shows BPB on PG19 (8,192-byte context) against training step. MambaByte-353M (353M parameters, purple line) descends fastest and reaches the lowest loss. Transformer-361M (brown) improves more slowly, while MegaByte-193M+177M with patch size 4 (blue) and patch size 8 (green) both underperform the Transformer. Gated-S4D-368M (yellow) initially tracks MambaByte but plateaus higher. The right panel normalizes by total training FLOPs, showing that MambaByte reaches the Transformer-361M's final loss at approximately 1.1 bits per byte using roughly 1 exaFLOP of training compute, while the Transformer requires over 6 exaFLOPs — MambaByte thus reaches the same performance with less than one-third of the compute, as the paper states. MegaByte-193M+177M with patch size 4 performs better than patch size 8 when parameter-matched but the paper notes that "when compute-normalized, they perform similarly" (Section 5.1), referring to the right panel where the two MegaByte curves largely overlap in FLOPs space despite different parameter counts.

#### Large-Scale PG19 Comparison Against Subword Models

Table 3 presents the large-scale results on PG19, converting all results to word-level perplexity for cross-model comparison. MambaByte-972M, trained on only 150B bytes, achieves 33.0 test PPL — outperforming all prior byte-level models: Transformer-320M (69.4 PPL, 400B bytes), PerceiverAR-248M (88.8 PPL, 400B bytes), and MegaByte-1.3B+350M (36.4 PPL, 400B bytes). The improvement over MegaByte is particularly notable since MambaByte-972M uses approximately 0.54× the FLOPs per byte (Table 1) and 0.375× the training data (150B vs. 400B bytes), yet achieves a 3.4 PPL absolute improvement (36.4 → 33.0). Against subword models, MambaByte-972M is competitive: it outperforms Transformer-XL (36.3 PPL), Compressive Transformer (33.6 PPL), Routing-490M (33.2 PPL), PerceiverAR-974.6M (28.9 PPL), Block-Recurrent-1.3B (26.5 PPL), and the authors' own subword Mamba-1.03B (33.9 PPL). The subword Mamba-1.03B was trained in a compute-matched setting (600B bytes effective based on the asterisk notation in Table 3, though the exact conversion is nuanced since subword sequences are shorter). The paper notes that subword Mamba achieves "near-optimal performance more efficiently than MambaByte, though not 4× faster as expected, but 2.2× faster" (Section 5.2), but that Mamba-1.03B's perplexity "does not improve significantly beyond 150B training bytes." The field marked with `−` for Block-Recurrent-1.3B's effective bytes trained indicates this information is unknown for that baseline.

#### Length Extrapolation

Figure 3 evaluates how byte-level models trained with 8,192-byte context extrapolate to longer sequences. MambaByte-353M maintains a flat word-level perplexity curve out to 32,768 bytes (4× training length), hovering around 20–40 PPL with minimal degradation. S4D-368M similarly extrapolates well but at higher absolute perplexity. The Transformer-361M diverges sharply beyond its training length due to rotary position embedding limitations. Notably, MegaByte-193M+177M with patch size 4 extrapolates better than with patch size 8, and both show degradation beyond the training length that MambaByte avoids. The paper hypothesizes this is "limited by the position embeddings" for Transformers, while for MegaByte the patching mechanism appears to introduce some position sensitivity even though the global module operates on compressed patches. Figure 4 extends this finding to the large-scale models with a sliding window evaluation. Both MambaByte-972M and Mamba-1.03B (subword) extrapolate impressively to 32,768 bytes (4× training for MambaByte, ~16× training for subword Mamba in terms of effective context), with MambaByte showing slightly better extrapolation — the paper attributes this to MambaByte training on "4× longer sequences at training despite both models processing the same effective number of bytes per training sequence" (Section 5.2). At the extreme of 32,768 bytes, MambaByte achieves approximately 32–33 PPL, comparable to its in-distribution performance.

#### Generation Speed

Table 5 benchmarks text generation speed for producing 8,192 bytes. MambaByte-972M generates in 29 seconds on an A100 80GB PCIe GPU. This is 3.2× faster than MegaByte-1.3B+218M at 93 seconds, and 4.6× faster than Transformer-350M at 132 seconds. The paper notes that the Transformer and MegaByte speeds are taken from Yu et al. (2023), while the lower half of Table 5 shows results "on the same hardware" using the open-source MegaByte implementation. With a sliding window of 2× bytes, MambaByte-972M takes 58 seconds (presumably due to recomputation or context management overhead). The larger MambaByte-1.6B generates in 36 seconds — slower than MambaByte-972M due to increased parameter count, but still faster than MegaByte-1.3B+218M which has comparable parameters. The paper attributes MambaByte's generation speed advantage to "its use of recurrent generation," which maintains constant time per step with no growing key-value cache (Section 5.3).

#### Speculative Decoding Speedup

Table 6 presents the speculative decoding results. MambaByte-972M without speculation serves as the 1× baseline. Subword Mamba-1.03B achieves 2.8× relative speedup by generating subword tokens (~4× fewer steps). MambaByte-972M with Mamba-110M subword drafting (drafting 3 subwords per iteration, accepting if drafted bytes are in the top-3 candidates of the byte-level verifier) achieves 2.6× relative speedup — matching the subword Mamba's generation speed within measurement tolerance. The faithfulness evaluation uses greedy-decoded MambaByte-972M as reference. The log-odds ratio is 0.89 for speculative MambaByte versus 0.10 for subword Mamba-1.03B, indicating that "our speculative decoding approach [is] more faithful to MambaByte-972M than the subword Mamba-1.03B" (Section 5.3). In other words, speculative decoding preserves the byte-level model's output distribution, while simply using the subword model produces substantially different text.

#### Robustness to Synthetic Noise

Table 4 reports perplexity degradation under six noise conditions, measured on even-indexed (clean) chunks when odd-indexed chunks are corrupted in the PG19 test set. Under character drop (0.05 probability), subword Mamba degrades by +16.9 PPL while MambaByte degrades by +8.5 PPL — roughly half the sensitivity. At 0.3 drop probability, the gap widens dramatically: subword Mamba degrades by +213.2 PPL while MambaByte degrades by only +31.7, a 6.7× difference. Under character repetition (0.05 prob.), both models show similar degradation (+6.3 vs. +6.2), but at 0.3 prob., the gap re-emerges (+28.4 vs. +26.6). The most striking result is under "antspeak" corruption (every character capitalized and padded with spaces): subword Mamba degrades by +58,300.0 PPL — essentially complete model collapse — while MambaByte degrades by only +28.3 PPL. Under uppercase with 0.3 probability, subword Mamba degrades by +18.3 versus MambaByte's +5.5, a 3.3× difference. Random case corruption produces a +20.8 vs. +7.7 gap. Character swapping at 0.3 probability shows a +630.6 vs. +28.7 gap — more than a 22× difference. The paper notes that these findings "align with those observed by Xue et al. (2022) in that byte-level models are significantly more resilient to accidental and adversarial spelling errors than subword models," but the key contribution is demonstrating this property persists in a model that matches subword performance on clean text (33.0 vs. 33.9 PPL, Table 3), which prior byte-level models could not achieve simultaneously.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**FLOPs-matched architecture comparison (Figure 1, right panel):** The compute-normalized training curves reveal that architectural choice matters more than raw parameter count for byte-level efficiency. Transformer-361M, MegaByte-193M+177M (patch 4), MegaByte-193M+177M (patch 8), and Gated-S4D-368M all converge to similar loss levels when normalized by FLOPs, suggesting that patch-based compression and gated SSM variants without selection offer no fundamental efficiency advantage over a well-optimized Transformer at this scale. MambaByte's selective SSM is the only architecture that achieves both lower absolute loss and dramatically better compute efficiency. The fact that MegaByte patch size 8 underperforms patch size 4 in parameter-matched space but the two converge in FLOPs-matched space is a non-obvious finding: the computational savings from larger patches are real, but the representational cost means you need more parameters to compensate, and the net FLOPs efficiency is similar.

**MambaByte vs. Mamba (subword) at equal compute (Table 3):** The comparison between MambaByte-972M (33.0 PPL) and Mamba-1.03B (33.9 PPL) serves as an ablation of tokenization holding architecture constant. Both models use the Mamba architecture, differ only in tokenization scheme (raw bytes vs. 32K SentencePiece subwords), and are trained in a compute-matched setting. The near-identical perplexity supports the paper's central claim that "if Mamba can be used for tokenized models, MambaByte should enable modeling byte-level sequences without the need for length-compression trade-offs" (Section 3). The fact that subword Mamba converges faster (2.2× fewer training bytes to reach near-optimal performance, per Section 5.2) but does not surpass MambaByte's final perplexity suggests that byte-level modeling has a higher data requirement but equal or better asymptotic performance — a finding the paper acknowledges but does not deeply analyze.

**Patch size ablation in MegaByte (Figure 1, left panel):** The comparison between MegaByte-193M+177M with patch size 4 versus patch 8 provides an implicit ablation of compression granularity. Patch size 4 performs better than patch size 8 at the same parameter count, suggesting that finer-grained patching preserves more useful information. However, the FLOPs-normalized curves (right panel) show both variants converging, indicating that the quality gain from smaller patches is offset by the increased computational cost. This supports the paper's argument that MambaByte's unified processing (no patching, no compression) is architecturally preferable — it gets the representational benefits of fine granularity without paying a computational penalty.

**Training data budget (Table 2 vs. Table 3):** MambaByte-353M trains on 30B bytes while MambaByte-972M trains on 150B bytes (Table 3: asterisked to indicate the authors' own training). Both outperform MegaByte variants trained on 80B and 400B bytes respectively. The paper does not provide an explicit data scaling ablation that varies training bytes while holding model size constant, but the cross-model comparison suggests MambaByte achieves better results with less data — likely because the architecture's linear-time complexity allows each training example to be processed more efficiently, effectively giving more "learning per FLOP." The paper notes that subword Mamba "achieves near-optimal performance more efficiently than MambaByte, though not 4× faster as expected, but 2.2× faster," indicating that byte-level modeling does incur a data efficiency penalty even within the Mamba architecture — the model needs more bytes of training data to learn the same linguistic patterns because it must discover byte-level regularities from scratch.

**Sliding window vs. full context for length extrapolation (Table 5, Figure 4):** The generation speed experiments test MambaByte with and without a sliding window. Without sliding window, 8,192 bytes are generated in 29 seconds. With a sliding window of 2× bytes (presumably meaning context is limited to 16,384 bytes and shifted during generation), the time increases to 58 seconds — a 2× slowdown. The resulting BPB improves from 0.883 to 0.863, a 0.02 BPB gain for a 2× generation time cost. The paper does not specify the sliding window stride or the exact mechanism, but the result shows a computation-quality tradeoff that the architecture supports — unlike Transformers, which require the full key-value cache regardless.

**Speculative decoding acceptance criterion and draft length (Table 6):** The speculative decoding configuration is an implicit ablation of the acceptance criterion: the verifier accepts drafted bytes if they fall in its top-3 candidates (β = 3), and the drafter produces 3 subwords per iteration (m = 3). The paper does not sweep β or m, so the sensitivity of the 2.6× speedup to these choices is unknown. The log-odds ratio of 0.89 versus 0.10 for subword Mamba shows that speculative decoding preserves the byte-level model's distribution, but the paper does not report acceptance rates (what fraction of drafted bytes/subwords are accepted), which would illuminate how often the correction step is invoked and whether the 2.6× speedup is robust to different text types.

**Faithfulness metric construction (Table 6):** The log-odds ratio metric compares the log-likelihood of a reference sequence (greedy MambaByte-972M output) under two models: pure MambaByte (the reference model) and the speculative decoding system (or subword Mamba for comparison). A ratio of 1.0 means identical distributions. The speculative approach achieves 0.89, indicating high but not perfect faithfulness — the speculative decoding output differs measurably from pure MambaByte. The paper does not analyze where the differences occur (e.g., rare words, long-range dependencies) or whether they affect downstream task performance.

**Diversity of noise conditions (Table 4):** The noise experiments cover six corruption types with varying probability, providing reasonable coverage of realistic input degradation: character-level deletion (Drop), duplication (Repetition), capitalization distortion (Uppercase, Random case, Antspeak), and transposition (Swap). However, all noise is synthetic and applied to text chunks — the paper does not evaluate on naturally noisy text (e.g., OCR output, user-generated content with genuine typos) or adversarial perturbations, which limits the ecological validity of the robustness claims. The chunking scheme (alternating clean and noisy chunks, measuring on clean chunks) tests whether noise disrupts the model's context representation, not whether the model can correctly process the noisy text itself.

### Critical Assessment

**Claim: MambaByte outperforms state-of-the-art byte-level models while being more compute-efficient.**

The evidence for this claim is strong but concentrated on a single model family (MegaByte) as the primary comparison point. Table 2 shows MambaByte-353M beating MegaByte-758M+262M on all five datasets with 0.63× the training data, and Figure 1 shows dramatically better compute-normalized training curves. However, the comparison is primarily against MegaByte variants — the other byte-level baselines (Transformer-320M, PerceiverAR-248M) are cited from prior work and are not FLOPs-matched to MambaByte, making the comparison informative but not rigorously controlled. The Gated-S4D-368M comparison in Figure 1 is FLOPs-matched and does show MambaByte's advantage, confirming that the selective mechanism (not just being an SSM) is responsible for the gains. A missing baseline would be a MambaByte variant without selectivity (using fixed Δ, B, C like S4) to isolate the contribution of the selection mechanism within the Mamba architecture on byte sequences.

**Claim: MambaByte is competitive with state-of-the-art subword Transformers on language modeling.**

Table 3 supports this claim with the 33.0 PPL figure, which beats several published subword models (Transformer-XL at 36.3, Compressive at 33.6, Mamba-1.03B at 33.9) and approaches the best reported results (Block-Recurrent-1.3B at 26.5, PerceiverAR-974.6M at 28.9). However, the comparison is not controlled: the subword models in Table 3 use different architectures, different training data volumes (150B vs. 400B vs. 1.68T bytes), and different context lengths. The most direct comparison is between MambaByte-972M (150B bytes, 33.0 PPL) and Mamba-1.03B (600B bytes effective, 33.9 PPL) — same architecture, different tokenization, not perfectly compute-matched since Mamba-1.03B was trained longer. The paper acknowledges this asymmetry by noting that subword Mamba converges faster (2.2×) but then plateaus, but a truly controlled experiment would train both models to convergence at identical FLOPs and compare. The claim "competitive with state-of-the-art subword Transformers" is broader than what the experiments directly demonstrate: the comparison is primarily on PG19, a single dataset, and against a subset of published subword models, not a comprehensive benchmark.

**Claim: MambaByte is significantly more robust to input noise than subword models.**

Table 4 provides dramatic evidence for this claim, with gaps of 6.7× to 22× in perplexity degradation under some conditions. The ant-speak result (+58,300 vs. +28.3) is particularly striking. The claim is well-supported for the specific synthetic noise types tested. However, the experimental design has important limitations: (a) noise is applied to alternating chunks, testing the model's ability to maintain coherent context representations despite corrupted input, not its ability to process corrupted text directly; (b) the noise types are synthetic and may not represent real-world corruption distributions; (c) only one dataset (PG19 test set) is used; (d) the results are reported as perplexity degradation without error bars, making it unclear whether the differences are statistically significant or how they vary across text types. The paper does not explore whether MambaByte's robustness advantage comes with any tradeoffs — for example, whether MambaByte is more susceptible to certain types of adversarial perturbations that exploit the byte-level representation.

**Claim: Speculative decoding with subword drafting achieves decoding speed matching subword models while preserving the byte-level model's output distribution.**

Table 6 shows a 2.6× speedup, matching subword Mamba's 2.8× within measurement tolerance. The faithfulness metric (log-odds ratio 0.89 vs. 0.10) supports the distribution preservation claim. However, several aspects weaken this evidence. First, the speedup is measured on a single GPU (A100 80GB PCIe) with a specific batch size (presumably 1, since this is autoregressive generation), and throughput may differ under different hardware configurations or with batching. Second, the faithfulness evaluation uses only 100 prompts from "common phrases in the PG19 dataset" — this is a small, potentially biased sample that may not represent diverse generation scenarios. Third, the paper does not report the acceptance rate of drafted subwords, which is the key determinant of speedup; without this, it is unclear whether the 2.6× speedup is typical or optimistic. Fourth, the drafter uses greedy decoding, and the verifier uses a top-3 acceptance criterion — the paper does not explore whether these choices are optimal or how speedup varies with different acceptance thresholds. Fifth, the extrapolation to longer contexts or different text types is not tested.

**Claim: Mamba's fixed-size hidden state removes the architectural barriers to byte-level modeling.**

The paper's conceptual architecture argument is supported by the empirical results (MambaByte matches subword Mamba's perplexity), but the paper does not provide direct evidence for the mechanism it proposes — namely, that the fixed hidden state of size `m × n_state × d` has sufficient capacity to compress byte sequences. The argument that `m × n_state × d ≫ L_ctx` (Section 3) is a necessary condition but not sufficient; the model must also learn to use this capacity effectively. The length extrapolation results (Figures 3 and 4) provide indirect support — if the state were saturated at 8,192 bytes, extrapolation to 32,768 would fail — but the paper does not analyze the hidden state dynamics (e.g., saturation metrics, information-theoretic capacity measurements) to validate the compression hypothesis. A missing experiment would be to vary `n_state` and measure whether performance degrades predictably when the state capacity is reduced, which would directly test the capacity-sufficiency argument.

**Missing ablations and experiments that would strengthen the paper:**

- **Varying SSM state dimension (n_state):** The paper uses `n_state = 16` throughout, following Mamba defaults. Testing `n_state = 4, 8, 32` would reveal whether byte-level modeling requires more state capacity than subword modeling, directly testing the capacity argument.
- **Training bytestacks with different context lengths:** The paper trains all models at 8,192 bytes. Training at 4,096 or 16,384 bytes would show whether the fixed-state compression argument holds at different compression ratios (bytes per state dimension) and whether the model saturates.
- **Purely byte-level baseline without patching at larger scale:** The Transformer-361M is trained on 8,192-byte sequences with full attention, showing it is possible but slow. Training a larger byte-level Transformer (matched to MambaByte-972M in parameters) would provide a stronger architecture comparison, though the computational cost would be prohibitive — this is exactly the paper's point, but it means the claim that "Transformers cannot do this" is argued from complexity analysis rather than demonstrated empirically at the 972M scale.
- **Downstream task evaluation:** All experiments are on language modeling perplexity. The robustness experiments hint at practical benefits, but the paper does not evaluate on tasks where byte-level modeling might show clearer advantages (e.g., spelling correction, code completion with unusual identifiers, multilingual text with script mixing, or handling of rare words in question answering).
- **Training curves for the noise experiments:** Table 4 reports degradation as a single number; showing how perplexity degrades as noise probability increases (a sweep) would reveal whether MambaByte's robustness is continuous or whether there is a phase transition beyond which even byte-level models collapse.
- **Comparison against other efficient architectures:** The paper compares primarily against MegaByte and Gated-S4D. Comparisons against other linear-time architectures proposed concurrently (e.g., RWKV, RetNet, Griffin) would contextualize whether Mamba's advantages for byte-level modeling are shared by other recurrent or linear-attention architectures.
- **Real-world noise evaluation:** Evaluating on naturally noisy text (e.g., social media posts, OCR output from historical documents, code with syntax errors) would complement the synthetic noise experiments and strengthen the practical robustness claim.
- **Scalability to larger models:** The largest MambaByte trained is 972M parameters. Whether the byte-level vs. subword parity holds at the 7B+ parameter scale is an open question the paper acknowledges is important for future work but does not address.

**Conditional validity of claims:**

The paper's central claims hold most strongly at the specific scales tested (353M and 972M parameters) on the specific datasets used (PG19, Stories, Books, ArXiv, Code). The claim that MambaByte outperforms prior byte-level models is robust at these scales. The claim of competitiveness with subword models is strongest for PG19 at the 972M scale; the medium-scale experiments (Table 2) compare only against byte-level baselines, not subword models. The claim of robustness to noise is demonstrated for six synthetic corruption types on one dataset; generalization to other corruption types, natural noise, or other datasets is plausible given prior work on byte-level robustness (Xue et al., 2022) but not directly tested here. The speculative decoding speedup claim is demonstrated on a single hardware configuration with a specific acceptance criterion and draft length; the speedup factor may vary substantially with different choices. The architectural claim about fixed-size memory enabling byte-level modeling is supported by the consistent performance of MambaByte across context lengths, but the specific mechanism (hidden state capacity vs. learned compression) is not directly tested — alternative explanations (e.g., the selective mechanism learning better byte-level representations independent of state capacity) are not ruled out.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### Limitation 1: All experiments rely on a single architecture family (Mamba), obscuring whether the findings generalize to other efficient sequence models

The paper positions MambaByte as evidence that SSMs enable token-free language modeling, but all experiments — both the primary MambaByte models and the speculative decoding system — use only the Mamba architecture (Gu & Dao, 2023). The introduction frames the contribution in terms of SSMs generally: "The recent Mamba state space model (SSM) development offers an appealing alternative approach with a fixed-sized memory state" (Abstract), and Section 3 argues that MambaByte's benefits stem from properties common to SSMs — the fixed-size hidden state, the parallel scan, the recurrent formulation. Yet the paper never tests whether an alternative SSM variant (e.g., S4, S4D, H3, or the gated S4D used as a baseline in Figure 1) or another linear-time architecture (RWKV, RetNet, Griffin; De et al., 2024; Arora et al., 2024) would achieve similar byte-level performance when scaled up.

**Consequence:** The paper cannot distinguish between claims specific to Mamba's selection mechanism and claims about SSMs or recurrent models in general. Figure 1 provides one relevant data point: Gated-S4D-368M underperforms MambaByte-353M at equal FLOPs, suggesting that mere linear-time recurrence is not sufficient — the selective mechanism matters. But Gated-S4D is only one alternative architecture at one scale. The paper's strongest conceptual claim — that "the fixed-sized memory state... naturally removes a major modeling and efficiency issue for byte-level language modeling" — is consistent with the data but is not tested independently of Mamba's specific design choices (selective Δ, B, C; the gating architecture; the 1D convolution). A model with fixed-size memory but without selection might not extrapolate as well, and a model with selection but different parameterization might behave differently. The 2.2× faster convergence of subword Mamba relative to MambaByte (Section 5.2) also raises the possibility that the byte-level training difficulty interacts with Mamba-specific properties in ways that would not hold for other recurrent architectures.

**Evidence:** Figure 1 compares MambaByte against Transformer, MegaByte, and Gated-S4D at the 350–370M parameter scale, but at 972M (Table 3), comparisons are only against Transformer variants and MegaByte. The speculative decoding system (Section 5.3) is built entirely around Mamba — the drafter is a subword Mamba model, and the efficiency arguments about parallel scan verification are specific to Mamba's CUDA kernel implementation. The paper does not cite or compare against other linear-time architectures proposed concurrently, such as RWKV or RetNet. The authors acknowledge that Mamba is "representative of the capabilities of many contemporary LLMs" (Section 4), but this is an assertion, not an empirical finding — the paper provides no evidence about what happens when a different recurrent or linear-attention architecture is applied to byte sequences at scale.

**Mitigation status:** Not addressed. The paper does not claim to study multiple SSM variants and does not suggest this as future work. The limitation is inherent in the paper's design as an application of Mamba to byte-level modeling rather than a systematic architecture comparison for token-free language models. A practitioner deciding whether to adopt token-free modeling would need to know whether the approach transfers to whatever efficient architecture they are using — the paper provides no guidance beyond the Mamba family.

### Limitation 2: The model scales tested (353M–972M parameters) are moderate, with no evidence that byte-level parity with subword models holds at the multi-billion-parameter scales typical of production LLMs

The largest MambaByte model trained is 972M parameters (MambaByte-1.6B is mentioned in Table 5 and Table 9 but is only used for a generation speed benchmark — no perplexity results are reported for it). The paper's headline finding — that byte-level Mamba matches subword Mamba in perplexity (33.0 vs. 33.9 PPL on PG19, Table 3) — is established at the ~1B parameter scale. This is substantially smaller than the 7B–70B+ models used in production LLMs. The paper acknowledges this indirectly in the conclusion: "These findings establish the possibility of token-free language modeling in future large models" — implying the result is demonstrated at current scale and projects to larger models, but is not verified there.

**Consequence:** The scaling behavior of byte-level vs. subword models at larger parameter counts is unknown and may not be favorable. Several mechanisms could cause the parity to break at scale. First, the hidden state capacity argument (Section 3: "m × n_state × d ≫ L_ctx") becomes less obviously true as models scale — if the state dimension grows proportionally with model width d, capacity grows linearly with parameters, but the amount of information in the context that needs compression may grow faster with model capability (larger models can extract more nuanced dependencies from the same text). Second, the 2.2× data efficiency gap observed at 1B scale (subword Mamba converges faster, Section 5.2) could widen at larger scales if the byte-level model requires proportionally more data to learn byte-level regularities — at 7B or 70B parameters, the absolute data volume required for convergence might become prohibitive. Third, the paper's central claim that byte-level and subword models achieve similar perplexity at the same FLOPs is demonstrated at one scale; whether the FLOP ratio remains constant or shifts with scale is untested. A practitioner considering a 7B+ byte-level model would have no empirical basis to predict whether the perplexity parity demonstrated at 1B parameters will persist.

**Evidence:** All perplexity results in Tables 2 and 3 are for models at or below ~1B parameters. The largest model trained is MambaByte-972M (Table 3) on 150B bytes. A MambaByte-1.6B model is configured in Table 9 but only evaluated for generation speed in Table 5 — no perplexity or training curve is reported for it. The FLOPs comparison in Figure 1 is at the 350M scale. The paper compares against subword models up to 1.3B parameters (Block-Recurrent-1.3B, MegaByte-1.3B+350M) and PerceiverAR-974.6M, but the state-of-the-art in language modeling at the time of publication was Llama 2 (7B–70B parameters; Touvron et al., 2023), which the paper cites but does not compare against.

**Mitigation status:** The authors are transparent about the scale limitation and frame it as a direction for future work: "These findings establish the possibility of token-free language modeling in future large models" (Section 7, Conclusion). They do not claim the result would hold at larger scales, but they also do not discuss why it might not. A scaling law analysis (varying model size at fixed byte/subword representation and measuring how the performance gap changes) would be the direct test, but the paper does not conduct one.

### Limitation 3: Speculative decoding speedup is measured under narrow conditions with unreported sensitivity to key hyperparameters

The 2.6× speedup from speculative decoding (Table 6) is presented as a headline result establishing that "MambaByte can be run as fast as the subword Mamba for text generation" (Section 5.3). However, the measurement conditions are specific and thinly documented: the drafter (Mamba-110M) uses greedy decoding and drafts exactly 3 subwords per iteration; the verifier accepts drafted bytes if they fall in its top-3 candidates (β = 3); speed is measured on 100 prompts drawn from "common phrases in the PG19 dataset"; evaluation uses a single GPU (A100 80GB PCIe) with unspecified batch size. The paper does not report the acceptance rate — the fraction of drafted subwords that pass verification — which is the primary determinant of speedup in speculative decoding.

**Consequence:** The generalizability of the 2.6× speedup to other settings is unknown. Several failure modes are plausible. First, if the drafted text has a low acceptance rate (e.g., on out-of-distribution text, code, or highly structured content), the correction step (autoregressive byte generation until a boundary) would be invoked frequently, and each correction requires multiple sequential verifier forward passes — the speedup could degrade substantially or even become a slowdown if the drafter and verifier disagree frequently. Second, the speedup depends on the ratio of drafter speed to verifier speed, which in turn depends on the relative model sizes — the paper tests only one drafter size (110M) paired with one verifier size (972M). A smaller drafter would be faster but have lower acceptance rates; a larger drafter would be more accurate but slower. The paper does not sweep this tradeoff. Third, the speedup is measured for generating 8,192 bytes, which is a specific generation length — the relative overhead of the verification step (one parallel scan per iteration) versus the drafting step (3 autoregressive subword steps) may change with generation length, since longer generations provide more opportunities for the parallel scan's efficiency to compound.

**Evidence:** Table 6 reports only a single speedup figure (2.6×) and a single faithfulness metric (log-odds ratio 0.89) from 100 prompts. The paper does not provide an ablation of (a) draft length (m = 1, 2, 3, 5, 10), (b) acceptance threshold (β = 1, 2, 3, 5, 10), (c) drafter size (70M, 110M, 350M), (d) generation length, or (e) text domain (PG19 vs. Stories vs. Code). The faithfulness evaluation uses greedy MambaByte as reference, but it is unclear whether the 0.89 log-odds ratio would hold for sampling-based generation (where the verifier would need to use rejection sampling to maintain the target distribution, as in Leviathan et al., 2023). The CUDA kernel extension for "resuming during the parallel scan" (Section 3 and Appendix F) is described but its correctness is not experimentally validated (e.g., by comparing parallel scan outputs with and without resumption).

**Mitigation status:** Not addressed. The paper does not discuss the sensitivity of speculative decoding to its hyperparameters, does not sweep acceptance rates, and does not acknowledge the narrow measurement conditions as a limitation. A practitioner implementing this system for a different model scale or text domain would need to re-tune these parameters from scratch without guidance from the paper.

### Limitation 4: Robustness evaluation is limited to synthetic noise on a single dataset, with no evaluation on naturally noisy text or downstream tasks where byte-level modeling might show practical advantages

The paper demonstrates MambaByte's robustness to noise using six synthetic corruption types (drop, repetition, antspeak, uppercase, random case, character swap) applied to the PG19 test set (Table 4). While the results are dramatic — subword Mamba collapses by +58,300 PPL under antspeak while MambaByte degrades by only +28.3 — the evaluation is entirely in terms of perplexity degradation on clean text chunks adjacent to corrupted chunks. The paper does not evaluate on (a) naturally noisy text (social media, OCR output, user-generated content with genuine typos, code with syntax errors), (b) downstream tasks where robustness matters (question answering with misspelled queries, machine translation of noisy input, code completion with irregular identifiers), or (c) tasks where byte-level modeling's finer granularity might provide benefits beyond robustness (e.g., spelling correction, morphological analysis, handling of rare or novel words).

**Consequence:** The practical significance of the robustness advantage is unclear. The experimental design — measuring perplexity on clean chunks when adjacent chunks are corrupted — tests whether noise disrupts the model's context representations, but it does not test whether the model can correctly process the noisy text itself (e.g., predict the next byte after a corrupted prefix). In real deployments, the model encounters the noise directly and must generate coherent continuations despite it. The paper provides no evidence that MambaByte's reduced perplexity degradation translates to better task performance under noise. Additionally, the single-dataset evaluation (PG19 only) leaves open whether the robustness advantage is domain-dependent — PG19 consists of formal, standardized English text from before 1919, which may be less representative of the types of noise encountered in modern user-generated text. The +58,300 PPL degradation for subword Mamba under antspeak is so extreme it suggests complete model breakdown on this specific corruption, but without evaluating on naturally noisy text, it is unclear whether this finding represents realistic worst-case behavior or an artificial stress test with limited ecological validity.

**Evidence:** Table 4 reports perplexity degradation for six noise types on PG19 only. The noise is applied to alternating 100-word chunks, and perplexity is measured on even-indexed (clean) chunks only. The paper cites Xue et al. (2022) to support the claim that byte-level models are robust to noise, stating "our findings align with those observed by Xue et al. (2022)," but Xue et al. (2022) evaluated ByT5 on downstream tasks including question answering, translation, and summarization under noise — a level of task evaluation absent from the current paper. The paper states in Section 5.2 that "tokenized vocabulary fundamentally limits subword models" and that "byte-level models are significantly more resilient to accidental and adversarial spelling errors than subword models," but this conclusion is based solely on perplexity, not on task performance.

**Mitigation status:** Not addressed. The paper does not discuss the gap between perplexity robustness and task robustness, does not evaluate on naturally noisy text, and does not propose downstream task evaluation as future work. The robustness claim in the abstract — "maintaining the benefits of token-free language models, such as robustness to noise" — is presented as established by the experiments, without caveats about the evaluation methodology or domain coverage.

### Limitation 5: Training on fixed 8,192-byte segments may create an artificial ceiling on the model's ability to learn very long-range dependencies, and the capacity of the hidden state to compress arbitrary-length contexts is argued but not tested

The paper argues that Mamba's fixed-size hidden state enables byte-level modeling because "in all but extreme cases, m × n_state × d ≫ L_ctx" — meaning the hidden state has sufficient capacity to compress the entire context (Section 3). The training setup reinforces this: all models are trained with exactly 8,192 bytes of context, and each training example is a contiguous segment from a single document. The length extrapolation results (Figures 3 and 4) show MambaByte maintaining performance at 4× and even 64× the training length, which the paper uses as evidence that the hidden state compression mechanism works. However, the paper does not directly probe whether the hidden state actually encodes all the relevant information from the context or whether the model achieves good perplexity by learning to attend primarily to local context (which would be consistent with the convolution and the selective mechanism focusing on nearby bytes) while discarding genuinely long-range dependencies.

**Consequence:** The paper cannot distinguish between two explanations for MambaByte's length extrapolation: (a) the hidden state successfully compresses long-range information (the paper's claimed mechanism) versus (b) the model learns to ignore most long-range dependencies and relies primarily on local patterns, with the fixed state serving as a short-term buffer rather than a long-term memory. If (b) is true, MambaByte's strong language modeling perplexity would not translate to tasks requiring genuine long-range reasoning (e.g., retrieving a character name mentioned 10,000 bytes ago, or maintaining coherence across document-length generation). The paper's training setup — 8,192-byte fixed segments — may itself encourage the model to learn local dependencies, since the model never sees training examples where crucial information is separated by more than 8,192 bytes. The fact that MambaByte trains on 4× longer sequences than subword Mamba (in raw bytes, though similar in information content) means the byte-level model has more opportunity to learn within-context dependencies, but it also means the model is never trained on dependencies spanning more than ~1,400 words (since 8,192 bytes ≈ 1,400 words at ~5.9 bytes/word). This is a moderate but not extreme context length — a standard subword Transformer with 2,048 tokens sees similar amounts of text.

**Evidence:** The length extrapolation experiments (Figures 3 and 4) measure perplexity at longer contexts but do not test whether the model actually uses the additional context — perplexity could remain flat if the model's predictions are dominated by local context regardless of how much additional text is provided. The paper does not conduct a "needle in a haystack" style experiment (inserting a specific fact early in a long context and testing retrieval at the end) or any other direct test of long-range dependency utilization. The hidden state capacity argument ("m × n_state × d ≫ L_ctx") is stated as self-evident in Section 3 but is never empirically tested by, for example, varying n_state and measuring whether performance degrades predictably when the state is too small. The paper notes that MambaByte shows "slightly better length extrapolation than the subword Mamba" (Section 5.2) and attributes this to training on 4× longer sequences, which suggests the effect is a consequence of training setup rather than an inherent property of the byte-level representation or the fixed-state compression mechanism.

**Mitigation status:** Not addressed. The paper presents the fixed-state memory as a sufficient explanation for MambaByte's performance and does not discuss the possibility that local context dominates the model's predictions. No experiments probe long-range dependency utilization. The authors do not suggest this as a direction for future work.

### Limitation 6: The comparison against subword models on PG19 does not control for training data volume, context length in information terms, or convergence

The paper's most important comparison — MambaByte-972M (33.0 PPL) vs. subword Mamba-1.03B (33.9 PPL) in Table 3 — is presented as evidence that byte-level models can match subword models on language modeling when both use the Mamba architecture. However, the comparison is not rigorously controlled on several dimensions. MambaByte-972M was trained on 150B bytes; subword Mamba-1.03B was trained for approximately 600B effective bytes (based on the asterisk in Table 3, indicating the authors' own training, and Section 5.2's discussion). Subword Mamba processes sequences that are ~4× shorter in raw token count, so for the same number of training steps and batch size, a subword model trained on N tokens sees approximately 4× the raw text of a byte-level model trained on the same number of bytes. The paper acknowledges that subword Mamba converges faster — "2.2× faster" (Section 5.2) — but this means the two models were trained to different degrees of convergence, with subword Mamba having received more effective training.

**Consequence:** The near-identical perplexity (33.0 vs. 33.9) may not represent a true asymptotic parity between byte-level and subword modeling within the Mamba architecture. If subword Mamba converges in 2.2× fewer training bytes (as the paper reports), then at equal training compute, subword Mamba would reach its best perplexity first, while byte-level MambaByte would still be improving. If MambaByte were trained to the same effective data scale (i.e., many more training bytes), its perplexity might improve further or, alternatively, might plateau below subword Mamba. The paper states that subword Mamba "does not improve significantly beyond 150B training bytes" (Section 5.2), which suggests MambaByte at 150B bytes is near its asymptotic performance, but this is an observation about the subword model's convergence curve, not a controlled demonstration that the byte-level model has converged to the same asymptote. The additional context that subword Mamba achieves "near-optimal performance more efficiently than MambaByte, though not 4× faster as expected, but 2.2× faster" introduces an ambiguity: if byte-level modeling is 2.2× less data-efficient, then at large scales where data is the limiting factor (rather than compute), a byte-level model would need proportionally more data to reach the same performance — an important practical consideration that the paper does not explore.

**Evidence:** Table 3 lists MambaByte-972M at 150B bytes trained and Mamba-1.03B at 600B bytes (with asterisks indicating the authors' own training). The paper acknowledges the data volume asymmetry implicitly by noting the 2.2× convergence efficiency gap, but does not train MambaByte to the same effective data volume as subword Mamba to establish asymptotic parity. The PG19 dataset contains ~11.7B bytes (Table 7), meaning 150B training bytes represent approximately 13 epochs over the training set — the model has seen each byte of training data multiple times, which may affect generalization in ways that differ between byte and subword representations. The subword Mamba's 600B effective bytes represent ~51 epochs over the same underlying text (since subword compression is ~4×).

**Mitigation status:** Partially addressed through transparency. The paper reports training data volumes explicitly (Table 3, asterisks) and acknowledges the convergence speed difference (Section 5.2). However, it does not discuss the implications of this difference for the parity claim, does not train to equal convergence, and does not address the data efficiency tradeoff as a limitation of byte-level modeling. A practitioner choosing between byte-level and subword Mamba would need to weigh the 2.2× data efficiency penalty against the robustness benefits, but the paper does not frame this as a tradeoff or provide guidance for making the decision at scale.
