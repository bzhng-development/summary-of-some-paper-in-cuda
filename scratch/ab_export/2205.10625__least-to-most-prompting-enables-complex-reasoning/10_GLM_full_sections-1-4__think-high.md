**2205.10625 — Least-to-Most Prompting Enables Complex Reasoning in Large Language Models**



## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **least-to-most prompting** — a strategy that tackles the challenge of generalizing to harder-than-demonstrated problems by first decomposing a complex task into a series of simpler subproblems (e.g., breaking “How many times can she slide before it closes?” into the intermediate question “How long does each trip take?”) and then solving them sequentially, with each solution building on previously solved subproblems. Evaluated on symbolic manipulation (last-letter concatenation), compositional generalization (SCAN), and math reasoning (GSM8K, DROP) using GPT‑3’s `code-davinci-002` model, least-to-most prompting achieves 99.7% accuracy on SCAN’s length split with only 14 exemplars — compared to just 16% for chain-of-thought prompting — and also substantially outperforms chain-of-thought on length‑12 last‑letter concatenation (74.0% vs. 31.8%). The method excels when the decomposition is straightforward, as in SCAN and last-letter concatenation, while on math word problems requiring more complex multi-step reasoning the gains are more modest (e.g., improving from 39% to 45% on GSM8K problems with ≥5 steps), establishing that its effectiveness hinges on the ability to reliably decompose the problem into simpler subparts.


## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Chain-of-Thought Fails When Problems Get Harder

The paper identifies a specific, well-defined gap in the capabilities of large language models when prompted with chain-of-thought (Wei et al., 2022). Chain-of-thought prompting—providing a few examples of step-by-step reasoning alongside the input—dramatically improves performance on a wide range of natural language reasoning tasks, from arithmetic to symbolic reasoning. However, the paper observes a consistent failure mode: **chain-of-thought performs well when the test problems are at the same complexity level as the exemplars, but its accuracy falls off steeply when the test problems are harder than those seen in the prompt**. This is termed the **easy-to-hard generalization** problem.

Concrete manifestations of this failure appear across all three task families studied in the paper:

- In the **last-letter-concatenation** task, where the input is a list of words and the output is the concatenation of their last letters, chain-of-thought prompting with exemplars containing only short lists (e.g., of length 2–3) achieves 84.2% accuracy on length‑4 lists, but drops to 31.8% on length‑12 lists. The model has never seen a demonstration of how to handle a long chain of concatenations, so it cannot reliably extrapolate.
- On the **SCAN compositional generalization benchmark** (Lake & Baroni, 2018), which requires mapping natural language commands to action sequences, chain-of-thought prompting yields only 16.2% accuracy on the length split—a split where test commands require action sequences longer than any in the training set. The model cannot compose known primitives into novel, longer sequences because the prompt exemplars all stay within a narrow length band.
- For **math word problems** in GSM8K (Cobbe et al., 2021), chain-of-thought performance drops from 76.68% on 2‑step problems to 39.07% on problems requiring five or more reasoning steps. The model has not learned how to break a complex, multi‑step problem into manageable intermediate subproblems; it attempts to solve everything in one long chain and frequently gets lost.

This gap matters both practically and conceptually. Practically, many real-world tasks—composing a multi‑clause instruction, solving a multi‑step mathematical problem, or executing an algorithmic procedure that scales with input length—inherently demand solving harder instances than any set of few‑shot exemplars can cover. An AI system that cannot generalize to harder problems from easy examples is severely limited in its ability to handle novel, complex requests without massive training data. Conceptually, the failure touches on one of the fundamental differences between human intelligence and contemporary machine learning: humans can learn a new skill from a few demonstrations and then apply it to more complex variations—even variations they have never encountered before—by mentally decomposing the problem and reusing partial solutions. Chain-of-thought prompting, for all its strengths, does not teach models this decomposition-and-reuse capability.

### Why This Problem Is Important

The easy-to-hard generalization gap is not just an academic curiosity; it represents a real bottleneck for deploying large language models as general reasoning engines. If a model can only solve problems at the same difficulty as the examples it was given, then the only way to handle harder problems is to provide more (and harder) examples—requiring either vast training corpora or, in the few-shot regime, prompts that already contain the very complexity they are supposed to solve. This runs counter to the promise of large language models as few-shot learners (Brown et al., 2020), which is that they should be able to adapt to new, unseen tasks from a small number of demonstrations.

Moreover, the paper’s results highlight that **chain-of-thought’s brittleness on harder problems is not due to a lack of underlying knowledge or capability, but rather due to an inability to structure the reasoning process appropriately**. When the model is given a decomposition into subproblems, it can often solve each subproblem correctly and, by chaining those solutions, arrive at the correct final answer. The difficulty lies in the model’s inability to generate that decomposition itself when the problem is more complex than any of the exemplars. This observation motivates a prompting strategy that explicitly teaches the model *how to do the decomposition*, not just how to do the reasoning steps.

Finally, the paper’s performance on SCAN—an almost completely solved benchmark in the neural‑symbolic literature—is particularly striking as a demonstration of practical importance. Prior work that achieved perfect accuracy on SCAN required either specialized neural‑symbolic architectures (Chen et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2020; Nye et al., 2020; Shaw et al., 2021; Kim, 2021) or sophisticated grammar induction and training on the full SCAN training set (over 15,000 examples). The fact that a generic, off‑the‑shelf language model can solve SCAN with 99.7% accuracy using just 14 hand‑crafted exemplars—and no training or fine‑tuning whatsoever—reconfigures what we think is possible for end‑to‑end neural models on compositional generalization tasks. It also provides a strong demonstration that the easy-to-hard generalization gap can be bridged by a better prompting strategy, at least when the decomposition is relatively straightforward.

### Prior Approaches and Where They Fall Short

To understand why least-to-most prompting is necessary, we need to examine what existing strategies do and why they fail on harder-than-demonstrated problems.

**Chain-of-thought prompting** (Wei et al., 2022) is the most direct predecessor. It works by providing a few exemplars that contain both the input and a step-by-step reasoning trace leading to the output. The model then generates a similar reasoning trace for a new input. This is highly effective when the new input requires the same number and type of reasoning steps as the exemplars. However, in a chain-of-thought prompt, each exemplar is **independently solved from scratch**. For example, an exemplar for “think, machine, learning” will compute the last letters of all three words and concatenate them in one pass. It does not use the output of “think, machine” as an intermediate building block. Consequently, the model never sees how to break a long list into a shorter list whose output it already knows, and then extend that output by one more letter. When faced with a 12‑word list, the model has no template for how to recursively build up the answer; it simply tries to compute all last letters and concatenate them in a single, long chain, which becomes increasingly error‑prone as the list grows. The paper’s error analysis confirms that most failures in chain-of-thought for long lists are concatenation errors (dropping or adding letters), not incorrect letter extraction.

**Standard few-shot prompting** (without chain-of-thought) is even worse. For the last-letter-concatenation task, it achieves 0% accuracy for all list lengths—the model simply cannot perform the multi‑step computation at all without the scaffolding of intermediate reasoning steps. For SCAN, standard prompting yields 16.7% at best (with `code-davinci-002`), and for math reasoning it hovers around 17% on GSM8K. Without explicit reasoning or decomposition, the model cannot go beyond surface‑level pattern matching.

**Prior work on task decomposition** exists but typically relies on either training a separate decomposition model or on a fixed, rule‑based decomposition. Perez et al. (2020) decompose multi‑hop questions into independent single‑hop subquestions using a trained model, and then answer them with a QA model. Wang et al. (2022a) use iterative prompting with continuous virtual tokens to progressively extract knowledge. Yang et al. (2022) decompose natural language questions into SQL via a rule‑based system, and Wu et al. (2022) let users manually chain LLM steps. None of these approaches teach the language model itself to decompose and sequentially solve in a purely few-shot, training‑free manner. They either require model training, hard‑coded rules, or human‑in‑the‑loop chaining.

**In the specific domain of compositional generalization (SCAN)**, prior work has taken two main paths. One line develops **neural‑symbolic architectures** that combine a neural controller with a symbolic execution engine (Chen et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2020) or that learn latent grammars (Kim, 2021; Nye et al., 2020). These models can achieve perfect accuracy on SCAN but require training on the full training set and often elaborate search procedures over a grammar space. Another line uses **data augmentation** (Andreas, 2020; Akyürek et al., 2021; Lake, 2019) to recombine fragments from training examples, but these methods still fail on the length split—they can generate new combinations of known sub‑commands, but cannot handle the recursion needed for arbitrarily long sequences. Both lines rely on having access to a large training set and a specialized training procedure. The paper’s approach with least-to-most prompting is fundamentally different: it uses only a handful of static prompt exemplars, no training, and a generic language model, yet achieves near‑perfect generalization.

### How This Paper Positions Itself Relative to Existing Work

The paper introduces least-to-most prompting not as a replacement for chain-of-thought, but as a **targeted extension designed to close the easy-to-hard generalization gap**. At a high level, it takes the same few-shot, reasoning‑trace paradigm but adds a crucial new ingredient: teaching the model to first **decompose** a complex problem into a series of simpler subproblems, and then **solve them sequentially**, where each solution is built from the answers to previously solved subproblems. This is explicitly analogized to the educational psychology technique of least-to-most prompting (Libby et al., 2008), where a teacher progressively breaks down a task for a student and uses the student’s success on earlier steps as scaffolding for later steps.

Crucially, the paper demonstrates that **the decomposition itself can be learned from just a few examples**, provided the task is one where the decomposition pattern is consistent and simple to illustrate. For the last-letter-concatenation task, a single decomposition exemplar (Table 1) shows how to turn any list into its sequential sublists, and the solving exemplars (Table 2) show a base case and a recursive step that uses the answer from the previous sublist. The model then applies this pattern to lists of arbitrary length. For SCAN, a small set of decomposition exemplars (Table 6) teaches the model to break complex commands into a sequence of short, primitive commands, and a separate set of mapping exemplars (Table 7) teaches how to translate each primitive command to its action sequence. The two stages together enable the model to handle commands of arbitrary complexity, because the decomposition stage reduces any novel, long command to a sequence of known, short commands, and the solving stage then processes those short commands using the knowledge from the mapping exemplars.

The paper explicitly contrasts this with chain-of-thought prompting, which uses the same mapping exemplars for SCAN but omits the decomposition step. Without decomposition, the model must map the entire long command to its action sequence in one shot—a task it has never seen demonstrated—and fails dramatically. In the last-letter-concatenation task, the chain-of-thought exemplars (Table 3) solve each list from scratch, without using the output of a shorter sublist as a building block, so the model never learns the recursive, length‑generalizing strategy.

Thus, the paper’s position is that **decomposition is the missing piece that enables easy-to-hard generalization**. It does not claim that least-to-most prompting is a universal remedy for all reasoning tasks; rather, its effectiveness depends on how straightforward it is to demonstrate the decomposition in the prompt. For tasks like SCAN and last-letter concatenation, where the decomposition is essentially algorithmic and can be illustrated with a handful of examples, least-to-most prompting achieves near‑perfect generalization far beyond any demonstration. For math word problems, where the decomposition is more ad‑hoc and problem‑specific, the paper shows that the gains are more modest—still significant on the hardest problems, but not transformative overall—because the model must learn a general decomposition strategy from just one or two examples, which is considerably harder.

By situating itself as a general, training‑free prompting strategy that can be combined with other techniques (like self‑consistency or chain‑of‑thought itself), the paper offers a practical, immediately deployable method for tasks where the difficulty comes from composition, length, or number of steps—exactly the tasks where chain‑of‑thought prompting is known to stumble. Its results on SCAN, in particular, serve as a strong proof of concept that large language models can acquire compositional generalization capabilities from a handful of examples if they are taught how to decompose and solve progressively, and that the gap between human intelligence (solving harder problems than seen) and machine intelligence can be narrowed through better prompting strategies.


## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation (Approachable Technical Breakdown)

**What the system is.** Least-to-most prompting is a purely inference-time strategy — it is not a model architecture, a training procedure, or a fine‑tuning technique. It is a *way of constructing and sequencing prompts* that teaches a frozen large language model to solve a hard problem by first decomposing it into simpler subproblems, and then solving those subproblems one by one, with each solution built from the answers to the previous subproblems.

**What problem it solves and the “shape” of the solution.** The paper targets the *easy‑to‑hard generalization* failure: a language model that can solve problems at the same complexity as its few‑shot exemplars but collapses when the test instance is harder (longer, more compositional, more steps). The solution shape is **two separate stages** — a decomposition stage that breaks the hard input into a sequence of easier sub‑problems, and a solving stage that processes that sequence recursively — both implemented by different, carefully crafted few‑shot prompts. For some tasks the two stages are merged into one composite prompt, but the underlying principle of decomposition‑then‑sequential‑solving remains.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

Imagine a user gives the system a complex question — say “How many times can Amy slide before the water slide closes?” with the surrounding story. The system’s pipeline has three major components:

1. **Decomposition prompt** — Contains a small set of example problems together with their decompositions into subproblems. The prompt is used to ask the language model: “Given this new hard problem, break it into a list of simpler subproblems that, when solved in order, will yield the final answer.”

2. **Subproblem-solving prompt** — Contains a set of examples showing how to solve individual subproblems. Crucially, the exemplars teach a *recursive* or *bootstrapping* pattern: the answer to a later subproblem is expressed in terms of the answer to an earlier, already‑solved subproblem. The prompt also includes a placeholder for previously solved subproblems and their answers.

3. **Sequential chaining loop** — The system takes the list of subproblems produced by the decomposition prompt, and then iterates through them one by one. For the first subproblem, it sends a prompt that includes the solving exemplars and the subproblem text. The model’s answer is recorded. For each subsequent subproblem, the prompt is augmented with the previous subproblem-answer pair, and the model is asked to solve the next subproblem, with the final subproblem being the original question itself. The last answer is the final output.

In some tasks the two stages are merged into a single prompt (e.g., the math reasoning case), but the logical flow — decompose, then sequentially solve — is still present.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence‑Based Technical Breakdown

This is an **analysis / method paper** whose core contribution is a prompting strategy. It is not a model architecture paper, nor does it involve training or fine‑tuning. The entire “system” is a procedure for constructing and issuing prompts to a black‑box language model at inference time. The central idea is that **decomposing a hard problem into a sequence of simpler subproblems and solving them recursively, using the answers to earlier subproblems as building blocks, is something a language model can learn from a handful of static examples — if the prompts are designed to teach that pattern explicitly**.

---

#### 3.4.1 The Two‑Stage Decomposition‑and‑Solving Paradigm

Least‑to‑most prompting is defined in Section 2 as a two‑stage process:

1. **Decomposition stage:** The prompt in this stage contains constant examples that demonstrate how to break a complex problem into a list of simpler subproblems, followed by the specific problem to be decomposed. The language model is queried to produce a list of subproblems.

2. **Subproblem solving stage:** The prompt in this stage consists of three parts: (i) constant examples that demonstrate how individual subproblems are solved; (ii) a (potentially empty) list of previously answered subquestions together with their generated solutions; and (iii) the next subquestion to be answered. The model is queried sequentially, and the answer to each subproblem is appended to the context before asking the next one.

The key design decision is **separating decomposition from solving**. If the two were combined into a single prompt (as in standard chain‑of‑thought, where each exemplar solves a complete problem from scratch), the model would never see the pattern of using partial results to build the final answer, and would therefore fail when the problem is longer or more complex than any exemplar. By splitting the two, the model sees a *decomposition pattern* that is independent of the problem’s length (e.g., “any list can be turned into its sequential sublists”) and a *solving pattern* that is explicitly recursive (e.g., “the answer to a longer list is the answer to the shorter list plus one more letter”). These two patterns, when combined, enable length generalization.

---

#### 3.4.2 Decomposition Prompt: Teaching the Model to Break Problems Down

The decomposition prompt is a few‑shot prompt that maps a complex input to a *list of subproblems*. The paper provides two concrete instantiations, for the last‑letter‑concatenation task and for SCAN.

**Last‑letter‑concatenation decomposition (Table 1).** The prompt contains one exemplar:

> Q: “think, machine, learning”  
> A: “think”, “think, machine”, “think, machine, learning”

The response is a list of sequential sublists that start from the first word and then add one word at a time. For a new list of arbitrary length, the model is given the same exemplar followed by the new list, and it produces an analogous sequence of sublists. The paper reports that this decomposition prompt achieves **100% accuracy** on lists up to length 12 — the model perfectly generalises the pattern of “create sequential sublists” from a single example.

**SCAN command decomposition (Table 6, full context in Appendix 8.2).** Here the decomposition is more elaborate because SCAN commands contain nesting and conjunctions. The prompt contains **8 exemplars** that show how to decompose a long command into a sequence of shorter, primitive commands. For instance:

> Q: “look opposite right thrice after walk”  
> A: “look opposite right thrice” can be solved by: “look opposite right”, “look opposite right thrice”. “walk” can be solved by “walk”. So, “look opposite right thrice after walk” can be solved by: “look opposite right”, “look opposite right thrice”, “walk”.

The model learns to identify the syntactic constituents of a command (e.g., clauses joined by “after” or “and”, modified by “twice”/“thrice”), and to replace each complex constituent with its simplest form. The output is a list of *short commands* that are either directly known primitives or that can themselves be further decomposed. This is a top‑down, recursive decomposition: the model does not solve the subcommands; it merely rewrites the problem into a list of smaller problems. The paper explicitly states that the decomposition prompt is separate from the mapping prompt that translates commands to actions; the decomposition prompt contains **no action sequences** and **no translations** to action sequences. Its only job is to produce the list of subcommands.

**Why decomposition can be taught with few examples.** The key property is that the decomposition rule is *systematic* and *length‑independent*. For the last‑letter task, the rule is “turn a list into its sequential sublists”, which can be illustrated with a single list of any length. For SCAN, the rules follow the compositional grammar of the synthetic language — once you see how “after”, “and”, “twice”, and “around” decompose, the pattern applies to any nesting depth. The paper explicitly notes that exceptional results on SCAN and last‑letter concatenation are achieved because “decomposition in these tasks is relatively straightforward” (Section 5).

---

#### 3.4.3 Subproblem‑Solving Prompt: Recursive Building from Prior Answers

Once the problem has been decomposed into a list of subproblems, the solving stage must process them *sequentially*, with each step using the output of the previous step. The prompt for this stage teaches a template that **explicitly references the answer to a previously solved subproblem** as a building block for the current subproblem.

**Last‑letter‑concatenation solving prompt (Table 2).** The prompt contains two exemplars that together form a base‑case and recursive‑step pair:

- **Base case:** `“think, machine” → “ke”` with full explanation of how last letters are extracted and concatenated.
- **Recursive step:** `“think, machine, learning” → “keg”`, but the response starts with *“think, machine” outputs “ke”*. The last letter of “learning” is “g”. Concatenating “ke”, “g” leads to “keg”.*

The crucial design is that the second exemplar does **not** compute the answer from scratch. Instead, it takes the output of the first, shorter list (“ke”) and concatenates it with the new last letter. This teaches the model: *when you need to solve a longer list, you can use the answer of the shorter list that you already solved, and just append the new piece.* The model then applies this pattern to any length.

**SCAN command‑mapping prompt (Table 7, full context in Appendix 8.2).** The prompt contains **14 exemplars** that map primitive commands to action sequences, using Python notation as an intermediate representation. For example:

> Q: “jump left”  
> A: The output of “jump left” concatenates: the output of “turn left”, the output of “jump”. “turn left” outputs “TURN_LEFT”. “jump” outputs “JUMP”. So concatenating the output of “turn left” and the output of “jump” leads to “TURN_LEFT” + “JUMP”. So the output of “jump left” is “TURN_LEFT” + “JUMP”.

The exemplars cover all primitive actions (`turn left`, `turn right`, `jump`, `run`, `look`, `walk`) and all modifiers (`twice`, `thrice`, `opposite`, `around`, `after`, `and`). The model is taught how to translate each primitive command into a Python expression (e.g., `“TURN_LEFT” * 2`, `(“TURN_RIGHT” + “RUN”) * 4`), and how to compose them when a command is built from subcommands (e.g., concatenating the output of a subcommand with another). The critical point is that these exemplars **do not show how to translate an entire long command from scratch**. They only translate short, primitive commands. The model’s ability to handle long commands comes from the decomposition stage: it breaks the long command into a sequence of short commands that are all in the set of primitives seen in the mapping prompt, and then each short command is translated by a call to the mapping prompt.

**Contrast with chain‑of‑thought.** In chain‑of‑thought prompting (Table 3 for last‑letter, the same mapping exemplars for SCAN but without decomposition), every exemplar solves its problem independently from scratch. For the last‑letter task, the chain‑of‑thought response to “think, machine, learning” explicitly lists all three last letters and concatenates them — it never uses the answer to “think, machine” as a building block. This means the model never sees the recursive pattern, and when faced with a 12‑word list, it just tries to do a single, flat 12‑step reasoning chain, which fails. The paper’s error analysis confirms that most chain‑of‑thought failures are concatenation errors (dropping or adding letters), not incorrect letter extraction.

#### 3.4.4 Chaining the Stages: From Subproblem List to Final Answer

The two stages are chained together by a simple iterative procedure (described in Section 2 and illustrated in Figure 1):

1. **Decomposition call.** The original problem is appended to the decomposition prompt (the few‑shot exemplars). The resulting text is sent to the language model, which outputs a list of subproblems (in the format demonstrated in the decomposition exemplars). For the last‑letter task, this is a list of sequential sublists; for SCAN, it is a list of short commands.

2. **Sequential solving loop.** The system initialises an empty “solved context”. For each subproblem in the order produced by the decomposition:
   - Construct a prompt that consists of: (i) the fixed solving exemplars (the recursive/mapping exemplars); (ii) the previously solved subproblem‑answer pairs (if any); (iii) the current subproblem text.
   - Send this prompt to the language model.
   - Append the model’s response to the “solved context” as a new previously‑solved pair.
   - If the current subproblem is the final subproblem (the original problem), the model’s response is taken as the final output.

The paper makes clear that for some tasks, the final subproblem **is** the original problem itself. For example, in the last‑letter task, the decomposition produces sequential sublists, and the last sublist is the full original list — so solving that last sublist yields the final answer. In SCAN, the decomposition produces a list of subcommands, and the last subcommand is the original command — so mapping that last subcommand yields the final action sequence. In the math reasoning case (Figure 1), the decomposition produces subquestions like “How long does each trip take?”, and after that is solved, the original question “How many times can she slide?” is asked again, with the answer to the intermediate question now in context.

**Appending vs. replacing context.** The model’s generated answer is appended to the prompt text. The prompt for the next subproblem thus contains the full history of the decomposition tree and all previous solutions. This is a form of *state passing through the context window* — the model’s own output becomes part of the input for the next step. There is no hidden state or memory beyond the text that is fed into the model; the model is stateless between calls.

#### 3.4.5 Adaptation for Math Word Problems: Merging Decomposition and Solving

For the GSM8K and DROP benchmarks (Section 3.3), the paper uses a **merged prompt** that combines decomposition and solving into a single pass. The prompt (Table 9) is:

> Q: Elsa has 5 apples. Anna has 2 more apples than Elsa. How many apples do they have together?  
> A: Let’s break down this problem: 1. How many apples does Anna have? 2. How many apples do they have together?  
> 1. Anna has 2 more apples than Elsa. So Anna has $2 + 5 = 7$ apples.  
> 2. Elsa and Anna have $5 + 7 = 12$ apples together.  
> The answer is: 12.

Here, the decomposition instruction is in the first part (“Let’s break down this problem …”) and the solving steps follow immediately. The model sees a single complete demonstration of how to decompose and solve a 2‑step problem. The paper’s hypothesis is that this single exemplar teaches the model to apply the same pattern to problems with more steps: the model will first decompose the problem into numbered subproblems, and then solve them sequentially, referencing earlier answers within the same generation. Because there is only one exemplar, the model must generalise the decomposition pattern from a 2‑step example to problems requiring 5 or more steps.

**Why merge for math but not for SCAN/last‑letter?** The paper notes that for SCAN and last‑letter, decomposition and solving are naturally separated: decomposition is about syntactic rewriting (turning a list into sublists, or a command into a sequence of subcommands) and solving is about semantic mapping (translating to action sequences). Keeping the prompts separate allows the decomposition prompt to be extremely simple (1 exemplar for last‑letter, 8 for SCAN) and the solving prompt to be a library of primitive mappings. For math word problems, the decomposition is not a mechanical syntactic rewriting — it requires semantic understanding of the problem’s structure. The paper tries a merged design because it is simpler, and because the two stages are interleaved: the decomposition of a subproblem often depends on the result of the previous subproblem. The paper acknowledges that one could “instead design two different prompts respectively for decomposition and subproblem solving, as the least‑to‑most prompts in the previous sections, to further improve performance”, suggesting that the merged approach is a deliberate simplification, not a fundamental requirement of the method.

---

#### 3.4.6 Design Choices and Trade‑offs

**Why separate decomposition and solving?** The core reason is that each stage teaches a different, reusable skill. The decomposition prompt teaches *how to break a problem down*, independent of what the subproblems mean. The solving prompt teaches *how to solve a small problem* (and how to use prior answers). If you combine them, the model sees a single, monolithic reasoning trace and does not learn that the decomposition is a separate, generalisable operation that can be applied to any length. This is why chain‑of‑thought fails on length generalisation: it never isolates the decomposition pattern.

**Use of Python notation as an intermediate representation.** For SCAN, both least‑to‑most and the baselines use Python expressions (e.g., `“TURN_LEFT” * 2`, `(“TURN_RIGHT” + “RUN”) * 4`) as the target output, and a postprocessing script expands the Python expressions to the final action sequences. The paper explains that this is to “make our prompts … concise and meet the input size limit of language models (usually up to 2048 tokens)”. Without this, the action sequences would be extremely long (e.g., repeating `TURN_LEFT LOOK` many times). The model is taught to produce these Python expressions as the “answer” to the command; a separate postprocessing step expands them. The paper shows in Appendix 8.4 that the model can also be prompted to expand these Python expressions with 99.7% accuracy, but for the main experiments the expansion is done by a script.

**Number of exemplars.** The paper uses:
- **Last‑letter concatenation:** decomposition prompt with 1 exemplar (Table 1), solving prompt with 2 exemplars (Table 2 — one base case, one recursive step). The total is 3 exemplars (or 4 in the 4‑shot least‑to‑most variant in Appendix 7.2.5). The paper also studies increasing the number of exemplars and finds that more examples help (Table 13), but 2‑shot least‑to‑most still outperforms 8‑shot chain‑of‑thought.
- **SCAN:** decomposition prompt with 8 exemplars (Appendix 8.2), command‑mapping prompt with 14 exemplars (Appendix 8.2). The 14 mapping exemplars are chosen to “fully cover the semantics of the SCAN commands”.
- **GSM8K / DROP:** a single merged prompt with 1 demonstration example (Table 9 for GSM8K; DROP uses up to 3 examples for solving and 5–6 for decomposition, as detailed in Appendix 9).

**Why does the method work on hard problems when the exemplars are easy?** Because the decomposition exemplars show a *length‑independent* pattern: “a list can be turned into its sequential sublists” is a rule that works for any length, even though the example shows a list of length 3. The solving exemplars show a *recursive* pattern: “the answer to a longer list is the answer to the shorter list plus one new piece.” The model can then apply this recursive template to arbitrary lengths, because it can repeatedly call the same pattern: for a 12‑word list, it first solves the 2‑word prefix using the base case, then uses the recursive template to extend to 3 words, then 4, …, up to 12. The model is doing *iterated decoding*, not, however, by internal recurrence; it is by issuing separate prompts where the context contains the previous solution. This is why the method works for length generalisation: it converts a long‑length problem into a sequence of short‑length problems, each of which is within the model’s capability, and the chaining is done outside the model through the prompt.

**Error modes.** The paper’s error analysis (Section 7.4, Appendix 8.2) reveals that even when least‑to‑most prompting fails, the failures are mostly not because the model cannot decompose or solve the subproblems, but because of subtle mistakes in applying the learned rules. For last‑letter, the main errors are concatenation errors (dropping a letter, adding a letter, or ordering wrong) and occasional wrong template application (using the recursive template instead of the base case for the first two words). For SCAN, the 13 failures in `code‑davinci‑002` are: 6 mis‑interpretations of “twice”/“thrice” following “around”, and the rest misinterpret “after” as “and”. This shows that the model has learned a nearly correct grammar, but still makes mistakes on the most complex compositional permutations — a reminder that the approach is teaching a pattern, not a formal proof.

**Combining with other prompting techniques.** The paper states that least‑to‑most prompting *can* be combined with chain‑of‑thought (e.g., using chain‑of‑thought for the solving prompt) and with self‑consistency (Wang et al., 2022b), but *does not need to be*. The experiments in the paper do not use self‑consistency; they use greedy decoding for the final evaluation. The method is presented as a standalone prompting strategy that, in its basic form, already exceeds the baselines by large margins.

**Relationship to the educational psychology concept.** The term “least‑to‑most prompting” is borrowed from Libby et al. (2008), where it refers to a teaching technique that starts with the least assistance and progressively adds more prompts until the learner succeeds. The paper inverts this for language models: here, the model is *given* the most difficult problem, and the prompting *produces* a sequence of easier and easier subproblems, then solves them in order. This is a “top‑down decomposition, bottom‑up resolution” approach, as the paper describes it.


## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Easy-to-Hard Generalization as a Distinct Failure Mode and Decomposition as the Missing Capability

The paper’s most fundamental conceptual move is **recasting the drop‑off in chain‑of‑thought accuracy not as a general reasoning failure but as a failure to produce the right problem decomposition**. Prior work (Wei et al., 2022) had established that LLMs can generate step‑by‑step rationales when the test problem is of comparable complexity to the exemplars, but it did not diagnose *why* performance collapses on harder instances. This paper provides a clear diagnosis: the model **possesses the individual reasoning steps** (extracting last letters, translating a short command, performing a single arithmetic operation) but **cannot structure them into a coherent, multi‑step plan** when the problem exceeds the complexity of any single demonstration. The evidence is in the error patterns — chain‑of‑thought failures on long lists are mostly concatenation errors (dropping or adding letters), not incorrect letter extraction (Table 14) — and in the fact that providing the correct decomposition turns a failed GSM8K problem into a successful one (Section 3.3). This is not an incremental observation; it is a *diagnostic reframing* that shifts the research question from “can models reason step‑by‑step?” to “can models learn to **generate** the right decomposition for a novel, harder problem?”. The idea that the bottleneck is decomposition rather than execution has direct implications for how we design prompts and for what we should expect from future models.

### Innovation 2: Two‑Stage Prompt Design as a Separation‑of‑Concerns That Teaches Recursive Generalization

Before this work, the dominant approach to few‑shot reasoning was to show a handful of complete, independently‑solved exemplars — chain‑of‑thought, for instance, solves each list from scratch (Table 3). The paper’s central design innovation is to **split the teaching signal into two distinct, reusable sub‑skills**: (i) a **decomposition pattern** that is length‑independent (e.g., “any list can be turned into its sequential sublists” — Table 1) and (ii) a **recursive solving pattern** that uses the output of an earlier, simpler subproblem as a building block (Table 2). By keeping these two prompts separate, the model is never shown a monolithic, length‑specific solution; instead, it learns a general **algorithm** — decompose into a sequence of small subproblems, then repeatedly apply a base case and a recursive step. This is a *fundamental advance over prior prompting strategies* because it teaches the model *how to handle arbitrarily long/complex inputs* without ever seeing an exemplar of that length or complexity. The contrast with chain‑of‑thought is stark: the same two exemplars, when presented in an independent‑solving format (Table 3), yield 31.8% accuracy on length‑12 lists, while the recursive, two‑stage format yields 74.0% (Table 13) — and even 8‑shot chain‑of‑thought cannot match 2‑shot least‑to‑most on long lists. The separation of decomposition from solving is the key enabler: if the two are merged (as in chain‑of‑thought), the model never sees the recursive pattern, and length generalisation collapses.

### Innovation 3: Prompting Alone Achieves Near‑Perfect Compositional Generalisation Without Specialised Architectures or Training

The paper’s result on SCAN — **99.7% accuracy on the length split with only 14 hand‑crafted exemplars** and no model training whatsoever — is not just a performance number; it is a *conceptual reframing* of what is required to achieve systematic compositional generalisation. For years, the dominant assumption in the SCAN literature was that solving the length split required either **specialised neural‑symbolic architectures** (Chen et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2020; Nye et al., 2020; Shaw et al., 2021; Kim, 2021) trained on the full training set (over 15,000 examples), or elaborate grammar induction and data‑augmentation procedures that still failed on the hardest split. The paper demonstrates that a generic, off‑the‑shelf language model can **internalise the entire compositional grammar of SCAN from a static, few‑shot prompt** when that prompt teaches it to decompose and solve progressively. This reconfigures the debate about neural network systematicity: it suggests that the limitation was never in the architecture’s expressive capacity but in the **training signal or prompting strategy**. The model does not need to learn the compositional rules from massive data; it needs to be shown how to apply them through a decomposition‑solving pipeline. The result also generalises across all SCAN splits (including the full dataset) and across multiple models (code‑davinci‑002, text‑davinci‑002, code‑davinci‑001), with the code‑davinci‑002 variant achieving 99.7% on the length split (Table 8). This is a powerful proof of concept that **prompting can substitute for architecture design and large‑scale training** on tasks where a systematic decomposition is available.

### Innovation 4: The Method’s Success Depends on the Systematicity of the Decomposition — a Boundary Condition That Explains When and Why It Works

Not all tasks benefit equally from least‑to‑most prompting. The paper’s results on math word problems (GSM8K, DROP) are **modest compared to SCAN** — a 1.5 percentage point overall gain on GSM8K, driven primarily by problems with ≥5 steps (45.23% vs. 39.07% for chain‑of‑thought; Table 12). The paper does not treat this as a weakness but as a **critical diagnostic insight**: the method works best when the decomposition is systematic and can be illustrated with a small number of examples. For SCAN and last‑letter concatenation, the decomposition rule (turn a command into subcommands, turn a list into sequential sublists) is **algorithmic and length‑independent**; a single well‑chosen exemplar can teach it. For math word problems, the decomposition requires **semantic understanding of the problem structure** and is highly variable — a one‑shot decomposition example (Table 9) can only weakly generalise to multi‑step problems with different structures. The paper explicitly states: “almost every problem in GSM8K that least‑to‑most prompting fails to solve can be eventually solved by using a manually crafted decomposition” (Section 3.3). This tells us that the **bottleneck is decomposition generation, not subproblem solving**. The insight is not that least‑to‑most prompting is limited; it is that the approach **offers a clean, practical boundary condition** for when a prompting‑only strategy can achieve dramatic easy‑to‑hard generalization — and that for tasks where decomposition is ad‑hoc and complex, the method still provides a useful diagnostic tool (if you can decompose it, you can solve it), but the decomposition itself may need to be learned through more training or more sophisticated prompting. This frames the future research direction as **improving decomposition generation** for semantic, non‑algorithmic problems.
