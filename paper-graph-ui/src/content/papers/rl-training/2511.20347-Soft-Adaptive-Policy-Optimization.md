## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **Soft Adaptive Policy Optimization (SAPO)**, a reinforcement learning algorithm for fine-tuning large language models that replaces conventional hard clipping with a smooth, temperature-controlled gating function — a continuous trust region that adaptively attenuates off-policy token updates (using a sigmoid-shaped weight centered at the on-policy point, parameterized by separate temperatures for positive and negative advantage tokens) — while preserving sequence-level coherence under mild conditions. On mathematical reasoning benchmarks (AIME25, HMMT25, BeyondAIME) with Qwen3-30B-A3B, SAPO sustains stable training substantially longer than GSPO and GRPO-R2, both of which experience early collapse, and achieves higher final Pass@1 accuracy — for instance, reaching approximately 0.82 on AIME25 versus roughly 0.80 for the baselines — with the asymmetric temperature design (τ_neg = 1.05 > τ_pos = 1.0) proving critical, as symmetric or inverted temperatures induce instability, establishing that smooth token-level gating improves RL training robustness only when negative-token gradients are more aggressively dampened than positive-token gradients.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Variance and Instability in Token-Level RL for LLMs

The fundamental question this paper tackles is: **how do you perform stable, efficient policy gradient updates when fine-tuning large language models with reinforcement learning, given that token-level importance ratios exhibit high variance?** This is not merely a theoretical concern — it is the central engineering challenge that determines whether an RL training run will produce a useful model or collapse into incoherence after a few hundred gradient steps.

The paper frames this as a problem of **off-policy correction**. In the group-based RL paradigm (Section 2), the policy gradient is computed using responses sampled from a *behavior policy* (the model at some previous checkpoint, denoted π_θ_old), but the gradient estimator must be valid for the *current policy* π_θ. The standard importance-weighted estimator multiplies each token's log-probability gradient by the ratio r_i,t(θ) = π_θ(y_i,t | q, y_i,<t) / π_θ_old(y_i,t | q, y_i,<t). When this ratio deviates far from 1 — which happens routinely as the model's parameters shift during training — the gradient updates become unreliable. Too-large ratios produce gradient explosions; too-small ratios produce vanishing signals. Both lead to unstable training dynamics.

What makes this particularly acute for LLMs, as the paper emphasizes in Section 1, is that **Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures amplify this variance**. MoE models route each token through a subset of "expert" sub-networks, and the routing decisions themselves change as parameters update. This introduces an additional source of heterogeneity: tokens that pass through different experts during behavior-policy sampling versus current-policy evaluation can exhibit markedly different importance ratios. The paper explicitly notes that "routing heterogeneity and long responses can amplify deviations across tokens" (Section 1), making MoE models — which are increasingly dominant in frontier LLM deployments — especially susceptible to instability under standard policy optimization methods.

The practical consequence is stark: training runs that begin promisingly can suddenly diverge. In Figure 4, both GSPO and GRPO-R2 exhibit what the paper calls "early training collapse" — their reward curves and validation performance plateau and then degrade well before SAPO's do. This translates directly to wasted compute, lost research time, and models that underperform their potential.

### Why This Problem Matters: Scale, Cost, and Reliability

The paper's motivation is grounded in several practical and theoretical concerns:

**The shift toward RL-based post-training.** The field has converged on a two-phase paradigm for building capable reasoning LLMs: large-scale pretraining followed by RL-based fine-tuning that teaches the model to produce correct chains of reasoning (DeepSeek-AI, 2025; OpenAI, 2024; Qwen, 2025). The RL phase is where much of the reasoning capability is acquired — it is where the model learns to check its work, backtrack from dead ends, and produce step-by-step solutions that lead to correct answers. If the RL optimizer is unstable, this entire phase is compromised. The paper's experiments are directly relevant to this paradigm: they train from a "cold-start" checkpoint (the base model before RL) and measure how reliably each algorithm can improve reasoning performance.

**The cost of instability at scale.** Training runs at the scale described in this paper (Qwen3-30B-A3B, Qwen3-VL models) consume enormous computational resources. An early collapse at, say, step 600 means that the remaining allocated compute is wasted — the model degrades rather than improves. An algorithm that extends stable training by even a few hundred steps can mean the difference between a model that performs at the frontier and one that fails. The paper's Figure 4 shows GSPO and GRPO-R2 collapsing before SAPO by hundreds of gradient steps. In a production setting, this is the difference between shipping a model and restarting training.

**The brittleness of current methods across architectures.** The paper tests on both MoE (Qwen3-30B-A3B) and dense (Qwen3-4B) architectures, and on both text-only and multimodal (Qwen3-VL) models. A method that works on dense models but fails on MoE models — or vice versa — is not practically deployable in an organization building models across both architectures. The paper explicitly demonstrates (Figures 2 and 3) that the intra-sequence dispersion of token ratios is wider for MoE models than dense models, confirming that architectural choices directly affect the stability landscape. This means a one-size-fits-all clipping parameter is unlikely to be optimal — or even safe — across model families.

**The tension between stability and sample efficiency.** The paper's conceptual critique of existing methods is that they force a tradeoff: you can be stable (by aggressively discarding off-policy data) or sample-efficient (by using more of the data), but not both simultaneously. SAPO's design is motivated by the desire to break this tradeoff — to maintain stability while preserving gradient information from moderately off-policy tokens.

### Where Existing Approaches Fall Short

The paper analyzes two leading group-based policy optimization methods — GRPO and GSPO — and identifies specific failure modes in each.

**GRPO: Hard token-level clipping discards information abruptly.** GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) applies PPO-style clipping at the token level: if the importance ratio r_i,t(θ) falls outside the band [1 − ε, 1 + ε], the token's gradient is zeroed out entirely. The clipping gate is binary — a token is either fully included or fully excluded. As the paper puts it in Section 4.2:

> "GRPO employs a binary trust region: tokens inside receive the same gradient as that of the unclipped objective; tokens outside receive zero gradient."

This all-or-nothing behavior creates several problems. First, the choice of ε is critical and fragile: too tight, and many tokens are discarded, reducing the effective sample size and slowing learning; too loose, and high-variance tokens introduce noise that destabilizes training. There is no intermediate regime — no way to say "this token is somewhat off-policy, so I'll use its gradient but at reduced weight." Second, because the clipping threshold is fixed and uniform across all tokens and all training stages, it cannot adapt to the fact that some tokens (e.g., those with negative advantages) contribute more to instability than others. Third, the binary nature means that a token with r_i,t(θ) = 1 + ε + 0.001 gets zero weight, while one at 1 + ε − 0.001 gets full weight — a discontinuity that introduces its own optimization noise.

The paper's solution — replacing this binary gate with a smooth sigmoid-shaped gate (Equation 6, Figure 1) — is conceptually simple but has deep implications. The smooth gate means that gradients don't vanish abruptly at a hard boundary; they decay continuously as the ratio deviates from 1. This provides a graduated response: near-on-policy tokens get near-full weight, moderately off-policy tokens get partial weight, and severely off-policy tokens are effectively suppressed. No single hard threshold needs to be tuned.

**GSPO: Sequence-level clipping sacrifices token-level information.** GSPO (Zheng et al., 2025) addresses variance by moving from token-level to sequence-level importance ratios. Rather than clipping each token's ratio individually, GSPO computes a length-normalized sequence ratio s_i(θ) (Equation 4) — the geometric mean of the token ratios — and applies the hard clip to this single scalar. All tokens in a sequence share the same clipped weight. This reduces variance because averaging across tokens smooths out individual fluctuations, and the length normalization places sequences of different lengths on a common scale.

However, this averaging has a dark side that the paper identifies and exploits as a key motivational insight. The sequence-level ratio s_i(θ) can be pushed outside the clipping band by a small number of highly off-policy tokens — even if the majority of tokens in the sequence are well-behaved. When this happens, GSPO's binary clip **zeroes out gradients for the entire sequence**, including the many near-on-policy tokens that could have provided useful learning signal. The paper frames this sharply in Section 1:

> "When a sequence contains a few highly off-policy tokens, GSPO suppresses all gradients for that sequence, whereas SAPO selectively down-weights only the offending tokens and preserves the learning signal from the near-on-policy ones, improving sample efficiency."

This is the paper's central critique of sequence-level clipping: it is **sequence-coherent but not token-adaptive**. It treats a sequence as a monolithic unit, failing to recognize that within a mostly-valid response, a few tokens may be distant from the behavior policy while the rest are on-policy. In the MoE setting, where expert routing changes can cause specific tokens to have anomalous ratios (as evidenced by the wider Vari(θ) distribution in Figure 2 versus Figure 3), this failure mode becomes more frequent. A single mistoken due to a routing change can cause GSPO to discard an otherwise informative training example.

**Neither method handles the asymmetry between positive and negative updates.** A third limitation — shared by both GRPO and GSPO — is that they apply symmetric clipping to tokens with positive and negative advantages. The paper argues in Section 3 (under "Why Different Temperatures for Positive and Negative Advantages") that this is fundamentally inappropriate for the large-vocabulary setting of LLMs. The argument, which the paper substantiates with a gradient analysis through the logits (Equation 9), runs as follows:

- A **positive advantage token** (bÂ_i,t > 0): the gradient increases the logit of the sampled token and decreases logits of all other tokens in the vocabulary. This is a concentrated update — one token goes up, the rest go down.
- A **negative advantage token** (bÂ_i,t ≤ 0): the gradient decreases the logit of the sampled token and increases logits of all other tokens. In a vocabulary of hundreds of thousands of tokens, this means the negative gradient diffuses to an enormous number of unsampled tokens — most of which are irrelevant — simultaneously raising their probabilities.

The paper's conclusion is crisp: "negative gradients diffuse to numerous irrelevant tokens — providing some regularization but also inducing instability, especially in off-policy scenarios" (Section 3). Despite this, both GRPO and GSPO clip positive and negative tokens identically. The paper's asymmetric temperature design (τ_neg > τ_pos, so negative-token gradients decay more rapidly as they go off-policy) directly addresses this gap.

**A missing conceptual bridge: no prior method is both sequence-coherent and token-adaptive.** The paper's taxonomy of existing methods is implicit but clear: GRPO is token-adaptive (it clips per-token) but not sequence-coherent (it has no mechanism to enforce consistency across tokens in a response). GSPO is sequence-coherent (the sequence ratio provides a holistic signal) but not token-adaptive (all tokens share the same fate under the sequence-level clip). SAPO is designed to be both: under typical conditions, the token-level soft gates average to a sequence-level gate (proved in Section 4.1), providing coherence; when conditions are violated by outlier tokens, SAPO retains its token-level adaptivity and suppresses only the outliers.

### How This Paper Positions Itself Relative to Existing Work

**Not a radically new paradigm, but a refined mechanism.** The paper does not propose to replace the group-based RL framework — it works within the same paradigm as GRPO and GSPO, using the same group-normalized advantages, the same importance ratio structure, and the same overall objective form (Equation 10). What changes is the **gating function** f_i,t(·). This is a deliberate, incremental improvement rather than a paradigm shift, and the paper is explicit about it: Section 4 frames all three methods as instances of a unified surrogate with algorithm-specific gating functions. SAPO's innovation is the particular choice of gating function — smooth, temperature-controlled, and asymmetric — and the demonstration that this choice matters enormously for training stability.

**Building on soft clipping from traditional RL.** The paper acknowledges that smooth gating functions have been explored in traditional RL (Chen et al., 2023, cited in Section 3). However, the paper argues that naïvely importing soft clipping into LLM training is insufficient — it must be combined with **(1)** a token-level design that naturally yields sequence-level coherence, and **(2)** asymmetric temperatures that account for the distinct stability profiles of positive and negative token updates. These two extensions are the paper's novel contributions beyond prior work on soft clipping.

**An empirical contribution as much as an algorithmic one.** The paper's primary mode of argument is not theoretical proof but empirical demonstration. The theoretical analysis in Section 4.1 (showing that SAPO reduces to a sequence-level soft gate under mild conditions) serves to build intuition and connect SAPO to GSPO, but the paper's real persuasive power comes from Figures 2–3 (validating the assumptions), Figure 4 (showing SAPO's stability advantage), and Figure 5 (demonstrating the necessity of asymmetric temperatures). The paper positions itself as providing a practical recipe — temperature values, architectural considerations, training setup — that practitioners can adopt directly.

**Targeting the MoE instability problem specifically.** While the method is general, the paper's language and experiments show a particular concern with MoE models. The abstract flags that variance is "exacerbated in Mixture-of-Experts models." The empirical validation of assumptions shows MoE models having wider dispersion (Figure 2 vs. Figure 3). The experiments use Qwen3-30B-A3B, an MoE architecture. The paper positions SAPO as especially valuable for the model architectures that are becoming standard in frontier LLM training, where existing methods are most brittle.

**A reliability argument, not just a performance argument.** The paper's headline result is not that SAPO achieves dramatically higher final accuracy than baselines — the improvement on AIME25 is from approximately 0.80 to 0.82 (Figure 4), a meaningful but incremental gain. Rather, the headline is that SAPO **sustains stable training** while baselines collapse. This is a reliability argument: SAPO makes RL training more predictable, less likely to fail catastrophically, and more efficient in its use of collected data (since near-on-policy tokens in otherwise-discarded sequences are preserved). In a research and engineering context where failed training runs are expensive and demoralizing, this reliability improvement is arguably more valuable than a few points of benchmark accuracy.

**Connecting to the broader self-improvement and reasoning literature.** The paper situates itself within the movement toward RL-based reasoning enhancement (Section 1 cites DeepSeek-R1, OpenAI's o-series, and Qwen3). By improving the stability of the RL optimizer, SAPO indirectly enables more ambitious reasoning training — longer training runs, more aggressive exploration, and more complex reward signals become feasible when the underlying optimizer is robust. The paper does not claim these downstream benefits explicitly, but the positioning is clear: SAPO is infrastructure for the next generation of reasoning-focused RL training.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

SAPO is a **drop-in replacement for the clipping mechanism** inside group-based policy gradient algorithms for LLM fine-tuning — it keeps the same group sampling, the same normalized advantages, and the same importance ratio structure as GRPO and GSPO, but substitutes their hard binary clipping with a smooth sigmoid-shaped gate that decays continuously as tokens drift off-policy. The problem SAPO solves is that **hard clipping forces a brittle tradeoff between stability and sample efficiency** (you either discard an entire sequence because of one bad token, or you accept noisy gradients from off-policy tokens), whereas SAPO's smooth gate provides a graduated response — near-on-policy tokens get near-full weight, moderately off-policy tokens get partial weight, and severely off-policy tokens are effectively suppressed — along with **asymmetric temperatures** that more aggressively dampen negative-advantage tokens because their gradients diffuse across the entire vocabulary and cause disproportionate instability.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The SAPO system operates within a standard group-based RL loop. The components and their information flow are:

1. **Base LLM (π_θ_old, the behavior policy):** generates G responses per query by sampling autoregressively. This is the model as it was at the start of the current training iteration — its parameters are frozen during rollout.
2. **Reward Function (R):** scores each complete response with a scalar reward (e.g., correctness on a math problem). These rewards are not learned; they come from task-specific evaluation.
3. **Advantage Normalizer:** computes the group-normalized advantage for each response: subtract the mean reward across the G responses and divide by their standard deviation. This produces Â_i values that are zero-mean and unit-variance within each group.
4. **Token-Level Importance Ratio Computer:** for every token position t in every response i, computes r_i,t(θ) = π_θ(y_i,t | q, y_i,<t) / π_θ_old(y_i,t | q, y_i,<t), the ratio between the current policy's probability and the behavior policy's probability for that token. This is the core signal of how "off-policy" each token has become.
5. **Soft Gate (the SAPO mechanism):** replaces hard clipping with a temperature-controlled sigmoid gate. For each token, it computes a weight w_i,t(θ) = sech²((τ_i / 2)(r_i,t(θ) − 1)), which peaks at 1 when r_i,t(θ) = 1 and decays smoothly as r_i,t(θ) deviates. The temperature τ_i takes value τ_pos if Â_i > 0 and τ_neg if Â_i ≤ 0, with τ_neg > τ_pos.
6. **Weighted Policy Gradient:** multiplies the token-level log-probability gradient by r_i,t(θ) · w_i,t(θ) · Â_i, sums over all tokens and all responses in the group, and averages across queries. This produces the parameter update.

Information flows sequentially: query → sampling G responses from π_θ_old → computing rewards → normalizing advantages → computing token-level importance ratios → applying the soft gate to produce weights → weighted gradient accumulation → parameter update → the updated model becomes the new π_θ_old for the next iteration.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This paper presents an **algorithmic refinement** within the group-based policy optimization framework for LLM RL fine-tuning. The central idea is that the gating function — which determines how much each token's gradient contributes to the parameter update based on its off-policy deviation — should be smooth, adaptive, and asymmetric, rather than a hard binary clip.

#### The Unified Surrogate Objective and Its Gradient

All three methods (GRPO, GSPO, SAPO) can be expressed as instances of a single surrogate objective, differing only in the choice of gating function `$f_{i,t}(\cdot)$`. The paper introduces this unified form early in Section 4 to make the algorithmic relationships transparent before diving into SAPO's specific design.

The unified surrogate is:

$$J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{q \sim \mathcal{D}, \; \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot|q)} \left[ \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^G \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} f_{i,t}\left(r_{i,t}(\theta)\right) \hat{A}_{i,t} \right]$$

where `$q$` is a query drawn from the query distribution `$\mathcal{D}$`, `$G$` is the number of responses sampled per query (the group size), `$y_i$` is the `$i$`-th sampled response with length `$|y_i|$` tokens, `$r_{i,t}(\theta) = \pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | q, y_{i,<t}) / \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t} | q, y_{i,<t})$` is the token-level importance ratio — the ratio of the probability the current policy assigns to token `$t$` of response `$i$` divided by the probability the behavior policy assigned to that same token — and `$\hat{A}_{i,t} = (R_i - \text{mean}(\{R_j\}_{j=1}^G)) / \text{std}(\{R_j\}_{j=1}^G)$` is the group-normalized advantage, which is the same scalar for every token within a given response.

**What it computes:** the expected value (over queries and sampled response groups) of the per-token gating-function value times the advantage, averaged over tokens within each response and over responses within each group. The expectation is taken with respect to the behavior policy's sampling distribution, but the policy ratio `$r_{i,t}(\theta)$` inside the gating function depends on the current policy `$\pi_\theta$`, making this a surrogate for the true RL objective (which would involve sampling from `$\pi_\theta$` directly). The gating function `$f_{i,t}$` acts as a regularizer: it limits how much influence a token has when the current and behavior policies diverge.

**Why this form:** this structure separates the problem into two parts: (1) a standardized advantage signal `$\hat{A}_{i,t}$` that encodes whether a token's response was better or worse than the group average, and (2) an algorithm-specific gating function that controls how aggressively to use that signal given the policy drift. By keeping `$\hat{A}_{i,t}$` fixed and varying only `$f_{i,t}$`, the paper can study the effect of different gating strategies while controlling for other factors.

The gradient of this unified surrogate with respect to the model parameters `$\theta$` is computed by differentiating through the importance ratio:

$$\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{q, \{y_i\}} \left[ \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^G \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} f'_{i,t}\left(r_{i,t}(\theta)\right) \; r_{i,t}(\theta) \; \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | q, y_{i,<t}) \; \hat{A}_i \right]$$

where `$f'_{i,t}(\cdot)$` is the derivative of the gating function with respect to its argument (the importance ratio), `$\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | q, y_{i,<t})$` is the token-level log-probability gradient (the direction in parameter space that increases the model's probability of outputting token `$y_{i,t}$`), and the expectation is over the same distribution as above.

**What it computes:** a weighted sum of per-token log-probability gradients. Each token's gradient vector is scaled by three factors: (1) the importance ratio `$r_{i,t}(\theta)$`, which corrects for the fact that the sample was drawn from the old policy rather than the current one — this is the standard importance-weighting term; (2) the gating derivative `$f'_{i,t}(r_{i,t}(\theta))$`, which is the algorithm-specific damping term that controls how much off-policy tokens contribute; and (3) the normalized advantage `$\hat{A}_i$`, which determines the direction and magnitude of the update — positive advantages encourage the model to produce tokens like these more often, negative advantages encourage the opposite.

**Why this form:** the product `$f'_{i,t}(r_{i,t}(\theta)) \cdot r_{i,t}(\theta)$` is the effective gradient weight. For the unclipped objective (where `$f_{i,t}(x) = x$`, giving `$f'_{i,t}(x) = 1$`), this reduces to `$r_{i,t}(\theta)$` — the standard importance weight. Different gating functions modify this product to achieve different regularization effects. The paper's analysis in Section 4 focuses on comparing the effective gradient weights produced by GRPO's hard clipping, GSPO's sequence-level hard clipping, and SAPO's soft gate.

---

#### SAPO's Gating Function and Objective

SAPO's core innovation is the specific choice of `$f_{i,t}$`. Rather than the piecewise constant functions used by GRPO and GSPO, SAPO uses a sigmoid-based smooth function with a temperature parameter that controls the rate of decay.

The SAPO surrogate objective is:

$$J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{q \sim \mathcal{D}, \; \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot|q)} \left[ \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^G \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} f_{i,t}\big(r_{i,t}(\theta)\big) \; \hat{A}_{i,t} \right]$$

where SAPO's gating function is:

$$f_{i,t}(x) = \sigma\big(\tau_{i,t} (x - 1)\big) \cdot \frac{4}{\tau_{i,t}}$$

and the temperature `$\tau_{i,t}$` is selected based on the sign of the advantage:

$$\tau_{i,t} = \begin{cases} \tau_{\text{pos}}, & \text{if } \hat{A}_{i,t} > 0 \\ \tau_{\text{neg}}, & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

with `$\sigma(z) = 1 / (1 + e^{-z})$` being the standard logistic sigmoid function, `$\tau_{\text{pos}}$` and `$\tau_{\text{neg}}$` being scalar temperature hyperparameters (set to `$\tau_{\text{pos}} = 1.0$` and `$\tau_{\text{neg}} = 1.05$` in the main experiments), and `$x = r_{i,t}(\theta)$` being the token importance ratio.

**What it computes:** for each token, the gating function takes the importance ratio `$r_{i,t}(\theta)$`, shifts it by 1 so that the on-policy point sits at zero, scales by the temperature `$\tau_{i,t}$`, passes through the sigmoid to produce a value between 0 and 1, and then multiplies by `$4/\tau_{i,t}$`. The result `$f_{i,t}(r_{i,t}(\theta))$` is a scalar that replaces the clipped ratio in GRPO's objective. The objective itself — the average of `$f_{i,t}$` times the advantage over all tokens and responses — is then maximized with respect to `$\theta$`.

**Why this form:** four design choices are embedded here:

1. **Shifting by 1:** the sigmoid is centered at `$r_{i,t}(\theta) = 1$`, which is the point where the current policy and behavior policy agree (perfectly on-policy). At this point, `$\sigma(0) = 0.5$`, and the factor `$4/\tau_{i,t}$` is chosen so that the derivative `$f'_{i,t}(1) = 1$`, meaning the on-policy gradient weight equals that of the unclipped objective. This preserves the correct on-policy behavior — SAPO does not distort gradients for tokens where no correction is needed.

2. **The factor `$4/\tau_{i,t}$`:** this is not arbitrary. Without it, the sigmoid would saturate at 1 for large `$r_{i,t}$`, producing a different asymptotic behavior. With it, the gating function's derivative (which produces the effective gradient weight) has a specific analytically convenient form, and the on-policy gradient condition `$f'_{i,t}(1) = 1$` is satisfied regardless of temperature. The factor `$4$` comes from the identity `$\sigma'(0) = \sigma(0)(1-\sigma(0)) = 0.25$`, so scaling by `$4/\tau_i$` and differentiating gives `$f'_{i,t}(1) = (4/\tau_i) \cdot \sigma'(0) \cdot \tau_i = 1$`.

3. **The sigmoid shape:** as `$r_{i,t}(\theta)$` grows, `$\sigma(\tau(r-1))$` approaches 1, so `$f_{i,t}$` approaches `$4/\tau$`. The sigmoid provides a smooth, monotonic transition between regimes — there is no discontinuity where a token's contribution suddenly drops to zero. This means that moderately off-policy tokens still contribute gradient signal, but at reduced magnitude, which improves sample efficiency by not discarding partially useful data.

4. **Temperature-dependent decay rate:** larger `$\tau$` means the sigmoid transitions more sharply (Figure 1, right panel). For `$\tau = 1.0$`, the gate is relatively broad; for `$\tau = 3.0$`, it decays much more quickly. This provides a continuous knob for controlling the width of the effective trust region — unlike `$\varepsilon$` in GRPO, which produces a binary inclusion/exclusion boundary, `$\tau$` produces a graduated response.

**The effective gradient weight.** Differentiating the gating function yields:

$$f'_{i,t}(r_{i,t}(\theta)) = 4 \; \sigma\big(\tau_i (r_{i,t}(\theta) - 1)\big) \; \big(1 - \sigma(\tau_i (r_{i,t}(\theta) - 1))\big)$$

Using the identity `$\sigma(z)(1 - \sigma(z)) = 1/4 \cdot \text{sech}^2(z/2)$`, this simplifies to:

$$f'_{i,t}(r_{i,t}(\theta)) = \text{sech}^2\!\left(\frac{\tau_i}{2} (r_{i,t}(\theta) - 1)\right)$$

The full gradient of the SAPO objective is therefore:

$$\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{q, \{y_i\}} \left[ \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^G \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} w_{i,t}(\theta) \; r_{i,t}(\theta) \; \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | q, y_{i,<t}) \; \hat{A}_i \right]$$

where the effective weight is:

$$w_{i,t}(\theta) = \text{sech}^2\!\left(\frac{\tau_i}{2} (r_{i,t}(\theta) - 1)\right)$$

and `$\text{sech}(z) = 2 / (e^z + e^{-z})$` is the hyperbolic secant function.

**What it computes:** the weight `$w_{i,t}(\theta)$` is what multiplies the importance-weighted log-probability gradient. The `$\text{sech}^2$` function has a bell shape: it peaks at `$1$` when `$r_{i,t}(\theta) = 1$`, decays to approximately `$0.5$` when `$|r_{i,t}(\theta) - 1| \approx 1.76 / \tau_i$`, and approaches zero rapidly for larger deviations. As `$\tau_i$` increases, the bell narrows, meaning more aggressive attenuation of off-policy tokens. Critically, the weight never reaches exactly zero for finite `$r_{i,t}$` — it decays asymptotically — so no token is completely excluded, but far-off-policy tokens contribute vanishingly small gradients.

**Why this form:** the `$\text{sech}^2$` function has several desirable properties that the binary indicator of GRPO lacks:

- **Continuity:** there is no cliff at a hard boundary. A token with `$r=1+\varepsilon+0.001$` and a token with `$r=1+\varepsilon-0.001$` get nearly identical weights, unlike in GRPO where one gets zero and the other gets full weight.
- **Peaked at 1:** the on-policy point receives maximum weight, so SAPO does not penalize the model for staying close to the behavior policy — it only attenuates when the policies diverge.
- **Smooth decay:** the decay rate is controlled by `$\tau$`, making the method tunable. The paper exploits this by using different `$\tau$` values for positive and negative tokens, which is the subject of the next section.
- **Analytic convenience:** the `$\text{sech}^2$` form makes the connection to GSPO's sequence-level analysis clean (Section 4.1), because it can be Taylor-expanded around the mean token log-ratio.

#### Asymmetric Temperature Design: Why τ_neg > τ_pos

The paper's most theoretically motivated design choice is the decision to use different temperatures for tokens with positive advantages versus negative advantages, specifically setting `$\tau_{\text{neg}} > \tau_{\text{pos}}$` so that negative-token gradients decay more rapidly as they go off-policy. The justification comes from analyzing how token-level gradients propagate through the logits of a large-vocabulary language model.

Consider a single token at position `$t$` in a response. The model computes a vector of logits `$\mathbf{z} = [z_1, z_2, \ldots, z_{|\mathcal{V}|}]$` over the vocabulary `$\mathcal{V}$`, where `$|\mathcal{V}|$` is typically hundreds of thousands. The probability of token `$v$` is given by the softmax: `$\pi_\theta(v | \text{context}) = \exp(z_v) / \sum_{v'} \exp(z_{v'})$`. The gradient of the token-level RL objective `$\log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | \text{context}) \cdot \hat{A}_{i,t}$` with respect to an arbitrary logit `$z_v$` is:

$$\frac{\partial \left[\log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | \text{context}) \hat{A}_{i,t}\right]}{\partial z_v} = \begin{cases} \left(1 - \pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | \text{context})\right) \cdot \hat{A}_{i,t}, & \text{if } v = y_{i,t} \text{ (the sampled token)} \\ -\pi_\theta(v | \text{context}) \cdot \hat{A}_{i,t}, & \text{otherwise (every unsampled token)} \end{cases}$$

where `$y_{i,t}$` is the token that was actually sampled in the response, `$\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | \text{context})$` is the probability the model assigned to that token, and `$\pi_\theta(v | \text{context})$` is the probability assigned to any other token `$v \neq y_{i,t}$`.

**What it computes:** for each logit `$z_v$` in the vocabulary, this equation gives the direction and magnitude by which the RL objective says to shift that logit. The gradient splits into two cases:

- **For the sampled token's logit** (`$v = y_{i,t}$`): the gradient is `$(1 - \pi_\theta(y_{i,t})) \cdot \hat{A}_{i,t}$`. If the advantage is positive, this pushes the sampled token's logit upward; if negative, downward.
- **For every other token's logit** (`$v \neq y_{i,t}$`): the gradient is `$-\pi_\theta(v) \cdot \hat{A}_{i,t}$`. If the advantage is positive, each unsampled logit is pushed downward proportional to its current probability; if negative, every unsampled logit is pushed upward proportional to its probability.

**Why this explains the asymmetry:** when the advantage is **positive**, the update is concentrated: one logit goes up by `$(1-\pi)\hat{A}$`, and the unsampled logits go down by tiny amounts proportional to their individual probabilities (most of which are near zero). The net effect is a focused, well-behaved update that increases the model's preference for the chosen token.

When the advantage is **negative**, the update is diffuse: the sampled token's logit goes down by `$(1-\pi)\hat{A}$`, but **every other logit in the vocabulary** goes up by `$\pi(v)\hat{A}$`. Even though `$\pi(v)$` is small for most tokens, there are `$|\mathcal{V}| - 1 \approx |\mathcal{V}|$` of them, so the total gradient mass distributed to unsampled tokens is `$\sum_{v \neq y} \pi(v)\hat{A} = (1 - \pi(y))\hat{A}$` — the same magnitude as the sampled token's gradient but spread across hundreds of thousands of logits. This means negative updates push up the probabilities of many inappropriate tokens simultaneously, increasing the risk of the model producing nonsensical outputs in the future.

The paper makes this argument explicit: "negative gradients diffuse to numerous irrelevant tokens — providing some regularization but also inducing instability, especially in off-policy scenarios." In off-policy settings, the importance ratio `$r_{i,t}(\theta)$` amplifies this diffusion, making the instability worse.

**How the asymmetric temperatures address this:** by setting `$\tau_{\text{neg}} > \tau_{\text{pos}}$`, SAPO makes the weight `$w_{i,t}(\theta) = \text{sech}^2((\tau_i/2)(r_{i,t}(\theta)-1))$` decay faster for negative-advantage tokens as they deviate from on-policy. Concretely, with `$\tau_{\text{neg}} = 1.05$` and `$\tau_{\text{pos}} = 1.0$`, a token with `$r = 1.5$` (moderately off-policy) receives weight approximately `$\text{sech}^2(1.05 \cdot 0.5 / 2) \approx \text{sech}^2(0.2625) \approx 0.93$` if positive, but `$\text{sech}^2(1.0 \cdot 0.5 / 2) \approx \text{sech}^2(0.25) \approx 0.94$` — wait, that would make the positive weight smaller. Let me recheck: `$\tau_{\text{neg}} = 1.05 > \tau_{\text{pos}} = 1.0$`. At `$r = 1.5$`, the deviation is `$0.5$`. For positive tokens: `$\text{sech}^2(1.0 \cdot 0.5 / 2) = \text{sech}^2(0.25) \approx 0.94$`. For negative tokens: `$\text{sech}^2(1.05 \cdot 0.5 / 2) = \text{sech}^2(0.2625) \approx 0.93$`. So negative tokens with `$r = 1.5$` receive about 0.93 weight vs. 0.94 for positive tokens — a small but systematic difference. More importantly, as `$r$` deviates further, the gap widens: at `$r = 2.0$`, positive weight is `$\text{sech}^2(0.5) \approx 0.79$`, negative weight is `$\text{sech}^2(0.525) \approx 0.76$`; at `$r = 3.0$`, positive is `$\text{sech}^2(1.0) \approx 0.42$`, negative is `$\text{sech}^2(1.05) \approx 0.39$`. The effect is that negative-advantage tokens that are significantly off-policy are more aggressively suppressed than similarly off-policy positive-advantage tokens, reflecting their greater potential to cause instability.

**Empirical validation of the asymmetry:** Figure 5 provides the critical evidence. Three configurations are compared: `$\tau_{\text{neg}} = 1.05 > \tau_{\text{pos}} = 1.0$` (the proposed asymmetric design), `$\tau_{\text{neg}} = \tau_{\text{pos}} = 1.0$` (symmetric), and `$\tau_{\text{neg}} = 0.95 < \tau_{\text{pos}} = 1.0$` (inverted asymmetry). The symmetric configuration shows early instability — the training reward and validation curves begin to oscillate and degrade. The inverted configuration `$(\tau_{\text{neg}} < \tau_{\text{pos}})$` is dramatically worse, collapsing almost immediately. Only the proposed asymmetric design `$(\tau_{\text{neg}} > \tau_{\text{pos}})$` maintains stable training throughout. This confirms that negative tokens are indeed the primary source of instability, and that more aggressive damping of their off-policy gradients is not just theoretically motivated but practically necessary.

#### Connection to GSPO: Sequence-Level Coherence from Token-Level Gates

A central claim of the paper is that SAPO provides both token-level adaptivity and sequence-level coherence — it can handle outlier tokens individually while still aligning with sequence-level rewards for well-behaved sequences. Section 4.1 provides the mathematical analysis that shows how SAPO naturally reduces to a sequence-level method under common conditions.

**The reduction starts with rewriting the effective gradient weight.** Under the small-step assumption that `$r_{i,t}(\theta) \approx 1$` (Assumption A1), the log-ratio approximation `$\log r_{i,t}(\theta) \approx r_{i,t}(\theta) - 1$` allows the sech² weight to be expressed in terms of the log-ratio:

$$w_{i,t}(\theta) = \text{sech}^2\!\left(\frac{\tau_i}{2} (r_{i,t}(\theta) - 1)\right) \approx \text{sech}^2\!\left(\frac{\tau_i}{2} \log r_{i,t}(\theta)\right) = g_{\tau_i}\!\left(z_{i,t}(\theta)\right)$$

where `$z_{i,t}(\theta) = \log r_{i,t}(\theta)$` is the token log-ratio, and `$g_\tau(z) = \text{sech}^2(\tau z / 2)$` is the gate function expressed in log-ratio space.

**The sequence-level connection uses a Taylor expansion.** The log of the sequence-level ratio (the geometric mean of token ratios) is the arithmetic mean of token log-ratios:

$$\log s_i(\theta) = \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log r_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} z_{i,t}(\theta) = \mu_i(\theta)$$

Expanding `$g_{\tau_i}(z_{i,t}(\theta))$` around `$\mu_i(\theta)$` using a second-order Taylor series:

$$g_{\tau_i}(z_{i,t}(\theta)) = g_{\tau_i}(\mu_i(\theta)) + g'_{\tau_i}(\mu_i(\theta))(z_{i,t}(\theta) - \mu_i(\theta)) + \frac{1}{2} g''_{\tau_i}(\xi_{i,t})(z_{i,t}(\theta) - \mu_i(\theta))^2$$

where `$\xi_{i,t}$` is some point between `$z_{i,t}(\theta)$` and `$\mu_i(\theta)$`.

Averaging this expansion over all tokens in sequence `$i$` causes the linear term to vanish because `$\sum_t (z_{i,t} - \mu_i) = 0$` by definition of the mean. This leaves:

$$\frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} g_{\tau_i}(z_{i,t}(\theta)) = g_{\tau_i}(\mu_i(\theta)) + \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} g''_{\tau_i}(\xi_{i,t})(z_{i,t}(\theta) - \mu_i(\theta))^2$$

The first term `$g_{\tau_i}(\mu_i(\theta)) = \text{sech}^2((\tau_i/2) \log s_i(\theta))$` is the sequence-level gate — it depends only on the sequence-level ratio `$s_i(\theta)$`. The second term is the error from the approximation, which is bounded by:

$$D_i(\theta) = \left| \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_t g_{\tau_i}(z_{i,t}) - g_{\tau_i}(\mu_i) \right| \leq \frac{1}{2} \sup_z |g''_{\tau_i}(z)| \cdot \text{Var}_i(\theta) = \frac{\tau_i^2}{4} \text{Var}_i(\theta)$$

where `$\text{Var}_i(\theta) = \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_t (z_{i,t}(\theta) - \mu_i(\theta))^2$` is the variance of token log-ratios within sequence `$i$`. The bound uses the fact that for `$g_\tau(z) = \text{sech}^2(\tau z / 2)$`, the maximum absolute second derivative is `$\sup_z |g''_\tau(z)| = \tau^2 / 2$`.

**What this means operationally:** if the within-sequence variance `$\text{Var}_i(\theta)$` is small — meaning all tokens in a sequence have roughly similar importance ratios, which is empirically common as shown in Figures 2–3 — then the average token-level gate equals the sequence-level gate `$g_{\tau_i}(\log s_i(\theta))$` to within a tight bound. Under this condition, the SAPO gradient reduces to:

$$\nabla_\theta J^{\text{SAPO}} \approx \mathbb{E}_{q, \{y_i\}} \left[ \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^G g_{\tau_i}\!\left(\log s_i(\theta)\right) \; \nabla_\theta \log s_i(\theta) \; \hat{A}_i \right]$$

where `$\nabla_\theta \log s_i(\theta) = \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_t \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | q, y_{i,<t})$` is the average per-token log-probability gradient for the sequence, and `$g_{\tau_i}(\log s_i(\theta))$` is the smooth sequence-level gate. This is structurally identical to what GSPO would produce if GSPO used a smooth sech² gate instead of hard clipping.

**Why this connection matters — the advantage over GSPO:** the reduction shows that when sequences are well-behaved (low `$\text{Var}_i$`), SAPO behaves like a sequence-level method with a continuous trust region, which is desirable because sequence-level methods align naturally with sequence-level rewards. However, when a sequence contains outlier tokens — tokens with ratios far from the mean — GSPO's hard sequence-level clip can push the entire sequence outside the clipping band, zeroing out gradients for all tokens including the well-behaved majority. SAPO, by contrast, defaults to its token-level gating: the outlier tokens get strongly downweighted by the sech² gate, but the near-on-policy tokens (which are close to `$\mu_i$` and at low variance positions) retain their informative gradients. The paper states this precisely: "when these conditions are violated due to heterogeneous or outlier tokens, SAPO selectively down-weights only the offending tokens while retaining informative gradients from near on-policy tokens within the same sequence."

This hybrid behavior — sequence-level when appropriate, token-level when necessary — is what the paper means by "sequence-coherent and token-adaptive." The empirical validation in Figures 2 and 3 confirms that the conditions for sequence-level behavior hold in the vast majority of training cases: `$r_{i,t}(\theta)$` is sharply concentrated around 1, and `$\text{Var}_i(\theta)$` typically stays below 0.02 for the MoE model and even lower for the dense model. The scatter plots in the right panels of Figures 2 and 3 show that `$D_i(\theta)$` (the actual approximation error) is consistently below the theoretical bound `$\tau_i^2 \text{Var}_i(\theta) / 4$`, confirming the Taylor analysis is practically tight.

#### Contrast with GRPO: Smooth Gates vs. Hard Clipping

SAPO's relationship to GRPO is structurally simpler than the GSPO connection: both operate at the token level, but they differ fundamentally in the shape of their gating function derivative `$f'_{i,t}$`.

GRPO's gating function (Equation 13) is piecewise linear with hard clipping:

$$f^{\text{GRPO}}_{i,t}(r_{i,t}(\theta); \hat{A}_i) = \begin{cases} \min(r_{i,t}(\theta), 1 + \varepsilon), & \hat{A}_i > 0 \\ \max(r_{i,t}(\theta), 1 - \varepsilon), & \hat{A}_i \leq 0 \end{cases}$$

Its derivative — the effective gradient weight — is a binary indicator:

$$f^{\text{GRPO} \prime}_{i,t}(r_{i,t}(\theta); \hat{A}_i) = \begin{cases} 1, & \hat{A}_i > 0 \text{ and } r_{i,t}(\theta) \leq 1 + \varepsilon \\ 0, & \hat{A}_i > 0 \text{ and } r_{i,t}(\theta) > 1 + \varepsilon \\ 1, & \hat{A}_i \leq 0 \text{ and } r_{i,t}(\theta) \geq 1 - \varepsilon \\ 0, & \hat{A}_i \leq 0 \text{ and } r_{i,t}(\theta) < 1 - \varepsilon \end{cases}$$

**What this means:** for tokens inside the clipping band `$[1 - \varepsilon, 1 + \varepsilon]$`, GRPO uses the full importance weight `$r_{i,t}(\theta)$` — these tokens contribute exactly as they would in the unclipped objective. For tokens outside the band, GRPO's gradient weight is zero — the token contributes nothing to the parameter update. There is no intermediate regime.

SAPO's effective gradient weight `$w_{i,t}(\theta) = \text{sech}^2((\tau_i/2)(r_{i,t}(\theta) - 1))$` replaces this binary indicator with a continuous bell-shaped function. Figure 1 (right panel) directly visualizes this contrast: the hard clip produces a rectangular "top-hat" weight function (purple line) that is 1 inside `$[1 - \varepsilon, 1 + \varepsilon]$` and 0 outside; the soft gate produces a smooth bell (green, yellow, blue lines) that peaks at 1 and decays continuously.

**Three practical advantages of the smooth gate over hard clipping:**

1. **Gradient continuity:** in GRPO, moving a token from `$r = 1 + \varepsilon$` to `$r = 1 + \varepsilon + 0.001$` changes its gradient contribution from full weight to zero — a discontinuity that introduces optimization noise, especially when parameters are near the boundary and small fluctuations cause tokens to flicker in and out of the included set. SAPO's smooth gate eliminates this discontinuity entirely: the weight changes continuously with `$r$`, so small fluctuations in the ratio produce small fluctuations in the weight.

2. **Information preservation:** tokens with `$r$` moderately outside GRPO's clipping band (say, `$r = 1.4$` with `$\varepsilon = 0.2$`) are completely discarded by GRPO, even though they still carry a valid (if somewhat noisier) learning signal. SAPO assigns these tokens a reduced but non-zero weight — approximately `$\text{sech}^2(0.2) \approx 0.96$` for `$\tau = 1.0$` — preserving useful gradient information while still downweighting it appropriately. The paper argues this improves "sample efficiency" because fewer tokens are wasted.

3. **Tunable width:** GRPO's strictness is controlled by `$\varepsilon$`, which creates a hard boundary. SAPO's strictness is controlled by `$\tau$`, which creates a graduated response. Increasing `$\tau$` makes the bell narrower (more aggressive attenuation), while decreasing `$\tau$` makes it broader (more permissive). This provides a more nuanced control surface: rather than choosing between "include" and "exclude," the practitioner controls how quickly influence decays with off-policy deviation.

**The on-policy calibration is preserved.** A critical detail: at `$r_{i,t}(\theta) = 1$`, both GRPO and SAPO produce a gradient weight of 1, and the unclipped objective also produces weight 1. This means that when the current policy exactly matches the behavior policy, all three methods produce identical gradients — there is no distortion at the on-policy point. SAPO's regularization only activates when policies actually diverge.

#### Empirical Validation of the Small-Step and Low-Dispersion Assumptions

The theoretical reduction of SAPO to a sequence-level method (Section 4.1) depends on two assumptions: (A1) `$r_{i,t}(\theta) \approx 1$` (small policy steps), and (A2) low within-sequence variance `$\text{Var}_i(\theta)$` of token log-ratios. The paper validates both empirically across more than `$10^5$` sequences and `$10^9$` tokens drawn from off-policy mini-batches during actual training.

**Figure 2 (MoE model, Qwen3-30B-A3B):** The left panel shows the histogram of `$r_{i,t}(\theta)$` values. The distribution is sharply peaked around 1, with values concentrated in approximately `$[0.6, 1.4]$`. The vast majority of tokens have ratios within `$\pm 0.2$` of the on-policy point. The middle panel shows the histogram of per-sequence log-ratio variance `$\text{Var}_i(\theta)$`. The distribution peaks near zero and extends to about 0.020, indicating that most sequences have tightly clustered token ratios, though there is a tail extending to higher variances (likely reflecting the heterogeneity induced by MoE expert routing). The right panel shows a scatter plot of `$\text{Var}_i(\theta)$` versus the actual approximation error `$D_i(\theta)$`, overlaid with the theoretical bound `$\tau_i^2 \text{Var}_i(\theta) / 4$`. The points cluster well below the bound, confirming that the Taylor error is small in practice.

**Figure 3 (dense model, Qwen3-4B):** The same three panels, with key differences. The `$r_{i,t}$` histogram (left) is even more tightly concentrated around 1 — approximately `$[0.8, 1.2]$` — reflecting the greater stability of dense architectures. The variance histogram (middle) extends only to about 0.006, an order of magnitude narrower than the MoE model. The scatter plot (right) shows even smaller approximation errors `$D_i(\theta)$`, clustering below 0.0014. This confirms that dense models satisfy the reduction conditions more strongly than MoE models, which is expected because MoE routing heterogeneity introduces additional sources of token-level variance.

**What these figures establish:** the assumptions (A1) and (A2) are not just theoretically convenient — they are empirically typical during RL training of both MoE and dense LLMs. This means the SAPO→GSPO reduction is practically relevant for the majority of training examples. The wider variance in MoE models (Figure 2 vs. Figure 3) also validates the paper's motivation: MoE architectures are more susceptible to the outlier-token problem that SAPO's token-level adaptivity addresses, making SAPO particularly valuable for these models.

#### Summary of Design Choices and Their Justifications

- **Sigmoid gate centered at `$r=1$` with `$4/\tau$` scaling:** preserves on-policy gradient equality (`$f'(1) = 1$` regardless of temperature) while providing smooth attenuation for off-policy tokens. This is preferable to hard clipping because it eliminates the discontinuity at the clip boundary and preserves partial gradient signal from moderately off-policy tokens.
- **`$\text{sech}^2$` effective weight:** emerges analytically from differentiating the sigmoid gate and provides a bell-shaped weight function that is peaked at on-policy, decays smoothly, and never reaches exactly zero — avoiding total exclusion of any token while effectively suppressing far-off-policy ones.
- **Asymmetric temperatures (`$\tau_{\text{neg}} > \tau_{\text{pos}}$`):** justified by the gradient analysis through logits (Equation 9), which shows that negative-advantage gradients diffuse across the entire vocabulary, making them inherently more destabilizing than positive-advantage gradients (which are concentrated). Empirically validated by Figure 5, showing that symmetric or inverted temperatures cause training instability.
- **Default temperature values (`$\tau_{\text{pos}} = 1.0$`, `$\tau_{\text{neg}} = 1.05$`):** chosen empirically for the Qwen3-30B-A3B experiments. The small gap (0.05) between the two temperatures indicates that only a modest asymmetry is needed — the instability from negative tokens is real but does not require dramatically different treatment.
- **Token-level operation with sequence-level reduction:** rather than committing to either token-level or sequence-level operation, SAPO's design naturally interpolates between the two regimes based on within-sequence variance. This is preferable to GSPO (which is always sequence-level, losing token information for heterogeneous sequences) and GRPO (which is always token-level, lacking the coherence that sequence-level methods provide for homogeneous sequences).
- **No additional hyperparameters beyond those already present:** SAPO introduces `$\tau_{\text{pos}}$` and `$\tau_{\text{neg}}$` but eliminates `$\varepsilon$` (the clipping range). The paper notes that SAPO does not require the routing replay stabilization mechanism used by GRPO-R2 in Zheng et al. (2025), which "improves exploration and reduces engineering overhead for RL systems" (Section 5.1).

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Recasting RL Stability as a Gating-Function Design Problem, Not a Clipping-Parameter Tuning Problem

The paper's most fundamental conceptual move is reframing the instability problem in LLM policy optimization from "find the right clipping threshold" to "design the right gating function." This is subtle but important: it shifts the design space from a single scalar parameter (ε) that produces a binary decision boundary to a continuous function that can express a graduated response to off-policy deviation.

**What the field assumed before this paper.** The dominant paradigm — inherited from PPO and carried into GRPO and GSPO — treats the clipping mechanism as an engineering necessity: a hard band that prevents importance ratios from growing too large. The tuning question is "what value of ε works best?" and the answer is always a compromise between stability (small ε, discarding more data) and sample efficiency (large ε, using more data but with noisier gradients). No prior group-based method questioned whether the binary nature of the clip itself was the problem — they all accepted that tokens are either in or out, and the only knob is where to draw the line.

**What SAPO does differently.** By reconceptualizing the gating function `f_{i,t}(·)` as the object of design rather than a fixed clip operation, the paper opens up a richer space of possibilities. The gating function can be smooth (continuous decay rather than sudden cutoff), temperature-controlled (different decay rates for different contexts), and asymmetric (different behaviors for positive and negative advantage tokens). This is not just replacing ε with a new parameter τ — it is replacing a binary indicator function with a continuous function class. The paper demonstrates this concretely in Figure 1 and Section 4, where GRPO, GSPO, and SAPO are all expressed as instances of the same unified surrogate (Equation 10) differing only in `f_{i,t}`. This framing makes the algorithmic relationship transparent: the methods are points in a design space, and SAPO occupies a region that prior work had left unexplored — the region of smooth, token-adaptive, temperature-controlled gates.

**Why this is a fundamental shift, not an incremental tweak.** A hard clip with parameter ε has exactly one degree of freedom: the boundary location. SAPO's soft gate with asymmetric temperatures has at least two degrees of freedom (τ_pos controls the width of the trust region; τ_neg independently controls how aggressively negative tokens are damped), and the smooth functional form provides an additional continuous dimension (the shape of the decay curve, which is sech² rather than a step function). More importantly, the conceptual shift matters: if you think the problem is "find the right ε," you'll never discover that asymmetric damping is necessary, because ε is shared across all tokens. Only by reframing the problem as gating-function design does the possibility of per-sign temperature control become natural. The paper's ablation (Figure 5) validates that this reframing is not merely aesthetic — the asymmetric design is required for stability, and a symmetric soft gate (which would be the natural extension if one only thought about smoothness) still collapses.

**Evidence anchor.** The unified surrogate derivation in Section 4 (Equations 10–14) and the effective gradient weight visualization in Figure 1 together establish that SAPO is distinguished by its choice of `f_{i,t}` relative to the same objective form used by GRPO and GSPO. The instability of the symmetric soft gate in Figure 5 proves that the design space beyond ε is nontrivial and that SAPO's specific point in that space is not obvious a priori.

---

### Innovation 2: Identifying and Exploiting the Fundamental Asymmetry Between Positive and Negative Token Updates in Large-Vocabulary RL

Prior to this work, the field treated positive and negative advantage tokens symmetrically in the clipping mechanism — a token with Â = +2 received the same clipping treatment as a token with Â = −2. SAPO identifies a structural reason why this is incorrect for large-vocabulary language models and provides both analytical justification (Equation 9) and empirical validation (Figure 5) that negative-token gradients are intrinsically more destabilizing and require more aggressive regularization.

**What the field assumed before.** GRPO applies the same ε-band to all tokens regardless of advantage sign (Equation 1). GSPO applies the same sequence-level clip regardless of advantage sign (Equation 3). The PPO lineage, from which these methods descend, was developed in continuous control and game-playing domains with small action spaces, where the diffusion-of-gradient argument does not apply — there is no vocabulary of hundreds of thousands of actions across which a negative update must be distributed. The default assumption was that symmetry is natural: if a 2× policy ratio is dangerous, it is equally dangerous whether the advantage is positive or negative, because what matters is the magnitude of the off-policy correction.

**What SAPO reveals.** The gradient decomposition in Equation 9 exposes a structural asymmetry that is specific to large discrete action spaces trained with softmax policies. A positive-advantage gradient concentrates on a single logit (the sampled token's), while a negative-advantage gradient distributes across *all other logits in the vocabulary*. For a vocabulary of 150,000 tokens, this means the negative update simultaneously nudges up 149,999 token probabilities — most of them irrelevant. The paper's phrase "diffuse to numerous irrelevant tokens — providing some regularization but also inducing instability, especially in off-policy scenarios" captures the tradeoff: negative updates are both necessary (they teach the model what not to say) and dangerous (they indiscriminately boost alternatives). The clipping mechanism should therefore be *asymmetric*: more conservative for negative tokens than positive ones.

**Why this is a genuine insight rather than an obvious observation.** The gradient derivation (Equation 9) is elementary calculus — any practitioner could write it down. What makes it an insight is connecting this gradient structure to the *off-policy problem*. The instability arises at the intersection of two phenomena: (1) negative gradients diffuse across the vocabulary (the gradient structure), and (2) importance ratios amplify these gradients when the policy has drifted from the behavior policy (the off-policy problem). Neither alone is sufficient to motivate asymmetric temperatures — positive gradients are also amplified by large importance ratios — but the combination means that off-policy negative tokens are doubly dangerous: they get amplified by the ratio *and* their gradient energy is diffused. The paper's contribution is seeing this connection and acting on it through the temperature design.

**Evidence anchor.** The temperature ablation in Figure 5 is the key demonstration. The symmetric configuration (τ_neg = τ_pos = 1.0) destabilizes; the inverted configuration (τ_neg < τ_pos, meaning negative tokens are *less* damped) collapses almost immediately; only τ_neg > τ_pos maintains stable training. This ordering — inverted worse than symmetric worse than asymmetric — is exactly what the gradient-diffusion argument predicts, providing strong causal evidence for the mechanism.

**Significance beyond this paper.** This insight has implications for any RL method operating on large-vocabulary language models, not just SAPO. It suggests that future policy-gradient methods should incorporate advantage-sign-dependent regularization by default — an adjustment that could be retrofitted into existing methods (e.g., using different ε values for positive and negative tokens in GRPO). The paper does not explore this retrofitting, but the insight itself is transferable.

---

### Innovation 3: Achieving Sequence-Level Coherence Without Sacrificing Token-Level Adaptivity — A Hybrid Regime That Prior Methods Could Not Express

The paper identifies a structural limitation in the existing taxonomy of group-based methods: GRPO is token-adaptive but sequence-incoherent (each token is clipped independently, with no mechanism to ensure within-sequence consistency); GSPO is sequence-coherent but token-inadaptive (a single off-policy token can suppress the entire sequence's gradient). SAPO is the first method in this family to achieve both properties simultaneously — and it does so not by averaging or ensembling the two approaches, but by having the token-level gate naturally concentrate to a sequence-level gate under typical conditions while retaining its token-level character when conditions are violated.

**What the field assumed before.** The implicit assumption in prior work was that you must choose: either operate at the token level (and accept that token-level clipping may produce inconsistent updates within a response) or operate at the sequence level (and accept that outlier tokens may cause entire sequences to be wasted). GSPO made the choice for sequence-level on the grounds that length normalization reduces variance and that sequence-level rewards are naturally aligned with sequence-level updates. GRPO made the choice for token-level on the grounds that per-token clipping provides finer control. No prior method attempted to have both, likely because it was not obvious that a method *could* have both without an explicit mixture or switching mechanism.

**What SAPO achieves.** The analysis in Section 4.1 shows that under empirically common conditions (A1: small policy steps, and A2: low within-sequence variance of token log-ratios), the average of SAPO's token-level soft gates approximates a sequence-level soft gate `g(log s_i(θ)) = \text{sech}^2((τ_i/2) \log s_i(θ))` to within a tight bound (Equation 21). This means that for *well-behaved* sequences — which constitute the majority of training examples as shown in Figures 2–3 — SAPO effectively operates as a sequence-level method, providing the coherence that GSPO offers. But when a sequence contains *outlier tokens* — a few positions where the importance ratio deviates substantially from the sequence mean — SAPO does not average their weight into the sequence gate and then apply a hard clip; instead, it applies its token-level gate to each token individually, strongly downweighting only the outliers while preserving gradients from the near-on-policy majority. This is the "selective attenuation" that the paper claims improves sample efficiency.

**Why this is a conceptual advance rather than just a nice property.** The innovation is not that the approximation holds — Taylor expansions are standard tools — but that the *same mechanism* naturally produces both behaviors without any explicit switching logic, difficulty estimation, or hyperparameter that controls the sequence-vs-token tradeoff. The sech² gate is a single function applied per-token; its average *becomes* a sequence-level gate under low dispersion, and its per-token application *becomes* selective attenuation under high dispersion. This emergent hybrid behavior is a consequence of the gate's mathematical form, not an engineered feature. It means the practitioner does not need to decide whether a sequence is "coherent enough" for sequence-level treatment — the gate handles the interpolation automatically.

**Evidence anchor.** Figures 2 and 3 provide the empirical foundation: they show that r_{i,t}(θ) concentrates around 1 and Var_i(θ) is small for the vast majority of training examples in both MoE and dense models, but that there is a tail of higher-variance sequences (especially in the MoE case) where token-level adaptivity matters. The scatter plots (right panels) confirm that the approximation error D_i(θ) is consistently below the theoretical bound, meaning the reduction is quantitatively reliable, not just a qualitative observation.

**Significance beyond this paper.** This emergent hybrid property — where a token-level operator concentrates to a sequence-level operator under low dispersion — is a design pattern that could influence future policy optimization methods. It suggests that the "right" level of operation (token vs. sequence) is not a binary choice but a spectrum, and that smooth gating functions provide a natural way to interpolate along that spectrum based on data characteristics. Methods that commit to one level or the other will inevitably be suboptimal for some fraction of training examples — SAPO shows that this fraction can be reduced without introducing additional complexity.

### Innovation 4: Demonstrating That Verifier Over-Optimization in RL for Reasoning Has a Parallel in Policy Gradient Instability — Both Stem from Unchecked Deviation from a Reference Distribution

This is a cross-cutting conceptual insight that the paper does not belabor but that emerges from the unified gating-function perspective in Section 4. The hard clipping in GRPO and GSPO is functionally analogous to the hard verification thresholds in test-time compute methods: both create a binary boundary beyond which information is discarded, and both are vulnerable to the problem that the boundary is simultaneously too strict (discarding useful signal) and too permissive (allowing unstable or over-optimized outputs through).

**The connection.** In test-time compute scaling, a process reward model (PRM) scores candidate solutions, and a search algorithm selects the best one. When the search budget is large, the optimizer can find solutions that score highly under the PRM but are actually incorrect — a phenomenon called verifier over-optimization. The standard mitigation is to limit the search budget or use weaker optimization methods, which is analogous to tightening the clipping band in PPO. But this creates the same tradeoff: too tight a budget/band discards genuinely good solutions/tokens, while too loose allows the optimizer to exploit the verifier's blind spots.

SAPO's soft gate can be understood as applying the same conceptual solution to policy gradients that a continuous trust region would apply to test-time search: rather than a binary accept/reject decision for off-policy tokens (analogous to keeping or discarding a candidate solution), it provides a graduated response that is most permissive near the reference point and increasingly conservative further away. The asymmetric temperature design adds another dimension: it recognizes that some deviations are more dangerous than others (negative-advantage tokens are like search paths that the verifier is especially bad at evaluating), and tailors the attenuation accordingly.

**Why this framing matters.** It unifies two seemingly disparate problems — RL training instability and test-time search over-optimization — under a common conceptual framework: both arise when an optimization process (parameter updates via policy gradients; candidate selection via search) is guided by an imperfect signal (the advantage estimator; the process reward model) and is allowed to deviate too far from a reference distribution (the behavior policy; the base model's sampling distribution) without adequate regularization. The solution in both cases is a continuous trust region that penalizes deviation smoothly rather than with a hard cutoff.

**Evidence anchor.** This insight is supported by the paper's entire empirical arc — the collapse of GRPO and GSPO in Figure 4 (analogous to verifier over-optimization degrading search results), the instability of symmetric and inverted temperatures in Figure 5 (analogous to overly aggressive search exploiting verifier weaknesses), and the sustained stability of SAPO (analogous to compute-optimal strategies that avoid the over-optimization regime). The paper does not make the PRM-search connection explicitly, but it is a natural extension of the gating-function framework that the paper establishes.

**Significance.** This framing suggests that advances in robust reward modeling and verifier design — active research areas in the RLHF and reasoning communities — may translate directly to improved policy optimization stability if their insights are encoded in the gating function. Conversely, the asymmetric temperature insight from SAPO (different attenuation for different "directions" of deviation) might inform verifier design, where certain types of solution paths may warrant different levels of trust.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The controlled experiments (Section 5.1) use mathematical reasoning queries for training and evaluate on three benchmarks: AIME25 (AIME, 2025), HMMT25 (HMMT, 2025), and BeyondAIME (Seed et al., 2025). The Qwen3-VL experiments (Section 5.2) train on a broad collection of text and multimodal tasks including mathematics, coding, and logical reasoning, with evaluation on AIME25, LiveCodeBench v6 (Jain et al., 2024), ZebraLogic (Lin et al., 2025), and MathVision (Wang et al., 2024). No explicit training set sizes are reported; the focus is on training dynamics and validation performance, not dataset scale.

- **Base model(s).** The controlled experiments use a cold-start checkpoint fine-tuned from Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base, a Mixture-of-Experts model with 30B total parameters and 3B activated parameters. The Qwen3-VL experiments use a preliminary cold-start checkpoint of Qwen3-VL-30B-A3B (also MoE). The MoE architecture is deliberately chosen because the paper's theoretical motivation emphasizes that routing heterogeneity amplifies token-ratio variance, making these models a stronger test of SAPO's claimed advantages. The dense-model analysis in Figures 2–3 uses Qwen3-4B to validate that assumptions hold across architectures, though no dense-model training runs are reported.

- **Metrics.** All controlled experiments report training reward (the scalar reward signal seen during RL training) and Pass@1 validation accuracy. Pass@1 is computed as the fraction of problems for which at least one correct answer appears in K sampled responses, where K = 16 for AIME25/HMMT25/BeyondAIME in Section 5.1, K = 32 for AIME25 and K = 8 for LiveCodeBench in Section 5.2. The Qwen3-VL experiments additionally report an aggregated validation score (Figure 6, right panel), though the aggregation method across the four benchmarks is not specified.

- **Baselines.** Two baselines are used throughout: **GSPO** (Zheng et al., 2025), which applies hard sequence-level clipping with length-normalized importance ratios (Equation 3–4), and **GRPO-R2**, which is GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) equipped with routing replay, a stabilization mechanism for MoE models described in Zheng et al. (2025) that replays expert routing decisions from the behavior policy during gradient computation to reduce variance. The controlled experiments use the same hyperparameter configurations for GSPO and GRPO-R2 as reported in Zheng et al. (2025), ensuring a fair comparison. No ablation comparing SAPO to standard GRPO (without routing replay) is reported, which is a notable omission since SAPO is positioned partly as a replacement for GRPO's hard token-level clipping.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** For the controlled experiments, each batch of rollout data is divided into four mini-batches for gradient updates (Section 5.1), and training runs to 1750 gradient steps (Figure 4). For Qwen3-VL training, each batch is split into two mini-batches, and training runs to approximately 500 gradient steps (Figure 6). The paper reports results under "comparable training budgets" and "equal compute budgets" but does not provide wall-clock time, FLOP counts, or GPU-hours — comparison is in terms of gradient steps, which is fair if per-step computation is equivalent across methods (which it should be, since SAPO only changes the objective computation, not the forward/backward pass cost). The paper does not discuss whether SAPO's sigmoid gate computation introduces any measurable overhead relative to GRPO's min/max clip or GSPO's sequence-ratio computation.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** No cross-validation, error bars, or confidence intervals are reported. Results are presented as single training curves over gradient steps (Figures 4–6). The lack of repeated runs means that differences between methods — e.g., the ~0.02 gap in AIME25 Pass@1 at step 1750 in Figure 4 — cannot be assessed for statistical significance. The batch-level reward curves show inherent noise, and without replicates it is unclear whether the observed stability advantages are systematic or partially attributable to random seed effects. The paper does not specify whether the reported curves represent a single training run or an average over multiple seeds.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Controlled Experiments: SAPO vs. GSPO and GRPO-R2 on Mathematical Reasoning

Figure 4 presents the central comparison: training reward and validation Pass@1 for SAPO, GSPO, and GRPO-R2 on a cold-start model from Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base across 1750 gradient steps on three held-out mathematical reasoning benchmarks. The headline results are:

**Training stability.** The training reward curve (Figure 4, top-left panel) shows SAPO maintaining a consistent upward trajectory throughout training, reaching approximately 0.84 at step 1750. In contrast, GSPO's training reward rises initially but begins to oscillate and decline after roughly step 500–750, ultimately falling to approximately 0.62–0.65 by step 1750. GRPO-R2's reward also peaks early and then degrades, ending at approximately 0.66. This is the paper's primary evidence for SAPO's stability advantage: while both baselines exhibit what the paper calls "early training collapse," SAPO continues to improve. The paper describes this as SAPO "sustaining coherent learning for a longer duration" (Section 1), though the quantitative duration is roughly 2–3× longer stable training (SAPO shows no visible degradation through 1750 steps, while baselines begin deteriorating by step 500–750).

**Validation performance.** On AIME25 (Figure 4, bottom-right panel), SAPO reaches approximately 0.82 Pass@1 at step 1750, compared to roughly 0.80 for GSPO and approximately 0.79 for GRPO-R2. The gap is modest — roughly 0.02–0.03 in absolute Pass@1 — but the trajectories tell a more important story: SAPO's curve rises monotonically through the end of training, while GSPO's and GRPO-R2's peak around step 500–750 and then decline. On HMMT25 (top-right panel), SAPO peaks at approximately 0.645–0.650 around step 1500, while GSPO collapses to roughly 0.50 by step 1750 and GRPO-R2 falls to roughly 0.525. On BeyondAIME (bottom-left panel), SAPO reaches approximately 0.58 at its peak versus roughly 0.49 for GSPO and 0.50 for GRPO-R2 at their respective peaks. Notably, SAPO's BeyondAIME curve also shows a slight decline after step 1500 (from ~0.58 to ~0.56), suggesting SAPO is not immune to eventual degradation but defers it significantly relative to the baselines.

An important detail: all three methods start from the same cold-start checkpoint, and their initial Pass@1 values are identical (e.g., ~0.71 on AIME25 at step 0). The divergence emerges entirely during RL training, confirming that the differences are due to the optimization dynamics, not initialization.

**No routing replay required.** The paper emphasizes that SAPO does not rely on routing replay — the MoE stabilization technique used in GRPO-R2 — to achieve its stability. This is meaningful because routing replay incurs an engineering cost (maintaining additional state for expert routing decisions) and may limit exploration by constraining the model to behavior-policy routing patterns. SAPO's stability comes purely from the objective function design, which the paper frames as a practical advantage for RL system implementation (Section 5.1: "SAPO does not rely on routing replay for stabilization or strong performance, which improves exploration and reduces engineering overhead for RL systems"). However, the paper does not report an ablation comparing SAPO against plain GRPO (without routing replay), which would directly test this claim by showing whether SAPO can stabilize training where vanilla GRPO cannot. The comparison is only against GRPO-R2, which is already the stabilized variant.

#### Temperature Asymmetry Ablation: τ_neg > τ_pos is Critical

Figure 5 presents a three-way comparison within SAPO: the proposed asymmetric design (τ_neg = 1.05 > τ_pos = 1.0), a symmetric design (τ_neg = τ_pos = 1.0), and an inverted design (τ_neg = 0.95 < τ_pos = 1.0). This is the paper's direct test of whether the asymmetric temperature design is necessary for stability.

**Training reward:** The asymmetric configuration maintains a smooth, rising reward curve reaching approximately 0.80 by step 1750. The symmetric configuration shows initial improvement similar to the asymmetric one but begins to oscillate noticeably after step 1000–1250, with larger-amplitude fluctuations in the reward signal — though it does not fully collapse to the degree that GSPO and GRPO-R2 do in Figure 4. The inverted configuration degrades dramatically: the reward curve starts to decline sharply after roughly step 500 and collapses to approximately 0.58 by step 1750, well below its peak.

**Validation Pass@1:** On AIME25, the asymmetric configuration reaches approximately 0.82; symmetric reaches roughly 0.81 (a small difference, but with increasing fluctuation); inverted falls to approximately 0.74–0.75. On HMMT25, the gap is more pronounced: asymmetric peaks around 0.65; symmetric peaks around 0.60–0.62 but with substantial oscillation (the curve shows a dip to ~0.56 around step 1250 then a partial recovery); inverted collapses to roughly 0.52. On BeyondAIME, asymmetric reaches ~0.58; symmetric peaks at ~0.55–0.56 but begins declining after step 1250; inverted falls to ~0.48.

**Interpretation:** The ordering — asymmetric > symmetric > inverted — is consistent across all three benchmarks and both training reward and validation curves. This is strong evidence that the asymmetric design is not merely a minor improvement but a necessary condition for SAPO's stability. The inverted case (where negative tokens are damped *less* than positive tokens) is catastrophic, confirming that negative-token gradients are indeed the primary source of instability, and that damping them more aggressively is the correct direction. The symmetric case occupies an intermediate position: it does not fully collapse like the baselines in Figure 4 but shows clear signs of instability — oscillations, partial degradation — that the asymmetric design avoids. This suggests that smooth gating *alone* is insufficient; the asymmetry is an essential component.

A nuance: the symmetric configuration's Pass@1 on AIME25 (roughly 0.81) is quite close to the asymmetric configuration's 0.82, and substantially above the GRPO-R2/GSPO baselines (~0.79–0.80). This implies that the smooth gate itself provides a meaningful stability improvement over hard clipping, with the asymmetry providing an additional but smaller marginal gain on this benchmark. On HMMT25, however, the asymmetric-symmetric gap is much larger (roughly 0.65 vs. 0.60–0.62), indicating that the benefit of asymmetry is task-dependent.

**What the paper does not show:** the temperature values (τ_pos = 1.0, τ_neg = 1.05) are a single operating point. No sweep over τ_pos or τ_neg values is reported. It is unclear whether 1.05 is near-optimal for τ_neg or whether larger values (e.g., 1.1, 1.2) would provide even better stability at the cost of slower learning. Similarly, no sweep over τ_pos is shown — the paper's theoretical analysis suggests that larger τ_pos would narrow the trust region for positive tokens, potentially trading off exploration for stability. This is a significant limitation for practitioners wanting to tune SAPO for their own models and tasks.

#### Large-Scale Qwen3-VL Training

Figure 6 shows results from training Qwen3-VL-30B-A3B (a multimodal MoE model) with SAPO, GSPO, and GRPO-R2 from a preliminary cold-start checkpoint across approximately 500 gradient steps. This experiment tests whether SAPO's advantages generalize to multimodal settings and to a different (though architecturally similar) base model.

**Training reward (Figure 6, left panel):** SAPO's reward curve rises consistently from approximately 0.52 at step 0 to roughly 0.66–0.67 at step 500. GSPO and GRPO-R2 also improve, but their trajectories are more volatile and terminate at lower values — approximately 0.63–0.64 for GSPO and roughly 0.62 for GRPO-R2. The reward curves show clear separation after step 200, with SAPO pulling ahead and maintaining a consistent lead.

**Aggregated validation score (Figure 6, right panel):** SAPO reaches approximately 0.755–0.76 at step 500, compared to roughly 0.745 for GSPO and approximately 0.74 for GRPO-R2. The gap is modest (~0.01–0.02) but consistent across the latter half of training. SAPO's curve shows steady improvement with no signs of plateauing or decline through step 500. GSPO's curve rises to approximately 0.745 by step 300 and then flattens; GRPO-R2's curve rises more slowly and reaches roughly 0.74 by step 500 with some oscillation.

**Key takeaway:** The advantages observed in the controlled mathematical reasoning setting (Figure 4) transfer to multimodal multi-task training — SAPO again provides more stable optimization and higher final performance. The stability advantage is less dramatic here because the baselines do not exhibit the catastrophic collapse seen in Figure 4 — training only runs to 500 steps, and it is possible that the collapse would emerge later. The paper does not report whether training beyond 500 steps would show similar degradation patterns. The per-benchmark breakdown of the aggregated validation score is not provided, so it is impossible to assess whether SAPO's advantage is uniform across tasks or concentrated in specific ones (e.g., math vs. coding vs. logical reasoning).

#### Empirical Validation of Assumptions (Figures 2–3)

While these figures are primarily empirical support for the theoretical analysis in Section 4.1, they also constitute experimental results in their own right. As described in prior sections, they confirm that r_i,t(θ) concentrates around 1 and that intra-sequence variance is small for both MoE (Figure 2) and dense (Figure 3) models during off-policy training. The key experimental finding is the architectural difference: the MoE model (Qwen3-30B-A3B) shows a wider r_i,t(θ) distribution (spanning roughly [0.6, 1.4] vs. [0.8, 1.2] for dense) and larger Var_i(θ) values (extending to ~0.020 vs. ~0.006 for dense). This provides empirical justification for the paper's focus on MoE models as the more challenging testbed, and indirectly supports the claim that SAPO's token-level adaptivity is especially valuable for MoE architectures where outlier tokens are more frequent.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Temperature asymmetry (Figure 5):** As described above, SAPO with τ_neg = τ_pos = 1.0 (symmetric soft gate) shows signs of instability relative to the proposed τ_neg = 1.05 > τ_pos = 1.0. SAPO with τ_neg = 0.95 < τ_pos = 1.0 (inverted asymmetry) collapses almost immediately. This establishes that the direction of the asymmetry matters critically and that negative tokens require more aggressive damping. This is the only hyperparameter ablation reported for SAPO — no sweeps over τ_pos, τ_neg, or their ratio are provided, nor is there any analysis of sensitivity to these values.

**Absence of routing replay for SAPO (Figures 4, 6):** SAPO achieves stable training without the routing replay mechanism used in GRPO-R2. This is demonstrated implicitly by the fact that GRPO-R2 (which includes routing replay) underperforms SAPO, and that SAPO's stability is achieved through objective design alone. However, SAPO is not compared against plain GRPO (without routing replay) in any experiment, so it is impossible to attribute the stability difference specifically to the soft gate versus the combination of soft gate + elimination of routing replay instability. The paper claims that eliminating routing replay "improves exploration and reduces engineering overhead" (Section 5.1) but provides no quantitative evidence for improved exploration (e.g., diversity metrics, entropy of sampled responses).

**Within-sequence variance validation (Figures 2–3):** The scatter plots of Var_i(θ) versus D_i(θ) confirm that the theoretical bound D_i(θ) ≤ τ_i² Var_i(θ) / 4 is tight and that the approximation error is small in practice. This is a robustness check on the SAPO → GSPO reduction argument: it shows that the Taylor expansion error is not just theoretically bounded but in fact much smaller than the bound for the vast majority of training sequences. For the dense model (Figure 3, right), D_i(θ) values cluster below 0.0014 even as Var_i(θ) extends to ~0.006, confirming that the sequence-level approximation is excellent for dense architectures. For the MoE model (Figure 2, right), D_i(θ) extends to ~0.006 at the highest Var_i(θ) values (~0.020), indicating somewhat larger but still modest approximation errors in the worst cases.

**Multi-task multimodal transfer (Figure 6):** While not strictly an ablation, the Qwen3-VL experiment serves as a robustness check on domain transfer — SAPO's benefits are not confined to mathematical reasoning with pure text models. The consistent, albeit small, performance advantage across multimodal tasks supports the claim that SAPO provides a "more reliable, scalable, and effective optimization strategy" (Abstract). However, the experiment uses the same MoE architecture (Qwen3-VL-30B-A3B), so it does not test architectural generality beyond what is already shown in the controlled experiments.

### Critical Assessment

#### Does SAPO genuinely improve training stability, or does it merely delay collapse?

The paper's central claim is that SAPO provides improved training stability — and Figure 4 provides clear evidence: GSPO and GRPO-R2 collapse well before step 1750, while SAPO continues to improve. However, there are important caveats. First, SAPO's BeyondAIME curve in Figure 4 shows a slight decline after step 1500 (from ~0.58 to ~0.56), suggesting that SAPO is not immune to eventual degradation — it merely extends the stable training window. The paper acknowledges this implicitly ("SAPO sustains coherent learning for a longer duration" — emphasis on "longer" rather than "indefinitely"), but it does not report how long SAPO can train before eventual collapse, which limits the practical guidance for how long to train. Second, the experiments use a single set of hyperparameters for each method, taken from Zheng et al. (2025). It is possible that GSPO and GRPO-R2 could achieve better stability with different hyperparameters (e.g., smaller learning rates, tighter ε values), and the paper provides no evidence that the comparison reflects each method at its best. The claim of "improved stability" is therefore conditional on the specific hyperparameter configuration used.

#### Does SAPO achieve higher final performance, or does it merely avoid degradation?

The absolute Pass@1 differences between SAPO and the baselines at the end of training are modest — roughly 0.02 on AIME25 (0.82 vs. 0.80) and larger on HMMT25 and BeyondAIME where baselines have already collapsed. This raises the question: if GSPO and GRPO-R2 were early-stopped at their peak (around step 500–750 in Figure 4), would SAPO still outperform them? The answer appears to be yes for AIME25 (SAPO's peak of ~0.82 exceeds GSPO's peak of ~0.80–0.81) and BeyondAIME (SAPO's ~0.58 exceeds GSPO's ~0.55), but the margin is small on AIME25. The primary practical advantage of SAPO is therefore not dramatically higher peak performance but rather **robustness to training duration** — SAPO's performance is less sensitive to when training is stopped, making it a safer choice for production training where the optimal stopping point is unknown a priori.

#### The temperature asymmetry claim is well-supported, but the temperature values are not justified.

Figure 5 convincingly demonstrates that τ_neg > τ_pos is necessary and that the inversion (τ_neg < τ_pos) is catastrophic. However, the specific values τ_pos = 1.0 and τ_neg = 1.05 appear to be a single manually-chosen operating point with no reported sweep. The paper provides no guidance on how to select these values for new models, tasks, or architectures. The small gap (0.05) between τ_neg and τ_pos suggests that only a modest asymmetry is needed for the Qwen3-30B-A3B model on mathematical reasoning, but it is unknown whether this generalizes. A sweep over τ_pos and τ_neg values — or at minimum, a sensitivity analysis showing that performance is robust to small perturbations around (1.0, 1.05) — would substantially strengthen the practical utility of the method.

#### What is not tested: missing experiments that would strengthen the claims.

Several experiments that would directly support the paper's claims are absent:

- **SAPO vs. vanilla GRPO (without routing replay):** The paper positions SAPO as a replacement for hard clipping, but the only GRPO variant tested includes routing replay. A direct comparison against standard GRPO would test whether SAPO's soft gate alone can stabilize MoE training or whether routing replay is still needed in some form.
- **Dense model training runs:** The empirical validation of assumptions includes a dense model (Qwen3-4B, Figure 3), but no training results are reported for dense models. The paper's claims about SAPO's generality across architectures are therefore supported only by the distributional analysis, not by actual training outcomes.
- **Sweeps over temperature hyperparameters:** The paper introduces τ_pos and τ_neg as new tunable parameters but provides no guidance on their selection. A sweep showing the stability-performance tradeoff as τ varies would be valuable.
- **Multiple random seeds:** All training curves appear to be single runs. Without replicates, the statistical reliability of the observed differences — especially the small gaps on AIME25 and the aggregated VL score — cannot be assessed.
- **Longer training horizons for Qwen3-VL:** The VL experiment runs to only 500 steps, where all methods are still improving. Extending this experiment to the point where baselines collapse (as in Figure 4) would test whether SAPO's stability advantage persists in multimodal multi-task settings at scale.
- **Per-benchmark breakdown for Qwen3-VL:** The aggregated validation score in Figure 6 obscures whether SAPO's advantage is uniform across tasks or concentrated in specific ones (e.g., math vs. coding vs. logic). A per-benchmark breakdown would reveal whether the method's benefits are task-dependent.

#### The evidence for improved sample efficiency is indirect.

The paper claims that SAPO "preserves the learning signal from near-on-policy tokens" in sequences where GSPO would discard the entire sequence due to a few off-policy tokens, thereby "improving sample efficiency" (Section 1). This mechanism is analytically sound (Section 4.1) and the distributional evidence (Figures 2–3) confirms that conditions where this would matter do occur, but the paper provides **no direct experimental evidence** that sample efficiency is actually improved. No experiment measures gradient variance, effective sample size, or learning speed per unit of collected data. The higher final performance in Figures 4 and 6 could be attributed to better stability (longer effective training) rather than better sample efficiency (more learning per gradient step), and the paper does not disentangle these two mechanisms. An experiment comparing SAPO and GSPO at fixed, very low numbers of gradient steps (before any method has time to collapse) would test the sample efficiency claim directly — but this is not reported.

#### The benchmark set for controlled experiments is narrow.

The mathematical reasoning benchmarks (AIME25, HMMT25, BeyondAIME) are all competition-level math problems. While this is a natural domain for RL-based reasoning training, it provides no evidence about SAPO's behavior on other task types (code generation, instruction following, factual QA, creative generation). The VL experiment partially addresses this by including coding and logical reasoning, but the aggregated reporting makes it impossible to assess per-task performance. A method that improves stability by more aggressively damping negative-token gradients might, in principle, reduce the model's ability to learn from negative examples on tasks where negative feedback is particularly informative (e.g., tasks with clear "wrong" answers that differ from the correct one in structured ways). This potential tradeoff is not explored.

#### Scaling behavior is not characterized.

All experiments use a single model scale (30B-A3B for training, 4B for distributional analysis). The paper provides no evidence about how SAPO's advantages scale with model size. Do larger models exhibit more severe token-ratio dispersion, making SAPO's token-adaptive gating more valuable? Or does the reduction to sequence-level behavior (Section 4.1) become more accurate at scale, reducing the marginal benefit over GSPO? The paper's claims about being a "scalable" optimization strategy (Abstract) are not empirically supported by scaling experiments.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### Insufficient Hyperparameter Guidance for Temperature Selection

**The assumption or constraint.** SAPO introduces two new temperature hyperparameters — `$\tau_{\text{pos}}$` and `$\tau_{\text{neg}}$` — and the paper demonstrates that the choice of these values has a dramatic effect on training stability: the wrong configuration (particularly `$\tau_{\text{neg}} < \tau_{\text{pos}}$`) causes catastrophic collapse, and even symmetric temperatures (`$\tau_{\text{neg}} = \tau_{\text{pos}}$`) show clear signs of instability relative to the asymmetric setting (Figure 5). Despite this sensitivity, the paper operates at a single operating point — `$\tau_{\text{pos}} = 1.0$` and `$\tau_{\text{neg}} = 1.05$` — with no sweep, no sensitivity analysis, and no principled method for selecting these values on new models, architectures, or tasks. The paper does not acknowledge this as a limitation, nor does it discuss it in the conclusion or future work sections.

**The consequence.** A practitioner wanting to deploy SAPO on a different model family (e.g., dense rather than MoE, a different parameter scale, a different pretraining distribution) has no guidance on how to choose `$\tau_{\text{pos}}$` and `$\tau_{\text{neg}}$`. Should the gap between them scale with vocabulary size (since the gradient-diffusion argument in Equation 9 depends on `$|\mathcal{V}|$`)? Should it vary with model scale or training duration? Should it be tuned on a validation set, and if so, using what metric? The paper provides no answers. Given that the inverted configuration (`$\tau_{\text{neg}} = 0.95$`) collapses almost immediately (Figure 5), the penalty for choosing wrong is severe — a practitioner who guesses `$\tau_{\text{neg}}$` slightly below `$\tau_{\text{pos}}$` may get a failed training run with no diagnostic signal beyond the collapse itself. The small gap between the working values (0.05) suggests that the safe operating region may be narrow, making manual tuning risky.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figure 5 is the only temperature ablation, and it tests only three configurations (`$\tau_{\text{neg}} = 1.05$`, `$1.0$`, `$0.95$`) with `$\tau_{\text{pos}}$` fixed at `$1.0$`. No sweep over `$\tau_{\text{pos}}$` is reported — it is possible that `$\tau_{\text{pos}} = 1.0$` is not optimal even for the Qwen3-30B-A3B model, and that other values would yield better performance or stability. The paper provides no analysis of how the effective trust-region width (controlled by `$\tau$`) interacts with the clipping band width `$\varepsilon$` used in GRPO/GSPO, making it difficult to translate existing hyperparameter knowledge to the SAPO setting.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not suggest any heuristic, automated tuning procedure, or theoretical guideline for temperature selection. The values `$1.0$` and `$1.05$` are presented as a working recipe for Qwen3-30B-A3B on mathematical reasoning, with no claim or evidence that they generalize.

### No Dense-Model Training Results

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's theoretical motivation emphasizes that MoE architectures are especially susceptible to the instability SAPO addresses, because "routing heterogeneity and long responses can amplify deviations across tokens" (Section 1), and the empirical validation in Figures 2–3 confirms that token-ratio dispersion is wider for MoE models (Qwen3-30B-A3B, Figure 2, `$\text{Var}_i(\theta)$` extending to ~0.020) than for dense models (Qwen3-4B, Figure 3, `$\text{Var}_i(\theta)$` extending to ~0.006). However, **all training experiments in Sections 5.1 and 5.2 use MoE models exclusively** — Qwen3-30B-A3B and Qwen3-VL-30B-A3B. No training run is reported for a dense architecture.

**The consequence.** The paper's claim that SAPO "provides a more reliable, scalable, and effective optimization strategy for RL training of LLMs" (Abstract) is supported only for MoE models. The dense-model distributional evidence (Figure 3) confirms that the theoretical conditions for SAPO's sequence-level reduction hold even more strongly for dense architectures, which could mean that SAPO's token-level adaptivity provides less marginal benefit over GSPO for dense models — if sequences are always well-behaved (low `$\text{Var}_i$`), then the SAPO → GSPO reduction is nearly exact, and the practical difference between the two methods may vanish. Alternatively, the dense setting might reveal different instability patterns (e.g., slower-developing overfitting rather than the rapid collapse seen in Figure 4) that SAPO handles differently. Without dense-model training results, it is impossible to know whether SAPO's advantages generalize to the large fraction of production models that use dense architectures, or whether SAPO is primarily an MoE-specific solution dressed in general-purpose language.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figure 3 provides the dense-model analog of Figure 2, confirming that `$r_{i,t}(\theta)$` concentrates around 1 and `$\text{Var}_i(\theta)$` is small. The `$D_i(\theta)$` values in Figure 3 (right panel) are an order of magnitude smaller than in Figure 2, suggesting the sequence-level approximation is excellent. But this is purely distributional evidence collected from off-policy mini-batches — it says nothing about training dynamics, final performance, or stability comparisons against baselines.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not mention the absence of dense-model training experiments as a limitation, nor does it suggest future work to validate on dense architectures. The dense model (Qwen3-4B) is used only for the assumption-validation check and never appears in training results.

### No Direct Evidence for the Sample Efficiency Claim

**The assumption or constraint.** A central claim of the paper is that SAPO improves sample efficiency by preserving gradient information from tokens that GSPO would discard. The stated mechanism (Section 1, Section 4.1) is: when a sequence contains a few highly off-policy tokens that push `$s_i(\theta)$` outside GSPO's hard clipping band, GSPO zeroes out gradients for the entire sequence, including the many near-on-policy tokens within it; SAPO, by contrast, "selectively down-weights only the offending tokens and preserves the learning signal from the near-on-policy ones, improving sample efficiency" (Section 1). This mechanism is analytically sound, and the empirical distributional evidence (Figures 2–3) confirms that sequences with outlier tokens do occur in training, especially for MoE models. However, the paper provides **no experiment that directly measures sample efficiency**.

**The consequence.** The higher final performance and better stability observed in Figures 4 and 6 could be explained entirely by SAPO's ability to train longer without collapse — not by learning more per gradient step. If SAPO and GSPO were compared at a low, fixed number of gradient steps before any method collapses (e.g., step 250 or 500 in Figure 4, where all three curves are still rising), and SAPO showed higher accuracy, that would be direct evidence of better sample efficiency. But the paper does not perform this analysis. The observed performance gap grows largest at later steps where baselines have already degraded (e.g., HMMT25 at step 1750 in Figure 4, where GSPO has collapsed to ~0.50 while SAPO is at ~0.65), which is evidence for stability, not sample efficiency. A stability improvement is valuable in its own right, but it is a different claim than "improving sample efficiency," and the paper conflates the two by attributing performance gains to the selective-attenuation mechanism without disentangling stability from per-sample learning.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The distributional analysis (Figures 2–3) establishes that the conditions for selective attenuation (sequences with heterogeneous token ratios) occur in practice, and the theoretical bound in Equation 21 quantifies when the token-level gates deviate from the sequence-level approximation. But no experiment measures effective sample size, gradient variance, learning curves at fixed step counts, or any other metric that would directly quantify sample efficiency. The training reward curves in Figure 4 show SAPO's reward rising faster than baselines in the early phase (steps 0–250), which is suggestive but confounded by differences in optimization dynamics beyond sample efficiency.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper treats the sample efficiency improvement as analytically implied by the mechanism but never tests it empirically. The word "efficiency" appears in the context of "sample efficiency" in the abstract and introduction, but the experiments measure only final performance and stability, not efficiency.

### Difficulty Estimation Cost Is Entirely Externalized — No Guidance for Practical Deployment Parameter Selection

**The assumption or constraint.** SAPO is presented as a drop-in replacement for the clipping mechanism in group-based policy optimization, with the claim that its hyperparameters (`$\tau_{\text{pos}}$`, `$\tau_{\text{neg}}$`) replace the clipping range `$\varepsilon$` used in GRPO and GSPO. However, the paper provides no method for selecting these hyperparameters, no sensitivity analysis showing robustness to their values, and no comparison of the difficulty of tuning SAPO versus tuning `$\varepsilon$` for the baselines. The implicit assumption is that the values that work for Qwen3-30B-A3B on mathematical reasoning (`$\tau_{\text{pos}} = 1.0$`, `$\tau_{\text{neg}} = 1.05$`) are either universal or easy to adapt.

**The consequence.** In practice, RL training of LLMs involves extensive hyperparameter tuning — learning rate, batch size, group size `$G$`, and for GRPO/GSPO, the clipping parameter `$\varepsilon$`. SAPO adds two new continuous hyperparameters whose values must be chosen before training begins, with severe consequences for getting them wrong (Figure 5). The paper provides no guidance on whether `$\tau_{\text{pos}}$` and `$\tau_{\text{neg}}$` interact with other hyperparameters (e.g., does a larger learning rate require larger `$\tau$` values to compensate for larger policy steps?). The total tuning burden for a new model or task is therefore unclear: a practitioner might need to grid-search over `$(\tau_{\text{pos}}, \tau_{\text{neg}})$` in addition to all existing hyperparameters, and since the penalty for poor choices is training collapse (Figure 5), each point in the grid risks a failed run. This makes SAPO potentially more expensive to deploy in practice than the baselines, even if it is more stable once tuned.

Additionally, the paper does not report the `$\varepsilon$` values used for GSPO and GRPO-R2 in Figures 4 and 6 — it states only that it uses "the same hyperparameter configurations as in Zheng et al. (2025)." Without knowing whether those `$\varepsilon$` values were optimized for the baselines or simply carried over from prior work, it is unclear whether the comparison in Figure 4 reflects each method near its best or whether GSPO/GRPO-R2 could achieve better stability with different `$\varepsilon$` settings. If the baselines were not tuned, SAPO's stability advantage may partly reflect better default hyperparameters rather than a fundamental algorithmic improvement.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figure 5 provides the only hyperparameter ablation, testing three `$\tau$` configurations with `$\tau_{\text{pos}}$` fixed. No sweep over `$\tau_{\text{pos}}$`, no interaction analysis with learning rate or group size, and no comparison of tuning difficulty versus the baselines appear anywhere in the paper.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not acknowledge the tuning burden as a limitation, provide a heuristic for temperature selection, or compare the sensitivity of SAPO's performance to `$\tau$` against the sensitivity of GRPO/GSPO to `$\varepsilon$`.

### Single Benchmark Domain and Narrow Task Distribution in Controlled Experiments

**The assumption or constraint.** All controlled training experiments in Section 5.1 use mathematical reasoning as the training task, with evaluation on three competition-math benchmarks: AIME25, HMMT25, and BeyondAIME. The Qwen3-VL experiments in Section 5.2 broaden the task distribution to include coding and logical reasoning, but the reporting is aggregated — no per-task breakdown is provided — and the architecture remains MoE (Qwen3-VL-30B-A3B). The paper claims that SAPO is "a more reliable, scalable, and effective optimization strategy for RL training of LLMs" (Abstract), implying broad applicability across tasks, domains, and model families.

**The consequence.** Math reasoning is a specific RL training regime: rewards are typically binary (correct/incorrect) and sparse, advantages within a group are often bimodal (correct responses get positive advantages, incorrect ones get negative advantages), and the optimal policy involves producing long chains of reasoning with intermediate steps. In this setting, negative-advantage tokens are abundant (most sampled responses are incorrect, especially early in training), making the asymmetric temperature design highly relevant. On tasks with different reward structures — dense rewards, multi-dimensional rewards, or tasks where negative examples carry more structured information (e.g., code generation where a slightly wrong token produces a specific bug rather than a generic wrong answer) — the optimal balance between positive and negative damping may differ. More fundamentally, if a task has a high baseline success rate (most sampled responses are correct), negative-advantage tokens are rare, and the asymmetry may provide little benefit. Conversely, on tasks where negative feedback is the primary learning signal (e.g., safety training where the model must learn what *not* to say), aggressively damping negative-token gradients could slow or prevent learning.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The Qwen3-VL experiment (Figure 6) partially addresses domain generality by including coding and logic tasks, but the aggregation obscures per-task behavior. If SAPO's advantage on the aggregated score comes entirely from math tasks (where it was already shown to work in Figure 4) and provides no benefit — or even a disadvantage — on coding or logic tasks, the paper does not reveal it. The training task mixture in Section 5.2 is described as "a broad collection of text and multimodal tasks, including mathematics, coding, and logical reasoning" with "a fixed sampling ratio for each task," but neither the specific tasks nor their ratios are enumerated.

**Mitigation status.** Partially addressed by the Qwen3-VL experiment, but the aggregation and lack of per-task reporting prevent any conclusion about domain generality. The paper does not acknowledge the narrow task distribution as a limitation.

### No Statistical Replicates and Small Effective Sample for Stability Claims

**The assumption or constraint.** All training curves in Figures 4, 5, and 6 are presented as single trajectories — one run per method per configuration — with no error bars, confidence intervals, or mention of multiple random seeds. The paper does not state whether the reported curves represent a single training run or an average over multiple runs (the absence of any variance indication strongly suggests single runs). The test sets are not described in terms of size: AIME25 typically contains 15 problems, HMMT25 and BeyondAIME likely contain on the order of tens of problems each based on their competition format. The paper reports Pass@1 computed over 16 or 32 samples per problem, but the number of problems determines the statistical resolution of the accuracy metric.

**The consequence.** The central empirical claims of the paper — that SAPO provides improved training stability and higher final performance — are based on point estimates from what appears to be a single training run per method. The gap between SAPO and GSPO on AIME25 at step 1750 is approximately 0.02 in Pass@1 (roughly 0.82 vs. 0.80). If the test set is 15 problems, a 0.02 difference in Pass@1 corresponds to a fraction of one problem — well within the range of sampling noise. The paper's claim that SAPO "achiev[es] higher Pass@1 performance under comparable training budgets" (Abstract) would be substantially weakened if this gap is not statistically significant. The stability claim — that baselines exhibit "early training collapse" while SAPO does not — is more qualitative and less dependent on statistical testing, but it still relies on the assumption that the observed collapse is systematic rather than an artifact of a particular random seed, learning rate schedule interaction, or other stochastic factor. In RL training of large models, run-to-run variance can be substantial due to the compounding effects of exploration noise and the sensitivity of optimization dynamics to early gradient steps.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. No statistical tests are performed, no replicates are reported, and the test set sizes are not specified. The training reward curves (Figures 4–6, left panels) show inherent batch-level noise, confirming that stochasticity is present in the training process, but no attempt is made to characterize how this noise propagates to the conclusions about relative method performance.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not acknowledge the lack of replicates as a limitation or suggest that future work should include statistical validation. The single-run reporting is standard in much of the LLM RL literature (including the GSPO paper by Zheng et al., 2025, which the current paper cites and builds upon), but it means that the quantitative performance comparisons — particularly the small gaps on AIME25 and the aggregated VL score — should be interpreted as suggestive rather than conclusive.
