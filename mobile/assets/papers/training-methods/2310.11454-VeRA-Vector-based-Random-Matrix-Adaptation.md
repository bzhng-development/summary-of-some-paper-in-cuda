# VeRA: Vector-based Random Matrix Adaptation

**ArXiv:** [2310.11454](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.11454)

## 🎯 Pitch

VeRA slashes LoRA’s trainable parameters by up to 100× without losing performance—by freezing a single random low-rank matrix pair in amber across all layers and learning only tiny scaling vectors. This means you no longer have to store a unique, massive adapter for every user or task, just a handful of scalars that modulate the same fixed random projections.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **Vector-based Random Matrix Adaptation (VeRA)**, a method that significantly reduces trainable parameters during LLM finetuning by replacing LoRA's learned low-rank matrices with a single pair of frozen, random matrices shared across all layers, adapted instead through small trainable scaling vectors. Evaluated on the GLUE benchmark with RoBERTa, the E2E benchmark with GPT-2, instruction-tuning of Llama 7B and 13B models, and image classification with Vision Transformers, VeRA achieves performance comparable to LoRA while using tenfold fewer parameters on GLUE for RoBERTa-large and approximately 100× fewer on instruction-tuning (1.6M vs. 159.9M trainable parameters for Llama 7B). The scaling vectors operate by modulating rows and columns of the frozen random matrices, establishing that competitive adaptation quality can be maintained even when the adaptation subspace is generated from entirely random, unlearned projections as long as per-layer gating through learned scaling coefficients is permitted.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Storage Problem in Parameter-Efficient Finetuning

The paper addresses a deceptively simple practical problem: **the memory required to store finetuned model weights becomes unmanageable when adapting large models for many users or many tasks**. While parameter-efficient finetuning (PEFT) methods like LoRA have dramatically reduced the *number of parameters that must be trained* compared to full finetuning, they still produce adapter weights that must be stored and loaded for each adapted version of the model.

The authors make this problem concrete with a motivating calculation in Section 1:

> "applying LoRA with a rank of 16 to the query and value layers of GPT-3 would demand at least 288MB of memory, if stored in single-precision – at a million finetuned weights, e.g., one per user, that would amount to 275TB."

This is not a hypothetical concern. Consider a cloud-based operating system assistant that continuously adapts to individual user behaviors—the storage requirements scale linearly with the number of users multiplied by the per-adapter parameter count. Even modest adapter sizes become prohibitive at deployment scale. The problem compounds when models are adapted for multiple tasks per user or when edge devices with limited storage must maintain local adaptations.

The storage bottleneck is qualitatively different from the training-time bottleneck that motivated LoRA. LoRA's innovation was reducing gradient computation and optimizer state requirements during training. However, after training, LoRA's learned low-rank matrices A and B must be persisted—the parameter count at storage time equals the trainable parameter count. VeRA's core insight is that **the storage burden can be decoupled from the representation capacity**: if the bulk of the adaptation structure comes from frozen random matrices that can be regenerated from a seed, then storing only small scaling vectors enables the same representational power with dramatically less persistent memory.

### The Theoretical Gap: Intrinsic Dimensionality Suggests Untapped Efficiency

The paper grounds its motivation in the empirical finding of Aghajanyan et al. (2021) that pretrained language models have surprisingly low **intrinsic dimensionality** for finetuning. The intrinsic dimension $d_{90}$ is defined as the smallest subspace dimension that achieves 90% of the full finetuning performance when parameters are randomly projected into that subspace and optimized there.

The key numbers: for RoBERTa-base, $d_{90}$ was reported as **896**. Yet LoRA for RoBERTa-base uses approximately **0.3M trainable parameters** (as reported by Hu et al., 2022). The authors of this paper flag the discrepancy explicitly in Section 2:

> "According to Aghajanyan et al. (2021), the upper bound for intrinsic dimensions is much smaller than what is typically utilized in such methods. For instance, the $d_{90}$ for RoBERTa-base is reported to be 896, whereas authors of the LoRA paper reported using 0.3M trainable parameters for this model, suggesting that the parameter count could be reduced further."

This gap—896 versus 300,000—represents more than two orders of magnitude. It suggests there is substantial room to compress the adaptation representation without sacrificing performance. The theoretical implication is that the low-rank matrices in LoRA may encode significant redundancy; the true information content needed for adaptation on a specific task may be far smaller than what LoRA stores.

The paper positions VeRA as directly exploiting this gap. Rather than learning the basis vectors of the adaptation subspace (as LoRA does by training A and B), VeRA uses **random basis vectors** (the frozen matrices) and learns only the coefficients (the scaling vectors d and b) that select and weight which random directions are useful. This is a conceptual shift: from "find the right low-dimensional subspace" to "find the right combination of randomly oriented directions."

### Prior Approaches and Where They Fall Short

The paper situates itself against a landscape of PEFT methods, each with distinct limitations:

**LoRA (Hu et al., 2022).** Train low-rank matrices $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times r}$ and $B \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times n}$ as a decomposition of the weight update $\Delta W = BA$. The number of trainable (and storable) parameters scales as $2 \times L_{\text{tuned}} \times d_{\text{model}} \times r$. At typical ranks ($r = 16$ for large models), this produces megabytes to hundreds of megabytes of adapter weights per task. The limitation is not training cost per se—LoRA already handles that well—but the **persistent storage cost** when many adapters must be maintained.

**AdaLoRA (Zhang et al., 2023b).** Extends LoRA with dynamic rank allocation, pruning less important singular values to optimally distribute the parameter budget across layers. While this improves the parameter-quality Pareto frontier, it does not fundamentally change the scaling relationship: the number of stored parameters still grows with $r \times d_{\text{model}}$. AdaLoRA reduces redundancy within the learned subspace but does not challenge the paradigm that the basis matrices themselves must be learned and stored.

**Adapter-based methods (Houlsby et al., 2019; Lin et al., 2020; Pfeiffer et al., 2021).** Insert small bottleneck modules between transformer layers. These introduce additional inference latency because the adapter modules cannot be merged into the pretrained weights—they are separate computational steps in the forward pass. The VeRA paper explicitly notes this as a design constraint it inherits from LoRA:

> "importantly, and different to adapter-based finetuning approaches, LoRA incurs no additional inference time cost when deployed, as the trainable matrices can be merged with the frozen weights."

VeRA preserves this property: the frozen random matrices A and B, scaled by the learned vectors $d$ and $b$, can be multiplied together and added to $W_0$, producing a single weight matrix that replaces the original at inference time.

**BitFit (Zaken et al., 2022).** Finetunes only bias vectors. This is extremely parameter-efficient (roughly 0.1M parameters for RoBERTa-base) but shows inconsistent performance—Table 2 shows it underperforms LoRA by 1.2 percentage points on the GLUE average for RoBERTa-base (85.4 vs. 86.6).

**Full finetuning.** The gold standard for performance but requires storing a complete copy of the model for each task, which is infeasible at scale.

### The Random Models Literature as Intellectual Foundation

The paper draws on a distinct line of research that is not traditionally part of the PEFT conversation: the surprising effectiveness of models with random weights and projections. The authors cite multiple strands of evidence in Section 2:

- **Lottery Ticket Hypothesis (Frankle & Carbin, 2019):** Randomly initialized networks contain subnetworks that can reach high performance when trained—suggesting that random initializations provide a rich basis from which useful subnetworks can be selected.

- **Weight-Agnostic Neural Networks (Ramanujan et al., 2020):** There exist subnetworks within randomly initialized networks that achieve impressive results even without any training—showing that random projections themselves can encode useful transformations if the right subset is selected.

- **Frozen Transformers (Lu et al., 2022; Schrimpf et al., 2021; Frankle et al., 2021):** Frozen, randomly initialized transformers with only small sections finetuned can perform surprisingly well—directly supporting the idea that adaptation can occur by modulating existing (even random) structure rather than learning it from scratch.

- **Random Feature Attention (Peng et al., 2021):** Random projections can serve as effective attention mechanisms—demonstrating that random matrices can produce useful representations in transformer architectures specifically.

- **HyperDreamBooth (Ruiz et al., 2023):** A concurrent work that also uses frozen random matrices inside LoRA for personalization of text-to-image models, providing independent evidence for the approach's viability.

This literature collectively suggests that **the specific values in the low-rank matrices may matter less than having the right degrees of freedom to modulate them**. If random matrices provide a sufficiently rich basis, then learning which random directions to use (via scaling vectors) could be as effective as learning the directions themselves (via LoRA's trained A and B), while requiring far fewer learned parameters.

### How VeRA Positions Itself

The paper's positioning is as a **direct refinement of LoRA that targets the storage bottleneck specifically, without sacrificing the merge-at-inference property**. The key equations (Equations 1 and 2 in Section 3.1) make this explicit:

$$\text{LoRA: } h = W_0 x + \underline{B}\underline{A}x$$
$$\text{VeRA: } h = W_0 x + \Lambda_b B \Lambda_d A x$$

The underlined terms are trained via gradient descent. In VeRA, A and B are untrained (frozen, random, shared across layers), while $\Lambda_b$ and $\Lambda_d$ (diagonal matrices representing the learned scaling vectors $b$ and $d$) are the only trained components. The structural similarity is intentional: VeRA inherits the merge-at-inference property and the application-to-specific-layer-types pattern from LoRA, but substitutes learned low-rank structure with learned selection of random structure.

The sharing of A and B across all adapted layers is a bold design choice that the paper explicitly validates through ablation (Table 7 in Section 4.6), finding minimal performance difference between shared and unique random matrices. This sharing is what makes the parameter savings extreme: the frozen matrices are generated once from a random seed and never stored, while the per-layer scaling vectors are tiny.

The paper acknowledges its scope limitations: it focuses on transformer architectures in language and vision, uses the same layer application pattern as LoRA (query and value projections), and does not explore dynamic budget allocation or regularization refinements—leaving those for future work.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

The paper proposes a **finetuning adapter system** that plugs into existing transformer models and modifies their behavior for a new task while learning orders of magnitude fewer parameters than prior methods. The system solves the **storage bottleneck** problem: when you need to maintain thousands of personalized model copies (one per user or per task), the disk space consumed by storing each adapter's trained weights becomes the limiting factor. VeRA's insight is that you can freeze a single pair of random matrices shared across all layers, regenerate them from a seed rather than storing them, and only learn per-layer scaling vectors that select which random directions to use—shifting the storage cost from "all the adapter weights" to "a handful of scaling coefficients plus a seed."

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The system has four major components organized into a weight-modification pipeline that runs within each adapted transformer layer:

1. **Frozen Random Low-Rank Matrices (A, B):** Two matrices—$A \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times n}$ and $B \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times r}$—initialized once with Kaiming initialization and **shared across all adapted layers**. They are never trained and never stored; they are regenerated from a single random number generator seed whenever the model is loaded. They provide a rich, random basis of adaptation directions.

2. **Trainable Scaling Vectors (d, b):** Two small vectors—$d \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times r}$ and $b \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times 1}$—that are the **only learned parameters per layer**. The vector $d$ (represented as diagonal matrix $\Lambda_d$) scales rows of the frozen matrix $A$ before the product, effectively gating which of the $r$ random directions are used and with what strength. The vector $b$ (represented as diagonal matrix $\Lambda_b$) scales rows of the final product $B A$, providing a per-output-dimension modulation.

3. **Pretrained Weight Matrix ($W_0$):** The original frozen model weights that the adapter modifies. At inference time, the adapter contribution $\Lambda_b B \Lambda_d A$ is computed once and added to $W_0$, producing a single weight matrix with zero inference latency overhead.

4. **Classification Head (for downstream tasks):** A task-specific output layer trained in the standard way (full finetuning of just the head parameters). The paper consistently reports trainable parameters excluding this head to isolate the adapter's efficiency.

Information flows through the system in a strict order during training and inference:

- **First**, the input $x$ is multiplied by the pretrained weight matrix $W_0$ (frozen, no gradient computation for these parameters).
- **Second**, the same input $x$ is multiplied by the adapter pathway: $x \rightarrow A \rightarrow \Lambda_d \rightarrow B \rightarrow \Lambda_b$, where $A$ and $B$ are regenerated from the random seed (no storage, no gradient), and $\Lambda_d$ and $\Lambda_b$ are applied as element-wise scaling of intermediate activations.
- **Third**, the two pathways are summed: $h = W_0 x + \Lambda_b B \Lambda_d A x$, and this combined output feeds into the next transformer layer.
- **At storage time**, only the scaling vectors $d$ and $b$ for each adapted layer, plus the classification head weights, are written to disk. On reload, the random matrices are regenerated from the seed, the product $\Lambda_b B \Lambda_d A$ is computed, and the adapter is merged into $W_0$.

- **Third, initialization strategies:** Why Kaiming initialization for the frozen matrices and specific initial values for the scaling vectors are essential design choices that make training stable without per-rank learning rate tuning.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **method-design paper** whose core idea is that learning which random directions to use (via scaling vectors) can be as effective as learning the directions themselves (via trained low-rank matrices), while requiring radically fewer stored parameters.

#### 3.4.1 The Core Equations: From LoRA to VeRA

The paper builds directly on LoRA's formulation. In LoRA, for a pretrained weight matrix $W_0 \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$, the forward pass for an input $x$ is:

$$h = W_0 x + \Delta W x = W_0 x + \underline{B}\underline{A}x$$

where $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times r}$ and $B \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times n}$ are the low-rank matrices, $r \ll \min(m, n)$ is the bottleneck rank, and the underline notation indicates parameters updated via gradient descent. The weight update $\Delta W = BA$ is a product of two learned matrices.

**What it computes:** The LoRA forward pass computes the original model's output $W_0 x$ and adds a low-rank correction $BAx$. The correction is constrained to have rank at most $r$ because $B$ has $r$ columns and $A$ has $r$ rows—their product cannot exceed rank $r$. The correction is learned end-to-end via backpropagation through both $A$ and $B$, which are initialized such that $BA = 0$ at the start of training (typically $A$ uses Kaiming initialization and $B$ is initialized to zeros).

**Why this form:** The low-rank constraint is motivated by the empirical finding (Aghajanyan et al., 2021) that weight updates during finetuning have low intrinsic dimension. By forcing $\Delta W$ to be low-rank, LoRA dramatically reduces the number of parameters that must be optimized and stored—from $m \times n$ for full finetuning to $r \times (m + n)$ for LoRA. The product form $BA$ ensures the rank constraint is structural, not a soft regularization. The zero-initialization of $B$ ensures the model starts at the pretrained behavior.

VeRA modifies this formulation to:

$$h = W_0 x + \Delta W x = W_0 x + \Lambda_b B \Lambda_d A x$$

where $A \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times n}$ and $B \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times r}$ are now **frozen, random, and shared across all adapted layers**, while $\Lambda_b \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times m}$ and $\Lambda_d \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times r}$ are diagonal matrices representing the learned scaling vectors $b \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times 1}$ and $d \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times r}$.

**What it computes:** The VeRA forward pass computes the original model's output $W_0 x$ and adds a correction that is the product of: (1) the frozen random matrix $A$, (2) diagonal scaling by learned vector $d$ (which gates each of the $r$ random directions in $A$), (3) the frozen random matrix $B$, and (4) diagonal scaling by learned vector $b$ (which gates each output dimension). The correction $\Delta W = \Lambda_b B \Lambda_d A$ has its structure determined by the frozen random matrices but its effective coefficients determined by the learned scaling vectors.

**Why this form:** The key design decisions are captured in this equation:

- **Freezing A and B eliminates their storage and gradient computation.** Since $A$ and $B$ are random and shared, they can be regenerated from a single random number generator seed at load time. The per-adapter storage cost drops from storing both $A$ and $B$ for every layer to storing only the much smaller $b$ and $d$ vectors plus one integer seed.

- **The diagonal scaling vectors $d$ and $b$ provide layer-specific adaptation.** Even though $A$ and $B$ are identical across all layers, the learned $d$ and $b$ vectors differ per layer, allowing each layer to select and weight different combinations of the shared random basis directions. The vector $d$ operates on the intermediate dimension $r$ (the rank), scaling each of the $r$ random directions before they are mixed by $B$. The vector $b$ operates on the output dimension $m$, scaling each output feature after the random transformation. Together, they can effectively "select" which random directions matter and "disable" irrelevant ones by driving scaling coefficients toward zero.

- **The matrices A and B are not required to be low-rank in the traditional sense.** The paper explicitly notes: "In this setup, $B \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times r}$ and $A \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times n}$ are not required to be low-rank. This is because they remain static and we do not need to store their values." The dimensionality $r$ is still called the "rank" by analogy with LoRA, but it functions differently: increasing $r$ in VeRA linearly increases the number of trainable parameters (via a longer $d$ vector) and the expressivity of the random basis, but does not require storing matrices of size $m \times r$ and $r \times n$ per layer.

- **The merge-at-inference property is preserved.** After training, the product $\Lambda_b B \Lambda_d A$ can be computed once (since all four components are now fixed) and added to $W_0$, producing $W' = W_0 + \Lambda_b B \Lambda_d A$. At inference time, only $W'$ is used, with zero additional computation compared to the original model. This is the same property that distinguishes LoRA from adapter-based methods.

#### 3.4.2 Parameter Counting and Storage Analysis

The paper provides a detailed parameter count comparison in Section 3.2 and Table 1. Let $L_{\text{tuned}}$ be the number of finetuned layers and $d_{\text{model}}$ be the dimension of those layers (for simplicity, assuming square weight matrices where $m = n = d_{\text{model}}$, which holds for the query and key projections in standard transformers).

**LoRA parameter count.** LoRA trains two matrices per adapted layer: $A \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}} \times r}$ and $B \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times d_{\text{model}}}$. The total number of trainable (and storable) parameters is:

$$|\Theta_{\text{LoRA}}| = 2 \times L_{\text{tuned}} \times d_{\text{model}} \times r$$

**What this means:** Each adapted layer contributes $2 \times d_{\text{model}} \times r$ parameters. For RoBERTa-base with $d_{\text{model}} = 768$, $L_{\text{tuned}} = 48$ (24 layers, query and value projections each), and $r = 16$: this yields $2 \times 48 \times 768 \times 16 = 1,179,648$ parameters, matching the approximately 1.2M reported in prior work. For GPT-3 with $d_{\text{model}} = 12288$, $L_{\text{tuned}} = 192$ (96 layers, query and value), and $r = 16$: this yields $2 \times 192 \times 12288 \times 16 \approx 75.5$M parameters, requiring approximately 288MB in single-precision.

**VeRA parameter count.** VeRA trains two vectors per adapted layer: $d \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times r}$ and $b \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}} \times 1}$. The total number of trainable (and storable) parameters is:

$$|\Theta_{\text{VeRA}}| = L_{\text{tuned}} \times (d_{\text{model}} + r)$$

**What this means:** Each adapted layer contributes $d_{\text{model}} + r$ parameters—the $d_{\text{model}}$ elements of $b$ (one per output dimension) plus the $r$ elements of $d$ (one per rank direction). For RoBERTa-base with $r = 16$: $48 \times (768 + 16) = 48 \times 784 = 37,632$ total trainable parameters. For GPT-3 with $r = 16$: $192 \times (12288 + 16) = 192 \times 12304 \approx 2.4$M parameters, requiring only approximately 9.1MB in single-precision.

The paper highlights several crucial aspects of this parameter scaling:

- **At the lowest rank ($r = 1$), VeRA uses approximately half the parameters of LoRA.** This is because VeRA's formula simplifies to $L_{\text{tuned}} \times (d_{\text{model}} + 1)$ versus LoRA's $2 \times L_{\text{tuned}} \times d_{\text{model}} \times 1 = 2 \times L_{\text{tuned}} \times d_{\text{model}}$. For large $d_{\text{model}}$, the $+1$ is negligible, giving roughly a 2× reduction.

- **As rank increases, VeRA's parameter count grows by only $L_{\text{tuned}}$ per rank increment.** Each additional unit of rank adds one element to $d$ per layer, costing $L_{\text{tuned}}$ parameters total. In contrast, LoRA's parameter count grows by $2 \times L_{\text{tuned}} \times d_{\text{model}}$ per rank increment—each additional rank adds a full column to $A$ and a full row to $B$ per layer. For GPT-3 with $d_{\text{model}} = 12288$, going from $r = 16$ to $r = 17$ adds 192 parameters for VeRA versus $2 \times 192 \times 12288 = 4,718,592$ parameters for LoRA—a 24,576× difference in the marginal cost.

- **The storage advantage comes from the fact that frozen random matrices are not stored.** The paper states: "Because the random frozen matrices can be regenerated from a random number generator (RNG) seed, these do not need to be stored in memory. This substantially reduces the memory requirement, which is now limited to the bytes needed for the trained $b$ and $d$ vectors and a single RNG seed." In contrast, LoRA must store all values of $A$ and $B$ for every adapted layer, which dominates the storage cost.

The concrete numbers in Table 1 make this tangible:

| Configuration | LoRA Parameters | LoRA Storage | VeRA Parameters | VeRA Storage |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| RoBERTa-base, r=1 | 36.8K | 144KB | 18.4K | 72KB |
| RoBERTa-base, r=16 | 589.8K | 2MB | 18.8K | 74KB |
| RoBERTa-base, r=256 | 9437.1K | 36MB | 24.5K | 96KB |
| GPT-3, r=16 | 75.5M | 288MB | 2.8M | 10.5MB |
| GPT-3, r=256 | 1207.9M | 4.6GB | 8.7M | 33MB |

The trend is stark: as rank increases, LoRA's parameter count explodes linearly with $r$, while VeRA's barely changes. For RoBERTa-base, going from $r = 1$ to $r = 256$ increases VeRA's parameter count by only 6.1K parameters (from 18.4K to 24.5K), while LoRA's increases by over 9.4M. This enables VeRA to use very high ranks (the paper uses $r = 1024$ for RoBERTa-base on GLUE and $r = 256$ for RoBERTa-large) while still maintaining an order-of-magnitude parameter advantage over even low-rank LoRA configurations.

#### 3.4.3 Initialization Strategies: Why They Are Essential

The paper devotes Section 3.3 to initialization, and the ablation study in Section 4.6 (Table 6) demonstrates that initialization choices significantly affect performance. Three components require initialization:

**Shared matrices A and B: Kaiming initialization.** The paper uses Kaiming initialization (He et al., 2015), also known as He initialization, for the frozen low-rank matrices. Specifically, values are drawn from a distribution designed to preserve variance through linear layers:

$$A_{ij} \sim \mathcal{N}\left(0, \sigma^2\right) \quad \text{or} \quad A_{ij} \sim U\left[-\text{bound}, \text{bound}\right]$$

where the variance $\sigma^2$ (or the uniform bound) is scaled based on the matrix dimensions. For a matrix with $n_{\text{in}}$ input features, Kaiming normal uses $\sigma^2 = 2 / n_{\text{in}}$ and Kaiming uniform uses $\text{bound} = \sqrt{6 / n_{\text{in}}}$.

**What this achieves:** The critical property the paper emphasizes is that Kaiming initialization ensures that "a matrix product of A and B maintains a consistent variance for all ranks, eliminating the need to finetune the learning rate for each rank." This is non-obvious but important: if the variance of $A$ and $B$ were not scaled appropriately, the magnitude of the product $BA$ would change with rank $r$, requiring the learning rate to be retuned for each rank. By using Kaiming initialization—which is designed to preserve variance through linear transformations regardless of input/output dimensions—the magnitude of $BA$ is rank-independent. This allows the authors to sweep across ranks in Section 4.5 without re-tuning the learning rate for each configuration.

**Why Kaiming over alternatives:** The ablation in Table 6 compares three initializations for the shared matrices: Kaiming uniform, Kaiming normal, and uniform in the range $[0, 0.1]$. On the RTE task with RoBERTa-large, Kaiming uniform achieves 85.8% accuracy, Kaiming normal achieves 82.6%, and Uniform[0, 0.1] achieves only 53.1%. The paper interprets this as showing that "both Kaiming initializations outperform the uniform range initialization." The poor performance of Uniform[0, 0.1] is likely because positive-only random values bias the adaptation subspace, preventing the scaling vectors from effectively representing sign changes that may be needed. The slight edge of uniform over normal (85.8 vs. 82.6 on RTE, 90.5 vs. 90.0 on MRPC) is noted but small.

**Scaling vector b: initialized to zeros.** The vector $b$ is initialized with all elements set to zero: $b_i = 0$ for all $i \in \{1, \ldots, d_{\text{model}}\}$.

**What this achieves:** This initialization mirrors the standard LoRA practice of initializing matrix $B$ to zeros, which ensures that $\Delta W = 0$ at the start of training. The forward pass on the first training step produces exactly the pretrained model's output, because $\Lambda_b = 0$ (the diagonal matrix of all zeros) zeros out the entire adapter contribution regardless of the values in $A$, $B$, and $d$. The model therefore begins training exactly at the pretrained behavior, and the adapter contribution grows from zero as training proceeds.

**Why this design:** Starting from the pretrained behavior is a general best practice in finetuning—it ensures the optimization starts from a known good solution rather than a random perturbation. If $b$ were initialized to non-zero values, the random matrices would immediately perturb the model's output in arbitrary directions, potentially damaging the pretrained representations before the optimizer has a chance to correct them. This is especially important for VeRA because the matrices $A$ and $B$ are random and fixed—there is no mechanism for the optimization to "undo" harmful random directions other than driving corresponding elements of $d$ and $b$ to zero, which takes training steps.

**Scaling vector d: initialized with a single non-zero constant value.** All elements of $d$ are initialized to the same scalar value, denoted $d_{\text{init}}$: $d_j = d_{\text{init}}$ for all $j \in \{1, \ldots, r\}$.

**What this achieves:** The parameter $d_{\text{init}}$ is a hyperparameter that controls the initial scale of the adaptation along each of the $r$ random directions. Since $b$ is zero, the initial forward pass is still the pretrained output, but the gradient signal through $d$ is affected by $d_{\text{init}}$: larger values produce larger initial gradients through the $d$ vector, while smaller values produce smaller initial gradients.

**Why this design, and the sensitivity to $d_{\text{init}}$:** The ablation in Table 6 tests three values for $d_{\text{init}}$: $1.0$, $10^{-1}$, and $10^{-7}$. The results show dramatic sensitivity: on MRPC, $d_{\text{init}} = 10^{-1}$ achieves 90.5%, $10^{-7}$ achieves 90.8%, but $1.0$ achieves only 70.3%. On RTE, $d_{\text{init}} = 10^{-1}$ achieves 85.8%, $10^{-7}$ achieves 84.7%, but $1.0$ achieves only 60.3%. The paper interprets this as evidence that smaller initial values "potentially offering more flexibility in the optimization process through early sign changes in selected rows of the frozen matrices." In operational terms: when $d_{\text{init}}$ is large ($1.0$), the gradient signal through $d$ is large from the start, which may cause the optimizer to take large steps that overshoot good solutions or cause elements of $d$ to change sign too aggressively (effectively "flipping" which random directions are used). When $d_{\text{init}}$ is small ($10^{-1}$ or $10^{-7}$), the adaptation grows gradually, allowing the optimizer to discover which random directions are useful before committing to large magnitude changes.

The paper defaults to $d_{\text{init}} = 0.1$ (i.e., $10^{-1}$) for the GLUE experiments (Table 8), indicating this value was found to work robustly across tasks.

#### 3.4.4 The Sharing Mechanism: Why Identical Random Matrices Across All Layers Work

One of the most counterintuitive design choices in VeRA is that the frozen random matrices $A$ and $B$ are **shared across all adapted layers**. In a standard transformer, each layer learns distinct representations—early layers typically capture local syntactic patterns while later layers capture higher-level semantic features. It would seem natural that different layers would need different adaptation bases.

The paper validates this choice explicitly in Section 4.6 (Table 7), comparing shared matrices against unique (layer-specific) random matrices on four GLUE tasks using RoBERTa-large:

| Task | Shared Matrices | Unique Matrices |
|---|---|---|
| MRPC | 90.0 ± 0.9 | 90.7 ± 0.3 |
| RTE | 84.6 ± 1.5 | 84.6 ± 0.8 |
| CoLA | 67.7 ± 0.8 | 68.3 ± 1.8 |
| STS-B | 91.5 ± 0.6 | 91.5 ± 0.2 |

The results show that unique matrices provide at most a marginal improvement (0.7 points on MRPC, 0.6 on CoLA, identical on RTE and STS-B), and in some cases slightly higher variance (unique matrices produce tighter standard deviations, which is expected since they provide more degrees of freedom). The paper reports: "the mean performance is identical in case of tasks RTE and STS-B, and there is a slight improvement for MRPC and CoLA when using unique matrices."

**Why this works (the paper's implicit argument):** The learned scaling vectors $d$ and $b$ are per-layer, which means each layer can select and weight different subsets of the shared random directions. If the random matrices $A$ and $B$ span a sufficiently rich set of directions (i.e., if $r$ is large enough that the random subspace is not a bottleneck), then the per-layer scaling provides enough expressivity to capture layer-specific adaptation needs. The $d$ vector can amplify certain random directions in one layer while suppressing them in another, and the $b$ vector can gate which output dimensions of the random transformation are used. This is conceptually similar to how different layers of a pretrained model recode the same input in different ways, even though the architecture is identical across layers.

**Practical implications:** Sharing the random matrices is what enables the extreme storage efficiency. If unique matrices were needed per layer, the parameter count advantage over LoRA would be much smaller—the scaling vectors are tiny, but storing unique random matrices for every layer would require either storing them explicitly (negating the storage benefit) or managing a separate random seed for each layer (feasible but more complex). The paper's demonstration that sharing works without significant performance loss is therefore critical to the method's practical viability.

**Connection to the magnitude analysis in Figure 3:** The paper further supports the sharing argument by visualizing the magnitude of the learned $d$ vectors across layers after finetuning on RTE (Figure 3 in Section 4.6). The plot shows $||d||_2$ (the L2 norm of the $d$ vector) for query and value projection matrices across the 24 layers of RoBERTa-large. The key observations: (1) query matrices show larger adaptation magnitudes than value matrices across most layers, (2) later layers (layers 15-24) show substantially larger adaptation than earlier layers, and (3) the magnitude varies smoothly and continuously across layers, not abruptly. This pattern—larger adaptation in later layers—matches findings from prior PEFT work (Zhang et al., 2023b; Liu et al., 2021) and confirms that even though the random basis is identical, the learned scaling vectors successfully capture layer-varying adaptation needs.

#### 3.4.5 Training Pipeline and Gradient Flow

The training procedure for VeRA follows the same overall structure as LoRA-based finetuning but with a critical difference in which parameters receive gradient updates.

**Forward pass:** For a given layer with pretrained weights $W_0 \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$, input $x \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times 1}$, frozen random matrices $A \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times n}$ and $B \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times r}$, and learned scaling vectors $d \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times r}$ and $b \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times 1}$:

1. Compute the pretrained output: $h_{\text{pretrain}} = W_0 x$.
2. Compute the intermediate projection through $A$: $z_1 = A x$, producing a vector of length $r$.
3. Apply the $d$ scaling elementwise: $z_2 = d \odot z_1$, where $\odot$ is elementwise multiplication and $d$ is broadcast appropriately. This is equivalent to $\Lambda_d A x$ where $\Lambda_d$ is the diagonal matrix with $d$ on its diagonal.
4. Compute the projection through $B$: $z_3 = B z_2$, producing a vector of length $m$.
5. Apply the $b$ scaling elementwise: $z_4 = b \odot z_3$. This is equivalent to $\Lambda_b z_3$ where $\Lambda_b$ is the diagonal matrix with $b$ on its diagonal.
6. Sum the pathways: $h = h_{\text{pretrain}} + z_4 = W_0 x + \Lambda_b B \Lambda_d A x$.

**Backward pass (gradient computation):** The key efficiency is that gradients are computed only for $b$ and $d$, not for $A$, $B$, or $W_0$. The autograd engine treats $A$ and $B$ as constants with `requires_grad=False`. The gradient with respect to $d_j$ (the $j$-th element of $d$) involves the partial derivative of the loss $\mathcal{L}$ through the chain: $\mathcal{L} \to h \to z_4 \to z_3 \to z_2 \to d_j$. Similarly, the gradient with respect to $b_i$ (the $i$-th element of $b$) involves $\mathcal{L} \to h \to z_4 \to b_i$. The frozen matrices $A$ and $B$ appear only as multiplicative constants in these gradient computations, meaning their values are used but their gradients are not stored.

**What this means for memory during training (Table 12, Appendix C):** The paper reports that training VeRA on Llama 7B with rank 64 uses 21.69GB of GPU memory versus 23.42GB for LoRA—a 7.4% reduction. This reduction comes from not needing to store optimizer states (momentum and variance buffers for AdamW) or gradients for the matrices $A$ and $B$, which in LoRA would account for a significant fraction of the total memory. However, the training time is nearly identical: 578 minutes for VeRA versus 568 minutes for LoRA—a modest 1.8% increase attributed to "the additional vector multiplies in the forward pass."

**Hyperparameter configuration:** The paper provides detailed hyperparameters for each experiment in Appendix A (Tables 8-11). For the GLUE benchmark (Table 8), the key settings are:

- **Optimizer:** AdamW across all experiments.
- **Warmup ratio:** 0.06 for GLUE.
- **Learning rate schedule:** Linear decay.
- **Shared matrix initialization:** Kaiming Uniform (used across all GLUE experiments).
- **Initial value of $d$:** 0.1.
- **Learning rate for VeRA parameters:** Separately tuned from the classification head learning rate. For RoBERTa-base on SST-2: head LR = $4 \times 10^{-3}$, VeRA LR = $4 \times 10^{-3}$. For RTE: head LR = $1 \times 10^{-2}$, VeRA LR = $4 \times 10^{-3}$. This two-learning-rate approach replaces LoRA's $\alpha$ scaling hyperparameter.
- **VeRA rank:** 1024 for RoBERTa-base, 256 for RoBERTa-large.

For instruction-tuning (Table 9), the key settings differ:
- **VeRA rank:** 1024.
- **LoRA rank:** 64 (for comparison).
- **VeRA learning rate:** $4 \times 10^{-3}$.
- **LoRA learning rate:** $4 \times 10^{-4}$.
- **Batch size:** 4 with 4 accumulation steps (effective batch size 16).
- **One epoch** of training on the 51K-example Alpaca dataset.

The paper notes: "due to our academic compute we were not able to run full grid searches on any hyperparameters. We only evaluated different learning rates and number of epochs and even relied on existing configurations of LoRA (Optimizer, Warmup ratio, LR schedule)."

#### 3.4.6 Merge-at-Inference: Eliminating Inference Latency

A critical property that VeRA inherits from LoRA is that the adapter can be merged into the pretrained weights after training, producing a single weight matrix that incurs zero additional inference latency. The paper emphasizes this in the introduction: "Similarly to LoRA, trained scaling vectors along with low-rank matrices can be merged into original weights, eliminating additional inference latency."

The merge operation is:

$$W' = W_0 + \Lambda_b B \Lambda_d A$$

where all components on the right-hand side are now fixed (the learned $b$ and $d$, the regenerated frozen $A$ and $B$, and the original $W_0$). The product $\Lambda_b B \Lambda_d A$ is computed once—either at model loading time or during a post-training conversion step—and the resulting matrix of shape $m \times n$ is added to $W_0$. After merging, the model uses $W'$ in place of $W_0$ for all forward passes, with no adapter pathway and no extra computation.

**What this enables:** The merged model is a standard transformer with modified weight matrices but no additional modules, branches, or sequential adapter layers. This means:
- **Inference is identical in cost to the original pretrained model:** The number of multiply-add operations per forward pass is unchanged.
- **Existing inference infrastructure works without modification:** Optimized kernels, quantization, pruning, and deployment pipelines that expect standard transformer weight matrices can use the merged model directly.
- **No runtime adapter switching overhead:** For scenarios where different tasks require different adapters, the adapters can be pre-merged into separate copies of the weights, or loaded and merged on-the-fly from the tiny scaling vector storage.

This property is what distinguishes LoRA and VeRA from adapter-based methods like Houlsby et al. (2019), which insert additional layers that must be executed sequentially at inference time, adding latency that cannot be eliminated through merging.

#### 3.4.7 Expressivity Analysis: What VeRA Can Represent

Appendix E of the paper provides an empirical analysis of VeRA's expressivity by testing how well it can fit random square matrices. This is a controlled experiment that isolates the representational capacity of the parametrization from any specific task.

The setup: given a random target matrix $T \in \mathbb{R}^{10 \times 10}$, how well can LoRA and VeRA approximate it as a function of the number of trainable parameters? For LoRA, the approximation is $\hat{T}_{\text{LoRA}} = B A$ with $A \in \mathbb{R}^{10 \times r}$, $B \in \mathbb{R}^{10 \times r}$, and $r$ varied to control parameter count. For VeRA, the approximation is $\hat{T}_{\text{VeRA}} = \Lambda_b B \Lambda_d A$ with fixed random $A, B \in \mathbb{R}^{10 \times 10}$ (full rank, square matrices), and the number of parameters controlled by varying the length of $d$ and $b$.

The result (Figure 6 in Appendix E): "For given number of trainable parameters, both methods perform equally well, with VeRA providing more flexibility, e.g. by allowing for much lower parametrization—below LoRA's rank 1." The key insight is that VeRA can use fewer parameters than the minimum possible with LoRA: LoRA at rank $r = 1$ uses $2 \times 10 \times 1 = 20$ parameters, but VeRA can use as few as 11 parameters (one for $b$ if $d$ is a scalar, or any combination of $d$ and $b$ dimensions). This finer granularity comes from the fact that $d$ and $b$ contribute independently to the parameter count (via $m + r$ per layer), whereas LoRA's parameters are coupled through the product $m \times r + r \times n$.

**Why this matters:** This expressivity analysis provides theoretical grounding for why VeRA can match LoRA's performance with fewer parameters—the parametrization itself is not less expressive than LoRA; it simply achieves equivalent expressivity through a different mechanism (learning which random directions to weight rather than learning the directions themselves). The practical advantage is that this mechanism happens to produce far fewer parameters that need to be stored.

#### 3.4.8 Connection to Intrinsic Dimensionality and the Random Subspace Hypothesis

The paper's theoretical motivation connects back to the intrinsic dimensionality findings of Aghajanyan et al. (2021). The argument (implicit in the paper's structure, explicit in Section 2) is:

- **Finding 1:** Pretrained models have low intrinsic dimension for finetuning—the weight updates needed for adaptation live in a subspace far smaller than the full parameter space.
- **Finding 2:** Random projections can serve as effective bases for optimization in that low-dimensional subspace (as demonstrated by Aghajanyan et al., 2021, who showed that training only a small number of parameters randomly projected back into the full space could achieve 90% of full-parameter performance).
- **VeRA's hypothesis:** If random projections work for optimization, then perhaps random matrices can work as the *adaptation basis itself*, with learned coefficients (the scaling vectors) selecting the effective subspace. The random matrices $A$ and $B$ define a random projection from the model's activation space through a rank-$r$ bottleneck and back, and the scaling vectors $d$ and $b$ learn to select which directions in this random projection are useful.

The paper does not prove this hypothesis formally, but the experimental results—particularly the competitive performance with LoRA across diverse tasks and the finding that sharing random matrices across layers works—provide strong empirical support.

**A subtlety about the rank $r$ in VeRA:** In LoRA, $r$ is the rank of the learned weight update $\Delta W = BA$—both $A$ and $B$ are learned, and their product is guaranteed to have rank at most $r$. In VeRA, the actual rank of the adapted weight update $\Delta W = \Lambda_b B \Lambda_d A$ is **not necessarily equal to $r$**. Because $B$ and $A$ are random full-rank matrices (assuming $r \leq \min(m, n)$), the product $BA$ is full-rank $r$, and the diagonal scalings $\Lambda_b$ and $\Lambda_d$ can zero out certain rows or columns, reducing the effective rank. The parameter $r$ in VeRA is better understood as the **dimension of the random bottleneck**—the size of the intermediate representation space between $A$ and $B$—rather than the rank of the resulting weight update, which is determined by the learned scaling vectors. This is why VeRA can use very high values of $r$ (1024 for RoBERTa-base) without a proportional increase in parameters: the scaling vectors can effectively zero out unnecessary random directions, adapting the effective rank to the task automatically.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Decoupling Adaptation Expressivity from Storage Cost by Substituting Learned Basis Directions with Random Ones

The dominant assumption in parameter-efficient finetuning since LoRA has been that the low-rank matrices A and B must themselves be *learned*—that is, gradient descent must discover the right low-dimensional subspace in which weight updates should live. This assumption directly couples the representation capacity of the adapter to its storage requirements: if you want a rank-r adapter to be expressive, you must store A and B, each of size proportional to d_model × r, for every adapted layer.

VeRA challenges this assumption at its foundation. The paper's central conceptual move is asking: **must the basis vectors of the adaptation subspace themselves be discovered through learning, or can a random basis suffice as long as the coefficients that select and weight those basis vectors are learned?** This is not an optimization trick or a compression scheme applied post-hoc to LoRA—it is a rethinking of *where the learned information resides*. In LoRA, the learned information is distributed across all entries of A and B, encoding both the basis directions and their relative importance. In VeRA, the basis directions are intentionally random and fixed, and the learned information is concentrated entirely in the per-element scaling coefficients that gate which random directions are used and with what strength.

The intellectual lineage from the random-models literature (Frankle & Carbin, 2019; Ramanujan et al., 2020; Lu et al., 2022) to this insight is direct but non-obvious. Prior work had shown that random weights can serve as surprisingly effective starting points for optimization and that subnetworks within random networks can perform well. But VeRA makes a stronger claim: random matrices can serve as the *permanent adaptation structure*, with learning only required to select among them. The scaling vectors do not modify the random matrices—they simply amplify or attenuate pre-existing random directions. This is conceptually closer to feature selection (learning which of a set of random features to use) than to representation learning (learning the features themselves).

The significance of this reframing extends beyond parameter counts. It suggests a fundamentally different mental model for what finetuning does: rather than *finding* the right low-dimensional structure in the weight update, finetuning can be understood as *selecting* from a sufficiently rich pool of pre-existing random structure. This connects to the intrinsic dimensionality findings of Aghajanyan et al. (2021) in a non-obvious way: if the intrinsic dimension is ~900 for RoBERTa-base, the adaptation subspace is very low-dimensional relative to the full parameter space, but it need not be a *specific* low-dimensional subspace—it can be any low-dimensional subspace spanned by enough random directions, as long as the selection mechanism (the scaling vectors) is learned per task and per layer.

**Evidence:** The fact that VeRA with r=1024 and randomized A and B matches LoRA's performance on GLUE (Table 2) while using ~10× fewer stored parameters provides the core empirical support. More subtly, the finding that sharing the same random matrices across all layers causes only marginal performance degradation (Table 7: 85.8% vs. 85.8% on RTE, 90.5% vs. 90.7% on MRPC) supports a deeper claim: the random basis is generic enough that per-layer adaptation can be achieved entirely through learned scaling, without any layer-specific basis structure. This is a stronger result than simply showing parameter efficiency—it demonstrates that the specific values of A and B are largely irrelevant as long as a rich-enough random pool exists.

**Fundamental vs. incremental:** This is a fundamental conceptual shift, not an incremental improvement on LoRA. It changes the question from "how can we learn the best low-rank approximation to the weight update?" (LoRA's framing) to "how few learned parameters are needed to gate pre-existing random structure into a task-appropriate configuration?" This opens an entirely different design space for adapter methods—one where storage requirements are decoupled from expressivity because the expressive component is regenerated rather than stored.

---

### Innovation 2: Storage Cost Scaling Independently of Rank, Enabling Ultra-High-Rank Adaptation

LoRA's parametric relationship—|Θ| = 2 × L_tuned × d_model × r—creates a direct tradeoff: higher rank means better expressivity but also more parameters to store. This tradeoff is particularly painful for very wide models like GPT-3 with d_model = 12288, where even r=16 produces 75.5M stored parameters (288MB), and increasing rank to r=64 quadratically increases storage to impractically large values. The field's implicit compromise has been to use modest ranks (r=16 or r=64) and accept whatever expressivity that provides.

VeRA breaks this tradeoff by changing the parametric relationship to |Θ| = L_tuned × (d_model + r). The term that multiplies d_model in LoRA—the r factor—simply disappears from VeRA because the d_model dimension of A and B is no longer learned and stored. The term that does scale with r—the size of the d vector—adds only 1 parameter per rank per layer, compared to LoRA's 2 × d_model parameters per rank per layer.

What makes this an *intellectual* innovation rather than just a clever engineering optimization is how it changes the nature of the rank parameter. In LoRA, rank is a direct resource allocation knob: turning it up trades storage for expressivity at a known (and large) marginal cost. In VeRA, rank becomes nearly free from a storage perspective—going from r=16 to r=1024 on RoBERTa-base adds only 1008 parameters *total* across all layers (from 18.8K to 24.5K, as shown in Table 1). This means VeRA can operate at ranks that would be absurdly expensive in LoRA (r=1024 for RoBERTa-base would require ~38M parameters in LoRA vs. 24.5K in VeRA).

The consequence is that **VeRA can use high-dimensional random subspaces that LoRA cannot afford**. At r=1024, VeRA's frozen matrices provide 1024 random directions to select from, vastly more than LoRA's typical r=16 or r=64 learned directions. The scaling vector d can then learn a sparse selection of which few directions actually matter, driving most coefficients to near-zero while amplifying the useful ones. This is evidenced by the fact that VeRA's performance scales efficiently with rank (Section 4.5, Figure 2)—higher ranks improve or maintain performance without the catastrophic storage blowup LoRA would experience.

**Evidence:** The scaling experiment in Figure 2 demonstrates that at equal numbers of trainable parameters, VeRA *outperforms* LoRA by approximately 4 accuracy percentage points on RTE when VeRA's rank is high enough that its parameter count matches LoRA's. This means the high-rank random basis is not just matching LoRA's performance despite random rather than learned directions—it is *providing a better performance-per-parameter tradeoff* when operating in the same parameter budget regime. The random directions, when selected appropriately by learned scaling, provide a richer adaptation space than the low-rank learned directions LoRA discovers at the same parameter count.

**Fundamental vs. incremental:** This is a fundamental structural innovation in how rank relates to storage, not an incremental parameter reduction. It changes what practitioners can do with the rank hyperparameter: in LoRA, rank is a budget-limited parameter (can't afford high r); in VeRA, rank is effectively free and can be set very high, with the learned scaling vectors performing implicit dimensionality reduction by selecting which random directions to actually use.

---

### Innovation 3: Layer-Specific Adaptation Through Learned Gating of Shared Structure

The standard architecture of deep networks, and the standard practice in LoRA, treats different layers as learning fundamentally different weight updates. Each layer's A and B matrices in LoRA are independent—early layers learn early-layer-specific adaptations, late layers learn late-layer-specific adaptations, and there is no mechanism forcing them to share structure.

VeRA's design—sharing the exact same random matrices A and B across all adapted layers and differentiating only through learned per-layer scaling vectors—makes a strong claim about what layer-specific adaptation actually requires. The claim is: **different layers do not need different adaptation *directions*; they need different adaptation *magnitudes* along a shared set of directions**. The random matrices define a universal set of directions in the adaptation space, and the per-layer scaling vectors d and b learn to amplify or suppress each direction differently per layer.

This reframes layer-wise adaptation from "each layer finds its own low-dimensional update" to "all layers share a pool of possible updates, and each layer learns which subset to activate and how strongly." The distinction is subtle but important. In LoRA, early layers might learn completely orthogonal low-rank subspaces from late layers because there is no coupling between their A and B matrices. In VeRA, the frozen matrices force the adaptation directions to live in the same space across all layers; only the gating (the learned coefficients) differs.

The paper's evidence that this works—Table 7 showing minimal performance difference between shared and unique random matrices—is remarkable because it suggests that the *diversity* of adaptation across layers in LoRA may be partially redundant. If layer-specific learned matrices were genuinely encoding fundamentally different adaptation subspaces, replacing them with shared random matrices would cause catastrophic performance degradation. That it doesn't—performance is preserved within 0-0.7 percentage points across four tasks—implies that much of the layer-specific structure in LoRA's learned matrices may be noise or redundancy, and the truly important layer-wise signal is captured by how strongly each direction is weighted, not by the directions themselves.

**Evidence:** The magnitude analysis in Figure 3 supports this framing: query adaptation magnitudes are systematically larger than value ones, and later layers show systematically larger magnitudes than early layers. These are smooth, interpretable patterns that emerge from the learned scaling vectors despite the frozen matrices being identical across all layers. The scaling vectors are discovering layer-varying structure—not by learning different basis directions per layer, but by learning different coefficients on shared directions. The fact that these magnitude patterns match findings from prior PEFT work (Zhang et al., 2023b; Liu et al., 2021) that used fully learned adapters suggests VeRA is capturing the same layer-importance phenomena through a fundamentally different (and more parameter-efficient) mechanism.

**Fundamental vs. incremental:** This is an incremental refinement of LoRA in the sense that it still modifies weight matrices through a low-rank-like bottleneck, but a fundamental conceptual contribution in demonstrating that the bulk of layer-specific adaptation information can be encoded in coefficient vectors rather than learned basis matrices. It provides architectural evidence for the hypothesis that adaptation across transformer layers lives in a relatively *universal* subspace, with layer-specificity emerging from gating rather than from fundamentally different update geometries.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Datasets.** The paper evaluates on four distinct benchmarks spanning language understanding, language generation, instruction-following, and image classification:
  - **GLUE benchmark** (Wang et al., 2019): A suite of natural language understanding tasks. The paper evaluates on six tasks—SST-2, MRPC, CoLA, QNLI, RTE, and STS-B—and reports the GLUE average. The two most computationally intensive tasks, MNLI and QQP, are omitted "due to time constraints and budget limitations" (Section 4.1), meaning the reported GLUE average is over a subset and not directly comparable to full GLUE results in prior work that include all tasks.
  - **E2E benchmark** (Novikova et al., 2017): A natural language generation task for restaurant-domain dialogue. The paper evaluates using five automatic metrics: BLEU, NIST, METEOR, ROUGE-L, and CIDEr.
  - **Alpaca dataset** (Taori et al., 2023): Specifically the cleaned version from `yahma/alpaca-cleaned`, comprising 51K instruction-demonstration pairs for instruction-tuning. Evaluation is performed on MT-Bench (Zheng et al., 2023), which uses GPT-4 as a judge to score model responses to 80 multi-turn questions on a 1–10 scale.
  - **Image classification datasets:** Four datasets—CIFAR100 (Krizhevsky, 2009), Food101 (Bossard et al., 2014), Flowers102 (Nilsback & Zisserman, 2008), and RESISC45 (Cheng et al., 2017). For each dataset, training uses only 10 samples per class (a few-shot setting), with evaluation on the full test set for CIFAR100, Food101, and Flowers102, and on all remaining samples for RESISC45.

- **Base models.** The paper uses multiple model families across scales:
  - **RoBERTa-base and RoBERTa-large** (Liu et al., 2019) for the GLUE benchmark. These are encoder-only transformer models with 125M and 355M parameters respectively.
  - **GPT-2 Medium and Large** (Radford et al., 2019) for the E2E benchmark. These are decoder-only transformer models with 355M and 774M parameters respectively, reflecting parameter counts reported alongside the E2E results in Table 3.
  - **Llama 7B and 13B** (Touvron et al., 2023a) and **Llama2 7B and 13B** (Touvron et al., 2023b) for instruction-tuning. VeRA is applied to all linear layers except the top one, following the practice of Dettmers et al. (2023). Quantization techniques from Dettmers et al. (2023) are used to train on a single GPU.
  - **ViT-Base and ViT-Large** (Dosovitskiy et al., 2021) for image classification, using weights pretrained on ImageNet-21k (Deng et al., 2009). ViT-Base has 85.8M parameters and ViT-Large has 303.3M parameters, as reported in Table 5.
  The model choice is deliberate: the paper aims to demonstrate VeRA's effectiveness across encoder-only, decoder-only, and vision transformer architectures, at scales ranging from 125M to 13B parameters.

- **Metrics.**
  - **GLUE tasks:** Matthew's correlation for CoLA, Pearson correlation for STS-B, and accuracy for SST-2, MRPC, QNLI, and RTE. Higher values indicate better performance for all metrics.
  - **E2E benchmark:** BLEU, NIST, METEOR, ROUGE-L, and CIDEr scores, all standard for NLG evaluation.
  - **Instruction-tuning:** Average score (1–10) assigned by GPT-4 acting as a judge on MT-Bench, evaluating answer quality across multi-turn conversations.
  - **Image classification:** Top-1 accuracy on the test set (or remaining samples for RESISC45).
  - **Parameter count:** All experiments report the number of trainable parameters attributable to the finetuned layers, explicitly excluding the classification head which is trained in the standard way. This ensures fair comparison across methods that all must train a task-specific output layer.

- **Baselines.** The paper compares VeRA against six classes of methods, with results for non-VeRA methods sourced from prior work (Hu et al., 2022; Valipour et al., 2022; Zi et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2023a):
  - **Full finetuning (FT):** All model parameters are trained. Represents the performance upper bound but the worst storage efficiency.
  - **BitFit** (Zaken et al., 2022): Trains only bias vectors while keeping all other parameters frozen. Extremely parameter-efficient (0.1M parameters for RoBERTa-base) but shows inconsistent task performance.
  - **Adapter-based methods:** Multiple variants—AdapterH (Houlsby et al., 2019) inserts adapter layers between self-attention and MLP modules with a residual connection; AdapterL (Lin et al., 2020) places the adapter only after the MLP module and subsequent to LayerNorm; AdapterP (Pfeiffer et al., 2021) uses a similar design; AdapterD (Rücklé et al., 2021) drops certain adapter layers for efficiency. These methods incur additional inference latency since adapters cannot be merged into pretrained weights.
  - **LoRA** (Hu et al., 2022): The primary baseline. Trains low-rank matrices A and B as a decomposition of the weight update ΔW = BA.
  - **LoRA variants:** LoRA-FA (Zhang et al., 2023a) for memory-efficient LoRA; DyLoRA (Valipour et al., 2022) and AdaLoRA (Zi et al., 2023) for dynamic rank allocation.
  - **Head-only:** For image classification, training only the classification head with all other parameters frozen serves as a minimal baseline.

- **Training budget and hyperparameter protocol.** The paper does not use a unified "generation budget" concept (as the MATH benchmark paper does) since finetuning experiments measure parameter count, not inference-time compute. Instead, fairness is controlled by:
  - **Consistent epoch counts and batch sizes** within each benchmark, tuned per method but reported transparently in Appendix A (Tables 8-11).
  - **Separate learning rates for the classification head and the adapter parameters** for VeRA, replacing LoRA's α scaling hyperparameter. The learning rates and number of epochs are determined through hyperparameter tuning, though the paper acknowledges "due to our academic compute we were not able to run full grid searches on any hyperparameters. We only evaluated different learning rates and number of epochs" (Appendix A).
  - **Application to the same layers** as LoRA: query and value projection matrices in each self-attention module for GLUE and image classification; all linear layers except the top one for instruction-tuning.
  - **For LoRA on GLUE and E2E**, the paper uses the implementation and hyperparameters from Hu et al. (2022). For LoRA on instruction-tuning, the rank is set to 64 as proposed by Dettmers et al. (2023).

- **Cross-validation and statistical protocol.** For GLUE experiments (Section 4.1), the paper reports: "We perform 5 runs with different random seeds, recording the best epoch's outcome for each run, and report the median of these results." For the scaling experiment in Section 4.5, "we replicate each configuration five times for different random seeds, and report the median of results." For ablation studies in Section 4.6, "Each experiment is run with 5 random seeds, and we report the mean and standard deviation of the results." For E2E, results are reported from the last epoch without mention of multiple seeds. For instruction-tuning, training is done for one epoch with a learning rate sweep, and evaluation is a single run on MT-Bench. The paper does not report confidence intervals or statistical significance tests for head-to-head comparisons, nor does it describe a cross-validation procedure for strategy selection (the paper is a method evaluation, not a strategy-selection paper, so cross-validation is less relevant than in the compute-optimal test-time scaling paper).

### Main Quantitative Results

#### GLUE Benchmark (Natural Language Understanding)

**Headline result:** VeRA achieves performance on par with LoRA on RoBERTa-large (87.8 average for both methods) while using 13× fewer trainable parameters (0.061M vs. 0.8M). On RoBERTa-base, VeRA trails LoRA by 1.4 points on the GLUE average (85.2 vs. 86.6) but uses 7× fewer parameters (0.043M vs. 0.3M).

**Detailed per-task comparison (Table 2):**

For **RoBERTa-base** (VeRA rank 1024, LoRA rank not explicitly stated but typical LoRA-base configurations use r=8 from Hu et al., 2022, resulting in ~0.3M parameters):
- SST-2: VeRA 94.6 vs. LoRA 95.1 (−0.5)
- MRPC: VeRA 89.5 vs. LoRA 89.7 (−0.2)
- CoLA: VeRA 65.6 vs. LoRA 63.4 (+2.2)
- QNLI: VeRA 91.8 vs. LoRA 93.3 (−1.5)
- RTE: VeRA 78.7 vs. LoRA 86.6 (−7.9)
- STS-B: VeRA 90.7 vs. LoRA 91.5 (−0.8)

The RTE gap is substantial (7.9 points) and represents the largest single-task underperformance. RTE (Recognizing Textual Entailment) is a relatively small dataset with 2.5K training examples, and the paper's later scaling experiment (Section 4.5, Figure 2) suggests this gap narrows when VeRA's parameter count is increased to match LoRA's. VeRA outperforms all adapter-based methods (AdptD at 0.3M and 0.9M parameters both score 83.0 and 84.2 average respectively) and BitFit (85.4), despite using fewer parameters than any of them.

For **RoBERTa-large** (VeRA rank 256):
- SST-2: VeRA 96.1 vs. LoRA 96.2 (−0.1)
- MRPC: VeRA 90.9 vs. LoRA 90.2 (+0.7)
- CoLA: VeRA 68.0 vs. LoRA 68.2 (−0.2)
- QNLI: VeRA 94.4 vs. LoRA 94.8 (−0.4)
- RTE: VeRA 85.9 vs. LoRA 85.2 (+0.7)
- STS-B: VeRA 91.7 vs. LoRA 92.3 (−0.6)

The average of 87.8 exactly matches LoRA, and VeRA outperforms LoRA on two of six tasks (MRPC and RTE). Against adapter methods, VeRA matches or exceeds all configurations: AdptP at 3M parameters scores 87.6, AdptP at 0.8M scores 86.8, AdptH at 6M scores 86.8, and AdptH at 0.8M scores 84.9. VeRA (0.061M) outperforms all of them while using 13–100× fewer parameters. LoRA-FA, which uses 3.7M parameters (60× more than VeRA), scores 87.7—slightly below both LoRA and VeRA.

**Key pattern:** The performance gap between VeRA and LoRA is larger on the smaller model (RoBERTa-base: −1.4 average) than on the larger model (RoBERTa-large: 0.0 average). This suggests that larger pretrained models may have weight update landscapes that are more amenable to random basis representation, or that the higher capacity of the larger model makes the adapter's precise form less critical—a finding consistent with the general trend that larger models are easier to finetune.

#### Scaling Behavior: Performance vs. Trainable Parameters

**Headline result:** On the RTE task with RoBERTa-large (Section 4.5, Figure 2), VeRA is significantly more parameter-efficient than LoRA: when VeRA uses a rank high enough to match LoRA's parameter count, it *outperforms* LoRA by 4 accuracy percentage points (approximately 88% vs. 84%).

**Detailed scaling comparison (Figure 2, inferred from text):**

The paper sweeps ranks r = {1, 4, 16, 64, 256, 1024} for VeRA and r = {1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64} for LoRA on the RTE task. The results, plotted as accuracy versus number of trainable parameters on a log-scale x-axis, reveal:

- **VeRA at r=1** uses approximately half the parameters of LoRA at r=1 (since VeRA's cost is L_tuned × (d_model + 1) versus LoRA's 2 × L_tuned × d_model × 1), yet achieves roughly comparable or slightly lower accuracy (exact numbers not reported in the text, only visible in Figure 2).
- **As rank increases**, LoRA's parameter count grows rapidly (2 × L_tuned × d_model per rank increment), while VeRA's grows slowly (L_tuned per rank increment). The LoRA curve moves substantially rightward on the parameter axis with each rank increase; the VeRA curve moves only slightly.
- **At the crossing point** where VeRA's rank is high enough that its parameter count approximately matches LoRA's parameter count at some lower rank, VeRA achieves approximately 88% accuracy versus LoRA's approximately 84%—a 4-point gap. This is the critical finding: at equal parameter budgets, the high-rank random basis with learned scaling provides better accuracy than the learned low-rank basis.
- **At the highest ranks tested**, VeRA (r=1024) approaches approximately 88–89% accuracy while using only 24.5K parameters (as reported in Table 1), while LoRA (r=64) achieves approximately 84% accuracy with roughly the same or more parameters.

**Interpretation:** This experiment is the strongest evidence that VeRA's random-basis approach is not just a cheaper approximation of LoRA but potentially a *better* parametrization at equal parameter counts. The random matrices at r=1024 provide 1024 directions to select from; the learned scaling vector d can amplify the useful ones and suppress the rest, effectively performing learned dimensionality reduction. LoRA at r=64 learns only 64 directions but stores them all, and the learned directions are constrained to be the same rank as the stored matrices. VeRA decouples the rank of the random pool (which can be very high, since no storage cost) from the effective rank of the adaptation (which is implicitly determined by the sparsity pattern of the learned scaling vectors).

**Caveat:** This experiment is on a single task (RTE) with a single model (RoBERTa-large). Whether the 4-point advantage generalizes to other tasks, models, and domains is not tested. The paper presents it as a proof-of-concept for VeRA's scaling properties rather than a universal claim of superiority.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

All ablation experiments in Section 4.6 use RoBERTa-large on the MRPC and RTE tasks, with 5 random seeds per configuration, reporting mean and standard deviation.

**Single scaling vector (d only vs. b only, Table 6a):** Omitting either scaling vector significantly degrades performance, confirming that both are necessary. The only-d configuration (omitting b, initializing d with zeros) achieves 89.7 ± 0.0 on MRPC and 67.0 ± 13.9 on RTE, versus the full VeRA's 90.5 ± 0.7 and 85.8 ± 0.7. The only-b configuration performs substantially worse: 81.6 ± 10.1 on MRPC and 64.3 ± 11.5 on RTE. The paper attributes the asymmetry to d's greater expressivity ("d modulates the rows of both low-rank matrices, thereby influencing a broader aspect of the final constructed matrix. In contrast, b only scales the rows of the final matrix resulting from the product of the low-rank matrices"). The extremely high standard deviations for both ablated configurations on RTE (±13.9 and ±11.5 respectively, versus ±0.7 for full VeRA) suggest training instability—without both scaling vectors, the optimization struggles to converge consistently across random seeds.

**Matrix initialization strategy (Table 6b):** Kaiming uniform and Kaiming normal initializations for the frozen matrices A and B both substantially outperform a naive uniform initialization. Kaiming uniform achieves 90.5 ± 0.7 on MRPC and 85.8 ± 0.7 on RTE; Kaiming normal achieves 90.0 ± 1.1 and 82.6 ± 5.2; Uniform[0, 0.1] achieves only 68.9 ± 1.3 and 53.1 ± 0.8. The dramatic degradation (37+ point drop on RTE) confirms that variance-preserving initialization is critical—without it, the random matrices produce arbitrarily scaled products that make gradient-based optimization through the scaling vectors difficult. The slightly better performance and lower variance of Kaiming uniform over Kaiming normal motivates its selection as the default across all main experiments.

**Scaling vector d initialization value (Table 6c):** The initialization constant for the d vector has a dramatic effect on performance, revealing sensitivity that practitioners must account for. At d_init = 10^(-1) (the default): MRPC 90.5 ± 0.7, RTE 85.8 ± 0.7. At d_init = 10^(-7): MRPC 90.8 ± 0.9, RTE 84.7 ± 0.9—roughly comparable to the default, with slightly higher MRPC and slightly lower RTE. At d_init = 1.0: MRPC drops to 70.3 ± 1.2, RTE drops to 60.3 ± 12.4—catastrophic degradation with extremely high variance on RTE. The paper interprets this as evidence that smaller initial values enable "more flexibility in the optimization process through early sign changes in selected rows of the frozen matrices." In practical terms: large initial d values produce large initial gradient signals that cause the optimizer to take aggressive steps, potentially overshooting and causing training instability. The fact that d_init = 10^(-1) and 10^(-7) both work well (despite being four orders of magnitude apart) suggests there is a threshold effect—values below ~1.0 avoid the instability, and the precise value within the stable regime matters less.

**Sharing random matrices across layers vs. unique per-layer matrices (Table 7):** This is the most theoretically significant ablation, testing whether the shared-matrices design (which enables the extreme storage savings) sacrifices performance. On four GLUE tasks with RoBERTa-large:
- MRPC: Shared 90.0 ± 0.9 vs. Unique 90.7 ± 0.3 (unique leads by 0.7)
- RTE: Shared 84.6 ± 1.5 vs. Unique 84.6 ± 0.8 (identical means; shared has higher variance)
- CoLA: Shared 67.7 ± 0.8 vs. Unique 68.3 ± 1.8 (unique leads by 0.6)
- STS-B: Shared 91.5 ± 0.6 vs. Unique 91.5 ± 0.2 (identical means; shared has higher variance)

The performance difference between shared and unique matrices is at most 0.7 points (MRPC), with two tasks showing identical mean performance. The standard deviations are generally tighter for unique matrices, which is expected since they provide more degrees of freedom. The key implication: the shared-matrices design, which enables the 10×+ storage reduction, costs at most 0.7 accuracy points on these tasks and often costs nothing. This ablation is what makes VeRA's storage claims credible—if unique matrices were necessary for good performance, the method's advantage over LoRA would largely evaporate.

**Magnitude of adaptation across layers (Figure 3):** This is not an ablation but a diagnostic analysis. The paper plots the L2 norm of the trained d vectors for query and value projection matrices across the 24 layers of RoBERTa-large after finetuning on RTE. The visualization reveals: (1) query matrices consistently show larger ||d||_2 than value matrices (5–12 vs. 4–9 range), (2) later layers (layers 15–24) show substantially larger adaptation magnitudes than early layers (layers 1–8), with a smooth increasing trend, and (3) the pattern is consistent rather than noisy, suggesting the learned scaling vectors capture structured layer-wise adaptation patterns despite the shared random basis. This matches prior findings from fully-learned PEFT methods (Zhang et al., 2023b; Liu et al., 2021) that later layers require more adaptation than early layers—VeRA reproduces this phenomenon through learned gating rather than learned basis matrices.

**Negative result: ReST^EM-style optimization not tested; training stability notes.** The paper does not report negative results for VeRA in the sense of failed experiments (unlike the compute-optimal test-time paper which documents ReST^EM degradation). However, several observations in the paper flag instability risks: (1) the extreme standard deviations for ablation configurations on RTE (Table 6a: ±11.5 to ±13.9 for single-vector variants) indicate that VeRA's training can become unstable when improperly configured; (2) the sensitivity to d_init (Table 6c) means a poor initialization choice can cause catastrophic performance collapse (60.3% vs. 85.8% on RTE); (3) the paper's acknowledgment that they did not perform full hyperparameter grid searches due to compute constraints means the reported VeRA results may be suboptimal relative to what could be achieved with more extensive tuning, which could either narrow or widen the gap to LoRA depending on whether VeRA or LoRA benefits more from tuning.

### Critical Assessment

#### Claim 1: VeRA matches LoRA's performance with 10× fewer parameters on GLUE for RoBERTa-large.

**What the experiments demonstrate:** Table 2 shows that VeRA (0.061M parameters) and LoRA (0.8M parameters) both achieve 87.8 average on the six-task GLUE subset for RoBERTa-large. This is an exact match on the aggregate metric. However, the per-task breakdown reveals variance: VeRA leads on MRPC (+0.7) and RTE (+0.7), trails on STS-B (−0.6), QNLI (−0.4), SST-2 (−0.2), and CoLA (−0.2). The aggregate match is therefore a result of small positive and negative deviations that cancel out, not uniform equivalence across all tasks.

**What was not tested:** The paper omits MNLI and QQP—two of the largest GLUE tasks—from the evaluation. The reported GLUE average is over 6 tasks rather than the standard 8, making direct comparison to prior GLUE results problematic. If VeRA were systematically weaker on larger datasets (which require more adaptation capacity), the omission of MNLI (393K training examples) and QQP (364K) could mask a meaningful performance gap. The paper attributes this omission to "time constraints and budget limitations" (Section 4.1), which is understandable but means the "matches LoRA" claim is demonstrated on a subset skewed toward smaller datasets (RTE has 2.5K, MRPC has 3.7K, CoLA has 8.5K, STS-B has 5.7K examples).

**Statistical rigor:** The paper reports medians of 5 runs for GLUE, which is appropriate. However, no confidence intervals or significance tests are reported for the difference between VeRA and LoRA averages. With only 5 runs per configuration and 6 tasks, it is unclear whether the 87.8 vs. 87.8 tie is robust or a consequence of limited sampling. A difference of 0.0 on the average with per-task differences of up to 0.7 suggests the overall match is genuine but the claim of "the same performance" (abstract) overstates what can be concluded from 5 runs on 6 tasks.

#### Claim 2: VeRA achieves a 10× reduction in parameters on image classification tasks and a 3× reduction on E2E.

**What the experiments demonstrate:** Table 5 shows VeRA using 24.6K vs. LoRA's 294.9K for ViT-Base (12× reduction) and 61.4K vs. 786.4K for ViT-Large (12.8× reduction). The parameter reduction is unambiguous. However, the performance is not "the same"—on ViT-Base, VeRA trails LoRA by 0.7–1.1 points on three of four datasets (Table 5), which is a meaningful gap in few-shot image classification where every percentage point matters. The abstract's claim of "yielding similar or better results" masks that LoRA leads on 3 of 4 ViT-Base datasets while VeRA leads on 3 of 4 ViT-Large datasets.

For E2E (Table 3), VeRA actually *outperforms* LoRA on BLEU for both GPT-2 Medium (70.1 vs. 68.9) and GPT-2 Large (70.3 vs. 70.1) while using 3.5× and 4.5× fewer parameters respectively. This is a stronger result than "matching"—VeRA is better on the primary metric with fewer parameters. However, only a single training run is reported for E2E (no mention of multiple seeds or standard deviations), so the statistical reliability of the BLEU improvements is unknown.

**What was not tested:** The paper does not explore whether the rank choice for VeRA (1024 for E2E, 256 for ViT) was optimized versus alternative ranks, nor whether LoRA's rank (8 for ViT, not reported for E2E but likely 4–16 based on Hu et al., 2022) was optimal for the parameter budget. The claim of "X× reduction" compares VeRA at its chosen rank against LoRA at the rank used in prior work, but a fairer comparison might match parameter counts and compare accuracy, or match accuracy and compare parameter counts. The scaling experiment in Section 4.5 does this for RTE and finds VeRA superior, but this analysis is not replicated for E2E or image classification.

#### Claim 3: VeRA matches LoRA on instruction-tuning with ~100× fewer parameters.

**What the experiments demonstrate:** Table 4 shows VeRA trailing LoRA by 0.11–0.26 MT-Bench points on three of four comparisons, and leading by 0.16 on Llama2 13B. These are remarkably small gaps given the 99× parameter reduction. The finding that VeRA *outperforms* LoRA on Llama2 13B (5.93 vs. 5.77) is notable and suggests the random-basis approach may scale favorably with model capability.

**Critical weaknesses:** (1) Only one training run is reported per configuration—with a single epoch of training and an 80-question evaluation set, the MT-Bench scores could easily vary by 0.2–0.5 points across random seeds or data orderings. The reported gaps (−0.26, −0.09, −0.11, +0.16) are all within a plausible noise range. (2) The paper does not report training curves or validation loss, making it impossible to assess whether VeRA and LoRA converged at different rates or to different optima given the fixed one-epoch budget. (3) The evaluation uses GPT-4 as a judge (MT-Bench), which introduces its own variance and potential biases—the paper does not report inter-judge agreement or multiple evaluation passes. (4) VeRA uses rank 1024 versus LoRA's rank 64—a 16× difference in the bottleneck dimension. The comparison is between methods at their individually tuned configurations, not at equal rank, making it impossible to isolate whether the performance comes from the random-basis design or simply from using a much higher-dimensional bottleneck.

**Missing experiment:** A comparison of VeRA and LoRA at equal parameter counts on instruction-tuning (e.g., LoRA at r=1 or r=2 to match VeRA's ~2M parameters) would test whether the random-basis approach provides benefits beyond just parameter reduction. The paper only compares VeRA at r=1024, ~2M parameters against LoRA at r=64, ~250M parameters, which confounds the method with the parameter budget.

#### Claim 4: VeRA introduces no additional inference time cost.

**What the experiments demonstrate:** This claim is not experimentally validated in the paper—it is a property of the architecture that follows from the merge-at-inference design. The paper does not benchmark inference latency, throughput, or memory usage for merged VeRA models versus merged LoRA models. The claim is logically sound (both produce a single modified weight matrix post-training) but is accepted on architectural grounds, not empirical measurement.

**What would strengthen it:** Inference benchmarks on a standard hardware setup comparing: (a) original pretrained model, (b) LoRA-merged model, and (c) VeRA-merged model, measuring latency, throughput, and memory usage. Since both LoRA and VeRA should be identical to the pretrained model after merging (same matrix dimensions, same operations), this would confirm that no hidden overhead exists (e.g., from numerical precision effects in the merge computation).

#### Overall Strengths of the Experimental Design

- **Multi-domain evaluation:** The paper tests VeRA across language understanding (GLUE), language generation (E2E), instruction-following (MT-Bench), and image classification (four datasets), spanning encoder-only, decoder-only, and vision transformer architectures at scales from 125M to 13B parameters. This breadth is unusual for a methods paper and substantially strengthens the generalizability claims.

- **Transparent hyperparameter reporting:** Appendix A provides detailed hyperparameter tables for all experiments, including learning rates, batch sizes, epoch counts, rank values, and optimization settings. This enables reproduction and fair comparison.

- **Well-chosen ablations:** The ablation study (Section 4.6) isolates the four critical design choices—two scaling vectors, matrix initialization, vector initialization, and shared vs. unique matrices—and demonstrates that each matters for performance. The inclusion of standard deviations across 5 seeds for ablations is good practice.

- **Scaling analysis (Figure 2):** The parameter-sweep experiment on RTE is the most informative result in the paper, directly comparing VeRA and LoRA at matched parameter counts and showing VeRA's superior parameter efficiency. This is the experiment that best isolates the method's contribution from the parameter budget.

#### Overall Weaknesses of the Experimental Design

- **Single-run evaluation for key claims:** The instruction-tuning results (Table 4), E2E results (Table 3), and image classification results (Table 5) do not report multiple seeds, standard deviations, or confidence intervals. The MT-Bench evaluation is particularly concerning since GPT-4 judging is known to have non-trivial variance, and the reported gaps (0.1–0.3 points) are small relative to plausible evaluation noise.

- **Incomplete GLUE benchmark:** Omitting MNLI and QQP removes the two tasks with the most training data, potentially biasing the comparison toward tasks where both methods have sufficient capacity and differences are harder to detect. A method that sacrifices adaptation capacity for parameter efficiency might show larger gaps on data-rich tasks where more capacity could be exploited.

- **No equal-parameter comparison on most benchmarks:** Except for the RTE scaling experiment, all comparisons are VeRA at its chosen (high) rank versus LoRA at the rank used in prior work. This answers "can VeRA match LoRA's published numbers with fewer parameters?" but not "is VeRA a more parameter-efficient parametrization at equal parameter budgets?" The RTE scaling result (Figure 2) suggests the answer to the second question might be yes, but this is not tested on other tasks.

- **Fixed LoRA rank comparisons:** For instruction-tuning, LoRA uses r=64 (from Dettmers et al., 2023). For ViT, LoRA uses r=8. For GLUE-base, LoRA uses approximately r=8. These are standard choices from prior work, but they may not be optimal for the specific models and datasets used. If LoRA were given the same hyperparameter tuning budget as VeRA, the gaps might change.

- **No wall-clock or memory benchmarking for inference:** The paper claims zero inference overhead but provides no empirical validation. Appendix C reports training time and memory (Table 12) showing VeRA's 7.4% GPU memory reduction and 1.8% training time increase versus LoRA, but inference is not measured.

- **Limited exploration of the rank parameter's effect:** The paper uses very different VeRA ranks across experiments (1024 for RoBERTa-base and GPT-2, 256 for RoBERTa-large and ViT) without a systematic study of how rank affects performance. The scaling experiment in Section 4.5 suggests higher ranks help VeRA, but the optimal rank seems to be chosen heuristically rather than through a principled sweep.

#### Conditional Nature of the Claims

The paper's claims hold under specific conditions that should be made explicit:

- **"Matches LoRA" holds when VeRA is given a substantially higher rank than LoRA.** VeRA uses r=1024 while LoRA uses r≈8 on RoBERTa-base, and r=256 vs. r≈16 on RoBERTa-large. The match is achieved by compensating for random (rather than learned) basis directions with many more of them—a design tradeoff that VeRA can afford because high rank doesn't increase storage cost.

- **"10× fewer parameters" compares VeRA at high rank against LoRA at low rank.** A fairer statement would be: "VeRA achieves comparable performance to LoRA using X× fewer stored parameters, enabled by substituting learned low-rank matrices with high-rank frozen random matrices gated by learned scaling vectors." The reduction factor depends on the specific ranks chosen for both methods.

- **The instruction-tuning claims are provisional.** With single training runs, unknown evaluation variance, and confounded rank and parameter budget, the 100× reduction figure should be treated as an existence proof (it's possible to get close with this few parameters) rather than a precise quantification of the performance gap.

- **The method has not been tested on tasks requiring large amounts of new knowledge.** All benchmarks use datasets where the pretrained model likely already possesses substantial relevant knowledge (GLUE tasks, E2E restaurant recommendations, Alpaca instructions, ImageNet-pretrained vision). A task requiring substantial new factual knowledge might stress the adapter's capacity in ways these benchmarks do not.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Cost and Feasibility of Deploying VeRA With Shared Random Matrices at Scale

**The assumption:** VeRA's central efficiency claim rests on the ability to regenerate the frozen random matrices A and B from a random number generator (RNG) seed at load time rather than storing them. The paper states in Section 3.2: "Because the random frozen matrices can be regenerated from a random number generator (RNG) seed, these do not need to be stored in memory. This substantially reduces the memory requirement, which is now limited to the bytes needed for the trained b and d vectors and a single RNG seed."

**The consequence:** This storage model requires that the matrices be regenerated deterministically whenever the adapter is loaded—a computation that scales as O(m × r + r × n) per adapted layer. For large models with many adapted layers, this regeneration step could become a significant latency bottleneck at deployment time, particularly when adapters must be swapped frequently (the very use case the paper motivates: "cloud-based AI services personalized for individual users" requiring "frequent swapping of numerous finetuned models," Section 5). The paper reports in Appendix C (Table 12) that training-time forward passes are 1.8% slower for VeRA than LoRA at equal rank, but this measurement includes the frozen matrices already in GPU memory. A deployment scenario where adapters are loaded from disk on demand would require either (a) regenerating the matrices on-the-fly (incurring a one-time compute cost per adapter swap), (b) caching regenerated matrices in memory (negating the storage advantage during active use), or (c) pre-computing and storing the merged weights (eliminating the need to swap adapters rapidly but requiring storage proportional to the number of active adapters). None of these scenarios are benchmarked or discussed.

**What evidence exists:** None. The paper provides no measurements of adapter loading time, matrix regeneration throughput, or end-to-end swap latency for a multi-user deployment scenario. The training-time forward pass comparison in Table 12 (578 vs. 568 minutes, 1.8% increase) measures a fundamentally different operation (cached in-memory matrix multiplication vs. generation from RNG seed) and does not capture the regeneration overhead. The storage figures in Table 1 count only the bytes for the trained vectors and assume the matrices are "free" from a storage perspective, but the compute cost of materializing them from the seed is entirely unaccounted for in any efficiency metric.

**Mitigation status:** Not addressed. The paper treats the "regenerate from seed" property as an unqualified advantage without analyzing the time-memory tradeoff it introduces. Future work on practical deployment would need to benchmark the regeneration cost across model scales and determine whether it dominates the latency budget in adapter-swapping scenarios, potentially requiring caching strategies that reduce the effective storage savings.

### The High VeRA Rank Required to Match LoRA Creates a Silent Inference Compute Penalty

**The assumption:** The paper compares VeRA at ranks r = 1024 (RoBERTa-base, GPT-2) or r = 256 (RoBERTa-large, ViT) against LoRA at much lower ranks (typically r = 8 or r = 16 for RoBERTa, r = 64 for Llama). The performance match is achieved by compensating for random (rather than learned) basis directions with many more of them—a design tradeoff enabled by VeRA's decoupling of rank from storage cost. The paper presents this as purely advantageous, since "increasing r leads to a linear increase in the number of trainable parameters via d" (Section 3.1) without increasing storage proportionally.

**The consequence:** While the *stored* parameter count remains low at high ranks, the *merge-at-inference* step requires computing the product Λ_b B Λ_d A, which involves a matrix multiplication chain whose cost is proportional to r. Specifically, the adapter contribution ΔW = B (Λ_d A) involves multiplying B ∈ ℝ^{m×r} by the intermediate result (Λ_d A) ∈ ℝ^{r×n}, requiring O(m × r × n) operations. For VeRA at r = 1024 versus LoRA at r = 16 applied to the same weight matrix, this merge-time computation is 1024/16 = 64× more expensive in FLOPs. Since the merge is performed once per adapter installation (not per inference forward pass), this is a one-time cost rather than a recurring inference cost, but it could become significant when adapters are installed and uninstalled frequently.

More importantly, the effective rank of the merged weight update W' = W_0 + Λ_b B Λ_d A is not constrained to be low-rank in the way LoRA's update is—the product of random full-rank matrices with diagonal scaling can produce a weight update that is dense and high-rank, potentially interacting differently with model quantization, pruning, or other compression techniques that exploit LoRA's known low-rank structure. The paper does not investigate whether VeRA-merged models maintain compatibility with standard post-training compression pipelines that have been validated for LoRA-merged models.

**What evidence exists:** The paper acknowledges the computational difference implicitly in Section 3.2: "In this setup, B ∈ ℝ^{m×r} and A ∈ ℝ^{r×n} are not required to be low-rank. This is because they remain static and we do not need to store their values." But it does not explore the downstream consequences of producing non-low-rank weight updates. Appendix C mentions "VeRA includes more operations than LoRA because of the additional vector multiplies in the forward pass" and reports a 1.8% training time increase, but this measures the forward pass cost during training—not the merge-time cost, which involves a full matrix-matrix multiply across the frozen matrices that is not part of the training forward pass.

**Mitigation status:** Not addressed. The paper does not benchmark merge time, measure the rank of the resulting weight updates, or test compatibility with compression pipelines. The claim of "no additional inference time cost" (Section 1) is technically correct for deployed inference after merging, but the cost of *achieving* that merged state is higher than for LoRA and is not accounted for in any reported metric.

### VeRA's Performance Advantage Shrinks or Vanishes on Smaller Models and Data-Scarce Tasks

**The assumption:** VeRA's central claim is that it matches LoRA's performance with far fewer parameters. The experimental results, however, reveal a consistent pattern: the performance gap between VeRA and LoRA is larger (favoring LoRA) on smaller models and on tasks with less training data, and narrows or reverses only on larger models.

**The consequence:** For practitioners deploying on smaller models or working with limited task-specific data, VeRA's parameter savings may come at a meaningful performance cost that the paper's aggregate numbers obscure. On RoBERTa-base, VeRA trails LoRA by 1.4 points on the GLUE average (85.2 vs. 86.6, Table 2), with a 7.9-point gap on RTE specifically. On ViT-Base, VeRA trails LoRA by 0.7–1.1 points on three of four datasets (Table 5: CIFAR100 84.8 vs. 85.9, Food101 89.0 vs. 89.9, RESISC45 77.0 vs. 77.7). The RTE gap is particularly concerning because RTE is one of the smaller GLUE datasets (2.5K training examples)—the very regime where parameter-efficient methods are most attractive, since full finetuning would overfit. A practitioner choosing between LoRA and VeRA for a small-dataset adaptation task on a moderate-scale model would face a nontrivial accuracy tradeoff that the paper's abstract ("maintains the same performance") does not surface.

On larger models, this gap largely disappears or reverses: RoBERTa-large shows identical averages (87.8 vs. 87.8, Table 2), ViT-Large shows VeRA leading on three of four datasets (Table 5), and Llama2 13B shows VeRA outperforming LoRA on instruction-tuning (5.93 vs. 5.77, Table 4). This suggests VeRA's random-basis approach requires a sufficiently high-dimensional model space and/or sufficient training data to be effective—when the model's representational capacity or the training signal is limited, the learned basis directions of LoRA provide information that random directions with learned gating cannot fully recover.

**What evidence exists:** The paper's own results demonstrate this pattern but do not discuss it. The GLUE results (Table 2) show it clearly: the base-vs-large comparison. The image classification results (Table 5) replicate it: ViT-Base VeRA trails, ViT-Large VeRA leads. The scaling experiment in Figure 2 shows that VeRA can match or exceed LoRA at sufficiently high parameter counts (using high ranks), but this experiment is on RoBERTa-large (not base) and RTE (where VeRA at r=1024 can compensate).

**Mitigation status:** The paper does not acknowledge this model-size dependence as a limitation. The finding is present in the data but not discussed in the text—the abstract and conclusion present the "matches LoRA" claim without caveating that this holds primarily for the larger model variants tested. Section 5 mentions "the applicability of the method across different architectures and domains remains an area for future research" but does not address the within-architecture size dependence already visible in their own results.

### The Method Introduces Two Sensitive Hyperparameters (d_init and Rank) Without Clear Tuning Guidance

**The assumption:** VeRA introduces the scaling vector initialization value d_init as a new hyperparameter with no analog in LoRA, and the effective rank r plays a different role than in LoRA (since high r is nearly free in storage but changes the random basis dimension and the size of the d vector). The paper chooses these values heuristically: d_init = 0.1 for most experiments, r = 1024 for RoBERTa-base/GPT-2, r = 256 for RoBERTa-large/ViT, with no systematic sweep or principled selection criterion.

**The consequence:** The ablation study (Table 6c, Section 4.6) reveals that d_init has a catastrophic effect on performance when chosen poorly: on RTE with RoBERTa-large, d_init = 1.0 drops accuracy from 85.8% to 60.3%—a 25.5-point collapse—with a standard deviation of ±12.4 indicating severe training instability. On MRPC, the drop is from 90.5% to 70.3%. This extreme sensitivity means that practitioners adopting VeRA must either (a) discover the stable regime through trial-and-error hyperparameter tuning (which the paper acknowledges it could not do comprehensively due to compute constraints), or (b) blindly use the paper's default values and hope they transfer to new models and tasks. The paper provides no guidance on how d_init should scale with model size, task difficulty, dataset size, or learning rate—it simply reports that 10^(-1) and 10^(-7) both work while 1.0 fails catastrophically. This is a large unstable region with a narrow window of viable values, and the boundary between "works well" and "complete failure" is not characterized.

The rank selection is similarly opaque. VeRA uses r = 1024 for RoBERTa-base and GPT-2 but r = 256 for RoBERTa-large—the scaling experiment in Figure 2 shows that higher rank generally improves performance for VeRA (the curve is monotonically increasing with rank), but the paper does not explain why it chose 256 rather than 512 or 1024 for the large model experiments, nor whether these choices were optimized. A practitioner tuning VeRA for a new model has no principled way to select r other than "as high as you can afford in compute" (since storage is nearly free), but the inference merge-time cost noted above complicates this.

**What evidence exists:** Table 6c provides direct evidence of d_init sensitivity. The extreme standard deviations on RTE for d_init = 1.0 (60.3 ± 12.4) suggest not just poor average performance but fundamentally unstable optimization that fails to converge consistently across random seeds. The paper's own discussion acknowledges the sensitivity: "the choice of d_init significantly influences the method's performance" (Section 4.6). The hyperparameter tables in Appendix A show that learning rates, rank, and d_init were selected without comprehensive sweeps ("due to our academic compute we were not able to run full grid searches on any hyperparameters," Appendix A).

**Mitigation status:** The paper does not propose a method for selecting d_init or rank automatically, nor does it provide a characterization of the stable region for d_init across model scales. The sensitivity is documented but not resolved—the paper simply reports that their chosen values work and notes that future work could explore "different initialization and regularization techniques" (Section 5). For a method whose primary selling point is practical deployability (storage reduction for many-user scenarios), this sensitivity represents a significant practical barrier: the cost of hyperparameter tuning to find the stable regime could offset the storage savings in many deployment contexts.

### No Evidence That VeRA Works for Tasks Requiring Substantial New Knowledge Acquisition

**The assumption:** All benchmarks evaluated in the paper involve tasks for which the pretrained model likely already possesses substantial relevant capabilities. GLUE tasks test natural language understanding on standard linguistic phenomena; E2E tests restaurant-domain text generation from structured meaning representations; Alpaca instruction-tuning teaches the model to follow a specific interaction format; and image classification on CIFAR100/Flowers102/Food101/RESISC45 uses ImageNet-21k-pretrained Vision Transformers where the visual features are largely transferable. The paper does not evaluate VeRA on tasks that require the model to acquire fundamentally new knowledge, learn new factual associations, or adapt to a substantially different data distribution from pretraining.

**The consequence:** The random-basis approach may fundamentally cap the adapter's capacity to represent weight updates that encode novel information not already present in the pretrained model's feature space. The frozen random matrices A and B define a fixed set of directions in the activation space; the scaling vectors can select and weight these directions but cannot create new directions that are not in the span of the random matrices. If adapting to a new domain requires weight updates that lie largely outside the subspace spanned by the random matrices (even at high r), VeRA's performance could degrade relative to LoRA, which can learn the necessary basis directions through gradient descent on A and B. The intrinsic dimensionality argument (Aghajanyan et al., 2021) suggests that for typical finetuning tasks, the needed update subspace is low-dimensional—but "low-dimensional" does not imply "spanned by random vectors" for tasks that are genuinely out-of-distribution relative to pretraining. A random subspace of dimension r = 1024 may still fail to capture the specific directions needed if those directions are highly task-specific, whereas LoRA's learned matrices can adapt to find exactly the right subspace.

**What evidence exists:** None. The paper provides no experiments on tasks involving new factual knowledge, domain shift, or capabilities not already present in the pretrained model. The closest proxy is the RTE task, where VeRA shows the largest gap to LoRA on RoBERTa-base (78.7 vs. 86.6, a 7.9-point deficit, Table 2)—RTE requires recognizing textual entailment, which involves logical reasoning that may not be as thoroughly covered in pretraining as sentiment analysis (SST-2) or paraphrase detection (MRPC). However, this is speculative; the paper does not analyze the types of errors VeRA makes or whether they cluster on examples requiring novel reasoning versus examples within the pretraining distribution.

**Mitigation status:** The paper acknowledges in Section 5 that "the applicability of the method across different architectures and domains remains an area for future research," but does not specifically flag the new-knowledge limitation. The experimental design does not include any probe for this failure mode (e.g., finetuning on a dataset with deliberately held-out factual knowledge, or evaluating on domain-shifted test sets). A practitioner considering VeRA for a task that genuinely extends beyond the pretrained model's knowledge—legal document processing for a model pretrained on general web text, medical coding, low-resource language adaptation—has no evidence to predict whether the random-basis approach will hold up or degrade substantially relative to LoRA.

### The Storage Advantage Partially Evaporates for Very High-Rank Deployments With Many Adapted Layers

**The assumption:** The paper's storage calculations in Table 1 compare VeRA and LoRA at specific rank configurations chosen for each experiment. The headline 10× reduction for RoBERTa-large on GLUE (0.061M vs. 0.8M) uses VeRA at r = 256 and LoRA at approximately r = 16. The paper emphasizes that VeRA's parameter count scales as L_tuned × (d_model + r), which is dominated by d_model when d_model is large.

**The consequence:** The term L_tuned × d_model in VeRA's parameter count—the b vector—is independent of rank and is therefore irreducible. For models with very large d_model and many adapted layers, this irreducible term can dominate, making the relative advantage over LoRA smaller than the paper's headline numbers suggest. The b vector costs one parameter per output dimension per adapted layer—for a model with d_model = 12288 (GPT-3 size) and L_tuned = 192 (96 layers × 2 projection types), b alone accounts for 192 × 12288 ≈ 2.4M parameters, regardless of the chosen rank r. When VeRA uses a high rank like r = 1024, the d vector adds another 192 × 1024 ≈ 0.2M parameters, for a total of ~2.6M. LoRA at r = 1 for the same model uses 2 × 192 × 12288 × 1 ≈ 4.7M parameters—only about 1.8× more than VeRA, not the 10–100× figures highlighted in the abstract. The dramatic reductions appear primarily when comparing high-rank VeRA against moderate-rank LoRA (e.g., VeRA r=1024 vs. LoRA r=16), not when comparing against the lowest-rank LoRA configurations that are closest in parameter budget.

Furthermore, the paper applies VeRA to more layers in some experiments (all linear layers for instruction-tuning) than in others (only query and value for GLUE), which changes the total parameter count substantially. The instruction-tuning experiments use L_tuned covering all linear layers—a much larger number than GLUE's query+value-only application—and this makes the irreducible L_tuned × d_model term proportionally larger. The paper does not provide a systematic breakdown of how the storage advantage scales with the number of adapted layers or with d_model.

**What evidence exists:** Table 1 provides the data: for RoBERTa-base, VeRA at r = 1 uses 18.4K parameters vs. LoRA's 36.8K—a 2× reduction, not 10×. At r = 256, VeRA uses 24.5K vs. LoRA's 9,437.1K—now a 385× reduction, but this is almost entirely because LoRA's parameter count exploded with rank while VeRA's barely moved. The comparison at r = 1 is the fairest equal-rank comparison and shows a much more modest advantage. For GPT-3, VeRA at r = 1 uses 2.4M vs. LoRA's 4.7M—again a 2× reduction. The paper's abstract and introduction emphasize the 10× and 100× figures without clarifying that these are achieved by combining VeRA's parameter efficiency with VeRA's ability to use very high ranks—the high rank is doing much of the work in creating a large numerical reduction factor by inflating LoRA's parameter count in the comparison.

**Mitigation status:** The paper provides the full Table 1 with numbers at multiple ranks, so a careful reader can extract the equal-rank comparisons. However, the text (Section 3.2) emphasizes the high-rank advantage: "Specifically, for the lowest rank (i.e., r = 1), VeRA requires approximately half the trainable parameters of LoRA. Moreover, as the rank increases, VeRA's parameter count increases by L_tuned for each increment, a substantial saving compared to LoRA's 2 L_tuned d_model." The framing is accurate but incomplete—it does not discuss the irreducible b-vector term or how the relative advantage depends on the ratio of r to d_model. A practitioner evaluating whether VeRA is worth adopting for their specific model and adaptation pattern would need to compute the numbers for their own setting, but the paper does not provide a formula or guideline for predicting the storage reduction as a function of model architecture and adaptation scope.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

VeRA introduces a **conceptual reframing** of what parameter-efficient finetuning actually requires. Since LoRA (Hu et al., 2022), the dominant assumption has been that the adaptation subspace—the low-rank matrices A and B—must themselves be *learned* via gradient descent. This assumption directly couples the adapter's expressivity to its storage footprint: a rank-16 adapter for GPT-3 needs 75.5M stored parameters because every entry of A and B must be persisted. VeRA challenges this assumption at its root by demonstrating that the adaptation subspace can be *random and fixed*, with learned adaptation emerging entirely from per-layer gating coefficients (the scaling vectors d and b) that select and weight which random directions to use.

This is not an incremental optimization on LoRA. It is a **fundamentally different division of labor between what is learned and what is stored**. In LoRA, the learned information is distributed across all entries of A and B—the basis directions themselves are discovered through optimization. In VeRA, the basis directions are intentionally random and shared, and the learned information is concentrated in the much smaller scaling vectors that gate access to those directions. The intellectual move is from "learn the right low-dimensional subspace" to "learn which combination of pre-existing random directions works"—a shift from representation learning to feature selection within a random projection space.

The practical consequence is that **rank and storage cost are decoupled**. In LoRA, increasing rank from 16 to 256 produces a 16× increase in stored parameters (75.5M → 1207.9M for GPT-3, Table 1). In VeRA, the same rank increase adds only ~6K parameters total (from 2.8M to 8.7M for GPT-3). This means VeRA can operate at ranks that would be absurdly expensive in LoRA (r=1024), providing a vastly richer pool of random directions to select from at negligible additional storage cost. The scaling experiment in Figure 2 demonstrates the consequence concretely: at equal parameter counts, VeRA outperforms LoRA by 4 accuracy points on RTE, suggesting the high-rank random basis with learned gating is a strictly more parameter-efficient representation than the learned low-rank basis.

**What this reframing enables:** The field can now explore adaptation methods where the expressive capacity (the size of the random basis) is split from the storage requirement (the learned gating coefficients). This opens a design space that LoRA's coupling of rank and storage made inaccessible: ultra-high-dimensional random projections combined with sparse learned selection, dynamic rank allocation through scaling vector sparsity rather than matrix pruning, and adaptation regimes where the random basis is regenerated on-the-fly rather than stored. The finding that sharing the same random matrices across all layers costs at most 0.7 accuracy points on GLUE (Table 7) further suggests that per-layer adaptation information is largely encoded in *coefficients* rather than *basis directions*—a finding with implications for understanding how finetuning modifies deep networks.

**Reconciling contradictions in the literature:** The paper bridges two previously separate literatures: the parameter-efficient finetuning community (which focused on learning compact adapter representations) and the random-models community (which demonstrated that frozen random networks can perform surprisingly well when select parts are trained). VeRA shows these are not contradictory—random structure can serve as the *basis* for adaptation, while learned structure (the scaling vectors) determines *which basis elements are used*. This resolves the apparent tension between "finetuning requires discovering task-specific weight update directions" (the PEFT perspective) and "random weights already contain useful subnetworks" (the lottery ticket perspective).

**Research directions that become more attractive:**
- **Verifier and search-based methods for adaptation:** The compute-optimal test-time scaling paper showed that verifier quality is the primary bottleneck for inference-time compute scaling. VeRA's decoupling of basis quality from storage suggests that verifier-guided adaptation—where a PRM or ORM scores candidate adaptations rather than candidate outputs—could explore very large random adaptation spaces at negligible storage cost, using the scaling vectors to capture which random directions the verifier prefers.
- **Sparse and structured gating beyond diagonal scaling:** VeRA uses simple element-wise scaling (diagonal matrices Λ_d and Λ_b). More sophisticated gating mechanisms—low-rank gating, attention over random directions, or learned sparse combinations—could extract more adaptation capacity from the same random basis at marginally higher storage cost, potentially closing the remaining gap to LoRA on smaller models.
- **Random basis generation strategies:** The paper uses Kaiming initialization for A and B, finding that uniform initialization in [0, 0.1] fails catastrophically (Table 6b: 53.1% vs. 85.8% on RTE). But there is no exploration of whether *which* random basis matters—could structured random matrices (e.g., those with block-sparse, circulant, or Fourier-based structure) provide better adaptation subspaces than unstructured Gaussian matrices? This becomes a new design axis entirely absent from LoRA's learned-matrix paradigm.

**Research directions that become less urgent:**
- **Further incremental reductions in LoRA's stored parameter count through matrix factorization tricks.** VeRA's ~100× reduction on instruction-tuning (1.6M vs. 159.9M parameters, Table 4) makes 2× or 3× improvements on LoRA's storage efficiency less practically impactful. The field's attention should shift to the gating mechanism's expressivity and the quality of the random basis, not to marginal compression of learned low-rank matrices.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Characterizing the stable region for d_init across model scales and architectures.** The ablation in Table 6c reveals a catastrophic failure mode: d_init = 1.0 drops RTE accuracy from 85.8% to 60.3% (±12.4 standard deviation) on RoBERTa-large. Yet d_init = 10^(-1) and 10^(-7) both work well, suggesting a threshold effect rather than a smooth sensitivity curve. A systematic study sweeping d_init across values from 10^(-8) to 10^0 on 3–4 model scales (e.g., RoBERTa-base/large, Llama 7B/13B) would map the stable region and determine whether the threshold scales with model width, depth, or learning rate. The critical measurement: at what d_init does training instability (high variance across seeds) emerge, and does this boundary shift with model size? The paper's existing hyperparameter choices (d_init = 0.1 for GLUE, presumably the same for other experiments) provide a single point; characterizing the basin would make VeRA practically adoptable without per-task tuning.

**VeRA on tasks requiring novel factual knowledge or domain shift.** The paper evaluates exclusively on benchmarks where the pretrained model likely possesses substantial relevant capabilities (GLUE linguistic phenomena, E2E restaurant recommendations, ImageNet-pretrained vision). A critical stress test: finetune VeRA and LoRA on a dataset that deliberately requires new factual associations—for example, a temporally-shifted version of a QA dataset where answers changed after the pretraining cutoff date, or a domain-specific corpus (legal, medical) with vocabulary and factual content absent from pretraining. The hypothesis: VeRA's random basis may be sufficient for *reorganizing* existing knowledge (which is what GLUE and instruction-tuning primarily require) but insufficient for *encoding* new knowledge that lies far from the pretrained feature space. Measuring the VeRA-LoRA performance gap as a function of the pretraining-to-finetuning distribution distance would define VeRA's applicability boundary—a finding with direct practical implications for deployment decisions.

**Does the random basis quality matter? Comparing structured vs. unstructured random matrices.** The paper uses Kaiming-initialized Gaussian or uniform matrices and finds that uniform initialization in [0, 0.1] fails catastrophically (Table 6b). But this only tests one axis of the initialization space. A systematic comparison of random matrix families—orthogonal (Haar-distributed), sparse (randomly zeroed entries), low-discrepancy (Sobol sequences), structured (Fastfood, circulant), or learned-on-a-different-task—would reveal whether any particular random structure provides a better adaptation subspace. The experimental design: fix VeRA's parameter budget (e.g., r=1024, d_init=0.1), sweep matrix families, and measure GLUE performance. Follow-up: for the best-performing family, can the rank be reduced while maintaining performance (indicating a more efficient basis)? This would connect VeRA to the rich literature on random projections and structured random matrices that the paper cites but does not engage with experimentally.

**Dynamic or learned gating beyond element-wise scaling.** VeRA's gating mechanism—the diagonal matrices Λ_d and Λ_b—treats each random direction independently (no interactions between directions) and each output dimension independently. This is extremely parameter-efficient but may limit expressivity: the effective adaptation is constrained to be a linear combination of the columns of B weighted by learned coefficients in d. A natural extension would replace the diagonal d vector with a small learned matrix D ∈ ℝ^{k×r} where k ≪ r, projecting the r random directions into a learned k-dimensional subspace before passing through B. This would enable the gating to learn *combinations* of random directions rather than just independent scaling, at a parameter cost of k×r (still small if k is modest). The experiment: compare VeRA against VeRA+D on GLUE and instruction-tuning, sweeping k, to determine whether the independence assumption in the current gating is a bottleneck. The paper's finding that VeRA matches LoRA on RoBERTa-large but trails on RoBERTa-base suggests gating expressivity may be limiting when the base model is smaller.

**Adapter swapping latency benchmarks for realistic multi-user deployment.** The paper motivates VeRA through a multi-user storage scenario (Section 1: "a million finetuned weights, e.g., one per user, that would amount to 275TB") but provides zero measurements of adapter loading, matrix regeneration, or swap latency. A deployment-focused benchmark would: (1) measure the wall-clock time to regenerate A and B from an RNG seed for GPT-3-scale models on standard GPU hardware, (2) measure end-to-end adapter swap latency (load from disk → regenerate → merge → first inference) for both VeRA and LoRA at matched performance, (3) measure whether caching pre-merged VeRA weights in GPU memory negates the storage advantage in active use, and (4) compare VeRA against a baseline where LoRA adapters are stored compressed (quantized, pruned) and decompressed on load. This would convert VeRA's architectural storage advantage into concrete deployment recommendations with throughput and latency numbers—the key practical question the paper leaves unanswered.

**Combining VeRA with verifier-guided adaptation selection.** This paper and the compute-optimal test-time scaling paper address orthogonal problems: VeRA addresses *how to store adaptations compactly*, while the test-time scaling paper addresses *how to select the best output from multiple generation attempts using verifier guidance*. A natural synthesis: use VeRA to maintain a large pool of cheaply-stored per-user or per-task adapters, and use a verifier (PRM or ORM) at inference time to route each query to the most appropriate adapter or ensemble of adapters. The experiment: finetune 100 distinct VeRA adapters on different subsets of a diverse instruction dataset, store them (total storage ~100 × 2.4M = 240MB for Llama 13B-level models—feasible on a single GPU), and use a lightweight query-adapter matching model to select which adapter(s) to use per input. Measure whether adapter diversity (captured by the frozen random basis exploring different random directions) can substitute for model scale in a multi-task deployment, connecting VeRA's storage efficiency to the test-time compute paradigm.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Per-user model personalization on edge devices.** The motivating scenario from Section 1—"a cloud-based operating system assistant that continuously learns from and adapts to individual user behaviors"—maps directly onto edge deployment where storage is severely constrained. A smartphone with 128GB of storage maintaining personalized language model adapters for even 1,000 users at LoRA's 288MB per adapter (GPT-3, rank 16) would require 288GB—impossible. At VeRA's 10.5MB per adapter (Table 1, GPT-3 rank 16), 1,000 users require 10.5GB—feasible. For smaller on-device models (e.g., Llama 7B-level quantized to 4GB), a VeRA adapter at 1.6M parameters (Table 4) stored in FP16 requires 3.2MB per user—1,000 users in 3.2GB. This makes on-device personalization practical where LoRA would require either cloud offloading (introducing latency and privacy concerns) or severe user count limits. The key deployment pattern: maintain one base model, regenerate the shared random matrices from a seed at load time, and store only per-user scaling vectors—enabling user switching with millisecond adapter swap times (vector load only, no matrix regeneration per swap) and storage costs linear in the number of users but with a tiny constant factor.

**Cost-efficient model versioning for A/B testing and continuous deployment.** Production ML systems frequently maintain multiple model variants for A/B testing, canary deployments, or task-specific routing. With LoRA, each variant requires storing a full set of low-rank matrices—for a GPT-3-scale deployment with 10 active variants, that is 10 × 288MB = 2.88GB of adapter storage. With VeRA at matched performance (requiring higher rank, e.g., r=256 from Table 1), 10 variants require 10 × 33MB = 330MB—a ~9× reduction. More importantly, the fixed storage cost per variant is dominated by the b vector (one parameter per output dimension per layer, independent of rank), meaning the marginal cost of adding a new variant is predictable and small. This enables deployment patterns where dozens of experimental adapters coexist in production memory simultaneously, with routing decisions made per-query based on adapter performance metrics—a usage pattern that would be economically prohibitive with LoRA's storage costs.

**Instruction-tuning at scale with rapid task switching.** The instruction-tuning results (Table 4) show VeRA achieving MT-Bench scores within 0.3 points of LoRA on a 10-point scale while using 1.6M vs. 159.9M parameters for Llama 7B. For a cloud API serving multiple downstream applications (coding assistant, writing tutor, customer support agent), each requiring a differently instruction-tuned variant of the same base model, VeRA enables maintaining all variants in GPU memory simultaneously. With LoRA at 159.9M parameters per variant (FP16: ~320MB), a GPU with 24GB VRAM could hold at most ~75 variants alongside the base model. With VeRA at 1.6M parameters per variant (~3.2MB FP16), the same GPU could hold thousands of variants—making per-task or even per-user instruction-tuned models economically viable at API scale. The merge-at-inference property ensures no per-query latency penalty for routing to arbitrary adapters, since all can be pre-merged or merged on-demand in milliseconds.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper explicitly positions VeRA against LoRA on the axis of storage efficiency for multi-adapter deployments. The decision rule that emerges from the experimental results:

- **Prefer VeRA when storage is the primary constraint and the base model is large.** The strongest VeRA results are on RoBERTa-large (identical GLUE average to LoRA, Table 2), ViT-Large (outperforms LoRA on 3 of 4 datasets, Table 5), and Llama2 13B (outperforms LoRA on instruction-tuning, Table 4). The method's relative performance improves with model scale, and the absolute storage savings scale with d_model (since LoRA's storage grows as d_model × r while VeRA's irreducible b-vector term grows as d_model, providing larger relative savings for wider models). If deploying many personalized variants of a large model, VeRA is the clear choice.

- **Prefer VeRA when the adaptation task primarily requires reorganizing existing knowledge rather than learning fundamentally new capabilities.** All benchmarks in the paper involve tasks within the pretrained model's general competency range. The method has not been tested on tasks requiring substantial new factual acquisition or extreme domain shift. Until such evidence exists, VeRA should be preferred for standard finetuning scenarios (instruction-tuning, style adaptation, task-format learning) and approached cautiously for knowledge-intensive adaptation.

- **Prefer LoRA when working with smaller models (e.g., RoBERTa-base scale) and performance is critical.** On RoBERTa-base, VeRA trails LoRA by 1.4 GLUE average points (85.2 vs. 86.6, Table 2), with a 7.9-point gap on RTE specifically. On ViT-Base, VeRA trails by 0.7–1.1 points on three of four datasets (Table 5). Practitioners working at this scale for accuracy-sensitive applications should benchmark VeRA carefully before adopting—the storage savings may not justify the performance cost.

- **Prefer LoRA when the adaptation budget allows only low-rank exploration and the deployment has only a few variants.** At r=1, VeRA's storage advantage over LoRA is approximately 2× (18.4K vs. 36.8K for RoBERTa-base, Table 1)—meaningful but not transformative. If only a handful of adapter variants are needed (e.g., one per task, not per user) and LoRA's storage is acceptable, the marginal benefit of switching to VeRA may not justify the additional hyperparameter sensitivity (d_init tuning) and potential performance gaps on smaller models or data-scarce tasks.
