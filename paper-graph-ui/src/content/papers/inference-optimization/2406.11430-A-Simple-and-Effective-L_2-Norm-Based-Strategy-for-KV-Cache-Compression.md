# A Simple and Effective $L_2$ Norm-Based Strategy for KV Cache Compression

**ArXiv:** [2406.11430](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.11430)

## 🎯 Pitch

This paper introduces a simple, training-free strategy for KV cache compression in decoder-only LLMs based on retaining only the key-value pairs whose key embeddings have the lowest L2 norm.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces a simple, training-free strategy for KV cache compression in decoder-only LLMs based on retaining only the key-value pairs whose key embeddings have the lowest L2 norm. The method is motivated by the empirical observation—demonstrated across Llama-2, Llama-3, and Gemma models on language modeling, needle-in-a-haystack, passkey retrieval, and LongBench tasks—that **a low L2 norm of a key embedding usually leads to a high attention score during decoding**, a correlation the authors quantify using a novel metric called **ALR** (attention loss, defined as the sum of dropped attention scores with ideal attention-based compression as the reference). By evicting KV pairs with the highest L2 norm rather than requiring attention scores, the approach achieves 50% cache reduction on language modeling and needle-in-a-haystack tasks with negligible accuracy loss and 90% reduction on passkey retrieval while maintaining 100% accuracy, establishing that the influence of cached key-value pairs can be determined from the key embeddings themselves without ever computing attention scores—making the method natively compatible with FlashAttention, unlike prior score-dependent compression schemes.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: The KV Cache Is a Memory Bottleneck That Grows Linearly

The fundamental problem this paper addresses is the **memory cost of the Key-Value (KV) cache** during autoregressive inference with large language models. To understand why this matters, we need to be precise about what the KV cache is and why it becomes problematic.

During autoregressive generation, a transformer-based LLM produces one token at a time, and each new token must attend to all previously generated tokens. Without caching, the model would recompute the key and value projections for every past token at each new generation step — an operation whose cost grows quadratically with sequence length. The KV cache eliminates this redundancy: after computing the key ($K$) and value ($V$) projections for a token during the pre-filling phase (when the input prompt is first processed), those projections are stored in memory. During subsequent decoding steps, the model simply retrieves the cached $K$ and $V$ rather than recomputing them, attending to the full history with $O(n)$ rather than $O(n^2)$ computation per step.

This is a massive efficiency win, but it creates a new problem: **memory consumption**. For a model with $L$ layers, $H$ attention heads, sequence length $n$, key dimension $d_k$, and precision (e.g., 16-bit), the total memory required is:

$$L \times H \times n \times d_k \times 2 \times \text{precision bytes}$$

The factor of 2 accounts for storing both keys and values. This grows linearly with $n$, and for long-context models handling tens or hundreds of thousands of tokens, the storage cost becomes the dominant constraint. But the issue is not just storage capacity — it is also **memory bandwidth**. During decoding, the entire KV cache (or a large portion of it) must be repeatedly read from high-bandwidth memory (HBM) to the streaming multiprocessor (SM) on the GPU. As the paper notes (citing Fu, 2024), this repeated reading of a potentially enormous cache creates a decoding latency bottleneck that is often the limiting factor in real-world deployment.

This problem has become increasingly pressing as the field pushes toward longer context lengths. Models like Gemini-Pro-1.5 (Reid et al., 2024), Claude-3 (Anthropic, 2024), and GPT-4 (Achiam et al., 2023) now handle hundreds of thousands of tokens, making KV cache management a first-order concern for anyone deploying LLMs at scale. The paper's opening framing captures this tension:

> "Handling long contexts is desirable for large language models (LLMs), as it allows them to perform tasks that require understanding long-term dependencies. [...] However, processing long-context inputs often results in a high decoding latency since it requires repeatedly reading a potentially large KV Cache from high-bandwidth memory (HBM) to the streaming multiprocessor (SM) during decoding."

Put simply: long contexts are valuable for model capability, but the KV cache that makes them computationally feasible simultaneously creates a memory bottleneck that limits their practical deployment. Compressing the KV cache — storing fewer key-value pairs without degrading model performance — is therefore a problem of both academic and industrial significance.

### Prior Approaches and Where They Fall Short

The paper categorizes existing KV cache compression methods into two broad families, and identifies specific limitations in each that motivate the proposed approach.

**Trainable approaches** involve modifying model architecture or fine-tuning to inherently manage the cache size. Examples include GQA (Grouped-Query Attention; Ainslie et al., 2023), which shares key-value heads across groups of query heads to reduce the total number of cached entries, and Dynamic Memory Compression (DMC; Nawrot et al., 2024), which merges tokens dynamically but requires expensive continual pre-training. The paper's implicit critique of this family is practical: modifying model architecture or running additional training is costly, and the resulting compression strategies are tied to a specific model rather than being applicable off-the-shelf to any pretrained LLM.

**Post-hoc (training-free) compression approaches** apply compression at inference time without modifying the underlying model. This is the family the paper contributes to. The dominant paradigm in prior work is **attention-score-based eviction**: use the attention scores that a query assigns to cached keys to determine which KV pairs are "important" and should be retained, and which can safely be discarded. Key works in this vein include:

- **H2O** (Heavy-Hitter Oracle; Zhang et al., 2024b): identifies important KV pairs by leveraging accumulated attention scores across all queries during generation, retaining tokens that consistently receive high attention.
- **FastGen** (Ge et al., 2023a): exploits the observation that different attention heads exhibit different attention patterns (some attend locally, some globally, some to punctuation/special tokens) and applies head-specific compression strategies based on these patterns.
- **SnapKV** (Li et al., 2024): selects KV pairs to retain based on attention scores computed using the user's query, effectively letting the model "tell you what it's looking for" before generation.

These methods share a critical architectural dependency: **they require computing attention scores to make eviction decisions**. This creates what the paper identifies as a decisive limitation:

> "post-hoc compression algorithms usually evict KV pairs based on attention scores, which is not compatible with FlashAttention and thus prevents their applications in modern LLMs inference systems."

FlashAttention (Dao et al., 2022) is the de facto standard for efficient attention computation in modern LLM serving systems. It is an I/O-aware exact attention algorithm that carefully manages data movement between GPU memory hierarchies, achieving dramatic speedups by avoiding materializing the full attention matrix in HBM. The key insight is that FlashAttention never explicitly stores the full $n \times n$ attention score matrix — it computes attention in tiles and keeps intermediate results in SRAM. Any compression method that needs to inspect attention scores to make eviction decisions is therefore fundamentally incompatible with FlashAttention, because those scores are never available as a complete matrix to query. This means that attention-score-based methods either require abandoning FlashAttention (sacrificing its computational benefits) or incurring additional overhead to materialize scores that FlashAttention was designed to avoid.

**Beyond compatibility issues, there is a deeper conceptual gap.** All score-based methods answer the question "which KV pairs are important?" by *querying the cached keys with a query vector* and observing which ones receive high attention. This is inherently a query-dependent measure — importance is defined relative to something you want to retrieve. But the paper's central observation suggests that importance may be determinable from the keys themselves, *before* any query arrives:

> "This finding indicates that the influence of a KV pair is potentially determined by the key embedding itself before being queried."

This reframes the compression problem entirely. Instead of "which tokens does this query attend to?" the question becomes "which tokens have intrinsic properties that predict they will be attended to by many queries?" If the latter can be answered reliably, compression can happen independently of attention computation and is therefore natively compatible with FlashAttention.

### Conflicting Signals in the Literature on Token Importance

The paper situates its contribution relative to seemingly contradictory findings about what makes tokens "important" in transformer models:

- **Xiao et al. (2024)** identified the phenomenon of "attention sinks": specific tokens (often the BOS token `<s>` and certain punctuation tokens) that consistently receive disproportionately high attention scores across many queries, regardless of the query's content. These tokens act as "sinks" that absorb attention mass during the softmax normalization. This work established that some tokens have stable, query-independent importance, but it did not connect this to a geometric property of the key embeddings that could be exploited for compression.

- **Darcet et al. (2024)** found that hidden states with *high* L2 norm tend to aggregate more important and global information in Vision Transformers, suggesting that norm magnitude could be a signal of importance. This seems to directly contradict the current paper's finding because *low* L2 norm keys receive high attention in LLMs. The paper explicitly acknowledges this tension:

> "Previous work finds the hidden states with high L2 norm usually aggregate more important and global information. On the other hand, our findings indicate that a low L2 norm of key embedding generally results in a high attention score."

This apparent contradiction is actually a productive one. It suggests that the relationship between embedding norm and importance is domain- and architecture-specific — in Vision Transformers, high-norm patches are globally informative; in LLM decoders, low-norm key embeddings correspond to tokens that act as attention sinks. The paper's contribution is to identify *which* direction of the correlation holds for LLM keys and to show it is stable enough across layers, heads, and models to serve as a reliable compression signal.

- **Cancedda (2024)** offers a theoretical lens through the concept of a "dark subspace" within the embedding space that attention sinks engage with. The paper connects its empirical findings to this framework, hypothesizing that low-norm key embeddings may be those that align most strongly with a shared direction in this dark subspace, making them attract attention from diverse queries.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper positions its contribution along several axes:

**1. Simplicity as a virtue.** The subtitle and opening of the paper invoke the "embarrassingly simple" trope deliberately. In a landscape where compression methods have grown increasingly complex — requiring per-head attention pattern classification (FastGen), accumulated attention score tracking (H2O), or end-to-end fine-tuning (DMC) — the authors propose using a single, pre-existing scalar property of each key embedding: its L2 norm. No additional models, no learned thresholds, no training. The paper frames this simplicity as a practical advantage: it can be applied "off-the-shelf to any transformer-based decoder-only LLM without the need for additional training or significant modifications."

**2. Novelty in the empirical finding, not the method per se.** The paper explicitly claims primacy on the *observation* — the correlation between L2 norm and attention score — while acknowledging that using L2 norm for decisions is not algorithmically novel:

> "we are the first, to the best of our knowledge, to point out the correlation between the L2 norm of the key embeddings and attention score."

The value is in demonstrating that this correlation is strong enough to drive a practical compression strategy, and in providing the analytical framework (the ALR metric) to quantify how that correlation varies across layers and heads.

**3. FlashAttention compatibility as a key differentiator.** The paper elevates FlashAttention compatibility from a nice-to-have to a central motivation:

> "unlike other compression methods, it can be easily integrated with the popular FlashAttention."

This is not just a feature list item — it is a response to a genuine deployment bottleneck. Modern LLM serving stacks (vLLM, TensorRT-LLM, etc.) rely heavily on FlashAttention for throughput. A compression method that forces users to abandon FlashAttention may provide memory savings but erode the wall-clock speedups that matter in production. By being FlashAttention-compatible, the L2 norm method can slot into existing optimized inference pipelines without requiring a rethink of the attention implementation.

**4. A bridge between attention sink theory and practical compression.** The paper does not merely observe the L2 norm-attention correlation — it connects it to the attention sink literature (Xiao et al., 2024) and the dark subspace hypothesis (Cancedda, 2024) to provide a mechanistic hypothesis for *why* low-norm keys attract attention. This transforms the empirical finding from a mystery ("it just works") to a grounded phenomenon that can be further investigated and potentially generalized. The connection to sparse activations (Figure 8, showing that low-norm key embeddings concentrate their mass in a few dimensions) provides a concrete measurable property that distinguishes sink tokens from regular tokens.

### Concurrent Work and the Broader Context

The paper notes concurrent work by Guo et al. (2024), which uses the L1 norm of *values* (not keys) in the KV cache for compression. This is a parallel development that uses a different norm (L1 vs. L2), a different component (values vs. keys), and still relies on attention scores, making it subject to the same FlashAttention incompatibility. The existence of this concurrent work suggests that using vector norms as importance signals is an idea whose time has come, but the specific instantiation — L2 of keys, no attention scores needed — is the present paper's contribution.

In summary, the paper addresses a pressing practical problem (KV cache memory bandwidth bottleneck), identifies a specific, consequential limitation of prior score-based methods (FlashAttention incompatibility), and positions a surprisingly simple observation (L2 norm-attention correlation) as the basis for a new class of compression strategies that are training-free, architecture-agnostic, and compatible with modern attention implementations.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

This is an **empirical observation paper** that proposes a training-free, heuristic-based KV cache compression strategy. The core idea is that, rather than using computationally expensive attention scores to decide which key-value pairs to keep in the cache, we can use a single pre-existing scalar — the L2 norm of each key embedding — to make the same decision, because low L2 norm strongly correlates with high future attention scores during decoding. The "system" being built is not a trained model or architectural modification, but rather a **selection rule** applied at inference time: when the KV cache reaches a predetermined budget, evict (discard) the key-value pairs whose key embeddings have the highest L2 norm, retaining only those with the lowest norm.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The compression pipeline has four conceptual components, though two of them (the base LLM and its KV cache) are standard infrastructure that the method plugs into without modification:

1. **Base Decoder-Only LLM** (e.g., Llama-2-7b, Llama-3-8b, Gemma-7b) — the pretrained transformer that performs autoregressive generation. It produces key embeddings $K$ and value embeddings $V$ at each layer and head during the pre-filling phase, and these are stored in the KV cache. The method makes no changes to the model's weights, architecture, or forward pass.

2. **KV Cache** — the standard memory store that holds all previously computed key-value pairs. During autoregressive decoding, newly generated tokens append to this cache. The cache grows until a pre-defined budget (maximum number of stored KV pairs) is reached, at which point the compression rule is triggered.

3. **L2 Norm Calculator** — a lightweight, stateless module that computes the Euclidean norm of each key embedding vector. For a key vector $k \in \mathbb{R}^{d_k}$, this is simply $\|k\|_2 = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{d_k} k_i^2}$. This computation is performed independently per head per layer and requires no learned parameters.

4. **Eviction Rule** — the decision procedure that, given the current cache size exceeds the budget, selects which KV pairs to discard. It sorts all cached keys by their L2 norm, retains the pairs with the lowest norms up to the budget limit, and drops the rest. The corresponding value vectors for evicted keys are also discarded. By default, the first two layers are exempted from compression (they are "skipped") because their attention-L2 correlation is low.

**Information flow at inference time:** A prompt enters the model → during pre-filling, key and value projections are computed and cached as usual → the autoregressive generation loop begins → when the cache size exceeds the pre-defined maximum, the L2 norm calculator scores all keys in the current layer/head → the eviction rule selects the lowest-norm keys to keep → the reduced cache is used for all subsequent attention computations → generation continues with the compressed cache.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the formal definition of **attention loss** (Equation 5), the core evaluation metric that quantifies how much a compression method "misses" compared to an oracle that knows the true attention scores. This metric underlies all subsequent analysis.
- **Second**, the **ALR metric** (Equations 6 and 7), which extends attention loss into a per-head, per-layer aggregate measure of how well L2-norm-based compression approximates the ideal attention-score-based compression. This is the paper's primary analytical tool for deciding *where* to apply compression.
- **Third**, the **L2 norm-attention correlation analysis** using the ALR metric across layers and heads (Figure 2), which produces the core empirical finding that motivates the compression strategy and determines the default policy of skipping early layers.
- **Fourth**, the **compression algorithm itself**, including the budget-triggered eviction procedure, the choice to keep low-norm keys rather than high-norm keys, the default layer-skipping rule, and why these specific design choices follow from the empirical analysis.
- **Fifth**, the **mechanistic hypothesis connecting low L2 norm to attention sink behavior** (the sparse activation analysis and dark subspace theory), which provides explanatory depth beyond the purely empirical correlation.
- **Sixth**, the **FlashAttention compatibility argument**, explaining exactly why score-free compression enables integration with FlashAttention while score-based methods cannot.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily an **empirical analysis paper** whose core contribution is the discovery and exploitation of a previously undocumented correlation between the L2 norm of key embeddings and their future attention scores. The "method" is a direct consequence of this correlation: sort cached keys by L2 norm and evict the ones with the highest values. The analytical machinery — the attention loss and ALR metrics — is developed to *validate* this correlation, not to perform the compression itself. The compression algorithm is remarkably simple; the intellectual contribution lies in establishing *why* it works and *where* (at which layers and heads) it is most reliable.

---

#### Formalizing Compression Quality: Attention Loss

To evaluate any KV cache compression method, we need a way to measure how much "information" is lost when certain key-value pairs are removed. The paper defines this loss in terms of the attention scores that the dropped tokens *would have received* if they had been retained — essentially, the attention mass that the model is prevented from allocating to the evicted tokens.

Let a prompt consist of $n$ tokens $(x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n)$. During the pre-filling phase, the LLM encodes these tokens into a KV cache. The model then autoregressively generates the next token $x_{n+1}$. When compression occurs, some key-value pairs are dropped and cannot be attended to by this next-token query.

The paper defines the **attention loss** caused by dropping $m$ key-value pairs as:

$$L^m_{l,h} = \sum_{p \in D_{l,h}} a_{l,h,p}$$

where $D_{l,h}$ is the set of positions of the $m$ dropped KV pairs in layer $l$, head $h$ (so $|D_{l,h}| = m$), and $a_{l,h,p}$ is the attention score that the query for token $x_{n+1}$ assigns to the $p$-th cached token in that layer and head.

**What it computes:** for a specific layer $l$, head $h$, and set of $m$ evicted token positions $D_{l,h}$, the attention loss is the sum of attention scores that the next-token query would have placed on those evicted positions. If the evicted tokens would have received high attention, $L^m_{l,h}$ is large; if they would have received negligible attention, $L^m_{l,h}$ is small. The sum ranges from 0 (evicted tokens had zero attention — perfect compression) to 1 (evicted tokens had all the attention — catastrophic compression, since softmax sums to 1).

**Why this form:** attention scores are the mechanism by which information from cached tokens influences the next-token prediction. A token receiving an attention score of $a$ contributes proportionally to the attention output (Equation 2: $\text{softmax}(QK^T/\sqrt{d_k}) \cdot V$). The sum of evicted attention scores directly measures how much of the model's "attention budget" is being thrown away. Alternatives like measuring embedding similarity or output perturbation would require extra forward passes; attention loss is theoretically grounded (it measures the direct signal removed from the attention mechanism) and computationally tractable to compute if attention scores are available.

**Critical caveat:** this metric requires knowing the attention scores from the future query $x_{n+1}$, which means it requires generating that token first and observing what it attended to. It is an *evaluation metric* for analyzing compression methods, not something that can guide compression decisions in real-time (since you would need to generate the token to know the scores, defeating the purpose of compression).

---

#### The ALR Metric: Quantifying L2 Norm-Attention Correlation

The attention loss $L^m_{l,h}$ by itself tells us how much a specific compression method loses, but it does not tell us how close that method is to optimal. To assess whether L2-norm-based compression is a good proxy for attention-score-based compression, the paper compares it against an idealized reference: what if you could drop the $m$ tokens with the *lowest* actual attention scores?

The paper defines two quantities to make this comparison. First, the **reference attention loss** $L^{m,\text{ref}}_{l,h}$ is the attention loss incurred by an oracle compression method that knows the true attention scores in advance and evicts the $m$ tokens with the smallest attention scores. This is the theoretical minimum achievable attention loss for a budget of $m$ evictions — no compression method can do better than this.

Second, the **difference** between the L2-norm method's attention loss and the oracle reference is:

$$Y^m_{l,h} = L^m_{l,h} - L^{m,\text{ref}}_{l,h}$$

where $Y^m_{l,h} \geq 0$ because the oracle achieves the minimum possible attention loss by construction, so any other method's loss is greater than or equal to the oracle's loss.

**What it computes:** for a fixed number of evicted tokens $m$, $Y^m_{l,h}$ measures how much *additional* attention mass the L2-norm method discards compared to the ideal attention-score-based method. A value of zero means L2-norm compression is perfectly equivalent to attention-score compression for that $(l, h, m)$ combination. A large positive value means the L2 norm is a poor proxy — the tokens with high L2 norm (which get evicted) actually had high attention scores (which should have been kept), so the method is discarding important tokens that the oracle would have retained.

**Why this form:** comparing against the oracle rather than an absolute threshold makes the metric independent of the absolute scale of attention scores, which varies across layers, heads, and inputs. It also directly addresses the research question: "Can we replace attention-score-based eviction with L2-norm-based eviction?" The difference from oracle quantifies exactly how much you lose by making that substitution.

To aggregate this difference across all possible numbers of evicted tokens (from $m = 1$ up to the full sequence length $n$), the paper sums over $m$:

$$Y_{l,h} = \sum_{m=1}^{n} Y^m_{l,h}$$

**What it computes:** $Y_{l,h}$ is the cumulative excess attention loss across all possible compression budgets for a specific layer $l$ and head $h$. It measures how well L2-norm ranking matches attention-score ranking *globally* — not just at a single budget level. A low $Y_{l,h}$ means that, on average across all eviction amounts, discarding high-L2-norm keys is nearly as good as discarding low-attention-score keys. A high $Y_{l,h}$ means there is a substantial gap between the two rankings.

**Why this form:** summing over $m$ avoids the need to commit to a specific compression ratio in advance. The metric captures whether the L2-norm ranking *consistently* matches the attention-score ranking. If the correlation were strong only at certain budgets (e.g., when evicting very few tokens) but weak at others, the sum would reflect this degradation. The paper names this quantity **ALR** (attention loss, using the ideal attention loss as reference), though the acronym is not explicitly expanded in the text — it is defined implicitly through Equation (7) and the surrounding discussion.

**Experimental instantiation:** The ALR is computed empirically by taking 1024 Wikipedia chunks of length 1024, running the model on each, recording the attention scores for the next-token prediction, and computing $Y_{l,h}$ for every layer-head pair. The results are visualized as heatmaps in Figure 2 for Llama-2-7b (left) and Llama-2-7b-32k (right).

#### The Layer-Head Correlation Analysis and the Skip-Layer Decision

Figure 2 presents the ALR ($Y_{l,h}$) as a heatmap, with layers on the y-axis (0 to 31 for Llama-2-7b, which has 32 layers) and heads on the x-axis (0 to 31, since Llama-2-7b has 32 heads per layer). Lower values (darker colors in the heatmap) indicate higher correlation between L2 norm and attention score — i.e., the L2 norm is a good proxy for attention-based importance. Higher values (lighter colors) indicate lower correlation.

The key empirical findings from this analysis, which directly motivate the compression algorithm's design, are:

- **Heads in the first two layers (layers 0 and 1) have consistently high ALR values.** The paper states: "heads in the first two layers [...] have relatively high Y values." This means the L2 norm is a poor predictor of attention importance in the very early layers. The likely mechanistic reason (not fully explored in the paper but consistent with transformer interpretability literature) is that early layers process low-level token representations where the relationship between embedding geometry and semantic importance is not yet established — attention in early layers may depend more on positional relationships or local syntax than on the stable "sink" properties that emerge in deeper layers.

- **Some middle layers (around layer 12) also show elevated ALR values.** The paper notes "some middle layers around the 12th layer have relatively high Y values." This is less consistent across models and may reflect architectural idiosyncrasies of Llama-2-7b's training.

- **Heads in all other layers have lower Y values**, indicating a high correlation between L2 norm and attention score. This is where the compression method is expected to work well.

**Design decision: skip the first two layers by default.** Based on this analysis, the paper adopts a default policy of not applying compression to layers 0 and 1. The statement is: "the heads in the first two layers usually have a low correlation between L2 norm and attention score, so we do not perform compression on these layers as default." The exact boundaries (skip 2 layers vs. skip 3) are explored in ablation experiments (Appendix A, Figure 13 for language modeling; Figures 16a-16d for needle-in-a-haystack and passkey retrieval). The results show that skipping only layer 0 leads to significant performance degradation on needle-in-a-haystack (Figure 16a), while skipping layers 0 and 1 largely recovers performance. Skipping an additional third layer or layer 12 provides no further benefit.

**Why this matters:** the layer-skipping rule is not an afterthought — it is a direct consequence of the ALR analysis and is essential to the method's performance. Applying L2-norm compression naively to all layers would violate the correlation assumption on the very layers where it does not hold, degrading accuracy. The skip-list is the mechanism that restricts the compression to layers where the empirical foundation (the L2-attention correlation) is valid.

The paper also observes that "patterns are quite consistent across all the models" when comparing the original Llama-2-7b, the long-context Llama-2-7b-32k, and Llama-2-7b-80k. This consistency suggests that the correlation is a property of the transformer architecture and training objective rather than an artifact of a specific model instance or context length, which is important for generalizability.

#### The Compression Algorithm: L2-Norm-Based KV Cache Eviction

With the analytical foundation established, the compression algorithm itself is straightforward. It operates independently at each layer (except skipped layers) and each head during the autoregressive decoding phase.

**Trigger condition:** the KV cache is allowed to grow normally until it reaches a pre-specified budget — a maximum number of stored KV pairs per layer per head. The paper evaluates budgets ranging from very aggressive (retaining only 10% of the cache, i.e., 90% compression) to conservative (retaining 90%, i.e., 10% compression). The budget is applied uniformly across eligible layers: "For language modelling, we let the KV Cache grow until a specific pre-defined length and subsequently start to discard the tokens with the highest L2 norm."

**What happens at eviction time:** when the cache size exceeds the budget:
1. For each cached token position in the current layer and head, compute the L2 norm of the key embedding vector: $\|k\|_2 = \sqrt{\sum_{i} k_i^2}$.
2. Sort all cached key vectors by their L2 norm in ascending order.
3. Retain the KV pairs corresponding to the keys with the smallest L2 norms, up to the budget limit.
4. Discard (evict) all remaining KV pairs from the cache — both keys and values.

**What is NOT done:** the algorithm never computes attention scores, never instantiates the full attention matrix, and never requires a forward pass beyond what the model already does for generation. The L2 norm is a property of the key embeddings that already exist in the cache — it is "free" to compute in the sense that no additional inference is required.

**Design decision: keep low norm, not high norm.** The paper explicitly tests the opposite strategy — keeping tokens with the *highest* L2 norm — and shows it catastrophically fails (Figure 4a: near-zero accuracy on needle-in-a-haystack; Figure 4b: zero accuracy on passkey retrieval; Figure 3: perplexity worse than random eviction). The authors state:

> "discarding tokens with low L2 [norm] impairs performance, even more so than random discarding, thus highlighting the importance of these low L2 norm keys."

This is a crucial result. It establishes that the correlation is asymmetric: low L2 norm specifically predicts high attention, but high L2 norm does NOT predict low attention in a symmetric way. The tokens with the very lowest L2 norms are disproportionately important; evicting them is catastrophic; evicting high-norm tokens is relatively safe. The asymmetry is consistent with the attention sink hypothesis — a few tokens serve as universal "attractors" and happen to have low L2 norm key embeddings.

**Comparison with random eviction:** The paper consistently includes a random eviction baseline in all experiments. Random eviction establishes the null hypothesis — if the L2 norm carried no information about importance, low-norm eviction would perform identically to random. The fact that low-norm eviction substantially outperforms random eviction (e.g., Figure 4a, where "keep low norm" maintains 99% accuracy at 50% compression while "keep random" drops to roughly 40-50%) confirms that the L2 norm signal is genuinely informative.

**Layer-level ablation of skip-list:** The paper investigates whether performance could be improved by also skipping compression on middle layers with elevated ALR (around layer 12). The results (Appendix A, Figure 13) show that for language modeling, "the different layer drop has little impact on final accuracy and perplexity. The difference becomes significant only when the KV Cache is pruned to retain only one thousand pairs." This means the skip-list of two layers is sufficient for moderate compression ratios, and adding more skipped layers provides marginal benefit only at extreme compression levels.

For the needle-in-a-haystack and passkey tasks (Appendix B, Figures 16a-16d), the analysis is more nuanced: skipping only layer 0 significantly degrades needle-in-a-haystack performance, but skipping layers 0 and 1 largely recovers it. Skipping an additional two layers (0, 1, and 12) shows comparable performance. The paper's default of skipping the first two layers is therefore a reasonable default that balances compression ratio (more skipped layers = less compression, since those layers retain full caches) against accuracy.

#### Mechanistic Hypothesis: Low L2 Norm, Sparse Activations, and Attention Sinks

The paper does not stop at the empirical correlation — it investigates *why* low-L2-norm keys attract high attention. This investigation yields a mechanistic hypothesis that connects the paper's findings to the broader attention sink literature.

**Key observation: low-norm key embeddings have sparse activations.** Figure 8 shows the per-dimension activation values of key embeddings for several tokens in a Llama-2-7b model. Specifically, for the BOS token `<s>` (which is an established attention sink), the activation pattern is starkly different from content tokens like "political" or "philosophy." The BOS token's key embedding concentrates its mass in a small number of dimensions — the paper identifies "∼50, ∼56 and ∼120" as the critical dimensions — while most other dimensions have values near zero. In contrast, content tokens have activations distributed more evenly across dimensions.

The paper describes this as: "tokens with lower L2 norm tend to exhibit sparse activations with only a few dimensions showing significantly high values, while the majority of the dimensions remain near zero."

**Why sparsity leads to high attention:** the connection to attention is through the dot-product in the attention score computation. The unscaled attention score between query $q$ and key $k$ is $q^T k$. If a key $k$ has most of its mass concentrated in a small set of dimensions, then the dot product is dominated by the query's values in those same few dimensions. This means that *many different queries* will produce similar dot products with this key, because they only need to align along those few critical dimensions. The key acts as a "focal point" or "sink" that attracts attention from diverse queries regardless of their specific content.

The paper states: "when the key embeddings of certain tokens are dominated by a limited set of dimensions, they create a focal point, attracting a wide range of queries – regardless of their individual content – and amplifying their attention weights."

**Why sparse keys have low L2 norm:** this is a straightforward geometric fact. If a vector concentrates its mass in a few dimensions (say, 3 out of 128 or 4096 dimensions), and those dimensions have moderate values while all others are near zero, the L2 norm (which sums squares across all dimensions) will be smaller than a vector that distributes comparable total mass across many dimensions. Sparsity naturally produces lower norms.

The hypothesis is therefore: **sparsity → low L2 norm → attention sink behavior → high attention scores**. The L2 norm is not a *cause* of high attention but a *convenient measurable proxy* for the underlying sparsity pattern that creates sink-like behavior.

**Causal intervention evidence (Figure 9):** the paper tests this hypothesis by performing a targeted intervention: zeroing out the specific peaked dimensions in low-norm key embeddings and observing the effect on attention maps. Figure 9 shows the results for four heads (0, 11, 23, 31) at layer 9 of Llama-2-7b.

- **Top row:** zeroing out random dimensions in the key embeddings produces negligible changes to the attention maps. The attention patterns before and after are essentially identical.
- **Bottom row:** zeroing out the specific peaked dimensions (the ∼50, ∼56, and ∼120 noted in Figure 8) dramatically alters the attention maps. The strong vertical bands (indicating tokens that consistently receive high attention across all query positions) largely disappear or are significantly attenuated.

This is a causal test: if the sparse dimensions are the mechanism by which low-norm keys attract attention, removing those dimensions should eliminate the attention preference. The result confirms the hypothesis. The paper states: "we zeroed out the dimensions responsible for the peaked activations in low-norm key embeddings and observed significant changes in attention maps. In contrast, altering random dimensions did not produce the same effect."

**Connection to Cancedda's "dark subspace" theory:** the paper cites Cancedda (2024), who proposed that attention sinks engage with other tokens through a "dark" subspace within the embedding space — a low-dimensional subspace that is orthogonal to or separate from the subspace where most semantic content is represented. The sparse activation pattern observed in this paper is consistent with this theory: the few highly active dimensions in sink-token key embeddings may correspond to the basis vectors of this dark subspace. Tokens with sparse key embeddings in these dimensions can serve as universal attention attractors because many query vectors also have non-zero components in this subspace, regardless of their semantic content in the "bright" subspace.

The paper summarizes: "Cancedda [2024] offers additional insight into this phenomenon, suggesting that attention sinks engage with other tokens through a 'dark' subspace within the embedding space."

**Limitations of the mechanistic explanation:** the paper is appropriately cautious about the depth of this explanation, listing as a limitation that "we do not have a comprehensive theoretical explanation for why this is the case. Understanding the underlying reasons behind the importance of the L2 norm would require further theoretical exploration and empirical validation." The mechanistic hypothesis is presented as a plausible direction grounded in evidence, not a settled explanation.

---

#### FlashAttention Compatibility: Why Score-Free Compression Matters

The paper elevates FlashAttention compatibility from a technical detail to a central motivation, and the argument deserves careful unpacking because it involves understanding what FlashAttention does and does not compute.

**What FlashAttention (Dao et al., 2022) does.** FlashAttention is an I/O-aware exact attention algorithm designed to minimize data movement between GPU high-bandwidth memory (HBM) and on-chip SRAM. The key architectural insight is that the standard attention computation materializes the full $n \times n$ attention score matrix $S = QK^T$ in HBM before applying softmax and multiplying by $V$. This is memory-intensive, with $O(n^2)$ HBM accesses. FlashAttention avoids this by computing attention in tiles:

1. Load a tile of $Q$ and a tile of $K$ from HBM into SRAM.
2. Compute the attention scores for that tile on-chip.
3. Apply softmax incrementally using an online algorithm (maintaining running max and sum for numerical stability).
4. Multiply by the corresponding tile of $V$ and accumulate the output.
5. Write only the final attention output (not the intermediate score matrix) back to HBM.

The critical consequence: **the full attention score matrix is never materialized in HBM.** It exists only transiently, tile by tile, in SRAM, and is discarded after each tile is processed. There is no point in the computation where you can "look at" all the attention scores at once.

**Why this breaks score-based compression.** Score-based compression methods like H2O, FastGen, and SnapKV all require access to attention scores to make eviction decisions. Specifically, they need to know, for each cached key, what attention score the current query (or an aggregation of past queries) assigns to it. With standard attention (without FlashAttention), the full score matrix is available, and you can simply read off the scores for each key. With FlashAttention, those scores are never stored — to get them, you would need to either:

1. Disable FlashAttention and use standard attention (sacrificing the 2-4× speedup in the attention computation itself).
2. Run a separate attention computation in parallel just to get scores (doubling the attention cost, defeating the purpose of compression).

Neither option is acceptable in production serving systems where throughput is paramount. The paper identifies this as a practical barrier: "post-hoc compression algorithms usually evict KV pairs based on attention scores, which is not compatible with FlashAttention [Dao et al., 2022] and thus prevents their applications in modern LLMs inference systems."

**Why L2-norm compression is FlashAttention-compatible.** The L2 norm of each key is a property of the key embedding itself, computed during or immediately after the key projection $K = XW_K$. It does not depend on any query vector and does not require instantiating the attention score matrix. The computation flow is:

1. During pre-filling, compute $K$ and $V$ as usual.
2. Compute $\|k\|_2$ for each key vector — this is a cheap per-token operation (sum of squares, square root).
3. Store the norms alongside the KV cache (a single float per token per head per layer — negligible storage overhead).
4. Use FlashAttention for the attention computation exactly as usual — no changes needed.
5. When eviction is triggered, sort by the pre-computed norms and drop the highest-norm entries.

The attention computation itself is completely unmodified. FlashAttention never "knows" that compression is happening — it simply operates on a smaller cache. The L2 norm computation is off the critical path and can potentially be fused with the key projection or computed in parallel.

**The practical implication:** this means L2-norm compression can be integrated into existing optimized inference frameworks (vLLM, TensorRT-LLM, DeepSpeed-Inference, etc.) without requiring those frameworks to abandon their FlashAttention implementations. The paper does not demonstrate actual integration into such frameworks — it uses HuggingFace for experiments — but the architectural argument is sound and is the primary practical differentiator from prior work.

**Comparison with FastGen under FlashAttention constraints:** to illustrate the advantage, the paper implements a FlashAttention-compatible version of FastGen (which normally uses attention scores to classify head patterns) by restricting it to only consider "local, punctuation and special tokens" — i.e., tokens that can be identified without attention scores. Figure 6 shows that L2-norm compression still outperforms this restricted FastGen on language modeling with Llama-3-8b, even though the comparison arguably favors FastGen by giving it some attention-independent heuristics that the original method was not designed around.

#### Summary of Design Choices and Their Justifications

- **L2 norm of keys rather than values:** keys are what get queried in the attention computation ($QK^T$), so their geometric properties directly affect attention scores. Values influence the output but not the attention distribution. Choosing keys is consistent with the theoretical mechanism (the dot product $q^T k$) and the empirical finding that low-norm keys correspond to attention sinks. (The concurrent work by Guo et al. 2024 used L1 norm of values, which the paper cites but does not adopt.)

- **Keep low-norm, not high-norm:** motivated by the empirical finding that low-L2-norm keys correlate with high attention scores, and verified by the ablation showing that keeping high-norm keys performs worse than random (Figures 3, 4a, 4b). The choice is non-obvious because prior work (Darcet et al. 2024) found high-norm hidden states to be important in Vision Transformers, highlighting that the relationship is architecture-dependent.

- **L2 norm rather than L1 norm or other statistics:** L2 norm is the standard Euclidean magnitude and has a direct geometric interpretation as the distance from the origin. It is also computationally trivial (no absolute values or sorting within the vector). The paper does not ablate this choice against other norms, so it remains an empirical default rather than a theoretically justified selection.

- **Skip the first two layers by default:** derived directly from the ALR analysis (Figure 2), which shows that layers 0 and 1 have low L2-attention correlation. The ablation in Appendix A (Figure 13) and Appendix B (Figures 16a-16d) confirms that skipping these layers is necessary for maintaining accuracy on long-context tasks, especially at aggressive compression ratios.

- **Uniform budget across eligible layers:** the paper applies the same retention ratio to all non-skipped layers. It acknowledges that per-head compression ratios could be beneficial ("we intend to investigate per-head compression ratios to leverage this observation" — Section 8 Limitations) but does not explore this in the current work.

- **Eviction at the cache level, not input filtering:** the method compresses the KV cache *during* autoregressive generation, not by filtering the input prompt upfront. This means the full prompt is initially encoded and cached (as required for the model to process the input correctly), and compression only begins when the cache exceeds the budget. This is important because dropping tokens from the input before encoding would change the representations of all tokens (since self-attention is computed over the full sequence during pre-filling).

- **No training, no fine-tuning, no architectural modifications:** this is the paper's key practical advantage claim. The method can be applied to any pretrained decoder-only transformer model without any modification. It is purely an inference-time heuristic. This contrasts with trainable approaches like DMC (Nawrot et al., 2024) that require continual pre-training, and with approaches like GQA (Ainslie et al., 2023) that modify the model architecture.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Redefining KV Cache Importance as an Intrinsic Property of Keys Rather Than a Query-Dependent Signal

The dominant conceptual framework in prior KV cache compression work treated token importance as inherently query-dependent: to know whether a cached key-value pair matters, you must observe which tokens receive high attention from actual queries during generation. This assumption is so deeply embedded that it is rarely stated explicitly — it is simply what H2O (Zhang et al., 2024b), FastGen (Ge et al., 2023a), and SnapKV (Li et al., 2024) all operationalize by using accumulated or query-specific attention scores as their importance signal.

This paper challenges that assumption at its foundation. The central finding — that the L2 norm of a key embedding, computable entirely from the key itself before any query arrives, strongly predicts its future attention scores — implies that **token importance in the KV cache is partially intrinsic to the cached representation, not purely defined by the queries that will later access it**. This is a conceptual reframing, not an algorithmic improvement. It shifts the compression problem from "which tokens does the model want to retrieve?" to "which tokens have geometric properties that predict they will be retrieved?"

The significance of this shift extends beyond the specific method proposed. It suggests that the attention mechanism in trained transformers encodes stable importance signals into the key embeddings themselves — signals that can be read out without performing attention. The implication is that the key projection $XW_K$ is not merely creating lookup vectors for later query matching, but is also encoding a form of "global importance prior" that is orthogonal to any specific retrieval operation. This aligns with the attention sink literature (Xiao et al., 2024) but goes further by identifying the geometric signature that distinguishes sink tokens from content tokens.

**Contrast with prior framing:** the standard approach asks "given query $q$ and key $k$, what is the attention score $q^T k$?" and uses the answer to rank importance. The paper's insight is that $\|k\|_2$ alone — without any $q$ — already provides substantial ranking information, because the model has learned to assign low norms to keys that will consistently attract attention across diverse queries. This is not obvious a priori; one might expect that the key embedding's direction (which determines dot-product alignment with queries) would dominate importance, with norm mattering only through the softmax temperature scaling. The finding that norm magnitude carries strong predictive power suggests the model uses norm as a separate communication channel for importance, perhaps exploiting the fact that softmax is sensitive to vector magnitudes.

**The asymmetry finding is crucial for depth of understanding:** the paper shows that keeping low-norm keys works well, but keeping high-norm keys is catastrophic — worse than random. If the correlation were simple and monotonic (low norm = high attention, high norm = low attention), the two strategies should be mirror images. The fact that they are not reveals that the relationship is not a simple correlation but reflects a specific mechanism: a small number of tokens have distinctly low norms and function as attention sinks, while the remaining tokens have higher norms that do not strongly predict their attention scores relative to each other. This asymmetry implies that the compression method is not selecting the "globally most important tokens" in a ranked order, but rather ensuring that the "universal sink tokens" (which are identifiable by their low norms) are retained, while all other tokens are largely interchangeable. This is a more nuanced picture than a simple norm-attention correlation.

**Tie to evidence:** the ALR heatmaps (Figure 2) quantify this correlation per layer and head, showing it is not uniform — it is absent in early layers and strong in deeper layers. This layer-wise variation is itself informative: it suggests that the encoding of intrinsic importance into key norm may be an emergent property of deeper transformer layers, consistent with the broader understanding that early layers process local syntax while later layers capture more global, abstract properties.

### Innovation 2: FlashAttention Compatibility as a First-Class Design Constraint That Dissolves a Previously Ignored Trade-off

Prior KV cache compression work treated the compression algorithm and the attention implementation as independent design choices. Methods like H2O and FastGen were developed assuming access to attention scores, implicitly expecting users to either run standard attention (without FlashAttention) or accept the overhead of running a separate score computation. The practical consequence — that these methods are incompatible with the attention implementation used in virtually all high-throughput LLM serving systems — was treated as an implementation detail rather than a fundamental limitation.

This paper elevates FlashAttention compatibility from a nice-to-have to a **hard design constraint that defines what kind of compression signals are admissible**. By committing to using only information available *before* the attention computation, the method forces a clean separation between compression (which operates on cached key properties) and attention (which operates on whatever remains in the cache). This is not merely "our method happens to work with FlashAttention" — it is "we deliberately restricted our search space to signals that do not require attention scores, because the dominant attention implementation in production renders score-based signals impractical."

**What makes this innovative is the reframing of the problem constraints.** The paper implicitly argues that a KV cache compression method that is incompatible with FlashAttention is not just "less efficient" but is solving a different problem: it compresses the cache at the cost of slowing down the attention computation itself, potentially negating the latency benefits of compression. By making FlashAttention compatibility a non-negotiable requirement, the paper identifies a class of compression methods — those using intrinsic key properties — that are architecturally orthogonal to the attention implementation and therefore preserve the full benefits of both optimization strategies (FlashAttention's I/O efficiency and KV cache compression's memory reduction).

**Comparison with prior work's implicit assumption:** H2O, FastGen, and SnapKV assume that the cost of accessing attention scores is acceptable or that FlashAttention can be modified to expose them. This paper argues — convincingly, given FlashAttention's design (tiled computation that never materializes the full score matrix) — that this assumption is false in practice. The L2-norm method is the first compression approach to treat score-inaccessibility as a design requirement rather than an unfortunate limitation.

**Significance beyond this paper:** this framing opens a new axis for KV cache compression research. Instead of asking "what is the best importance signal?" researchers can now ask "what is the best importance signal *that can be computed without attention scores*?" This constrains the search space in productive ways and aligns compression research with the realities of production inference stacks. It also suggests that other pre-attention signals — key embedding statistics beyond L2 norm, value embedding properties, positional encodings, or layer-specific features — may be worth investigating under the same constraint.

**Tie to evidence:** the paper does not benchmark against score-based methods under equivalent FlashAttention conditions (since those methods cannot run under FlashAttention), but it does demonstrate that its method achieves competitive compression ratios (50% on language modeling and needle-in-a-haystack, 90% on passkey retrieval) while making the architectural argument for compatibility. The FastGen comparison (Figure 6) — where FastGen is stripped of its attention-score dependency and reduced to static heuristics, and still underperforms — provides indirect evidence that the constraint is real and that methods designed for it (L2 norm) outperform methods retrofitted to it.

### Innovation 3: The ALR Metric as a Diagnostic Tool That Localizes Where a Compression Heuristic Is Reliable

Prior work on KV cache compression evaluated methods end-to-end: apply compression, measure downstream task performance (perplexity, accuracy), and report whether it works. This black-box evaluation answers "does the method work on average?" but provides no insight into *where* it works, *why* it might fail, or *how* to improve it. If a compression method degrades performance, the developer has no diagnostic for whether the importance signal is fundamentally flawed or merely misapplied at certain layers.

The paper introduces **ALR** (a cumulative attention loss metric comparing L2-norm eviction against an oracle with access to true attention scores) as a **per-layer, per-head diagnostic** that quantifies how well the L2 norm substitutes for attention scores at each position in the model. This is not a contribution to the compression algorithm itself — it is a contribution to the *analysis methodology* for compression heuristics.

**What makes this distinctive:** ALR decomposes the global performance of a compression strategy into its layer-wise and head-wise components. Instead of saying "L2-norm compression works well" or "it degrades at high compression ratios," ALR says "the L2 norm is an excellent proxy for attention scores in layers 3-31, heads 0-31, but a poor proxy in layers 0-1 and some heads in layer 12." This localization is actionable: it directly motivates the skip-layer policy that is essential to the method's performance.

**Comparison with prior evaluation:** prior methods used aggregate metrics (perplexity on language modeling, accuracy on downstream tasks) that obscure the internal structure of the compression problem. If H2O performs poorly at 50% compression, is it because the accumulated attention scores are a bad signal, or because the signal is good but the compression is being applied uniformly across layers where it should be adaptive? The paper's ALR framework answers this question cleanly for the L2-norm case and provides a template for how similar diagnostics could be applied to other heuristics.

**The diagnostic concept generalizes beyond L2 norm:** ALR is defined generically — it compares any compression method's attention loss against the oracle reference. The paper applies it to L2-norm ordering, but the same framework could compare L1-norm ordering, value-norm ordering, position-based ordering, random ordering, or any other heuristic against the same oracle. This makes ALR a reusable tool for the compression literature, not just a validation of this paper's specific claim. The heatmap visualization (Figure 2) is a compact representation that lets researchers quickly identify which layers and heads are problematic for a given heuristic.

**Tie to evidence:** the ALR heatmaps in Figure 2 directly produce the layer-skipping rule, and the ablation experiments in Appendix A and B (Figures 13, 16a-16d) validate that following the ALR's guidance (skip layers 0 and 1) improves performance. This closes the loop from diagnostic → design decision → improved outcomes, demonstrating the metric's practical value.

### Innovation 4: A Causal, Mechanistic Hypothesis Connecting Sparse Key Embeddings to Attention Sink Behavior

The paper goes beyond correlation ("low L2 norm predicts high attention") to propose and partially test a mechanistic explanation for *why* this correlation exists. This is a conceptual contribution that bridges the empirical compression literature with the theoretical attention sink literature, and it is unusual for a systems/compression paper to engage at this level of mechanistic depth.

**The chain of reasoning:**
1. Low-L2-norm key embeddings exhibit sparse activations — a few dimensions have large values, most are near zero (Figure 8).
2. Sparse key embeddings act as "universal attractors" because the dot product $q^T k$ is dominated by the query's values in those few active dimensions, meaning many different queries will produce similar, high dot products (the "focal point" argument).
3. High dot products translate to high attention scores after softmax normalization, creating attention sink behavior.
4. **Causal test (Figure 9):** zeroing out the specific peaked dimensions in sparse key embeddings dramatically alters attention maps, while zeroing out random dimensions has negligible effect.

This is significant because it transforms the L2 norm from a "mysterious heuristic that happens to work" into a **measurable proxy for a specific, interpretable mechanism** (sparse embedding → attention sink). It also explains the asymmetry finding (low-norm tokens are special; high-norm tokens are not) — only tokens with sufficiently sparse key embeddings act as sinks, and these tokens happen to have low norms as a geometric byproduct of sparsity. Most tokens have distributed embeddings with higher norms and do not act as sinks; their relative norms are not predictive of their relative attention scores because they are not participating in the sink mechanism at all.

**Connection to prior theory:** the paper explicitly links its sparsity finding to Cancedda's (2024) "dark subspace" theory, which posits that attention sinks engage through a low-dimensional subspace orthogonal to semantic content. The sparse dimensions identified in Figure 8 may be the basis vectors of this dark subspace. The paper thus provides an empirical grounding for a previously abstract theoretical construct — the dark subspace has a measurable signature (specific dimensions in the key embedding space with concentrated activation), and intervening on those dimensions (Figure 9) causally affects attention patterns.

**Limitations and honesty:** the paper appropriately lists the lack of a comprehensive theoretical explanation as a limitation. The mechanistic hypothesis is not a proven theorem — it is a plausible account grounded in two pieces of evidence (the sparsity observation and the causal intervention) that is consistent with prior theory. The paper's willingness to speculate while clearly marking the boundary between demonstrated fact and interpretation is a strength of the presentation.

**Significance for future work:** if the sparse-embedding → attention-sink mechanism is correct, it suggests new directions for compression and model design. Could attention sinks be explicitly engineered into the key projection? Could models be trained to concentrate sink behavior in the first few token positions (which are easiest to retain under any compression scheme) rather than distributed across punctuation and BOS tokens? The mechanistic framing opens these questions in a way that the purely empirical correlation does not.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The paper evaluates on four categories of tasks: (1) **language modeling** on English Wikipedia (50 chunks per experiment, averaged) and a code dataset (Appendix A); (2) the **needle-in-a-haystack task** (Kamradt, 2023), a synthetic long-context retrieval task where a fact ("needle") is placed at varying depths within a long document ("haystack") and the model must retrieve it; (3) the **passkey retrieval task** (Mohtashami and Jaggi, 2023), where a random passkey is inserted at a random position in a long context and the model must reproduce it; and (4) **LongBench** (Zhang et al., 2024a), specifically the NarrativeQA, Qasper, HotpotQA, 2WikiMQA, and QMSum subsets, which test diverse long-context abilities including question answering and summarization. Needle-in-a-haystack and passkey retrieval are "pressure tests" designed to evaluate whether compression preserves the model's ability to attend to critical information buried in long contexts.

- **Base model(s).** The primary models are **Llama-2-7b** (Touvron et al., 2023), the long-context variant **Llama-2-7b-80k** (Fu et al., 2024), **Llama-2-7b-longlora-32k-ft** (Chen et al., 2023, a version fine-tuned for 32k context), **Llama-3-8b** (Dubey et al., 2024), the long-context **Llama-3.1-8b**, and **Gemma-7b**. All are decoder-only transformer models in the 7-8 billion parameter range. The authors justify this scale as representative of widely-used open LLMs, though they acknowledge (Section 8, Limitations) that testing is limited to "relatively small models" and generalization to larger scales requires future work. The long-context variants (80k, 32k, and Llama-3.1) are specifically included because KV cache compression becomes critical at extended context lengths.

- **Metrics.** The paper uses multiple metrics depending on the task:
  - **Perplexity** (log PPL) and **next-token accuracy** for language modeling — measures of how well the model predicts held-out tokens under compression.
  - **Overall accuracy** for needle-in-a-haystack (fraction of queries where the model correctly retrieves the needle) and passkey retrieval (fraction of positions where the passkey is correctly reproduced).
  - **Task-specific scores** for LongBench subsets (e.g., F1 for QA, ROUGE for summarization), following the standard LongBench evaluation protocol.
  - **Attention loss** ($L^m_{l,h}$, Equation 5) and **ALR** ($Y_{l,h}$, Equation 7) for the analytical correlation studies — these are not task metrics but diagnostic measures of how well L2-norm ordering approximates attention-score ordering. Attention loss is computed as the sum of attention scores of evicted tokens for a next-token prediction; ALR aggregates this across all eviction budgets with the oracle attention-based eviction as reference. The ALR heatmaps (Figure 2) are computed over 1024 Wikipedia chunks of length 1024 each.

- **Baselines.** The paper compares against several eviction strategies, all applied within the same L2-norm framework but with different selection criteria:
  - **No compression:** the full KV cache is retained — this is the upper bound on performance.
  - **Keep low norm (the proposed method):** retain keys with the lowest L2 norm, evict those with the highest.
  - **Keep high norm:** the inverse strategy — retain keys with the highest L2 norm, evict those with the lowest. This tests the direction of the correlation.
  - **Keep random:** randomly select which KV pairs to retain, with no signal. This is the null hypothesis — if L2 norm carries no useful information, low-norm and random eviction should perform identically.
  - **FastGen** (Ge et al., 2023a): a prior attention-score-based compression method, implemented in a FlashAttention-compatible version that uses only local tokens, punctuation, and special tokens (since the full FastGen requires attention scores that FlashAttention does not expose). This baseline appears only in the language modeling comparison (Figure 6) and was implemented specifically to provide a point of comparison against a prior method forced to operate under the same score-inaccessibility constraint.

  Note that the paper does *not* compare against the full versions of H2O, SnapKV, or FastGen (with attention scores) because those methods are incompatible with FlashAttention and the paper's framing is that FlashAttention compatibility is a hard requirement. This is a deliberate scope limitation, not an omission — the paper is making an architectural argument, not claiming superiority over score-based methods in absolute terms.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The "budget" in all compression experiments is expressed as a **compression ratio** — the fraction of KV pairs evicted — or equivalently a **keep ratio** — the fraction retained. For example, 50% compression means half the cache is evicted; 90% compression means only 10% is retained. Compression ratios are swept from 10% to 90% in most experiments. For language modeling, the budget is also expressed as a maximum number of stored KV pairs (e.g., "max kv 2000" in Figure 3), after which the cache size is capped and eviction begins. All comparisons are at equivalent cache budgets: for a given compression ratio, low-norm, high-norm, and random eviction all retain the same number of KV pairs. There is no separate accounting for the cost of computing L2 norms because it is effectively free (a sum-of-squares over already-computed key vectors).

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not employ cross-validation in the traditional sense because the method has no learned parameters or hyperparameters tuned on validation data. Language modeling results (Figure 3) are averaged over 50 chunks; ALR computations (Figure 2) are averaged over 1024 chunks of length 1024. Needle-in-a-haystack results are reported as overall accuracy across all depth and context-length combinations (with detailed heatmaps in Appendix B). Passkey retrieval results are reported as accuracy as a function of passkey insertion position (Figures 19-20 in Appendix B). The paper does not report confidence intervals or statistical significance tests; most comparisons rely on visually clear separations between curves (e.g., low-norm vs. random vs. high-norm) rather than formal hypothesis testing.

---

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Language Modeling: 50% Compression With Negligible Perplexity Increase

Figure 3 shows perplexity (log scale) as a function of input sequence length for Llama-2-7b, Llama-3-8b, and Gemma-7b on Wikipedia text. The key finding is that **retaining only the lowest-L2-norm keys (with a maximum cache size of 2000 tokens) tracks the uncompressed perplexity curve almost exactly until the pre-training context length is exceeded**, at which point perplexity rises naturally due to the model's context limit, not due to compression.

For Llama-2-7b (which has a pre-training context of 4096 tokens), the "keep low norm" curve with `max kv 2000` stays within approximately 0.1-0.2 log PPL of the no-compression baseline up to input length ~4000, after which all curves diverge as the input exceeds the model's trained context window. The "keep random" curve shows substantially higher perplexity (roughly 0.5-1.0 log PPL worse) at the same cache budget, and the "keep high norm" curve is worse still (roughly 1.5-2.0 log PPL above no-compression) — catastrophically worse than random.

For Llama-3-8b, the pattern is consistent: `max kv 2000` with low-norm retention closely tracks no-compression, while random and high-norm retention show clear degradation. Notably, the FastGen baseline (restricted to local, punctuation, and special tokens) with `max kv 2000` performs worse than low-norm retention, with a gap that widens at longer input lengths (approximately 0.3-0.5 log PPL above low-norm at 8000 tokens).

For Gemma-7b, the same ordering holds: low-norm retention is closest to no-compression, random is worse, high-norm is worst.

**Why this matters:** a 2000-token cache limit represents roughly 50% compression at the typical input lengths tested (4000-8000 tokens). The fact that low-norm retention achieves this with minimal perplexity cost, while the inverse strategy (keeping high-norm) performs *worse than random*, directly validates the paper's central claim: low-L2-norm keys capture disproportionately important information, and the correlation is directional (low norm → important, but high norm does not → unimportant in a symmetric sense).

The paper also reports next-token accuracy in Appendix A (Figures 10-13), showing similar trends. For Llama-2-7b with `max kv 2000`, next-token accuracy drops from roughly 0.78 (no compression) to approximately 0.76 — a ~2% relative decline — at input lengths within the training context. The supplementary code dataset results (Appendix A) show consistent patterns, confirming that the finding is not specific to Wikipedia text.

#### Needle-in-a-Haystack: 50% Compression With 99% Accuracy Retention

Figure 4a reports overall accuracy on the needle-in-a-haystack task for Llama-2-7b-80k (which supports up to 80k token contexts) across compression ratios from 10% to 90%. The headline result is:

> "the model can preserve its performance on the needle-in-a-haystack task while compressing 30% of the KV Cache, and maintain 99% accuracy when compressing 50% of the KV Cache"

At 0% compression (no compression), accuracy is approximately 99-100%. At 30% compression with low-norm retention, accuracy remains at approximately 99-100%. At 50% compression, low-norm retention achieves ~99% accuracy. At 70% compression, low-norm retention drops to approximately 80-85% accuracy. At 90% compression, it falls to roughly 50-60%.

The comparison with other strategies is stark:
- **Keep high norm:** accuracy drops to near zero at *all* compression ratios, including 10%. The model cannot retrieve the needle *at all* when only high-L2-norm keys are retained.
- **Keep random:** accuracy decays much faster than low-norm. At 30% compression, random eviction already drops to roughly 60-70% (vs. 99-100% for low-norm). At 50%, random is at roughly 40-50% (vs. 99% for low-norm).At 90%, random is near zero.

**What this demonstrates:** the needle-in-a-haystack task specifically tests whether the model can attend to a single critical piece of information buried in a long context. If compression evicts the KV pairs representing the needle, the model fails. The fact that low-norm retention maintains near-perfect accuracy at 50% compression means the *needle tokens do not have high L2 norm keys* — they are not among the tokens being evicted. Conversely, the fact that high-norm retention fails at even 10% compression means the needle tokens *are* among those with high L2 norm — evicting even a small fraction of the highest-norm keys removes the critical information.

The detailed heatmaps in Appendix B (Figure 17) show that the performance is consistent across needle depths and context lengths when the skip-list is set correctly (skip layers 0 and 1). With `skip layers: 0,1` and `keep ratio: 0.7` (30% compression), the overall score is 0.997. With `skip layers: 0` only (compressing layer 0) and the same 30% compression, the overall score drops to 0.876, with failures concentrated at specific depth-context combinations.

Results for Llama-2-7b-longlora-32k-ft (Figure 15a in Appendix B) are consistent: low-norm retention maintains high accuracy at 30-50% compression, while high-norm and random eviction degrade rapidly. An interesting detail: at 10% compression, low-norm retention actually *slightly exceeds* the no-compression baseline (the paper notes "the performance even increases slightly when we compress 10% of KV Cache"), suggesting that removing a small number of high-norm tokens may eliminate some noise or distraction from the cache.

#### Passkey Retrieval: 90% Compression With 100% Accuracy

Figure 4b reports accuracy on passkey retrieval for Llama-2-7b-80k. The result is the most dramatic in the paper:

> "the model can achieve 100% accuracy on the passkey retrieval task even when compressing 90% of the KV Cache"

Low-norm retention maintains 100% accuracy from 10% through 90% compression. Random eviction drops to roughly 80% at 50% compression and collapses to near-zero at 90%. High-norm retention yields zero accuracy at all compression ratios.

The per-position breakdown in Appendix B (Figures 19-20) shows that low-norm retention achieves 100% accuracy regardless of where the passkey is inserted (tested across positions from 0 to ~30,000). With skip layers 0 and 1 at 90% compression (retaining only 10% of KV pairs), accuracy is uniformly 100% across all positions.

**Why passkey retrieval is easier than needle-in-a-haystack:** the passkey task requires the model to retrieve a specific, unique token sequence (a random number) that appears nowhere else in the context. The needle-in-a-haystack task requires retrieving a fact that may share vocabulary with the surrounding text, making it a harder retrieval problem. The fact that passkey retrieval is perfectly robust to 90% compression while needle-in-a-haystack degrades at 70% suggests that the compression method is better at preserving unique or distinctive tokens (which are likely to have low L2 norm, consistent with the sparse-activation hypothesis) than at preserving tokens that blend with their context.

**Critical detail:** the passkey results depend on the skip-layer configuration. Figures 16b and 16d show that if only layer 0 is skipped (not layers 0 and 1), passkey accuracy at 90% compression drops to roughly 50-60% (with a U-shaped curve across compression ratios — accuracy is lower at intermediate ratios and recovers slightly at 90%). Skipping layers 0 and 1 restores near-perfect accuracy. This validates the ALR-based decision to skip the first two layers and shows that improper skip-list configuration can produce misleadingly poor results.

#### LongBench: Robust Performance at 50% Compression Across Diverse Tasks

Figure 5 summarizes average scores across five LongBench subsets (NarrativeQA, Qasper, HotpotQA, 2WikiMQA, QMSum) for Llama-3.1-8b and Llama-2-7b-80k. The key pattern:

- **Low-norm retention at 50% compression:** Llama-3.1-8b drops from approximately 44 (no compression) to approximately 40 — a ~9% relative decline. Llama-2-7b-80k drops from roughly 34 to roughly 31 — a similar ~9% relative decline.
- **Low-norm retention at 90% compression:** Llama-3.1-8b falls to roughly 20-25; Llama-2-7b-80k falls to roughly 15-20. This is a substantial degradation but still far above the near-zero scores of alternative strategies.
- **High-norm retention at any compression level (including 10%):** scores collapse to near zero for both models. This is consistent across all five subsets.
- **Random retention:** decays faster than low-norm but not as catastrophically as high-norm. At 50% compression, random scores are roughly 50-60% of the no-compression baseline for Llama-3.1-8b, compared to ~90% for low-norm.

The per-subset breakdowns in Appendix B (Figures 21 and 22) show that the pattern is consistent across all five tasks. For example, on NarrativeQA with Llama-2-7b-80k at 50% compression: low-norm achieves approximately 20 (slightly below the no-compression score of roughly 22), random drops to roughly 10, and high-norm is near zero. On Qasper: low-norm is approximately 34 vs. no-compression at roughly 36, random at roughly 25, high-norm near zero.

**What this demonstrates:** LongBench tasks test diverse long-context abilities — multi-hop QA, summarization, narrative understanding — rather than simple retrieval. The fact that low-norm compression works across all these tasks (with only moderate degradation at 50% compression) suggests the method preserves not just "needle" tokens but the broader information content needed for comprehension and reasoning. The consistent catastrophic failure of high-norm retention across all tasks reinforces the directionality of the correlation.

#### Comparison With FastGen: L2-Norm Outperforms a FlashAttention-Compatible Adaptation

Figure 6 compares L2-norm compression against a FlashAttention-compatible version of FastGen on Llama-3-8b language modeling. The FastGen variant retains only local tokens, punctuation, and special tokens (since it cannot access attention scores under FlashAttention). The results show:

> "Our method still outperforms FastGen with up to 50% KV Cache eviction."

At `max kv 2000` (50% compression at typical input lengths), low-norm retention tracks no-compression closely (perplexity within ~0.2 log PPL), while FastGen diverges by roughly 0.3-0.5 log PPL, with the gap widening at longer sequences. Next-token accuracy shows a corresponding gap.

**Important caveat:** this comparison is somewhat unfair to FastGen. The original FastGen uses attention scores to classify head patterns and select tokens adaptively. Stripping it of attention scores and reducing it to static heuristics (always keep local, punctuation, and special tokens) removes its core mechanism. The comparison therefore tests "best static heuristic under FlashAttention constraints" rather than "L2-norm vs. FastGen in general." The paper's framing is that this is a fair comparison *given the FlashAttention constraint* — both methods operate without attention scores, and L2-norm is the stronger performer under that constraint.

#### ALR and Correlation Analysis: Quantifying Where L2 Norm Is a Reliable Signal

Figure 2 presents ALR heatmaps for Llama-2-7b (left) and Llama-2-7b-32k (right), with layers 0-31 on the y-axis and heads 0-31 on the x-axis. Darker values indicate lower ALR (better correlation). The key findings are:

- **Layers 0-1:** consistently high ALR values (light colors) across most heads, indicating poor L2-attention correlation. This is the empirical basis for skipping these layers.
- **Some middle layers (around layer 12):** elevated ALR in specific heads, though less pronounced and less consistent than layers 0-1.
- **All other layers (3-11, 13-31):** predominantly low ALR values (dark colors), indicating strong correlation. This is where the compression method is reliable.
- **Pattern consistency across models:** the two heatmaps (base Llama-2-7b and 32k long-context version) show highly similar patterns, supporting the claim that the correlation is a stable property of the architecture/training rather than an artifact of context length or specific model instance.

Figure 7 provides a detailed, quantitative view of attention loss as a function of compression ratio for two representative cases:
- **Layer 7, Head 10 (high correlation):** the attention loss curves for "sort by L2-Norm" and "sort by attention score" (the oracle) are nearly identical across all compression ratios from 0% to 99%. At 50% compression, both lose approximately 0.002 attention mass — essentially zero. Even at 99% compression, the L2-norm curve (attention loss ~0.008) is only slightly above the oracle (attention loss ~0.006). This means L2-norm eviction is almost perfectly equivalent to attention-score eviction in this head.
- **Layer 0, Head 0 (low correlation):** the L2-norm curve deviates substantially from the oracle. At 50% compression, the oracle loses approximately 0.05 attention mass while L2-norm loses approximately 0.10 — roughly 2× worse. At 99% compression, the oracle loses ~0.4 while L2-norm loses ~0.9 — the L2-norm method is discarding tokens that would have received substantial attention. This quantifies *why* skipping early layers is necessary.

The paper averages these results over 1024 Wikipedia chunks of length 1024 to produce the ALR heatmaps and the attention loss curves.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Skip-layer configuration (Appendix A, Figures 10-13; Appendix B, Figures 16a-16d):** The paper systematically tests skipping different layer sets. For language modeling (Figure 13), skipping layers 0 and 1 vs. skipping layers 0, 1, and 12 produces nearly identical perplexity and next-token accuracy, with differences emerging only at extreme compression (max kv 1000, roughly 75-80% compression). For needle-in-a-haystack (Figure 16a), skipping only layer 0 produces an overall accuracy of roughly 60% at 50% compression with Llama-2-7b-80k, while skipping layers 0 and 1 achieves near-100%. Skipping three layers (0, 1, and 2) is comparable to skipping two. The heatmap in Figure 17a (skip layer 0, 30% compression, overall score 0.876) vs. Figure 17b (skip layers 0 and 1, same compression, overall score 0.997) visually confirms the improvement is concentrated at specific depth-context combinations. For passkey retrieval (Figure 16b), the U-shaped accuracy curve when skipping only layer 0 is particularly notable — accuracy drops to roughly 50% at intermediate compression ratios (30-50%) and partially recovers at 90%. The paper does not explain this U-shape, but it likely reflects a regime where moderate compression disproportionately evicts important tokens (because some sink tokens in layer 0 are among the highest-norm keys) while extreme compression forces retention of some important tokens by simple volume.

**Eviction strategy direction (Figures 3, 4a, 4b, 5):** The consistent inclusion of "keep high norm" as a control demonstrates that the correlation is directional. On every task and at every compression ratio, keeping high-norm keys performs *worse than random* — often catastrophically (near-zero accuracy). This asymmetry is the most consistently replicated finding in the paper and is central to the mechanistic claim that low-norm keys are special (attention sinks) while high-norm keys are not symmetrically "unimportant."

**Layer-level ALR variation across models (Figure 2):** The paper compares ALR heatmaps for base Llama-2-7b and the 32k long-context variant, finding "patterns are quite consistent across all the models." This is a robustness check showing that the L2-attention correlation is not an artifact of a specific training run or context length. The paper mentions but does not show similar heatmaps for Llama-3 and Gemma, so the cross-model consistency claim is primarily supported for Llama-2 variants.

**Causal intervention on sparse dimensions (Figure 9):** Zeroing out the specific peaked dimensions in low-norm key embeddings dramatically alters attention maps, while zeroing out random dimensions has negligible effect. This ablation tests the mechanistic hypothesis (sparse activations → attention sink) rather than the compression method itself, but it provides crucial supporting evidence that the L2 norm is a proxy for a real, causally active property of the key embeddings rather than a spurious correlation. The experiment is qualitative (visual comparison of attention maps before and after intervention) rather than quantitative, but the effect is visually unambiguous.

**Dataset diversity (Figures 3 and Appendix A):** The language modeling experiments are run on both Wikipedia and code datasets, with consistent results. The long-context experiments span synthetic retrieval tasks (needle-in-a-haystack, passkey) and diverse natural tasks (LongBench subsets covering QA, summarization, and multi-hop reasoning). The consistency of the low-norm advantage across these qualitatively different tasks strengthens the claim that the correlation is a general property of the model rather than task-specific.

**Model diversity:** The method is tested on Llama-2-7b, Llama-2-7b-80k, Llama-2-7b-longlora-32k-ft, Llama-3-8b, Llama-3.1-8b, and Gemma-7b. The consistent performance across these models (all decoder-only transformers in the 7-8B range) supports the claim of broad applicability within this model class. However, the limitation section acknowledges that all models are "relatively small" and from two families (Llama and Gemma), leaving open the question of whether the correlation holds at larger scales (e.g., 70B, 405B) or in different architectures (e.g., non-RoPE positional encodings, mixture-of-experts).

**LongBench per-subset consistency (Figures 21, 22):** The low-norm > random > high-norm ordering holds across all five LongBench subsets individually, not just in aggregate. This is important because the subsets test qualitatively different capabilities (NarrativeQA: narrative understanding; Qasper: scientific paper QA; HotpotQA: multi-hop reasoning; 2WikiMQA: multi-entity QA; QMSum: meeting summarization). If the correlation were driven by a single task type (e.g., retrieval), it might fail on summarization or reasoning; the per-subset results show it does not.

**Concurrent work comparison (discussed in Section 6):** The paper notes that Guo et al. (2024) concurrently proposed using the L1 norm of *values* (not keys) for compression, combined with attention scores. The present paper's use of L2 norm of *keys* without attention scores is a distinct approach, and the paper's experimental results can be seen as an implicit ablation showing that keys (not values) and L2 (not L1) can work without attention scores. However, no direct comparison with the Guo et al. method is provided.

### Critical Assessment

The experiments provide strong evidence for a specific empirical claim: **the L2 norm of key embeddings in decoder-only LLMs (Llama-2, Llama-3, Gemma at 7-8B scale) correlates with future attention scores such that retaining low-norm keys and evicting high-norm keys preserves model performance substantially better than random eviction, and the inverse strategy (retaining high-norm) is catastrophic.** This claim is supported by consistent results across multiple models, multiple task types, multiple compression ratios, and multiple metrics. The evidence for it is robust.

However, the paper makes a broader claim in its framing: that this method is a practical KV cache compression strategy that can be deployed in production inference systems. This claim requires evidence that goes beyond what is presented, and several gaps exist between the experimental results and the deployment claim.

**The experiments demonstrate that L2-norm-based eviction is better than random eviction and better than the inverse heuristic. They do not demonstrate that it is better than or competitive with the state-of-the-art score-based methods (H2O, SnapKV, full FastGen) when those methods are run without the FlashAttention constraint.** The paper's framing makes this comparison irrelevant by arguing that score-based methods are categorically disqualified due to FlashAttention incompatibility. This is a valid architectural argument, but it means the paper's claim is not "L2-norm compression outperforms prior methods" but rather "L2-norm compression performs well under constraints where prior methods cannot operate." The distinction matters for practitioners who might be willing to forgo FlashAttention if the compression benefits are large enough — the paper provides no data to guide that decision.

**The FlashAttention compatibility is argued architecturally but not demonstrated experimentally.** The paper uses HuggingFace implementations for all experiments, which do not use FlashAttention by default (or may use it only in certain configurations). There is no experiment showing wall-clock speedup on a FlashAttention-based serving stack with L2-norm compression integrated. The claim that the method "remains compatible with FlashAttention, enabling broader applicability" (Abstract) is a theoretical compatibility claim, not a demonstrated practical benefit. A deployment-focused paper would ideally show latency/throughput measurements with and without compression under FlashAttention, but such measurements are absent.

**The difficulty estimation or budget-setting problem is not solved.** The compression ratio (or maximum cache size) is a hyperparameter that must be set by the user. The paper treats it as an independent variable being swept but provides no guidance on how to choose it for a given model, task, or hardware constraint. In practice, the optimal ratio would depend on input length, task difficulty, and acceptable accuracy degradation — none of which are characterized. The paper's results show that 50% compression is safe across tasks and 90% works for passkey retrieval but not needle-in-a-haystack, but there is no systematic study of the accuracy-compression tradeoff curve that would guide budget selection.

**The layer-skipping rule is based on analyzing Llama-2-7b and applied uniformly to all models.** Figure 2 shows ALR heatmaps for Llama-2-7b variants, but the paper does not produce equivalent heatmaps for Llama-3 or Gemma to verify that the first-two-layers-skip rule transfers. The ALR patterns could be architecture-specific — Llama-3 uses grouped-query attention (GQA), which Llama-2 does not, potentially changing the per-head correlation structure. The paper's results with Llama-3 and Gemma use the same skip-list derived from Llama-2 analysis, which may be suboptimal for those models.

**The 90% compression result on passkey retrieval is impressive but potentially task-specific.** The passkey is a unique random number — it is maximally distinctive. The sparse-activation/attention-sink mechanism the paper hypothesizes would naturally preserve such distinctive tokens (they may be the sink tokens themselves). For tasks where the critical information is more distributed or semantically integrated with context, the compression performance may degrade faster. The LongBench results at 50% compression (9% accuracy decline) are more representative of realistic degradation than the passkey results.

**The test sets for needle-in-a-haystack and passkey retrieval are synthetic and small-scale.** The paper does not report the number of test instances per data point in the compression ratio sweeps (Figures 4a, 4b). Without knowing the sample size, it is difficult to assess the reliability of the reported accuracies — a 99% accuracy at 50% compression on 100 test instances has a different interpretation than the same number on 10,000 instances. The LongBench evaluation uses standard test splits, which is stronger.

**The 7-8B model scale is not representative of production deployments where KV cache compression matters most.** The paper's own motivation emphasizes that KV cache compression is critical for long-context models handling tens or hundreds of thousands of tokens. The largest models where this is most pressing (70B, 405B, and proprietary models) are not tested. The authors acknowledge this limitation. The concern is not just about scaling the method — it is about whether the L2-norm-attention correlation is an architectural universal or a property of the specific model scale and training recipe used. Larger models may have different key embedding geometry, different attention sink patterns, or different layer-wise correlation structure.

**Missing ablation: L2 norm vs. other key statistics.** The paper commits to L2 norm without comparing against alternatives like L1 norm, variance, entropy, or maximum absolute value. The L1 norm of values was explored concurrently (Guo et al., 2024), but no comparison with L2 norm of values or L1 norm of keys is provided. It is possible that another statistic of the key embeddings correlates even more strongly with attention scores than L2 norm. The paper's empirical finding is specifically about L2 norm, but the methodology (ALR analysis) could be applied to compare different statistics, and the absence of such a comparison leaves open the question of whether L2 norm is the best choice or merely a good-enough choice.

**Missing ablation: per-head or per-layer adaptive compression ratios.** The paper uses a uniform compression ratio across all non-skipped layers and all heads. The ALR analysis (Figure 2) shows that correlation strength varies across layers and heads — some heads in middle layers also show elevated ALR. An adaptive strategy that compresses less aggressively in low-correlation heads and more aggressively in high-correlation heads could improve the accuracy-compression tradeoff. The paper acknowledges this in the limitations ("we intend to investigate per-head compression ratios to leverage this observation") but does not explore it experimentally.

**The FastGen comparison (Figure 6) is between a static heuristic (FlashAttention-compatible FastGen) and another static heuristic (L2 norm).** It does not compare against the actual FastGen with attention scores, which would be the relevant baseline if one were willing to forgo FlashAttention. This comparison is useful for the paper's narrative but does not establish L2-norm superiority over score-based methods in absolute terms.

In summary, the experimental section convincingly validates the paper's central empirical finding — the L2-norm-attention correlation as a reliable, directional signal for KV cache eviction — across a reasonable range of models, tasks, and compression ratios. The strongest contribution is the demonstration that this simple heuristic works at all, and works substantially better than random, which is non-obvious and well-supported. The paper is weaker in establishing the practical deployability of the method: no wall-clock benchmarks, no comparison against score-based methods in their native (non-FlashAttention) setting, no guidance on budget selection, and no evaluation at the model scales where KV cache compression is most critical. The claims about FlashAttention compatibility and deployment readiness are architectural arguments supported by the design of the method, not by experimental evidence of end-to-end performance in a production stack.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### All Results Are on 7–8B Parameter Models From Two Families; Generalization to Larger Scales and Different Architectures Is Untested

**The assumption or constraint.** Every experiment in the paper uses decoder-only transformer models in the 7–8 billion parameter range: Llama-2-7b (and its 80k and 32k long-context variants), Llama-3-8b, Llama-3.1-8b, and Gemma-7b. The authors are transparent about this scope restriction, explicitly stating in Section 8 (Limitations):

> "we tested only on relatively small models (Llama family and Gemma up to 8 billion parameters). In future work, we will assess our method on larger-scale models to ensure our findings generalize"

**The consequence.** The practical motivation for KV cache compression — the memory bandwidth bottleneck during decoding — is most acute for the largest models deployed in production (70B, 405B, and proprietary models in the hundreds-of-billions range). A 7B model with a 2000-token KV cache has relatively modest memory requirements that may not require compression at all in many deployment scenarios. The models where compression would deliver the greatest practical value are precisely those not tested.

This is not merely a matter of scaling up a working method. There are specific reasons the L2-norm-attention correlation might not transfer to larger models:

- **Larger models have more representational capacity** and may develop different key embedding geometries. The sparse-activation/attention-sink mechanism documented in Figures 8 and 9 could be an emergent property of the 7–8B scale that changes qualitatively at larger scales, where the model has more dimensions to distribute representations and may not need to concentrate sink behavior in a few sparse dimensions.
- **Architectural differences accumulate at scale.** Larger models frequently employ techniques not present in the tested models: grouped-query attention (GQA) is used in Llama-3-8b but the paper does not analyze how GQA's key-head sharing affects the per-head L2-attention correlation structure; mixture-of-experts (MoE) architectures introduce sparse routing that could fundamentally change attention patterns; and different positional encoding schemes (the tested models all use RoPE) could affect the geometric relationship between key norm and attention.
- **The ALR-based layer-skipping rule was derived from Llama-2-7b heatmaps** (Figure 2) and applied to all models without re-deriving ALR for Llama-3 or Gemma. If the per-layer correlation structure differs at larger scales, the skip-list of layers 0 and 1 may be suboptimal or insufficient. The paper does not produce ALR heatmaps for Llama-3 or Gemma to validate the transfer.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The cross-model consistency among the tested 7–8B models provides some evidence that the correlation is not an idiosyncratic property of a single training run — it appears in Llama-2 (pre-RoPE, MHA), Llama-3 (GQA, different training data), and Gemma (different architecture and training recipe). This consistency at the 7–8B scale does not, however, constitute evidence about the 70B+ scale. The paper provides no experiments, no theoretical scaling argument, and no analysis of how key embedding geometry changes with model size to support extrapolation.

**Mitigation status.** The limitation is acknowledged but completely unaddressed experimentally. The authors frame it as future work. No partial evidence (e.g., analyzing a 13B model, probing how key norm distributions change from 7B to 13B, or citing related work on attention sink behavior at larger scales) is provided to bridge the gap.

---

### The L2 Norm Has No Comprehensive Theoretical Justification; The Method Is a Heuristic Whose Failure Modes Are Poorly Understood

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper explicitly disclaims theoretical understanding of its central phenomenon. Section 8 (Limitations) states:

> "while we show that the L2 norm played a significant role in our experiments, we do not have a comprehensive theoretical explanation for why this is the case. Understanding the underlying reasons behind the importance of the L2 norm would require further theoretical exploration and empirical validation."

The method rests entirely on an empirically observed correlation. The mechanistic hypothesis — sparse key embeddings → low L2 norm → attention sink behavior → high attention scores (Section 5) — is presented as a plausible direction, not a validated theory. The causal intervention in Figure 9 (zeroing specific peaked dimensions in key embeddings alters attention maps) provides supporting evidence that sparse dimensions are causally active, but it does not close the explanatory gap between "sparse dimensions exist" and "the L2 norm is the optimal or even a principled statistic for identifying them."

**The consequence.** Without theoretical grounding, practitioners have no framework for predicting when the method will fail, diagnosing failures when they occur, or adapting the method to new settings. Several specific failure modes are opaque:

- **Why L2 norm rather than L1, variance, sparsity count, or any other statistic?** The paper never ablates L2 norm against alternatives. It is possible that another key statistic correlates even more strongly with attention scores or is more robust across layers and models. Without theory, the choice of L2 norm is an empirical default whose optimality is unknown.
- **Why does the correlation fail in early layers?** The paper documents that layers 0 and 1 have low L2-attention correlation (Figure 2) and uses this to motivate the skip-list, but it provides no explanation for *why* early layers differ. Is it a property of how representations develop through the network? A consequence of positional encoding interactions? A training dynamics artifact? Without understanding, one cannot predict whether the same skip-list transfers to other architectures, nor can one design models or training procedures to make early layers more amenable to compression.
- **What is the causal direction?** The paper frames low L2 norm as a proxy for attention sink behavior, but there is no experiment establishing that low L2 norm *causes* high attention (as opposed to both being correlated effects of some third factor, like token frequency or positional role). The intervention in Figure 9 zeros out specific dimensions — it does not manipulate the L2 norm directly. A demonstration that artificially reducing the L2 norm of arbitrary tokens increases their attention scores would strengthen the causal claim, but such an experiment is absent.
- **Could an adversary exploit this?** If low-L2-norm tokens receive disproportionate attention, a malicious user could potentially craft inputs where critical information is embedded in tokens engineered to have high L2 norm, causing the compression method to evict them. The paper does not consider adversarial robustness.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The evidence for the correlation is strong empirically (Figures 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 7 all converge on the same finding). The evidence for the mechanism is suggestive but incomplete (Figures 8 and 9). The evidence for why L2 norm specifically is the right statistic is absent (no comparison with alternatives). The paper does not characterize the residual errors — when low-norm eviction fails, which specific tokens or attention patterns are responsible? Without a failure analysis, the method is a black box.

**Mitigation status.** The limitation is acknowledged but not addressed. The paper calls for "further theoretical exploration and empirical validation" as future work. There is no attempt to develop even a partial theoretical framework (e.g., analyzing the relationship between key embedding norm and the softmax temperature, studying the gradient dynamics that produce sparse key embeddings during training, or connecting to the extensive literature on representation geometry in transformers).

---

### Difficulty Estimation Is Not Solved; the User Must Choose a Compression Ratio Without Guidance, and the Optimal Ratio Is Task-Dependent

**The assumption or constraint.** In all experiments, the compression ratio (or maximum cache size) is treated as an independent variable swept across a range (10% to 90% compression). The paper evaluates performance *given* a chosen budget but provides no mechanism for *selecting* the budget. There is no adaptive or task-conditioned budget selection. The paper states in Section 4:

> "For language modelling, we let the KV Cache grow until a specific pre-defined length and subsequently start to discard the tokens with the highest L2 norm."

The pre-defined length (`max kv 2000` in Figure 3, varying budgets in Figures 4 and 5) is a hyperparameter set by the experimenter with knowledge of the model's context length and the task's requirements. In deployment, a user would need to specify this budget without such knowledge.

**The consequence.** The paper's headline results — "50% compression without losing accuracy" on language modeling, "90% compression with 100% accuracy" on passkey retrieval — are conditional on knowing the right compression ratio for the task. A user who sets the ratio too aggressively degrades accuracy; a user who sets it too conservatively leaves memory savings on the table. The paper provides no principled way to navigate this tradeoff.

This problem is particularly acute because **the acceptable compression ratio is highly task-dependent** in ways the paper documents but does not systematize:

- **Passkey retrieval tolerates 90% compression with perfect accuracy** (Figure 4b). Needle-in-a-haystack degrades to ~80% at 70% compression (Figure 4a). LongBench shows a ~9% accuracy decline at 50% compression (Figure 5). A user deploying a general-purpose system that handles diverse queries cannot select a single ratio and expect consistent performance.
- **The degradation is not monotonic in a simple way.** Figure 16b shows a U-shaped accuracy curve for passkey retrieval when compressing layer 0 — accuracy drops at intermediate compression ratios and partially recovers at 90%. This suggests that moderate compression can be *more harmful* than aggressive compression in some regimes, presumably because it selectively evicts a critical subset of sink tokens while retaining enough to partially function.
- **The optimal ratio likely depends on input length.** The paper tests fixed maximum cache sizes (e.g., 2000 tokens) against varying input lengths (0–8000 tokens), meaning the effective compression ratio changes across the x-axis of Figure 3. A `max kv 2000` budget is 0% compression at 2000 tokens, 50% at 4000 tokens, and 75% at 8000 tokens. The fact that perplexity tracks no-compression until exceeding the pre-training context length suggests the method adapts implicitly to length, but only because the budget is fixed — a ratio-based budget (always compress 50%) would behave differently at different lengths.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figures 3, 4a, 4b, and 5 all show performance across a sweep of compression ratios, and the task-dependence is clearly visible. The paper documents the phenomenon implicitly through its multi-task evaluation but does not analyze it explicitly as a budget-selection problem. There is no figure plotting the accuracy-vs-compression tradeoff curve, no characterization of the Pareto frontier, and no attempt to predict the maximum safe compression ratio from task or input properties.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper treats the compression ratio as an experimental variable rather than a deployment challenge. There is no suggestion of an adaptive method (e.g., monitoring perplexity or attention entropy during generation to dynamically adjust the budget), no learned budget predictor, and no characterization of how to set the ratio for a new task or model. This is a gap between the paper's analytical contribution (demonstrating that L2 norm is a useful signal at *some* budget) and its practical deployability (where a user needs to pick *which* budget).

### No Wall-Clock Time or Throughput Benchmarks; the Practical Speedup From Compression Under FlashAttention Is Not Measured

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's central practical claim is that L2-norm compression is compatible with FlashAttention, unlike prior score-based methods, and therefore can deliver memory savings without sacrificing the I/O efficiency that makes FlashAttention valuable in production. However, this claim is argued architecturally, not demonstrated experimentally. All experiments are conducted using HuggingFace model implementations (stated in Appendix E: "In all experiments, we used the HuggingFace library and did not change the model's default hyperparameters"). The paper includes no latency measurements, no throughput benchmarks, no memory profiling, and no integration with a FlashAttention-based serving framework such as vLLM, TensorRT-LLM, or DeepSpeed-Inference.

The Abstract states the method "remains compatible with FlashAttention, enabling broader applicability." The compatibility is defined in principle — the method does not require attention scores, and FlashAttention does not expose them, so there is no architectural conflict. But compatibility in principle does not guarantee practical benefit.

**The consequence.** Several important practical questions are unanswered:

- **What is the actual wall-clock speedup?** KV cache compression reduces memory capacity pressure (you store fewer KV pairs) and memory bandwidth pressure (you read fewer bytes from HBM to SRAM during each decoding step). But the L2 norm computation, sorting, and eviction add overhead. Is this overhead negligible, or does it eat into the bandwidth savings? For what sequence lengths and batch sizes does the net effect become positive? Without benchmarks, a practitioner cannot estimate the latency improvement they would observe by deploying the method.
- **How does compression interact with other optimizations in a production stack?** Production serving systems employ multiple overlapping optimizations: continuous batching, paged attention (Kwon et al., 2023), prefix caching, quantization, and kernel fusion. Does L2-norm compression compose well with these, or does it create new bottlenecks (e.g., the sorting step may not fuse cleanly with attention kernels, or the variable cache sizes after eviction may complicate memory management in paged attention)?
- **What is the memory savings in absolute terms?** The paper reports compression ratios (50%, 90%) but not absolute memory measurements. For a 7B model at a given sequence length and precision, what is the actual GPU memory reduction? How does this translate to increased batch size or maximum context length? These engineering metrics are absent.
- **Does FlashAttention compatibility actually matter in the tested setting?** The experiments use HuggingFace, which may use standard attention or an SDPA backend that is not equivalent to the production FlashAttention kernels. The paper has not demonstrated that its method works correctly *with FlashAttention specifically* in an end-to-end inference loop — only that it does not require API access to attention scores that FlashAttention does not provide.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** There is no experimental evidence addressing any of these questions. The paper provides a schematic argument for FlashAttention compatibility in Section 1 and Section 6, and a theoretical memory calculation in Section 2 (the factor of $L \times H \times n \times d_k \times 2 \times \text{precision}$), but no runtime measurements of any kind. The FastGen comparison (Figure 6) uses the same HuggingFace infrastructure and does not constitute a FlashAttention benchmark.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not acknowledge the absence of throughput benchmarks as a limitation. The FlashAttention compatibility claim remains at the level of architectural argumentation rather than empirical demonstration. This is the most significant gap between the paper's claims and its evidence regarding practical deployability.

### The Method Never Combines With or Compares Against Score-Based Methods in Their Native (Non-FlashAttention) Setting; the Relative Performance Is Unknown

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's framing makes FlashAttention compatibility a hard design constraint, arguing that score-based methods (H2O, FastGen, SnapKV) are "not compatible with FlashAttention and thus prevents their applications in modern LLMs inference systems" (Section 1). Based on this framing, the paper does not compare L2-norm compression against the full versions of these prior methods with access to attention scores. The only comparison against a prior method is against a modified FastGen stripped of its attention-score dependency (Figure 6), which the paper acknowledges is a restricted version.

This is a legitimate framing choice — the paper is evaluating a different class of methods (score-free) under a specific architectural constraint. But it also means the paper provides no evidence about how L2-norm compression performs *relative to the state of the art* when the FlashAttention constraint is not imposed.

**The consequence.** A practitioner deciding whether to adopt L2-norm compression needs to answer a comparative question: is switching to L2-norm (and retaining FlashAttention) better than using H2O or SnapKV (and forgoing FlashAttention)? The paper provides no data to answer this. It is possible that score-based methods achieve substantially better accuracy at equivalent compression ratios, and that a user willing to accept the FlashAttention incompatibility would prefer them. It is also possible that L2-norm is competitive or superior. Without the comparison, the paper's results establish a lower bound (L2-norm is good) but not a relative ordering.

This gap is particularly relevant because:

- **Score-based methods have access to more information.** They observe the actual attention scores between the current query and cached keys — the exact signal that determines which tokens influence the output. L2-norm is a proxy for this signal. In principle, the proxy should be noisier than the signal itself. The question is how much noisier, and whether the noise matters at typical compression ratios.
- **The paper's ALR analysis (Figure 7) shows that even in high-correlation heads, L2-norm is not perfect.** At 99% compression in Layer 7 Head 10, L2-norm attention loss is ~0.008 vs. the oracle's ~0.006 — a ~33% relative gap. This gap exists at the token level and could matter for tasks requiring fine-grained attention to specific tokens. Score-based methods would not have this gap.
- **Some production deployments may not use FlashAttention** or may use variants that expose attention scores. The paper's hard constraint is valid for FlashAttention-based stacks but not universal.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The only head-to-head comparison is Figure 6, where L2-norm outperforms FlashAttention-compatible FastGen. This tells us L2-norm is better than a static heuristic (keep local, punctuation, special tokens) under the score-free constraint, but it says nothing about how L2-norm compares to *actual* FastGen (which classifies head patterns using attention scores and selects tokens adaptively). The ALR analysis (Figures 2 and 7) uses the oracle (ideal attention-score-based eviction) as a reference, implicitly quantifying the gap between L2-norm and score-based methods. In high-correlation heads, this gap is small (~0.002 attention loss at 50% compression in Figure 7a); in low-correlation heads (which are skipped), the gap is large. But ALR measures attention loss in a single next-token prediction, not downstream task performance, so it does not directly translate to the practical accuracy/capability difference between L2-norm and score-based methods.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper's framing treats the comparison as out of scope by arguing score-based methods are categorically infeasible. The limitation section does not acknowledge this as a missing comparison. A fair assessment would either provide the comparison (acknowledging it requires non-FlashAttention attention) or explicitly state that the contribution is evaluated only within the FlashAttention-compatible regime and relative performance against unconstrained methods is unknown.

### No Per-Head or Per-Layer Adaptive Compression Ratios; the Uniform Budget Across All Eligible Layers Leaves Potential Savings Unrealized

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper applies a uniform compression ratio to all layers that are not skipped (layers 2–31 by default). Every eligible layer retains the same fraction of KV pairs. This is despite the ALR analysis (Figure 2) showing that the L2-attention correlation varies substantially across layers and heads — some middle-layer heads (around layer 12) show elevated ALR values comparable to the early layers that are skipped entirely.

The paper acknowledges this in Section 8 (Limitations):

> "we observed (Figure 2) that compressing based on L2 norm can be less effective depending on the layer and head considered, and we intend to investigate per-head compression ratios to leverage this observation."

**The consequence.** A uniform budget is simultaneously too aggressive for some heads (where the L2-attention correlation is relatively weak, and compressing at the uniform rate evicts tokens that would have received non-trivial attention) and too conservative for others (where the correlation is extremely strong, and more aggressive compression would save additional memory with minimal accuracy impact). The optimal allocation would compress more aggressively in high-correlation heads and less aggressively (or not at all, as with the skip-list) in low-correlation heads.

The magnitude of this inefficiency is not measured. The paper's ablation on skipping additional middle layers (Appendix A, Figure 13; Appendix B, Figures 16a-16d) shows that skipping layer 12 in addition to layers 0 and 1 produces minimal further improvement on language modeling and needle-in-a-haystack, suggesting that the uniform budget is not catastrophically suboptimal. However, this ablation only tests the binary decision (compress at uniform rate vs. do not compress at all) — it does not test whether a *reduced* compression ratio on low-correlation heads would outperform both skipping and uniform compression by preserving important tokens that would be evicted under uniform compression while still achieving some memory savings.

More fundamentally, the uniform budget approach means the method's headline compression ratios (50%, 90%) apply only to the compressed layers. Since layers 0 and 1 are skipped entirely, the *global* compression ratio (fraction of total KV cache evicted) is strictly lower than the per-layer ratio. For a 32-layer model skipping 2 layers, a 50% per-layer compression ratio on the remaining 30 layers translates to roughly 47% global compression — a small but systematic overstatement when the headline number is used.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The ALR heatmaps (Figure 2) provide the per-head, per-layer diagnostic that would enable adaptive compression, showing substantial variation even among non-skipped layers. The attention loss curves (Figure 7) for two representative heads quantify the accuracy of L2-norm ordering at different compression ratios, revealing that even in "good" heads, L2-norm is not a perfect proxy. These analyses *motivate* per-head adaptation but the paper does not implement or evaluate it.

**Mitigation status.** Acknowledged as future work but not explored experimentally. The paper does not even report a simple variant — e.g., compressing less aggressively (but not skipping) in layers with intermediate ALR, or using the ALR values to set per-layer retention ratios — that would provide a lower bound on the benefits of adaptive allocation. The uniform budget remains an unexploited opportunity for improving the accuracy-compression tradeoff.

### The Test Sets for Synthetic Long-Context Tasks Have Unreported Sample Sizes, and No Confidence Intervals Are Reported Anywhere in the Paper

**The assumption or constraint.** Throughout the experimental section, results are reported as point estimates (accuracy percentages, perplexity values, ALR numbers) without any measure of statistical uncertainty. There are no confidence intervals, no standard deviations, no error bars, and no significance tests. For most experiments, the paper reports the number of samples over which results are averaged — 50 chunks for language modeling (Section 3, Appendix A), 1024 chunks for ALR computation (Appendix E) — but for the needle-in-a-haystack and passkey retrieval tasks, the sample sizes are not stated anywhere in the main text or appendices.

**The consequence.** The reliability of the reported results, particularly the high-precision claims ("99% accuracy at 50% compression," "100% accuracy at 90% compression"), cannot be assessed. If the needle-in-a-haystack evaluation uses, say, 100 test instances, a 99% accuracy means 99 correct out of 100 — a single additional failure would drop the reported accuracy to 98%. Without knowing the denominator, the precision of the headline numbers is uninterpretable.

This matters especially for the results that support the strongest claims:

- The passkey retrieval result (100% accuracy at 90% compression, Figure 4b) is the most dramatic finding in the paper. If this is based on a small number of test positions, the 100% could be a consequence of limited sampling rather than perfect robustness. The per-position breakdown in Appendix B (Figures 19-20) shows accuracy on the y-axis and passkey position on the x-axis, but the number of trials per position is not reported.
- The needle-in-a-haystack heatmaps in Appendix B (Figures 17, 18) show scores at different depth and context-length combinations as continuous color values, suggesting interpolation or averaging over multiple trials, but the number of trials per cell is unspecified.
- The LongBench results (Figures 5, 21, 22) use standard test splits with known sizes, which is stronger. But even here, no measure of variance across instances is reported — the average scores could mask high variance where the method works perfectly on some instances and fails catastrophically on others.

The absence of uncertainty quantification also affects the apparent precision of comparisons. When the paper states that low-norm retention at 50% compression achieves 99% accuracy vs. random at 40-50% (Section 4, needle-in-a-haystack discussion), the gap is visually obvious in Figure 4a, but without error bars, one cannot determine whether the 99% is 99 ± 0.5 or 99 ± 5.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides sample sizes for language modeling (50 chunks) and ALR computation (1024 chunks) in Appendix E. For needle-in-a-haystack and passkey retrieval, no sample sizes are reported. No experiment in the paper includes error bars, confidence intervals, or statistical tests. The ALR heatmaps (Figure 2) aggregate over 1024 chunks, which should produce relatively stable estimates, but the variability across chunks is not visualized or reported.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed or acknowledged. This is a methodological weakness that affects the strength of the paper's quantitative claims, particularly the headline compression ratio results on synthetic tasks. A standard remedy — reporting confidence intervals or at minimum stating the number of test instances — would substantially strengthen the reported results.
