# Apriel-1.5-OpenReasoner: RL Post-Training for General-Purpose and Efficient Reasoning

**ArXiv:** [2604.02007](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.02007)

## 🎯 Pitch

Training a single model to reason well across math, code, and logic usually inflates trace length, but this work shows you can simultaneously boost accuracy and shrink reasoning chains by 30–50%—without disclosing hidden recipes. The trick is a difficulty-aware length penalty that lets the model spend more tokens only on hard problems, combined with adaptive domain sampling that prevents faster tasks from dominating training.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces a fully reproducible multi-domain RL post-training recipe that trains a 15B-parameter open-weight LLM (Apriel-Base) across five public domains—mathematics, code generation, instruction following, logical puzzles, and function calling—using reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR). The work contributes two named mechanisms: **adaptive domain sampling**, which dynamically corrects for drift in the effective training mixture caused by heterogeneous rollout dynamics across domains (e.g., coding problems generate longer rollouts and complete more slowly than math problems, skewing the mixture away from configured ratios), and a **Difficulty-Aware Length Penalty**, which modulates the standard length penalty by problem difficulty so that harder problems receive a weaker penalty and are permitted to reason longer (e.g., scaling the penalty by the group solve rate, with incorrect rollouts always receiving the full penalty). Trained under a strict 16K-token output budget, Apriel-1.5-OpenReasoner generalizes to 32K tokens at inference, matches or exceeds comparable-size open-weight reasoning models on AIME 2025, GPQA, MMLU-Pro, and LiveCodeBench while producing 30–50% shorter reasoning traces—pushing the Pareto frontier of accuracy versus token budget and establishing that multi-domain RL post-training can simultaneously improve reasoning quality and inference efficiency, but only when domain balance is actively maintained and length control is adapted to problem difficulty rather than applied uniformly.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Gap: Reproducible Multi-Domain RLVR Recipes Are Missing

The paper addresses a specific and growing problem in the open-weight LLM ecosystem: while frontier models increasingly rely on **multi-domain Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards** (RLVR) to build general-purpose reasoning capabilities, the actual training recipes—domain mixtures, hyperparameter configurations, and the mechanisms for managing joint optimization across heterogeneous domains—are rarely disclosed in sufficient detail to be reproducible. The authors make this explicit in the introduction:

> "Despite their open weights, the underlying training recipes, domain mixtures, and multi-domain optimization details are often not fully disclosed. This limits reproducibility and understanding of which ingredients drive strong multi-domain reasoning performance."

This gap is not merely academic. The lack of disclosed recipes means that each new open-weight model release provides weights but no clear pathway for the community to replicate, study, or improve the training process. Researchers who want to understand *why* a model performs well on certain benchmarks—whether it is the domain mixture, the choice of RL algorithm, or a specific length-penalty strategy—have no ground truth to work from. Practitioners who want to adapt these recipes to new domains or base models are left to reverse-engineer the training pipeline from fragmentary descriptions.

The paper positions itself as a direct response to this opacity, offering what it describes as "a fully reproducible multi-domain RL post-training recipe" with "all training configurations and domain mixtures" documented. This reproducibility commitment explicitly follows the transparency principles of the LLM360 initiative (Liu et al., 2024), which advocates for complete disclosure of training data, code, and configurations—not just model weights.

### Why This Problem Matters: The Practical and Scientific Stakes

The importance of this gap operates on two levels.

**Practically**, multi-domain RLVR is the dominant post-training paradigm for reasoning models, adopted by Qwen-3.5, GLM, Kimi k2, OLMo 3, Nemotron-Cascade, and others. Each of these models must solve the same underlying challenge: training across domains with profoundly different characteristics—mathematics problems might generate short, easily-verified rollouts, while coding problems produce long rollouts requiring sandboxed execution. Without proper management, these differences silently shift the effective training mixture away from the intended ratios, biasing the model toward overrepresented domains. If the community lacks reproducible recipes, every new training run must rediscover solutions to these same problems, wasting compute and slowing progress.

**Scientifically**, there is a deeper question about *what actually matters* in multi-domain RLVR. Is the choice of policy optimization algorithm (PPO vs. GRPO vs. GSPO) critical? Or do domain balancing and length control dominate? Is it better to train domains jointly or sequentially? Without open recipes that isolate variables, the field cannot answer these questions. Each closed recipe conflates many design choices, making it impossible to attribute performance differences to specific components. By releasing a fully-documented recipe, the paper enables controlled ablation studies—their own domain mixture ablation (Table 3) is a start, showing that a balanced five-domain mixture outperforms both uniform weighting and math-code-only training, but the open pipeline enables the community to run many more such experiments.

There is also an **inference efficiency** stake. Reasoning models are notorious for generating excessively long chain-of-thought traces, a phenomenon termed "overthinking" (Chen et al., 2025). These long traces increase inference cost and latency, making models impractical for deployment even when they achieve high accuracy. The paper notes:

> "models with long chain-of-thought traces increase inference cost and latency, making efficiency critical for practical deployment."

Any RLVR recipe that improves accuracy while controlling output length therefore has direct economic implications for organizations deploying these models. The paper's explicit framing around "pushing the Pareto frontier of accuracy versus token budget" (Figure 1) reflects this practical concern.

### Existing Approaches and Their Shortcomings

The paper identifies two broad families of prior work—joint multi-domain optimization and sequential domain-wise training—and explains how each handles (or fails to handle) the practical challenges of multi-domain RLVR.

**Joint multi-domain optimization** (used by OLMo 3 and Kimi K2) trains across all domains simultaneously. This has the advantage of allowing cross-domain transfer—shared reasoning patterns (e.g., systematic verification, backtracking) can be reinforced from multiple domains at once. However, the paper identifies a concrete, underappreciated failure mode: **domain drift**. As the authors explain:

> "When training across multiple domains simultaneously, rollouts are generated asynchronously, and domains vary widely in rollout length, problem difficulty, verification latency, and sample efficiency. As a result, faster or easier domains tend to complete rollouts sooner and produce a disproportionate share of collected trajectories, shifting the training mixture away from intended domain mixture ratios."

This is not a hypothetical concern. In a system like PipelineRL, where rollout generation and training proceed concurrently using in-flight weight updates, the training mixture at any given moment depends on which domains happen to have completed rollouts ready for consumption. Mathematics problems with lightweight exact-match verification will complete much faster than coding problems requiring sandboxed execution, meaning the effective training mixture naturally drifts toward math-heavy proportions regardless of the configured domain weights. Without correction, the model receives a biased training signal that favors overrepresented domains.

The paper also notes that existing joint optimization approaches typically rely on **static domain mixtures** (OLMo 3) or do not disclose their domain balancing mechanisms at all. Static mixtures are configured once and assumed to hold throughout training, but they cannot account for the dynamic, runtime-dependent drift described above.

**Sequential domain-wise training** (used by Nemotron-Cascade and GLM) trains domains one after another rather than jointly. This avoids the domain drift problem entirely—each domain is trained in isolation, so there is no competition for rollout slots. However, the paper identifies two significant costs to this approach:

> "simplifies optimization but can limit cross-domain transfer and requires careful management of forgetting across stages"

The cross-domain transfer limitation is intuitive: if mathematics is trained first and code generation later, the model cannot reinforce general reasoning patterns (like verification) from both domains simultaneously, and may specialize differently than it would under joint training. The forgetting concern is equally practical: later training stages can overwrite capabilities acquired in earlier stages, requiring either replay mechanisms or careful checkpoint management that add complexity to the training pipeline.

**Length control mechanisms** represent a third axis of prior work with identified shortcomings. The paper catalogs several existing approaches:

- **Test-time budget forcing** (Muennighoff et al., 2025): apply a fixed token budget at inference, forcing the model to stop generating at a predetermined length. This is simple but ignores problem difficulty entirely—a complex AIME problem and a simple arithmetic check get the same budget.
- **Budget-conditioned training** (Aggarwal & Welleck, 2025): train the model to accept budget tokens during rollout generation, teaching it to respect length constraints. This adds training complexity but still applies a uniform budget.
- **Training-time penalties** (Kimi Team et al., 2025): apply a fixed penalty to long rollouts during training. This discourages verbosity but again treats all problems identically.
- **Difficulty-aware mechanisms** (Fang et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025; Zhang & Zuo, 2025; Shen et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025b): these are closer in spirit to what the paper proposes, using routing, adaptive objectives, or preference optimization to modulate length based on difficulty. But the authors argue these approaches introduce significant complexity—auxiliary models for difficulty estimation, additional training phases, or changes to the policy loss—that risks destabilizing the already-complex joint multi-domain optimization.

The authors summarize their position on prior length control work:

> "Our approach formulates length control as a difficulty-aware penalty integrated directly into the reward function, requiring no auxiliary models, no additional training phases, and no changes to the policy loss, making it straightforward to combine with multi-domain RL training where such added complexity risks destabilizing an already complex multi-domain optimization."

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper positions itself at the intersection of three tensions in contemporary RLVR research:

**1. Reproducibility vs. frontier performance.** Rather than pushing for state-of-the-art accuracy at any cost, the paper explicitly trades off some potential accuracy gains for full reproducibility. The choice of Apriel-Base as the starting point reflects this: unlike newer base models that already incorporate RL or preference optimization in their post-training pipeline (which would make it impossible to cleanly attribute downstream behavior to the RLVR recipe), Apriel-Base provides "a clean starting point for studying multi-domain RLVR at a practical model scale" (Section 5.1). The paper also chooses GSPO (Group Sequence Policy Optimization) as the policy objective over the more widely-used GRPO, citing GSPO's reduced variance from sequence-level importance ratios—a design choice justified by training stability rather than raw accuracy scores.

**2. Joint training with explicit domain balancing.** By choosing joint multi-domain optimization (following OLMo 3 and Kimi K2) rather than sequential training (Nemotron-Cascade, GLM), the paper commits to the harder problem—managing heterogeneous rollout dynamics—in exchange for potential cross-domain transfer benefits. The adaptive domain sampling mechanism is the paper's solution to the drift problem that prior joint-training approaches either ignored or handled with static mixtures. It is conceptually simple: track the ratio of completed rollouts per domain and adjust sampling probabilities to steer toward the target mixture, with clipping bounds to prevent extreme corrections. But its presence in the pipeline addresses a real failure mode that the paper argues has been underappreciated.

**3. Difficulty-aware length control with minimal complexity.** The Difficulty-Aware Length Penalty (DAP) is the paper's response to the overthinking problem. It is designed to be **incrementally simple**: it modifies only the reward function $r_i = R(x, \hat{y}_i) + \lambda P(l_i)$ by making $\lambda$ dependent on the group solve rate $s$, rather than fixed at 1. No new model components, no additional training phases, no changes to the GSPO objective. For correct rollouts, $\lambda = s^\gamma$—when $s$ is low (hard problem, few correct in the group), the penalty is weak, allowing longer reasoning. For incorrect rollouts, $\lambda = \lambda_f = 1.0$, preserving the full penalty so the model is still discouraged from generating wrong-but-long answers. This is elegant: the difficulty signal comes for free from the group-based reward structure already present in GSPO, where multiple rollouts per prompt are sampled and their relative rewards are used to compute advantages. The solve rate $s$ is simply the fraction of correct rollouts in the group, requiring no extra computation.

The paper's positioning can be summarized as: **we are not claiming to beat all models on accuracy alone (though we are competitive); we are claiming to shift the accuracy-efficiency frontier while doing so with a fully open recipe that others can replicate, study, and improve.** This is a different kind of contribution from a typical SOTA-chasing paper, and the framing around Pareto frontiers (Figure 1) and token efficiency (Table 2) reflects this consistently.

### Underlying Assumptions and Design Philosophy

Several implicit assumptions shape the paper's approach:

- **On-policy data matters.** The choice of PipelineRL as the training system reflects a bet that maintaining near-on-policy data—via in-flight weight updates where inference workers continuously receive updated weights without pausing—is important for RLVR success. This is consistent with evidence that off-policy degradation hurts RL for LLMs (Tajwar et al., 2024), but it introduces the asynchronous rollout dynamics that necessitate adaptive domain sampling in the first place. A fully synchronous system would avoid domain drift but at the cost of GPU idle time waiting for slow domains.

- **Joint training enables cross-domain transfer worth the complexity.** The paper never proves this assumption directly (no ablation comparing joint vs. sequential training is reported), but the choice to follow the joint paradigm implies belief that shared reasoning patterns—verification, backtracking, subgoal setting—benefit from simultaneous multi-domain reinforcement. The per-domain reward curves (Figure 5, Appendix C) showing no degradation across any domain provide indirect evidence that joint training is at least not harmful.

- **Length control should be difficulty-aware, not uniform.** This is the central assumption behind DAP and is argued by negative example: the fixed length penalty variant in Table 2 achieves shorter outputs than DAP (desirable) but at the cost of significantly lower accuracy (undesirable), particularly on AIME-25 where the accuracy gap is 6.6 percentage points. This supports the claim that a uniform penalty over-penalizes legitimate long reasoning on hard problems.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

This paper builds a **multi-domain reinforcement learning post-training system** that takes a pretrained 15B-parameter language model (Apriel-Base), trains it simultaneously across five diverse reasoning domains using programmatically verifiable rewards, and produces a model (Apriel-1.5-OpenReasoner) that generates more accurate answers with substantially shorter reasoning traces. The core problem it solves is that joint multi-domain RL training suffers from two practical pathologies — domain mixture drift (faster domains dominate the training data) and uniform length penalties (which over-penalize legitimate long reasoning on hard problems) — and the solution shape is two lightweight mechanisms (adaptive domain sampling and difficulty-aware length penalty) that correct these pathologies without adding model components, auxiliary training phases, or changes to the policy loss.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The system has five major components connected in an asynchronous pipeline:

1. **Domain-Weighted Sampler** — selects which domain's training data to use for the next rollout, with adaptive correction to maintain target domain ratios despite heterogeneous rollout completion speeds.
2. **Rollout Actors** — generate completions from the current policy (the model being trained), receiving in-flight weight updates so that generated data stays near-on-policy.
3. **Domain-Specific Verifiers** — compute the reward for each rollout using programmatic checks (exact match for math, sandboxed test execution for code, constraint satisfaction fraction for instruction following, task-specific logic for puzzles, function-name-and-argument validity for function calling).
4. **Difficulty-Aware Reward Computation** — combines the base reward with a length penalty whose strength is modulated by the estimated difficulty of the prompt (derived from the group solve rate), so hard problems get weaker penalties and easy problems get the standard penalty.
5. **GSPO Trainer** — consumes the (prompt, rollout, reward) tuples, computes sequence-level advantages from group-relative rewards, and updates model parameters using clipped importance-weighted policy gradients.

Information flows as follows: the sampler picks a domain → an actor draws a prompt from that domain and generates G rollouts (group size=8) under the current policy → each rollout is scored by the domain-specific verifier → the length penalty (difficulty-modulated) is added to produce the final reward → advantages are computed within the group by normalizing rewards → the trainer applies the GSPO update to the model → updated weights are sent to the actors. All stages run concurrently: actors generate while the trainer updates, with weights flowing continuously.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the formal RLVR objective and the GSPO policy optimization algorithm, since these define *what* is being optimized and *how* gradients are computed — the mathematical foundation everything else builds on.
- **Second**, the adaptive domain sampling mechanism, because it governs *which data* enters the training pipeline at each step and addresses the mismatch between configured and effective domain mixtures.
- **Third**, the five training domains and their verifiers, since these define the reward signals that drive learning and the heterogeneous rollout dynamics that necessitate adaptive sampling in the first place.
- **Fourth**, the Difficulty-Aware Length Penalty (DAP), because it modifies the reward signal to be difficulty-sensitive — building on the group-based reward structure already present in GSPO — and is the mechanism that controls reasoning length without changing the policy loss.
- **Fifth**, the complete training configuration (hyperparameters, hardware, domain mixture selection), which ties together all components into a concrete, reproducible recipe.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **systems and methods paper** whose core idea is that multi-domain RL post-training can simultaneously improve reasoning accuracy and inference efficiency, but only if two practical problems — domain mixture drift and uniform length penalties — are addressed with lightweight, integrated mechanisms rather than complex add-ons or manual tuning.

#### The RLVR Objective and GSPO Policy Optimization

The training objective in RLVR is to maximize the expected reward of responses generated by the policy (the language model) given prompts from a training distribution. This is formalized as:

$$J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}, y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot|x)} [R(x, y)]$$

where $\mathcal{D}$ is the distribution over training prompts, $\pi_\theta$ is the language model policy parameterized by weights $\theta$, $y$ is a complete response (token sequence) sampled autoregressively from $\pi_\theta$ given prompt $x$, and $R(x, y)$ is a scalar reward evaluating the correctness of $y$ for $x$.

**What it computes:** the expected reward over prompts drawn from the training mixture and responses sampled from the current policy. The expectation is over two sources of randomness: which prompt is selected (from $\mathcal{D}$) and which response the model generates (from $\pi_\theta$, which involves sampling tokens one at a time). The output is a single scalar representing average performance.

**Why this form:** this is the standard expected return objective from reinforcement learning. What makes it suitable for LLM post-training is that the reward $R(x, y)$ comes from programmatic verifiers rather than human labels, enabling large-scale training without annotation cost. The dependence on $y \sim \pi_\theta$ (on-policy sampling) is critical: the objective encourages the model to produce responses that *it can actually generate*, not just responses that look good in the training data. This is why off-policy data degrades performance (Tajwar et al., 2024) — it evaluates $\pi_\theta$ on responses it might not be able to produce.

**The paper's policy optimizer is GSPO (Group Sequence Policy Optimization).** The key insight behind GSPO is a mismatch between how standard RL algorithms compute importance weights and how rewards are assigned in RLVR. Standard algorithms like GRPO compute per-token importance ratios:

$$\frac{\pi_\theta(y_t | x, y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t | x, y_{<t})}$$

This means that each token in a response receives its own importance weight based on how much more (or less) likely that token is under the current policy versus the old policy that generated the response. However, rewards in RLVR are **sequence-level** — the verifier scores the entire response, not individual tokens. This token-level reweighting introduces high-variance gradients because individual tokens can have extreme importance ratios even when the overall response probability hasn't changed much, and it destroys the signal about which *responses* (as wholes) are better or worse.

GSPO replaces the per-token importance ratio with a **sequence-level importance ratio**:

$$s_i(\theta) = \exp\left( \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})} \right)$$

where $y_i$ is the $i$-th response in the group, $|y_i|$ is its length in tokens, $y_{i,t}$ is the $t$-th token of that response, and $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ is the policy that generated the group of responses (the "old" policy frozen at the start of the rollout generation).

**What it computes:** the exponential of the *average* log-probability ratio across all tokens in the response. The inner sum $\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}$ is the total log-importance of the response — how much more likely the entire sequence is under the current policy versus the old policy. Dividing by $|y_i|$ gives the per-token average, making sequences of different lengths comparable. Taking the exponential converts back from log-space to a multiplicative weight. The result is a single scalar per response that represents how much the current policy has shifted its probability mass toward (or away from) that response relative to the old policy.

**Why this form:** the length normalization by $\frac{1}{|y_i|}$ is the critical design choice. Without it, shorter responses would always have smaller total log-importance (simply because they have fewer tokens to accumulate ratios over), and longer responses would dominate the importance-weighted gradients regardless of their quality. The per-token average unifies the numerical range of $s_i(\theta)$ across responses of different lengths. By applying a single importance weight to all tokens in a response, GSPO aligns the unit of optimization (the response) with the unit of reward (also the response), eliminating the high-variance per-token reweighting that causes training instability in GRPO.

The full GSPO objective is:

$$J_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}, \{y_i\} \sim \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}} \left[ \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^G \min\left( s_i(\theta) \hat{A}_i, \text{clip}(s_i(\theta), 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon) \hat{A}_i \right) \right]$$

where $G$ is the group size (number of responses sampled per prompt), $\hat{A}_i$ is the advantage of response $i$ (computed by normalizing rewards within the group), $\epsilon$ is the clipping parameter, and the min and clip operations implement PPO-style trust region constraints at the sequence level.

**What it computes:** for each prompt, sample $G$ responses from the old policy, compute each response's advantage (how much better or worse its reward is compared to the group average), compute each response's sequence-level importance ratio (how much the current policy prefers that response relative to the old policy), and take a gradient step that increases the probability of high-advantage responses (up to a clipping limit) and decreases the probability of low-advantage responses. The min operation ensures that when the importance ratio moves too far from 1 (the policy changed too much), the gradient is clipped to prevent destructive updates. The expectation averages this over prompts.

**Why this form:** the clipping with min mirrors PPO's conservative policy iteration — it prevents the policy from changing so much in a single update that it destroys previously learned behavior. The asymmetric clipping (different $\epsilon_{\text{low}}$ and $\epsilon_{\text{high}}$ values, as used in this paper following DAPO (Yu et al., 2025)) allows the policy to increase probability of good responses more aggressively than it decreases probability of bad ones, reflecting the asymmetry that over-optimism (exploring new behaviors) is less harmful than over-pessimism (forgetting good behaviors).

**Advantage computation.** The paper follows the DAPO approach for computing advantages within each group. For a group of $G$ responses with rewards $r_1, ..., r_G$, the advantage of response $i$ is:

$$\hat{A}_i = \frac{r_i - \mu}{\sigma}$$

where $\mu = \frac{1}{G} \sum_{j=1}^G r_j$ is the group mean reward and $\sigma$ is the group standard deviation. This normalizes rewards to zero mean and unit variance within each group, so only *relative* quality (better or worse than the group average) drives the gradient, not absolute reward magnitude. This is important because different domains have different reward scales and different base difficulty levels — without normalization, high-reward domains would dominate the gradient regardless of their configured mixture weight.

The paper uses group size $G = 8$ and clips with $\epsilon_{\text{low}} = 3 \times 10^{-3}$ and $\epsilon_{\text{high}} = 4 \times 10^{-3}$, following the "clip-higher" asymmetric clipping from DAPO. These small epsilon values indicate that the policy is updated conservatively — the importance ratio is allowed to deviate only slightly from 1 before clipping kicks in. This is consistent with the on-policy nature of the training: since weights are updated in-flight (actors continuously receive new weights), the policy is never far from the behavior policy, so small-clip conservative updates suffice.

#### Adaptive Domain Sampling

The paper's adaptive domain sampling mechanism solves a specific operational problem in multi-domain RL training: the **effective training mixture drifts away from the configured target mixture** because different domains complete rollouts at different rates.

**The problem.** In PipelineRL, actors generate rollouts asynchronously across multiple domains. Different domains have different characteristics:
- Mathematics problems use lightweight exact-match verification, so rollouts complete quickly.
- Code generation problems require sandboxed execution against test cases, which is slower.
- Logical puzzles involve task-specific programmatic verifiers with varying computational cost.
- Instruction-following and function-calling domains have their own verification costs.

Additionally, domains differ in rollout length (code tends to produce longer outputs than math) and in sample efficiency (harder domains produce fewer correct rollouts, and zero-advantage group filtering — where groups with all identical rewards are discarded because they provide no learning signal — removes more trajectories from harder domains). Together, these effects mean that at any given point in training, the set of completed rollouts available for training does not reflect the configured domain mixture. Over many steps, the model sees more data from fast, easy domains than intended, biasing its learning.

**The mechanism.** Let $\mathcal{D}$ be the set of five domains, and let $w_d > 0$ be the configured target weight for domain $d$, where $\sum_{d \in \mathcal{D}} w_d = 1$. The target weights are static and configured before training (e.g., 0.40 for math, 0.25 for code, 0.15 for logic, 0.10 for instruction-following, 0.10 for function calling). When an actor needs to sample a new problem for a rollout, it computes:

$$\alpha_d = \text{clip}\left( \frac{w_d}{n_d / N}, 0.1, 10.0 \right)$$

where $n_d$ is the number of rollouts completed from domain $d$ so far in training, $N = \sum_{j \in \mathcal{D}} n_j$ is the total number of completed rollouts across all domains, and $n_d / N$ is the current fraction of completed rollouts that come from domain $d$.

**What $\alpha_d$ computes:** an adjustment factor that corrects for over- or under-representation. If domain $d$ is perfectly on-target (its actual completion fraction $n_d/N$ equals its target weight $w_d$), then $\alpha_d = 1$ and no correction is applied. If domain $d$ is under-represented ($n_d/N < w_d$), the ratio $w_d / (n_d/N)$ is greater than 1, so $\alpha_d > 1$, meaning domain $d$ gets up-weighted to compensate. If domain $d$ is over-represented ($n_d/N > w_d$), the ratio is less than 1, so $\alpha_d < 1$, down-weighting it. The clipping bounds $[0.1, 10.0]$ prevent extreme corrections — for example, if a domain has produced zero completions (so $n_d = 0$, making the denominator zero or near-zero), the ratio would diverge, but $\alpha_d$ is capped at 10.0. If a domain is massively over-represented (e.g., $n_d/N$ is 100× the target), $\alpha_d$ would approach 0 but is floored at 0.1 so the domain isn't fully excluded. If a domain has no completions yet ($n_d = 0$), the paper sets $\alpha_d = 10.0$ directly.

**The sampling probability for each domain is then:**

$$p_d = \frac{w_d \alpha_d}{\sum_{j \in \mathcal{D}} w_j \alpha_j}$$

This is the target weight multiplied by the adjustment factor, normalized to sum to 1 across domains.

**What $p_d$ computes:** a dynamic sampling distribution that steers the effective training mixture toward the target ratios. When a domain falls behind its target, $\alpha_d > 1$ increases its sampling probability above $w_d$, causing it to be selected more frequently and catch up. When a domain gets ahead, $\alpha_d < 1$ reduces its probability below $w_d$, giving other domains more opportunities. On average, the fraction of completed rollouts from each domain converges to $w_d$.

**Initial phase:** At the very start of training (fewer than 50 total completions), the system uses the static distribution $p_d = w_d$ rather than the adaptive formula. This avoids wild swings when $n_d$ values are tiny and $n_d/N$ estimates are noisy. Once 50 completions have accumulated, the adaptive correction activates and runs for the remainder of training.

**Once a domain is selected, a specific prompt is drawn uniformly at random from that domain's dataset.** There is no per-prompt weighting or difficulty-based filtering — all prompts within a domain have equal probability of being selected, and difficulty modulation happens downstream in the reward computation (via DAP), not in the sampling stage.

**Why this design?** The authors chose this adaptive mechanism over several alternatives:

- **Static mixtures** (as used in OLMo 3) are simpler but cannot correct for runtime-dependent drift. If coding problems take 3× longer than math problems, a static mixture of 0.25 code and 0.40 math will actually produce an effective mixture closer to 0.10 code and 0.55 math (roughly speaking), because more math rollouts complete in the same wall-clock time. Static mixtures require the practitioner to manually inflate slow-domain weights to compensate, but the correct inflation factor depends on relative rollout times, which vary with hardware, model size, and prompt distribution — making them fragile and run-specific.

- **Sequential training** (as used in Nemotron-Cascade) avoids the drift problem entirely by training domains one at a time, but the authors argue this sacrifices cross-domain transfer and introduces forgetting concerns. The adaptive sampler enables joint training without drift, capturing the benefits of both paradigms.

- **More complex adaptive schemes** (e.g., PID controllers, multi-armed bandits) would require tuning additional hyperparameters and might introduce their own instabilities. The simple ratio-based adjustment with clipping is transparent, requires no tuning beyond the clip bounds (which are set to wide defaults), and directly addresses the drift problem.

**A critical subtlety not discussed in the paper:** the adaptive sampler corrects for **rollout completion rates** but not for **zero-advantage filtering**. If a domain produces many rollouts that all have zero reward (common in hard domains early in training), those rollouts are completed and counted in $n_d$, but the resulting training groups may be filtered out (since all-reward-zero groups provide zero advantage and no gradient). This means the effective *training data* mixture (gradients actually applied) can still differ from the *rollout completion* mixture (which the sampler targets). The paper acknowledges that "zero-advantage group filtering introduces an additional imbalance" but does not describe a correction for it beyond the existing adaptive sampler, suggesting this is a known limitation.

#### The Five Training Domains and Their Verifiers

Each domain is defined by a public dataset and a programmatic verifier that produces a scalar reward. The domains were selected to cover diverse reasoning modalities — formal mathematics, code execution, natural language constraint satisfaction, symbolic logic, and structured API interaction — providing a broad test of multi-domain RLVR. Table 1 summarizes them.

**Mathematics (target weight 0.40, ~129K problems).** The training data comes from Open-Reasoner-Zero (Hu et al., 2025), which curated a collection spanning a range of difficulty levels from high-school mathematics to competition-level problems (AIME, MATH, NuminaMath, Tulu3 MATH, plus a filtered subset of OpenR1-Math-220k). The verifier extracts the model's final numerical or symbolic answer using a rule-based parser and compares it to the ground truth via exact match. The reward is binary: 1 if the extracted answer matches, 0 otherwise. This is the largest domain by both configured weight and dataset size, reflecting the centrality of mathematical reasoning in contemporary RLVR research (following DeepSeek-R1 and others).

**Code Generation (target weight 0.25, ~24K problems).** The training data comes from the TACO dataset (Li et al., 2023), containing competition-style programming problems from online judges (CodeChef, Codeforces, HackerRank, GeeksforGeeks) and prior datasets (APPS, CodeContests, Description2Code). Problems rated 'HARD' or 'VERY_HARD' by the dataset's annotations are excluded, leaving approximately 24K problems. The verifier executes the model's generated code in a sandboxed environment (SandboxFusion, from ByteDance) against provided test cases. The reward is binary: 1 only if all test cases pass, 0 otherwise. This domain is notable for its slow verification — sandboxed execution takes significantly longer than exact-match math verification — which directly contributes to the domain drift problem addressed by adaptive sampling.

**Instruction-Following (target weight 0.10, ~95K problems).** The training data comes from IF-RLVR (Pyatkin et al., 2025), containing instruction-response pairs with verifiable output constraints. Examples include: "write a summary in exactly three sentences" or "list five items without using the letter 'e'." The verifier checks each constraint programmatically and computes the reward as the fraction of constraints satisfied, producing a continuous reward between 0 and 1 (rather than binary). This domain tests a different kind of reasoning — the model must understand natural language constraints and produce output that satisfies them, which is closer to real-world deployment scenarios than formal math or code.

**Logical Puzzles (target weight 0.15, ~12K problems).** This domain adapts the INTELLECT-3 logic environment (Prime Intellect Team et al., 2025), based on SynLogic (Liu et al., 2025a). It consists of 29 puzzle types spanning symbolic deduction, constraint satisfaction, and combinatorial search: Boolean expressions, grid-based puzzles (Sudoku), arithmetic puzzles (Game of 24), and interactive logic games (Minesweeper). Problems are programmatically generated with adjustable difficulty. Rewards are computed using task-specific verifiers from the i3-logic library. The dataset is the smallest of the five (~12K problems), which matters because smaller datasets risk overfitting during extended RL training.

**Function Calling (target weight 0.10, ~4K problems).** This domain uses single-turn tasks from BFCL v4 (Patil et al., 2025). The model must generate a valid function call (function name and arguments) in response to a user query. The verifier extracts the predicted function name and arguments using Python's `ast` module and checks correctness: the function name must match exactly, and all argument values must fall within predefined valid sets (not requiring exact string matches — e.g., a date argument that is semantically correct even with different formatting might be accepted). The reward is binary: 1 for fully correct calls, 0 otherwise. This is the smallest domain (~4K problems) and the most specialized — it tests structured generation rather than free-form reasoning — but is included to demonstrate that the multi-domain recipe can incorporate tool-use capabilities.

**Domain mixture selection.** The target weights (0.40/0.25/0.15/0.10/0.10) were not chosen theoretically but empirically: the authors trained on several candidate mixtures (listed in Table 5, Appendix B) and selected the one that achieved the best mean validation accuracy across the five domains on held-out validation sets (10% of training data). The candidate mixtures included a uniform mixture (equal weight to all five), a math-and-code-only mixture (50/50 math/code, excluding the other three domains), and four variants with different ratios. The selected mixture heavily weights math and code (65% combined), which is consistent with prior work showing these domains are most effective for building general reasoning capabilities, while still including smaller weights for instruction-following, logic, and function calling to prevent catastrophic specialization.

**A design choice worth noting:** the paper never trains on instruction-following or function-calling alone to isolate their contribution. The domain mixture ablation (Table 3) compares the full five-domain mixture against uniform and math-code-only, but there is no "add instruction-following to math-code" step — it's all-or-nothing per domain. This means the evidence for including these domains is that the full mixture outperforms math-code-only, but we cannot distinguish whether instruction-following, logic, or function calling is driving the improvement, or whether all three are necessary.

#### Difficulty-Aware Length Penalty (DAP)

The DAP mechanism addresses a specific tension in RLVR training: **reasoning models naturally generate long chain-of-thought traces**, which improves accuracy on hard problems but wastes tokens on easy ones, and a uniform length penalty (applied equally to all problems) trades off this tension poorly — it shortens all outputs uniformly, improving efficiency but degrading accuracy on problems that genuinely need long reasoning.

**The standard length penalty.** Before DAP, the paper uses a standard linear length penalty that applies within a buffer zone before the maximum output length. Let $L$ be the maximum output length (16,000 tokens during training), $B$ be the buffer width, and $l_i$ be the length (in tokens) of rollout $\hat{y}_i$. The penalty is:

$$P(l_i) = \begin{cases} \frac{(L - B) - l_i}{B}, & L - B < l_i \leq L \\ 0, & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

where $L$ is the maximum output length (16,000 tokens), $B$ is the buffer width (2,000 tokens from the hyperparameter table), and $l_i$ is the length of rollout $i$ in tokens.

**What $P(l_i)$ computes:** a penalty that is zero for rollouts shorter than $L - B$ (14,000 tokens when $L=16,000$ and $B=2,000$), then increases linearly from 0 to 1 as the rollout length approaches $L$. A rollout at exactly $L$ tokens gets penalty 1. Rollouts beyond $L$ are truncated and receive the full penalty. The linear ramp means the model is encouraged to stay below 14,000 tokens (no penalty zone) and increasingly discouraged from approaching the 16,000-token limit.

**Why this form:** the buffer zone avoids penalizing moderately long rollouts that are within the model's natural generation range, focusing the penalty only on rollouts that are pushing against the budget. This is gentler than a flat penalty on all rollouts (which would universally discourage long reasoning) or a hard truncation at $L$ (which provides no gradient to teach the model to stay below the limit). The linear ramp provides a continuous, differentiable signal — a rollout at 15,500 tokens gets penalty 0.75, while a rollout at 15,000 gets 0.50, so the model can learn to gradually shorten without a cliff.

**The combined reward with standard penalty is:**

$$r_i = R(x, \hat{y}_i) + \lambda P(l_i)$$

where $R(x, \hat{y}_i)$ is the task reward from the domain-specific verifier (0 or 1 for math/code/logic/function calling; 0 to 1 continuous for instruction-following), and $\lambda$ is the penalty strength. In the standard setting, $\lambda = 1$ for all rollouts — all problems get the same penalty.

**The problem with $\lambda = 1$ everywhere.** On a hard AIME problem where the model needs 15,500 tokens to work through a complex solution, a 0.75 penalty on a correct answer reduces the reward from 1.0 to 1.75 (if positive reward minus positive penalty — wait, the penalty is added, so 1.0 - 0.75 = 0.25 for a correct-but-long answer. Actually, the paper writes $r_i = R(x, \hat{y}_i) + \lambda P(l_i)$, with the penalty *added*, and since $P(l_i)$ is defined as $(L-B-l_i)/B$, a longer rollout makes this term more negative, so it effectively subtracts). This means a correct answer at 15,500 tokens gets substantially lower reward than a correct answer at 13,000 tokens, potentially discouraging the model from producing long solutions even when they're necessary. Conversely, on an easy problem where the model could answer in 5,000 tokens, the penalty provides no signal at all (since $P(l_i) = 0$ for $l_i < L - B$), so the model has no incentive to be concise.

**DAP modulates $\lambda$ by difficulty.** The key idea is to estimate problem difficulty from the **group solve rate** — the fraction of rollouts in the group that are correct — and use this to scale $\lambda$. For a prompt $x$ with $N$ rollouts in the group (where $N = G = 8$), compute:

$$s = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N c_i$$

where $c_i = \mathbb{1}[R(x, \hat{y}_i) = 1]$ is an indicator for whether rollout $i$ is correct. $s \in [0, 1]$ is the solve rate — the fraction of the group that answered correctly.

**What $s$ computes:** an empirical difficulty estimate that comes for free from the group-based sampling already done for GSPO advantage computation. If $s$ is close to 1 (most rollouts correct), the problem is easy. If $s$ is close to 0 (few or no correct rollouts), the problem is hard. This is an **on-policy** difficulty estimate — it reflects the current policy's capability, not a static problem difficulty label — so it naturally adapts as the model improves during training.

**DAP sets $\lambda$ as follows:**

$$\lambda = \begin{cases} 1, & l_i \geq L \land \neg \text{finished}_i \quad \text{(upper-bound guard)} \\ s^\gamma, & c_i = 1 \\ \lambda_f, & c_i = 0 \end{cases}$$

where $s \in [0, 1]$ is the group solve rate, $\gamma \geq 0$ is a constant exponent, $\lambda_f \in [0, 1]$ is a fixed scaling factor for incorrect rollouts, $l_i$ is the rollout length, $L = 16,000$ is the maximum output length, and $\text{finished}_i$ indicates whether the rollout reached a natural end-of-sequence token (as opposed to being truncated at the budget).

**What this computes:** three cases:
- **Upper-bound guard:** if a rollout is truncated at the maximum length without finishing naturally, it always receives the full penalty ($\lambda = 1$). This prevents degenerate solutions where the model exhausts the budget on purpose, since truncated rollouts are penalized regardless of difficulty or correctness.
- **Correct rollouts ($c_i = 1$):** the penalty strength is scaled by $s^\gamma$. When $s$ is low (hard problem, few correct answers), $s^\gamma$ is small, so $\lambda$ is small — the penalty is relaxed. When $s$ is high (easy problem, most answers correct), $s^\gamma$ is close to 1, so $\lambda$ is close to 1 — the standard penalty applies. At $\gamma = 1.0$ (the paper's setting), the scaling is linear: if 2 out of 8 rollouts are correct ($s = 0.25$), the penalty is reduced to 25% of its standard strength.
- **Incorrect rollouts ($c_i = 0$):** always receive the fixed penalty strength $\lambda_f$, regardless of the group solve rate. With $\lambda_f = 1.0$ (the paper's setting), incorrect rollouts get the full standard penalty. This ensures the model is always discouraged from producing long *wrong* answers, even on hard problems.

**A concrete example:** Suppose a group of 8 rollouts for a hard AIME problem has 1 correct answer and 7 incorrect ($s = 0.125$). For the correct rollout at 15,000 tokens ($P = 0.5$), DAP sets $\lambda = 0.125^{1.0} = 0.125$, so the penalty is $0.125 \times 0.5 = 0.0625$, and the reward is $1.0 - 0.0625 = 0.9375$. For the incorrect rollouts, $\lambda = 1.0$, so a 15,000-token incorrect rollout gets penalty $1.0 \times 0.5 = 0.5$ and reward $0.0 - 0.5 = -0.5$. The model is strongly rewarded for the correct-but-long answer and strongly penalized for incorrect-but-long answers, preserving the incentive to get the answer right while relaxing the pressure to be short. On an easy problem with $s = 1.0$ (all correct), a 15,000-token rollout gets $\lambda = 1.0^1 = 1.0$, the full penalty of 0.5, and reward $1.0 - 0.5 = 0.5$ — the model is incentivized to be more concise.

**Why this form?** DAP is designed with several constraints in mind:

- **No additional training overhead.** The solve rate $s$ is computed from the group data already collected for GSPO advantage computation. No auxiliary model, no additional forward passes, no separate difficulty estimation phase. This is critical for integrating with multi-domain RL training, where adding complexity risks destabilization.
- **No changes to the policy loss.** DAP modifies only the reward $r_i$ (upstream of the advantage computation), not the GSPO objective, clipping, or gradient computation. This means it is orthogonal to the choice of RL algorithm and could be combined with PPO, GRPO, or REINFORCE without modification.
- **Automatic adaptation to improving capability.** Because $s$ is computed from on-policy rollouts, it reflects the current model's skill level. A problem that starts hard (low $s$) and becomes easy as the model improves (high $s$) will automatically transition from relaxed penalty to standard penalty, so the model learns to be concise on problems it has mastered while remaining permissive on problems it is still learning.
- **Incorrect rollouts always penalized fully.** Setting $\lambda_f = 1.0$ ensures the model cannot exploit the relaxed penalty to generate long, confident-sounding but wrong answers — the penalty for wrong answers is always the standard one, regardless of difficulty. This is an important guard against reward hacking, where the model might learn to generate very long outputs on hard problems to appear "thoughtful" without actually solving them.
- **Upper-bound guard against truncation gaming.** The guard that forces $\lambda = 1$ for truncated rollouts prevents the model from learning to hit the token limit on purpose (e.g., generating padding or repetitive content to reach the budget and then stopping, which would not be penalized under a solve-rate-scaled $\lambda$).

**Hyperparameter settings.** The paper uses $\gamma = 1.0$, $\lambda_f = 1.0$, and $B = 2000$ tokens. These were selected from a sweep over $(B, \gamma, \lambda_f) \in \{(2000, 1.0, 1.0), (2000, 0.5, 0.5)\}$ (Table 5, Appendix B). The $B = 2000$ buffer width means rollouts shorter than 14,000 tokens (with $L = 16,000$) incur zero penalty — about 87.5% of the budget is penalty-free. The choice of $\gamma = 1.0$ (linear solve-rate scaling) over $\gamma = 0.5$ (square-root scaling, which would apply a stronger penalty even on hard problems) suggests that the authors found more aggressive relaxation (allowing harder problems to reason much longer) to be beneficial. The choice of $\lambda_f = 1.0$ over $\lambda_f = 0.5$ means incorrect rollouts get the full standard penalty, not a reduced one — maximizing the penalty gap between correct and incorrect answers.

**Interaction with zero-advantage filtering.** A subtle interaction: if all 8 rollouts in a group are correct ($s = 1.0$) and all are short enough to avoid the buffer zone ($l_i < 14,000$), then the group has zero reward variance ($r_i = 1.0$ for all $i$), and the group is filtered out (discarded) because it provides no learning signal. This is correct behavior — if a problem is trivially easy for the current policy, training on it provides no improvement signal. If some rollouts are correct and some incorrect, the group passes filtering and DAP modulates penalties as described. If all rollouts are incorrect ($s = 0$), then DAP applies the standard penalty to all, and the group may still provide a signal if some incorrect answers are closer to correct than others (e.g., partial credit for instruction-following where reward is continuous), or it may be filtered out if all get the same reward. The interaction is that DAP and zero-advantage filtering both depend on group reward statistics, but DAP uses the statistics to modulate penalties while filtering uses them to discard uninformative groups — they are complementary rather than conflicting.

#### Complete Training Configuration

The paper provides a detailed training configuration to enable reproducibility. All jobs ran on 8 compute nodes, each with 8 NVIDIA H100 GPUs (64 GPUs total). Per node, 4 GPUs are allocated to rollout generation (actors) and 4 to training.

**Training duration and checkpoint selection.** The model trains for 250 optimization steps, determined by training for 400 steps total and evaluating checkpoints on held-out validation sets (10% of training data) every 50 steps starting at step 150. Step 250 yielded the best mean validation accuracy across the five domains. At 36 wall-clock hours for 250 steps, each optimization step takes approximately 8.6 minutes.

**Output budget and length generalization.** The model is trained with a strict 16,000-token output budget ($L_{\text{out}} = 16\text{k}$) but evaluated at inference with a 32,000-token budget. This length generalization — performing well at test time with double the training-time token budget — is a notable finding that the paper attributes to RLVR teaching general reasoning strategies rather than budget-specific heuristics. The 16K training budget was chosen from a sweep over $\{16\text{k}, 20\text{k}, 24\text{k}\}$ (Table 5, Appendix B).

**Policy optimizer and clipping.** The training uses GSPO with the "clip-higher" asymmetric clipping from DAPO (Yu et al., 2025): $\epsilon_{\text{low}} = 3 \times 10^{-3}$ and $\epsilon_{\text{high}} = 4 \times 10^{-3}$. Group size $G = 8$. Learning rate $1.25 \times 10^{-6}$, swept over $\{7.5 \times 10^{-7}, 1.0 \times 10^{-6}, 1.25 \times 10^{-6}, 2.5 \times 10^{-6}, 5.0 \times 10^{-6}\}$.

**Numerical precision.** Following Khatri et al. (2025), the final vocabulary projection (lm_head) is computed in FP32 on both actors and training workers for numerical stability, while the rest of the model remains in BF16. This is a practical fix for a known issue: the large vocabulary projection matrix can accumulate significant numerical error in BF16 during gradient updates, and switching it to FP32 prevents instability without substantially increasing memory usage (since it's only one matrix).

**On-policy data flow.** PipelineRL enables in-flight weight updates: inference workers continuously receive updated model weights without pausing generation. This keeps data near-on-policy (responses are generated by recent but not necessarily the absolute latest weights) while maintaining high GPU utilization, since there is no synchronization barrier where all actors must stop while the trainer updates. The world fractions configuration (4/0/4 — 4 actor GPUs, 0 preprocessor GPUs, 4 trainer GPUs per node) reflects a balanced allocation between generation and training.

**Why PipelineRL?** The paper chose PipelineRL for its specific throughput-freshness trade-off. Alternatives include:
- Synchronous systems (generate a batch of rollouts with frozen weights, train on them, update weights, repeat): perfectly on-policy but GPU-idle during the generation phase.
- Fully asynchronous systems (actors generate continuously with whatever weights they have, trainer trains on whatever rollouts are available): maximum GPU utilization but policy staleness can degrade learning (the model trains on data generated by arbitrarily old policies).
- PipelineRL's in-flight updates (actors get updated weights asynchronously, without a generation pause): a middle ground that the authors argue is sufficient for near-on-policy learning while maintaining high throughput.

**Domain mixture and hyperparameter selection.** All hyperparameters (learning rate, clipping values, domain rates, DAP parameters, output budget) were selected via grid search over the candidate sets in Table 5 (Appendix B), using held-out validation accuracy as the selection criterion. The selected configuration is the one that achieved the best mean validation performance across domains. This means the paper's reported test-set results (Table 2) are not contaminated by hyperparameter overfitting to the test benchmarks — the selection used separate validation data.

**Training seeds.** The paper reports results averaged across two independent training seeds to reduce variance. This is important because RL training can be sensitive to random initialization and rollout stochasticity, and single-seed results may overstate or understate true performance.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Adaptive Domain Sampling Identifies and Solves a Previously Unarticulated Failure Mode in Joint Multi-Domain RL

The field's standard approaches to multi-domain RLVR fall into two camps: joint training with static domain mixtures (OLMo 3) or sequential domain-wise training to avoid mixture drift entirely (Nemotron-Cascade, GLM). The paper's adaptive domain sampler is not just an engineering fix — it **names and diagnoses a specific pathology** that prior joint-training work either ignored or managed with brittle manual tuning. The pathology is that *effective training mixture drifts away from configured ratios* because domains complete rollouts at different rates, and this drift is dynamic (depends on current model capability, hardware, and problem distribution) rather than static (knowable in advance and correctable via fixed inflation factors).

What makes this contribution conceptually interesting is that the paper establishes domain drift as a *first-class optimization problem* within RL post-training, not an implementation detail. The observation that "faster or easier domains tend to complete rollouts sooner and produce a disproportionate share of collected trajectories" (Section 4.1) may seem obvious in retrospect, but it was not previously documented as a systematic source of training bias in joint multi-domain RL pipelines. The paper's evidence that domain mixture matters — the full five-domain mixture outperforms both uniform weighting and math-code-only training across all four benchmarks (Table 3) — substantiates the claim that preserving target ratios is genuinely load-bearing, not cosmetic.

The adaptive correction itself ($\alpha_d = \text{clip}(w_d / (n_d/N), 0.1, 10.0)$) is deliberately simple, and that simplicity is the point. Prior approaches to handling domain imbalance in multi-task learning (gradient surgery, dynamic loss weighting, uncertainty-based weighting) introduce auxiliary optimization objectives and hyperparameters that interact unpredictably with the RL policy loss. The paper demonstrates that tracking rollout completion counts — a statistic already available in any asynchronous RL system — and using them to adjust sampling probabilities is sufficient to correct the drift. This is a **negative result of sorts**: sophisticated domain balancing mechanisms may be unnecessary. The contribution is partly the demonstration that a lightweight proportional controller works, and partly the argument that *any* active correction is necessary in the first place.

The connection to the broader reproducibility agenda matters here. Static mixtures are a reproducibility trap: "reproducing" a paper's configured domain ratios produces a different effective mixture on different hardware or at different model scales because relative domain completion speeds change. Adaptive sampling decouples the *stated recipe* from the *runtime dynamics*, enabling genuinely reproducible training runs.

### Innovation 2: Difficulty-Aware Length Penalty as a Principled Unification of Length Control and Difficulty Estimation

The paper's Difficulty-Aware Length Penalty makes a sharp conceptual move that distinguishes it from prior length-control work: it **co-opts the group-based advantage computation already present in GSPO as a free difficulty signal**, rather than building a separate difficulty estimation pipeline. This matters because prior difficulty-aware length control methods (Fang et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025; Zhang & Zuo, 2025; Shen et al., 2025) introduce auxiliary models, separate training phases, or modifications to the policy loss — each of which is a potential failure point in the already-complex multi-domain RL setting.

The intellectual contribution is the recognition that $s = \frac{1}{G} \sum c_i$, the fraction of correct rollouts in a group, is simultaneously a difficulty estimate (low $s$ means the problem is hard for the current policy) *and* a reward-modulation signal (low $s$ means correct answers should be penalized less for length). This dual-use of existing computation is not obvious a priori: one could view the group solve rate merely as a normalization statistic for advantage computation. Repurposing it as a difficulty signal that modulates length penalties is a reframing that costs nothing in additional compute.

The **asymmetry of the penalty** — correct rollouts on hard problems get relaxed penalties ($\lambda = s^\gamma$, small when $s$ is small), while incorrect rollouts always get the full penalty ($\lambda_f = 1.0$) — encodes an implicit hypothesis about what makes reasoning efficient. The hypothesis is: *the model should learn to be concise on problems it has mastered (high solve rate, standard penalty) but permissive on problems it is still learning (low solve rate, relaxed penalty for correct answers only).* By never relaxing the penalty for incorrect answers, DAP prevents the model from exploiting the relaxed regime to generate long, confident-sounding wrong answers — a form of reward hacking that uniform-length-penalty approaches would not prevent.

The ablation evidence (last two rows of Table 2) makes the case concretely: the standard length penalty produces shorter outputs (desirable) but at a 6.6% accuracy cost on AIME-25 (undesirable). DAP recovers this accuracy with a negligible 2% token increase and adds 28% more tokens on GPQA and MMLU-Pro but with only 0.9% and 1.7% accuracy gains. This is not a simple "more tokens → more accuracy" story — the standard penalty *overshoots* on hard problems (penalizing necessary reasoning) while DAP *allocates* tokens where they matter. The distinction between correlation (longer outputs sometimes accompany higher accuracy) and causation (difficulty-adaptive penalties enable longer reasoning *specifically on problems that need it*) is the paper's conceptual advance.

The **absence of an auxiliary model** is itself a substantive design claim. The paper argues implicitly that for multi-domain RL, the most robust difficulty signal is on-policy (reflecting the current model's actual capability) rather than static (precomputed problem difficulty labels) or model-based (a separate difficulty predictor). On-policy difficulty estimates automatically adapt as the model improves — a problem that starts hard and becomes easy will transition from relaxed to standard penalty without any intervention. This is not a new idea (curriculum learning has used on-policy difficulty estimates), but its integration into a length penalty via the solve rate — requiring zero additional forward passes, zero auxiliary models, and zero policy-loss changes — is what makes it a practical advance rather than a theoretical curiosity.

### Innovation 3: Reproducibility as a First-Class Research Contribution in RL Post-Training

The paper's most unconventional contribution is treating **full reproducibility** — releasing all training configurations, domain mixtures, hyperparameters, and code — as a primary research output on par with accuracy scores. This is not an engineering footnote; it is a deliberate intellectual positioning that challenges how the field evaluates RL post-training work.

The status quo that the paper critiques is specific: frontier open-weight models (Qwen-3.5, GLM, Kimi k2, OLMo 3, Nemotron-Cascade) release weights but "the underlying training recipes, domain mixtures, and multi-domain optimization details are often not fully disclosed" (Section 1). The consequence is a field where each new model release is a point measurement — high accuracy on benchmarks — with no ability to attribute that accuracy to specific design choices (domain mixture, RL algorithm, length penalty, training duration). Progress becomes a function of who has the most compute and the best-guess configuration, not of accumulated knowledge about what works and why.

By releasing the full recipe alongside the model weights, the paper **converts a black-box system into an experimental platform**. The domain mixture ablation (Table 3) is an example of the kind of experiment this enables, but it's only a start. The paper's release enables the community to run controlled experiments that the original authors did not: Does DAP help more with GSPO than with other policy optimizers? Does adaptive sampling matter less when using sequential domain training? Does the 16K training budget generalize to 64K at inference? These are questions that simply cannot be asked of closed-recipe models.

This contribution is **methodological rather than algorithmic**. The paper is arguing, by example, that reproducibility standards in RL post-training should be raised to match those in pretraining (where LLM360 and similar initiatives advocate full transparency of training data, code, and configurations). The argument carries weight because the paper demonstrates that a fully open recipe can be competitive — Apriel-1.5-OpenReasoner matches or exceeds comparable-size models on accuracy while using substantially fewer tokens (Table 2) — so the trade-off between openness and performance is not as steep as the field has implicitly assumed.

A subtle aspect of this contribution is the **choice of base model**. By selecting Apriel-Base — a model explicitly trained without RL or preference optimization, with fully documented pretraining and instruction-tuning data — the paper ensures that any downstream behavioral changes can be confidently attributed to the RLVR recipe. This is in contrast to training on a base model like Qwen3, which already incorporates undisclosed RL and preference optimization in its post-training, making it impossible to isolate the effect of any new recipe. The paper thus makes a causal claim (RLVR causes these improvements) that would be confounded on a less transparent base model, and this causal clarity is part of the reproducibility contribution.

### Innovation 4: Joint Training Without Catastrophic Interference as an Existence Proof

The paper provides an **existence proof** that five diverse reasoning domains can be trained jointly via RLVR without any domain experiencing reward degradation or negative transfer. The per-domain reward curves (Figure 5, Appendix C) show all five domains exhibiting clear upward trends over 250 optimization steps, with no domain's reward regressing. This is a non-trivial result: joint multi-task RL is known to be difficult due to conflicting gradients, interference between task-specific and shared representations, and the tendency for high-reward or fast-converging domains to dominate the optimization.

What makes this an insight rather than just a training curve is what it implies about the *structure of reasoning*. If the five domains — mathematics (formal symbolic manipulation), code generation (algorithmic implementation), logical puzzles (constraint satisfaction and search), instruction-following (natural language constraint interpretation), and function calling (structured API interaction) — can be trained jointly without interference, it suggests that **the underlying reasoning capabilities reinforced by RLVR (verification, backtracking, subgoal setting, systematic step-by-step deduction) transfer across these superficially different domains**. The paper does not prove this directly — there is no experiment showing that training on math improves code generation — but the absence of negative transfer is a necessary condition for the cross-domain transfer hypothesis, and establishing that condition is a contribution.

The reasoning trace analysis (Figure 6, Appendix D) provides indirect support. The shift from 21% non-productive steps in Apriel-Base to 14% in Apriel-1.5-OpenReasoner, and the increase in non-linear reasoning behaviors from 11% to 17%, cannot be attributed to any single domain — the three smaller domains (logic, instruction-following, function calling) constitute only 35% of the training mixture. The efficiency gains must therefore arise from reasoning patterns reinforced across multiple domains simultaneously, consistent with the joint-training hypothesis.

This contribution is conceptually distinct from the paper's main claims about adaptive sampling and DAP. Those are *mechanisms for managing joint training*; this is **evidence that joint training works at all**, which was not guaranteed a priori. The field has seen both successful joint training (OLMo 3) and successful sequential training (Nemotron-Cascade), with no published comparison establishing which paradigm is superior. The paper does not provide that comparison either — there is no sequential-training baseline — but it does establish a clean datapoint: with the right domain balancing and length control, five-domain joint RLVR is stable and effective. This shifts the default assumption from "joint training is risky and requires careful management" toward "joint training is viable and the management mechanisms are known."

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Datasets.** The paper evaluates on four external held-out reasoning benchmarks, none of which appear in the training data: **AIME 2025** (30 competition-level math problems; Balunović et al., 2025), **GPQA** (198 graduate-level science questions; Rein et al., 2024), **MMLU-Pro** (12,032 multi-domain knowledge and reasoning questions; Wang et al., 2024), and **LiveCodeBench v5** (315 coding problems from July–December 2024; Jain et al., 2025). These span mathematics, science, general knowledge, and code generation, providing coverage across reasoning modalities without overlapping with the five training domains' data sources.

- **Base models.** The primary model is **Apriel-Base** (Apriel-1.5-15b-Thinker), a 15B-parameter decoder-only LLM initialized from Pixtral-12B via depth upscaling, then continually pretrained and instruction-tuned with explicit reasoning traces (Radhakrishna et al., 2025). It was chosen specifically because it has *not* undergone RL or preference optimization, making downstream behavioral changes attributable to the RLVR recipe. For the FLOPs-matched comparison and baseline benchmarking, three comparable-scale open-weight reasoning models are used: **Nemotron-Cascade-14B-Thinking** (14B, trained via sequential domain-wise RL; Wang et al., 2025a), **Qwen3-14B** (14B dense, multi-stage SFT + RL post-training; Yang et al., 2025), and **Phi-4-reasoning** (14B, SFT on o3-mini traces followed by RLVR; Abdin et al., 2025).

- **Metrics.** The primary metric is **pass@1 accuracy** — the fraction of problems for which a single sampled answer matches the ground truth — averaged across problems, then across multiple evaluation runs (with different sampling seeds), and finally across two independent training seeds. The secondary metric is **average output tokens per completion**, measured as the mean number of generated tokens before the end-of-sequence token or the 32K budget is reached. Together, these capture the accuracy-efficiency Pareto frontier that the paper seeks to push.

- **Baselines.** Four baselines are compared in Table 2: **Apriel-Base** (the pretrained model before any RLVR, establishing the starting point); **Apriel-Base + RLVR with standard length penalty** (identical training recipe but with uniform λ = 1 for all rollouts, isolating the effect of DAP); and three external models of comparable scale — **Phi-4-reasoning** (14B), **Qwen3** (14B), and **Nemotron-Cascade** (14B). All baselines are evaluated under identical conditions (temperature 0.6, top-p 0.95, 32K max output tokens) to enable fair comparison.

- **Generation budget.** The primary axis of comparison in the main results is the **32K-token output budget** at inference, matching the evaluation protocol used by Guo et al. (2025) for DeepSeek-R1. All models are evaluated under this identical per-problem token cap. The paper also reports training-time budgets (16K maximum output tokens during RL post-training) and notes that the model generalizes beyond this training constraint to the 32K evaluation budget — a length generalization result that is reported but not systematically studied.

- **Statistical protocol.** To reduce variance from sampling stochasticity, evaluation is repeated multiple times per benchmark with different random seeds: **10 runs** for AIME 2025 (30 problems), **5 runs** for GPQA (198 problems), **3 runs** for LiveCodeBench v5 (315 problems), and **1 run** for MMLU-Pro (12,032 problems, where variance is inherently lower due to size). Per-problem accuracy is first averaged within each run, then across runs, and finally across two independent training seeds (two complete training runs from different random initializations). The paper reports mean accuracy and, for the domain mixture ablation (Table 3), standard deviations across evaluation runs for AIME-25, GPQA, and LCB (MMLU-Pro standard deviations are unavailable due to single evaluation).

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Accuracy–Efficiency Trade-off Across Benchmarks

The central result is that Apriel-1.5-OpenReasoner achieves accuracy comparable to or exceeding all same-scale baselines while generating substantially fewer output tokens — i.e., it strictly dominates on the accuracy-efficiency Pareto frontier (Table 2, Figure 1).

**AIME 2025.** Apriel-1.5-OpenReasoner reaches **78.3% accuracy with 11.3K mean output tokens**, the highest accuracy among all models. Nemotron-Cascade achieves 76.0% with 19.0K tokens (41% more tokens for 2.3% lower accuracy); Qwen3 achieves 68.0% with 16.9K tokens; Phi-4-reasoning achieves 57.3% with 12.5K tokens. Relative to Apriel-Base (73.3% with 16.6K tokens), RLVR with DAP improves accuracy by 5.0 percentage points while reducing token count by ~32%.

**GPQA.** Apriel-1.5-OpenReasoner achieves **69.8% with 5.8K tokens**, again the highest accuracy. Nemotron-Cascade scores 68.4% with 10.6K tokens (83% more tokens for 1.4% lower accuracy); Qwen3 scores 64.3% with 6.7K tokens; Phi-4-reasoning scores 64.8% with 3.5K tokens (highest efficiency but substantially lower accuracy). Relative to Apriel-Base (68.8% with 10.5K tokens), accuracy is roughly preserved (+1.0 point on an absolute basis) while token count drops by ~45%.

**MMLU-Pro.** All models cluster within a narrow accuracy band: 75.6–77.7% (a 2.1 percentage point spread). Apriel-1.5-OpenReasoner scores **77.3% with 1.9K tokens**, second only to Qwen3 (77.7% with 2.4K tokens) but using 21% fewer tokens. Nemotron-Cascade scores 76.8% with 3.6K tokens; Phi-4-reasoning scores 77.1% with 3.4K tokens. Relative to Apriel-Base (76.4% with 3.5K tokens), token count drops by ~46%.

**LiveCodeBench v5.** Apriel-1.5-OpenReasoner achieves **70.8% with 7.4K tokens**, essentially tied with Nemotron-Cascade (70.7% with 16.0K tokens) while generating **less than half** the tokens. Qwen3 scores 65.9% with 12.2K tokens; Phi-4-reasoning scores 56.9% with 14.4K tokens. Relative to Apriel-Base (61.4% with 14.9K tokens), accuracy improves by 9.4 percentage points with a 50% token reduction — the largest joint improvement across any benchmark.

**The Pareto interpretation (Figure 1).** The scatter plot in Figure 1 visualizes each model as a point in (mean output tokens, accuracy) space for each benchmark. Apriel-1.5-OpenReasoner appears in the **upper-left region** (higher accuracy, fewer tokens) relative to all baselines across all four benchmarks, with particularly dramatic gaps against Nemotron-Cascade on GPQA and LiveCodeBench. The implicit claim is that this point is not on the previous Pareto frontier — i.e., to achieve comparable accuracy with prior models, you would need to accept substantially higher token cost, and to achieve comparable token cost, you would need to accept lower accuracy.

---

#### Impact of the Difficulty-Aware Length Penalty vs. Standard Length Penalty

The last two rows of Table 2 isolate the effect of DAP by comparing against an identical training run that uses the standard (uniform) length penalty.

**Accuracy recovery.** Relative to the standard length penalty, DAP improves accuracy on all four benchmarks: +6.6 percentage points on AIME-25 (71.7% → 78.3%), +0.9 on GPQA (68.9% → 69.8%), +1.7 on MMLU-Pro (75.6% → 77.3%), and +3.1 on LiveCodeBench (67.7% → 70.8%).

**Token cost.** This accuracy improvement comes with a modest increase in output tokens: +2% on AIME-25 (11.1K → 11.3K), +6% on LiveCodeBench (7.0K → 7.4K), and approximately +28% on GPQA (4.5K → 5.8K) and MMLU-Pro (1.5K → 1.9K). The authors argue this represents an efficient allocation: DAP spends additional tokens where they are most valuable (hard problems), rather than uniformly shortening all outputs.

**Interpretation.** The standard penalty *overshoots* — it produces shorter traces everywhere but at the cost of accuracy on benchmarks where long reasoning is genuinely necessary (especially AIME-25). DAP recovers this accuracy with a small token budget increase on math/code benchmarks and a larger increase on knowledge benchmarks. This pattern is consistent with the hypothesis that AIME-25 and LiveCodeBench contain a wider range of problem difficulties, making difficulty-aware allocation more impactful, whereas GPQA and MMLU-Pro problems are more uniformly moderate, so relaxing the penalty adds tokens without dramatic accuracy gains.

---

#### Domain Mixture Ablation

Table 3 compares three training domain mixtures, all using identical hyperparameters (including DAP): the paper's selected mixture (M/C/L/I/F = 0.40/0.25/0.15/0.10/0.10), a uniform mixture (all five domains equally weighted at 0.20 each), and a math-and-code-only mixture (0.50/0.50 mathematics and code, excluding instruction-following, logic, and function calling).

**The selected mixture achieves the highest accuracy on all four benchmarks while maintaining competitive output length.** On AIME-25, the selected mixture reaches 78.3% (±2.8% std) vs. 75.7% (±5.2%) for math-code-only and 75.0% (±5.5%) for uniform. On GPQA, 69.8% (±2.4%) vs. 69.1% (±1.7%) vs. 67.7% (±2.1%). On MMLU-Pro, 77.3% vs. 75.9% vs. 76.1% (no standard deviations — single evaluation). On LiveCodeBench, 70.8% (±1.5%) vs. 67.2% (±1.1%) vs. 70.5% (±0.5%).

**The math-code-only mixture is competitive on AIME-25 and GPQA but drops notably on LiveCodeBench** (67.2% vs. 70.8%), which the authors interpret as evidence that the three excluded domains (logic, instruction-following, function calling) contribute meaningfully to code generation performance. This is a non-obvious finding: code generation is superficially similar to mathematics, but apparently benefits from training on instruction-following (which teaches constraint satisfaction) and/or logical puzzles (which teach systematic search).

**The uniform mixture underperforms the selected mixture on accuracy across all four benchmarks**, despite higher training diversity. The authors interpret this as evidence that equal weighting "dilutes the contribution of impactful domains" — specifically, mathematics and code generation, which are 65% of the selected mixture but only 40% of the uniform mixture, likely provide stronger general reasoning signals than the smaller domains.

**Output token counts are comparable across mixtures**, ranging from 11.3–11.9K on AIME-25 and 5.8–6.2K on GPQA, suggesting that the domain mixture affects accuracy primarily through the *quality* of learned reasoning patterns rather than through their verbosity.

#### Reasoning Trace Analysis: Where Do the Efficiency Gains Come From?

To investigate *how* RLVR reduces output length without sacrificing accuracy, the paper segments reasoning traces from Apriel-Base and Apriel-1.5-OpenReasoner on AIME 2025 into discrete steps using GPT-5.4, then analyses per-step token counts and classifies each step into one of eight behavioral categories.

**Tokens per step, not fewer steps.** Figure 3a shows that the average number of reasoning steps per trace is comparable between Apriel-Base and Apriel-1.5-OpenReasoner. However, Figure 3b shows that Apriel-1.5-OpenReasoner uses approximately **35% fewer tokens per step** on correct responses. The efficiency gain comes from *tighter expression of each reasoning step*, not from shallower reasoning (fewer steps). This is consistent with the interpretation that RLVR teaches the model to express the same reasoning content more concisely — eliminating redundant exposition, reducing calculation verbosity, and stripping filler — rather than teaching it to skip reasoning steps.

**Fewer non-productive steps, more non-linear reasoning.** Figure 6 (Appendix D) shows the step-type distribution, extending the taxonomy of Gandhi et al. (2025) with four additional categories. The most notable shift: **non-productive steps** (restating previously established results, re-deriving conclusions already reached, filler meta-commentary) drop from **21% in Apriel-Base to 14% in Apriel-1.5-OpenReasoner** — a 7-percentage-point reduction. Meanwhile, the combined share of **non-linear reasoning behaviors** (verification, backtracking, subgoal setting, and backward chaining) increases from **11% to 17%**. Forward reasoning and problem setup remain roughly stable. The reduction in non-productive steps directly explains part of the token efficiency gain: the model wastes fewer tokens revisiting already-established results. The increase in non-linear reasoning behaviors suggests RLVR encourages the model to engage in more sophisticated reasoning patterns — explicitly verifying intermediate results, backtracking from dead ends, and decomposing problems into subgoals — behaviors that Gandhi et al. identified as enabling self-improvement in reasoning models.

**Absence of evidence for catastrophic forgetting or mode collapse.** The per-domain reward curves (Figure 5, Appendix C) show all five domains exhibiting clear upward trends over 250 optimization steps, with no domain's reward regressing. This is a sanity check for joint multi-domain training: it demonstrates that the domain balancing and length penalty mechanisms do not cause any domain to be sacrificed for others, and that negative transfer between domains is not occurring. All domains benefit from the shared training, with mathematics and function calling showing the steepest early improvements and instruction-following showing more gradual but consistent progress.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Difficulty-Aware Length Penalty hyperparameter sweep.** The paper sweeps DAP parameters $(B, \gamma, \lambda_f)$ over two settings: $(2000, 1.0, 1.0)$ and $(2000, 0.5, 0.5)$. The selected configuration $(2000, 1.0, 1.0)$ — linear solve-rate scaling, full penalty for incorrect rollouts — outperforms the alternative with $\gamma = 0.5$ (square-root scaling, which would apply a stronger penalty even on hard problems) and $\lambda_f = 0.5$ (which would partially relax the penalty on incorrect rollouts). The paper does not report detailed ablation numbers comparing these two settings, but the selection implies that **linear solve-rate scaling and full penalty on incorrect rollouts are both beneficial**.

**Domain mixture selection via sweep.** Five candidate domain mixtures were evaluated (Table 5, Appendix B): uniform (0.20/0.20/0.20/0.20/0.20), the selected mixture (0.40/0.25/0.15/0.10/0.10), and three intermediate variants (0.35/0.25/0.15/0.15/0.10; 0.30/0.30/0.15/0.15/0.10; 0.35/0.20/0.15/0.15/0.15). The selected mixture achieved the best mean validation accuracy across the five domains. A math-code-only variant (0.50/0.50/0/0/0) was included as a ablation to test whether the three smaller domains contribute at all (see domain mixture ablation in Table 3). The paper does not report validation accuracies for the non-selected mixtures beyond the three evaluated on test benchmarks, so the sensitivity of results to small mixture perturbations is unknown.

**Learning rate sweep.** The learning rate was swept over $\{7.5 \times 10^{-7}, 1.0 \times 10^{-6}, 1.25 \times 10^{-6}, 2.5 \times 10^{-6}, 5.0 \times 10^{-6}\}$. The selected value $1.25 \times 10^{-6}$ is relatively small, consistent with conservative on-policy updates where the policy should not change too much per step (to avoid exploiting stale advantages from slightly-off-policy data).

**GSPO clipping parameter sweep.** Two clipping configurations were tested: $(3 \times 10^{-4}, 4 \times 10^{-4})$ and $(3 \times 10^{-3}, 4 \times 10^{-3})$. The selected larger-clip configuration $(3 \times 10^{-3}, 4 \times 10^{-3})$ allows slightly more aggressive policy updates, suggesting the in-flight weight updates in PipelineRL keep the policy close enough to the behavior policy that the smaller clip was unnecessarily conservative.

**Output token budget sweep.** Training budgets of 16K, 20K, and 24K tokens were evaluated. The 16K budget was selected, with the paper noting that the model generalizes to 32K at inference (length generalization). The paper does not report how accuracy or token efficiency varied across these budgets, so the sensitivity of results to this choice is unclear. The 16K selection likely reflects a preference for efficiency (training on shorter rollouts is faster and may encourage conciseness) over potential accuracy gains from longer training rollouts.

**Training seed averaging to assess stability.** Results are averaged across two independent training seeds. For AIME-25 with the selected mixture, the standard deviation across evaluation runs within a training seed is ±2.8%, and the paper reports that the two-seed average reduces seed-specific noise. The standard deviations in Table 3 for AIME-25 are substantial (±5.5% for the uniform mixture, ±5.2% for math-code-only), reflecting the inherent variance of evaluating on only 30 problems. The paper does not report cross-seed variance, so the stability of the training procedure across random initializations is partially but not fully characterized.

**Adversarial / stress-testing of the adaptive domain sampler.** The paper does not report explicit ablation of adaptive vs. static domain sampling (e.g., training with the same mixture but without the α correction). This is a notable omission: the adaptive sampler is presented as a key contribution, but its causal effect on training stability and final performance is not isolated. The paper argues for its necessity by describing the domain drift pathology (Section 4.1) but provides no empirical evidence that drift actually degrades performance when left uncorrected.

### Critical Assessment

**Claim: "Multi-domain RLVR with adaptive domain sampling and DAP produces a model that matches or exceeds comparable-size open-weight reasoning models on accuracy while generating 30–50% shorter reasoning traces."** This claim is **strongly supported by Table 2** across all four benchmarks. The token reductions relative to Nemotron-Cascade (the strongest accuracy competitor) are 41% (AIME-25), 45% (GPQA), 47% (MMLU-Pro), and 54% (LiveCodeBench). Relative to Apriel-Base, reductions are 32%, 45%, 46%, and 50%. The accuracy comparison is more nuanced: Apriel-1.5-OpenReasoner achieves the highest accuracy on AIME-25 (+2.3 points over Nemotron-Cascade), the highest on GPQA (+1.0 point), second-highest on MMLU-Pro (−0.4 points vs. Qwen3), and essentially tied on LiveCodeBench (+0.1 point vs. Nemotron-Cascade). This is not a clean sweep — on MMLU-Pro and LiveCodeBench, accuracy is statistically indistinguishable from the best competitor given evaluation variance — but the token efficiency advantage is unambiguous and large.

**However, the paper does not establish that these efficiency gains come from the named mechanisms (adaptive sampling, DAP) rather than from other aspects of the training recipe (GSPO, domain mixture choice, training duration, base model properties).** The only isolated ablation is DAP vs. standard length penalty (last two rows of Table 2), which shows DAP contributes significantly to accuracy recovery (+6.6 points on AIME-25) with modest token cost. But there is no ablation isolating the adaptive domain sampler (static vs. adaptive), no comparison against GRPO or PPO (to test whether GSPO matters), and no comparison against sequential domain training (to test whether joint training matters). The paper demonstrates that a *specific combination* of design choices works well, but attribution of the gains to any single mechanism — beyond DAP's demonstrated contribution — is speculative.

**Claim: "Adaptive domain sampling preserves target domain ratios despite heterogeneous rollout dynamics."** This claim is **mechanistically plausible but empirically unverified**. The paper describes the adaptive sampler in detail and explains why domain drift would occur without it, but never reports actual domain completion ratios with and without the adaptive correction. There is no figure showing the sampling distribution converging to the target ratios over time, no measurement of how much the effective mixture drifts when adaptive sampling is disabled, and no ablation comparing final model performance with static vs. adaptive sampling. The claim that drift "biases the model toward overrepresented domains at the expense of generalization" (Section 1) is reasonable given known multi-task learning dynamics, but it is a *hypothesis about a failure mode*, not a *demonstrated failure with a demonstrated fix*. The paper's empirical contribution on this point is limited to providing the mechanism and arguing for its necessity.

**Claim: "Training under a strict 16K-token budget generalizes to 32K tokens at inference."** This claim is **supported by the existence of strong 32K-evaluation results from 16K-trained models** in Table 2, but it is **not systematically studied**. The paper does not report how accuracy varies with inference budget (e.g., 16K vs. 24K vs. 32K tokens at test time), does not compare a 16K-trained model against a 32K-trained model at 32K inference, and does not analyze *why* length generalization occurs (e.g., does the model learn to use the extra budget productively, or does it simply stop early as it would have at 16K?). The claim as stated — "generalizes to 32K tokens at inference" — is true (the model works at 32K) but doesn't establish whether the generalization is *useful* (does the model genuinely benefit from the extra budget?) or merely *not harmful* (the model doesn't break when given more tokens than it was trained with). The output token counts in Table 2 (5.8K for GPQA, 1.9K for MMLU-Pro) suggest the model rarely uses its full 32K budget, so length generalization may be moot for these benchmarks.

**Missing: difficulty estimation validation.** DAP uses the group solve rate $s = \frac{1}{G} \sum c_i$ as a difficulty signal, which modulates $\lambda$. This relies on the assumption that $s$ accurately reflects problem difficulty — an assumption the paper never validates. If the model produces $s=0$ because it's random rather than because the problem is hard, DAP would incorrectly relax the penalty. If the model produces $s=1$ because it memorized the training problem rather than because it's easy, DAP would incorrectly apply the full penalty. The paper provides no analysis of solve-rate distributions across domains, no correlation between solve rate and external difficulty labels (e.g., MATH difficulty levels), and no investigation of whether DAP's modulation is well-calibrated. The mechanism is elegant on paper but empirically unvalidated as a difficulty estimator.

**Missing: comparison against non-GSPO policy optimizers.** The paper adopts GSPO, citing its reduced variance from sequence-level importance ratios. But there is no comparison against GRPO (the more widely-used alternative) or PPO (the original RL for LLMs approach). This leaves open the question of whether GSPO is actually important for the reported results, or whether similar performance could be achieved with GRPO and the same domain mixture, adaptive sampling, and DAP recipe. Given that many comparable models (Qwen3, Kimi K2) use GRPO, this is a significant gap in the ablation suite.

**Missing: sensitivity analysis for the number of training steps.** The paper trains for 250 steps, selected by evaluating checkpoints every 50 steps and choosing the one with best validation accuracy. But the sensitivity of results to this choice is not reported — would 200 steps have sufficed? Would 300 steps degrade (overfitting)? The per-domain reward curves (Figure 5) suggest continued improvement at 250 steps for some domains (math, instruction-following) and possible plateauing for others (code generation, logic, function calling), but there is no test-set evaluation at alternative step counts.

**Single model family and scale.** All experiments use a single base model (Apriel-Base, 15B, Pixtral-derived). The paper argues this model is "representative" and chosen for its clean RL-free starting point, but the claim that the recipe generalizes to other model families (Llama, Gemma, Qwen base models) or scales (7B, 70B) is untested. The findings about DAP and adaptive sampling may depend on properties specific to Apriel-Base's pretraining data, architecture, or initial reasoning capabilities.

**Benchmark size and evaluation variance.** AIME 2025 has only 30 problems. Even with 10 evaluation runs and two training seeds, standard deviations in Table 3 reach ±5.5% for the uniform mixture — meaning a single-seed result could vary by ~5 percentage points purely from sampling noise. The paper's headline AIME-25 accuracy of 78.3% has a ±2.8% standard deviation per training seed, so the true performance could reasonably be anywhere from ~73–84%. This makes precise accuracy comparisons between models on AIME-25 unreliable. GPQA (198 problems, ±1.7–2.4%) and LiveCodeBench (315 problems, ±0.5–1.5%) have tighter confidence intervals, and MMLU-Pro (12,032 problems) is the most reliable but also the least discriminative (all models score within 2.1 points). The paper's accuracy claims on AIME-25 should be interpreted as directional rather than precise.

**Training data leakage risk.** The paper uses public training datasets (Open-Reasoner-Zero for math, TACO for code, IF-RLVR for instruction-following, SynLogic for logic, BFCL v4 for function calling) and evaluates on held-out benchmarks (AIME 2025, GPQA, MMLU-Pro, LiveCodeBench). The authors assert these are external and held-out, but given that the math training data includes AIME problems up to 2023 and the evaluation uses AIME 2025, there is no direct overlap. However, the paper does not discuss potential indirect leakage: the training data includes MATH, NuminaMath, and OpenR1-Math-220k problems that may share problem structures or templates with AIME 2025 or MMLU-Pro. This is not unique to this paper (it affects all RLVR-trained math models) but is a concern for claims about *generalization* as opposed to *test-set performance within the training distribution's problem family*.

**The Pareto frontier claim is sensitive to the choice of baselines.** Figure 1 plots Apriel-1.5-OpenReasoner against three comparable-scale models and Apriel-Base. These are the right baselines for the paper's claims, but a stronger test would include models that explicitly optimize for efficiency (e.g., models trained with budget conditioning or other length-control mechanisms) rather than models that happen to have different accuracy-efficiency trade-offs as a byproduct of their training recipe. The claim "pushing the Pareto frontier" is relative to the specific set of models evaluated, not an absolute claim about the achievable frontier.

**The paper does not disentangle the effects of GSPO, adaptive sampling, and DAP.** The final model incorporates all three simultaneously (plus the specific domain mixture, learning rate, and training duration). Only DAP is ablated in isolation (via the standard penalty baseline). This means that while the paper demonstrates a *recipe* that works, it does not provide a *decomposition* of why it works. A researcher wanting to adopt only the adaptive sampler or only DAP cannot determine from these experiments which component is load-bearing for which aspect of performance. This limits the paper's scientific contribution relative to its engineering contribution (a working recipe).

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### Lack of Causal Evidence for Adaptive Domain Sampling

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper introduces adaptive domain sampling as a key contribution and argues that without it, heterogeneous rollout dynamics cause the effective training mixture to drift away from configured ratios, biasing the model. The authors state:

> "Without correction, this drift biases the model toward overrepresented domains at the expense of generalization." (Section 1)

**The consequence.** The paper provides no empirical evidence that domain drift actually degrades performance when left uncorrected. There is no ablation comparing the adaptive sampler against a static-sampling baseline with the same configured domain mixture. A practitioner cannot determine whether adaptive sampling is load-bearing — i.e., whether disabling it would cause measurable performance degradation — or whether the static mixture alone would suffice given the paper's 250-step training duration (which may be short enough that drift has not yet accumulated significantly). If static sampling works equally well, the adaptive mechanism adds implementation complexity with no benefit. Conversely, if drift is genuinely harmful, the paper has not established the magnitude of the harm, making it impossible to judge whether alternative (simpler) corrections — such as manually inflating slow-domain weights — would be adequate.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. The paper describes the mechanism (Section 4.1), provides the mathematical formulation (Equations 4–5), and explains why drift would occur in principle, but never reports domain completion ratios with and without the correction, never shows the sampling distribution converging to target ratios over time, and never ablates adaptive vs. static sampling on final performance. The domain mixture ablation (Table 3) varies the *configured* mixture but always uses adaptive sampling — it does not test whether the adaptive component matters given a fixed mixture.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper provides no ablation, no measurement of drift magnitude, and no suggestion for future work on this question. The adaptive sampler's contribution remains mechanistically plausible but causally unverified.

---

### Difficulty-Aware Length Penalty Relies on an Unvalidated Difficulty Signal

**The assumption or constraint.** DAP modulates the length penalty strength using the group solve rate $s = \frac{1}{G} \sum c_i$, which serves double duty as a difficulty estimate: low $s$ implies a hard problem, high $s$ implies an easy one. The mechanism assumes that $s$ accurately reflects problem difficulty and that its relationship with optimal penalty strength is well-calibrated by the chosen exponent $\gamma = 1.0$.

**The consequence.** If $s$ is an unreliable difficulty signal — for instance, if the model produces a low solve rate because it is generating near-random outputs rather than because the problem is genuinely hard, or if it produces a high solve rate because it has memorized the training problem rather than because the problem is easy — then DAP will incorrectly modulate the penalty. A hard problem misclassified as easy would receive an overly aggressive penalty, discouraging necessary long reasoning. An easy problem misclassified as hard would receive an overly relaxed penalty, permitting unnecessary verbosity. The asymmetry of the penalty (incorrect rollouts always get the full penalty, $\lambda_f = 1.0$) partially protects against the latter failure — wrong answers are always penalized regardless of estimated difficulty — but the former failure (over-penalizing necessary reasoning on misclassified hard problems) has no guard. The paper also provides no evidence that $s$ correlates with external difficulty metrics (e.g., MATH dataset difficulty levels, human difficulty ratings, or alternative difficulty estimators), so the fundamental assumption that group solve rate captures *difficulty* rather than *noise* or *memorization* is untested.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The comparison of DAP vs. standard length penalty (last two rows of Table 2) shows that DAP improves accuracy with modest token increases, which is consistent with DAP allocating tokens more effectively. However, this comparison does not validate $s$ as a difficulty estimator — it shows only that the *net effect* of using $s$ to modulate $\lambda$ is positive compared to $\lambda = 1$ everywhere. A poorly calibrated difficulty signal could still yield net improvements if the standard penalty is sufficiently harmful, masking the miscalibration. The paper provides no analysis of solve-rate distributions across domains or problems, no correlation between solve rate and any external difficulty label, and no investigation of whether DAP's modulation is well-calibrated (e.g., whether $\gamma = 1.0$ is near-optimal, or whether a different exponent would perform substantially better).

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper sweeps only two DAP configurations: $(B, \gamma, \lambda_f) \in \{(2000, 1.0, 1.0), (2000, 0.5, 0.5)\}$ and selects the former based on validation accuracy, which provides minimal evidence about calibration. There is no sensitivity analysis over $\gamma$, no difficulty-estimation validation, and no discussion of when $s$ might be an unreliable difficulty signal. The paper suggests no future work on improving or validating the difficulty estimation component of DAP.

---

### Hyperparameter Sensitivity Is Not Characterized, Limiting Reproducibility Across Model Families and Scales

**The assumption or constraint.** All experiments use a single base model (Apriel-Base, 15B parameters, derived from Pixtral-12B via depth upscaling and continual pretraining). The paper selects this model explicitly because it is "trained without RL or preference optimization" and has "fully documented" pretraining data and recipe (Section 5.1), making it a clean starting point. However, this also means that the selected hyperparameters — learning rate, clipping values, domain mixture, DAP parameters, training duration, and output budget — are tuned to one specific model's architecture, pretraining distribution, and initial reasoning capabilities.

**The consequence.** A practitioner attempting to apply this recipe to a different base model (e.g., Llama, Gemma, Qwen, or a different scale such as 7B or 70B) has no guidance on which hyperparameters are model-specific and which are universal. The learning rate of $1.25 \times 10^{-6}$ was selected from a sweep over five values (Table 5), but the optimal learning rate may depend on model scale, pretraining objective, and weight initialization. The GSPO clipping parameters ($\epsilon_{\text{low}} = 3 \times 10^{-3}, \epsilon_{\text{high}} = 4 \times 10^{-3}$) were selected from only two candidate pairs, and their appropriateness depends on how quickly the policy diverges under in-flight weight updates — a property that varies with model size, architecture, and the diversity of the training data. The domain mixture (40% math, 25% code, 15% logic, 10% instruction-following, 10% function calling) was selected by sweeping five candidate mixtures (Table 5) and choosing the one that maximizes validation accuracy *on Apriel-Base* — there is no reason to believe this same mixture is optimal for a model with different pretraining strengths and weaknesses. The 16K training output budget was selected from a sweep over $\{16\text{k}, 20\text{k}, 24\text{k}\}$, and its interaction with the 32K evaluation budget's length generalization is not characterized — on a model that generates longer traces by default, the optimal training budget might differ.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper reports hyperparameter sweep results only implicitly: the selected values are listed (Tables 4 and 5), but no learning curves, validation-accuracy surfaces, or sensitivity analyses are shown. The paper averages results over two training seeds, which provides a lower bound on seed sensitivity for Apriel-Base, but no analogous stability characterization exists for hyperparameter perturbations. The domain mixture ablation (Table 3) shows that the selected mixture outperforms uniform and math-code-only mixtures, but this characterizes only one hyperparameter dimension (domain ratios) and only at three points in that space.

**Mitigation status.** The paper partially addresses this by releasing the full training configuration, code, and pipeline, which enables the community to run sensitivity analyses and re-tune for other base models. However, the paper itself provides no guidance on how hyperparameters should be adjusted for new settings, no recommendations for which parameters are most sensitive, and no characterization of the stability of results under perturbations. This is consistent with the paper's contribution being a *specific reproducible recipe* rather than a *generalizable methodology* with transferable hyperparameter heuristics, but practitioners should expect to re-sweep hyperparameters when adapting to new models.

### Single Model Scale and Single Evaluation Regime Leave Scaling Behavior Uncharacterized

**The assumption or constraint.** All experiments use a 15B-parameter model trained with a 16K output budget and evaluated at 32K tokens on four benchmarks (AIME 2025, GPQA, MMLU-Pro, LiveCodeBench). The paper does not study how the recipe's effectiveness varies with model scale (e.g., 7B vs. 70B), training budget (e.g., 8K vs. 64K token caps), evaluation budget (e.g., 16K vs. 64K at test time), or benchmark difficulty distribution.

**The consequence.** Several practically important questions are left unanswered. First, **does the efficiency advantage scale with model size?** On a 7B model, reasoning capabilities are weaker, and the base model may not benefit as much from RLVR (since there are fewer correct solutions in the proposal distribution to reinforce). On a 70B model, stronger base capabilities might make the efficiency gains even larger — or might make them unnecessary (since the larger model already reasons concisely). Second, **how does the length generalization (16K training → 32K evaluation) behave at larger gaps?** The paper claims "generalizes to 32K" but does not test 64K or 128K evaluation budgets, which would matter for the hardest reasoning problems that genuinely require very long chains-of-thought. It is possible that the model's conciseness training causes it to under-use available budget even when reasoning would benefit from it — the observed token counts (5.8K for GPQA, 1.9K for MMLU-Pro) suggest the model rarely approaches its 32K cap, but a harder benchmark might exceed the model's learned "comfort zone" for reasoning length. Third, **does the recipe work on benchmarks where accuracy is far from saturation?** MMLU-Pro scores cluster within 2.1 points (75.6–77.7% across all models), leaving little room to demonstrate meaningful accuracy improvements. A benchmark with lower baseline accuracy and more room for improvement would provide a stronger test of RLVR's effectiveness.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper reports only one model scale (15B) and one evaluation regime (32K max tokens). The 30–50% token reduction claim (Section 5.2) is measured relative to Apriel-Base and Nemotron-Cascade at 15B, with no evidence that similar reductions would occur at other scales. The length generalization claim (16K training → 32K inference) is supported by the existence of strong 32K results from 16K-trained models (Table 2), but the paper does not systematically study how performance degrades or improves as the evaluation budget deviates from the training budget — there is no experiment comparing 16K, 24K, 32K, and 64K evaluation budgets on the same model, which would establish whether length generalization is robust or fragile.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not mention model-scale generalization or evaluation-budget sensitivity as limitations, nor does it suggest these as future work. The choice of a single 15B model is partially justified by the reproducibility goal (Section 5.1): using a well-documented base model at a practical scale. But the absence of even a single smaller-scale training run (e.g., 7B) means the recipe's scaling properties are entirely uncharacterized.

### No Joint Optimization of Search and Length Control; No Combined Evaluation with Inference-Time Compute Scaling

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper evaluates Apriel-1.5-OpenReasoner under a standard single-sample decoding protocol (temperature 0.6, top-p 0.95, single completion per problem). The RLVR training uses group-based advantages (G = 8 rollouts per prompt) and DAP modulates the penalty based on group solve rates, but at inference time, the model is used like a standard LLM — one forward pass, one output. The paper does not study how the trained model performs when combined with inference-time compute scaling techniques (majority voting, best-of-N sampling, verifier-guided search, or sequential revisions), which are the standard deployment strategy for reasoning models and which interact non-trivially with the length-control mechanisms learned during training.

**The consequence.** This creates a gap between the training objective (which optimizes for single-sample reward under difficulty-adaptive length constraints) and the deployment objective (which is typically to maximize pass@k accuracy under a total token budget that can be spent across multiple samples). A model trained with DAP — which relaxes length penalties on hard problems — may produce longer individual rollouts than a model trained with a uniform penalty, even if those longer rollouts are individually more accurate. But if the deployment strategy uses majority voting over N samples, the trade-off between per-sample length and per-sample accuracy shifts: longer individual samples reduce N (since the total token budget is fixed), potentially negating the accuracy benefit of longer reasoning. Conversely, if deployment uses best-of-N with a verifier, the DAP-trained model's ability to produce longer correct solutions on hard problems might be perfectly complementary. The paper's evaluation protocol (single-sample pass@1) cannot distinguish these cases, leaving practitioners without guidance on how to deploy the model in the inference-time-compute-scaling regimes that are standard practice for reasoning models (DeepSeek-R1, Kimi K2, Nemotron-Cascade all report pass@k or majority-voting results).

**What evidence exists in the paper.** All results in Table 2 and Figure 1 use pass@1 with a single sample per problem. The paper does not report pass@k, majority voting, best-of-N, or any inference-time compute scaling results. The DAP vs. standard-penalty comparison (last two rows of Table 2) shows that DAP increases both accuracy and per-sample token counts (modestly on AIME-25 and LiveCodeBench, more substantially on GPQA and MMLU-Pro), but there is no analysis of how these per-sample changes affect total token cost under multi-sample aggregation strategies. The related work section (Section 3) discusses inference-time budget forcing and budget-conditioned training, but the paper never evaluates its own model under those protocols.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not discuss this gap, does not suggest pass@k or multi-sample evaluation as future work, and does not acknowledge the mismatch between the group-based training signal (which sees 8 rollouts per prompt and computes relative advantages) and the single-sample evaluation protocol. This is a significant limitation for practitioners who intend to deploy the model under the standard reasoning-model evaluation regimes used by the baselines the paper compares against.

### Base Model Selection Confounds Attribution of Gains to the RLVR Recipe

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper selects Apriel-Base as the starting point because it "has not been previously trained with RL, making it a clean starting point for studying multi-domain RLVR" (Section 5.1). This is methodologically sound for establishing that RLVR *causes* the observed improvements (since there is no prior RL or preference optimization to confound the attribution). However, it also means that Apriel-Base is a *specific* model with specific pretraining characteristics — it was initialized from Pixtral-12B via depth upscaling, continually pretrained, and instruction-tuned with explicit reasoning traces (Radhakrishna et al., 2025). These properties may make it particularly amenable to RLVR improvement in ways that a different base model with different pretraining or instruction-tuning would not replicate.

**The consequence.** The reported gains — 30–50% token reduction with maintained or improved accuracy — may overstate what a practitioner would achieve when applying the same recipe to a different base model. Specifically, Apriel-Base's instruction tuning with "explicit reasoning traces" may have taught the model to produce reasoning but not to produce *concise* reasoning, leaving substantial room for RLVR to compress verbose traces. A model instruction-tuned for conciseness (or trained with length penalties from the start) might show much smaller token reductions. Conversely, Apriel-Base's pass@1 on AIME 2025 is already 73.3% (Table 2) — near the top of the comparable-model range — so the additional 5-point gain from RLVR pushes from already-strong to slightly-stronger. A weaker base model might benefit more from RLVR in absolute accuracy terms, while a stronger base model might benefit less (diminishing returns). The paper provides no evidence that the recipe transfers to a base model with different properties, and the choice of a base model that was explicitly designed for reasoning (with documented reasoning-trace instruction tuning) means the starting conditions are favorable for RLVR improvement in ways that a generic instruction-tuned model's might not be.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper compares Apriel-1.5-OpenReasoner against three external models (Phi-4-reasoning, Qwen3, Nemotron-Cascade) that use different base models and different post-training recipes. These comparisons establish that the *final model* is competitive, but they do not establish that the *RLVR recipe* would produce similar improvements if applied to those models' base versions. The paper does not train on any base model other than Apriel-Base, does not report Apriel-Base's properties (e.g., reasoning trace verbosity, per-domain pass@1 before RLVR) in sufficient detail to assess transferability, and does not discuss how base model characteristics might modulate recipe effectiveness.

**Mitigation status.** The paper is transparent about the base model choice and justifies it on reproducibility grounds (Section 5.1). The authors note they chose Apriel-Base over the newer Apriel-1.6 (which incorporates RL in its post-training) and over Qwen3 (which describes training data "only at a high level"), both to maintain attribution clarity. This is a valid trade-off — clean causal attribution at the cost of uncertain transferability — but the paper does not explicitly flag transferability as a limitation, nor does it suggest multi-base-model replication as future work. A practitioner considering this recipe for a non-Apriel model should treat the reported gains as an existence proof (RLVR can produce these improvements on at least one base model) rather than a guaranteed outcome.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper makes a **methodological intervention** rather than an algorithmic breakthrough. Its primary effect on the field, if its approach is adopted, would be to raise the standard for what constitutes a complete contribution in RL post-training research — from "we release weights and a high-level description" to "we release weights, training configurations, domain mixtures, hyperparameter sweeps, and code sufficient to reproduce the full training run." This is not a paradigm shift in how RL training works (the components — GSPO, length penalties, domain mixing — are all incremental variants of established techniques), but it is a **reframing of what the community should expect from an RL post-training paper**: reproducibility as a first-class contribution, not an afterthought.

The paper's most specific landscape-changing claim is empirical rather than methodological: it demonstrates that **joint multi-domain RLVR with lightweight domain balancing and difficulty-aware length control can simultaneously improve accuracy and reduce output length by 30–50%** relative to comparable-scale models (Table 2). This is not a new architectural component but a demonstration that careful management of two previously underappreciated pathologies — domain mixture drift and uniform over-penalization of long reasoning — unlocks efficiency gains that prior joint-training approaches left on the table. If these results replicate across model families and scales, they establish that **the default recipe for multi-domain RLVR should include active domain balancing and difficulty-adaptive length control**, not as optional enhancements but as necessary components of a stable and efficient training pipeline.

**Reconciling contradictions.** The paper resolves no direct empirical contradictions in the literature, because the contradictions it addresses are methodological rather than empirical. The core tension it engages with is between joint multi-domain training (OLMo 3, Kimi K2) and sequential domain-wise training (Nemotron-Cascade, GLM). Prior to this work, the field had examples of both paradigms succeeding with different design choices, but no principled account of *why you would choose one over the other* or *what mechanisms are necessary to make joint training work reliably*. The paper provides one answer: joint training is viable if you implement adaptive domain sampling to prevent mixture drift, and difficulty-aware length control to prevent over-penalization of hard problems. It does not prove joint training is *superior* to sequential training (no such ablation exists), but it shifts the burden of evidence: sequential training can no longer claim to be necessary for stable multi-domain optimization, because joint training with appropriate corrections demonstrably works.

**Research directions that become more attractive:**

- **Open-recipe RL post-training as a shared experimental platform.** The paper's full release — model weights, training configurations, domain mixtures, hyperparameter sweeps, and code — converts Apriel-1.5-OpenReasoner from a point measurement into a *controllable experimental system*. Researchers can now run ablation studies that were previously impossible on closed-recipe models: swap GSPO for GRPO, disable adaptive sampling, vary DAP hyperparameters, add or remove domains, and measure the causal effect of each change on final performance and reasoning trace structure. This lowers the barrier to entry for RL post-training research from "first build a complete multi-domain RL pipeline from scratch" to "modify an existing working pipeline and measure the difference." The downstream effect could be a shift toward more controlled, hypothesis-driven RL post-training research, as opposed to the current regime where each new model release is a monolithic black-box system.

- **Reproducibility as a competitive advantage in the open-weight ecosystem.** The paper implicitly argues that full transparency — releasing not just weights but the complete training recipe — is not in tension with competitive performance. Apriel-1.5-OpenReasoner matches or exceeds comparable-scale models on accuracy while using fewer tokens. If this pattern holds (open-recipe models can be competitive with closed-recipe models), it creates pressure on other open-weight model developers to disclose their recipes, since the claimed trade-off between openness and performance weakens. This would accelerate the field's collective understanding of which RL post-training design choices matter, rather than leaving each new model release as an opaque combination of undisclosed ingredients.

**Research directions that become less attractive or are challenged:**

- **Complex difficulty-estimation pipelines for length control.** The paper demonstrates that a dead-simple difficulty signal — the group solve rate $s = \frac{1}{G}\sum c_i$, already computed for GSPO advantage normalization — can effectively modulate length penalties. This challenges the necessity of more elaborate approaches that train auxiliary difficulty predictors (Han et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025), introduce separate training phases for difficulty-conditioned policy optimization (Shen et al., 2025), or require explicit budget tokens (Wen et al., 2025). Unless these more complex methods demonstrate substantially better accuracy-efficiency trade-offs than DAP on comparable benchmarks and model scales, the burden of proof shifts onto them to justify their added complexity. DAP's virtue is not that it is optimal — the paper never claims it is — but that it achieves competitive efficiency with zero additional training overhead, auxiliary models, or policy-loss changes. This sets a high bar for any future length-control mechanism that adds complexity.

- **Static domain mixtures in joint training pipelines.** The paper argues, though does not prove, that static domain mixtures are fragile because the effective mixture drifts as a function of relative domain rollout speeds, which vary with hardware, model scale, and problem distribution. If this argument is correct — and future work validates it with explicit static-vs-adaptive ablations — then static mixtures become an anti-pattern for reproducible multi-domain RLVR. The practical implication is that papers reporting joint training results without describing their domain balancing mechanism should be viewed with skepticism: the reported configured mixture may bear little relationship to the effective training mixture, and the results may not be reproducible on different hardware. This would shift the community norm from "report configured mixture ratios" to "demonstrate that effective mixture ratios match configured ratios throughout training."

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Static vs. adaptive domain sampling ablation with measured mixture drift.** The most important unresolved question in the paper is whether adaptive domain sampling actually matters for final performance, or whether the domain drift it corrects is benign at the 250-step training duration used here. A strong follow-up would train two models with identical hyperparameters — one with adaptive sampling enabled, one with static sampling using the same configured mixture — and measure three outcomes: (a) the actual domain completion ratios over time in both conditions, quantifying the drift magnitude; (b) final test-set accuracy on all four evaluation benchmarks; and (c) per-domain reward curves to detect whether any single domain is sacrificed under static sampling. If the static-sampling model performs comparably, the adaptive sampler is unnecessary at this training duration (though potentially load-bearing at longer durations). If the static-sampling model degrades, the experiment quantifies the cost of ignoring drift and establishes adaptive sampling as a necessary component of reproducible multi-domain RLVR. This ablation is feasible immediately using the paper's released code and PipelineRL.

**Cross-model-family replication at the same parameter scale (15B).** The paper's results are demonstrated on exactly one base model (Apriel-Base, derived from Pixtral-12B via depth upscaling). A critical stress test is whether the same recipe — identical domain mixture, GSPO configuration, DAP hyperparameters, and training duration — produces comparable accuracy-efficiency gains when applied to a different 15B-scale base model with different pretraining data and architecture. A strong candidate would be a 14B Llama or Qwen base model (without prior RL or preference optimization). The outcome of interest is not just final accuracy but whether the token-efficiency gains (30–50% reduction vs. the base model) transfer, and whether the shift in reasoning behavior (fewer non-productive steps, more non-linear reasoning, per Figure 6) replicates. A negative result — the recipe works on Apriel-Base but not on Llama/Qwen — would indicate that the gains are specific to Apriel-Base's pretraining or instruction-tuning characteristics, limiting the recipe's generality. A positive result would substantially strengthen the paper's claim to have found a *general* multi-domain RLVR recipe rather than an Apriel-specific configuration.

**DAP solve-rate calibration study with external difficulty labels.** DAP uses the group solve rate $s$ as an implicit difficulty estimate, but the paper never validates whether $s$ correlates with ground-truth difficulty. A calibration study would take a dataset with external difficulty labels (e.g., MATH difficulty levels 1–5, or human-annotated difficulty ratings for a subset of the training data), compute the group solve rate $s$ from Apriel-Base and from intermediate RLVR checkpoints, and measure: (a) the rank correlation between $s$ and external difficulty labels at each training stage; (b) whether the relationship is monotonic (does $s$ decrease smoothly with problem difficulty, or is it noisy?); and (c) whether DAP's $\lambda = s^\gamma$ modulation, with $\gamma = 1.0$, produces penalty strengths that are well-calibrated to external difficulty (e.g., do hard problems with low external difficulty ratings consistently receive weak penalties, and easy problems consistently receive standard penalties?). A finding that $s$ is poorly correlated with external difficulty — for instance, because the model's pass rate is dominated by memorization or randomness rather than genuine problem difficulty — would undermine the theoretical motivation for DAP (even if DAP empirically outperforms the standard penalty, the mechanism would be doing something other than difficulty-adaptive length control). A finding of strong correlation would validate DAP's conceptual foundation and motivate more sophisticated difficulty-to-penalty mappings (e.g., learned functions rather than the fixed $s^\gamma$ form).

**Inference-time compute scaling under varying output budgets.** The paper evaluates models at pass@1 with a 32K output budget, but standard deployment for reasoning models uses inference-time compute scaling (majority voting, best-of-N, verifier-guided search). A follow-up would evaluate Apriel-1.5-OpenReasoner under fixed total token budgets: for a given budget $T$ (e.g., 128K tokens total), compare the pass@k accuracy achieved by (a) single long samples (one generation up to 128K tokens, if the model generalizes that far), (b) best-of-N with N samples averaging $T/N$ tokens each, and (c) majority voting with the same token allocation. The experiment would answer whether the model's conciseness training (DAP encourages short reasoning on easy problems) makes it more sample-efficient under best-of-N (since shorter per-sample traces allow more samples within the token budget), or whether the conciseness comes at the cost of per-sample accuracy that parallel sampling cannot recover. It would also characterize whether the 16K-to-32K length generalization extends further to 64K or 128K, and whether the model *uses* the extra budget when it is available (producing longer but more accurate traces) or *ignores* it (stopping early as trained). This is the most practically important follow-up for deployment, since the baselines the paper compares against (Nemotron-Cascade, Qwen3) are typically evaluated under these multi-sample protocols.

**GSPO vs. GRPO comparison within the same recipe.** The paper adopts GSPO over the more widely-used GRPO, citing reduced variance from sequence-level importance ratios. But the paper never compares GSPO against GRPO (or PPO) within the same training recipe. A controlled experiment would train two models with identical domain mixtures, adaptive sampling, DAP, and training duration — differing only in the policy optimizer (GSPO vs. GRPO). Outcomes of interest include: (a) training stability (reward variance over steps, occurrence of reward collapse or mode collapse), (b) final accuracy and token efficiency on the four evaluation benchmarks, and (c) sensitivity to hyperparameters (does GRPO require different learning rates or clipping values to achieve comparable stability?). If GRPO achieves comparable or better results, GSPO's claimed advantage is not load-bearing for this recipe, and practitioners can use the more standard algorithm. If GSPO is substantially more stable or achieves better accuracy-efficiency, the paper provides evidence that the choice of policy optimizer matters and that GSPO should be preferred for multi-domain RLVR. This ablates a specific design choice the paper advocates but never validates.

**Scaling study: 7B and 70B training runs with the same recipe.** The paper's recipe is demonstrated at exactly one model scale (15B). A scaling study would train 7B and 70B variants using the same PipelineRL setup, domain mixture, adaptive sampling, DAP configuration, and training duration (or duration adjusted to comparable optimization steps in terms of tokens processed). Key measurements: (a) does the 30–50% token reduction relative to the base model scale with model size, or is it largest at intermediate scales where the base model is verbose but not yet naturally concise? (b) Do the DAP parameters ($\gamma = 1.0$, $\lambda_f = 1.0$, $B = 2000$) transfer across scales, or do larger models (which generate longer traces) need a wider buffer $B$, and smaller models (which generate shorter traces) need a narrower one? (c) Does the domain mixture that is optimal at 15B remain optimal at 7B and 70B, or do stronger models benefit more from harder domains (math, logic) and weaker models benefit more from easier domains (instruction-following)? This would transform the paper from a point recipe into a scaling-informed methodology, with practical guidance for practitioners at different compute budgets.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Cost-efficient batch inference for reasoning workloads.** An organization running large-scale batch evaluation of math or code problems (e.g., automated grading, curriculum generation, or synthetic data production for self-improvement pipelines) can deploy Apriel-1.5-OpenReasoner in place of comparable-scale reasoning models and achieve the same or higher accuracy with roughly half the output tokens. On LiveCodeBench, for example, the model matches Nemotron-Cascade's accuracy (70.8% vs. 70.7%) while generating 7.4K tokens vs. 16.0K — a 54% reduction (Table 2). For a batch of 100,000 coding problems, this reduces total output tokens from 1.6 billion to 740 million, directly translating to lower inference costs (fewer GPU-hours for generation) and lower latency (shorter per-problem generation time). The benefit compounds for applications that chain multiple reasoning calls (e.g., iterative debugging, multi-step planning) where each step's token savings multiply across the chain.

**On-device or edge deployment where token budget is hardware-constrained.** For deployment scenarios where the maximum context length is limited by device memory or latency requirements (e.g., mobile reasoning assistants, embedded coding copilots, or real-time tutoring systems), the model's 30–50% token reduction relative to same-scale alternatives directly expands the range of problems solvable within the hardware budget. On GPQA, Apriel-1.5-OpenReasoner uses 5.8K tokens vs. Nemotron-Cascade's 10.6K to achieve higher accuracy (69.8% vs. 68.4%). In a memory-constrained setting with an 8K context window, the Apriel model can complete nearly all GPQA problems within budget, while Nemotron-Cascade would be truncated on a substantial fraction, likely degrading accuracy beyond what Table 2 reports. The model's training with a 16K budget and generalization to 32K further suggests it can operate efficiently in regimes with tight output caps without special budget-forcing logic.

**Base model for domain-specific RL fine-tuning with controlled verbosity.** A team wanting to specialize a reasoning model for a narrow domain (e.g., medical diagnosis reasoning, legal analysis, or scientific hypothesis generation) could start from Apriel-1.5-OpenReasoner rather than from a pretrained base model, and run additional RLVR fine-tuning on their domain-specific data. The advantage is that the model's baseline behavior already produces concise, efficient reasoning traces (Figure 6: 14% non-productive steps vs. 21% in the base model), so domain-specific fine-tuning starts from a policy that is already trained to avoid verbosity and overthinking. If the domain-specific data includes problems of varying difficulty, DAP can be re-enabled with the same configuration to preserve difficulty-adaptive length control during fine-tuning, or the penalty can be disabled entirely if the domain requires uniformly long reasoning and the efficiency training provides a useful starting point for the policy. The key benefit is avoiding the common failure mode where RL fine-tuning on a narrow domain causes the model to develop verbose, repetitive reasoning patterns that then need to be corrected with a separate length-control phase.

**Open experimental platform for RL post-training curriculum design.** Beyond direct deployment, the paper's fully reproducible pipeline serves as a testbed for designing and evaluating training curricula. A research team investigating whether the order of domain exposure matters (e.g., train on math first, then code; or interleave easy and hard problems within each domain) can modify the adaptive domain sampler to implement a curriculum schedule — the sampler already controls domain selection probabilities at each step, so extending it to time-varying target weights is a minimal code change. The team can then measure whether curriculum training improves final accuracy or reduces training time compared to the paper's uniform-domain-sampling baseline, while controlling for all other aspects of the pipeline (GSPO, DAP, model architecture) that are held constant. This reduces the cost of curriculum learning experiments from "build an entire multi-domain RL pipeline" to "modify a few lines in the domain sampler," which could accelerate research on an understudied aspect of RL post-training.
