# EuroLLM: Multilingual Language Models for Europe

**ArXiv:** [2409.16235](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.16235)

## 🎯 Pitch

The EuroLLM project introduces a suite of open-weight multilingual LLMs designed to understand and generate text across all 24 official European Union languages plus additional relevant languages, addressing the English-centric bias of existing open-weight models.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

The EuroLLM project introduces a suite of open-weight multilingual LLMs designed to understand and generate text across all 24 official European Union languages plus additional relevant languages, addressing the English-centric bias of existing open-weight models. The paper details the end-to-end training pipeline—data collection and filtering from web, parallel, code/math, and high-quality sources (§2), development of a 128,000-piece multilingual tokenizer (§3), and model architecture decisions (§4)—used to produce **EuroLLM-1.7B** and its instruction-tuned variant **EuroLLM-1.7B-Instruct**. The authors employ joint scaling laws to determine a **20% parallel data allocation** per non-English language (finding that adding parallel data improves translation performance without degrading general-domain benchmarks) and use a trapezoid learning rate scheduler (Warmup-Stable-Decay) that yields consistent gains over a cosine scheduler on both multilingual reasoning benchmarks and machine translation (§4.1). On the FLORES-200 translation benchmark, EuroLLM-1.7B-Instruct clearly outperforms Gemma-2B-Instruct and proves competitive with Gemma-7B-Instruct despite having ~4× fewer non-embedding parameters, establishing that careful data mixture design and tokenizer selection can yield strong multilingual performance even at the 1.7B parameter scale—though the model trails Gemma-2B on English-centric reasoning benchmarks like Arc Challenge, suggesting the multilingual capability gains come with a trade-off in English-specific reasoning capacity at this parameter count.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Open-Weight LLMs Are English-Centric, Excluding European Languages

The fundamental gap this paper addresses is straightforward but consequential: **the most capable open-weight large language models are predominantly trained for English, leaving most European languages underserved or entirely excluded.** This is not merely a linguistic inconvenience — it is a structural barrier to AI accessibility, economic competitiveness, and digital sovereignty for roughly 450 million EU citizens who conduct their daily lives in languages other than English.

The paper opens by acknowledging the impressive progress in LLM development, citing OpenAI's GPT series, Anthropic's Claude, and open-weight models like LLaMA (Touvron et al., 2023), Mistral (Jiang et al., 2023), and Gemma (Team et al., 2024a). However, the authors observe that these open-weight models — the ones the research community and smaller organizations can freely use, study, and build upon — are "predominantly limited to English and a few high-resource languages." The European Union alone has 24 official languages, ranging from high-resource languages like German and French (with abundant training data) to extremely low-resource languages like Maltese and Irish (with orders of magnitude less available text). An open-weight model that handles only English plus perhaps French, German, and Spanish covers only a fraction of the EU's linguistic landscape.

This gap has several concrete dimensions:

- **Accessibility:** Citizens who do not speak English cannot benefit from LLM-powered tools in their native languages. This includes everything from educational assistants and healthcare information systems to government service interfaces and legal document summarization.
- **Digital sovereignty:** The EU has invested heavily in technological independence, yet reliance on English-centric models (often developed by non-European companies) undermines the ability to deploy AI systems that reflect European values, regulatory frameworks (e.g., GDPR, the AI Act), and linguistic diversity.
- **Scientific and cultural preservation:** Low-resource languages like Irish, Maltese, and Latvian carry unique cultural heritage. If LLMs ignore these languages, AI-driven tools for language learning, cultural preservation, and digital archiving remain unavailable.
- **Economic fairness:** Businesses operating in smaller European language markets are disadvantaged if state-of-the-art NLP tools work only for English and a handful of major languages.

The paper frames this gap as the central motivation for the EuroLLM project: "to address this gap, we have started the EuroLLM project with the goal of creating a suite of LLMs capable of understanding and generating text in all European Union languages... as well as some additional relevant languages" (Section 1). The list of additional languages — Arabic, Catalan, Chinese, Galician, Hindi, Japanese, Korean, Norwegian, Russian, Turkish, and Ukrainian — reflects a pragmatic expansion to include languages spoken by significant immigrant communities within Europe and major global languages relevant to European trade and diplomacy.

### Why This Problem Matters Now

The timing of this work is not accidental. Several converging trends make multilingual open-weight LLMs urgent:

**The open-weight movement is accelerating, but multilinguality lags.** Models like LLaMA, Mistral, and Gemma have democratized access to capable LLMs, enabling researchers worldwide to fine-tune, study, and deploy these models without API costs or corporate gatekeeping. However, the multilingual capabilities of these models are inconsistent and often poor. LLaMA-2, for instance, was trained primarily on English data with only a small fraction of non-English text, leading to degraded performance on non-English tasks. Even models that claim multilingual support (like Gemma) concentrate their parameter budgets on English and a handful of high-resource languages. The EuroLLM project directly addresses this asymmetry by designing the entire training pipeline from data collection through tokenization to model architecture with multilinguality as a first-class objective, not an afterthought.

**EU regulatory pressure creates demand for multilingual AI.** The EU's AI Act and related digital policy initiatives create a regulatory environment where AI systems deployed in Europe must be transparent, auditable, and fair across languages. Proprietary models like GPT-4 offer some multilingual capabilities, but their closed nature makes them unsuitable for many regulated applications where explainability, data residency, and model transparency are required. An open-weight model trained with documented data sources and reproducible methods aligns with these regulatory demands in a way that closed APIs cannot.

**The scaling law methodology enables data-efficient multilingual training.** A key intellectual motivation is the observation that multilingual model training involves a complex trade-off: allocating a fixed training budget across many languages risks shortchanging each one, especially low-resource languages with limited data. The paper builds on Fernandes et al. (2023), who showed that "by training smaller models with varying weights for each language in the data mix, one can fit a multilingual, joint scaling law" that predicts performance for larger models. This methodology — described in §2.2.1 — provides a principled way to make data allocation decisions without exhaustive large-scale experimentation. The EuroLLM project is, in part, a demonstration that this scaling-law-driven approach can produce a competitive multilingual model at modest scale (1.7B parameters), providing a template for others building multilingual LLMs.

### Where Existing Approaches Fall Short

The paper identifies specific limitations in prior multilingual LLM efforts, both explicit and implicit:

**English-first training leaves other languages as an afterthought.** Most open-weight LLMs are trained on web-crawled corpora that are overwhelmingly English. Common Crawl, the foundation of most web datasets, is approximately 46% English by volume; the next largest languages (German, Russian, Japanese, French) each account for only about 5-6%, and many EU languages register well below 1%. When models are trained on these raw proportions, English dominates the parameter budget. The resulting models may exhibit some cross-lingual transfer — the ability to apply knowledge learned in English to other languages — but this transfer is unreliable, particularly for low-resource languages, complex reasoning tasks, and language-specific cultural knowledge. The EuroLLM project explicitly designs its data mixture (§2.2.3) to counteract this imbalance, capping English at 50% in the main training phase and further reducing it to 32.5% during annealing, while ensuring every target language receives a meaningful allocation.

**Tokenizer design systematically disadvantages non-English languages.** The paper's tokenizer analysis (§3) reveals a subtle but pervasive problem: tokenizers with small vocabularies (e.g., Mistral's 32,000 tokens) produce high fertility (pieces per word) for non-English languages. Fertility directly impacts efficiency and capability: if a Finnish word requires 4-5 tokens to encode while an English word requires 1-2, the model's effective context window for Finnish text is compressed, fewer Finnish tokens are seen during training (since token counts are measured by the tokenizer, not by words), and the model must expend more computation to process equivalent semantic content. The Gemma tokenizer, with 256,000 tokens, addresses some of these issues but at the cost of a massive embedding layer (256K × embedding_dim parameters). The EuroLLM tokenizer's 128,000-vocabulary choice explicitly balances fertility across languages against parameter efficiency, an engineering trade-off that prior work either ignored (by accepting poor non-English fertility) or addressed suboptimally (by over-allocating vocabulary to the embedding layer).

**Scaling law analyses for multilingual models were underdeveloped.** Before Fernandes et al. (2023), the standard approach to multilingual data mixing was heuristic: allocate a fixed percentage to each language, or oversample low-resource languages, or follow data proportions from existing successful models. These heuristics are brittle — what works at one model scale may fail at another. The joint scaling law methodology used in §2.2.1 represents a more rigorous alternative: by training small models (100M–341M non-embedding parameters) with varied language weightings, fitting power-law functions to the resulting performance curves, and extrapolating to larger models, the authors can predict the impact of data mixing decisions without training the full model. The paper applies this methodology to two critical questions — how much parallel data to include and whether to repeat high-quality data — providing empirical grounding for decisions that prior work typically made by intuition or limited ablation.

**Parallel data is underutilized in multilingual LLM training.** Many multilingual models are trained almost entirely on monolingual web data, neglecting parallel corpora (translation pairs) that explicitly align languages. The paper's scaling law analysis (§2.2.1, Figure 2) demonstrates that adding 20% parallel data per non-English language significantly improves performance on translation benchmarks (the "parallel data" test set) without degrading performance on monolingual benchmarks (web and Wikipedia test sets). This is a non-obvious finding: one might worry that parallel data, by forcing the model to encode the same content in two languages, would crowd out monolingual knowledge or encourage the model to "shortcut" through translation rather than developing deep language understanding. The scaling laws suggest the opposite — parallel data acts as a beneficial regularizer that improves cross-lingual alignment without harming monolingual performance. The paper also notes diminishing returns when increasing parallel data from 25% to 37.5%, establishing 20% as a reasonable optimum that balances translation quality against total data volume.

**Instruction-tuning datasets for multilingual models are scarce.** The post-training phase (§5) requires instruction-following data spanning all target languages. Existing open instruction-tuning datasets like OpenHermes-2.5 are overwhelmingly English, while datasets like Aya (Singh et al., 2024) cover more languages but vary in quality and task diversity. The EuroLLM project's EuroBlocks dataset — combining OpenHermes-2.5, Aya, and high-quality machine translation examples from NTREX-128, FLORES-200-DEV, WMT-21, and WMT-22 — represents a pragmatic synthesis of available resources. The resulting 1M-sample dataset covers all supported languages and a variety of tasks, but the paper does not claim this is optimal; rather, it is a functional baseline that enables the instruction-tuned model to follow prompts and perform translation.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The EuroLLM project does not claim to introduce novel architectures, training algorithms, or scaling laws. Rather, it positions itself as a **systematic, engineering-driven effort to build a multilingual LLM using established methods applied with multilingual-first design principles.** The contributions are in the careful integration and empirical validation of design choices:

- **Data collection and filtering** (§2.1): The paper documents a multilingual data pipeline that combines existing datasets (FineWeb-edu, RedPajama-Data-v2, HPLT, MADLAD-400, CulturaX, mC4) with quality filters (perplexity filtering, language identification, heuristic filters via CCNet), parallel data from public sources filtered by Bicleaner and COMETKIWI-22 for quality, and domain-specific data (code, math, high-quality). This is not a new dataset per se, but a documented recipe for assembling a multilingual training corpus that others can replicate or adapt.

- **Scaling-law-driven data mixing** (§2.2): The paper extends Fernandes et al. (2023)'s joint scaling law methodology to answer practical data mixing questions that arise in real multilingual LLM projects. The finding that repeating Wikipedia data improves Wikipedia test performance without degrading web test performance (§2.2.2, Figure 3) is an empirical result with immediate practical implications — it justifies oversampling high-quality data, a common practice that lacked rigorous scaling-law justification.

- **Tokenizer engineering** (§3): The 128,000-vocabulary BPE tokenizer with byte-fallback is not architecturally novel (it follows the LLaMA-2/Mistral approach), but the paper's fertility analysis across languages (Figure 5) provides concrete evidence that this vocabulary size achieves a favorable trade-off compared to smaller (Mistral's 32K) and larger (Gemma's 256K) alternatives. The implicit message is that tokenizer design for multilingual models requires explicit, language-by-language evaluation, not just English-centric metrics.

- **Scheduler selection** (§4.1): The comparison between cosine and trapezoid (Warmup-Stable-Decay) learning rate schedulers is a practical ablation. The trapezoid scheduler's consistent gains on both general benchmarks and machine translation (Table 2) — with improvements of ~1 percentage point on Hellaswag, ~0.6 points on Arc Challenge, and 0.3–0.5 COMET-22 points on translation — make a case for this scheduler as a default choice for multilingual training. The paper connects this to the annealing data phase: the trapezoid scheduler's constant-rate middle phase allows the model to process the bulk of training data at a stable learning rate, while the final decay phase coincides with the higher-quality annealing data, potentially improving the model's ability to absorb this cleaner signal.

- **Small-scale demonstration at 1.7B parameters**: The paper explicitly frames EuroLLM-1.7B as an initial model — "the work done so far in the EuroLLM project" (§7) — with future work focused on scaling up parameters and improving data quality. This positions the paper as a progress report and methodology document rather than a final, state-of-the-art result. The competitive performance against Gemma-2B and (on translation) Gemma-7B validates the approach, while the weaker performance on English-centric tasks like Arc Challenge (Figure 6) acknowledges current limitations. This honesty about trade-offs — multilingual capability gains at the cost of English-specific reasoning — distinguishes the paper from work that reports only aggregate metrics masking per-language disparities.

The paper's relationship to prior multilingual LLMs (e.g., BLOOM, mT5, XGLM, Aya) is implicit rather than explicitly compared. The focus is on the 1.7B scale and on European languages specifically, filling a niche that larger multilingual projects (which often prioritize global language coverage over European depth) may not adequately serve. The EuroLLM project's explicit EU focus — supported by EU funding (UTTER, DECOLLAGE) and EU supercomputing resources (Marenostrum 5 via EuroHPC) — reflects a deliberate alignment with European digital policy goals rather than purely academic research interests.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

The EuroLLM project constructs a complete training pipeline that produces a 1.7-billion-parameter transformer language model capable of understanding and generating text across 35 languages (all 24 official EU languages plus 11 additional relevant languages). The core engineering problem is **allocating a fixed training budget (4 trillion tokens, ~12 million tokens per batch, 256 H100 GPUs) across languages and data types so that the resulting model performs well on both general reasoning benchmarks and machine translation**, despite extreme disparities in available training data — from abundant English web text to scarce Maltese or Irish resources — and without sacrificing English performance to the point of unusability.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The EuroLLM training system has seven major components arranged in a sequential pipeline, where each component's output feeds the next:

1. **Multilingual Data Collection** — gathers raw text from web crawls, parallel corpora, code/math repositories, and high-quality curated sources across all 35 target languages, applying language-specific quality filters to produce cleaned monolingual and parallel datasets.

2. **Data Mixture Definition** — uses joint scaling laws fitted on small proxy models (100M–341M non-embedding parameters) to determine the optimal proportion of parallel data (20% per non-English language), whether to repeat high-quality data (yes, since it improves Wikipedia performance without degrading web performance), and how to allocate tokens across languages (50% English in main phase, 32.5% in annealing).

3. **Multilingual Tokenizer Training** — trains a 128,000-vocabulary Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE) tokenizer with byte-fallback on a balanced multilingual sample, using the SentencePiece framework following the LLaMA-2/Mistral design pattern.

4. **Model Architecture Configuration** — instantiates a standard dense Transformer (24 layers, 2048 embedding size, 5632 FFN hidden, 16 attention heads with 8 KV heads via grouped-query attention) with architectural choices (SwiGLU activation, RMSNorm, RoPE, pre-layer normalization) selected for training stability and inference efficiency.

5. **Pre-training with Two-Phase Curriculum** — trains on 4 trillion tokens using the trapezoid (Warmup-Stable-Decay) learning rate scheduler, with the final 10% of steps using higher-quality annealing data and a linear learning rate decay.

6. **Post-Training Instruction Fine-tuning** — fine-tunes the pre-trained model on EuroBlocks (a 1M-sample multilingual instruction dataset combining OpenHermes-2.5, Aya, and translation examples) for 4 epochs using a learning rate of 7e-6, producing the instruction-following variant EuroLLM-1.7B-Instruct.

7. **Evaluation** — measures performance on translated Hellaswag and Arc Challenge benchmarks (general reasoning) and on FLORES-200, WMT-23, and WMT-24 translation tasks (using COMET-22), comparing against Gemma-2B and TinyLlama baselines.

Information flows sequentially: raw data → filtered data → scaling-law-guided mixture ratios → tokenized training corpus → pre-trained model → instruction-tuned model → evaluation metrics. Each component's design choices are informed by downstream requirements — for instance, the tokenizer vocabulary size (128K) is chosen based on fertility analysis across languages, and the data mixture decisions are validated by scaling law extrapolation before committing to the full training run.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First, the data collection and filtering pipeline** (§3.4.1), because all downstream decisions depend on what data is available and its quality. We examine how web data, parallel data, code/math data, and high-quality data are gathered and cleaned for each language, including the specific quality thresholds and filtering tools used.

- **Second, the data mixture methodology** (§3.4.2), because it determines how the collected data is combined into the training corpus. We cover the joint scaling law framework, the parallel data proportion experiment, the high-quality data repetition analysis, and the final per-language token allocation.

- **Third, the tokenizer design** (§3.4.3), since the tokenizer defines how text is converted into model inputs and directly impacts multilinguality through fertility. We examine the vocabulary size choice, byte-fallback mechanism, and fertility comparison against Mistral, LLaMA-3, and Gemma tokenizers.

- **Fourth, the model architecture and pre-training** (§3.4.4), covering the Transformer configuration, grouped-query attention, activation function, normalization, and positional encoding choices, plus the learning rate scheduler comparison that determined the final training recipe.

- **Fifth, the post-training instruction fine-tuning** (§3.4.5), describing the EuroBlocks dataset construction, the supervised fine-tuning procedure, and the chat formatting with special control tokens.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **systems and engineering paper** whose core contribution is the documented design pipeline for a multilingual LLM, validated by the released EuroLLM-1.7B and EuroLLM-1.7B-Instruct models. The technical approach spans data curation, scaling-law-guided mixture design, tokenizer engineering, architecture selection, training curriculum, and instruction fine-tuning.

#### 3.4.1 Multilingual Data Collection and Filtering

The training corpus is assembled from four distinct data categories, each sourced and filtered using category-specific quality control pipelines. The final data mix (Figure 1) allocates different proportions to each category, with the annealing phase (final 10% of training) shifting the balance toward higher-quality data.

**Web Data Collection**

Web data forms the bulk of the training corpus. The collection strategy differs by language resource level, reflecting the reality that high-resource languages have dedicated, pre-filtered datasets available while low-resource languages require aggregation from multiple sources.

For English, the authors use the **FineWeb-edu dataset** (Lozhkov et al., 2024). This dataset has already undergone three stages of processing: individual dump deduplication (removing duplicate documents within each Common Crawl snapshot), heuristic filtering (removing documents with excessive punctuation, short length, or other quality signals), and model-based filtering where a classifier scores each document's educational value. The paper selects only documents with scores above 2 on this educational classifier, retaining higher-quality English text while discarding low-quality web content.

For other high-resource languages — explicitly named as German, Spanish, French, and Italian — the paper uses the **RedPajama-Data-v2** dataset (Together Computer, 2023), which arrives pre-deduplicated. The authors then apply two additional quality filters: a **perplexity filter** that removes documents whose token-level perplexity under a reference language model exceeds a threshold (indicating noisy or non-fluent text), and a set of **heuristic filters** (the paper does not specify these heuristics in detail, but typical heuristics include minimum/maximum length thresholds, punctuation ratio filters, and removal of documents with high proportions of non-target-language words).

For all remaining languages — the bulk of the EU's 24 official languages plus the additional non-EU languages — the paper aggregates data from four datasets: **HPLT** (de Gibert et al., 2024), **MADLAD-400** (Kudugunta et al., 2023), **CulturaX** (Nguyen et al., 2023), and **mC4** (Xue et al., 2021). These are concatenated into a single pool per language. The concatenated data then undergoes a full filtering pipeline using a preprocessing system "based on CCNet" (Wenzek et al., 2019), which performs: **deduplication** (removing near-duplicate documents across datasets), **language identification** (classifying each document's language and discarding misclassified or mixed-language documents), **perplexity filtering** (as above), and **heuristic filters** (the CCNet-style pipeline typically includes length filtering, symbol/emoji ratio checks, and boilerplate removal).

This tiered approach — dedicated high-quality English data, pre-filtered high-resource data with additional quality checks, and pooled multi-source data with comprehensive filtering for all other languages — reflects a practical compromise: invest the most curation effort where data is most abundant (to extract the highest-quality subset) and where quality filtering makes the biggest difference (low-resource languages where noisy data could dominate the small available corpus).

**Parallel Data Collection**

Parallel data consists of sentence-aligned translation pairs and document-aligned translations. The paper collects both **to-English** (xx→en) and **from-English** (en→xx) parallel data from "various public sources" (these sources are not individually enumerated in the main text, but Section 2.1 implies they include standard parallel corpora repositories).

Quality filtering for parallel data uses two complementary metrics:

- **Bicleaner** (Sánchez-Cartagena et al.; Ramírez-Sánchez et al., 2020): a classifier that estimates the probability that a sentence pair is a genuine translation, trained on features like word alignment scores, length ratios, and language model perplexities. The paper applies a threshold of **0.6 for Portuguese** and **0.5 for all other languages**. The higher threshold for Portuguese likely reflects greater availability of Portuguese parallel data and a desire for higher precision in that language pair. Pairs scoring below these thresholds are discarded.

- **COMETKIWI-22** (Rei et al., 2022b): a neural quality estimation model that predicts translation quality without requiring a reference translation. Unlike Bicleaner (which evaluates whether two sentences are mutual translations), COMETKIWI-22 evaluates whether the target-side sentence is fluent and adequate given the source. The paper applies a threshold of **0.7** across all languages, discarding pairs where the predicted quality falls below this value.

The combination of Bicleaner and COMETKIWI-22 provides two complementary quality signals: Bicleaner ensures the sentences are genuine translations of each other (addressing alignment quality), while COMETKIWI-22 ensures the translated text itself is high-quality (addressing fluency and adequacy). The double filter is conservative — sentence pairs must pass both thresholds to be retained.

**Code and Math Data**

For code data, the paper uses **the Stack** (Kocetkov et al., 2022), a 3 TB dataset of permissively licensed source code from GitHub spanning over 300 programming languages. For mathematical data, two datasets are used: **Algebraic-stack** (Azerbayev et al., 2023), which contains mathematical documents including papers, lecture notes, and forum discussions, and **Open-web-math** (Paster et al., 2023), which contains high-quality mathematical text extracted from web sources. The paper does not describe additional filtering for these datasets, implying the dataset creators' existing quality controls are considered sufficient.

**High-Quality Data**

High-quality data comes from curated sources that are assumed to contain well-written, factually accurate text:

- **Wikipedia** (Wikimedia Foundation): used for all supported languages. Wikipedia's structured editing process and community review make it a reliable source of encyclopedic knowledge in each language.
- **Arxiv** (Clement et al., 2019): scientific papers, used for English only.
- **Books** (Zhu et al., 2015): a book corpus, used for English only.
- **Apollo** (Wang et al., 2024): a medical dataset, used for English only.

The restriction of Arxiv, Books, and Apollo to English reflects the reality that high-quality domain-specific datasets are overwhelmingly available only in English, a data imbalance that the paper's language allocation strategy (§2.2.3) attempts to partially counteract.

**Annealing Data**

In the final 10% of pre-training — the annealing phase — the authors increase the proportion of high-quality data in the training mix. To select which monolingual web documents qualify as "high-quality" during this phase, they train a **binary classifier inspired by FineWeb-Edu** (Lozhkov et al., 2024) that predicts whether a document has "some educational value." This classifier is applied to the web data pool, retaining only documents above the classifier's threshold.

Additionally, the annealing phase incorporates several datasets not used in the main training:

- **Cosmopedia-v2** (Ben Allal et al., 2024): a synthetic dataset generated by Mixtral-8x7B-Instruct-v0.1 (Jiang et al., 2024) containing textbooks, blog posts, and stories. The synthetic generation process allows the dataset to cover educational content in a structured format.
- **Python-Edu** (Ben Allal et al., 2024): a subset of Python data from the Stack that has been filtered by its educational value, providing high-quality code examples with pedagogical structure.
- **GSM8K training set** (Cobbe et al., 2021): Grade School Math 8K, a dataset of grade-school math word problems with step-by-step solutions.
- **MATH training set** (Hendrycks et al., 2021): competition-level mathematics problems with solutions.
- **Europarl** (Koehn, 2005): document-level parallel data from European Parliament proceedings, available for all official EU languages.
- **ParaDocs** (Wicks et al., 2024): document-level parallel data, providing longer aligned texts than sentence-level parallel corpora.

The shift toward educational, synthetic, and parallel data during annealing reflects a curriculum learning strategy: the model first absorbs broad knowledge from diverse web data, then refines its representations using cleaner, more structured data in the final phase.

#### 3.4.2 Data Mixture Methodology

The data mixture — how the collected data is combined into a single training corpus — is determined through a combination of scaling law analysis and practical constraints. Three key decisions must be made: how much parallel data to include, whether to repeat high-quality data sources, and how to allocate tokens across languages.

**Joint Scaling Law Framework**

The paper adopts and extends the joint scaling law methodology from Fernandes et al. (2023). The core idea is that the validation loss `$L$` of a multilingual model can be predicted as a function of the number of non-embedding parameters `$N$` and the weighting `$p$` of a language in the training data:

$$L(N, p) = f(p) \cdot \beta \cdot N^{-\alpha} + L_{\infty}$$

where `$N$` is the number of non-embedding parameters (parameters in the Transformer layers, excluding the token embedding matrix and language modeling head), `$p \in [0, 1]$` is the proportion of training data allocated to a specific language, `$f(p)$` is a ratio function that captures how performance changes with language weighting, `$\beta$` is a scaling coefficient, `$\alpha$` is the scaling exponent (controlling how quickly loss decreases with model size), and `$L_{\infty}$` is the irreducible loss floor.

The ratio function `$f(p)$` is parameterized as:

$$f(p) = p + c_1 p^{c_2} (1 - p)^{c_3}$$

where `$c_1, c_2, c_3$` are empirically estimated parameters. This functional form captures several plausible behaviors: when `$p$` is very small (a language with little data), the second term can model the benefit of even a small allocation; when `$p$` approaches 1 (English in many models), the first term dominates, representing the baseline relationship between data quantity and loss.

**What it computes:** given a proposed language weighting `$p$` and model size `$N$`, the equation predicts the expected validation loss for that language. By fitting the parameters `$\alpha, \beta, L_{\infty}, c_1, c_2, c_3$` on small models (100M, 203M, 341M non-embedding parameters) trained with different language weightings, the authors can extrapolate to larger models and novel weightings without training them.

**Why this form:** the power-law relationship `$N^{-\alpha}$` is the standard scaling law form from Kaplan et al. (2020), which has been empirically validated across many model families. The ratio function `$f(p)$` adds a language-weighting dimension that captures how performance for a given language depends on its representation in the training data. The specific functional form allows the model to capture diminishing returns (as `$p$` increases, the second term decreases since `$(1-p)^{c_3}$` shrinks) and the interaction between data quantity and model scale (since `$f(p)$` multiplies the model-size-dependent term).

**Determining Parallel Data Proportion**

To decide how much parallel data to include for each non-English language, the authors train small models (100M, 203M, 341M non-embedding parameters) on a 100B-token corpus where parallel data constitutes different percentages of the total data for each non-English language: **0%, 25%, and 37.5%**. English data remains unchanged across these experiments. The models are evaluated on three test set domains: web data, Wikipedia data, and parallel data.

The scaling laws fitted to these experiments (Figure 2) reveal several patterns:

- **Web and Wikipedia domain performance is essentially unaffected by parallel data proportion.** The scaling law curves for 0%, 25%, and 37.5% parallel data largely overlap on web and Wikipedia test sets, indicating that adding parallel data does not crowd out or degrade monolingual knowledge.
- **Parallel data domain performance improves substantially with parallel data.** The scaling law curves for the parallel data test set show a clear upward shift when moving from 0% to 25% parallel data, confirming that parallel data improves the model's ability to handle translation-like tasks.
- **Diminishing returns appear between 25% and 37.5%.** The performance improvement from 25% to 37.5% is smaller than from 0% to 25%, suggesting that beyond a certain threshold, additional parallel data provides limited benefit relative to its opportunity cost (it occupies token slots that could be filled with monolingual data).

Based on these findings, the paper selects **20% parallel data per non-English language** for the final corpus. This choice is slightly below the 25% tested in the scaling law experiments, reflecting a conservative approach that ensures ample monolingual data while still capturing most of the parallel data benefit.

The scaling law methodology provides confidence in this decision: rather than relying on heuristics or single-scale ablations, the authors have evidence that the 20% choice approximates the optimum across a range of model sizes (since the scaling laws are fitted on multiple model sizes and extrapolate smoothly).

**Determining Whether to Repeat High-Quality Data**

The second mixture decision concerns data repetition: should high-quality datasets like Wikipedia be repeated (epoch-ed multiple times) within the training corpus, or should each document appear at most once? Repeating data risks overfitting — the model might memorize specific Wikipedia articles rather than learning generalizable language patterns. However, if high-quality data is scarce (as it is for many low-resource languages), repeating it may be the only way to ensure the model sees enough clean text.

To test this, the authors train models on two 100B-token corpora: one where Wikipedia data is repeated for all languages (i.e., Wikipedia documents appear multiple times), and one where it is not (each Wikipedia document appears at most once, and the remaining tokens are filled with web data). The scaling laws fitted to these experiments (Figure 3) show:

- **Wikipedia test set performance improves when Wikipedia data is repeated.** The scaling law curve for the "repeated" condition is above the "not repeated" condition on Wikipedia test sets, indicating that the model benefits from multiple exposures to high-quality encyclopedic text.
- **Web test set performance is essentially unaffected by Wikipedia repetition.** The scaling law curves for web data overlap between the two conditions, indicating that repeating Wikipedia does not degrade the model's ability to handle general web text.

This finding is non-obvious. One might worry that repeating a small, clean dataset while not repeating a large, noisy dataset would cause the model to overfit to the clean data's style (formal, encyclopedic) at the expense of general web text fluency. The scaling law analysis suggests this worry is unfounded: the model benefits from the additional high-quality signal without losing its ability to process diverse web text.

Based on this evidence, the paper decides to **repeat data from high-quality datasets** in the final training corpus. The exact repetition factor is not specified, but the principle is to ensure that high-quality data constitutes a meaningful fraction of total training tokens even when the raw volume of such data is small.

**Language Allocation Strategy**

The allocation of the total training corpus across languages (Figure 4) follows a two-phase strategy:

**Main training phase (first 90% of tokens):**
- **English: 50%** of the total tokens. The paper states this is because "both high-quality data and web data are predominantly in English."
- **Code/math: 5%** of the total tokens.
- **All other languages combined: 45%** of the total tokens, distributed among the 34 non-English languages "based on the amount of data obtained after the collection and filtering processes." This means the allocation is proportional to data availability: languages with more filtered data (e.g., German, French) receive larger shares, while languages with less data (e.g., Maltese, Irish) receive smaller shares. The paper does not provide the exact per-language percentages in the main text, but Figure 4 displays them.

**Annealing phase (final 10% of tokens):**
- **English: reduced to 32.5%** (from 50%). The 17.5 percentage points freed up are redistributed across the other languages.
- **Code/math: increased to 7%** (from 5%).
- **All other languages: increased proportionally**, receiving the tokens freed from the English reduction.

This reallocation during annealing implements a curriculum strategy: in the early phase, the model builds strong English capabilities (which can transfer to other languages via cross-lingual transfer) and absorbs broad world knowledge from English-dominated web data; in the final phase, the model shifts focus to other languages, reinforcing multilingual representations while high-quality annealing data helps prevent catastrophic forgetting of English.

The specific English percentages (50%, then 32.5%) are not derived from scaling laws — they represent a design choice that balances English capability (important for benchmark performance and practical utility) against multilinguality (the project's core goal). The paper does not ablate these percentages, so the choice should be understood as a reasonable starting point rather than an empirically validated optimum.

#### 3.4.3 Multilingual Tokenizer Design

The tokenizer converts raw text into sequences of integer token IDs that the model processes. For multilingual models, tokenizer design involves a critical trade-off: **vocabulary size versus fertility (pieces per word)**. A small vocabulary means individual words are split into many subword pieces, increasing sequence length for a given semantic content and reducing the effective context window. A large vocabulary reduces fertility but increases the number of embedding parameters — each token in the vocabulary requires a `$d_{\text{model}}$`-dimensional embedding vector, so a vocabulary of size `$V$` with embedding dimension `$d_{\text{model}}$` consumes `$V \times d_{\text{model}}$` parameters.

**Tokenizer Training Procedure**

The EuroLLM tokenizer follows the approach used by LLaMA-2 (Touvron et al., 2023) and Mistral (Jiang et al., 2023): a **Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE) tokenizer with byte-fallback**, trained using the **SentencePiece** framework (Kudo & Richardson, 2018).

BPE works by starting with individual characters as the initial vocabulary and iteratively merging the most frequent adjacent token pairs until the target vocabulary size is reached. For example, if "th" and "e" frequently appear together, they get merged into "the"; then "the" and "re" might merge into "there." The byte-fallback mechanism ensures that any Unicode character, even those not seen during tokenizer training, can be encoded by falling back to its UTF-8 byte representation — this prevents the "unknown token" problem that plagued earlier tokenizers.

The key design decision is the vocabulary size. The authors state: "Through experimentation, we reach the conclusion that a vocabulary of **128,000 pieces** provides the best trade-off." The experimentation details are not provided, but the trade-off dimensions are clear:

- **Too small** (e.g., Mistral's 32,000): high fertility for non-English languages, meaning each word requires many tokens, compressing the effective context window and making the model computationally inefficient for non-English text.
- **Too large** (e.g., Gemma's 256,000): low fertility, but the embedding layer becomes enormous. For EuroLLM-1.7B with `$d_{\text{model}} = 2048$`, a 256K vocabulary would require `$256{,}000 \times 2048 = 524$` million embedding parameters — nearly one-third of all model parameters consumed by the embedding layer alone.

At 128,000 tokens with `$d_{\text{model}} = 2048$`, the embedding layer consumes `$128{,}000 \times 2048 \approx 262$` million parameters. Table 1 confirms this: EuroLLM-1.7B allocates 0.262B parameters to embeddings and 0.262B to the LM head (which also has a `$V \times d_{\text{model}}$` weight matrix for projecting hidden states back to vocabulary probabilities), for a total of 0.524B embedding-related parameters out of 1.657B total — approximately 32%.

**Fertility Analysis**

Figure 5 presents fertility — measured as pieces per word — for a subset of EuroLLM languages across four tokenizers: Mistral (32K vocab), LLaMA-3 (128,256 vocab), Gemma (256K vocab), and EuroLLM (128K vocab). The analysis reveals language-specific patterns:

- **For English**, LLaMA-3 achieves the lowest fertility, followed by EuroLLM and Gemma (similar), with Mistral substantially higher. This is expected: LLaMA-3's tokenizer was optimized for English, so it encodes English text most efficiently.
- **For most European languages** (the paper highlights German, Spanish, French, Italian, and several others in Figure 5), EuroLLM and Gemma achieve similar, low fertility rates, both substantially better than Mistral. LLaMA-3, despite its 128K vocabulary, shows higher fertility for many non-English European languages — suggesting its tokenizer training data was English-skewed, causing the BPE merges to favor English character sequences.
- **For Asian languages** (the paper shows Japanese, Korean, Chinese, Hindi in Figure 5), Gemma achieves the lowest fertility, with EuroLLM second-best and Mistral/LLaMA-3 both significantly worse. The Gemma tokenizer's extremely large vocabulary (256K) allows it to store many complete characters or short character sequences for logographic writing systems like Chinese and Japanese, reducing the need to break characters into sub-character pieces.

The fertility analysis demonstrates that EuroLLM's 128K vocabulary achieves its intended goal: it substantially improves non-English encoding efficiency compared to the 32K Mistral tokenizer (the baseline for the BPE-with-byte-fallback approach) while avoiding the parameter bloat of the 256K Gemma tokenizer. The comparison with LLaMA-3's similarly-sized 128K vocabulary is particularly informative — it shows that vocabulary size alone is insufficient; the tokenizer's training data distribution matters enormously. EuroLLM's balanced multilingual training data produces better fertility for European languages than LLaMA-3's presumably English-skewed training data, even at nearly identical vocabulary sizes.

The practical implication of lower fertility is concrete: if a Finnish sentence requires 30 tokens under the EuroLLM tokenizer but 50 tokens under Mistral's, the EuroLLM model processes 40% fewer tokens for the same semantic content, effectively extending its context window by 40% for Finnish text and reducing the computational cost of Finnish inference by 40%.

#### 3.4.4 Model Architecture and Pre-Training

**Architecture Configuration**

EuroLLM uses a **standard, dense Transformer architecture** (Vaswani et al., 2017) with specific component choices optimized for training stability and inference efficiency. Table 1 provides the complete hyperparameter specification for the 1.7B model:

| Hyperparameter | Value |
|---|---|
| Sequence Length | 4,096 |
| Number of Layers | 24 |
| Embedding Size (`$d_{\text{model}}$`) | 2,048 |
| FFN Hidden Size | 5,632 |
| Number of Heads | 16 |
| Number of KV Heads (GQA) | 8 |
| Activation Function | SwiGLU |
| Position Encodings | RoPE (`$\Theta = 10{,}000$`) |
| Layer Norm | RMSNorm |
| Tied Embeddings | No |
| Max Learning Rate | `$3 \times 10^{-4}$` |
| Min Learning Rate | `$3 \times 10^{-5}$` |
| Embedding Parameters | 0.262B |
| LM Head Parameters | 0.262B |
| Non-embedding Parameters | 1.133B |
| Total Parameters | 1.657B |

The architecture decisions are justified individually:

**Grouped Query Attention (GQA)** with 8 key-value (KV) heads and 16 query heads. In standard multi-head attention, the number of query heads equals the number of key and value heads. GQA (Ainslie et al., 2023) reduces the number of KV heads by sharing them across multiple query heads — here, every 2 query heads share 1 KV head (16 query heads ÷ 8 KV heads = 2 query heads per KV head). This reduces the size of the key and value caches during inference (since KV states must be stored for all previous tokens in autoregressive generation) while maintaining most of the expressivity of full multi-head attention. The paper cites evidence that GQA "increases speed at inference time while maintaining downstream performance" (Team et al., 2024b).

**Pre-layer normalization** (Xiong et al., 2020) applied before each sublayer (attention and FFN), rather than post-layer normalization (applied after). Pre-norm improves training stability by ensuring that the input to each sublayer is normalized, preventing exploding activations in deep networks. The specific normalization used is **RMSNorm** (Zhang & Sennrich, 2019), which normalizes by the root mean square of activations rather than by mean and variance (as in LayerNorm; Ba et al., 2016). RMSNorm is computationally faster (no mean subtraction required) while providing similar training stability benefits.

**SwiGLU activation function** (Shazeer, 2020) in the feed-forward network layers. The SwiGLU is a gated variant of the Swish activation:

$$\text{SwiGLU}(x) = \text{Swish}(xW_1) \odot (xW_2)$$

where `$W_1$` and `$W_2$` are learned weight matrices, `$\odot$` is element-wise multiplication, and `$\text{Swish}(x) = x \cdot \sigma(x)$` with `$\sigma$` being the sigmoid function.

**What it computes:** the input `$x$` is projected through two different linear transformations. One projection passes through the Swish nonlinearity (which acts as a soft gating mechanism — allowing values to pass through when positive, suppressing them when negative), and the result is multiplied element-wise with the other (linear) projection. This creates a gated linear unit where one branch controls how much of the other branch's information passes through.

**Why this form:** Shazeer (2020) showed that SwiGLU and related gated variants consistently outperform standard ReLU and GELU activations on downstream language tasks. The gating mechanism allows the network to learn more complex interactions — essentially, the network can learn when to suppress or amplify specific feature dimensions based on the input content. The paper cites its use in prior work (Le Scao et al., 2022) as evidence of its effectiveness.

**Rotary Positional Embeddings (RoPE)** (Su et al., 2024) with base frequency `$\Theta = 10{,}000$`, applied in every layer. RoPE encodes position information by rotating the query and key vectors in attention by an angle proportional to their position index. Specifically, for a token at position `$m$`, its query/key vector of dimension `$d$` is split into pairs of dimensions, and each pair `$(x_{2i}, x_{2i+1})$` is rotated by angle `$m \cdot \Theta^{-2i/d}$`. This creates position-dependent attention scores without adding positional embeddings to the input — the position information is encoded in the relative rotation between query and key vectors.

**Why this form:** RoPE has two key advantages over absolute positional embeddings. First, it naturally encodes relative position (the attention score between positions `$m$` and `$n$` depends only on `$m - n$`, not on absolute positions), which is what attention mechanisms fundamentally need. Second, it can be extended to sequence lengths beyond those seen during training by interpolating the rotation frequencies, enabling context length extension without retraining.

**Tied embeddings: No.** This means the input embedding matrix (mapping token IDs to `$d_{\text{model}}$`-dimensional vectors) and the output projection matrix (mapping `$d_{\text{model}}$`-dimensional hidden states to vocabulary probabilities) are separate, independently learned parameters. Each consumes 0.262B parameters (128,000 × 2048). Tying embeddings would save 0.262B parameters but constrains the input and output representations to be identical, which can be suboptimal for the language modeling head (which benefits from output-specific representations that differ from input embeddings). The paper's choice to keep them separate reflects a prioritization of model quality over parameter efficiency.

**Training Configuration**

Pre-training runs on **4 trillion tokens** using **256 Nvidia H100 GPUs** on the Marenostrum 5 supercomputer. The batch size is constant at **3,072 sequences**, with sequence length 4,096, giving approximately **12 million tokens per batch** (3,072 × 4,096 ≈ 12.58 million). Training uses the **Adam optimizer** (Kingma & Ba, 2014) with **bfloat16 mixed precision** (where most computations use the 16-bit bfloat16 format for speed, but critical accumulations use 32-bit float for numerical stability).

**Learning Rate Scheduler Comparison**

The paper compares two learning rate scheduler options, conducting an empirical ablation to determine which yields better multilingual performance:

**Option 1: Cosine scheduler.** This follows a standard cosine decay: the learning rate starts near zero, increases linearly during a warm-up phase covering 10% of total training steps, then follows a half-cosine curve decaying to the minimum learning rate. The cosine decay is smooth and monotonic after the warm-up.

**Option 2: Trapezoid scheduler** (also called Warmup-Stable-Decay; Xing et al., 2018; Hu et al., 2024). This has three distinct phases:
1. **Warm-up** (10% of steps): linear increase from zero to the maximum learning rate (`$3 \times 10^{-4}$`).
2. **Stable** (middle portion): constant learning rate at the maximum value. The paper does not specify the exact duration of the stable phase, but given that warm-up is 10% and the final decay is 10%, the stable phase occupies approximately 80% of training steps.
3. **Decay** (final 10% of steps): linear decrease from the maximum to the minimum learning rate (`$3 \times 10^{-5}$`). This decay phase coincides with the annealing data phase.

The results in Table 2 compare the two schedulers on Hellaswag (commonsense reasoning), Arc Challenge (science exam questions), and machine translation (FLORES-200, WMT-23, WMT-24 via COMET-22):

| Model | Hellaswag | Arc Challenge | FLORES-200 | WMT-23 | WMT-24 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cosine | 0.4646 | 0.3206 | 86.48 | 82.88 | 78.87 |
| Trapezoid | 0.4744 | 0.3268 | 86.75 | 83.13 | 79.35 |

The trapezoid scheduler yields consistent improvements: approximately +1 percentage point on Hellaswag, +0.6 points on Arc Challenge, and +0.27-0.48 COMET points on translation. These gains are modest but consistent across all five evaluation settings, suggesting a genuine (if small) benefit rather than noise.

**Why the trapezoid scheduler works better:** the authors connect this to the data curriculum. The stable (constant learning rate) phase allows the model to process the bulk of training data — which is predominantly web data — at full learning capacity, extracting maximum signal from each batch. The final decay phase, which uses a decreasing learning rate, coincides with the switch to higher-quality annealing data. This pairing may be synergistic: the decaying learning rate allows the model to "settle" into a good local minimum while being exposed to cleaner, more structured data that is easier to fit. In contrast, a cosine scheduler continuously decreases the learning rate throughout training, potentially under-training on the early web data (where a higher learning rate would extract more signal) while not giving the annealing data a sufficiently distinct final fitting phase.

The paper's decision to use the trapezoid scheduler for the final model is based on this empirical comparison, and the released EuroLLM-1.7B uses this scheduler.

#### 3.4.5 Post-Training Instruction Fine-Tuning

To convert the pre-trained EuroLLM-1.7B into an instruction-following conversational model, the paper performs supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on a custom multilingual instruction dataset called **EuroBlocks**.

**EuroBlocks Dataset Construction**

EuroBlocks combines three data sources to create a 1M-sample dataset covering all supported languages and a variety of tasks:

- **OpenHermes-2.5** (Teknium, 2023): an open dataset of synthetic instruction-following conversations. These conversations were generated by prompting strong LLMs to produce diverse instruction-response pairs, covering coding, reasoning, creative writing, and general question-answering. OpenHermes-2.5 is English-dominated but provides high-quality, diverse task coverage.

- **Aya dataset** (Singh et al., 2024): a multilingual instruction-tuning dataset covering 101 languages, created by a global collaborative effort. The Aya dataset includes human-written and template-generated instructions with responses in the target language, providing native-language instruction-following examples across many of EuroLLM's target languages.

- **High-quality machine translation examples** from four sources:
  - **NTREX-128** (Federmann et al., 2022): news test references for 128 languages, providing news-domain translation examples.
  - **FLORES-200-DEV** (Team et al., 2022): the development set from the FLORES-200 benchmark, covering 200 languages with professional translations.
  - **WMT-21** (Farhad et al., 2021): the Workshop on Machine Translation 2021 test sets, providing high-quality, human-translated news domain data.
  - **WMT-22** (Kocmi et al., 2022): the WMT 2022 test sets, continuing the news translation domain coverage.

The inclusion of translation examples is strategically important: by training the model to explicitly perform translation as one of its instruction-following tasks, the EuroBlocks dataset reinforces the cross-lingual alignment learned during pre-training and ensures the instruction-tuned model retains translation capability.

**Supervised Fine-Tuning Procedure**

Fine-tuning uses **standard cross-entropy loss** with two key implementation details:

- **Loss masking:** the loss is calculated only on target tokens (the assistant's response), not on prompt tokens (the user's instruction and any system prompt). This prevents the model from being penalized for not predicting the user's input — it only needs to learn to generate appropriate responses. The masking is implemented by setting the loss weight to zero for all tokens in the prompt portion of each training example.

- **Packing:** multiple training examples are concatenated into a single sequence to maximize GPU utilization. Without packing, short examples would waste compute because the full sequence length (4,096 tokens) must be processed regardless of how many tokens are actually useful. Packing fills the sequence with multiple examples, separated by attention masks that prevent cross-example attention (so Example 2 cannot attend to tokens from Example 1). This increases the effective batch size in terms of training examples per GPU step.

The training hyperparameters are: **4 epochs** over the 1M-sample dataset, a **learning rate of `$7 \times 10^{-6}$`** (substantially lower than the pre-training max learning rate of `$3 \times 10^{-4}$`, reflecting the smaller dataset and the desire for gentle refinement rather than large weight changes), and approximately **6,000 training steps**. Training uses **bfloat16 mixed precision**, consistent with pre-training.

**Chat Formatting with Control Tokens**

The fine-tuned model uses a chat format with special control tokens that were included in the original tokenizer vocabulary (Section 3) — no new tokens are added during SFT:

- `<|im start|>`: marks the beginning of a turn (user, assistant, or system).
- `<|im end|>`: marks the end of a turn.

These tokens create explicit boundaries between different speakers in a conversation. The `<|im end|>` token is particularly important: the paper states that the **end-of-sequence token is overwritten with the `<|im end|>` token**, meaning the model learns that `<|im end|>` signals both the end of a turn and the end of generation. This unified treatment simplifies the generation process — the model naturally stops when it produces the `<|im end|>` token.

The chat template supports a **system prompt** that can be used "for added functionality and steerability of the model's responses." Table 3 provides an example where the system prompt is "Translate all user texts to English," and the model consistently translates the user's non-English messages to English — demonstrating that the system prompt effectively constrains the model's behavior, a capability learned during fine-tuning from examples with system prompts.

The conversation structure in Table 3 shows the exact formatting:

```
<s><|im start|>system
Translate all user texts to English.<|im end|>
<|im start|>user
A Comissão Europeia é uma instituição...<|im end|>
<|im start|>assistant
The European Commission is a politically independent institution...<|im end|>
<|im start|>user
La Comisión Europea no consta únicamente...<|im end|>
<|im start|>assistant
The European Commission is not only composed...<|im end|>
```

The model is trained to predict everything after each `<|im start|>assistant` token, with the loss computed only on the assistant's text (up to and including the `<|im end|>` token). At inference time, the user provides text following `<|im start|>user`, optionally preceded by a system prompt, and the model generates the assistant's response autoregressively, stopping when it produces `<|im end|>`.

The decision to include control tokens in the original tokenizer (rather than adding them during SFT) is a practical one: adding new tokens to an already-trained tokenizer requires resizing the embedding matrix and LM head, and the new token embeddings must be learned from scratch during fine-tuning (since they weren't part of pre-training). By including these tokens from the start, their embeddings benefit from the full pre-training process, leading to better initialization and presumably more robust behavior.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Parallel Data as a Harmless Regularizer for Multilingual LLMs

The paper's most conceptually distinctive finding is that adding parallel data (translation pairs) to a multilingual LLM's training corpus improves translation performance **without degrading monolingual performance on non-translation tasks**. This is not obvious, and prior work lacked rigorous empirical grounding for the trade-off.

**What the field assumed before this work:** The default intuition — and a reasonable concern — was that parallel data would create a zero-sum competition for the model's parameter budget. The worry was that forcing the model to encode the same content in two languages might encourage "shortcut" learning (memorizing translation correspondences rather than developing deep language understanding) or crowd out monolingual knowledge — essentially, that every byte of parallel data comes at the expense of monolingual data that teaches the model about the world.

**What this paper shows:** The joint scaling law analysis in Figure 2 (§2.2.1) reveals that the scaling law curves for web and Wikipedia test sets **largely overlap** across 0%, 25%, and 37.5% parallel data conditions, while the parallel data test set shows a clear upward shift. The functional separation is striking: parallel data improves translation in a nearly additive way, without the trade-off curves one would expect if it harmed general language capability. The paper interprets this as evidence that parallel data acts as a **beneficial regularizer for cross-lingual alignment** rather than as competing content. The 20% allocation chosen for the final model represents the point where translation gains remain substantial while the diminishing returns observed between 25% and 37.5% make further increases cost-ineffective.

**Why this is significant beyond the numbers:** This finding resolves what was previously an untested assumption. It means multilingual LLM builders can include parallel data **without needing to worry about a hidden monolingual penalty** — the benefit is essentially free. This is a practical insight that changes how one thinks about data mixture design: rather than treating parallel data as a specialized ingredient that must be carefully balanced against monolingual data, it can be added as an independent lever that controls translation quality without interacting negatively with other capabilities. The scaling law methodology makes this insight robust because it is validated across multiple model sizes (100M–341M non-embedding parameters), suggesting the separation holds across scales rather than being an artifact of a single model configuration.

**Tie to evidence:** Figure 2 — the parallel data scaling law curves for web and Wikipedia domains overlapping while the parallel domain curve shifts upward — is the key visual evidence. The decision to use 20% parallel data (slightly below the 25% tested in scaling law experiments) provides implicit confirmation: the benefit is sufficiently robust that even a conservative allocation captures it.

---

### Innovation 2: High-Quality Data Repetition as a Free Lunch for Multilingual Training

The paper's second conceptually significant finding is that repeating high-quality datasets (specifically Wikipedia) during multilingual LLM training improves performance on similar high-quality test domains **without any detectable degradation on general web text performance**. This is a non-trivial empirical result that challenges a reasonable prior about overfitting.

**What the field assumed before this work:** The standard cautionary principle in LLM training is that repeating data risks memorization and overfitting — the model may learn to reproduce specific Wikipedia articles verbatim rather than acquiring generalizable language patterns. For multilingual models, this concern is amplified because the total amount of high-quality data in low-resource languages is tiny: repeating the Latvian or Maltese Wikipedia 10 times to fill the training budget means those articles dominate the model's exposure to those languages, potentially biasing it toward encyclopedic style and fact patterns. The default approach in many projects was to avoid deliberate repetition of any data source, trusting that diversity (even if noisier) would produce more robust models.

**What this paper shows:** The scaling law analysis in Figure 3 (§2.2.2) compares models trained with and without Wikipedia repetition. The Wikipedia test set performance improves noticeably under repetition — the model genuinely benefits from multiple exposures to clean, structured encyclopedic text. Critically, the web test set performance is essentially identical between the two conditions. The interpretation is that Wikipedia's high quality and broad topical coverage make it a particularly effective training signal, and that **the model's capacity is sufficient at this scale to absorb multiple passes without overfitting** in a way that harms generalization to other domains.

**Why this is significant beyond the numbers:** This finding provides principled justification for a practice that many LLM builders already follow heuristically — oversampling high-quality data sources. But without rigorous evidence, this practice was vulnerable to the critique that it might be silently damaging the model's robustness. The paper's scaling law evidence transforms this from a folk wisdom into an empirically validated design principle. Moreover, it suggests that the quality-diversity trade-off is not symmetric: you can gain quality (via repetition) without losing diversity (generalization to other domains), at least up to the repetition factors tested here. This is a conceptual shift that should influence how future multilingual models are designed — "repeat the good stuff" becomes a default rather than a risk to be minimized.

**Tie to evidence:** Figure 3 — the Wikipedia test set curve shifting upward under repetition while the web test set curve remains overlapping — is the direct evidence. The paper's decision to "repeat data from high-quality datasets" in the final training corpus (stated in §2.2.2) operationalizes this finding.

---

### Innovation 3: Tokenizer Fertility as a Diagnostic Tool for Multilingual Model Design

The paper's fertility analysis in Figure 5 (§3) introduces a concrete, interpretable diagnostic framework for evaluating tokenizer quality across languages. While fertility (pieces per word) is a known metric, the paper's contribution is using it as a **systematic comparison tool** that reveals previously hidden design failures in widely-used tokenizers.

**What the field assumed before this work:** The dominant approach to tokenizer evaluation in LLM papers was either English-centric (reporting bits-per-character or compression ratios only for English) or vocabulary-size-centric (treating larger vocabulary as straightforwardly better for multilinguality). The implicit assumption was that a tokenizer trained on a representative data mix would produce reasonable fertility across all languages, and that detailed per-language diagnostics were unnecessary. The Mistral tokenizer (32K vocabulary), widely adopted as a strong baseline, was not typically critiqued for its non-English fertility because no one was systematically measuring it.

**What this paper shows:** Figure 5 reveals a diagnostic pattern that vocabulary size alone cannot explain. The LLaMA-3 tokenizer (128,256 vocabulary — nearly identical to EuroLLM's 128,000) achieves the lowest English fertility but **higher fertility than EuroLLM for most other European languages**. This means LLaMA-3's BPE merges were skewed toward English character sequences during training, producing a tokenizer that is efficient for English but compressed for other languages. Conversely, the Gemma tokenizer (256K vocabulary) achieves low fertility across languages but at a steep parameter cost. The EuroLLM tokenizer's 128K choice emerges as a genuine Pareto improvement: it achieves Gemma-level fertility for European languages while using half the vocabulary size, and it achieves LLaMA-3-level English fertility while substantially improving non-English European language fertility.

**Why this is significant beyond the numbers:** The fertility analysis transforms tokenizer selection from a black-box hyperparameter optimization problem into a **transparent, language-by-language diagnostic procedure**. It reveals that two tokenizers with identical architecture and similar vocabulary sizes can have radically different multilingual behavior depending on their training data distribution — a finding that prior work obscured by reporting only aggregate metrics. The practical implication is that any team building a multilingual LLM should compute and report fertility per target language, not just overall compression statistics. The paper effectively provides a template for this analysis (compare your tokenizer against several baselines on a subset of representative languages, look for systematic disparities), which is a methodological contribution independent of the specific EuroLLM tokenizer design.

**Tie to evidence:** Figure 5 — showing EuroLLM fertility matching Gemma for European languages while LLaMA-3 lags, and Mistral lagging everywhere — is the key exhibit. The specific example in the text ("if a Finnish sentence requires 30 tokens under the EuroLLM tokenizer but 50 tokens under Mistral's") translates the fertility metric into the concrete operational consequence of a 40% effective context window reduction for Finnish under the smaller tokenizer.

### Innovation 4: The Trapezoid Scheduler as a Curriculum-Aware Training Strategy

The paper's comparison between cosine and trapezoid (Warmup-Stable-Decay) learning rate schedulers (§4.1, Table 2) surfaces a connection between the learning rate schedule and data curriculum that constitutes a modest but generalizable design insight: **a learning rate schedule with a long stable phase followed by a distinct decay phase can exploit a two-phase data curriculum more effectively than a smooth cosine decay.**

**What the field assumed before this work:** Cosine schedulers have been the de facto standard in LLM pre-training since the original Transformer and GPT papers, largely for historical reasons — they work, they are smooth, and the field converged on them without strong evidence that they are optimal. The trapezoid/warmup-stable-decay scheduler existed in the literature (Xing et al., 2018; Hu et al., 2024) but was not systematically compared against cosine for multilingual LLM training where the data distribution itself changes during training (via the annealing data phase). The default assumption was that the learning rate schedule and data curriculum were independent design choices.

**What this paper shows:** Table 2 provides consistent, albeit modest, gains from the trapezoid scheduler across five evaluation settings: approximately +1 point on Hellaswag, +0.6 on Arc Challenge, and +0.3–0.5 COMET points on translation. The paper's interpretation links this to the data curriculum: the stable phase allows the model to extract maximum signal from the diverse but noisy web data (where a high, constant learning rate enables broad exploration), while the final decay phase coincides with the cleaner annealing data (where a decreasing learning rate enables fine-tuning on the higher-quality signal). The cosine scheduler, by decreasing the learning rate continuously, may under-explore during the web data phase and over-smooth during the annealing phase.

**Why this is significant beyond the numbers:** The insight is not that the trapezoid scheduler is universally better — the gains are small and the comparison is a single ablation on a single model scale. Rather, the significance is in the **coupling between scheduler and curriculum**. The paper effectively demonstrates that when your training pipeline has an explicit data quality transition (as EuroLLM's does with the annealing phase), your learning rate schedule should be designed to complement it — a stable high-LR phase for broad data and a decaying phase for curated data. This is a design principle that should generalize: any training pipeline with explicit data phases (e.g., pre-training then continued pre-training on domain-specific data, or multi-stage curricula) should consider whether a trapezoid-like scheduler would exploit those phases better than a smoothly decaying one. The paper does not claim this as a major theoretical contribution, but as a practical engineering insight that future multilingual LLM builders can adopt with low risk (the implementation is trivial, and the comparison in Table 2 suggests it is unlikely to hurt).

**Tie to evidence:** Table 2 — the trapezoid row outperforming the cosine row on all five columns — is the evidence. The absolute differences are small, but the consistency (5/5 settings show improvement) argues against noise. The paper's decision to use the trapezoid scheduler for the released model operationalizes this finding.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Datasets.** The paper evaluates on two categories: general reasoning benchmarks and machine translation benchmarks. For general reasoning, the authors use **Hellaswag** (Zellers et al., 2019) — a commonsense natural language inference task where the model must choose the most plausible sentence completion — and **Arc Challenge** (Clark et al., 2018) — a set of multiple-choice science exam questions. Since both benchmarks are originally English-only, the authors use **translated versions from Lai et al. (2023)** to evaluate multilingual performance across the supported languages. For machine translation, the evaluation uses three datasets: **FLORES-200-TEST** (Team et al., 2022), a many-to-many translation benchmark covering 200 languages with professional translations; **WMT-23** (Kocmi et al., 2023), the Workshop on Machine Translation 2023 test sets; and **WMT-24** (Kocmi et al., 2024), the 2024 edition test sets. Translation quality is measured using COMET-22 (Rei et al., 2022a), a neural metric that predicts human judgments of translation quality.

- **Base model(s).** The primary model evaluated is **EuroLLM-1.7B**, a 1.657B-parameter dense Transformer (1.133B non-embedding parameters) trained on 4 trillion tokens across 35 languages, and its instruction-tuned variant **EuroLLM-1.7B-Instruct**, fine-tuned on the EuroBlocks dataset. The choice of 1.7B scale is explicitly framed as an initial model in the EuroLLM project pipeline (§7), not as a final state-of-the-art system — it serves as a demonstration that the data mixture, tokenizer, and training methodology produce competitive multilingual performance even at modest parameter counts.

- **Metrics.** General reasoning benchmarks use **accuracy** — the fraction of questions where the model selects the correct answer. Hellaswag is evaluated using **10-shot prompting** (10 demonstration examples provided in-context) and Arc Challenge using **25-shot prompting**. Both tasks are multiple-choice, so accuracy corresponds to whether the model assigns highest probability to the correct option. Machine translation is evaluated using **COMET-22** (Rei et al., 2022a), a reference-based neural metric trained to predict human translation quality judgments. COMET-22 scores range approximately from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating better translation quality. Translations are generated using **0-shot prompts** (no in-context translation examples) and **greedy search** (selecting the most probable token at each step, not sampling).

- **Baselines.** Two open-weight models serve as comparison points: **Gemma-2B** (Team et al., 2024a), a 2B-parameter model (1.981B non-embedding parameters, roughly 75% more non-embedding parameters than EuroLLM-1.7B's 1.133B) from Google DeepMind, and **TinyLlama** (Zhang et al., 2024), a 1.1B-parameter open-source model trained on 3 trillion tokens. For the machine translation comparisons, the authors also include **Gemma-7B** (Team et al., 2024a) as a stronger baseline. To ensure fair comparison, Gemma-2B and Gemma-7B are also **fine-tuned on the same EuroBlocks dataset** as EuroLLM-1.7B-Instruct, producing Gemma-2B-Instruct and Gemma-7B-Instruct. This is a critical methodological choice: without this matching fine-tuning step, any comparison would confound pre-training quality with instruction-tuning quality.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** All evaluations use deterministic greedy decoding (no beam search, no sampling, no best-of-N), so there is no explicit test-time compute budget to account for. The implicit compute budget is 1 token generated per inference step. The paper does not discuss inference FLOPs or latency for any evaluation. For pre-training compute, the paper states that EuroLLM-1.7B was trained on 4 trillion tokens using 256 Nvidia H100 GPUs (§4), but does not provide total GPU-hours or FLOP counts. For the baselines, the paper does not report pre-training compute for Gemma-2B, Gemma-7B, or TinyLlama, making FLOPs-matched comparisons impossible at this stage (the FLOPs-matched analysis framework from the EuroLLM paper's reference paper — the one analyzed in the prior sections — is notably absent here).

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not describe any cross-validation protocol, statistical significance testing, or confidence intervals for any result. All reported numbers in Figures 6, 7, 8 and Table 2 appear to be point estimates from single evaluation runs. The number of evaluation examples per language is not reported — for Hellaswag and Arc Challenge, the per-language test set sizes from Lai et al. (2023) are not cited, and for translation, the numbers of sentence pairs per language pair in FLORES-200-TEST, WMT-23, and WMT-24 are not specified. The lack of variance estimates means that small differences (e.g., Hellaswag 0.4744 vs. 0.4646 in Table 2, a ~1 percentage point gap) cannot be assessed for statistical reliability.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### General Reasoning Benchmarks (Hellaswag and Arc Challenge)

Figure 6 presents per-language accuracy for EuroLLM-1.7B, EuroLLM-1.7B-Instruct, Gemma-2B, and TinyLlama on Hellaswag (10-shot) and Arc Challenge (25-shot), using the translated test sets from Lai et al. (2023). The languages shown include English and a selection of target languages — from the figure, the languages appear to span a range of resource levels (German, Spanish, French, Italian, Dutch, Portuguese, and several others).

**Hellaswag results (Figure 6, top panel):**

EuroLLM-1.7B matches or outperforms both Gemma-2B and TinyLlama **on all languages except English**. On English, the models are in a narrow range — from the figure, all three models appear to score between roughly 0.40 and 0.45, with EuroLLM-1.7B likely near the lower end. The paper states this explicitly: "On Hellaswag, EuroLLM-1.7B matches or outperforms Gemma-2b and TinyLlama on all languages besides English, which showcases its increased multilinguality." The instruction-tuned variant, EuroLLM-1.7B-Instruct, shows slightly better results than the base EuroLLM-1.7B across most languages. The magnitude of improvement is small — perhaps 1–3 percentage points — but consistent.

The standout finding is the performance on non-English languages. For languages like German, Spanish, French, and Italian, EuroLLM's advantage over Gemma-2B and TinyLlama is visually clear in Figure 6, with gaps that appear to be in the 5–15 percentage point range depending on language and baseline. This is the direct evidence that EuroLLM's multilingual-first design (balanced data mixture, 128K multilingual tokenizer, parallel data inclusion) yields superior multilingual reasoning compared to models that are primarily English-trained with multilingual capabilities as a secondary objective.

**Arc Challenge results (Figure 6, bottom panel):**

The pattern on Arc Challenge is more nuanced. EuroLLM-1.7B **outperforms TinyLlama on all languages**, but **underperforms Gemma-2B on most languages**. The paper acknowledges this directly: "On Arc Challenge, EuroLLM-1.7B outperforms TinyLlama on all languages but is worse than Gemma-2b." The paper attributes this to parameter count: "This can be caused by the lower number of parameters (EuroLLM-1.7B has 1.133B non-embedding parameters while Gemma-2B has 1.981B)." This explanation is plausible — Arc Challenge is a harder reasoning task than Hellaswag (requiring scientific knowledge rather than commonsense sentence completion), and the 75% parameter deficit would naturally disadvantage EuroLLM. However, the paper does not test this hypothesis by, for example, comparing EuroLLM against a Gemma variant with matched non-embedding parameters, so the parameter-count explanation remains suggestive rather than demonstrated.

EuroLLM-1.7B-Instruct again shows a slight improvement over the base model. The consistency of the instruction-tuning gain across both benchmarks (Hellaswag and Arc Challenge) and across languages suggests that the EuroBlocks fine-tuning provides a small but genuine boost to reasoning capabilities, not just instruction-following formatting. This is consistent with findings from other work (e.g., Chung et al., 2024; Ouyang et al., 2022) showing that instruction tuning often improves benchmark performance even for tasks that do not require explicit instruction following.

**Cross-lingual performance patterns:** Figure 6 reveals several details:

- For Hellaswag, the performance gap between English and non-English languages is substantially smaller for EuroLLM than for Gemma-2B or TinyLlama. In other words, EuroLLM achieves more uniform performance across languages, consistent with its design goal of balanced multilinguality. Gemma-2B and TinyLlama show sharp English peaks with noticeable drops for other languages — the characteristic signature of English-centric training.

- For Arc Challenge, all models show substantial English advantage, and EuroLLM's per-language scores are roughly 5–10 points lower than Gemma-2B's across languages. The English-first training of Gemma-2B appears to confer an advantage on this knowledge-intensive task that EuroLLM's more multilingual-balanced training cannot overcome at this parameter scale.

- The instruction-tuned models (EuroLLM-1.7B-Instruct) do not close the gap with Gemma-2B on Arc Challenge, suggesting that fine-tuning on EuroBlocks — which includes reasoning-style examples from OpenHermes-2.5 — provides only marginal knowledge injection compared to the knowledge encoded during pre-training.

#### Machine Translation Results (FLORES-200, WMT-23, WMT-24)

The machine translation evaluation compares EuroLLM-1.7B-Instruct against Gemma-2B-Instruct and Gemma-7B-Instruct, all fine-tuned on the EuroBlocks dataset and evaluated with 0-shot prompts and greedy decoding. Translation quality is measured via COMET-22 scores.

**FLORES-200 results (Figure 7):**

Figure 7 shows COMET-22 scores for both translation directions: English-to-XX (EN→XX, top panel) and XX-to-English (XX→EN, bottom panel), for a range of target languages (the figure displays a subset of the supported languages with individual bars per model per language).

The headline result is that **EuroLLM-1.7B-Instruct clearly outperforms Gemma-2B-Instruct on all language pairs and in both translation directions.** The paper states this explicitly: "We can see that EuroLLM-1.7B-Instruct clearly outperforms Gemma-2b-Instruct on all languages pairs and datasets." The magnitude of the advantage varies by language — for some high-resource European languages (German, Spanish, French), the gap appears to be in the 2–5 COMET point range, while for lower-resource languages the gap can be larger. The paper does not provide exact per-language differences, but the visual evidence in Figure 7 supports a consistent EuroLLM advantage.

More strikingly, EuroLLM-1.7B-Instruct is **competitive with Gemma-7B-Instruct** despite having roughly 4× fewer non-embedding parameters (1.133B vs. ~7B non-embedding parameters for Gemma-7B). The paper states: "and is competitive with Gemma-7b-Instruct despite the much lower number of parameters." This is the single most impressive result in the paper — a 1.7B model with carefully designed multilingual training approaching the translation quality of a 4× larger model. The competitiveness varies by language: for some languages, Gemma-7B-Instruct maintains a clear edge (2–5 COMET points), while for others, EuroLLM-1.7B-Instruct matches or even slightly exceeds it.

The directionality analysis in Figure 7 (EN→XX top, XX→EN bottom) reveals that the advantage over Gemma-2B-Instruct holds in both directions, suggesting that EuroLLM's multilingual training improves both target-side generation (writing in non-English languages) and source-side comprehension (understanding non-English input to produce English output). This is consistent with the parallel data's bidirectional nature (xx→en and en→xx) providing training signal for both skills.

**WMT-23 and WMT-24 results (Figure 8):**

Figure 8 presents COMET-22 scores on the WMT-23 and WMT-24 test sets, which represent news-domain translation (a different domain from FLORES-200's more general text). The results are consistent with FLORES-200: EuroLLM-1.7B-Instruct outperforms Gemma-2B-Instruct and is competitive with Gemma-7B-Instruct.

The WMT results strengthen the translation findings by demonstrating domain transfer: EuroLLM's translation advantage is not specific to the FLORES domain (which includes Wikipedia and news-like text that may overlap with training data) but generalizes to the news-specific WMT test sets. The COMET-22 scores on WMT are systematically lower than on FLORES-200 for all models — FLORES-200 scores (Figure 7) appear to range from roughly 70 to 95 COMET points, while WMT scores (Figure 8) appear to range from roughly 75 to 85 — reflecting the greater difficulty of news-domain translation with its specialized terminology and complex sentence structures.

**Quantitative context from Table 2 (scheduler comparison):** Table 2 provides the aggregate (language-averaged) COMET-22 scores for the trapezoid-scheduler EuroLLM-1.7B model:
- FLORES-200: 86.75
- WMT-23: 83.13
- WMT-24: 79.35

These aggregate scores average across all language pairs and both directions, so they obscure per-language and per-direction variation, but they provide a useful single-number summary. The WMT-24 score (79.35) being notably lower than WMT-23 (83.13) and FLORES-200 (86.75) suggests that WMT-24 either contains harder language pairs, more challenging text, or that the models show a temporal degradation on newer test data (WMT-24 being more recent than WMT-23 and FLORES-200, which was released in 2022).

**The EuroBlocks fine-tuning as a control variable:** A critical aspect of the translation comparison is that Gemma-2B and Gemma-7B are fine-tuned on the same EuroBlocks dataset as EuroLLM-1.7B. This means the translation quality differences cannot be attributed to EuroLLM having access to better instruction-tuning data — the fine-tuning is identical. Instead, the differences must stem from pre-training: EuroLLM's multilingual data mixture, tokenizer, and training curriculum produce a model that is better prepared to leverage the translation examples in EuroBlocks. This is strong evidence for the multilingual-first pre-training approach: a model that has seen balanced multilingual data and parallel corpora during pre-training can absorb translation-specific fine-tuning more effectively than an English-centric model of similar or even larger size.

**What the paper does not report for translation:** The paper does not provide any of the following, all of which would strengthen the translation evaluation: (1) per-language-pair COMET scores with exact numbers rather than bar chart estimates, (2) human evaluation of translation quality (COMET is a learned metric that may have its own language biases), (3) comparison against dedicated machine translation systems (e.g., NLLB, Google Translate) to contextualize whether the LLM-based translations are practically useful or merely interesting research results, (4) statistical significance tests for the pairwise comparisons, especially the "competitive with Gemma-7B" claim where the differences appear small in several languages, (5) analysis of translation errors or failure modes that would reveal whether EuroLLM makes different types of mistakes than the baselines.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Learning rate scheduler: cosine vs. trapezoid**: Table 2 shows that the trapezoid (Warmup-Stable-Decay) scheduler yields consistent improvements over the cosine scheduler across all five evaluation settings: Hellaswag (+0.98 percentage points, 0.4744 vs. 0.4646), Arc Challenge (+0.62 points, 0.3268 vs. 0.3206), FLORES-200 (+0.27 COMET points, 86.75 vs. 86.48), WMT-23 (+0.25 points, 83.13 vs. 82.88), and WMT-24 (+0.48 points, 79.35 vs. 78.87). The gains are small in absolute terms (0.3–1.0 points) but consistent across all five metrics, suggesting a genuine effect rather than noise. The paper's interpretation — that the stable phase enables better learning from the diverse web data while the final decay phase complements the annealing data switch — is plausible but unverified by additional ablations. The paper does not test alternative scheduler configurations (e.g., different warm-up ratios, different stable-phase durations, different decay schedules), so the reported trapezoid configuration should be understood as one empirically effective setting among many possible alternatives. The scheduler comparison is performed on models that appear otherwise identical in architecture and data (the paper does not specify whether both were trained for the full 4 trillion tokens, but Table 2's caption implies they are comparable EuroLLM-1.7B variants), making this a clean ablation. However, the comparison is based on a single training run per scheduler — without multiple seeds, the differences cannot be statistically distinguished from run-to-run variance.

**Joint scaling law analysis for parallel data proportion**: Figure 2 shows the scaling law curves for models trained with 0%, 25%, and 37.5% parallel data per non-English language, evaluated on web, Wikipedia, and parallel data test sets. This is not a standard ablation of the final model but a separate scaling law experiment using smaller models (100M–341M non-embedding parameters) to inform the data mixture decision. The key results are: (1) web and Wikipedia domain curves overlap across parallel data percentages, indicating that parallel data does not harm monolingual performance; (2) parallel data domain curve shows a clear upward shift from 0% to 25%, with diminishing returns from 25% to 37.5%. The curves are fitted from data points at three model sizes and three parallel data percentages, giving nine training runs total (though the paper does not explicitly state whether all combinations were trained — the 0% condition may only be tested at a subset of model sizes). The fitted curves are smooth extrapolations that would benefit from additional data points (e.g., 10%, 15% parallel data) to more precisely locate the optimum. The final choice of 20% parallel data (below the tested 25%) reflects the paper's interpretation that diminishing returns begin below 25%, but this is an interpolation from the fitted curves rather than a directly tested value.

**Joint scaling law analysis for high-quality data repetition**: Figure 3 compares scaling laws for models trained with and without Wikipedia data repetition, evaluated on web and Wikipedia test sets. The Wikipedia test set curve shifts upward under repetition, while the web test set curves overlap. This ablation uses the same small-model methodology as the parallel data analysis, with training runs at multiple model sizes for each condition (repetition vs. no repetition). The finding that repetition helps Wikipedia-domain performance without harming web-domain performance is clean and well-supported by the figure — the curves are visually well-separated on Wikipedia and overlapping on web data across the tested model size range. What the ablation does not test is whether the benefit extends beyond Wikipedia: would repeating other high-quality sources (Arxiv, Books) produce similar domain-specific gains without web degradation? The paper's decision to repeat "data from high-quality datasets" (plural) generalizes from Wikipedia evidence to other high-quality sources without direct empirical support. Additionally, the repetition factor is not specified — the scaling law experiment likely uses a fixed repetition factor (perhaps repeating Wikipedia to match the token count of non-repeated web data), but without knowing this factor, other practitioners cannot directly adopt the finding.

**Tokenizer fertility comparison across vocabulary sizes**: Figure 5 is presented as a diagnostic analysis rather than an ablation, but it functions as an implicit ablation of vocabulary size's effect on multilinguality. By comparing EuroLLM (128K), Mistral (32K), LLaMA-3 (128K), and Gemma (256K) tokenizers on the same set of languages, the figure demonstrates that (1) vocabulary size strongly affects fertility for non-English languages, (2) training data distribution matters independently of vocabulary size (LLaMA-3 and EuroLLM have nearly identical vocab sizes but different fertility patterns), and (3) EuroLLM's 128K choice achieves similar fertility to Gemma's 256K for European languages while using half the vocabulary. What this analysis does not show is the downstream impact of fertility on model performance — the paper does not train EuroLLM models with different vocabulary sizes and compare their benchmark results. The implicit claim (that 128K provides "the best trade-off") is based on a balancing of fertility improvements against embedding parameter cost, not on direct performance measurements. A complete ablation would train small models with 32K, 64K, 128K, and 256K vocabularies and measure both efficiency (tokens processed per second, memory usage) and benchmark performance across languages.

**Instruction-tuning effect on reasoning benchmarks**: Figure 6 shows EuroLLM-1.7B-Instruct scoring slightly higher than EuroLLM-1.7B on both Hellaswag and Arc Challenge across most languages. The paper notes this briefly ("Interestingly, EuroLLM-1.7B-Instruct leads to slightly better results than EuroLLM-1.7B for both benchmarks") without quantifying the improvement or analyzing its cause. This is an informal ablation of the EuroBlocks fine-tuning stage, suggesting that instruction tuning provides a small reasoning boost beyond its primary purpose of enabling instruction following. However, the comparison is between different training stages of the same model (pre-trained vs. fine-tuned), not a controlled ablation where only the fine-tuning dataset varies. The improvement could result from the additional training tokens (EuroBlocks adds 1M examples × 4 epochs), the specific content of EuroBlocks (which includes reasoning examples from OpenHermes-2.5), or the chat formatting itself (which may improve the model's ability to process multiple-choice prompts). Without comparing against an equivalent number of continued pre-training tokens or against fine-tuning on a different dataset, the cause of the improvement remains ambiguous.

**Negative results / limitations implicitly shown**: Several design choices are validated by the absence of degradation:
- Parallel data does not harm monolingual performance (Figure 2).
- High-quality data repetition does not harm web domain performance (Figure 3).
- The trapezoid scheduler does not harm any metric while improving all five (Table 2).
- The EuroBlocks fine-tuning does not harm (and slightly improves) reasoning benchmarks (Figure 6).

These "no harm" findings are important for practical adoption — they reduce the risk that a multilingual-first design incurs hidden costs. However, they are also somewhat unsurprising: the scaling law analyses in Figures 2 and 3 use small models and limited training budgets (100B tokens), and it is possible that degradation would appear at larger scales or longer training if, for example, repeated Wikipedia data eventually causes memorization and reduces generalization. The paper cannot rule this out without repeating the scaling law analyses at larger model sizes.

### Critical Assessment

The experimental results in this paper demonstrate a meaningful achievement — a 1.7B-parameter multilingual model that outperforms similarly-sized English-centric models on multilingual reasoning and translation tasks — but there is a substantial gap between what the experiments demonstrate and what they are used to claim. A critical reader should distinguish between the **well-supported finding** (careful multilingual design improves multilingual performance at a given parameter budget) and the **overclaimed implication** (that the specific design choices — 128K tokenizer, 20% parallel data, trapezoid scheduler — are individually validated as optimal).

**On the claim that EuroLLM's multilingual-first design produces competitive multilingual performance:** This is well-supported by Figures 6, 7, and 8. EuroLLM-1.7B matches or outperforms Gemma-2B on all non-English Hellaswag languages (Figure 6, top) and on all translation tasks (Figures 7, 8). The translation results are particularly strong: EuroLLM-1.7B-Instruct not only beats Gemma-2B-Instruct (which has ~75% more non-embedding parameters) but is competitive with Gemma-7B-Instruct (~6× more non-embedding parameters). These are genuine, reproducible comparisons — same fine-tuning data, same evaluation protocol, same decoding strategy. The evidence is clean: multilingual-first design works.

However, the performance on English is systematically lower than English-centric models. On Arc Challenge (Figure 6, bottom), EuroLLM-1.7B trails Gemma-2B on most languages, and the gap is particularly clear on English. This is the multilingual trade-off that the paper acknowledges: allocating only 50% of training tokens to English (versus presumably 80%+ for Gemma and TinyLlama) means the model has less English knowledge and reasoning capacity. The paper frames this acceptingly — it is the intended design — but a user expecting EuroLLM to be a drop-in replacement for an English-centric model would be disappointed. The paper could strengthen this aspect by explicitly quantifying the English performance cost: for each 10% reduction in English training data, how much does English benchmark performance drop, and is this linear or does it accelerate at low English allocations?

**On the claim that the 128K tokenizer provides the "best trade-off":** This claim is not experimentally validated for model performance. The evidence in Figure 5 is a fertility analysis — pieces per word across languages — which shows that 128K achieves good fertility for European languages while keeping embedding parameters at 262M. What is missing is a **performance ablation**: train EuroLLM-1.7B (or smaller proxy models) with 32K, 64K, 128K, and 256K tokenizers on the same data and compare downstream benchmark scores. Fertility is a proxy metric — lower fertility should mean more efficient encoding and potentially better performance, but the relationship is not guaranteed. A larger vocabulary might hurt performance if the embeddings are undertrained (since each token embedding receives fewer gradient updates), or a smaller vocabulary might help by forcing the model to learn more compositional representations. Without this ablation, the "best trade-off" claim is a reasonable hypothesis grounded in parameter-count arithmetic and fertility curves, but it is not demonstrated. The comparison with LLaMA-3's identically-sized vocabulary in Figure 5 is revealing — it shows that training data distribution dominates vocabulary size — which actually weakens the claim that vocabulary size alone determines the trade-off.

**On the claim that the trapezoid scheduler is superior to cosine:** Table 2 provides a single comparison on a single model with a single trapezoid configuration. The differences (0.25–0.98 points) are small relative to typical run-to-run variance in LLM training — without error bars or multiple training runs, we cannot distinguish a genuine scheduler effect from random seed variation. The paper's hypothesis about coupling the scheduler to the data curriculum (stable phase for web data, decay phase for annealing data) is intuitively appealing but untested: an ablation that uses the trapezoid scheduler without annealing data (or cosine with annealing data) would isolate whether the scheduler or the data causes the improvement. As presented, the trapezoid result is **suggestive but not conclusive** — it justifies using this scheduler for the released model (since it certainly does not hurt) but does not establish it as a general principle for multilingual training.

**On the claim that parallel data is harmless to monolingual performance (the "harmless regularizer" insight):** Figure 2 supports this for models up to ~341M non-embedding parameters trained on 100B tokens. The paper extrapolates via fitted scaling laws to larger models, which is methodologically sound but empirically unverified at the 1.7B scale. The key concern is that "harmless" might only hold for relatively small amounts of parallel data (the paper tests up to 37.5%) and for relatively short training (100B tokens). At 4 trillion tokens with 20% parallel data per non-English language, the model sees vastly more total parallel tokens than in the scaling law experiments. It is possible — though the paper's scaling law extrapolation predicts otherwise — that at some threshold, parallel data starts to crowd out monolingual knowledge in ways that the 100B-token experiments cannot detect. Testing this would require a scaling law experiment at larger token budgets or model sizes, which is expensive but would substantially strengthen the claim.

**On the claim that high-quality data repetition is beneficial without cost:** Figure 3 supports this for Wikipedia repetition, again using small models (up to 341M non-embedding parameters) and 100B tokens. The generalizability to other high-quality sources (the paper repeats "high-quality datasets" broadly, including Arxiv, Books, Apollo) is assumed rather than tested. The repetition factor matters — repeating Wikipedia 10 times is different from repeating it 3 times — but the paper does not specify the factor used or test different repetition rates. The risk is that practitioners adopt "repeat everything high-quality" as a blanket principle and encounter overfitting in domains (like Books) where the data is less diverse than Wikipedia (which covers millions of topics). A more thorough analysis would test repetition effects separately for each high-quality source and at multiple repetition rates.

**Missing experiments that limit the strength of conclusions:**

- **No performance by language resource level.** The paper aggregates results by language (Figures 6, 7, 8) but does not categorize languages by resource level (high/medium/low) and analyze whether EuroLLM's advantage is concentrated in high-resource languages where data is plentiful or extends to the low-resource languages (Maltese, Irish, Latvian) that are the motivation for the project. The FLORES-200 and WMT results show a subset of languages in Figures 7 and 8 — it is unclear whether low-resource languages are included or whether they show the same EuroLLM advantage.

- **No comparison against non-English-centric multilingual models.** The baselines (Gemma, TinyLlama) are English-centric models with some multilingual capability. Comparing against dedicated multilingual models like BLOOM (Scao et al., 2022), mT5 (Xue et al., 2021), XGLM (Lin et al., 2022), or the Aya model (Üstün et al., 2024) would contextualize EuroLLM's performance within the broader multilingual LLM landscape. The paper's focus on European languages makes this comparison particularly relevant — how does EuroLLM compare to models that also prioritized multilinguality but with different design choices?

- **No ablation of the 50% English allocation.** The English percentage (50% main phase, 32.5% annealing) is a critical design choice that directly affects the English-multilingual trade-off. The paper does not justify it empirically. A scaling law analysis varying English percentage (similar to the parallel data analysis) would reveal how sensitive performance is to this choice and whether, for example, 40% English with more non-English data would improve multilingual scores without proportional English losses.

- **No analysis of the instruction-tuning data breakdown.** EuroBlocks combines OpenHermes-2.5 (predominantly English reasoning), Aya (multilingual diverse tasks), and translation data. The paper does not ablate these components to determine which contributes most to EuroLLM-1.7B-Instruct's performance. Does Aya data drive the Hellaswag improvements? Does the translation data dominate the translation quality? Could a smaller, more targeted instruction dataset achieve the same results?

- **No evaluation of generation quality beyond multiple-choice and translation.** All evaluations are either multiple-choice accuracy (Hellaswag, Arc Challenge) or reference-based translation quality (COMET-22). These metrics capture different aspects of model capability, but neither evaluates open-ended generation quality, factual accuracy, or instruction-following faithfulness — the capabilities that users actually experience when interacting with a chat model. A human evaluation of EuroLLM-1.7B-Instruct's responses (fluency, helpfulness, factuality) across languages would provide a more complete picture of its practical utility.

- **No scaling analysis of the model itself.** All scaling law experiments are on small models (100M–341M). The paper does not train intermediate-scale models (e.g., 500M, 1B) to validate that the scaling law extrapolations hold. Given that the released model is 1.7B, a confirmation that the scaling trends continue to this scale — rather than bending or reversing — would increase confidence in the data mixture decisions.

**What the experiments do and do not demonstrate:**

The experiments **do** demonstrate that a multilingual-first design pipeline (balanced data, large multilingual tokenizer, parallel data inclusion, curriculum annealing) can produce a 1.7B model with strong multilingual reasoning and translation capabilities, outperforming similarly-sized English-centric models on non-English tasks. This is a genuine, valuable result for practitioners building multilingual LLMs.

The experiments **do not** demonstrate that the specific parameter choices (128K vocabulary, 20% parallel data, 50% English, trapezoid scheduler) are optimal or even near-optimal. The paper provides evidence that these choices work well, but not that alternatives would work worse. The scaling law analyses narrow the space of plausible designs but do not locate precise optima — they justify the chosen configurations as reasonable rather than uniquely correct.

The experiments **do not** address whether the gains persist or amplify at larger model scales. The paper explicitly frames EuroLLM-1.7B as a first step toward larger models (§7), so the scaling behavior of these design choices — whether the multilingual advantage grows, shrinks, or plateaus with more parameters — remains an open question that future EuroLLM releases could address.

The experiments **do not** establish that EuroLLM's performance is sufficient for practical applications. The COMET scores (79–87) indicate decent translation quality, but without human evaluation, the gap between "competitive on a benchmark" and "useful for real translation tasks" is unknown. Similarly, Hellaswag and Arc Challenge accuracy scores (0.3–0.5 range for most non-English languages) represent above-chance but far-from-perfect performance — the model makes frequent errors on these tasks, and the practical implications of this error rate for downstream applications are unexplored.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### 1. Difficulty Estimation Cost Is Unaccounted For, Potentially Dominating the Headline Efficiency Gains

**The assumption or constraint:** The compute-optimal test-time scaling framework depends on knowing each prompt’s difficulty *before* allocating the inference budget. The paper’s method for estimating difficulty — generating 2,048 samples per question and averaging either ground-truth correctness (oracle) or the PRM’s final-answer scores (predicted) — is extremely expensive. The authors acknowledge this in Section 3.2:

> "estimating difficulty in this way still incurs additional computation cost during inference... our experiments do not account for this cost largely for simplicity"

This is not an incidental detail. Generating and scoring 2,048 complete solutions per question consumes more compute than the largest test-time budgets studied (256–512 generations). In effect, the difficulty estimation step alone often exceeds the entire problem-solving budget that the paper optimizes over.

**The consequence:** The claimed 4× efficiency gains over best-of-N sampling (Figures 4 and 8) are computed *after* difficulty is known, without amortizing the cost of learning it. For a realistic deployment where difficulty must be estimated from scratch for each incoming prompt, the total cost would be difficulty estimation + strategy execution. Since the estimation cost (2,048 generations) dominates the execution budget (16–256 generations) for most settings, the true end-to-end efficiency could be substantially worse — possibly negative — compared to simply running best-of-N with the combined budget. The $4\times$ figure should therefore be understood as an **upper bound on achievable efficiency assuming a free oracle for difficulty**, not as a realized deployment gain. A practitioner who naively adopts the reported numbers would be disappointed when total inference cost includes the difficulty assessment overhead.

**What evidence exists in the paper:** The paper does not measure the total cost (estimation + execution) in any experiment. All compute-optimal scaling curves in Figures 4 and 8 use the x-axis as the execution budget only. There is no experiment that varies the number of difficulty-estimation samples, measures how estimation accuracy degrades with fewer samples, or reports total FLOPs per question. The predicted-difficulty variant (using PRM scores instead of ground-truth correctness) is equally expensive — it still requires 2,048 generations — so it offers no cost reduction. Section 3.2 flags this as future work:

> "this incurs additional computation cost during inference which could be reduced by, e.g., pretraining or finetuning models to directly predict difficulty of a question"

But no such model is trained or evaluated.

**Mitigation status:** Not addressed in the current work. The authors explicitly defer this to future research. The paper provides no estimates of how much the difficulty estimation budget could be reduced without degrading strategy selection quality, nor does it experiment with adaptive difficulty assessment (e.g., starting with a small number of samples and deciding whether more are needed). Until this gap is closed, the compute-optimal framework is a laboratory analysis, not a deployable system.

### 2. All Results Are on a Single Benchmark (MATH) with a Single Model Family (PaLM 2-S\*), Leaving Cross-Domain and Cross-Model Generalization Untested

**The assumption or constraint:** Every experiment in the paper — the scaling law analyses, the search algorithm comparisons, the revision model evaluations, the FLOPs-matched pretraining comparison — uses the MATH benchmark (Hendrycks et al., 2021) with PaLM 2-S\* (Codey) as the base model. The authors justify this in Section 4:

> "we believe this model is representative of the capabilities of many contemporary LLMs"

But they provide no evidence for this representativeness claim, and they do not test other models, other benchmarks, or other task families.

**The consequence:** Several aspects of the paper’s findings could be specific to MATH (competition-level symbolic math reasoning) and PaLM 2-S\* (a particular model with particular calibration properties). The PRM’s quality and its over-optimization behavior (where beam search degrades easy-problem performance at high budgets, Figure 3 right) depend on PaLM 2-S\*‘s output distribution — a model with different error patterns or different confidence calibration might exhibit qualitatively different difficulty-dependent scaling curves. Similarly, the revision model’s ability to learn from incorrect in-context examples depends on the base model’s in-context learning capabilities, which vary substantially across model families. The hard-problem failure mode (difficulty bin 5, where no method makes progress regardless of budget) could be less severe or more severe on benchmarks with different difficulty profiles relative to the model’s capabilities. A practitioner training a revision model for code generation or logical reasoning cannot assume the sequential-vs-parallel optimal ratios (Figure 7) or the difficulty bin thresholds will transfer — they might need to redo the entire scaling law analysis on their own domain and model.

**What evidence exists in the paper:** The results themselves demonstrate domain-specificity, albeit implicitly. The base model achieves only 10–19% pass@1 on MATH — this is relatively low performance, meaning a large fraction of questions fall into the hard bins where test-time compute provides minimal benefit. On an easier benchmark or with a stronger base model, the difficulty distribution would shift, and the compute-optimal strategies would change. The paper does not run any experiments on code generation (HumanEval, MBPP), logical reasoning (ARC, LogiQA), scientific QA, or any non-math domain. The test set is 500 questions, split into five difficulty quintiles of ~100 each — a small sample on which strategy selection is performed.

**Mitigation status:** Not addressed. The authors do not claim cross-domain generalization. Section 8 briefly mentions this as a scope limitation but does not plan cross-domain experiments for future work. A reader should treat all quantitative findings (the optimal parallel data ratio, the specific difficulty bin boundaries, the $4\times$ efficiency gain magnitude) as specific to MATH + PaLM 2-S\* until replicated elsewhere.

### 3. Verifier Over-Optimization Is Documented but Not Solved, Capping the Gains from Additional Test-Time Compute

**The assumption or constraint:** All search-based methods (beam search, lookahead search, best-of-N weighted) depend on the process reward model (PRM) to score and select solutions. The paper’s PRM is trained via Monte Carlo rollout supervision on PaLM 2-S\* outputs (Section 5.1, Appendix D), producing a verifier that is correlated with solution correctness but imperfect. The paper explicitly documents that this imperfection leads to over-optimization: search algorithms find solutions that score highly under the PRM but are actually incorrect, and this effect worsens with more aggressive optimization.

**The consequence:** The over-optimization creates a **hard performance ceiling** that no amount of additional test-time compute can surpass. On easy problems (difficulty bins 1–2), beam search actually *degrades* performance at high budgets — the verifier is exploited rather than genuinely helpful (Figure 3, right). On medium problems (bins 3–4), beam search outperforms best-of-N at low-to-medium budgets but flattens and sometimes declines at high budgets (Figure 3, left). Lookahead search — the most powerful optimization algorithm since it simulates multiple steps forward for more accurate scoring — paradoxically underperforms simpler methods at the same budget (Figure 3, left) because its additional compute amplifies the over-optimization rather than improving genuine solution quality. The compute-optimal policy partially *mitigates* this by routing easy problems away from aggressive search, but it does not *solve* the underlying problem: on medium problems where beam search is deployed, over-optimization still limits scaling. The paper’s qualitative examples in Appendix M (Figure 29, etc.) show degenerate outputs — repetitive low-information steps, overly short 1–2 step solutions — that score highly under the PRM but are clearly wrong to a human.

**What evidence exists in the paper:** Figure 3 (right) is the clearest evidence: beam search accuracy on bin 1 (easiest) decreases from roughly 78% to 77% as budget grows from 4 to 256, while best-of-N weighted increases from 68% to 88%. This is the signature of over-optimization — search finds PRM-pleasing but incorrect solutions that best-of-N, with its weaker optimization, does not encounter. Figure 3 (left) shows beam search flattening at ~34% while best-of-N weighted continues improving to ~38% at 512 generations. Lookahead search underperforms at all budgets. Appendix M provides qualitative examples.

**Mitigation status:** The paper acknowledges over-optimization as a central limiting factor (Section 8) but does not propose or test any solution. The compute-optimal policy is a workaround — it avoids aggressive search where the verifier is least reliable — but this means the system cannot benefit from additional compute on easy problems (where the verifier is exploited) or push past the flattening point on medium problems (where the verifier saturates). The paper’s discussion in Section 8 suggests future work on more robust verifiers (e.g., adversarial training, ensembles, constrained search with KL penalties), but none of these are explored. A practitioner deploying this method today would find that increasing the test-time compute budget beyond a certain point yields zero or negative returns, and this ceiling is set by verifier quality, not by the search algorithm or revision strategy.

### 4. The $14\times$ Larger Pretraining Baseline Is Weak — Not Compute-Optimally Trained and Using Only Greedy Decoding — Making the FLOPs-Matched Comparison Favorable to Test-Time Compute

**The assumption or constraint:** The FLOPs-matched comparison in Section 7 pits PaLM 2-S\* with compute-optimal test-time strategies against a model with approximately $14\times$ more parameters. The larger model uses greedy decoding — no majority voting, no best-of-N, no search of any kind. Additionally, the paper scales model parameters while holding training data fixed, acknowledging that this departs from compute-optimal pretraining (Hoffmann et al., 2022):

> "We choose this setting as it is representative of a canonical approach to scaling pretraining compute and leave the analysis of compute-optimal scaling of pretraining compute where the data and parameters are both scaled equally to future work."

**The consequence:** Both design choices make the pretraining baseline **weaker than a fair comparison would warrant**, potentially overstating the case for test-time compute. A compute-optimally trained larger model (scaling both parameters and data according to Chinchilla) would likely outperform a parameter-only-scaled model, reducing or reversing the reported advantages of test-time compute. More critically, giving the larger model even a modest test-time compute budget — say, best-of-8 or majority voting over 8 samples — would create a much stronger baseline. The paper’s central claim that “a smaller model augmented with compute-optimal test-time strategies can outperform a ~14× larger model” (Section 1, Figure 1) is technically true for the specific baseline chosen, but it compares a highly optimized inference pipeline against a deliberately unoptimized baseline. A fairer comparison would match total FLOPs but allow both models to use test-time compute, allocating the budget optimally for each. The paper never tests this.

**What evidence exists in the paper:** Figure 9 and the bar charts in Figure 1 report the FLOPs-matched results. The $14\times$ larger model’s greedy performance is shown as stars on Figure 9 — these are single-point estimates with no test-time augmentation. The paper provides no FLOPs-matched comparison where the larger model also gets a test-time compute budget. Section 7 acknowledges the parameter-only scaling choice as a limitation but does not explore how results would change with compute-optimal pretraining.

**Mitigation status:** The paper is transparent about the parameter-only scaling limitation (quoted above) and frames the compute-optimal pretraining comparison as future work. However, the greedy decoding limitation — giving the larger model no test-time compute — is not discussed as a limitation at all. The paper’s framing in Section 1 and Figure 1 presents the result without this caveat. A practitioner reading the headline numbers (+27.8% relative improvement on easy questions at $R \ll 1$) should understand that these are upper bounds on the advantage of test-time compute over pretraining, likely shrinking substantially against a properly optimized larger-model baseline.

### 5. The Revision Model Has a 38% Correct-to-Incorrect Reversion Rate, Making Sequential Revision Chains Unstable Without Post-Hoc Selection

**The assumption or constraint:** The revision model is trained exclusively on sequences where all in-context answers are incorrect, followed by a single correct answer (Section 6.1). The training data construction procedure pairs independently sampled correct and incorrect solutions post-hoc, using the incorrect answer with the smallest character-level edit distance to the correct one as the final incorrect-context example. At inference time, the model generates a chain of revisions, conditioning each new attempt on the previous ones.

**The consequence:** Since the model has never seen a correct answer in its context during training, it has no signal for what to do when it encounters one at inference time. The paper reports that approximately **38% of correct answers produced during a revision chain get “revised” back to incorrect answers** in the subsequent step (Section 6.1). This creates a fundamental instability: revision chains are not monotonically improving — the model can and does undo its own correct work, sometimes multiple times within a single chain. The practical result is that you cannot simply take the final revision as the output; you must instead run majority voting or verifier-based selection across the entire chain to identify the best answer (as described in Section 6.1). This adds overhead and means that many of the revisions generated in a long chain are wasted — producing incorrect revisions of previously correct answers serves no purpose and consumes the generation budget. A user who naively takes the last revision output would experience substantially worse performance than the reported numbers (which use within-chain selection).

**What evidence exists in the paper:** Section 6.1 states the 38% reversion rate. Figure 6 (left) shows that per-step pass@1 fluctuates in the 23–25% range across revision steps 5–64 rather than monotonically improving, consistent with a model that sometimes reverts correct answers. The paper’s mitigation — best-of-N weighted selection or majority voting across the chain — is described in Section 6.1 and used in all revision experiments.

**Mitigation status:** Partially mitigated via post-hoc selection, but not solved at the model level. The within-chain selection (picking the best answer from any step in the chain) converts the reversion problem from a catastrophic failure (wrong answer output) to an efficiency cost (wasted revisions). However, this means that a budget of $N$ sequential revisions does not yield $N$ genuinely useful improvement steps — some fraction of the budget is consumed generating and then rejecting incorrect revisions of previously correct answers. The paper does not explore training the revision model to recognize when no revision is needed (e.g., by including “no change needed” examples in training), nor does it experiment with early stopping within revision chains based on verifier confidence. The 38% figure suggests this is a substantial inefficiency that grows with chain length — in a chain of 64 revisions, perhaps ~24 revisions are actively harmful. A more principled solution would substantially improve the efficiency of sequential revision.

### 6. Sequential Search and Revisions Incur Serial Latency That Is Not Accounted For, Limiting Practical Applicability in Latency-Sensitive Settings

**The assumption or constraint:** The paper measures test-time compute in “generations” — the number of complete solutions sampled — as a proxy for total FLOPs. However, generations are not all created equal in terms of wall-clock time. Sequential operations (beam search steps, revision chain steps) are inherently serial — each step depends on the previous one and cannot be parallelized. Parallel operations (best-of-N sampling) can be executed simultaneously given sufficient hardware. The paper does not discuss this latency distinction.

**The consequence:** The compute-optimal strategies often favor substantial sequential computation. For revisions, the optimal policy on easy problems uses purely sequential chains — a budget of 128 generations as a single chain of 128 sequential revisions (Figure 7). For search, beam search — which is sequential across steps — is favored on medium problems. In both cases, the wall-clock latency is $N\times$ the latency of a single generation (for purely sequential strategies), versus approximately 1× for purely parallel strategies (since all samples can be generated simultaneously). A strategy that allocates 128 generations as 64 sequential × 2 parallel (a hybrid configuration that Figure 7 shows to be near-optimal for medium problems) takes roughly $64\times$ longer than a fully parallel best-of-128 strategy, despite having the same total FLOPs budget. For interactive applications (chat assistants, real-time code completion, live translation), this latency differential makes many of the paper’s optimal strategies impractical regardless of their FLOPs efficiency. A deployment with a 2-second latency budget might be limited to best-of-4 or best-of-8 parallel, making the sequential-intensive strategies studied in this paper irrelevant for that use case.

**What evidence exists in the paper:** The paper does not measure, model, or discuss wall-clock latency in any experiment. All cost accounting is in generations/FLOPs. The sequential-to-parallel ratio sweeps in Figure 7 (for revisions) and the search algorithm comparisons in Figure 3 (comparing beam search vs. best-of-N) are presented solely in terms of accuracy-per-generation, with no latency axis. The latency implications are not mentioned in the limitations (Section 8) or anywhere in the text.

**Mitigation status:** Not addressed at all. The paper treats all generations as interchangeable units of compute, ignoring a first-order practical constraint for many deployment scenarios. A practitioner building a real-time system would need to redo the analysis with a latency budget constraint — the optimal strategy under a latency constraint (e.g., purely parallel up to some parallelization limit) could be entirely different from the FLOPs-optimal strategy. The paper’s framework could be extended to incorporate latency by adding a parallelism constraint or a latency weight to the optimization objective, but this extension is not explored or suggested.
