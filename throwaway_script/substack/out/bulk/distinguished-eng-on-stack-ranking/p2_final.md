Here are the takeaways from this Bryan Cantrill interview (went from new grad to Distinguished Engineer at Sun, competed with AWS at Joyent, and founded Oxide Computer Company):

**Working at Sun Microsystems in the ’90s**

Bryan graduated in 1996 into a tech landscape dominated by Microsoft, which he refused to join on principle. He grew up in the personal-computer era of DOS and early Windows, where “if an application misbehaved, the machine would just reboot”—a world without memory protection that he describes as “garbage in a way that is kind of unfathomable.” It was only when he encountered Unix on a workstation in college that he realized what computing could be: concurrent windows, memory protection, a command line that didn’t crash. Looking back, he pins the problem on Microsoft’s DNA: “They were a compiler company, not an operating systems company.” Apple’s Mac wasn’t much better at the time.

Beyond the technical mediocrity, Microsoft was profoundly anti-competitive. Bryan describes their pattern—one “you would see repeated since”—of **announcement as weapon**: they’d promise that a promising startup’s product would become a forthcoming OS feature, ship nothing, and freeze the market. “The term vaporware, I think if it didn’t originate with Microsoft, Microsoft definitely perfected the art of vaporware.” That behavior was later confirmed by judicial findings of fact in the antitrust case, though Bryan notes that Gates and the company changed substantially after hitting middle age.

He joined Sun almost reluctantly. He’d interviewed with other groups and found them fine but unenergizing, and he “was kind of accustomed to being the youngest person in a group” but didn’t want to be isolated. Then he met Kevin Clark and Jeff Bonwick: “it was like a bolt of lightning.” They shared his passion for ripping out broken kernel code and replacing it with something beautiful. He vividly remembers flying back and thinking “oh my God, I’m going to go work for Sun.” At the time, Sun was the only company still truly invested in the operating system—what Bryan calls “Unix’s darkest hour”—and that kernel-development group became his home for 14 years.

**His growth to Distinguished Engineer**

Sun’s ladder—Member of Technical Staff, Staff Engineer, Senior Staff, Distinguished Engineer—may have originated there (or perhaps Xerox PARC). Bryan came in as an MTS3 rather than the typical MTS2 for new grads, partly because he’d already done OS kernel development over two summers at QNX in Canada. But he stresses he was “very much not focused on my grade, my rank or promotion.” His philosophy: **“I would much rather be under promoted than over promoted.”** He wanted to do the most important work, and Sun—for all its faults—was accommodating of headstrong engineers.

Annual performance reviews at Sun were counterproductive. “Formalized performance review never resulted in me having higher performance,” he says. Reviews tended to mirror the self-review “albeit with grammatical errors introduced,” and even promotion reviews were “just not uplifting.” The real highlights were nailing a hard bug or completing a significant body of work with colleagues. The promotion process itself had a bizarre kink: Sun had three ratings (superlative, excellent, good) with a forced distribution, and if you got “superlative” they *had* to promote you—but they also didn’t want to promote anyone faster than every 18 months. So managers would apologetically give “excellent” to someone doing extraordinary work, trapped by the system.

The Distinguished Engineer promotion was the most political step. There were two paths in: get voted in by existing DEs in what Bryan calls “the wrong place for democracy in a society,” or arrive via acquisition when a startup’s CTO negotiated the title. Bryan was spared the politics because Sun’s CTO Greg Papadopoulos presented his case. “He put apparently my materials up and it’s like, I don’t expect anyone to vote against this. Take your vote.” It was unanimous. Bryan took it as validation of his approach: do indisputable work and the title follows. He’s since eliminated ranks entirely at Oxide—**“I think ranks are corrosive. I don’t think they get people to do their best work.”**

**Why goaling on promotion is bad**

Ryan pitches the two standard career philosophies—focus purely on the work and let promotions follow, or explicitly goal on the next level. Bryan rejects both and offers a third path: interrogate *why* you want the title. “Because if that’s the goal, if the goal is to be a Distinguished Engineer, you get there, you’ll be Distinguished Engineer. Now what?” He warns that fixating on external validation is “a recipe for a midlife crisis” and suggests introducing ambitious young engineers to “some miserable DEs”—people who achieved the title and found it hollow. The real work is finding what gives you meaning.

On the desire for financial independence: Bryan is skeptical of the “retire early” framing. He recounts the dot-com bust as a clarifying moment. Most people around him had made a fortune on paper and lost every penny; the ones who actually cashed out in ’99-’00 often weren’t that interested in tech to begin with, and they struggled profoundly over the next decade. “You don’t have to work, so like now what do you do?” Some went through phases of buying six houses before realizing that “six houses are kind of like a pain in the ass.” His prescription: find work that is intrinsically meaningful *now*, not later. “If you’re doing this so you can leave, maybe you shouldn’t be in this industry at all.”

He also identifies a pattern he calls **“postpartum in engineering”**: the emotional crash after shipping something you were singularly focused on. He saw it coming when Oxide shipped its first rack and proactively planned for it. Even when the achievement is objectively extraordinary—like shipping a hardware product—the sudden absence of that all-consuming goal can leave people adrift. The same dynamic afflicts people who hit financial independence and realize the number didn’t come with a purpose.

**Stack ranking and layoffs**

Sun didn’t practice the full rank-and-yank that Intel did, but Bryan calls stack ranking **“organizational cancer.”** It creates perverse incentives: managers strategically keep dead weight on their teams as “fodder” to throw into the wood chipper during forced cuts. He saw this firsthand at Sun, where someone pulled him aside and explained that a conspicuously unproductive person was actually protecting everyone else because “they get a good” rating every cycle. “Stack ranking fundamentally teaches you that your team are adversaries,” he says. “It is the team that wins or loses, the team that succeeds.”

After the dot-com bust, Sun’s stock lost 98% of its value—trading below cash at one point—and the layoffs were relentless. Bryan counted 35 rounds of layoffs over roughly eight years. Employees built what they called **“the obits”**: a daily script that scraped the employee directory to see who was no longer at the company. Some days 1,500 or 3,000 names would disappear. Sun’s transparency meant this was tolerated—it “was a pretty transparent company” where “no one was truly in charge,” which Bryan calls both its strength and its weakness. The period from 2001 to roughly 2006 was “nuclear winter” in Silicon Valley; tech was “basically dead for five years,” with a capital deficit so severe that Y Combinator wouldn’t form until 2006.

**Why he hated the Oracle acquisition**

The acquisition itself was dramatic: IBM and HP were both jockeying to buy Sun punitively, each trying to sabotage the other, when Oracle swept in. Bryan was initially optimistic that Oracle’s business execution could complement Sun’s engineering culture. He was wrong. Oracle, in his view, is “an octopus that knows how to feed itself”—focused entirely on “getting effectively a monopoly, a natural monopoly over its own customers, asphyxiating competition and then raising the rent.” Sun, by contrast, “always endeavoring to do the right thing by its customers.” Many of Sun’s seminal technologies—NFS, SPARC, Java, Solaris, DTrace, ZFS—emerged because the company rewarded technical boldness and gave engineers tremendous freedom.

He felt ashamed working at Oracle in a way he’d never felt at Sun. “I can’t work for a company that has that kind of profound distrust. That so eroded my own sense of meaning.” He quit after roughly 45 days. Meanwhile, some engineers were desperately angling to get *on* the layoff list before the acquisition closed, because the severance packages were rich. There was “an absolute line at the trough for how can I get laid off first.” Bryan wasn’t among them—he’d wanted to make the merger work—but he understands why they did.

He chokes up recalling Scott McNealy’s epitaph for Sun: **“kicked butt, had fun, didn’t cheat, loved our customers, changed computing.”** He adopted that as Oxide’s mission statement verbatim. “That was just not Oracle. Oracle’s not interested in that.”

**Why Bezos is the apex predator of capitalism**

After Sun, Bryan joined Joyent, which ran a public cloud competing directly with AWS. That experience gave him a front-row seat to Amazon’s execution. He calls Bezos **“the apex predator of capitalism”** because of how Amazon played its hand once it hit the cloud-computing mother lode. From roughly 2010 to 2015, every re:Invent brought price cuts and a flood of new services. Customers loved it. Meanwhile, Amazon refused to break out AWS revenue in earnings calls, which “apparently you can get away with as a public company.” The combination—relentless price cuts plus opaque financials—convinced the market that cloud was a terrible business no one should enter. But Joyent was actually in the business and knew the margins were excellent.

“He’s like, I’m gonna press my advantage. I’m gonna make it so no one can compete with me. And I’m gonna do it honestly, like the right way. I’m gonna do it by giving a great product at a reasonable price. Damn, yeah, that’s good. That’s a win-win.” Bryan describes competing with Amazon as brutal: “their execution was extraordinary.” Still, he found lanes where Joyent could compete. Today, he notes, AWS is a different company—you don’t get the price cuts or the can’t-live-without new services at re:Invent anymore.

**Differences between CTO and VP**

Bryan held both titles at Joyent but doesn’t put much stock in the distinction. A CTO is arguably more outward-facing (“Chief Travel Officer”) while a VP of Engineering handles internal mechanics. What he *did* care about was that Joyent not hire an external CTO over him. As long as the role stayed internal or he had input, he was fine being VP of Engineering in perpetuity. The title itself didn’t matter; the autonomy and alignment did.

**Starting Oxide Computer Company**

After Joyent was acquired by Samsung, Bryan and his colleague Steve knew they wanted to start a company together. They took long walks in San Francisco generating ideas—and almost all of them were bad. They fell into the trap of trying to come up with things venture capitalists would fund rather than things in their hearts. Then Bryan was digging up an old VC’s email address and found a message from 18 months prior: “I will fund literally anything you put in front of me.” He turned to Steve: “We should just go big. We should do the thing that is in our heart. We should build the computer that we want to build. We should build a rack-scale machine.”

When they pitched the same VC, he cut Bryan off 45 seconds in: “If you are talking about starting a computer company, I want nothing to do with it.” Bryan pointed out the email. The VC replied, “Doesn’t sound like me.” But Bryan knew the real reason: the VC had two big zeros in hardware, and one looked a lot like Oxide. When a VC has been burned, they become risk-averse in that domain—you need “optimistic” (or naive) investors who can “envision the world as it could be, as opposed to getting all mired in the way the world is.” The VC did offer to help, and Bryan had a concrete ask: connect him with the founders of that failed company so he could understand everything that went wrong.

That conversation was invaluable. The company had built something like an early Nitro-style offload card, found a single customer (a huge bank that wanted it for six Active Directory servers), mistook that for product-market fit, raised a ton of money, hired a huge go-to-market team, and then discovered the bank only needed six units. They were eventually acquired, but the founder was bitter and trapped. When Bryan described Oxide’s rack-scale vision, the founder called it **“a suicide mission.”** Bryan wrote that down and underlined it: “Okay, we’re on a suicide mission. That’s fun.”

On GPUs: Oxide has deliberately focused on general-purpose CPU compute, storage, and networking—not accelerators. “There are effectively two doors for Oxide. One is labeled compete with Nvidia, and the other is labeled partner with Nvidia. And I didn’t want to do either of those things.” Nvidia is too proprietary to allow the deep hardware-software co-design Oxide requires. Meanwhile, on the CPU side, the incumbents (HP, Dell, Super Micro) are “doing the same kind of junk honestly that they have been doing,” leaving Oxide alone as a hardware-software product company. Customers with massive GPU fleets keep coming to Oxide for their CPU needs anyway: “When you’re sitting there on ChatGPT and it’s surfing the web… that is a CPU that’s surfing the web.”

**Revisiting past takes**

Bryan gamely lets Ryan grill him on past statements. The 1996 Usenet post—“Have you ever kissed a girl?”—was an obscure Saturday Night Live reference (William Shatner at a Trekkie convention: “Get a life, people!”) that Bryan now firmly regrets. “If you could have told me… 30 years in the future you’re gonna be asked about this, I’d be like, what the hell?” He’s never met the recipient, David S. Miller, but read later that Miller viewed the exchange as a learning moment and was “being very generous to me.”

On a 2022 tweet—“failure is almost always due to bad leadership”—he stands by it. But he pushes back on the premise that Sun “failed.” “Sun is founded in 1983 and is invaded in 2008, 2009. That’s a good run.” Sun was a Fortune 200 company; lots of kids went to college because their parents worked there. “Sun did not succeed to the scope of its own ambition, but Sun is not a failure. Sun is a success.” Its end was partly structural—it got strung out on dot-com margins, embraced x86 too late, became fractured through layoffs—and partly natural lifecycle. “The way a forest fire in a normal healthy forest is part of the life cycle of a forest—you need that to have rebirth.”

On Musk and Twitter: Bryan calls it “a tire fire,” and the specific reason Oxide pulled its presence is that he can’t have an Oxide tweet sitting next to “crazy racism, crazy anti-Semitism, crazy anti-Islam, just crazy hate.” He rejects the free-speech framing: “It’s so deeply offensive that I don’t want my content to be anywhere near it.”

**AI boom and bust advice**

Bryan once thought software was immune to the boom-bust cycles of the oil patch (his grandfather was a petroleum engineer). He was wrong. But the cycles are endemic for a reason: “we get so optimistic that we kind of get ahead of ourselves from an optimism perspective. We also get ahead of ourselves from a pessimism perspective.” His advice for navigating AI hype and layoffs: **be your own judge.** In 1996, people told him operating systems were done. In 2019, VCs told him you can’t start a computer company—and those same VCs were saying “we only fund SaaS.” If LLM-assisted coding puts a squeeze on SaaS but SaaS is in your heart, ignore the pessimism. Conversely, don’t chase a hot space purely because it’s hot. “Don’t do the things that like, well, I’m doing this because it’s a hot space. You should do this because… this is the dent I want to kick in the universe.”

**When he was happiest in his career**

When a friend asked Bryan, before he started Oxide, *why* he wanted to found a company, the answer wasn’t financial return (“that’s just not good life advice”). The happiest moments of his career had been **working on an incredible team** where “everyone individually is like, I don’t think we can pull this off. This is actually too difficult to pull off. And then everybody works together, compliments one another, and you pull it off.” He’d experienced that with DTrace, with Fishworks, and at Joyent with LX and Triton. That became the bedrock of Oxide. The organizational model is **a heist movie**: “you’ve got your safe cracker, you’ve got your getaway driver, your demolitions expert”—each person’s skill set arrives at exactly the right moment. He and Steve built a company around that team dynamic, and it’s why they’ve pulled off what they have with a team so small it shocks people.

**Top career regret**

Bryan made what he calls “the worst hire in human history” at Joyent—someone who presented under an assumed name and had just gotten off parole for violent felonies from San Quentin. “And that’s not what made him a bad employee.” The experience forced him to strip hiring to the studs and rebuild it from first principles: a writing-intensive process that probes intrinsic motivation. That process became the hiring process at Oxide, and Bryan believes the extraordinary Oxide team is a direct result. “I needed that guy.” He wouldn’t remove the mistake from his past even if he could—without it, he’d have kept hiring naively, not selecting for values, and “I don’t think Oxide makes it.” The only timeline edit he might make is the Usenet post.

**Advice for younger self**

He wouldn’t give himself any. “Jesus, don’t fuck it up.” He feels extraordinarily lucky—right place, right time too many times to count, and he trusted his gut when it was the contrary thing to do. The mistakes became load-bearing: “I wouldn’t want to not make those mistakes. Those mistakes were really important.” He’s been blessed with incredible colleagues, and he wouldn’t risk tampering with any of it.

---

The thread running through Bryan’s career is a refusal to let external structures define meaning—whether that’s the **“suicide mission”** of starting a hardware company, the **heist-movie team model** that fueled every peak moment, or his instinct that **“I would much rather be under promoted than over promoted.”** His war against stack ranking as **“organizational cancer”** is a natural extension: teams succeed, not individuals measured against each other. Even the worst-hire-in-human-history catastrophe became load-bearing because it forced him to rebuild hiring around intrinsic motivation—the same inner compass that told him to ignore VCs who said “you can’t start a computer company,” to honor Scott McNealy’s **“kicked butt, had fun, didn’t cheat”** epitaph as Oxide’s mission, and to side-eye the hollowness of any title divorced from the work itself.
