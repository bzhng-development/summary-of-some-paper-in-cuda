Here are the takeaways from this Ryan Peterman interview (went from Senior to Staff Software Engineer at Meta, built his career on video processing and compute efficiency):

**What Staff-Level Scope Actually Is**

Ryan defines Staff-level work as needing to hit three high-level characteristics simultaneously. First, **complexity**—solving problems that senior engineers genuinely cannot solve, not just doing more of the same work. Second, **impact**—the wins span beyond your immediate team and achieve a significant part of collaborative, cross-org goals. Third, **workstream size**—there are many engineers involved and a roadmap that stretches across multiple quarters. He’s emphatic that you cannot grow to the Staff level "no matter how hard you work" if the work itself doesn’t fit these criteria. The raw effort isn’t the bottleneck; the nature of the scope is. Once you understand the bar, the whole game becomes locating problems that meet it and then delivering on them. His own gap going from Senior to Staff was precisely this—his manager paired him with mentors to fix it, and following their advice got him promoted "in 2 halves."

**The "Top Down" Approach: Mining Your Leadership Chain**

The most straightforward way to find Staff-level scope is to simply ask for it. Ryan argues that it is "part of the engineering manager’s job to know what the top problems their teams are facing," which means your manager and skip manager are already carrying a mental list of unsolved, high-stakes problems. By directly asking what’s top-of-mind for them, you surface work that comes with built-in alignment on its importance and business impact because the leadership chain already believes it matters. This approach "is easiest since there is already alignment on the work’s importance and potential business impact." Sourcing work this way doubles as a trust-building mechanism: being consistent and reliable on past projects makes directors willing to hand you bigger things. In Ryan's own case, his director gave him the opportunity to lead two org-wide initiatives precisely because that track record of reliability existed, and those initiatives directly contributed to his promotion.

**The "Bottom Up" Approach: Creating Scope Without Permission**

The riskier but more common path Ryan observes for unusually fast growth is the "bottom up" approach, where the impactful work is something management hasn’t yet identified. There's an extra step of convincing people the work is important, but the structural advantage is that "no one needs to trust you with the project; you create the scope yourself." This flips the dependency: you don’t wait to be handed something big. He outlines two specific ways he’s seen engineers generate this kind of scope from scratch. The first is **leveraging domain expertise**: ideas for improvement flow naturally when you have a strong end-to-end understanding of a system. Ryan’s own deep background in video processing for organic media gave him "strong opinions of how things should be." When he started digging into the ads video pipeline—a related but separate area—he immediately saw "a ton of opportunities to improve it." That understanding translated into massive business impact through "many halves of collaboration with the ads team." The second is **discovering a significant problem** by digging around in less mature areas. His flagship Staff-promo project followed this pattern: video compute efficiency was a less mature space with lots of opportunity, and when he investigated, he realized the company was literally running out of compute. Chasing that problem down turned into a massive initiative, drove huge savings, and was consequential enough that "even Zuck wrote about it."

A critical behavior distinction Ryan draws between Senior and Staff engineers is exactly this: thinking beyond your immediate team. When Staff-level scope doesn't exist on your own team—and often it doesn't—you look outward at partnerships or across the broader engineering org to find it. "That is a key behavior difference between the Staff and Senior levels." The entire promotion arc, in his telling, hinges on first internalizing the complexity-impact-workstream bar, then actively hunting for problems that clear it, whether handed down from leadership or dug up yourself in adjacent, less-mature spaces.

---
Ryan’s framing recasts the Staff promo from a performance review to a **scope discovery** problem: you cannot out-work a role whose problems don’t meet the bar. His **top down** and **bottom up** approaches are less about personality and more about sourcing strategy—mining existing leadership pain versus creating unrecognized scope through **domain expertise** or **discovering a significant problem** in unmined terrain. Both paths converge on the same insight he hammered home for himself in those two halves of mentorship: the work exists, but you have to leave your immediate team to find it.
