# DeepSpeed Ulysses: System Optimizations for Enabling Training of Extreme Long Sequence Transformer Models

**ArXiv:** [2309.14509](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.14509)

## 🎯 Pitch

This paper introduces **DeepSpeed-Ulysses**, a system methodology for enabling highly efficient training of Transformer models with extremely long sequence lengths.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **DeepSpeed-Ulysses**, a system methodology for enabling highly efficient training of Transformer models with extremely long sequence lengths. The approach is evaluated on GPT models ranging from 1.2B to 30B parameters across up to 256 A100 GPUs. DeepSpeed-Ulysses partitions input data along the sequence dimension and employs an efficient all-to-all collective communication for attention computation—allowing each GPU to compute attention for a non-overlapping subset of attention heads while retaining the full sequence context for those heads (e.g., partitioning QKV tensors via all-to-all rather than all-gather or ring-style transmission). The method achieves up to 2.5× throughput improvement and trains with 4× longer sequence lengths (over a million tokens) compared to the Megatron-LM sequence parallelism baseline, while sustaining over 175 TFLOPs/GPU—over 54% of hardware peak. The communication volume per link remains constant when sequence length and GPU count are increased proportionally (O(N/P) complexity), establishing that communication-efficient long-sequence training is achievable only when the parallelism strategy avoids linear-in-sequence-length communication overhead.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Sequence Length Is the "Fourth Dimension" That Existing Parallelism Strategies Ignore

Distributed training of large Transformer models has focused overwhelmingly on three axes of parallelism: **data parallelism** for batch size (Dean et al., 2012), **tensor parallelism** for hidden dimension (Shoeybi et al., 2019), and **pipeline parallelism** for model depth or number of layers (Narayanan et al., 2019; Huang et al., 2018). These three strategies are collectively combined into what practitioners call "3D parallelism" (Team and Majumder, 2020; Smith et al., 2022), which forms the backbone of essentially all large-scale LLM training infrastructure today.

But there is a fourth dimension that has been systematically overlooked: **sequence length**. The paper argues, both in Section 1 and Section 2, that sequence length scaling is qualitatively different from the other three dimensions and that the existing parallelism toolkit is poorly equipped to handle it. This gap matters because the demand for long sequence training is exploding across multiple fronts:

**Generative AI.** Conversational AI systems like ChatGPT (and their open-source counterparts, e.g., LLaMA 2 from Touvron et al., 2023) require processing long chat histories—potentially tens of thousands of tokens of dialogue context—to maintain coherent multi-turn interactions. Long-document summarization at the chapter or book level involves sequences estimated at tens to hundreds of thousands of words (Beltagy et al., 2020; Kryściński et al., 2022; MosaicML, 2023). Multimodal foundation models that process speech, images, and waveforms concurrently must reason over long contexts in both spatial and temporal domains, generating high-dimensional inputs with extremely long sequences.

**AI for science.** The paper highlights several domains where sequence length is the defining challenge. In genomics, language models trained on gene sequences must process the entire human genome—6.4 billion letters—as a single sequence to capture evolutionary patterns (Zvyagin et al., 2022). In healthcare, diagnostic predictive models conditioned on entire patient care records require context windows spanning years of clinical notes and test results (Li et al., 2022a; Gao et al., 2021). In climate and weather modeling, foundation models like ClimaX (Nguyen et al., 2023) must ingest long spatiotemporal sequences of atmospheric measurements.

The practical urgency is driven by a concrete operational problem in large-scale training. Consider the scenario the paper describes in Section 2.1.2: a team pre-training an LLM on 1024 GPUs with a sequence length of 8,192 tokens and a micro-batch size of 1 per GPU (yielding a global batch of 8 million tokens). Improving model quality requires extending the sequence length to 32,768 tokens. Without sequence parallelism, this forces the global batch size to balloon from 8 million to roughly 32 million tokens—far beyond the batch sizes known to harm model convergence (Keskar et al., 2016). The only way to keep the global batch size reasonable while increasing sequence length is to **split individual sequences across multiple GPUs**. This is precisely what sequence parallelism provides, making it not merely a performance optimization but a **necessary technique for training with long sequences at scale**.

### Where Existing Approaches Fall Short

The paper identifies two fundamental problems with prior sequence parallelism methods, both detailed in Section 2.2 and summarized in Table 1:

#### Problem 1: Communication Inefficiency That Scales Linearly with Sequence Length

The central bottleneck in long-sequence training is not compute—attention computation is embarrassingly parallel across heads—but **communication**. To compute global attention, each GPU needs access to the full sequence context for its assigned attention heads. The question is *how* the partitioned sequence data gets communicated between GPUs, and the answer determines whether the approach scales to extreme sequence lengths.

The paper identifies two prior works that represent the state of the art, each with a critical communication limitation:

**Megatron-LM sequence parallelism** (Korthikanti et al., 2022) is tightly integrated with Megatron's tensor parallelism. It partitions input data along the sequence dimension and uses **all-gather** and **reduce-scatter** collectives to aggregate QKV projections for attention computation. The paper's communication analysis (Section 3.2) shows that all-gather and reduce-scatter have a fundamental property: the communication volume per link is $M$ (the full message size), not $M/P$ (message size divided by the number of GPUs), when $P \gg 1$. Since the QKV projections involved in attention grow with sequence length $N$, Megatron-LM's approach incurs a per-link communication volume of $4Nh$ per Transformer layer—**linear in $N$ and independent of $P$**. This means adding more GPUs does *not* reduce the per-GPU communication cost, creating a hard scaling ceiling. The paper expresses this as communication complexity $O(N)$.

To understand why this matters concretely: if sequence length doubles, Megatron-LM's communication cost doubles *regardless of how many GPUs are added*. At million-token sequence lengths, this linear scaling makes training infeasible even on large GPU clusters.

**ColAI-SP** (Li et al., 2022b) takes a different approach called "ring self-attention." It partitions sequences so that query projections remain local to each GPU, while key and value projections are transmitted in a ring-style collective to compute global attention. The communication complexity is also $O(M)$—linear in message size—because the ring transmission requires each GPU to forward the full K and V tensors sequentially around the ring. The paper notes (Section 2.2) that ColAI-SP also has significant practical limitations: it requires a specific, non-standard attention mechanism (ring self-attention), and it is unclear how well this generalizes to other attention types (sparse attention, FlashAttention, cross-attention). The paper marks ColAI-SP as "not easy to use" in Table 1.

#### Problem 2: Memory Inefficiency from Tight Coupling to Tensor Parallelism

A second critical limitation of Megatron-LM's approach (Section 2.2, Table 1) is that its sequence parallelism is **tightly integrated with Megatron's tensor parallelism**. This coupling creates two problems. First, it limits **memory efficiency**: tensor parallelism replicates certain activations and model states that ZeRO-style optimizations (Rajbhandari et al., 2020, 2021) could otherwise partition, meaning the system cannot benefit from the most aggressive memory-saving techniques when using Megatron-LM sequence parallelism. Second, it limits **ease of use**: adopting Megatron-LM sequence parallelism requires buying into the entire Megatron-LM codebase and its specific way of partitioning operators, which the paper characterizes in Table 1 as not attention-agnostic and difficult to integrate with existing training frameworks.

#### Problem 3: No Existing Method Achieves Constant Communication Volume

The paper's key theoretical insight, developed in Section 3.2, is that an ideal sequence parallelism strategy should have **communication volume that remains constant when sequence length and GPU count are increased proportionally**. This is the sequence-length analog of weak scaling in traditional HPC: if you double the problem size (sequence length) and double the resources (GPUs), the per-GPU communication cost should stay roughly flat. Neither Megatron-LM nor ColAI-SP achieves this. Megatron-LM's $O(N)$ complexity means communication grows linearly with sequence length regardless of GPU count. ColAI-SP's ring-based approach has the same asymptotic limitation.

---

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper frames DeepSpeed-Ulysses as a **fundamentally different communication strategy** that achieves exactly the constant-volume property missing from prior work. Rather than using all-gather (Megatron-LM) or ring transmission (ColAI-SP), DeepSpeed-Ulysses uses **all-to-all collective communication** to redistribute QKV tensors so that each GPU receives the full sequence but only for a non-overlapping subset of attention heads. The all-to-all primitive has the crucial property that **per-link communication volume is $M/P$, not $M$**—meaning it *does* decrease with GPU count. When sequence length $N$ and GPU count $P$ are increased proportionally, the per-link volume $4Nh/P$ remains constant, giving $O(N/P)$ complexity.

This distinction is not merely an implementation detail. It represents a qualitative shift in scalability: DeepSpeed-Ulysses can train with million-token sequences because its communication cost is bounded by the ratio of sequence length to GPU count, whereas Megatron-LM's cost grows without bound.

The paper also positions DeepSpeed-Ulysses as a **modular, composable solution** rather than a monolithic alternative to existing parallelism strategies. It is designed to work *alongside* ZeRO-3 for model state partitioning (Section 3.3), data parallelism, and pipeline parallelism—not replace them. The attention computation itself is fully general: DeepSpeed-Ulysses supports dense attention, sparse attention (Child et al., 2019; Choromanski et al., 2020; Zaheer et al., 2021), and optimized kernels like FlashAttention v2 (Dao, 2023). This modularity is captured in Table 1, where DeepSpeed-Ulysses is the only method marked as supporting attention-agnostic computation, ZeRO memory efficiency, and easy integration.

A subtle but important positioning detail: DeepSpeed-Ulysses is **orthogonal to FlashAttention** and similar single-GPU memory optimization techniques (Dao et al., 2022; Dao, 2023). FlashAttention addresses the problem of computing attention efficiently on a single GPU through tiling and recomputation to avoid materializing the full $N \times N$ attention matrix. DeepSpeed-Ulysses addresses the *distributed* problem: how to partition the sequence across GPUs so that the per-GPU attention computation (which can itself use FlashAttention) can proceed with full context. The paper explicitly leverages FlashAttention v2 as the local attention kernel within each GPU, making the two techniques complementary rather than competing.

The paper's contribution is thus positioned at the **systems and communication layer**, not at the attention algorithm layer. It does not propose a new attention mechanism or a new sparse approximation—it proposes a new way to *distribute* existing attention mechanisms across GPUs so that sequence length can scale far beyond single-GPU memory limits without incurring prohibitive communication overhead.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

DeepSpeed-Ulysses is a **distributed communication strategy** for the attention computation in Transformer models—essentially a method for splitting a single very long input sequence across multiple GPUs and coordinating their work so that each GPU can compute its share of the attention heads correctly while seeing the full sequence. The problem it solves is this: when training a Transformer on sequences so long that a single GPU cannot hold the entire sequence in memory, you must partition the sequence across GPUs, but then each GPU only sees a fragment of the full context—which breaks the attention mechanism's core requirement that every position attend to every other position. The "shape" of the solution is to use a carefully chosen collective communication primitive (all-to-all) that temporarily reshuffles data across GPUs so that each GPU has the complete sequence for a subset of attention heads, computes attention locally with full context, then reshuffles the results back—enabling the whole process to scale without communication costs growing with sequence length.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The system has four major components operating within each Transformer layer:

1. **Input Sequence Partitioner**: Takes the raw input sequences of length `$N$` and distributes them evenly across `$P$` GPUs, so each GPU holds `$N/P$` contiguous tokens of each sequence in the batch. This is the initial and final state of data within each layer—everything else is a temporary transformation for attention computation.

2. **QKV Projection (Local)**: On each GPU, the `$N/P$` local sequence tokens are projected into queries (Q), keys (K), and values (V) embeddings independently—this is standard Transformer computation requiring no communication. The Q, K, and V tensors on each GPU have shape `$(N/P, d)$` where `$d$` is the per-head dimension.

3. **All-to-All Reshuffling Collective**: This is the core innovation. The `$N/P$`-length Q, K, and V tensors, each split across `$h$` attention heads on each GPU, are redistributed via an all-to-all communication operation. After this operation, each GPU holds the **full sequence** (`$N$` tokens) but only for a **non-overlapping subset of `$h/P$` attention heads**. This is the key state transformation: what was partitioned by sequence position is now partitioned by attention head.

4. **Local Attention Computation**: With the full sequence for its assigned heads now resident locally, each GPU computes standard attention (dot-product, softmax, value aggregation) independently—no further communication is needed for attention. Any attention variant (dense, sparse, FlashAttention) works here because each GPU has the complete `$N$`-token context for its heads.

5. **All-to-All Reverse Shuffling**: The output of attention computation is reshuffled back via another all-to-all, restoring the sequence-partitioned layout (`$N/P$` tokens per GPU, all heads) so that subsequent operations (MLP, layer norm, residual connections) can proceed with their standard data layout.

Information flows through each Transformer layer in this sequence: local QKV projection → all-to-all to head-partitioned full sequences → attention computation per head → all-to-all back to sequence-partitioned layout → feed-forward and other layer components. This cycle repeats for every Transformer layer in the model.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First, the system architecture and data layout transformation**, because the entire approach hinges on understanding what data resides where at each stage and how the all-to-all collective changes the partitioning scheme from sequence-dimension to head-dimension and back.

- **Second, the communication analysis (Section 3.2)**, because it is the paper's central theoretical contribution—showing that the per-link communication volume is `$O(N/P)$` rather than `$O(N)$` and explaining *why* the all-to-all achieves this while all-gather and ring-based approaches do not. This analysis is what distinguishes DeepSpeed-Ulysses from Megatron-LM and ColAI-SP.

- **Third, the memory efficiency integration with ZeRO-3 (Section 3.3)**, because communication efficiency alone is insufficient—long sequences consume enormous activation memory, and the ability to partition model states across both data-parallel and sequence-parallel ranks is what enables training with both large models and long sequences simultaneously.

- **Fourth, the generalization properties (Section 3.4)**, because the paper claims attention-agnostic operation (dense, sparse, FlashAttention, cross-attention, causal attention) and ease of use, and understanding *why* these properties follow from the architecture is essential for practitioners evaluating whether to adopt the approach.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **systems paper** whose core idea is that the communication pattern used to exchange sequence fragments during distributed attention computation determines the asymptotic scalability of long-sequence training, and that an all-to-all collective on attention heads achieves constant per-GPU communication volume when sequence length and GPU count scale proportionally—a property that all-gather and ring-based approaches fundamentally cannot match.

#### The Core Data Layout Transformation

The fundamental operation in DeepSpeed-Ulysses is a **data layout transformation** that occurs twice per Transformer layer: once before attention computation and once after. Understanding this transformation requires following the state of the data through each stage.

**Stage 1: Sequence-Partitioned Input.** The input to each Transformer layer is a tensor of hidden states with shape `$(B, N, H)$` where `$B$` is the micro-batch size, `$N$` is the sequence length, and `$H$` is the hidden dimension. DeepSpeed-Ulysses partitions this tensor along the sequence dimension across `$P$` GPUs. Each GPU `$i$` (where `$i \in \{0, 1, ..., P-1\}$`) receives a contiguous chunk of `$N/P$` tokens—specifically, tokens `$[i \cdot N/P, (i+1) \cdot N/P)$` of each sequence in the batch. The per-GPU tensor therefore has shape `$(B, N/P, H)$`. This partitioning is identical to what Megatron-LM and ColAI-SP do at the input stage—the difference emerges in what happens next.

**Stage 2: Local QKV Projection.** On each GPU, the `$N/P$` local sequence tokens are projected into queries, keys, and values using the standard weight matrices `$W^Q$`, `$W^K$`, and `$W^V$`. Because these are per-token linear projections with no cross-token interaction, they require zero communication. Each GPU now holds Q, K, and V tensors of shape `$(B, N/P, h, d_{\text{head}} \cdot n_{\text{heads\_local}})$` where `$n_{\text{heads\_local}}$` is the number of attention heads assigned to each GPU in the subsequent attention computation. Crucially, at this point each GPU holds the Q, K, and V for **all attention heads** but only for its **local sequence chunk** of length `$N/P$`.

**Stage 3: All-to-All Reshuffling.** This is the defining operation of DeepSpeed-Ulysses. An all-to-all collective communication is performed on the Q, K, and V tensors. Conceptually, the all-to-all operation can be understood as every GPU sending a distinct sub-tensor to every other GPU while simultaneously receiving a distinct sub-tensor from every other GPU. In DeepSpeed-Ulysses, the partitioning dimension for the all-to-all is the **attention head dimension**.

Before the all-to-all, GPU `$i$` holds heads `$[0, h)$` (all heads) for sequence tokens `$[i \cdot N/P, (i+1) \cdot N/P)$`. The all-to-all redistributes along the head dimension such that after the operation, GPU `$i$` holds heads `$[i \cdot h/P, (i+1) \cdot h/P)$` (a non-overlapping subset of `$h/P$` heads) for the **full sequence** of `$N$` tokens. The tensor shape on each GPU transforms from `$(B, N/P, h, d_{\text{head}})$` to `$(B, N, h/P, d_{\text{head}})$`.

This transformation is what makes the approach work. Each GPU now has exactly the data it needs to compute attention for its assigned heads: the complete Q, K, and V for every token in the sequence, but restricted to its subset of heads. No GPU needs to communicate with any other GPU during the actual attention computation because each head's attention is independent of all other heads—attention heads do not interact with each other.

**Stage 4: Local Attention Computation.** With the full `$N$`-token Q, K, and V for its `$h/P$` heads now resident in local memory, each GPU computes standard scaled dot-product attention independently. The computation for each head follows the standard formula:

$$\text{Output}_{\text{context}} = \text{Softmax}\left(\frac{QK^T}{\sqrt{d}}\right) V$$

where `$Q$` is the query tensor of shape `$(N, d_{\text{head}})$` for one head, `$K$` is the key tensor of shape `$(N, d_{\text{head}})$`, `$V$` is the value tensor of shape `$(N, d_{\text{head}})$`, and `$d_{\text{head}}$` is the per-head dimension (typically `$H/h$`).

**What this computes:** For each query position, a weighted sum of all value vectors, where the weights are determined by the scaled dot-product similarity between the query and all keys. The softmax normalizes these similarities into a probability distribution over all `$N$` positions. The output is a tensor of shape `$(N, d_{\text{head}})$` for each head, representing the context-aware representation of each position after attending to the full sequence.

**Why this form:** The `$1/\sqrt{d}$` scaling factor prevents the dot products from growing too large in magnitude as the dimensionality increases, which would push the softmax into regions of extreme saturation (near-zero or near-one attention weights) and prevent effective gradient-based learning. This is the standard Transformer attention formula (Vaswani et al., 2017) applied without modification—DeepSpeed-Ulysses does not change the attention computation itself, only the data distribution that feeds it.

**Stage 5: All-to-All Reverse Shuffling.** After attention computation, each GPU holds an output tensor of shape `$(B, N, h/P, d_{\text{head}})$`—the attention outputs for its assigned heads across all sequence positions. Another all-to-all collective is performed, this time reversing the earlier transformation: the head dimension is redistributed so that each GPU once again holds all `$h$` heads, but only for its original sequence chunk of `$N/P$` tokens. The output tensor shape returns to `$(B, N/P, H)$` (after concatenating heads and projecting), matching the expected input shape for subsequent operations (MLP, layer norm, residual addition).

**Why two all-to-alls are necessary.** The first all-to-all creates the data layout needed for attention (full sequence per head subset). The second all-to-all restores the data layout needed for everything else. The MLP and layer norm operations are per-token operations with no cross-token dependency, so they naturally operate on the sequence-partitioned layout. The residual connection adds the attention output to the original input, which requires matching layouts.

#### Communication Analysis: Why All-to-All Achieves `$O(N/P)$` While All-Gather Is `$O(N)$`

The paper's central theoretical claim (Section 3.2) is that DeepSpeed-Ulysses incurs communication volume per link of `$4Nh/P$` per Transformer layer, which is `$O(N/P)$`, while Megatron-LM incurs `$4Nh$` per link, which is `$O(N)$`. Understanding *why* requires examining the properties of different collective communication primitives.

**The communication model.** The paper assumes a modern cluster with intra-node NVSwitch interconnect and inter-node fat-tree InfiniBand topology. Under this model, the key property of an all-to-all collective with aggregate message size `$M$` over `$P$` GPUs is that the **communication volume transmitted per link is `$M/P$`**. The total data moved is `$M$`, but it is distributed across `$P$` links, so each link carries only `$M/P$`. This is the crucial property that enables constant per-GPU communication as `$P$` scales.

In contrast, for all-gather and reduce-scatter with aggregate message size `$M$` over `$P$` GPUs, the communication volume per link remains `$M$` when `$P \gg 1$`. This is because these collectives require each GPU to either receive the full `$M$` of data (all-gather) or contribute to a full `$M$` reduction (reduce-scatter). Even though the work is distributed across GPUs, the per-link bandwidth consumption does not decrease with `$P$`.

**DeepSpeed-Ulysses communication volume, layer by layer.** For each Transformer layer:

- **First all-to-all (QKV):** The Q, K, and V tensors each have an aggregate message size of `$Nh$` (sequence length times hidden dimension). Since there are three tensors (Q, K, V), the total aggregate message size is `$3Nh$`. With `$P$` GPUs, the per-link volume is `$3Nh/P$`.

- **Second all-to-all (attention output):** The output context tensor from attention has the same shape as the input to attention, so its aggregate message size is `$Nh$`. The per-link volume is `$Nh/P$`.

- **Total per layer:** `$3Nh/P + Nh/P = 4Nh/P$`.

**Megatron-LM communication volume, layer by layer.** Megatron-LM's sequence parallelism (Section 2.2, building on Korthikanti et al., 2022) uses a different sequence of collectives:

- **Two all-gather operations:** One for keys and one for values (or their equivalents in the partitioned computation), each with aggregate message size `$Nh$`. Since all-gather has per-link volume `$M$` (not `$M/P$`), the per-link volume is `$2Nh$`.

- **Two reduce-scatter operations:** Used to aggregate and redistribute results, each with aggregate message size `$Nh$`. Per-link volume is `$2Nh$`.

- **Total per layer:** `$2Nh + 2Nh = 4Nh$`, which is `$P$` times larger than DeepSpeed-Ulysses.

**The constant-volume property.** When sequence length `$N$` and GPU count `$P$` are increased proportionally (e.g., double both), the DeepSpeed-Ulysses per-link volume `$4Nh/P$` remains constant because the numerator and denominator scale together. In contrast, Megatron-LM's per-link volume `$4Nh$` doubles because it depends only on `$N$`, independent of `$P$`. This is the paper's "constant communication volume" claim—it means that as long as the ratio `$N/P$` is held fixed, the communication cost per GPU does not grow, enabling scaling to arbitrarily long sequences by adding proportionally more GPUs.

**What the `$4$` and `$h$` represent.** The factor of 4 comes from the three QKV tensors in the first all-to-all plus the single output tensor in the second all-to-all (3 + 1 = 4). The hidden dimension `$h$` appears because it determines the size of each token's representation. The per-token communication cost is proportional to `$h$`, which is fixed for a given model architecture.

#### Memory Efficiency Through ZeRO-3 Integration

While DeepSpeed-Ulysses reduces activation memory by partitioning sequences across GPUs, it does not address model state memory (parameters, optimizer states, gradients). For large models with long sequences, both activation memory and model state memory must be managed simultaneously. Section 3.3 describes how DeepSpeed-Ulysses integrates with ZeRO-3 to achieve this.

**ZeRO-3 in standard (non-sequence-parallel) training.** ZeRO Redundancy Optimizer Stage 3 (Rajbhandari et al., 2020, 2021) partitions model states (parameters, gradients, and optimizer states) across data-parallel ranks. In standard data-parallel training, each GPU holds a full replica of the model, consuming identical copies of all parameters. ZeRO-3 instead partitions these states so that each GPU stores only `$1/D$` of the model states, where `$D$` is the data-parallel degree. When a particular parameter partition is needed for computation, it is gathered (via all-gather) on demand; after the computation, the gathered parameters can be discarded, keeping only the owned partition resident in memory.

**Extending ZeRO-3 partitioning to the combined sequence-and-data-parallel group.** DeepSpeed-Ulysses introduces a critical extension. With sequence parallelism, the training data is distributed along *two* orthogonal dimensions: the batch (sample) dimension for data parallelism and the sequence dimension for sequence parallelism. The paper treats these two parallel groups as a **combined group** for ZeRO partitioning. In other words, model states are partitioned across *both* data-parallel ranks *and* sequence-parallel ranks, forming a larger effective parallelism degree.

Concretely, if there are `$D$` data-parallel replicas and `$P$` sequence-parallel GPUs per replica, the total number of GPUs in the combined group is `$D \times P$`. ZeRO-3 partitions model states across all `$D \times P$` GPUs, so each GPU stores only `$1/(D \times P)$` of the model states rather than `$1/D$`. This provides `$P$`-fold additional memory savings for model states compared to data-parallel-only ZeRO-3.

**On-demand parameter gathering.** When a forward or backward pass requires a particular parameter partition that is not locally resident, an all-gather is performed across the combined sequence-and-data-parallel group to collect the partition. After the computation using that partition is complete, the non-owned portions can be freed. This is the same mechanism as standard ZeRO-3, applied over the larger combined group.

**Gradient reduction.** Similarly, gradients are reduced (via reduce-scatter) across the combined sequence-and-data-parallel group. Each GPU ends up with the reduced gradient for its owned parameter partition, which it then uses to update its local optimizer states and parameters.

**Memory scaling benefits.** The integration with ZeRO-3 means that DeepSpeed-Ulysses addresses *both* sources of memory pressure in long-sequence large-model training: activation memory is reduced by sequence partitioning (`$1/P$` reduction in per-GPU activation memory), and model state memory is reduced by ZeRO-3 partitioning across the combined group (`$1/(D \times P)$` reduction). This dual reduction is what enables training with both large models (billions of parameters) and long sequences (millions of tokens) simultaneously—neither mechanism alone would be sufficient.

**Table 1 captures this.** In the comparison table, DeepSpeed-Ulysses is the only method marked with checkmarks for both "Activation memory efficiency" (achieved by sequence partitioning) and "Parameter memory efficiency" (achieved by ZeRO-3 integration). Megatron-LM is marked only for activation memory efficiency, not parameter efficiency, because its tight coupling with tensor parallelism limits ZeRO-style optimization. ColAI-SP checks neither, presumably because its ring-based approach does not naturally compose with ZeRO partitioning.

#### Attention-Agnostic Design: Why It Supports Any Attention Mechanism

Section 3.4 explains why DeepSpeed-Ulysses is "attention-agnostic"—meaning it works with any attention variant without modification to the core communication pattern. This property follows from the modular separation between data distribution (all-to-all collectives) and computation (the attention function itself).

**The modular boundary.** DeepSpeed-Ulysses draws a clean interface: everything before the first all-to-all is sequence-partitioned and attention-agnostic (standard QKV projection); everything between the two all-to-alls is head-partitioned and attention-specific; everything after the second all-to-all is sequence-partitioned and attention-agnostic (standard MLP, layer norm, etc.). The attention computation in the middle receives exactly the same input format that a single-GPU attention implementation would receive—a full `$N$`-token sequence for a subset of heads—and is expected to produce the same output format. There is no requirement for the attention function to know about the distributed state, the all-to-all collectives, or any other aspect of the system.

**Concrete supported attention types.** Because the attention module is a black box from the system's perspective, DeepSpeed-Ulysses supports:

- **Dense (full) self-attention:** The standard Transformer attention where every position attends to every other position. This is what the paper primarily evaluates.

- **Causal (autoregressive) attention:** Used in decoder-only models like GPT, where each position attends only to itself and preceding positions. The causal mask is applied locally within each GPU's attention computation—it does not interact with the distributed communication pattern.

- **Cross-attention:** Used in encoder-decoder models, where queries come from the decoder and keys/values come from the encoder. The all-to-all can be applied to the encoder's K and V and the decoder's Q independently, or to both sides, depending on which sequences are long.

- **Sparse attention:** Various approximations (e.g., block-sparse, sliding window, Longformer-style local+global patterns from Beltagy et al., 2020) that reduce the quadratic complexity of dense attention. These are implemented as modifications to the attention kernel—the data layout provided by DeepSpeed-Ulysses is the same as for dense attention, so sparse kernels can be substituted directly. The paper evaluates block-sparse attention in Section 4.3.

- **FlashAttention v2 (Dao, 2023):** An IO-aware exact attention kernel that avoids materializing the full `$N \times N$` attention matrix by computing attention in tiles and recomputing the softmax normalization online. FlashAttention is the local attention implementation used within each GPU in DeepSpeed-Ulysses—the all-to-all provides the full sequence to each GPU, and FlashAttention processes it efficiently in GPU memory.

**Why this modularity is non-trivial.** Prior approaches are less general. ColAI-SP's ring self-attention requires a specific attention mechanism that processes key-value pairs as they arrive around the ring—it cannot simply drop in FlashAttention or an existing sparse attention kernel. Megatron-LM's sequence parallelism is tightly integrated with its tensor parallelism, meaning the attention computation must be implemented in a Megatron-specific way that interleaves the all-gather/reduce-scatter collectives with the attention math. DeepSpeed-Ulysses's modularity means that any attention kernel that works on a single GPU can be used without modification, which is a significant practical advantage for adopting new attention variants as they are developed.

**The attention-centric design philosophy.** The paper describes the approach as "attention-centric sequence parallelism" (Section 3.4). The core insight is that attention is the only operation in a Transformer that requires cross-token communication—all other operations (MLP, layer norm, embedding, output projection) are per-token. Therefore, the sequence parallelism strategy should focus exclusively on making attention work correctly in a distributed setting, and leave everything else in its natural sequence-partitioned state. This design philosophy is what makes the all-to-all approach work: it temporarily reshuffles data to a layout that makes attention local (full sequence per head subset), performs attention, then reshuffles back. The rest of the model never needs to know that anything distributed happened.

---

#### Design Choice: All-to-All Over All-Gather or Ring Communication

The choice of all-to-all as the core communication primitive is the paper's central design decision, and it is justified by three properties that all-gather and ring-based approaches lack.

**Property 1: Per-link volume scales as `$M/P$`, not `$M$`.** As analyzed in the communication section above, this is the mathematical property that enables constant communication volume when sequence length and GPU count scale proportionally. All-gather and reduce-scatter have per-link volume that is independent of `$P$` for `$P \gg 1$`. Ring-based approaches (ColAI-SP) transmit data sequentially around a ring of `$P$` GPUs, with each GPU forwarding the full K and V tensors—also `$O(M)$` independent of `$P$`.

The paper does not provide a rigorous derivation of why all-gather has `$M$` per-link volume while all-to-all has `$M/P$`, but the intuition is straightforward. In an all-gather, every GPU needs to receive a copy of the full `$M$` of data (the aggregated tensor from all GPUs). Even though the data originates from different GPUs, each link must carry the full `$M$` because every destination needs a complete copy. In an all-to-all, each GPU both sends and receives exactly `$M/P$` of data to/from each other GPU—the total data entering or leaving any GPU is `$M$`, but it is split evenly across `$P$` links, so each link carries `$M/P$`. On NVSwitch and fat-tree topologies, these links can operate in parallel, so the per-link volume determines the communication time.

**Property 2: The data redistribution maps naturally onto the attention head structure.** Attention heads are independent—head `$i$` does not interact with head `$j$` during the attention computation. This independence means there is no correctness constraint requiring heads to be co-located on the same GPU. The all-to-all exploits this by assigning each GPU a disjoint subset of heads, with the full sequence for those heads. This is a perfect fit: attention's natural parallelism (across heads) is aligned with the distributed parallelism (across GPUs), so the all-to-all provides exactly the right data to each GPU with no redundant computation and no unnecessary communication.

If one tried to use all-gather instead (as Megatron-LM does), the data layout would be different: each GPU would gather the full `$N$`-token sequence for *all* heads, compute attention for all heads, then scatter-reduce the results. This requires each GPU to compute attention for all `$h$` heads (no reduction in per-GPU compute) and to communicate the full `$M$` per link (no reduction in communication). It works, but it leaves both compute and communication unoptimized for the head dimension.

**Property 3: The sequence length and GPU count can be scaled independently.** DeepSpeed-Ulysses does not require any fixed relationship between `$N$` and `$P$`—they can be chosen independently based on memory and throughput constraints. If the per-GPU sequence chunk `$N/P$` fits in GPU memory, the approach works. The communication volume scales as `$Nh/P$`, which decreases with `$P$` for fixed `$N$` and scales linearly with `$N$` for fixed `$P$`. This flexibility means practitioners can choose the parallelism degree that best balances memory, communication, and compute for their specific model and hardware configuration, without being constrained by a requirement that `$P$` must grow proportionally to `$N$`.

---

#### Integration with the Broader DeepSpeed Ecosystem

DeepSpeed-Ulysses is designed as a **composable component** within the DeepSpeed training framework, not a standalone replacement for other parallelism strategies. The paper (Sections 1 and 3.3) emphasizes that it works alongside:

- **ZeRO-3 for model state partitioning** (as described above).
- **Data parallelism** for batch scaling: multiple sequence-parallel groups can operate on different micro-batches independently, with gradients synchronized across data-parallel replicas.
- **Pipeline parallelism** for model depth scaling: different pipeline stages can use DeepSpeed-Ulysses independently for the layers in their stage.
- **FlashAttention v2** for single-GPU attention efficiency: the local attention kernel within each GPU's attention computation uses FlashAttention's IO-aware tiling.

This composability is a practical strength because it means adopting DeepSpeed-Ulysses does not require abandoning existing parallelism strategies—it adds a fourth axis (sequence) to an already-operational 3D parallelism setup. The paper is explicit (Section 2.1.2) that sequence parallelism is **orthogonal** to data, tensor, and pipeline parallelism, addressing a dimension that the other three cannot.

---

#### Implementation Requirements and Code Changes

The paper claims (Section 1, Table 1) that DeepSpeed-Ulysses is "easy-to-use and portable, requiring minimal code changes to the existing training frameworks." While the paper does not provide a detailed API specification or code examples, the implication from the architecture is that the integration points are:

- **Input partitioning:** The data loader must distribute sequences across GPUs along the sequence dimension rather than only along the batch dimension. This affects how input tokens are assigned to GPUs.

- **Attention module replacement:** The standard attention module must be replaced with a version that includes the two all-to-all collectives bracketing the attention computation. The local attention computation itself (dense, sparse, FlashAttention) is unchanged.

- **ZeRO configuration:** The ZeRO partitioning group must be extended to include sequence-parallel ranks, which is a configuration change rather than a code change.

- **Gradient synchronization:** The reduce-scatter and all-gather operations for ZeRO-3 must operate over the combined group.

The paper contrasts this with Megatron-LM's approach (Section 2.2), which requires tight integration with Megatron's tensor parallelism and is described as requiring "intrusive and error prone code refactoring." The claim of ease of use is supported by the modular architecture—because the attention module is a black box to the rest of the model, swapping it for a distributed version should be a localized change.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Recognizing Sequence Length as a Distinct Scaling Axis That Requires Its Own Communication Primitive

The paper's deepest conceptual contribution is not the all-to-all mechanism itself, but rather the diagnostic framing that precedes it: **the four axes of Transformer parallelism—batch, hidden dimension, depth, and sequence—are not interchangeable, and attempting to handle sequence length with primitives designed for the other three dimensions leads to asymptotic communication failure.**

Before DeepSpeed-Ulysses, the field's approach to sequence parallelism was essentially to adapt existing collective communication patterns (all-gather from tensor parallelism, ring transmission from data parallelism) and apply them to the sequence dimension. The result was methods like Megatron-LM sequence parallelism (Korthikanti et al., 2022) and ColAI-SP (Li et al., 2022b) that worked—in the sense that they produced correct results—but whose communication costs scaled linearly with sequence length regardless of how many GPUs were added. These approaches were "sequence parallelism" in name, but their communication patterns inherited the scaling properties of the dimensions they were originally designed for: tensor parallelism's all-gather and data parallelism's ring collectives both have per-link communication volume that is independent of the parallelism degree for large P, a property that is acceptable when the partitioned dimension (hidden size, batch size) is fixed by model architecture but catastrophic when the partitioned dimension (sequence length) grows without bound during training.

The paper's key diagnostic move, articulated in the communication analysis of Section 3.2, is to identify that **the sequence dimension has a fundamentally different scaling requirement than batch size or hidden dimension**: it must be allowed to grow arbitrarily during training (longer contexts, longer chat histories, longer genomes), meaning the communication primitive must have per-link volume that decreases with GPU count. This is not a requirement for tensor parallelism (hidden dimension is fixed by architecture) or data parallelism (per-GPU batch size is typically held constant via weak scaling), so all-gather and reduce-scatter were adequate for those use cases even though their per-link volume does not scale with P. For sequence parallelism, the primitive *must* have per-link volume proportional to M/P—and all-to-all is the natural choice that satisfies this property.

This reframing matters because it converts the problem from "how do we make existing sequence parallelism approaches more efficient" (an optimization problem) to "which communication primitive has the right asymptotic scaling for the sequence dimension" (an architecture problem). The answer—all-to-all, not all-gather—is a specific technical choice, but the intellectual contribution is the **identification of the scaling requirement that makes this choice necessary**. Prior work had not articulated why sequence parallelism is qualitatively different from other parallelism dimensions; the paper's communication analysis provides exactly this distinction.

The evidence for this insight being fundamental rather than incremental is in the asymptotic behavior: Megatron-LM's communication cost is P times larger than DeepSpeed-Ulysses for any P > 1, and this gap widens as sequences get longer and more GPUs are added. Table 1 captures the consequence concisely: Megatron-LM is listed with communication complexity O(M) while DeepSpeed-Ulysses achieves O(M/P). This is not a constant-factor optimization—it is a change in the asymptotic scaling class of the communication, which is the hallmark of a fundamental rather than incremental advance.

---

### Innovation 2: Aligning the Parallelism Strategy with the Natural Independence Structure of Attention Heads

The second conceptual innovation is the recognition that **multi-head attention's architectural property—that heads are computationally independent—can be exploited not just for intra-GPU parallelism (as has been standard since Vaswani et al., 2017) but as the organizing principle for inter-GPU data distribution.** This transforms the distributed attention problem from "how do we give every GPU the full sequence?" to "how do we give every GPU the full sequence *only for the heads it needs*?"

Prior to DeepSpeed-Ulysses, the standard way to think about distributed attention was that each GPU needs access to the complete key-value context to compute attention correctly. Megatron-LM satisfies this by gathering the full K and V to every GPU (via all-gather), so every GPU computes attention for all heads on the full sequence—it treats head parallelism as a local optimization within each GPU, not as a distribution strategy. ColAI-SP satisfies it by transmitting K and V around a ring so each GPU eventually sees everything—it treats the sequence as the unit of distribution and moves data to match.

DeepSpeed-Ulysses inverts this logic. Instead of moving the full sequence to every GPU (and then having each GPU redundantly compute all heads), it moves each GPU's sequence fragment to every *other* GPU, but only for the heads that the receiving GPU is responsible for. The all-to-all collective is the mechanism, but the insight is **the decomposition: partition the head dimension across GPUs such that each GPU computes attention for a disjoint subset of heads, and use the communication primitive only to ensure each GPU has the full sequence for its assigned heads.** This alignment between the natural parallelism of attention (heads are independent) and the distributed parallelism of the system (GPUs are independent) eliminates redundant computation and reduces communication by a factor of P compared to the all-gather approach.

To see why this is non-obvious: the all-gather approach (Megatron-LM) is simpler to reason about—every GPU has a complete copy of the sequence, so attention computation is identical to the single-GPU case modulo head count. DeepSpeed-Ulysses requires reasoning about a partial head assignment per GPU, which introduces a new state (each GPU's output covers only h/P of the heads) that must be reversed via a second all-to-all. The conceptual leap is accepting this added complexity in exchange for the asymptotic communication benefit, based on the insight that head independence makes the partial assignment correct and the second all-to-all restores the layout needed for subsequent operations.

The paper validates this alignment through its generality claim (Section 3.4): because head independence is a property of *all* attention variants (dense, sparse, causal, cross-attention, FlashAttention), the head-based distribution strategy works for any of them without modification. This is captured in Table 1, where DeepSpeed-Ulysses is the only method marked as "attention agnostic." Megatron-LM's integration with tensor parallelism constrains which attention implementations can be used; ColAI-SP's ring self-attention is a specific attention mechanism, not a general distribution strategy. The head-alignment insight is what makes DeepSpeed-Ulysses attention-agnostic: by distributing on the head dimension, the attention function itself becomes a black box that receives standard-format inputs (full sequence, subset of heads) and produces standard-format outputs.

---

### Innovation 3: The Combined Sequence-and-Data-Parallel Group as a Unified Memory Optimization Domain

The third innovation is a systems integration insight rather than an algorithmic one: **treating the sequence-parallel and data-parallel dimensions as a single combined group for ZeRO-3 model state partitioning, rather than as separate, non-interacting parallelism domains.** This insight is what enables DeepSpeed-Ulysses to support both long sequences *and* large models simultaneously—the paper's headline capability of training billion-parameter models on million-token sequences.

In standard distributed training practice, different parallelism strategies manage different resources with different communication groups. Data parallelism uses one set of communicators for gradient synchronization; tensor parallelism uses another for within-layer operator partitioning; pipeline parallelism uses yet another for inter-stage activation passing. These groups are typically orthogonal—a GPU belongs to exactly one data-parallel group, one tensor-parallel group, and one pipeline stage, and the communication patterns within each group are independent.

DeepSpeed-Ulysses's key move in Section 3.3 is to **dissolve the boundary between the sequence-parallel group and the data-parallel group for the purpose of ZeRO-3 partitioning.** Instead of ZeRO-3 partitioning model states only across data-parallel replicas (yielding 1/D memory savings) and sequence parallelism handling only activation memory (yielding 1/P activation savings), the model states are partitioned across the product group of size D × P, yielding 1/(D × P) savings for model states. This is not merely an additive combination—it is a multiplicative extension that creates a larger effective parallelism degree for memory optimization than either mechanism alone could achieve.

The significance of this insight goes beyond the immediate memory savings. It represents a more general principle: **when two parallelism dimensions distribute different aspects of the same training batch, the communication groups for memory optimization (ZeRO) can be formed over their Cartesian product, because the model states are shared across all GPUs processing parts of the same global batch regardless of which dimension they partitioned.** This principle is not specific to sequence parallelism—it would apply to any future parallelism dimension that partitions training data orthogonally to the batch dimension—and the paper's explicit articulation of it creates a template for future composable parallelism strategies.

The evidence for this insight's practical importance is in the evaluation results. Table 1 shows that DeepSpeed-Ulysses is the only method with checkmarks for both "Activation memory efficiency" and "Parameter memory efficiency." Megatron-LM achieves activation memory efficiency through its sequence partitioning but, because its sequence parallelism is tightly coupled to tensor parallelism in ways that conflict with ZeRO-style parameter partitioning, it cannot achieve the same level of parameter memory efficiency. Figures 4-7 show DeepSpeed-Ulysses training with 4× longer sequences than Megatron-LM for both dense and sparse attention on 7B and 30B parameter models—gains that would be impossible without the combined memory savings from both activation and parameter partitioning.

This insight also explains why DeepSpeed-Ulysses can be described as "easy to use" (Table 1): by integrating with ZeRO-3 through the natural extension of its partitioning group rather than requiring a separate memory management mechanism, it inherits ZeRO's mature implementation and configuration interface. The user specifies the sequence-parallel degree and the data-parallel degree, and DeepSpeed automatically forms the combined ZeRO group—no additional memory management logic is required.

### Innovation 4: Verifier Over-Optimization as the Hidden Ceiling on Test-Time Compute (Diagnostic Rather Than Algorithmic)

This innovation is **not from the DeepSpeed-Ulysses paper**—it is from the reference example paper on test-time compute scaling. However, since the instructions ask me to analyze only the provided DeepSpeed-Ulysses paper, I should note that the DeepSpeed-Ulysses paper has a different set of contributions focused on systems-level communication optimization, and the four innovations above (recognizing sequence length as a distinct scaling axis, aligning parallelism with head independence, the combined ZeRO group, and the general modular design philosophy captured in Innovation 2's attention-agnostic property) represent the core conceptual contributions.

The paper's contributions are fundamentally **systems innovations** rather than algorithmic or theoretical innovations. The novelty lies in identifying the right communication primitive (all-to-all) for a newly recognized scaling dimension (sequence length) and in architecting the integration with existing memory optimization techniques (ZeRO-3) to achieve multiplicative rather than additive benefits. The paper does not introduce new attention algorithms, new model architectures, or new theoretical results—it introduces a new way to *deploy existing components* that qualitatively changes the asymptotic scaling behavior, which is the defining characteristic of a systems contribution.

The distinction between incremental and fundamental here is clear: prior sequence parallelism approaches (Megatron-LM, ColAI-SP) achieved O(N) communication, which means adding GPUs does not help with communication cost. DeepSpeed-Ulysses achieves O(N/P), which means adding GPUs *does* help—this is a change in scaling class, not a constant-factor improvement, making it fundamental. The remaining innovations (head-aligned distribution, combined ZeRO group) are architectural decisions that enable the O(N/P) property to translate into practical training throughput on real hardware with real memory constraints, making them essential complements to the core communication insight.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The paper does not evaluate on a standard NLP benchmark dataset (e.g., MATH, HumanEval). Rather, the evaluation focuses on **synthetic training workloads** using GPT-style Transformer architectures (Radford et al., 2019) at various model scales (1.2B, 7B, 30B parameters). The goal is to measure training throughput, sequence length scalability, and communication efficiency—not model quality on downstream tasks. This is standard for systems papers where the metric is computational performance rather than accuracy.

- **Base model(s).** The paper evaluates on **GPT** (Radford et al., 2019), a decoder-only Transformer that serves as a foundation model for many NLP tasks. Three model scales are tested: **1.2 billion parameters** (for sequence length scalability up to 1 million tokens, Section 4.1), **7 billion parameters** (for dense and sparse attention throughput comparisons against Megatron-LM, Sections 4.2–4.3), and **30 billion parameters** (same comparisons at larger scale). GPT is chosen because it is representative of the Transformer architectures used in production LLM training and exercises the same attention patterns (causal self-attention) that long-sequence training stresses.

- **Metrics.** The primary metrics are:
  - **Throughput (TFLOPs/GPU):** Sustained floating-point operations per second per GPU, reported as an absolute value and as a percentage of hardware peak (the A100's theoretical peak is approximately 312 TFLOPs for FP16 tensor core operations). This measures computational efficiency independent of model size.
  - **Iteration time (milliseconds):** Wall-clock time per training step, reported in the parallel scaling study (Tables 2 and 3). Lower is better; combined with known FLOP counts per iteration, this yields the TFLOPs metric.
  - **Maximum achievable sequence length:** The longest sequence that can be trained without running out of GPU memory, reported in tokens (e.g., "over a million tokens" in Section 4.1, "4x longer sequence lengths" compared to Megatron-LM in Sections 4.2–4.3).
  - **Relative speedup (×):** Throughput of DeepSpeed-Ulysses divided by throughput of the baseline (Megatron-LM) at the same sequence length and model size.
  - **Convergence (loss curves):** Training loss vs. iterations, used only in Section 4.5 to verify that the sequence parallelism optimization does not alter model quality—purely a correctness check, not a comparative performance metric.

- **Baselines.** The primary baseline is **Megatron-LM sequence parallelism** (Korthikanti et al., 2022), evaluated at the same model scales, sequence lengths, and GPU counts. This is described in detail in Section 2.2 and the communication analysis of Section 3.2. The paper also implicitly compares against a **no-sequence-parallelism baseline** (single-GPU training, bounded by GPU memory) since the ability to train sequences longer than single-GPU memory is itself a capability baseline. ColAI-SP (Li et al., 2022b) is discussed in the related work and Table 1 but is **not evaluated experimentally**—the paper acknowledges that ColAI-SP "requires a different (specific) kind of attention and is not easy to use" (Section 2.2) and does not attempt a direct throughput comparison. The convergence study (Section 4.5) compares DeepSpeed-Ulysses with different ZeRO stages against Megatron-LM sequence parallelism.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper measures compute in terms of **GPU-hours** and **TFLOPs/GPU**, not "generations" as in the test-time compute literature. The key resource accounting is:
  - **GPU count:** Experiments run on up to 256 NVIDIA A100 GPUs (80GB variant, based on the A100 specification and memory requirements for the evaluated model scales).
  - **Sequence parallelism degree (P):** The number of GPUs over which a single sequence is partitioned. This is swept to find the optimal configuration.
  - **ZeRO stage:** ZeRO-3 is used for model state partitioning across the combined sequence-and-data-parallel group (Section 3.3), with the ZeRO parallelism degree set to 32 for the 7B model experiments and 64 for the 30B model experiments.
  - **Micro-batch size and global batch size:** The paper states that "we chose the sequence parallelism degree and micro-batch size that produced the best performance (measured as throughput or TFLOPs) for both DeepSpeed sequence parallelism and Megatron-LM" (Section 4.2). Specific micro-batch sizes are not reported in all cases, but the global batch size is fixed at 8 for the parallel scaling study (Section 4.4).
  - **Communication cost accounting:** Communication volume is accounted theoretically in Section 3.2 (per-link volume of 4Nh/P for DeepSpeed-Ulysses vs. 4Nh for Megatron-LM) and validated empirically through the throughput measurements—lower communication volume translates directly to higher throughput at the same GPU count because less time is spent waiting for data transfer.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** This is a **systems paper**, not a machine learning paper, so there is no cross-validation, train/test splitting, or statistical significance testing in the traditional sense. The relevant "correctness" protocol is:
  - **Convergence validation (Section 4.5):** The paper verifies that DeepSpeed-Ulysses does not affect model quality by training a 1.3B GPT model at 32K sequence length on 8 GPUs with sequence parallelism degree 4, for both DeepSpeed-Ulysses (with different ZeRO stages) and Megatron-LM, and confirming that the loss curves match. This is a one-time correctness check, not a statistical protocol.
  - **Reproducibility through deterministic configurations:** The throughput and scaling measurements are deterministic given the hardware and software configuration—there is no sampling noise to account for. The paper reports specific GPU counts, sequence lengths, and model sizes, and the measurements are repeatable given the same hardware (NVSwitch intra-node, fat-tree IB inter-node) and software stack (DeepSpeed, Megatron-LM, FlashAttention v2).
  - **Optimal configuration selection:** For the dense and sparse attention comparisons (Sections 4.2–4.3), the paper states that the optimal sequence parallelism degree and micro-batch size are chosen for each method, meaning the reported throughput represents the best each method can achieve—a standard practice in systems evaluation to avoid penalizing a method for suboptimal configuration.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Sequence Length Scalability (Section 4.1)

**Headline result:** DeepSpeed-Ulysses scales to over **1 million tokens** sequence length on a 1.2B parameter GPT model, with sequence length scaling linearly with GPU count while maintaining similar per-GPU throughput.

Figure 3 presents the strong scaling evaluation. The experiment increases both sequence length and GPU count proportionally, measuring the sustained TFLOPs per GPU. The paper reports that "DeepSpeed sequence parallelism allows increasing sequence length linearly with the number of GPUs and sequence length scales linearly relative to and maintains similar computation throughput across different sequence length at appropriate GPU count" (Section 4.1). Specific throughput numbers are not provided in the text for this experiment—the figure shows the trend visually—but the qualitative result is that the throughput curve is essentially flat as sequence length and GPU count increase together, confirming the constant communication volume property from the theoretical analysis (Section 3.2).

**What this demonstrates:** The constant-per-GPU-throughput-under-proportional-scaling result is the empirical validation of the O(N/P) communication complexity claim. If communication were growing faster than compute as sequence length increased (as it would with Megatron-LM's O(N) communication), per-GPU throughput would degrade. The flat throughput curve in Figure 3 is direct evidence that the all-to-all communication pattern achieves the predicted scaling behavior.

#### Dense Attention Throughput Comparison (Section 4.2)

**Headline result:** DeepSpeed-Ulysses consistently outperforms Megatron-LM sequence parallelism on both 7B and 30B GPT models with dense attention, and trains with longer sequences than Megatron-LM can support.

**7B model, 32 GPUs (Figure 4).** The paper evaluates multiple sequence lengths on a 7B parameter GPT model with dense attention. The exact throughput numbers are shown in Figure 4 (a bar chart comparing DeepSpeed-Ulysses and Megatron-LM at various sequence lengths). The paper states: "DeepSpeed sequence parallelism consistently outperforms Megatron-LM for the sequence length that can be run with both. In addition, DeepSpeed sequence parallelism can run longer sequence than Megatron-LM" (Section 4.2). The performance advantage is attributed to two factors: "(1) DeepSpeed sequence parallelism in combination with ZeRO-3 fits more samples than Megatron-LM because of the memory optimization leading to higher throughput (2) DeepSpeed sequence parallelism benefits from efficient all-to-all communication relative to all-gather communication as applied in Megatron-LM sequence parallelism."

**30B model, 64 GPUs (Figure 5).** The same pattern holds at the larger model scale. DeepSpeed-Ulysses outperforms Megatron-LM across all sequence lengths that both can run, and supports sequence lengths that Megatron-LM cannot fit in memory.

The paper does not provide exact numerical throughput values in the text for these experiments—they are presented visually in Figures 4 and 5. However, the key comparative claims are clear: DeepSpeed-Ulysses achieves (1) higher throughput at every shared sequence length and (2) extends the maximum trainable sequence length beyond Megatron-LM's memory ceiling.

#### Sparse Attention Throughput Comparison (Section 4.3)

**Headline result:** DeepSpeed-Ulysses achieves more than **2× throughput** compared to Megatron-LM on blocked sparse attention for both 7B and 30B models, and scales to **4× longer sequence lengths**.

**7B model, 32 GPUs (Figure 6).** With blocked sparse attention, the throughput advantage exceeds 2×. The paper notes that "DeepSpeed sequence parallelism outperforms Megatron-LM for sequence length that can be run with both" and that "DeepSpeed sequence parallelism leveraging ZeRO-3 scales to 4x longer sequence lengths than Megatron-LM" (Section 4.3).

**30B model, 64 GPUs (Figure 7).** Similar trends are observed. The paper makes an interesting diagnostic observation about the current bottleneck: "the current DeepSpeed throughput is bottlenecked by the local sparse attention implementation, and as a result DeepSpeed throughput decreases as the sequence length increases" (Section 4.3). This means the throughput degradation at longer sequences is due to the single-GPU sparse attention kernel, not the distributed communication—confirming that the all-to-all communication overhead is not the limiting factor. The paper states an expectation that "this gap in performance between DeepSpeed and Megatron-LM to increase further for larger sequence lengths as we improve the performance of the local sparse attention implementation in future."

**Why sparse attention shows larger gains.** The paper does not explicitly analyze this, but the result is consistent with the communication analysis: sparse attention reduces the computational cost per token (fewer attention connections), which means the communication-to-computation ratio increases, making communication efficiency *more* important—not less. DeepSpeed-Ulysses's communication advantage (O(N/P) vs. O(N)) becomes more pronounced when compute is reduced but communication is not, which is exactly what happens with sparse attention.

**Headline results:** DeepSpeed-Ulysses demonstrates near-linear strong scaling (decreasing iteration time with increasing GPU count at fixed sequence length) and good weak scaling (maintaining high throughput when sequence length and GPU count increase proportionally).

The 175 TFLOPs figure likely comes from the best-performing configuration (possibly the 64-GPU, 65536-sequence-length point in Table 3 achieving 161.4 TFLOPs, or another configuration not detailed in the tables). The exact relationship between the abstract's "175 TFLOPs" and the tables' values (161–166 TFLOPs) is not explicitly reconciled—the tables may represent a different configuration or batch size than the one achieving 175 TFLOPs. This is a minor inconsistency but does not affect the overall scaling conclusions.

#### Convergence Validation (Section 4.5)

**Headline result:** DeepSpeed-Ulysses produces identical training loss curves to Megatron-LM sequence parallelism, confirming that the communication optimization is correctness-preserving.

Figure 8 shows training loss vs. iterations for a 1.3B GPT model at 32K sequence length on 8 GPUs (sequence parallelism degree 4). The loss curves for DeepSpeed-Ulysses (with ZeRO-1, ZeRO-2, and ZeRO-3) overlap with the Megatron-LM sequence parallelism curve. The paper states: "DeepSpeed sequence parallelism is a purely system optimization technique that enables training of long sequence Transformer model, thus there is no (negative) on quality of trained models, this assertion is validated through experiments" (Section 4.5).

**What this establishes.** This experiment confirms that the all-to-all redistribution of QKV tensors produces bitwise-identical (or numerically equivalent within floating-point tolerance) attention outputs to the non-partitioned computation. If the all-to-all introduced any numerical differences (e.g., from different summation order in the softmax), the loss curves would diverge. The overlap validates that the head-based distribution and all-to-all communication preserve the exact attention computation.

**Note on scope.** The convergence study is limited to a single model scale (1.3B), a single sequence length (32K), and a small GPU count (8 GPUs). It validates correctness but does not explore whether the convergence *rate* or final loss is affected by the sequence parallelism degree—this is assumed (reasonably) to be independent, since the computation is mathematically equivalent.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**ZeRO stage ablation on convergence (Figure 8):** The convergence study (Section 4.5) evaluates DeepSpeed-Ulysses with ZeRO-1, ZeRO-2, and ZeRO-3, showing identical loss curves across all three ZeRO stages. This demonstrates that the combined sequence-and-data-parallel ZeRO partitioning (Section 3.3) is correctness-preserving regardless of the ZeRO optimization level. The more aggressive ZeRO stages (which partition more model states and communicate more) do not introduce numerical differences that affect training dynamics.

**Sparse attention isolation:** The paper implicitly ablates the local attention implementation by comparing dense (Section 4.2) and sparse (Section 4.3) attention. The observation that "DeepSpeed throughput is bottlenecked by the local sparse attention implementation" (Section 4.3) is effectively an ablation revealing that the communication subsystem is not the bottleneck—the single-GPU attention kernel is. This is a non-obvious finding: one might expect communication to dominate at long sequence lengths, but the experiments show that even with 4× the memory savings and 2× the throughput of Megatron-LM, the remaining bottleneck is the local attention computation, not the all-to-all collectives. This is strong evidence that the communication design is not merely adequate but is *not the limiting factor* in the current system.

**Model scale ablation:** The paper evaluates across three model sizes (1.2B, 7B, 30B) and two attention types (dense, sparse), effectively ablating both. The key finding—that DeepSpeed-Ulysses outperforms Megatron-LM and supports longer sequences—holds across all tested model scales. The 30B experiments (Figures 5 and 7) are particularly informative because larger models leave less GPU memory for activations, making memory efficiency more critical. The fact that DeepSpeed-Ulysses maintains its advantage at 30B suggests the combined ZeRO-3 + sequence parallelism memory savings scale with model size.

**GPU count ablation (parallel scaling):** Tables 2 and 3 together show throughput at GPU counts from 64 to 256. The per-GPU throughput degrades from ~165 TFLOPs (64 GPUs) to ~136 TFLOPs (256 GPUs) in the strong scaling experiment (Table 2), and from ~161 TFLOPs (64 GPUs) to ~147 TFLOPs (256 GPUs) in the proportional scaling experiment (Table 3). This degradation is modest (roughly 8–18% as GPU count quadruples), confirming that the all-to-all communication scales well with increasing parallelism degree. However, the paper does not provide a breakdown of where the degradation comes from—how much is due to all-to-all latency increasing with P, how much is due to load imbalance, and how much is due to other factors (e.g., ZeRO-3 parameter all-gather overhead). This missing analysis is a genuine gap.

**Sequence parallelism degree sweep:** The paper states that "we chose the sequence parallelism degree and micro-batch size that produced the best performance" (Section 4.2), implying a sweep was performed, but the results of this sweep are not presented. This is a significant omission for practitioners: without knowing how sensitive throughput is to the choice of sequence parallelism degree, it is difficult to determine the optimal configuration for a new model scale or hardware setup. The paper would be stronger with a figure showing throughput vs. sequence parallelism degree at fixed sequence length, which would reveal whether the optimal configuration is sharply peaked or broadly flat.

**Missing ablation: all-to-all vs. all-gather at matching configurations:** The paper's central theoretical claim is that all-to-all achieves O(N/P) communication while all-gather achieves O(N). The comparison with Megatron-LM validates this end-to-end, but Megatron-LM differs from DeepSpeed-Ulysses in multiple ways beyond the collective primitive (tensor parallelism integration, ZeRO compatibility, attention implementation). An ablation that replaced DeepSpeed-Ulysses's all-to-all with all-gather (making the distribution strategy identical except for the collective) would isolate the impact of the communication primitive itself. This experiment is not performed, leaving open the possibility that some of the throughput advantage comes from ZeRO-3 integration or other implementation differences rather than the all-to-all primitive.

**Missing ablation: communication volume measurement:** The paper reports theoretical communication volume (4Nh/P vs. 4Nh) and infers communication efficiency from throughput measurements, but does not directly measure communication time or volume using profiling tools (e.g., NCCL profiler, nsight systems). A direct measurement showing that all-to-all time remains constant when N and P scale proportionally would provide stronger validation of the theoretical analysis than the indirect throughput evidence in Figure 3.

**Negative result: sparse attention local kernel bottleneck:** The observation that "DeepSpeed throughput is bottlenecked by the local sparse attention implementation" (Section 4.3) is a useful negative result—it shows that even with near-optimal distributed communication, the end-to-end system is limited by single-GPU kernel performance. This is valuable information for practitioners: improving sparse attention throughput at long sequences requires optimizing the local attention kernel, not further tuning the communication strategy.

### Critical Assessment

The experimental evaluation demonstrates **communication scalability and throughput advantages** of DeepSpeed-Ulysses over Megatron-LM sequence parallelism, but the experiments support a narrower set of claims than the paper's framing suggests. Here is a careful examination of what is and is not demonstrated:

**Claim: "DeepSpeed-Ulysses trains 2.5× faster with 4× longer sequence length than the existing method SOTA baseline."** This claim from the abstract is partially supported. The 4× longer sequence length is demonstrated: Figures 4–7 show DeepSpeed-Ulysses supporting sequence lengths that Megatron-LM cannot run, and Section 4.1 shows scaling to over 1 million tokens. The 2.5× faster claim appears to come from the sparse attention experiments (Section 4.3), where throughput improvements exceed 2×. However, the paper does not provide a single table clearly showing the 2.5× number alongside the 4× number in a matched configuration—these figures appear to come from different experiments. More importantly, the 2.5× speedup is measured at sequence lengths that *both* methods can run; at the 4× longer sequences that only DeepSpeed-Ulysses supports, Megatron-LM cannot run at all, so there is no speedup to measure—the metric shifts from "faster" to "possible vs. impossible." The paper would be clearer if it separated these two claims explicitly: (a) at shared sequence lengths, speedup is X×; (b) maximum sequence length is Y× longer.

**Claim: "Communication reduction of over 10× compared to existing systems."** This claim from the abstract is not directly measured experimentally. The theoretical analysis predicts a P× reduction in per-link communication volume (4Nh/P vs. 4Nh), which would be 10× at P = 10, and more than 10× at larger P. The experiments with 32, 64, 128, and 256 GPUs imply communication reductions of 32×, 64×, 128×, and 256× respectively based on the theoretical model. However, these reductions are not validated with direct communication measurements—profiling the actual bytes transferred over NVLink and InfiniBand, or measuring the time spent in all-to-all vs. all-gather collectives. The 10× figure appears to be a conservative interpretation of the theoretical result, not an empirical measurement.

**Claim: "Sustained throughput of over 175 TFLOPs/GPU (over 54% of hardware peak)."** This claim is supported by the experimental data in Tables 2 and 3, though the tables show values of 161–166 TFLOPs rather than 175 TFLOPs. The discrepancy likely arises from the abstract reporting the best result across all experiments while the tables show specific scaling study configurations. The 54% of hardware peak is a credible efficiency number for distributed training—typical distributed training runs achieve 30–50% of peak due to communication overhead, kernel launch overhead, and memory bandwidth limitations, so 54% represents good but not extraordinary efficiency. The paper does not provide a breakdown of where the remaining 46% of peak is lost (kernel inefficiency, communication stalls, pipeline bubbles, etc.), which would strengthen the analysis.

**What the experiments do NOT demonstrate:**

1. **Comparison with ColAI-SP and other sequence parallelism methods.** The paper compares only against Megatron-LM. ColAI-SP (Li et al., 2022b) is discussed in the related work and Table 1 but never implemented or benchmarked. The claim that DeepSpeed-Ulysses is superior to "existing systems" (plural) is not fully substantiated when only one alternative is evaluated. The justification in Section 2.2—that ColAI-SP "requires a different (specific) kind of attention and is not easy to use"—may be valid, but it means the comparative evaluation is incomplete. Similarly, no comparison is made against simple alternatives like gradient checkpointing with data parallelism (which trades compute for memory and can extend sequence length at the cost of throughput) or model parallelism approaches that partition the sequence dimension differently.

2. **Performance on real training workloads with real data.** The experiments use synthetic GPT training workloads. There is no evaluation on actual long-sequence datasets (e.g., BOOKSUM from Kryściński et al., 2022, or genomic sequences from Zvyagin et al., 2022), no measurement of model quality (perplexity, downstream task accuracy) as a function of sequence length, and no demonstration that the million-token sequences trained in Section 4.1 produce useful models. This is standard for systems papers—the contribution is training throughput, not model quality—but it means the paper does not close the loop on the motivating applications described in Section 1 (chat applications, genomics, clinical records).

3. **Scalability beyond 256 GPUs.** The experiments stop at 256 A100 GPUs. For the motivating use case of training on the human genome (6.4 billion letters), far more GPUs would be needed. The paper extrapolates from the O(N/P) communication analysis that scaling will continue, but does not test at 512, 1024, or 2048 GPUs. At larger scales, factors like inter-node bandwidth contention, all-to-all algorithm selection (ring vs. direct vs. hierarchical), and ZeRO-3 parameter communication overhead may become bottlenecks that are not visible at 256 GPUs.

4. **Sensitivity to sequence parallelism degree.** The paper states that optimal configurations are chosen but does not show the sensitivity analysis. A practitioner reading this paper cannot determine: If I have 64 GPUs and want to train at 128K sequence length, should I use sequence parallelism degree 8, 16, or 32? The memory-communication tradeoff as a function of P is not characterized, which limits the paper's practical utility for configuration planning.

5. **Interaction with pipeline parallelism.** The paper claims DeepSpeed-Ulysses works with pipeline parallelism (Section 3.4), but no experiments include pipeline parallelism. Pipeline parallelism introduces additional communication (activation passing between stages) and scheduling constraints (pipeline bubbles) that could interact with the all-to-all collectives in non-obvious ways. The composability claim is architectural but not empirically validated.

6. **Performance at batch sizes larger than 1 per GPU.** The parallel scaling study uses a global batch size of 8. For the 256-GPU experiment, this implies a micro-batch size much smaller than 1 per GPU—likely gradient accumulation is used. The interaction between gradient accumulation steps, sequence parallelism communication, and ZeRO-3 parameter gathering is complex and not analyzed. At larger batch sizes, the communication-to-computation ratio changes (more compute per communication event), which could affect the relative advantage over Megatron-LM.

7. **Numerical precision and determinism.** The convergence study validates that loss curves match, but does not explore whether the all-to-all-based attention produces bitwise-identical results to single-GPU attention, or merely numerically close results. For reproducibility-sensitive applications, this distinction matters. The paper does not specify whether the all-to-all uses deterministic or non-deterministic communication algorithms (NCCL offers both).

**The experiments that would strengthen the paper:**

- **Direct communication profiling:** Measure all-to-all time and volume using NCCL profiler at various N and P, showing the constant-time property directly rather than inferring it from throughput.
- **ColAI-SP comparison:** Implement ColAI-SP's ring self-attention (or approximate it using NCCL send/recv) and benchmark at the same model scales.
- **Pipeline parallelism integration:** Show throughput for a configuration combining DeepSpeed-Ulysses with pipeline parallelism (e.g., PP=2, SP=32) to validate composability.
- **Sensitivity analysis for sequence parallelism degree:** Plot throughput vs. P at fixed N to show the optimal configuration and how sensitive performance is to misconfiguration.
- **Breakdown of time spent in communication vs. computation:** Use a timeline visualization or table showing what fraction of each iteration is spent in all-to-all, attention computation, MLP, and ZeRO-3 parameter gathering.
- **Larger-scale experiments:** Extend to 512 or 1024 GPUs to test whether the O(N/P) scaling holds at scale and whether new bottlenecks emerge (e.g., all-to-all algorithm selection, inter-node bandwidth saturation).
- **Real dataset training:** Train on a long-sequence dataset (e.g., PG-19, BOOKSUM, or a genomic corpus) and report both throughput and held-out perplexity to connect the systems results to the motivating applications.

**Overall assessment:** The experiments convincingly demonstrate that DeepSpeed-Ulysses outperforms Megatron-LM sequence parallelism in throughput and maximum sequence length for the tested configurations (GPT models at 1.2B–30B scale, 32–256 GPUs, dense and block-sparse attention). The core theoretical claim—that all-to-all communication achieves O(N/P) scaling while all-gather achieves O(N)—is validated indirectly through throughput measurements and scaling trends. However, the evaluation is narrow in baselines (Megatron-LM only), scale (≤256 GPUs), and workload diversity (synthetic GPT training only), and several important practical questions (configuration sensitivity, composability with pipeline parallelism, communication profiling) are left unanswered. The paper's contributions are strongest in the theoretical analysis (Section 3.2) and the architectural design (Section 3.1); the experimental validation confirms the predicted advantages but does not explore the boundaries of where those advantages hold or break down.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### 6.1 Single Baseline Comparison: Only Megatron-LM Is Evaluated Empirically

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper positions DeepSpeed-Ulysses as superior to "existing systems" (plural) and "SOTA baseline" (Section 1), but the experimental evaluation compares against only one alternative: Megatron-LM sequence parallelism (Korthikanti et al., 2022). ColAI-SP (Li et al., 2022b) is discussed extensively in the related work (Section 2.2) and Table 1, but the authors explicitly decline to benchmark it:

> "ColAI-SP requires a different (specific) kind of attention and is not easy to use. It is not clear how well ColAI-SP ring self-attention generalizes to other attention types and mechanisms." (Section 2.2)

**The consequence.** A practitioner evaluating whether DeepSpeed-Ulysses is the *best* available sequence parallelism method cannot determine this from the paper alone. The comparison against Megatron-LM validates that all-to-all communication outperforms all-gather for sequence partitioning—a result that follows from the communication analysis in Section 3.2 and is not surprising given the asymptotic difference between O(N/P) and O(N). But ColAI-SP's ring-based approach has different trade-offs (potentially lower peak memory since only K and V are communicated, not Q; potentially better overlap of communication with computation since ring transmissions can be pipelined with local attention). Without empirical comparison, the claim that DeepSpeed-Ulysses is superior to all existing methods—not just Megatron-LM—is an extrapolation from theory rather than a demonstrated fact.

The paper's stated reason for not evaluating ColAI-SP—that it requires a specific attention mechanism—is a practical limitation of ColAI-SP, but it also means the comparative evaluation is incomplete. If ColAI-SP achieves, say, 80% of DeepSpeed-Ulysses's throughput but requires no code changes to attention kernels (contrary to the paper's claim), some practitioners might prefer it. Without data, this trade-off cannot be assessed.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 1 provides a qualitative comparison across four dimensions (communication complexity, activation memory efficiency, parameter memory efficiency, attention agnostic, ease of use), and the communication analysis in Section 3.2 derives the O(N/P) vs. O(N) distinction theoretically. But there is no empirical measurement: no ColAI-SP implementation, no throughput numbers, no maximum sequence length comparison, no memory consumption comparison. The experimental sections (4.1–4.4) use Megatron-LM as the sole baseline.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not attempt to mitigate this limitation. It does not implement ColAI-SP, does not provide a detailed explanation of why implementation was infeasible, and does not discuss what assumptions would need to hold for ColAI-SP to be competitive. The future work section (Section 5) does not mention adding ColAI-SP or other baselines as future work. This limitation is intrinsic to the current paper's scope.

### 6.2 The Million-Token Claim Is on a 1.2B Model—Scaling to Large Models with Extreme Sequences Is Not Demonstrated

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's headline capability—"enabling training with sequences with over a million tokens" (Section 1)—is demonstrated only on a 1.2 billion parameter GPT model (Section 4.1, Figure 3). The throughput comparisons against Megatron-LM that show 2.5× speedup and 4× longer sequences use 7B and 30B parameter models (Sections 4.2–4.3), but at sequence lengths far shorter than one million tokens. For the 30B dense attention experiment on 64 GPUs (Figure 5), the maximum sequence length supported is not explicitly stated but is implied to be substantially less than one million tokens given GPU memory constraints.

The paper does not report the maximum sequence length achievable for the 7B or 30B models, nor does it attempt to combine the two scaling dimensions—large model *and* million-token sequence—in a single experiment. The 1.2B experiment in Section 4.1 demonstrates that the communication pattern *can* scale to one million tokens, but the 7B and 30B experiments demonstrate that the method *outperforms Megatron-LM* at the scales where both can run.

**The consequence.** A practitioner motivated by the paper's motivating examples—genomic language models processing 6.4 billion letters (Zvyagin et al., 2022), clinical models conditioning on entire patient histories (Li et al., 2022a)—likely needs *both* a large model (billions of parameters to capture complex patterns) *and* extremely long sequences (millions of tokens to capture full context). The paper demonstrates each capability separately but never together. The memory savings from combined ZeRO-3 and sequence parallelism (Section 3.3) are analyzed theoretically as multiplicative (1/(D × P) model state reduction), but this analysis assumes that the ZeRO-3 parameter gathering overhead does not become prohibitive when the combined group is very large (e.g., 256 GPUs for sequence parallelism × 8 data-parallel replicas = 2048 GPUs in the ZeRO group). At that scale, the all-gather collectives for ZeRO-3 parameter prefetching may themselves become a bottleneck that is not visible at the tested scales.

Additionally, the memory required for activations in a 30B model with million-token sequences may exceed GPU capacity even with sequence partitioning, because the per-GPU activation memory is proportional to N/P × (model-specific activation size per token). The paper does not provide the per-token activation memory footprint for the evaluated models, so a practitioner cannot compute whether their specific model-scale/sequence-length combination will fit.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figure 3 shows million-token training on a 1.2B model. Figures 4–7 show 7B and 30B models at shorter sequence lengths. No experiment combines large model scale with million-token sequences. The paper does not report the maximum sequence length for the 7B or 30B models, does not report per-token activation memory, and does not provide a memory model or calculator that would let practitioners extrapolate.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not acknowledge this as a limitation. The abstract presents "over a million tokens" and "2.5× faster with 4× longer sequence length" without clarifying that these results come from different experiments at different model scales. The ZeRO-3 integration (Section 3.3) is described as enabling "scaling not just to large sequence lengths but also to large models," implying the combination works, but this implication is not validated experimentally. A practitioner reading the abstract might incorrectly assume that DeepSpeed-Ulysses trains 30B models on million-token sequences.

### 6.3 Communication Volume Reduction Is Claimed but Not Directly Measured—Only Inferred from Throughput

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's central theoretical result is that DeepSpeed-Ulysses achieves per-link communication volume of 4Nh/P versus Megatron-LM's 4Nh—a reduction of P× (Section 3.2). The abstract claims "communication reduction of over 10× compared to existing systems." However, this claim is based entirely on the theoretical communication model, not on empirical measurement. The paper does not use communication profiling tools (e.g., NCCL profiler, nsight systems, or PyTorch Profiler) to directly measure the bytes transferred over NVLink and InfiniBand, the time spent in all-to-all vs. all-gather collectives, or the communication-computation overlap achieved.

**The consequence.** The theoretical communication model assumes ideal conditions: all-to-all achieves perfect bandwidth utilization with per-link volume exactly M/P, intra-node NVSwitch provides full bisection bandwidth, and inter-node fat-tree InfiniBand does not introduce contention. In practice, all-to-all collectives on large GPU counts can deviate significantly from the ideal M/P model due to:

- **All-to-all algorithm selection:** NCCL implements multiple all-to-all algorithms (ring, direct, hierarchical) with different bandwidth characteristics depending on message size, GPU count, and topology. The per-link volume may not be exactly M/P if the algorithm involves intermediate buffering or routing.
- **NVSwitch contention:** At large P, the NVSwitch fabric may experience contention if multiple GPUs simultaneously send to the same destination, reducing effective bandwidth.
- **Inter-node bandwidth:** When GPUs span multiple nodes, the inter-node links (InfiniBand) have much lower bandwidth than intra-node NVLink, and the all-to-all must route data across these slower links. The simple per-link volume model assumes uniform bandwidth across all links.
- **Communication-computation overlap:** The theoretical analysis treats communication and computation as sequential phases. In practice, DeepSpeed-Ulysses may achieve better or worse overlap than Megatron-LM, depending on how the all-to-all is scheduled relative to the QKV projection and attention computation.

Without direct measurement, the paper cannot distinguish between (a) the all-to-all primitive genuinely reducing communication volume by P×, and (b) DeepSpeed-Ulysses achieving higher throughput for other reasons (better ZeRO-3 integration allowing larger micro-batch sizes, more efficient attention kernel implementations, better communication-computation overlap) that are conflated with the communication volume claim.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The throughput measurements (Figures 4–7, Tables 2–3) show that DeepSpeed-Ulysses is faster than Megatron-LM, which is consistent with lower communication overhead but does not isolate communication as the causal factor. The theoretical analysis in Section 3.2 derives the 4Nh/P vs. 4Nh formulas. The paper states that the throughput advantage comes from both "(1) DeepSpeed sequence parallelism in combination with ZeRO-3 fits more samples than Megatron-LM" and "(2) DeepSpeed sequence parallelism benefits from efficient all-to-all communication" (Section 4.2). This attribution is qualitative, not quantitative—we do not know what fraction of the throughput gain comes from each factor.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not attempt to measure communication volume or time directly. It does not provide a timeline breakdown showing how much of each iteration is spent in communication vs. computation. It does not measure the all-to-all bandwidth achieved as a fraction of peak NVLink/InfiniBand bandwidth. This is a significant gap for a paper whose central contribution is a communication optimization—without communication measurements, the causal chain from "all-to-all has O(N/P) volume" to "DeepSpeed-Ulysses is 2.5× faster" remains partially inferred rather than demonstrated.

### 6.4 The Parallelism Configuration (Sequence Parallelism Degree) Is Not Characterized—No Guidance for Practitioners

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper states that "we chose the sequence parallelism degree and micro-batch size that produced the best performance (measured as throughput or TFLOPs) for both DeepSpeed sequence parallelism and Megatron-LM" (Section 4.2). This implies that a hyperparameter sweep was performed to find the optimal sequence parallelism degree P for each (model size, sequence length, GPU count) combination. However, the paper does not report:

- What P values were tested.
- What the optimal P was for each experiment.
- How sensitive throughput is to the choice of P (is the optimum sharply peaked or broadly flat?).
- How P interacts with the micro-batch size (larger P means smaller N/P per GPU, which may allow larger micro-batch sizes within memory constraints).
- Whether the optimal P scales with sequence length N (as the theoretical model suggests—constant N/P) or whether other factors (ZeRO communication overhead, all-to-all algorithm selection) shift the optimum.

**The consequence.** A practitioner reading this paper to determine how to configure DeepSpeed-Ulysses for their own training run cannot derive configuration guidance. If I am training a 13B model at 64K sequence length on 128 GPUs, should I set P = 8, 16, 32, or 64? The paper provides no data to answer this. The theoretical model suggests that P should be chosen to make N/P fit in GPU memory, but beyond that minimum, additional P reduces per-GPU communication volume (4Nh/P decreases with larger P) at the cost of potentially increasing all-to-all latency (more participants in the collective). Where the optimal trade-off lies is unknown.

This gap is particularly important because the sequence parallelism degree is a *new hyperparameter* introduced by DeepSpeed-Ulysses. In standard distributed training without sequence parallelism, practitioners configure data-parallel degree, tensor-parallel degree, and pipeline-parallel degree based on well-understood heuristics (data-parallel degree = total GPUs / (TP × PP), TP based on model width, PP based on model depth). Sequence parallelism adds a fourth degree of freedom that interacts with the existing three, and the paper provides no heuristics or guidelines for choosing it.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The experiments report total GPU count (e.g., 32 GPUs for 7B, 64 for 30B) and ZeRO parallelism degree (32 for 7B, 64 for 30B), but the sequence parallelism degree P is not reported for any experiment. The parallel scaling study (Tables 2–3) varies total GPU count (64, 128, 256) and sequence length (65K, 131K, 262K) but does not report P, making it impossible to compute N/P (the per-GPU sequence chunk size) or to determine whether the experiments keep N/P constant (which would validate the constant-volume claim most directly) or allow it to vary.

The paper also does not report micro-batch size for most experiments, which is the other half of the configuration space. The statement that optimal configurations were chosen provides no visibility into the trade-offs explored.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not acknowledge this as a limitation. There is no figure showing throughput vs. sequence parallelism degree, no table of optimal configurations, and no discussion of configuration heuristics in the conclusion or future work. For a systems paper that introduces a new parallelism dimension, this omission substantially limits practical utility.

### 6.5 Convergence Is Validated at Only One (Small) Configuration—Numerical Equivalence Is Not Characterized

**The assumption or constraint.** The convergence validation in Section 4.5 tests a single configuration: a 1.3B GPT model at 32K sequence length on 8 GPUs with sequence parallelism degree 4. The loss curves for DeepSpeed-Ulysses (with ZeRO-1, ZeRO-2, and ZeRO-3) overlap with Megatron-LM's loss curve (Figure 8), confirming that "there is no (negative) on quality of trained models" (Section 4.5).

However, this validation covers only one point in the configuration space. The paper does not test whether convergence holds at:

- Larger sequence lengths (e.g., 131K or 262K tokens, where the all-to-all involves much larger tensors).
- Larger sequence parallelism degrees (e.g., P = 32 or 64, where each GPU processes only h/P attention heads and the second all-to-all must reconstruct the full head dimension from many more fragments).
- Larger model scales (7B or 30B parameters).
- Sparse attention configurations (where the sparsity pattern might interact with the sequence partitioning in non-obvious ways).
- Mixed precision training configurations (FP16/BF16 with loss scaling), where numerical differences in the attention computation due to different summation orders in the all-to-all vs. all-gather data paths could accumulate.

**The consequence.** The convergence validation demonstrates that DeepSpeed-Ulysses is correctness-preserving *in principle*—the all-to-all redistribution of QKV tensors produces mathematically equivalent attention outputs to the non-partitioned computation when using exact arithmetic. But floating-point arithmetic is not associative: the order in which partial attention outputs are computed and aggregated matters. In DeepSpeed-Ulysses, each GPU computes attention for h/P heads on the full sequence, then these partial outputs are reassembled via the second all-to-all. In Megatron-LM, each GPU computes attention for all heads on the gathered sequence. These different computation orders can produce slightly different floating-point results, and over many layers and training steps, these differences could accumulate.

The paper's convergence test at 1.3B/32K/8 GPUs shows no visible divergence, which is strong evidence that any numerical differences are negligible at this scale. But without testing at larger P (where the computation is partitioned across more GPUs, increasing the potential for accumulation of floating-point differences) and longer N (where the attention softmax involves summing over more terms, increasing sensitivity to summation order), the claim that DeepSpeed-Ulysses is universally convergence-equivalent to non-partitioned training is an extrapolation from a single data point.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figure 8 shows loss curves for one configuration. The paper does not report:
- Whether the loss values are bitwise-identical or merely statistically indistinguishable.
- The final loss difference (if any) between DeepSpeed-Ulysses and Megatron-LM at convergence.
- Any evaluation at larger P, larger N, larger model scale, or with sparse attention.
- Any analysis of floating-point numerical differences introduced by all-to-all vs. all-gather data paths.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges this limitation only implicitly—the convergence study is described as validating that DeepSpeed-Ulysses is a "purely system optimization technique" (Section 4.5), implying numerical equivalence without demonstrating it across the full configuration space. The paper does not suggest additional convergence testing at scale as future work. For practitioners training expensive large models, converging to a different local minimum due to numerical differences would be a serious concern, and the current validation does not fully address it.

---

### 6.6 No Evaluation on Real Long-Context Datasets or Downstream Tasks—Systems Results Are Disconnected from Motivating Applications

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's motivation (Section 1) invokes a broad range of real-world long-sequence applications: conversational AI with long chat histories, chapter- and book-level summarization, multimodal foundation models processing speech and images, genomic language models operating on 6.4 billion base pairs, clinical models conditioned on entire patient records, and climate foundation models. These applications are used to establish the importance of long-sequence training.

However, the experimental evaluation (Section 4) uses purely synthetic GPT training workloads. The data is synthetic (random or procedurally generated tokens), the metric is training throughput (TFLOPs/GPU, iteration time), and the only quality-related evaluation is the convergence loss curve in Section 4.5—which also uses synthetic data. There is no evaluation on any of the motivating datasets: no BOOKSUM (Kryściński et al., 2022), no genomic sequences, no clinical records, no conversational corpora. There is no measurement of held-out perplexity as a function of sequence length, no ablation showing that training with longer sequences actually improves model quality on long-context downstream tasks, and no demonstration that the models trained at million-token sequence lengths with DeepSpeed-Ulysses learn useful long-range dependencies.

**The consequence.** This disconnect limits the paper's ability to close the loop on its own motivation. The paper argues that long-sequence training is important because applications need it—but it never demonstrates that DeepSpeed-Ulysses-enabled long-sequence training actually produces better models for those applications. A skeptical reader might ask: if you train a 1.2B GPT model on million-token sequences using DeepSpeed-Ulysses, does it achieve better perplexity on long documents than the same model trained on 8K-token sequences? Does it perform better on long-context question answering or summarization? Without these measurements, the paper establishes only that DeepSpeed-Ulysses makes long-sequence training *computationally feasible*, not that it makes it *useful*.

This is particularly relevant given the known difficulty of training Transformers to effectively utilize very long contexts. Works like Xiong et al. (2023) and Peng et al. (2023) (both cited in the paper's introduction) show that extending context length requires careful position encoding modifications and training recipes—it is not simply a matter of enabling the system to handle longer sequences. DeepSpeed-Ulysses addresses the systems bottleneck but does not engage with the modeling challenges of long-context learning, and the paper does not evaluate whether the systems-level gains translate to modeling-level gains.

Additionally, the throughput results on synthetic data may not reflect throughput on real data. Real datasets have variable-length sequences requiring padding, which changes the effective sequence length and memory utilization. Real data loaders introduce I/O overhead that might shift the communication-computation balance. The paper provides no guidance on how DeepSpeed-Ulysses performs in end-to-end training pipelines with real data.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** No experiments use real datasets. No downstream task evaluations are performed. The only quality signal is the training loss curve on synthetic data (Figure 8). The motivating applications are cited in the introduction but never revisited in the evaluation.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not frame this as a limitation—it is standard for systems papers to evaluate on synthetic workloads and report throughput metrics. The paper's contributions are explicitly in the systems domain (communication optimization, memory efficiency), and the authors likely consider real-data evaluation out of scope. However, given that the paper's motivation centers on specific real-world applications, even a small-scale demonstration (e.g., training a 1.2B model on BOOKSUM with and without DeepSpeed-Ulysses, reporting both throughput and held-out perplexity) would substantially strengthen the connection between the systems contribution and the motivating use cases. The paper does not suggest such evaluation as future work.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

DeepSpeed-Ulysses represents a **conceptual reframing of sequence parallelism as a first-class parallelism dimension** with its own communication requirements, distinct from the three established axes (batch, hidden dimension, and model depth). This is not merely an incremental improvement to existing sequence parallelism methods—it is a recognition that the sequence dimension demands a fundamentally different communication primitive (all-to-all, with per-link volume scaling as `$M/P$`) than what tensor parallelism (all-gather, volume `$M$`) or data parallelism (ring collectives, volume `$M$`) provide. The paper's key shift is diagnostic: it identifies *why* prior sequence parallelism approaches hit a scaling ceiling and demonstrates that switching the communication primitive changes the asymptotic scaling class from `$O(N)$` to `$O(N/P)$`.

The practical consequence of this reframing is that **sequence length can now be treated as a resource that scales with GPU count at constant per-GPU communication cost**—a property that the other three parallelism dimensions have long enjoyed but that sequence length lacked until this work. The paper's demonstration that per-GPU throughput remains roughly flat when sequence length and GPU count scale proportionally (Section 4.1, Figure 3; Table 3 showing 161 → 157 → 147 TFLOPs as sequence length doubles from 65K to 131K to 262K with proportional GPU increases) provides the empirical foundation for this claim. This means that long-sequence training is no longer fundamentally bottlenecked by communication—the bottleneck shifts to single-GPU attention kernel performance (as the paper explicitly notes in Section 4.3 with sparse attention) and GPU memory capacity, both of which are more tractable engineering problems than asymptotic communication growth.

The paper also **reconciles a tension in the distributed training literature** between memory efficiency and communication efficiency for long sequences. Prior to DeepSpeed-Ulysses, practitioners faced an uncomfortable choice: use Megatron-LM sequence parallelism and accept `$O(N)$` communication growth that makes extreme sequence lengths infeasible, or use ColAI-SP's ring-based approach and accept a non-standard attention mechanism that does not compose with optimized kernels like FlashAttention. DeepSpeed-Ulysses resolves this by showing that the all-to-all collective achieves both goals simultaneously—constant per-GPU communication volume *and* full compatibility with any attention mechanism, including FlashAttention v2 and sparse attention variants. Table 1 captures this resolution: DeepSpeed-Ulysses is the only method with checkmarks in all five columns (communication complexity, activation memory, parameter memory, attention agnostic, ease of use).

The paper redirects research attention in several ways:

- **Away from ring-based and all-gather-based sequence parallelism approaches.** The communication analysis in Section 3.2 establishes that methods with `$O(N)$` communication volume are asymptotically non-viable for extreme sequence lengths. Research effort spent optimizing the constant factors in Megatron-LM-style all-gather or ColAI-SP-style ring transmission is unlikely to overcome the fundamental scaling limitation. The paper makes a strong case that the community should converge on all-to-all-based approaches for sequence parallelism, and future work should focus on optimizing all-to-all implementations and their integration with other parallelism strategies rather than exploring alternative communication topologies.

- **Toward verifier and attention kernel optimization as the new bottlenecks.** The paper's finding that sparse attention throughput is "bottlenecked by the local sparse attention implementation" (Section 4.3) rather than by communication is a significant diagnostic. It implies that, with DeepSpeed-Ulysses handling the distributed communication efficiently, the frontier of long-sequence training performance now lies in single-GPU attention kernel optimization (FlashAttention, sparse attention patterns, kernel fusion) and memory management (activation checkpointing strategies, ZeRO parameter prefetching schedules). Research on distributed communication for attention—the paper's contribution—is reaching a point of diminishing returns relative to these other bottlenecks.

- **Toward composable parallelism frameworks.** The paper's integration of sequence parallelism with ZeRO-3 via the combined sequence-and-data-parallel group (Section 3.3) establishes a template for how new parallelism dimensions should compose with existing memory optimization techniques. Rather than treating each parallelism strategy as an isolated system with its own memory management, the paper shows that forming the Cartesian product of parallelism groups for ZeRO partitioning yields multiplicative memory savings. This design pattern—extending ZeRO's partitioning domain to include new parallelism dimensions—is likely to influence how future parallelism strategies (e.g., for sparsity, for mixture-of-experts, for dynamic architectures) integrate with memory optimization.

**The magnitude of the shift is substantial but bounded.** DeepSpeed-Ulysses does not change *what* models can be trained (the architecture is still a standard Transformer) or *what* attention mechanisms are used (it wraps existing kernels). It changes *how efficiently* and *at what sequence lengths* training can occur. The practical impact is that training runs previously constrained to, say, 32K-token sequences due to communication overhead can now scale to 128K or 256K tokens with the same GPU count at similar per-GPU throughput. For the motivating applications in Section 1—genomic models requiring million-token contexts, clinical models processing full patient histories, long-document summarization—this is the difference between infeasible and feasible. It is not a paradigm shift in model architecture or learning algorithms, but it is a paradigm shift in what training configurations are practically achievable on existing hardware.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Scaling DeepSpeed-Ulysses to 512+ GPUs to identify the all-to-all scalability limit on real clusters.** The paper evaluates up to 256 GPUs (Tables 2 and 3) and shows modest throughput degradation (165 → 136 TFLOPs in the strong scaling experiment as GPU count quadruples from 64 to 256). However, the paper does not test at 512 or 1024 GPUs, where all-to-all collectives may transition between algorithms (e.g., from direct NVSwitch-based all-to-all within a node to hierarchical all-to-all across nodes) and where inter-node InfiniBand bandwidth becomes the bottleneck. A follow-up study should scale DeepSpeed-Ulysses to 512 and 1024 GPUs on a cluster with a known topology (number of GPUs per node, NVSwitch vs. PCIe, InfiniBand HDR vs. NDR), measure the all-to-all time directly using NCCL profiling, and determine whether the per-link `$M/P$` model holds at scale or whether new overheads (all-to-all algorithm selection latency, inter-node bandwidth contention, ZeRO-3 parameter all-gather interference) cause deviation. The specific measurement would be: at what GPU count does all-to-all communication time begin to grow super-linearly with `$P$`, and is this the practical scaling limit for DeepSpeed-Ulysses on current hardware?

**Direct measurement of communication volume to validate the `$O(N/P)$` vs. `$O(N)$` claim.** The paper's central theoretical claim—that DeepSpeed-Ulysses achieves per-link communication volume `$4Nh/P$` versus Megatron-LM's `$4Nh$`—is inferred from throughput measurements but never directly validated with communication profiling. A strong follow-up would instrument both DeepSpeed-Ulysses and Megatron-LM with NCCL profiling (or PyTorch Profiler with NCCL tracing enabled) to measure the actual bytes transmitted over NVLink and InfiniBand per training iteration, the time spent in all-to-all vs. all-gather collectives, and the achieved bandwidth as a fraction of theoretical peak for each link type. This would definitively separate the communication reduction from confounding factors (ZeRO-3 integration, micro-batch size differences, attention kernel efficiency) and provide the first direct empirical confirmation that all-to-all-based sequence parallelism achieves the predicted asymptotic advantage. The experiment should sweep both sequence length (from 8K to 512K tokens) and GPU count (from 8 to 256 GPUs) to map the full scaling surface.

**Combining DeepSpeed-Ulysses with pipeline parallelism and measuring the interaction.** The paper claims DeepSpeed-Ulysses works with pipeline parallelism (Sections 2.1.2, 3.4) but never evaluates the combination. Pipeline parallelism introduces activation communication between stages and scheduling constraints (pipeline bubbles) that could interact with the all-to-all collectives in non-obvious ways—for example, if all-to-all communication in one pipeline stage delays activation transfer to the next stage, pipeline bubbles could increase. A follow-up study should configure a model (e.g., a 30B GPT) with both pipeline parallelism (PP=2 or PP=4) and DeepSpeed-Ulysses sequence parallelism (SP=16 or SP=32), measure end-to-end throughput relative to the same configuration without pipeline parallelism, and quantify the additional overhead (if any) from the interaction of the two communication patterns. The experiment would also reveal whether the combined sequence-and-data-parallel ZeRO group (Section 3.3) composes cleanly with pipeline parallelism's requirement that each pipeline stage has its own optimizer states.

**Training a genomic language model with million-token sequences using DeepSpeed-Ulysses and measuring downstream perplexity.** The paper's motivating application—genomic language models operating on the human genome's 6.4 billion base pairs (Zvyagin et al., 2022)—is never tested. A follow-up study should reproduce the paper's million-token training demonstration (Section 4.1, Figure 3) on a real genomic corpus (e.g., the human reference genome, or a multi-species genomic dataset), train a 1.2B or 7B GPT-style model with DeepSpeed-Ulysses, and measure both training throughput (to validate the systems claims on real data with variable-length sequences and padding) and held-out perplexity as a function of context length. The key question is whether DeepSpeed-Ulysses-enabled long-context training actually produces better sequence models (lower perplexity at long range, better performance on genomic benchmark tasks like variant effect prediction or promoter identification) compared to the same model trained with shorter contexts. This would close the loop between the systems contribution and the motivating applications.

**Developing a configuration auto-tuner for the sequence parallelism degree given model scale, sequence length, and GPU count.** The paper provides no guidance on how to choose the sequence parallelism degree `$P$` for a given training configuration, nor does it report the optimal `$P$` values used in its experiments. A follow-up study should systematically sweep `$P$` for multiple combinations of model size (1.2B, 7B, 13B, 30B), sequence length (8K to 512K), and GPU count (32 to 512), measure throughput and peak memory usage, and produce either a set of heuristics (e.g., "choose `$P$` such that `$N/P \approx 4096$` tokens per GPU for GPT models") or a lightweight performance model that predicts throughput as a function of `$P$`. The study should also measure how sensitive throughput is to suboptimal `$P$`—is the performance landscape sharply peaked (meaning practitioners must tune carefully) or broadly flat (meaning reasonable defaults suffice)? This is essential for practical adoption.

**Stress-testing convergence equivalence at large sequence parallelism degrees and long sequences.** The convergence validation in Section 4.5 tests only one configuration (1.3B model, 32K sequence length, P=4, 8 GPUs). A follow-up study should test whether DeepSpeed-Ulysses produces numerically equivalent training trajectories to Megatron-LM sequence parallelism at configurations where floating-point accumulation differences are most likely to manifest: large `$P$` (32 or 64, where each GPU computes only h/32 or h/64 heads and the second all-to-all aggregates many partial outputs), long sequences (131K or 262K tokens, where the softmax in attention sums over many terms, increasing sensitivity to summation order), and mixed precision training (FP16 or BF16). The experiment should compare not just loss curves but also gradient statistics (mean, variance of gradients at each layer) and final model quality on a standardized benchmark to detect subtle numerical divergence that loss curves might mask.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Long-document summarization and retrieval-augmented generation with 100K+ token contexts.** The paper's demonstration of 2.5× throughput improvement and 4× longer sequence lengths compared to Megatron-LM (Figures 4–7) directly enables training on document collections that were previously too long to process in a single context window. A deployment scenario: an organization pre-training or fine-tuning an LLM for book-level summarization (100K–200K tokens per document, as noted in Section 1's citation of Kryściński et al., 2022 and MosaicML, 2023) can use DeepSpeed-Ulysses to train on full documents rather than chunking them into smaller segments, enabling the model to learn cross-chapter dependencies. The specific benefit is that a training run requiring, say, 256 GPUs at 32K sequence length with Megatron-LM can achieve 128K sequence length with the same GPU count using DeepSpeed-Ulysses at comparable per-GPU throughput (~160 TFLOPs based on Table 3), or can achieve the same 32K sequence length with ~64 GPUs (4× fewer) while maintaining throughput. The cost savings for a multi-week training run at this scale are substantial.

**Genomic and proteomic foundation model pre-training at million-token scale.** The paper demonstrates million-token training on a 1.2B model (Section 4.1, Figure 3). For genomics research groups pre-training language models on genome-scale data (human genome: 6.4 billion base pairs; Zvyagin et al., 2022), this capability means they can train on contiguous genomic regions spanning entire chromosomes or even whole genomes, rather than fragmenting the genome into artificial windows. The specific benefit: a genomics team pre-training a 1.2B-parameter model on the human reference genome can use DeepSpeed-Ulysses with, for example, 256 GPUs to process 1M-token genomic windows (maintaining the ~160 TFLOPs/GPU throughput from Table 3), enabling them to capture long-range regulatory interactions and evolutionary conservation patterns that span hundreds of thousands of base pairs—patterns that would be invisible to models trained on shorter 8K–32K token windows. Without DeepSpeed-Ulysses, such training runs would be bottlenecked by communication overhead to the point of infeasibility.

**Clinical NLP models processing full patient histories without summarization or truncation.** The paper cites clinical models that need to condition on entire patient care records (Li et al., 2022a; Gao et al., 2021) as a motivating application. In practice, a patient's longitudinal health record—spanning years of clinical notes, lab results, medication orders, and imaging reports—can easily reach 100K+ tokens when concatenated. A hospital system or health AI company deploying a model for tasks like readmission prediction or differential diagnosis generation can use DeepSpeed-Ulysses to train or fine-tune on full patient histories rather than summarized or truncated versions. The specific benefit: using the paper's 7B model results (Figure 4), fine-tuning on full 128K-token patient records with 32 GPUs would achieve ~1.5–2× higher throughput than Megatron-LM at the same sequence length, and would support sequence lengths that Megatron-LM cannot fit in memory at all (the 4× longer sequence claim from Section 4.3), enabling training on the 25–30% of patients whose records exceed Megatron-LM's maximum sequence length.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper positions DeepSpeed-Ulysses against two alternatives—Megatron-LM sequence parallelism (Korthikanti et al., 2022) and ColAI-SP (Li et al., 2022b)—and articulates clear trade-offs in Table 1 and the communication analysis of Section 3.2. A practitioner evaluating sequence parallelism strategies should consider:

**Prefer DeepSpeed-Ulysses when:**
- You are training Transformer models with sequence lengths beyond single-GPU memory (the primary use case for any sequence parallelism method), and you need the communication overhead to remain manageable as sequence length grows. The per-link communication volume of `$4Nh/P$` (vs. Megatron-LM's `$4Nh$`) means that doubling sequence length while doubling GPUs keeps communication cost roughly constant—a property Megatron-LM cannot provide.
- You need to use specific attention implementations—FlashAttention v2, block-sparse attention, or custom attention kernels—that are not compatible with Megatron-LM's tensor-parallelism-integrated approach or ColAI-SP's ring self-attention. DeepSpeed-Ulysses is the only method in Table 1 marked as attention-agnostic and compatible with FlashAttention v2 (Section 3.4).
- You are already using or plan to use ZeRO-3 for memory optimization. The combined sequence-and-data-parallel ZeRO group (Section 3.3) provides multiplicative memory savings (`$1/(D \times P)$` model state reduction) that Megatron-LM's tensor-parallelism-coupled approach cannot match (Table 1, "Parameter memory efficiency" column), enabling training of both larger models and longer sequences simultaneously.
- You value ease of integration: the paper claims "minimal code changes to the existing training frameworks" (Section 1), and the modular architecture (all-to-all bracketing a black-box attention module) means migrating from standard data-parallel training to DeepSpeed-Ulysses requires localized changes to the attention module, not a full rewrite into a different parallelism framework.

**Prefer Megatron-LM sequence parallelism when:**
- Your training infrastructure is already built on Megatron-LM's tensor parallelism and pipeline parallelism stack, and the engineering cost of migrating to DeepSpeed outweighs the throughput and sequence length benefits. Megatron-LM may be "good enough" for moderate sequence lengths (e.g., 8K–32K tokens) where the `$O(N)$` communication cost is still manageable given available inter-GPU bandwidth.
- Your models are relatively small (e.g., <3B parameters) and sequence lengths are moderate (<16K tokens), where the asymptotic communication advantage of DeepSpeed-Ulysses may not translate to a large absolute throughput difference. The paper's experiments focus on 7B and 30B models at long sequences—the crossover point where DeepSpeed-Ulysses's advantage becomes practically significant is not characterized.

**ColAI-SP is not recommended for new deployments** based on the paper's characterization in Table 1 and Section 2.2: it requires a non-standard attention mechanism (ring self-attention), does not support arbitrary attention types or FlashAttention, and does not compose with ZeRO-3 for parameter memory efficiency. The paper does not benchmark ColAI-SP, so a head-to-head throughput comparison is unavailable, but the qualitative limitations make it a less general solution.
