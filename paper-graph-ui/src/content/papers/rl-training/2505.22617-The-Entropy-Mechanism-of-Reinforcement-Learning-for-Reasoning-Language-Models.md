# The Entropy Mechanism of Reinforcement Learning for Reasoning Language Models

**ArXiv:** [2505.22617](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.22617)

## 🎯 Pitch

RL training for reasoning LLMs collapses policy entropy so predictably that final performance can be forecast from entropy alone via $R = -a \exp H + b$ — performance is literally *traded* from a depleting reserve of uncertainty. The collapse is driven by a consistent positive covariance between token probability and advantage, and simply detaching gradient updates for a tiny fraction of high-covariance tokens can boost entropy over 10× and unlock up to 6.4% accuracy gains on reasoning benchmarks.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper analyzes a major obstacle in scaling reinforcement learning (RL) for reasoning with LLMs: the sharp, early collapse of **policy entropy** (the model's uncertainty over token choices) during training, which consistently leads to performance saturation. Through experiments spanning 4 model families (Qwen2.5, Mistral, LLaMA, DeepSeek-Math) across math and coding benchmarks, the authors establish an empirical law — a transformation equation $R = -a \exp H + b$ linking policy entropy $H$ to downstream performance $R$ — that makes the performance ceiling fully predictable from the exhaustion of entropy ($H = 0$, $R = -a + b$). To understand *why* entropy collapses, the paper derives a theoretical result showing that entropy change is driven by the **covariance between action probability and advantage** (a high-probability action with high advantage reduces entropy, while a rare action with high advantage increases it), and validates this empirically by matching covariance and entropy-difference curves. Building on this mechanism, the authors propose two lightweight interventions — **Clip-Cov** (randomly detaching gradient updates for a small fraction of high-covariance tokens) and **KL-Cov** (applying a KL penalty to tokens with the largest covariances) — which lift policy entropy by over 10×, enabling sustained exploration and yielding average accuracy gains of 2.0% on a 7B model and 6.4% on a 32B model across seven mathematical reasoning benchmarks, establishing that entropy control unlocks further RL scaling only when the base model already possesses latent capabilities for the training distribution.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: RL for Reasoning LLMs Systematically Loses the Ability to Explore

The central problem this paper tackles is subtle but devastating for scaling reinforcement learning: **when you apply RL to improve a language model's reasoning, the model rapidly becomes overconfident and stops exploring, which then prevents any further improvement.** This isn't a minor inefficiency — it's a hard ceiling that makes additional training compute largely wasted.

To make this concrete, consider what happens during a typical RL training run for mathematical reasoning (detailed in Section 2.3 and Figure 2). Across 11 different base models spanning four model families and sizes from 0.5B to 32B parameters, the authors observe a consistent pattern: **over 73% of the total entropy loss and 76% of the total performance gain occur in just the first 200 gradient steps** (out of 2400 total). By step 800 — only one-third of training — over 93% of performance gains and 94% of entropy losses have already happened. The remaining 1600 training steps yield marginal returns, meaning the model has essentially stopped learning. This is what the paper terms "entropy collapse": the policy's uncertainty over token choices drops sharply and monotonically toward near-zero, and once it's gone, the model cannot discover new reasoning strategies.

The problem can be understood through the classic exploitation-exploration tradeoff from reinforcement learning (Sutton, 1988). Exploitation means using strategies the model already knows work well — selecting tokens it is confident will lead to high rewards. Exploration means trying new, uncertain actions that might discover even better strategies. **Policy entropy** is the mathematical quantity that measures this exploratory capacity: it quantifies how spread out the model's probability distribution is over possible next tokens. High entropy means the model is considering many diverse options; low entropy means it has locked onto a narrow set of "safe" choices.

What makes this problem particularly acute for LLMs, as opposed to traditional RL settings, is the combination of two factors. First, LLMs start from a strong pretrained prior — they already have well-formed probability distributions over tokens from massive pretraining, which means their initial entropy is concentrated on tokens the model already believes are good. Second, verifiable reasoning tasks (math problems, coding challenges) provide binary success/failure signals that strongly reinforce whatever limited strategies happened to work early in training, rapidly concentrating probability mass onto those strategies and extinguishing alternative paths. The result is a self-reinforcing cycle: the model becomes confident, exploits what it knows, gets positive rewards for those exploitations, becomes more confident, and quickly exhausts its capacity to discover anything new.

### Why This Problem Matters: The Stakes for Scaling RL

This problem is not merely an academic curiosity — it has direct, practical consequences for how the field invests training compute. The paper positions the issue within a broader trend: the consensus is growing that training compute will increasingly shift from pretraining to post-training, particularly RL (Silver & Sutton, 2025). If this shift is to succeed, RL must be scalable — meaning that investing more training compute should yield proportionally better reasoning capabilities. Entropy collapse breaks this scalability.

The empirical law the paper discovers makes the consequences quantitative. The fitted equation $R = -a \exp H + b$ (where $R$ is validation accuracy and $H$ is policy entropy, detailed in Section 2.4) reveals that **performance is literally traded from entropy in a predictable, exponential manner**. When entropy reaches zero (which it inevitably does without intervention), performance hits a hard ceiling at $R = -a + b$. This ceiling is not just an empirical observation — it follows directly from the mathematics. If all probability mass concentrates on a single action at each step, the model has exactly one way to solve each problem, and if that way is wrong for some problems, there is no mechanism to discover alternatives. The "bitter lesson" (Sutton, 2019) that general methods leveraging computation beat hand-crafted approaches only holds if computation can actually be leveraged — entropy collapse makes further computation pointless.

The practical implications extend in several directions:

- **Training efficiency**: Organizations running large-scale RL on reasoning tasks are currently spending the majority of their compute budget on training steps that produce negligible improvement. Understanding *when* this happens and *why* enables more efficient allocation — either by stopping training earlier or by deploying interventions that prevent the collapse in the first place.

- **Model scaling**: The paper shows (Figure 7) that the coefficients $a$ and $b$ in the entropy-performance relationship scale log-linearly with model size within a model family. This means the ceiling $b - a$ is higher for larger models, but the *mechanism* of collapse is the same — larger models still hit their ceiling, just at a higher absolute performance level. Without addressing entropy collapse, simply scaling model size yields diminishing returns from RL, because each model quickly exhausts its exploratory budget regardless of size.

- **Self-improvement and continual learning**: A long-term vision for AI systems involves models that can continuously improve through interaction with the environment, learning from their own successes and failures. If RL training systematically destroys the model's ability to explore, this vision is fundamentally compromised — the model would lock into early strategies and never adapt to novel situations or harder problems that require different approaches.

- **The "elicitation vs. creation" debate**: There is active discussion in the field about whether RL for reasoning actually creates new capabilities or merely elicits capabilities already latently present from pretraining (Yue et al., 2025). The paper's finding conditionally supports the elicitation view: if entropy collapses and the model stops exploring, it can only manifest what it already implicitly knows, never genuinely discovering novel reasoning patterns. However, the paper argues this is not an intrinsic limitation of RL itself — it's a consequence of the entropy mechanism, which means it can potentially be overcome.

### Prior Approaches and Where They Fall Short

The paper does not emerge in a vacuum. Several lines of prior work have recognized the importance of entropy in RL and attempted to manage it, but each falls short in the specific context of RL for LLM reasoning.

**Entropy regularization in classical RL.** The most well-established approach is to add an entropy bonus to the reward function, explicitly incentivizing the policy to maintain high entropy. This idea originates in maximum entropy RL (Ziebart et al., 2008; Toussaint, 2009) and has been incorporated into major algorithms: A3C (Mnih et al., 2016) uses an entropy bonus, Soft Actor-Critic (Haarnoja et al., 2018) makes entropy maximization a core objective, and PPO (Schulman et al., 2017b) commonly includes an entropy term in its loss function. In traditional RL domains (Atari games, continuous control), this approach works reasonably well: the entropy bonus counteracts premature convergence and maintains enough stochasticity for continued learning.

However, Section 4.1 of this paper demonstrates that **naively applying entropy loss to LLM RL is ineffective and brittle**. Figure 9 shows the results: with entropy coefficients of 0.0001 or 0.001, the effect on policy entropy is negligible — the collapse proceeds essentially unchanged. With a coefficient of 0.01, the entropy *explodes* rather than stabilizing, causing the policy to diverge. Even at the "sweet spot" of 0.005, where entropy is successfully stabilized, the validation accuracy does not outperform baselines that simply let entropy collapse. The authors note that this extreme sensitivity to the entropy coefficient (a factor of 10 between "no effect" and "divergence") makes this approach impractical for LLM training, where each run is expensive and tuning hyperparameters by trial and error is costly.

**KL regularization to a reference model.** Another common approach, especially in RLHF-style training (Ouyang et al., 2022), is to penalize the KL divergence between the current policy and a frozen reference model (usually the pretrained or SFT model). The intuition is that this prevents the policy from drifting too far from the initial distribution, indirectly preserving some of the reference model's entropy. Figure 10 shows the results: while KL regularization does stabilize entropy at various levels (depending on the coefficient $\beta$), it **uniformly degrades performance** relative to the unregularized baseline. The paper hypothesizes that KL to a reference model is too blunt an instrument — it constrains all tokens equally, preventing both harmful overconfidence and beneficial specialization.

**The clip-higher technique.** A more recent approach, introduced in the DAPO work (Yu et al., 2025), modifies PPO's clipping mechanism. Standard PPO clips the importance sampling ratio $\frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}$ to $[1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon]$ to prevent overly large updates. Clip-higher raises the upper threshold (to 0.28 in the DAPO paper), which allows tokens whose probability has increased more aggressively. Since these are typically tokens with positive advantages, this implicitly encourages the model to increase probability on more diverse good actions rather than collapsing onto a single one. The paper uses clip-higher as its main baseline (Table 2) and acknowledges its effectiveness in increasing entropy in early training (Figure 11, left panels). However, the authors observe that clip-higher **becomes unstable as training progresses**, with performance saturating and eventually declining, while their proposed methods maintain stable entropy curves throughout.

**Process reward models and implicit rewards.** Some works attempt to improve exploration by providing denser reward signals, such as process reward models (PRMs) that score intermediate reasoning steps rather than just final answers (Lightman et al., 2023; Cui et al., 2025; Yuan et al., 2025). The intuition is that step-level feedback gives the model more nuanced guidance, potentially preventing it from collapsing onto strategies that happen to produce correct final answers through flawed reasoning. While these methods improve overall performance, the paper's entropy collapse observations persist even when using PRIME (Cui et al., 2025), which employs an implicit PRM, as shown in Figure 6 — the entropy-performance relationship is algorithm-independent.

**Off-policy and multi-stage training.** Some recent work (Yan et al., 2025; Luo et al., 2025) has observed different entropy patterns when using off-policy data or multi-stage training curricula. The paper explicitly notes this (Section 2.6), acknowledging that the entropy collapse pattern may not be universal under all training configurations. However, for the standard paradigm that has produced the strongest public reasoning results — on-policy RL from a base model with verifiable rewards (the "Zero" setting following DeepSeek-AI et al., 2025) — entropy collapse is consistent and severe.

The fundamental gap across all these prior approaches is that **they treat entropy management as a generic regularization problem — adding a term to the loss or modifying a threshold — without understanding the mechanistic driver of entropy change**. This makes them either too weak to prevent collapse (small entropy coefficients, clip-higher at late training), too strong and destabilizing (large entropy coefficients), or too broad to allow beneficial specialization (KL to reference). None of them are guided by a theory of *why* entropy decreases during LLM RL training, which means none of them can intervene precisely on the cause.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper's core positioning move is to **shift from treating entropy collapse as a regularization problem to treating it as a mechanism problem**. Rather than asking "what penalty should we add to the loss to keep entropy high?", the authors ask "what mathematical process causes entropy to decrease, and can we intervene directly on that process?"

This leads to the paper's three-stage structure, each building on the previous:

**Stage 1: Characterize the phenomenon empirically.** Before intervening, the paper establishes that entropy collapse is not an occasional bug but a universal, predictable pattern under standard RL setups for reasoning LLMs. The exponential fitting $R = -a \exp H + b$ (Section 2.4, Figures 3-4) demonstrates that performance and entropy are linked by a simple functional form that holds across model families, model sizes, and task domains. The coefficients $a$ and $b$ are shown to be algorithm-independent (Figure 6) and to scale predictably with model size (Figure 7). This empirical law is positioned as an analog to neural scaling laws (Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022), but applied to the *dynamics* of RL training rather than the *statics* of pretraining loss.

**Stage 2: Derive the mechanism theoretically and validate empirically.** The paper develops a theory of entropy dynamics for softmax policies (LLMs) under policy gradient algorithms. The key derivation (Lemma 1, Proposition 1, Theorem 1 in Section 3) shows that the step-wise change in policy entropy equals (up to a negative sign and scaling factor) the **covariance between the log-probability of an action and the change in that action's logit**. Under policy gradient, this logit change is proportional to $\pi_\theta(a|s) \cdot A(s,a)$, so the covariance becomes between $\log \pi_\theta(a|s)$ and $\pi_\theta(a|s) \cdot A(s,a)$. Under natural policy gradient, the $\pi_\theta(a|s)$ factor drops out, giving a cleaner covariance between $\log \pi_\theta(a|s)$ and $A(s,a)$ directly (Theorem 2).

The intuition is powerful: if high-probability actions tend to have high advantages (positive covariance), entropy decreases — the model is correctly confident and becomes more so. If low-probability (rare) actions have high advantages (negative covariance), entropy increases — the model discovers that surprising actions work well and diversifies. The paper validates this theory by plotting the covariance term and the negative entropy difference on the same axes (Figure 8, left) and showing they closely match, with covariance positive throughout training (explaining the monotonic entropy decline).

This theoretical machinery gives the paper a principled lens that prior work lacked: **entropy collapse happens because high-probability actions consistently receive high advantage estimates early in training, creating positive covariance that drives entropy down; as training progresses, covariance remains positive but shrinks, continuing to drag entropy toward zero even after performance saturates.**

**Stage 3: Design targeted interventions based on the mechanism.** If positive covariance on specific tokens is the driver of entropy collapse, then the natural intervention is to **limit the update magnitude on tokens with the highest covariance**. This is exactly what Clip-Cov and KL-Cov do (Section 4.2). Unlike a uniform entropy bonus that pushes on all tokens equally, or a KL penalty that constrains all tokens toward a reference distribution, these methods identify a specific, small subset of tokens (the top 0.02% to 0.2% by covariance, as shown in Table 1) and selectively reduce their influence on the gradient update.

The paper explicitly frames Clip-Cov and KL-Cov as **instantiations of a principle rather than as novel algorithms per se**. The principle is: identify the tokens that disproportionately drive entropy decrease (high covariance between log-probability and advantage) and prevent them from collapsing the policy's uncertainty. Clip-Cov does this by randomly detaching a fraction of high-covariance tokens from gradient computation. KL-Cov does it by adding a KL penalty specifically to the highest-covariance tokens. Both are simple (requiring only a few lines of code modification, as shown in Listing 1) and both produce controllable, sustained increases in policy entropy (Figure 12) that translate to improved downstream performance (Table 2, Figure 11).

### Connection to Broader Research Themes

The paper situates its contribution within several broader conversations:

**The predictability of neural network training.** Just as scaling laws enable prediction of pretraining loss from smaller model runs (Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022), the paper's $R = -a \exp H + b$ law enables prediction of RL training outcomes from early training dynamics. Figure 5 demonstrates this directly: fitting the function using only the first 36 training steps (15% of total) and extrapolating to 236 steps yields an average RMSE of 0.9% on math and 1.2% on coding. This prediction capability has practical value for deciding whether to continue an expensive training run or abort it early.

**The exploitation-exploration tradeoff in the era of large pretrained models.** Traditional RL treats the exploitation-exploration tradeoff as something to be balanced throughout training — the agent must explore to discover good strategies and exploit to consolidate them. LLMs enter RL with a dramatically different starting point: they already have strong, well-calibrated priors from pretraining, meaning their initial exploitation is quite effective. The paper's finding that covariance is highest on easy problems (Figure 8, right) and that early covariance is strongly positive suggests that LLMs are *correctly* confident early in training — they really do know which actions are good on many problems. The problem is that this correct exploitation extinguishes exploration before the model can learn to handle harder problems where its initial intuitions are wrong. This reframes the challenge: it's not that LLMs explore poorly in some absolute sense, but that their exploitation is so effective that it prematurely shuts down the exploration needed for further improvement.

**The latent capabilities hypothesis.** The finding that entropy collapse imposes a predictable ceiling $R = -a + b$ naturally raises the question: where does this ceiling come from? The paper's results suggest it is determined by the base model's pretrained capabilities and the training data distribution (the coefficients $a$ and $b$ vary with model size and training data, as shown in Figures 7 and 13). This aligns with the view that RL primarily elicits existing capabilities rather than creating new ones — the ceiling is the limit of what can be extracted from the pretrained model given the training data. However, the paper's entropy-control methods show that this ceiling can be raised by maintaining exploration, suggesting that with proper entropy management, RL might go beyond pure elicitation and enable the model to compose its existing knowledge in genuinely novel ways that never appeared in pretraining.

## 3. Technical Approach

This is primarily a **mechanistic analysis paper** that first empirically characterizes and theoretically explains why policy entropy collapses during RL for reasoning LLMs, then uses that mechanistic understanding to design precise, lightweight interventions that prevent the collapse and enable sustained exploration.

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

The "system" being analyzed is a reinforcement learning training pipeline where a pretrained language model (the policy) is fine-tuned to solve reasoning tasks (math problems, coding challenges) by sampling multiple solutions, checking which are correct, and updating the model to favor successful strategies. The core problem this pipeline faces is that the model's **policy entropy** — its uncertainty over which tokens to generate — drops sharply and monotonically to near zero early in training, after which the model can no longer discover new reasoning paths and performance plateaus. The solution is not to add a generic entropy bonus to the loss function (which the paper shows is brittle and ineffective), but to identify the precise mathematical mechanism driving entropy decrease — the **covariance between action log-probability and advantage** — and intervene selectively on the small fraction of tokens where this covariance is highest.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The training system has four major components that interact in a cyclical process:

1. **Policy Model (πθ):** A pretrained base LLM (e.g., Qwen2.5-7B) that autoregressively generates complete solutions to reasoning problems. It starts with high entropy (diverse token choices) from pretraining and is gradually fine-tuned by RL. This is the "actor" being optimized.

2. **Rollout Generation Engine:** At each training step, this component samples `K` complete solutions (typically `K = 8`) per prompt from the current policy at temperature 1, producing a batch of `N` total rollouts across `|D|` prompts. These rollouts are the raw material for estimating which actions (token choices) are good and which are bad.

3. **Advantage Estimator (Verifier + GRPO Normalization):** For each sampled solution, the verifier (a rule-based checker for math answers or unit tests for code) provides a binary or scalar reward `r(y)`. The advantage `At` for each token at position `t` in the solution is then computed using **group-wise normalization**: for all `K` solutions to the same prompt, the rewards are standardized by subtracting their mean and dividing by their standard deviation. This gives positive advantages to tokens in above-average solutions and negative advantages to tokens in below-average ones. The paper primarily uses GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) for this step (Equation 3).

4. **Entropy-Aware Policy Update Module:** This is the paper's core contribution. Using the policy gradient theorem, the system computes per-token losses. But before applying updates, it computes the **token-wise covariance** between log-probability and advantage (Equation 10). Tokens with extremely high covariance — typically the top 0.02% to 0.2% — are identified. In **Clip-Cov**, a random fraction of these high-covariance tokens have their gradients detached (i.e., they contribute zero to the parameter update). In **KL-Cov**, a KL divergence penalty between the current policy and the old policy is added specifically to the highest-covariance tokens. This selective suppression prevents these outlier tokens from dominating the entropy decrease while leaving the majority of tokens to update normally.

Information flows cyclically: the policy generates rollouts → the verifier scores them → advantages are computed via group normalization → the policy gradient loss is calculated per token → per-token covariances are computed → high-covariance tokens are suppressed via clipping or KL penalty → the policy parameters are updated → the cycle repeats with the updated policy.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

#### The RL Objective and GRPO Advantage Estimation

The paper adopts the standard RL formulation for verifiable reasoning tasks. Given an input prompt `x` (a math problem or coding challenge), the policy `πθ` — the language model being trained — autoregressively generates an output sequence `y = {y1, ..., yT}` consisting of `T` tokens. The goal is to maximize expected reward:

$$J(\theta) := \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}, y \sim \pi_\theta(x)} \left[ r(y) \right]$$

where `$\mathcal{D}$` is the training distribution (a set of reasoning prompts), `$\pi_\theta(x)$` is the distribution over output sequences induced by the current policy given prompt `x`, and `$r(y)$` is the reward signal — a scalar indicating whether the final answer extracted from sequence `y` is correct.

**What it computes:** This is the standard expected-reward objective in RL. The expectation is over two sources of randomness: prompts sampled from the training set, and output sequences sampled from the policy's own generative distribution given each prompt. The reward `r(y)` is deterministic given the sequence (verifiable by checking the final answer against ground truth), so the objective simply measures the fraction of problems the policy solves correctly in expectation.

**Why this form:** This objective avoids the reward hacking problems that plague learned reward models. Because the reward comes from a verifier (executing code or checking mathematical equivalence), it is the ground-truth correctness signal — there is no gap between a proxy reward and true quality. This means any entropy-performance relationship observed is due to the policy dynamics themselves, not to overoptimization of an imperfect reward signal.

To optimize this objective, the paper uses the Policy Gradient theorem (Williams, 1992). The gradient of `$J(\theta)$` with respect to policy parameters `$\theta$` is:

$$\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}, y \sim \pi_\theta(x)} \left[ \sum_{t=0}^T \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_t | y_{<t}) A_t \right]$$

where `$\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_t | y_{<t})$` is the gradient of the log-probability of token `$y_t$` given its prefix `$y_{<t}$` (the "score function"), and `$A_t$` is the **advantage** — a scalar measuring how much better or worse the action (choosing token `$y_t$` at time `$t$`) is compared to the average action at that state.

**What it computes:** For each token generated during a rollout, the gradient pushes the policy parameters in a direction that increases the log-probability of that token if its advantage is positive, and decreases it if the advantage is negative. The magnitude of the update is proportional to the advantage. The sum aggregates these per-token updates into a single gradient vector for the entire trajectory.

**Why this form:** The Policy Gradient theorem provides an unbiased estimate of the true gradient of expected reward, requiring only that we can sample from the policy and compute advantages — it does not require a differentiable model of the environment. This is essential because the "environment" here is the verifier, which is non-differentiable (a string matching or unit test operation). The `$\log \pi_\theta$` form emerges from the derivative of the logarithm: `$\nabla_\theta \pi_\theta = \pi_\theta \cdot \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta$`, which allows sampling-based gradient estimation.

The critical design choice is **how to estimate the advantage** `$A_t$`. In the simplest setting (vanilla REINFORCE, Williams, 1992), if only a trajectory-level reward is available (no intermediate rewards), the advantage for every token is simply the total reward: `$A_t = r(y)$`. However, this has high variance because all tokens in a correct solution get the same positive signal regardless of how much each individual token contributed, and all tokens in an incorrect solution get zero regardless of whether most steps were actually good.

GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization; Shao et al., 2024) addresses this by leveraging the fact that multiple solutions can be sampled for the same prompt. For each prompt `$x$`, the system samples `$K$` complete solutions `$y^1, ..., y^K$`. The advantage for any token in solution `$y^i$` is:

$$A_t = \frac{r(y^i) - \text{mean}(r(y^{1:K}))}{\text{std}(r(y^{1:K}))}$$

where `$\text{mean}(r(y^{1:K}))$` is the average reward across the `$K$` solutions for this prompt, and `$\text{std}(r(y^{1:K}))$` is the standard deviation.

**What it computes:** The advantage is the **normalized deviation** of this solution's reward from the average reward of its peer group (other solutions to the same problem). If all solutions get the same reward (all correct or all incorrect), the advantages are zero for all tokens — this prompt contributes nothing to the gradient (in practice, the paper filters out such prompts; Section 2.2). If one solution is correct while others are incorrect, tokens in the correct solution receive positive advantages and tokens in incorrect ones receive negative advantages. The normalization by standard deviation ensures the scale of advantages is comparable across prompts.

**Why this form:** Group normalization acts as a baseline that reduces variance without introducing bias. In standard REINFORCE, a learned value function `$V(s)$` is subtracted from the reward to get advantage `$A = r - V$`, which requires training an additional model. GRPO achieves a similar effect for free: because solutions to the same prompt share the same difficulty and the same expected reward under the current policy, the group mean is an empirical approximation of that expected reward. If the policy is better than average (solves a problem most solutions get wrong), it receives a positive signal; if worse, a negative one. The paper also experiments with RLOO (Kool et al., 2019; Ahmadian et al., 2024), which uses a similar leave-one-out baseline, and PRIME (Cui et al., 2025), which uses an implicit process reward model for advantage estimation. The key result (Figure 6) is that the entropy-performance relationship is **algorithm-independent** — GRPO, RLOO, PRIME, and REINFORCE++ all produce the same functional form between entropy and performance, suggesting the dynamics are driven by something deeper than the specific advantage estimator.

Finally, to handle off-policy data (solutions generated by a previous version of the policy) and constrain update sizes, the paper uses the standard PPO surrogate loss (Schulman et al., 2017b) in most experiments:

$$\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_t \left[ \min \left( \frac{\pi_\theta(y_t | y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t | y_{<t})} A_t, \text{clip}\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_t | y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t | y_{<t})}, 1 - \epsilon, 1 + \epsilon\right) A_t \right) \right]$$

where `$\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$` is the policy that generated the rollout data, the ratio `$\frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}$` measures how much the current policy's probability for a token differs from the old policy's, `$\epsilon = 0.2$` is the clipping threshold, and the min operation ensures the update is conservative — it clips large probability increases when advantage is positive and large probability decreases when advantage is negative.

**What it computes:** For each token, if the ratio is within `$[1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon]$`, the loss is just the standard policy gradient `$\text{ratio} \cdot A_t$`. If the ratio exceeds this range and would make the update too aggressive, the gradient is clipped to the boundary value, preventing the policy from changing too much in a single update step.

**Why this form:** PPO's clipping mechanism provides a simpler alternative to trust-region methods (like TRPO; Schulman et al., 2015) that constrain the KL divergence between old and new policies. The min operation ensures the objective is a lower bound on the true expected reward, making optimization safe. This is the standard backbone on which the paper's covariance-aware modifications are built — Clip-Cov and KL-Cov are inserted into this loss computation by selectively modifying which tokens participate and how they are penalized.

#### Measuring Policy Entropy and the Empirical Performance-Entropy Law

The central quantity the paper tracks is **policy entropy**, measured on the training data. For a policy model `$\pi_\theta$` and training dataset `$\mathcal{D}$`, the average token-level entropy is:

$$H(\pi_\theta, \mathcal{D}) = -\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}, \pi_\theta} \left[ \log \pi_\theta(y_t | y_{<t}) \right] = -\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}|} \sum_{x \in \mathcal{D}} \frac{1}{|y|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y|} \mathbb{E}_{y_t \sim \pi_\theta} \left[ \log \pi_\theta(y_t | y_{<t}, x) \right]$$

where `$|\mathcal{D}|$` is the number of prompts in the batch, `$|y|$` is the length of the generated response in tokens, and `$\mathbb{E}_{y_t \sim \pi_\theta}$` denotes the expectation over token choices from the policy's output distribution.

**What it computes:** For each token position in each generated response, the model's probability distribution over the vocabulary is queried. The log-probability of the token that was actually chosen is extracted, negated, and averaged across all tokens and all prompts. High entropy means the model is spreading probability across many possible tokens (it is uncertain); low entropy means probability is concentrated on a few tokens (it is confident). The paper computes this per-batch on prompts randomly sampled from the training dataset, not on a fixed validation set, which means it tracks the policy's uncertainty on exactly the data it is being trained on.

**Why this form:** This is the standard Shannon entropy of a categorical distribution, applied at each generation step. It is the natural measure of exploratory capacity because it quantifies how "spread out" the action distribution is. A policy at the entropy maximum (uniform over vocabulary) can explore any token equally; a policy at entropy zero (deterministic) has exactly one choice at each step and cannot discover alternative reasoning paths.

Throughout training, the paper records pairs of `(H, R)` — the policy entropy on the training batch and the validation accuracy on held-out benchmarks (MATH500, AIME, AMC, etc.). By plotting these points for hundreds of training steps, a clear pattern emerges that is well-approximated by an exponential function:

$$R = -a \exp(H) + b$$

where `$R$` is the validation accuracy (percentage), `$H$` is the policy entropy, and `$a, b$` are positive fitting coefficients.

**What it computes:** Given the entropy `$H$` at any point during training (which can be measured on a training batch), this equation predicts the validation performance `$R$` the model will achieve. The exponential form `$\exp(H)$` means that as entropy drops linearly, performance improves at a decelerating rate: early entropy decreases yield large performance gains, but late entropy decreases yield diminishing returns.

**Why this form:** The exponential functional form is not derived theoretically — it is purely empirical, chosen because it fits the data across all experimental configurations with only two parameters. The paper contrasts this with the linear or power-law relationships that would arise if performance scaled directly with certainty. The exponential says that **performance is saturating in certainty**: going from total uncertainty (high `$H$`) to moderate uncertainty yields large gains, but pushing from moderate uncertainty to near-certainty yields almost nothing. This makes sense intuitively: the low-hanging fruit of RL is to concentrate probability on strategies that are clearly better than random guessing, but once the model has locked onto a reasonable strategy, further concentration just eliminates alternative approaches without finding better ones.

The physical interpretation of the coefficients is straightforward. Differentiating the equation gives `$dR/dH = -a \exp(H)$`, so **`$a$` is the rate at which the model converts entropy into performance** — a larger `$a$` means a steeper initial improvement per unit of entropy consumed. When entropy is fully depleted (`$H = 0$`), performance reaches its ceiling: `$R_{\max} = b - a$`. So **`$b - a$` is the maximum achievable validation accuracy** given the model and training data, and **`$b = R_{\max} + a$`** is the sum of this ceiling plus the "efficiency" coefficient.

The paper demonstrates three key properties of these coefficients:

**Property 1: Algorithm independence (Figure 6).** Whether using GRPO, RLOO, PRIME, or REINFORCE++, the fitted curves for the same base model (Qwen2.5-7B) on the same data nearly coincide. The coefficients `$a$` and `$b$` are determined by the policy model and training data, not by how advantages are estimated. This is a striking finding because it means the entropy-performance tradeoff is a *property of the model-data pair*, not an artifact of a particular RL algorithm. It also explains why researchers using different algorithms observe similar training dynamics.

**Property 2: Log-linear scaling with model size (Figure 7).** Within the Qwen2.5 model family (0.5B, 1.5B, 3B, 7B, 32B parameters without embeddings), both `$a$` and `$b$` scale log-linearly with parameter count on both math and coding tasks. This means plotting `$\log(\text{parameters})$` against `$a$` (or `$b$`) yields a straight line, analogous to how pretraining loss scales with compute in neural scaling laws. The practical implication: by training small models in a family and fitting their `$a$` and `$b$` values, one can extrapolate to predict the RL training dynamics and final performance of larger models *without actually training them*.

**Property 3: Predictability from early training (Figure 5).** Because the functional form `$R = -a \exp(H) + b$` has only two parameters, it can be fit using data from the initial portion of training where entropy is still relatively high. The paper demonstrates this by fitting the curve using only the first 36 training steps (roughly 15% of total) and using the fitted function to predict performance over the next 200 steps. The average RMSE is 0.9% on math and 1.2% on coding. The final-step prediction RMSE (the error when predicting the converged performance) is 0.5% on math and 1.9% on coding. This means one can decide early in training whether the run is worth continuing or if the model will plateau at an unacceptably low accuracy.

#### Theoretical Derivation of Entropy Dynamics

The empirical law `$R = -a \exp(H) + b$` establishes that entropy collapse is a severe bottleneck, but it does not explain *why* entropy decreases during training. To design interventions, the paper needs a mechanistic theory of what drives entropy change at the gradient level. This theoretical analysis proceeds in three steps, each building on the previous.

**Step 1: Entropy change for a general softmax policy (Lemma 1).**

The starting observation is that language models are **softmax policies**: the probability of choosing token `$a$` given state `$s$` (where `$s$` is the prefix `$y_{<t}$`) is:

$$\pi_\theta(a|s) = \frac{\exp(z_{s,a})}{\sum_{a' \in \mathcal{A}} \exp(z_{s,a'})}$$

where `$z_{s,a}$` is the output **logit** — the raw, unnormalized score that the neural network assigns to token `$a$` at state `$s$`, and `$\mathcal{A}$` is the vocabulary. The logit is a function of the model parameters `$\theta$` (so `$z_{s,a} = f_\theta(s, a)$`), and it is what gets updated during gradient descent.

The paper first considers a simplified setting: a **tabular softmax policy**, where each state-action pair `$(s, a)$` has its own independent parameter `$\theta_{s,a}$` (i.e., the logit `$z_{s,a}$` is directly stored as a learnable scalar). This is not how neural networks work in practice (parameters are shared across states), but it is a standard analytical tool (used in Agarwal et al., 2021; Liu, 2025) that captures the essential gradient dynamics while making the math tractable. The key insight is that for any softmax policy, the entropy change between two consecutive parameter updates (steps `$k$` and `$k+1$`) can be expressed in terms of the logit changes:

$$H(\pi_\theta^{k+1} | s) - H(\pi_\theta^k | s) \approx -\text{Cov}_{a \sim \pi_\theta^k(\cdot|s)} \left( \log \pi_\theta^k(a|s), z_{s,a}^{k+1} - z_{s,a}^k \right)$$

where `$\pi_\theta^k$` is the policy at step `$k$` (before the update), `$\pi_\theta^{k+1}$` is the policy at step `$k+1$` (after the update), `$\log \pi_\theta^k(a|s)$` is the log-probability of action `$a$` under the old policy, and `$z_{s,a}^{k+1} - z_{s,a}^k$` is the change in the logit for action `$a$` resulting from the gradient update.

**What it computes:** This equation says that the change in entropy at state `$s$` equals (up to the negative sign) the **covariance** between two quantities across the action distribution: (1) how likely each action was under the old policy (log-prob), and (2) how much each action's logit changed. Covariance measures whether these two quantities tend to move in the same direction (positive covariance) or opposite directions (negative covariance). If high-probability actions also get larger logit increases (positive covariance), entropy decreases. If low-probability actions get larger logit increases (negative covariance), entropy increases.

**Why this form:** The derivation (provided in full in Appendix E.2) uses a first-order Taylor expansion of the entropy function `$H(\pi_\theta|s)$` around `$\pi_\theta^k$` and the fact that for a tabular softmax policy, `$\frac{\partial H}{\partial z_{s,a}} = \pi_\theta(a|s)\log\pi_\theta(a|s) + H(\pi_\theta|s)\pi_\theta(a|s)$`. After algebraic manipulation, the inner product between the gradient of entropy and the logit changes simplifies to the negative covariance expression. This form is powerful because it decomposes entropy change into a property of the current policy (action probabilities) and a property of the update algorithm (logit changes) — the next step is to plug in what the logit changes actually are for specific RL algorithms.

The paper notes that this Lemma is adapted from Liu (2025), who derived it in a blog post analyzing RL policy entropy convergence. The contribution here is applying it to the specific context of LLM reasoning and using it to design interventions.

**Step 2: Logit changes under Policy Gradient (Proposition 1) and Natural Policy Gradient (Theorem 2).**

The logit change `$z_{s,a}^{k+1} - z_{s,a}^k$` depends on the RL algorithm. Under vanilla Policy Gradient (where the gradient of the objective `$J(\theta)$` with respect to logits is computed via Equation 2 and the logits are updated as `$z^{k+1} = z^k - \eta \nabla_z J$` with learning rate `$\eta$`), the change is:

$$z_{s,a}^{k+1} - z_{s,a}^k = \eta \cdot \pi_\theta(a|s) \cdot A(s,a)$$

where `$\eta$` is the learning rate, `$\pi_\theta(a|s)$` is the probability the current policy assigns to action `$a$`, and `$A(s,a)$` is the advantage of that action.

**What it computes:** The logit for action `$a$` increases by an amount proportional to the product of how likely the action is (its probability) and how good it is (its advantage). If an action is both probable and advantageous, its logit gets a large boost. If it's improbable or disadvantageous, the update is small or negative. The learning rate `$\eta$` scales the overall magnitude.

**Why this form:** The derivation (Appendix E.3) uses the fact that for tabular softmax policies, the derivative of the log-probability with respect to the logit is a sparse indicator: `$\frac{\partial \log\pi_\theta(a'|s)}{\partial z_{s,a}} = \mathbb{1}[a=a'] - \pi_\theta(a|s)$`. Substituting this into the policy gradient expression and applying Lemma 3 (the expectation of advantage over actions is zero) yields the clean product form. This is notable because it shows the logit update is **action-specific** — each action `$a$` is updated based on its own probability and advantage, not on global averages.

Plugging Proposition 1 into Lemma 1 yields Theorem 1, the **entropy change under Policy Gradient**:

$$H(\pi_\theta^{k+1} | s) - H(\pi_\theta^k | s) \approx -\eta \cdot \text{Cov}_{a \sim \pi_\theta^k(\cdot|s)} \left( \log \pi_\theta^k(a|s), \pi_\theta^k(a|s) \cdot A(s,a) \right)$$

**What it computes:** The entropy decrease is proportional to the covariance between the log-probability of an action and the product `$\pi_\theta(a|s) \cdot A(s,a)$`. The inner product `$\pi_\theta(a|s) \cdot A(s,a)$` is the policy-gradient-weighted advantage — it upweights advantages of actions the policy already favors (multiplying by probability) and downweights advantages of actions the policy rarely takes.

**Why this form:** The `$\pi_\theta(a|s)$` factor inside the covariance means that high-probability actions contribute disproportionately to entropy change. Even if a rare action has a very high advantage, its contribution is dampened by its low probability, so it has little effect on entropy. Conversely, a high-probability action with even a modestly positive advantage will strongly decrease entropy. This provides a mechanistic explanation for why entropy collapses quickly: early in training, the policy already has well-calibrated confidence from pretraining (Kadavath et al., 2022) — actions the model thinks are good actually are good, creating positive covariance across the majority of generated tokens. The model reinforces its existing beliefs, concentrating probability further, until the high-probability actions are so concentrated that no negative-covariance tokens (rare actions with high advantage) can counteract the entropy decrease.

For completeness, the paper also presents Theorem 2 (from Liu, 2025), the **entropy change under Natural Policy Gradient** (Kakade, 2001):

$$H(\pi_\theta^{k+1} | s) - H(\pi_\theta^k | s) \approx -\eta \cdot \text{Cov}_{a \sim \pi_\theta^k(\cdot|s)} \left( \log \pi_\theta^k(a|s), A(s,a) \right)$$

where the `$\pi_\theta(a|s)$` factor inside the covariance is absent because natural policy gradient preconditions the update with the Fisher information matrix, effectively normalizing by the policy's own probability in a way that cancels out the `$\pi_\theta$` factor. This yields a cleaner relationship: entropy change is covariance between log-probability and raw advantage.

**What it computes:** Under NPG, a rare action with high advantage can increase entropy just as effectively as a common action with high advantage can decrease it, because the `$\pi_\theta$` weighting is removed. This means natural policy gradient is, in principle, more exploration-friendly: it does not dampen the signal from improbable but good actions.

**Why this form matters for the paper:** Although natural policy gradient is rarely used directly in LLM training (its second-order optimization is computationally prohibitive at scale), its introduction of a KL-divergence constraint between successive policies is the intellectual precursor to PPO's clipping mechanism. The paper uses Theorem 2 to motivate its covariance-aware interventions: since PPO with clipping approximates a trust-region update similar to NPG, the relevant covariance for entropy control is between log-probability and raw advantage — which is what Equation 10 computes. This connection is explicitly noted in Section 4.2: "Natural policy gradient is rarely used in post-training of LLMs because of its time-consuming second-order optimization. But its introduction of target function with KL distance as constraint share a similar idea with TRPO and PPO. For this reason, we apply Theorem 2 into algorithms like PPO later in this section."

**Step 3: Empirical validation of the theory (Figure 8).**

The theoretical derivation produces a testable prediction: if we compute the covariance term `$\text{Cov}_{a}(\log \pi_\theta(a), \pi_\theta(a) \cdot A(a))$` on actual training data and compare it to the negative step-wise entropy difference `$-(H^{k+1} - H^k)$`, the two curves should track each other closely.

To test this, the paper runs on-policy GRPO (without PPO clipping, to match the pure policy gradient assumption) on Qwen2.5-7B and computes both quantities per training batch. Since the policy generates entire sequences rather than individual actions at isolated states, the paper adopts a **bandit setting**: the entire prompt `$x$` is the state, and the entire response `$y$` is a single action. The covariance is computed at the response level:

$$\text{Cov}_{y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot|x)} \left( \log \pi_\theta(y|x), \pi_\theta(y|x) \cdot A(y, x) \right)$$

where `$\log \pi_\theta(y|x)$` is the length-normalized log-probability of the full response: `$\log \pi_\theta(y|x) = \frac{1}{|y|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y|} \log \pi_\theta(y_t | y_{<t}, x)$`. This normalization ensures that response length does not confound the covariance measurement.

**Key empirical findings from Figure 8:**

1. **The covariance term and negative entropy difference closely match.** The left panel of Figure 8 shows both curves over 2000 training steps. They rise and fall together, with similar magnitudes and turning points. This provides strong support for Theorem 1 — the theoretical relationship between covariance and entropy change holds in practice for LLM policy gradient training.

2. **Covariance is positive throughout training.** The covariance curve stays above zero for the entire training run, never crossing into negative territory. This explains the monotonic entropy decrease: if covariance were sometimes negative, entropy would occasionally increase, but since it remains positive, entropy is always being driven downward.

3. **Covariance is highest early and decays over time.** The initial spike in covariance coincides with the rapid entropy drop seen in the first ~200 steps (cf. Figure 2). As training progresses, covariance shrinks but remains positive, corresponding to the slower, grinding entropy decrease in later stages. This matches the intuition: early in training, the policy's high-probability actions are well-calibrated to actual advantage (learned from pretraining), producing strong positive covariance. As the policy specializes, the alignment between probability and advantage weakens (the model has already exploited the obvious patterns), but does not reverse.

4. **Covariance varies with problem difficulty.** The right panel of Figure 8 bins training prompts by accuracy (into three groups: accuracy ~0.125, ~0.5, ~0.875). The easiest prompts (highest accuracy) show the highest covariance, while the hardest prompts show near-zero covariance. This makes sense: on easy problems, the model is confident and correct, so high-probability actions genuinely have high advantages (positive covariance). On hard problems, the model is uncertain and its confidence is poorly calibrated to actual outcomes, so there is little covariance. This means the entropy collapse is primarily driven by the easy problems where the model's existing knowledge suffices — the model over-exploits on problems it already partially knows how to solve, and this over-exploitation spills over to reduce exploration on harder problems where it needs to discover new strategies.

#### The Covariance-Aware Interventions: Clip-Cov and KL-Cov

The theoretical analysis answers *why* entropy collapses (persistent positive covariance between action probability and advantage) and identifies *where* the collapse originates (high-covariance tokens). This naturally motivates a targeted intervention: rather than adding a uniform entropy bonus to all actions or penalizing deviation from a reference model, **selectively suppress the gradient contribution of tokens with the highest covariance**. These outlier tokens are disproportionately responsible for driving entropy down (Table 1 shows that the top 0.02% of tokens by covariance have a mean covariance of 5.654, compared to 0.003 for the average token — over 1800× larger).

**Step 1: Computing token-wise covariance (Equation 10).**

The interventions operate at the token level within individual rollout responses. For a batch of `$N$` total tokens across all sampled responses (across all prompts), the token-wise centered cross-product is:

$$\text{Cov}(y_i) = \left( \log \pi_\theta(y_i) - \frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^N \log \pi_\theta(y_j) \right) \cdot \left( A(y_i) - \frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^N A(y_j) \right)$$

where `$y_i$` is the `$i$`-th token in the batch (with `$i$` indexing across all tokens in all responses, not just within one sequence), `$\log \pi_\theta(y_i)$` is the log-probability the current policy assigns to this token given its prefix, and `$A(y_i)$` is the advantage for the entire response that this token belongs to (since GRPO provides response-level advantages, all tokens in the same response share the same advantage value).

**What it computes:** This is the token's contribution to the batch-level covariance. Each token's log-probability is centered by subtracting the batch mean log-probability. Each token's advantage is centered by subtracting the batch mean advantage. The product of these centered values measures whether this token's log-probability and advantage deviate from their means in the same direction (positive product = positive contribution to covariance) or opposite directions (negative product = negative contribution). The expectation of this quantity across the batch equals the covariance in Theorem 2 (since no `$\pi_\theta$` weighting, following the natural policy gradient formulation).

**Why this form uses Theorem 2 rather than Theorem 1:** The paper explicitly adopts the NPG covariance (log-probability vs. raw advantage) rather than the PG covariance (log-probability vs. `$\pi_\theta \cdot A$`). The reasoning is that PPO's clipping mechanism approximates a trust-region constraint similar to NPG, so the relevant quantity for entropy dynamics under the actual training algorithm (PPO with GRPO advantages) is better captured by the unweighted advantage. This is a design choice motivated by the algorithmic context rather than a purely theoretical claim.

**Step 2: Clip-Cov — randomly detaching high-covariance tokens (Equations 11-12).**

Clip-Cov prevents a small fraction of the highest-covariance tokens from contributing to the policy gradient update. The procedure has three sub-steps:

First, a subset of tokens is identified based on covariance thresholds:

$$I_{\text{clip}} = \{ i \sim \text{Uniform} \left( \{ i \;|\; \text{Cov}(y_i) \in [\omega_{\text{low}}, \omega_{\text{high}}] \}, \; \lfloor r \cdot N \rfloor \right) \}$$

where `$r$` is the **clip ratio** (the fraction of all tokens to be detached, typically `$2 \times 10^{-4}$` or 0.02% of tokens), `$\omega_{\text{low}}$` is the lower bound on covariance for selection (set to 1 in experiments), and `$\omega_{\text{high}}$` is the upper bound (set to 5 in experiments).

**What this does:** From all tokens in the batch whose covariance falls in the interval `$[1, 5]$`, a random subset of size `$\lfloor r \cdot N \rfloor$` is selected. The selection is uniform — every token in the interval has equal probability of being chosen, regardless of whether its covariance is 1.1 or 4.9. The interval `$[1, 5]$` is chosen because these values far exceed the average covariance (~0.003, per Table 1), targeting truly outlier tokens that disproportionately drive entropy decrease. The randomness in selection (as opposed to deterministic top-k) is a deliberate choice to avoid introducing systematic bias — it means any token with covariance above 1 has some chance of being suppressed, but not all such tokens are suppressed, preventing the policy from learning to game the selection criterion.

Second, the selected tokens are detached from gradient computation in the PPO loss:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{Clip-Cov}}(\theta) = \begin{cases} \mathbb{E}_t \left[ \frac{\pi_\theta(y_t|y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t|y_{<t})} A_t \right], & t \notin I_{\text{clip}} \\ 0, & t \in I_{\text{clip}} \end{cases}$$

where `$t$` indexes tokens by their position within their response sequence, and each token position `$t$` uniquely maps to a batch index `$i$` in `$I_{\text{clip}}$`.

**What this computes:** For the vast majority of tokens (99.98% at the default ratio), the standard PPO loss is applied — the importance-sampled ratio times advantage determines the gradient. For the selected high-covariance tokens, the loss contribution is set to zero, so they contribute nothing to the parameter update. This is equivalent to temporarily removing these tokens from the training batch.

**Why this form:** Clip-Cov is simpler than the standard PPO clipping — it does not apply the `$\text{min}(\text{ratio} \cdot A, \text{clip}(\text{ratio}, 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon) \cdot A)$` formulation to the detached tokens, because the point is to prevent them from having any effect at all, not to constrain their effect. The paper notes that this simplification (omitting the min over clipped and unclipped objectives) is motivated by the desire to directly stop entropy-driving tokens rather than merely dampening them.

The **default hyperparameters** for Clip-Cov are: `$r = 2 \times 10^{-4}$`, `$\omega_{\text{low}} = 1$`, `$\omega_{\text{high}} = 5$`. With a batch size of `$N = 2048$` prompts × 8 responses × average response length, this means only a few dozen tokens are detached per update — a tiny fraction of the total gradient computation.

**Step 3: KL-Cov — applying KL penalty to top-covariance tokens (Equations 13-14).**

KL-Cov takes a different approach: instead of removing high-covariance tokens from the gradient, it penalizes the policy for changing too much on those specific tokens. The procedure:

First, identify the top-k proportion of tokens by covariance:

$$I_{\text{KL}} = \{ i \;|\; \text{Rank}(\text{Cov}(y_i)) \leq k \cdot N \}$$

where `$k$` is the **penalty proportion** — the fraction of tokens that will receive the KL penalty (set to `$2 \times 10^{-3}$` for Qwen2.5-7B and `$2 \times 10^{-4}$` for Qwen2.5-32B), and `$\text{Rank}$` sorts tokens in descending order of covariance (rank 1 = highest covariance).

**What this does:** Unlike Clip-Cov's random selection from an interval, KL-Cov deterministically selects the `$k \cdot N$` tokens with the absolute largest covariance values. These are the tokens most responsible for the batch-level entropy decrease, and they receive an additional penalty in the loss.

Second, the PPO loss is augmented with a KL penalty specifically for these tokens:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{KL-Cov}}(\theta) = \begin{cases} \mathbb{E}_t \left[ \frac{\pi_\theta(y_t|y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t|y_{<t})} A_t \right], & t \notin I_{\text{KL}} \\ \mathbb{E}_t \left[ \frac{\pi_\theta(y_t|y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t|y_{<t})} A_t - \beta \; D_{\text{KL}}\left(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t|y_{<t}) \;||\; \pi_\theta(y_t|y_{<t})\right) \right], & t \in I_{\text{KL}} \end{cases}$$

where `$\beta$` is the KL penalty coefficient (set to 1 in experiments), and `$D_{\text{KL}}(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}} || \pi_\theta) = \sum_{a} \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a|y_{<t}) \log\frac{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a|y_{<t})}{\pi_\theta(a|y_{<t})}$` is the forward KL divergence from the old policy to the current policy for this token position.

**What this computes:** For the top-`$k$` fraction of tokens (those with highest covariance), the loss includes an additional term `$\beta \cdot D_{\text{KL}}$` that penalizes the current policy for diverging too far from the old policy (the policy that generated the rollouts). The forward KL direction (`$\pi_{\text{old}}$` as the reference, `$\pi_\theta$` as the comparison) means the penalty is large when the current policy assigns low probability to tokens the old policy assigned high probability to — i.e., when the model is "forgetting" its previous knowledge by reducing probability on previously favored tokens. For the remaining majority of tokens, the standard PPO loss applies.

**Why this form:** The KL penalty serves a similar purpose to PPO's clipping — it prevents the policy from changing too much in a single update. But by applying it only to the highest-covariance tokens, KL-Cov targets the specific tokens where policy change would most reduce entropy, while leaving low-covariance tokens free to update normally. This is more surgical than a global KL penalty to a reference model (which, per Figure 10, degrades performance by constraining all tokens equally). The choice of forward KL (as opposed to reverse KL `$D_{\text{KL}}(\pi_\theta || \pi_{\text{old}})$`, which would penalize the policy for putting probability on *new* tokens the old policy ignored) is deliberate: the goal is to prevent the policy from abandoning its existing diverse distribution, not to prevent it from exploring new tokens. This is consistent with the entropy-control philosophy: maintain diversity by preserving existing probability mass, not by forcing model to be uniform.

The **default hyperparameters** for KL-Cov are: `$k = 2 \times 10^{-3}$` (0.2% of tokens) for 7B models and `$k = 2 \times 10^{-4}$` (0.02% of tokens) for 32B models, with `$\beta = 1$`. The different `$k$` values for different model sizes reflect that larger models have larger vocabularies and longer responses, so the same absolute number of outlier tokens corresponds to a smaller fraction of total tokens.

#### Pseudocode Implementation (Listing 1)

The paper provides a concrete implementation sketch showing that both methods require modifying only a few lines of the policy loss computation. I'll walk through the logic structure rather than reproducing the pseudocode verbatim.

The function `compute_policy_loss` takes `old_log_prob` (log-probabilities of tokens under the rollout policy), `log_prob` (log-probabilities under the current policy), `advantages` (response-level advantages from GRPO), and method-specific parameters.

**Common computation:** The importance sampling ratio `$\text{ratio} = \exp(\text{log_prob} - \text{old_log_prob})$` gives `$\pi_\theta / \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$`. The preliminary policy gradient loss is `$\text{pg_losses1} = -\text{ratio} \times \text{advantages}$` (negative because gradient descent minimizes loss, while we want to maximize reward). The token-wise covariance is computed as `$\text{covs} = (\text{log_prob} - \text{mean}(\text{log_prob})) \times (\text{advantages} - \text{mean}(\text{advantages}))$`.

**For Clip-Cov:** A second loss array `$\text{pg_losses2} = -\text{clip}(\text{ratio, clip_range_lb, clip_range_ub}) \times \text{advantages}$` computes the clipped version. An index set `clip_idx` is formed by randomly selecting `select_num` tokens from those whose covariance falls within `[cov_lb, cov_ub]`. Both `pg_losses1[clip_idx]` and `pg_losses2[clip_idx]` are detached (`.detach_()`), meaning their gradient contributions are zeroed. The final loss is `$\text{max}(\text{pg_losses1}, \text{pg_losses2}).\text{mean}()$`, which is the standard PPO min-of-surrogate formulation, but with high-covariance tokens excluded from both terms.

**For KL-Cov:** The KL penalty is approximated as `$\text{kl_penalty} = |\text{log_prob} - \text{old_log_prob}|$` (the absolute difference in log-probabilities, which is a first-order approximation to the KL divergence between categorical distributions — this is computationally cheaper than computing the full sum over the vocabulary). The top-`$k$` tokens by covariance are identified via `$\text{select_idx} = \text{topk}(\text{covs}, k=\text{select_num}, \text{largest}=\text{True})$`. The policy gradient loss for these selected tokens is augmented: `$\text{pg_losses1}[\text{select_idx}] \;+\!\!= \;\text{kl_coef} \times \text{kl_penalty}[\text{select_idx}]$`. The final loss is `$\text{pg_losses1.mean}()$`.

**What this implementation reveals:** Both methods are extremely lightweight. Computing token-wise covariance requires only element-wise operations on existing tensors (log-probabilities and advantages are already computed for the policy gradient). The selection step is a simple thresholding or top-k operation. The gradient modification is a single `.detach_()` call or an addition to the loss tensor. No additional forward passes, no auxiliary models, no extra memory beyond storing the covariance vector. This is a key practical advantage: the overhead is negligible relative to the cost of generating rollouts and computing the base policy gradient.

#### Connection to Existing Techniques and Design Rationale

**Why covariance-aware rather than entropy-aware?** A natural alternative would be to directly penalize tokens that decrease entropy, using the theoretically derived quantity `$\log\pi_\theta(a|s) \cdot \pi_\theta(a|s) \cdot A(s,a)$` (from Theorem 1) as a per-token weight in the loss. The paper's choice to use the simpler covariance `$(\log\pi_\theta - \bar{\log\pi_\theta}) \cdot (A - \bar{A})$` (from Theorem 2) is motivated by the NPG/PPO connection discussed above, but also by computational simplicity — covariance requires only centered products, whereas the PG form requires multiplying by `$\pi_\theta$` which can be numerically unstable for very small probabilities. The covariance form also has the property that it automatically adapts to the batch-level statistics: a token's covariance is measured relative to the mean log-probability and mean advantage in the current batch, which provides a natural normalization.

**Why intervene on only ~0.02% of tokens?** Table 1 shows that the covariance distribution is extremely heavy-tailed: the top 0.02% of tokens have a mean covariance over 1800× the global mean. This suggests that entropy collapse is driven by a tiny minority of outlier tokens — the "pivotal tokens" that the paper refers to in Section 4.5. Intervening on 0.02% of tokens is sufficient to dramatically change the entropy curve while leaving 99.98% of the gradient update unchanged, preserving the beneficial exploitation that RL is supposed to do. Intervening on more tokens (e.g., the top 2% or 20%) would start to impair learning on tokens where the positive covariance is actually correct and desirable.

**Why does the clip ratio/intervention fraction differ between 7B and 32B models?** The paper uses `$r = 2 \times 10^{-4}$` for Clip-Cov on both model sizes, but `$k = 2 \times 10^{-3}$` (7B) vs. `$k = 2 \times 10^{-4}$` (32B) for KL-Cov. The authors do not provide a detailed justification for these choices, but the pattern is consistent with larger models requiring less intervention — perhaps because larger models have inherently more diverse output distributions (higher initial entropy) and thus need less aggressive entropy preservation.

**Relationship to PPO clipping and clip-higher.** Standard PPO clips the importance ratio to `$[1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon]$` with `$\epsilon = 0.2$`. This clipping applies uniformly to all tokens. The clip-higher technique (Yu et al., 2025) raises only the upper threshold to 0.28, which means tokens whose probability increases (ratio > 1) can increase more before being clipped. Since these increases are driven by positive advantages, clip-higher effectively allows the model to *more aggressively* increase probability on tokens it already favors (high probability, positive advantage) — which, per Theorem 1, are exactly the tokens that decrease entropy. So clip-higher slightly worsens entropy collapse in exchange for faster initial learning. Clip-Cov and KL-Cov take the opposite approach: they *restrict* updates on high-covariance tokens, which counteracts entropy collapse. The paper's experiments show that clip-higher provides early gains but becomes unstable (Figure 11), while the covariance-aware methods provide sustained improvement.

**Why does the method work better on larger models?** Table 2 shows gains of 2.0% on average for 7B models vs. 6.4% for 32B models, with particularly dramatic improvements on AIME (15% and 14.6% absolute gains on AIME24 and AIME25 for the 32B model). The paper's interpretation (Section 4.3): "We infer that this is because the 32B model possesses greater potential from pretraining compared to the 7B model. Once the 'exploration curse' caused by entropy collapse is lifted, the 32B model is able to explore more diverse and higher-quality policies." In other words, entropy collapse hurts larger models more because they have more latent capabilities that can only be extracted through exploration — a 7B model may simply lack the capacity to solve AIME problems regardless of exploration, so preventing entropy collapse doesn't help as much. This aligns with the finding that `$a$` scales with model size (Figure 7): larger models can convert more entropy into performance, so preserving entropy gives them more "fuel" to burn.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Reframing Entropy Collapse as a Predictable, Law-like Phenomenon Rather Than an Occasional Instability

The field's prior engagement with policy entropy in LLM training was largely anecdotal and inconsistent. Some works observed entropy declines and added entropy bonuses as a generic countermeasure (Mnih et al., 2016; Schulman et al., 2017b); others simply omitted entropy regularization without systematic justification (Cui et al., 2025; Hu et al., 2025); and a few reported distinct entropy patterns under different training configurations (Luo et al., 2025; Yan et al., 2025). There was no consensus on whether entropy collapse was universal, when it occurred, or what its consequences were. The default assumption, inherited from classical RL, was that entropy regularization is "nice to have" but not essential — something to tune if training becomes unstable, not a first-class diagnostic of whether training will succeed at all.

This paper fundamentally elevates entropy from a secondary training metric to a **primary, law-like variable** that governs the trajectory of RL for reasoning LLMs. The contribution is not the observation that entropy decreases — many practitioners have seen this in their logs — but the demonstration that entropy and downstream performance are linked by a precise, simple functional form `R = -a exp H + b` that holds with remarkable consistency across 4 model families, 11 models (0.5B to 32B parameters), two task domains (math and coding), and 4 RL algorithms (GRPO, RLOO, PRIME, REINFORCE++). This is established in Figures 3 and 4, where 200+ data points per run are fit with only two parameters.

The power of this reframing is threefold. First, it makes entropy collapse **quantitatively predictable**: given the coefficients `a` and `b` (which the paper shows scale log-linearly with model size within a family; Figure 7), one can compute the exact validation accuracy the model will achieve at any entropy level — including the ceiling `b - a` at `H = 0`. This transforms entropy from a "hmm, that looks low" qualitative concern into a precise forecast of where training will plateau. Second, it reveals that the entropy-performance tradeoff is **not algorithm-specific** (Figure 6): GRPO, RLOO, PRIME, and REINFORCE++ all trace the same curve for the same model-data pair. This means the relationship is a property of the model's pretrained knowledge interacting with the training data, not an artifact of how advantages happen to be estimated. Third, it enables **early-stopping decisions**: Figure 5 shows that fitting the curve from the first 15% of training steps predicts final performance with 0.5–1.9% RMSE, meaning one can decide whether a run is worth continuing before most of the compute budget is spent.

This is not an incremental extension of existing entropy regularization work — it is a qualitatively different way of thinking about entropy. Rather than treating entropy as a knob to be tuned (add a coefficient, see if it helps), the paper treats it as a **state variable** of the training process, analogous to how neural scaling laws treat loss as a function of compute. The entropy-performance law provides the same kind of predictive and diagnostic power for RL training that scaling laws provide for pretraining: it says "given this model and this data, this is the best you can do, and here is exactly how much entropy you will consume to get there." The fact that the coefficients `a` and `b` are model-and-data-specific but algorithm-independent further reinforces this framing — they are **intrinsic properties** of the policy-data pair, like critical exponents in a physical system, not hyperparameters to be optimized.

### Innovation 2: A Mechanistic Theory of Entropy Dynamics That Explains *Why* Collapse Happens and Identifies the Causal Driver

Prior to this work, if you asked an RL practitioner why policy entropy decreases during LLM training, the best answer would have been vague: "the model becomes more confident as it learns" or "the policy concentrates on high-reward actions." These are descriptions of what happens, not explanations of the causal mechanism. Existing RL theory (Agarwal et al., 2021) provided convergence guarantees for policy gradient methods but did not characterize the *dynamics* of entropy specifically, nor did it identify what properties of the training data or advantage estimates drive entropy change. The maximum entropy RL literature (Ziebart et al., 2008; Haarnoja et al., 2018) added entropy bonuses to objectives but did not derive how entropy evolves under standard (non-entropy-regularized) policy gradient — it treated entropy as something to be externally maintained, not as a quantity with its own internal dynamics.

This paper provides exactly such a mechanistic account, and its central finding is that **entropy change is driven by the covariance between action log-probability and advantage**. This emerges from the mathematical structure of softmax policies (Lemma 1, Appendix E.2), which shows that the Taylor expansion of entropy change equals (negative) covariance between log-probability and logit change. Under Policy Gradient, logit change is proportional to `πθ(a|s) · A(s,a)` (Proposition 1), giving the covariance form in Theorem 1. Under Natural Policy Gradient, the `πθ` factor cancels, giving the cleaner covariance between log-probability and raw advantage (Theorem 2).

The significance of this finding is not the equations themselves — Lemma 1 is adapted from Liu (2025), and the derivations are standard applications of the policy gradient theorem for tabular softmax policies — but what the equations **reveal about the training dynamics that was previously invisible**. The theory makes a specific, falsifiable prediction: the step-wise entropy difference and the covariance term should track each other closely. Figure 8 (left) validates this: the two curves match in shape, timing, and magnitude across the entire training run. More importantly, the theory explains *persistent* entropy decrease: covariance remains positive throughout training (Figure 8, left), meaning high-probability actions consistently receive positive advantages, so each gradient step pushes entropy downward. This is not a failure of calibration — early in training, high-probability actions genuinely are better-than-average (Figure 8, right, showing higher covariance on easier problems) — but the cumulative effect is to starve the policy of the exploration needed to solve harder problems.

This mechanistic understanding is what makes the paper's interventions (Clip-Cov, KL-Cov) fundamentally different from prior entropy regularization approaches. A generic entropy bonus (Figure 9) pushes *all* tokens toward higher entropy, regardless of whether they are driving collapse or not — hence its extreme sensitivity to the coefficient. A KL penalty to a reference model (Figure 10) constrains *all* tokens toward a fixed distribution, preventing both harmful overconfidence and beneficial specialization. By contrast, the covariance theory identifies a **specific causal agent** — the small fraction of tokens where log-probability and advantage have strongly aligned positive deviations (Table 1: top 0.02% of tokens have ~1800× the average covariance) — and enables intervention that is both precise (only ~0.02% of tokens affected) and principled (based on the derived entropy dynamics, not an ad hoc regularizer).

This represents a shift from **regularization-as-medicine** (add a penalty term to counteract an undesirable dynamic) to **mechanism-based control** (identify and suppress the causal driver of the undesirable dynamic). The former is always brittle because the regularization fights against the natural gradient dynamics; the latter is more robust because it directly addresses the cause. The paper's finding that both Clip-Cov and KL-Cov maintain stable entropy curves throughout training (Figure 11) while clip-higher — which increases entropy indirectly by relaxing PPO's upper clip — becomes unstable and eventually declines, is evidence for the superiority of mechanism-based over regularization-based approaches.

### Innovation 3: The Concept of "Pivotal Tokens" — Entropy Collapse Is Driven by a Tiny Minority of Outlier Tokens

A striking empirical finding that emerges from the covariance analysis is the extreme concentration of entropy-driving influence. Table 1 shows that the top 0.02% of tokens by covariance have a mean covariance of 5.654, compared to 0.003 for the aggregate mean — a factor of over 1800×. Even the top 2% (mean 1.385) are ~450× the global average. This means that the entropy collapse observed across entire training runs — the 73% entropy loss in the first 200 steps that the paper documents in Figure 2 — is disproportionately driven by a handful of tokens per batch.

The paper does not dwell on this point theoretically, but it is a significant empirical insight with practical implications. It explains why Clip-Cov and KL-Cov can be so effective while touching only `r = 2 × 10^{-4}` (0.02%) of tokens: they are not randomly suppressing 0.02% of the gradient; they are selectively targeting the tokens that would otherwise dominate the entropy dynamics. It also explains why generic entropy regularization fails: an entropy bonus applied uniformly to all tokens must be large enough to counteract these extreme outliers, but at that magnitude it destabilizes the millions of normal tokens where the policy's confidence is well-calibrated and beneficial.

This concept of "pivotal tokens" — tokens whose gradient contributions have outsized influence on a macro-level training property (entropy) — is novel in the context of LLM RL. It suggests that the policy's output distribution has a heavy-tailed structure where a small fraction of token positions (likely those where the model is both very confident and correct, such as common function words in correct solutions or key mathematical operators in successful derivations) dominate the reduction in exploratory capacity. The paper explicitly notes this in Section 4.5: "This means several 'pivotal' tokens are crucial for the entropy of LLMs." This opens up a new axis for understanding and controlling LLM training dynamics — not through global hyperparameters or uniform regularizers, but through identifying and selectively modulating the small number of gradient contributions that disproportionately shape the learning trajectory.

### Innovation 4: An Empirical Demonstration That the Entropy-Governed Ceiling Is Not Intrinsic to RL — It Can Be Broken Through Mechanistic Intervention

A pessimistic reading of the entropy-performance law `R = -a exp H + b` is that RL for LLM reasoning has a hard, predictable ceiling determined by the base model and training data: when entropy reaches zero, performance saturates at `b - a`, and no amount of additional training compute can push past this bound. This would imply that RL is fundamentally an elicitation process — it extracts what the pretrained model already knows but cannot create genuinely new capabilities (a view argued by Yue et al., 2025, among others).

The paper's intervention results provide a compelling counterargument. Clip-Cov and KL-Cov do not merely slow down the approach to the ceiling — they **raise the ceiling itself**. Figure 11 shows that while the baseline GRPO policy entropy plateaus near zero and validation accuracy saturates, the covariance-controlled runs maintain entropy levels 10× higher throughout training and continue to improve in accuracy beyond the baseline's saturation point. For the 32B model, the gains are dramatic: 15% absolute improvement on AIME24 and 14.6% on AIME25 (Table 2). These are not marginal gains from slightly better hyperparameter tuning — they represent the model solving problems that the baseline's converged policy could not touch.

The significance of this finding is that it **decouples the entropy-performance relationship from a fixed ceiling**. The law `R = -a exp H + b` describes the dynamics *when entropy is allowed to collapse*, but the collapse itself is not inevitable — it can be prevented by suppressing high-covariance tokens. Once prevented, the model can continue to explore and improve beyond the original ceiling. This suggests that the ceiling `b - a` is not an absolute limit of the base model's capabilities, but rather a consequence of the standard RL training dynamics that prematurely extinguish exploration before the model can learn to solve harder problems. With proper entropy management, RL can do more than elicit existing knowledge — it can enable the model to compose its pretrained capabilities in ways that never appeared in training, effectively creating new reasoning strategies.

This finding also provides a perspective on *why* the gains are larger for larger models (2.0% average for 7B vs. 6.4% for 32B). Larger models have more latent capabilities from pretraining — a broader space of possible reasoning strategies they could in principle discover — but this broader space is also more vulnerable to premature collapse because the model's initial confidence is spread across more options. Preserving entropy allows the larger model to explore more of this latent space and find strategies the baseline never discovered. This aligns with the scaling of coefficients in Figure 7: larger models have larger `a` (they can convert more entropy into performance), so denying them that entropy by letting it collapse leaves more potential performance on the table.

This is not merely an empirical claim about two new techniques (Clip-Cov and KL-Cov). It is a **proof of concept** for a broader principle: that mechanistic understanding of training dynamics enables interventions that overcome apparent ceilings. The paper does not claim that Clip-Cov and KL-Cov are the optimal or final methods for entropy control — Section 4.5 explicitly notes that "it still remains open whether there exists an optimal entropy value to balance the exploration and training stability." Rather, it demonstrates that the ceiling described by `b - a` is not a law of nature but an artifact of a specific, identifiable, and correctable dynamic (positive covariance between high-probability actions and advantages). This reframes the entire enterprise of scaling RL for LLMs: the bottleneck is not the model's capacity or the training data's coverage, but the systematic loss of exploratory ability during training, which can be addressed through principled, lightweight modifications to the policy update.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The primary training and evaluation tasks are mathematical reasoning and coding. For math, the evaluation datasets include MATH500 (Hendrycks et al., 2021), AIME 2024 and AIME 2025 (Li et al., 2024), AMC (Li et al., 2024), OlympiadBench (He et al., 2024), OMNI-MATH (Gao et al., 2024), and Minerva (Lewkowycz et al., 2022). For code, the test sets are drawn from Eurus-2-RL-Code (Cui et al., 2025) and KodCode (Xu et al., 2025). Due to inherent differences in the initial reasoning abilities between model families, the training data difficulty level is adjusted per family to stabilize the RL process — for example, Qwen2.5 and Mistral-24B use Eurus-2-RL-Math for math training, while other families use GSM8K (Cobbe et al., 2021); these details are enumerated in Appendix A.

- **Base model(s).** The paper spans 4 model families and 11 base models: the Qwen2.5 family (0.5B, 1.5B, 3B, 7B, 32B parameters; Qwen et al., 2025), the Mistral family (Mistral-7B-v0.3, Mistral-Nemo-Base-2407, Mistral-Small-3.1-24B-Base-2501; Jiang et al., 2023), the LLaMA family (LLaMA3.2-3B, LLaMA3.1-8B; Meta, 2024), and DeepSeek-Math-7B-Base (Shao et al., 2024). This breadth is chosen to demonstrate that the entropy collapse phenomenon and the entropy-performance law are not idiosyncratic to a single architecture or scale — the paper explicitly states it aims to show universality across "widely used open-source base models" (Section 2.2). All RL runs start from base models in the "Zero" setting (DeepSeek-AI et al., 2025), meaning no supervised fine-tuning on reasoning traces precedes the RL phase.

- **Metrics.** The core metric for the entropy-performance law is the pair `(H, R)` recorded throughout training. Policy entropy `H(πθ, D)` is computed as the average token-level entropy on training batches: `H = -1/|D| ∑_{x∈D} 1/|y| ∑_{t=1}^{|y|} E_{y_t~πθ}[log πθ(y_t | y_{<t}, x)]` (Equation 5). Validation performance `R` is the accuracy (percentage of correct answers) on the held-out evaluation benchmarks listed above. For the covariance validation experiments (Section 3.3), the paper tracks the step-wise entropy difference `H(πθ^{k+1}) - H(πθ^k)` and the covariance term from Theorem 1, using length-normalized log-probabilities for responses. For intervention experiments (Section 4.3), the metrics are test-set accuracies per benchmark plus training dynamics (entropy curves, response length, and average test accuracy across all benchmarks).

- **Baselines.** The primary baseline is vanilla GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) with the default PPO clipping threshold `ε = 0.2` and no entropy regularization or reference KL penalty. The paper also compares against **GRPO with clip-higher**, which raises the upper clipping threshold to 0.28 following the DAPO recipe (Yu et al., 2025) — this is the strongest published baseline for entropy-aware GRPO modifications. For the entropy regularization ablation (Section 4.1), the baselines include standard entropy loss addition `L_ent = L - αH(πθ)` with coefficients `α ∈ {0.0001, 0.001, 0.005, 0.01}` and KL penalty to a reference model `L_KL = L + β D_KL(πθ || π_ref)` with coefficients `β ∈ {0.001, 0.01, 0.05, 0.1}`. For the algorithm-independence analysis (Figure 6), the paper also runs RLOO (Kool et al., 2019; Ahmadian et al., 2024), PRIME (Cui et al., 2025), and REINFORCE++ (Hu, 2025) on Qwen2.5-7B.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper does not express test-time compute in FLOPs; rather, it operates at the training-compute level. The standard protocol (Section 2.2) uses a batch size of 256 prompts, with 8 sampled responses per prompt (`K = 8`), and performs multiple policy update epochs per rollout batch (typically 1 rollout followed by 8 policy updates, following standard PPO/GRPO practice). The maximum generation length is 8192 tokens. For evaluation on AIME and AMC, the rollout temperature is set to 0.6; for all other test sets, greedy decoding is used. The paper measures training progress in gradient steps — typical runs span 2,400 steps (e.g., Figure 2) or until convergence.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The entropy-performance fitting (Figures 3-4, 6) uses all recorded `(H, R)` pairs across the entire training run — typically 200+ data points per model — and fits the two-parameter exponential `R = -a exp(H) + b` to these points. The quality of fit is assessed visually and by the ability to predict late-stage performance from early-stage data (Figure 5), where the function is fit using only the first 36 training steps and used to predict the next 200 steps, with RMSE reported. For the intervention experiments (Table 2), the paper reports results averaged across the full set of evaluation benchmarks, but does not specify whether multiple random seeds are used or confidence intervals are computed — a notable omission. The difficulty-based covariance analysis (Figure 8, right) bins training examples into three groups based on their accuracy (0.125, 0.5, 0.875), using the group-based sampling structure inherent to GRPO (8 responses per prompt, enabling per-prompt accuracy estimation).

### Main Quantitative Results

#### The Entropy Collapse Phenomenon and Its Universality (Section 2.3)

The paper's first empirical finding is that across 11 different RL runs spanning four model families and sizes from 0.5B to 32B, policy entropy consistently drops sharply and monotonically toward zero. Figure 2 aggregates these runs and shows that **73% of the total entropy loss and 76% of the total performance gain occur in the first 200 gradient steps** (out of 2400 total steps). By step 800 — one-third of training — over 93% of performance gains and 94% of entropy losses have already been realized. The remaining 1600 steps yield marginal improvement. The absolute entropy values (shown in Figure 11, left panels) drop from initial levels of approximately 0.5–0.7 nats (varying by model) to below 0.05 nats, approaching zero.

This phenomenon is not stated as a percentage relative to some maximum possible entropy (which would be `log(|V|)`, where `|V|` is the vocabulary size, typically ~10–15 nats for modern tokenizers). Rather, the paper reports **absolute entropy values** on the training data distribution, which starts far below the theoretical maximum because pretrained models already concentrate probability. The "93% of entropy losses" in Figure 2 refers to the fraction of the total observed entropy decrease during training, not 93% of the vocabulary-level maximum entropy.

#### The Entropy-Performance Fitting Law (Section 2.4)

The central empirical contribution is the discovery that across all experimental configurations, the relationship between policy entropy `H` and validation performance `R` is well-approximated by the two-parameter exponential:

> `R = -a exp(H) + b`

as shown in Figures 3 and 4. Specific fitting results include:

- **Qwen2.5 family (math, Figure 3, left):** The 0.5B model starts at entropy ~2.0 and accuracy ~5%, rising to ~12% at `H ≈ 0`. The 32B model starts at entropy ~4.0 and accuracy ~15%, rising to ~42% at convergence. All intermediate sizes fall on smooth curves consistent with the exponential form.

- **Mistral family (math, Figure 3, middle):** The Mistral-Small-3-24B achieves the highest ceiling at ~30% accuracy; Mistral-7B-v0.3 peaks near 10%.

- **LLaMA and DeepSeek families (math, Figure 3, right):** DeepSeek-Math-7B-Base achieves the highest ceiling at ~16% (reflecting its math-specific pretraining); LLaMA3.2-3B peaks at ~5%.

- **Coding task (Figure 4):** The same functional form holds, with Qwen2.5-32B reaching ~42% coding accuracy, Mistral-Small-3-24B reaching ~33%, and LLaMA3.1-8B reaching ~12%.

The paper quantifies the predictive power of this law in Figure 5. Fitting the function using only the first 36 training steps (roughly 15% of the total 236 steps shown) and extrapolating yields:
- **Math task:** Average RMSE of 0.9% across all predicted steps, 0.5% RMSE for the final-step performance prediction.
- **Code task:** Average RMSE of 1.2% across all predicted steps, 1.9% RMSE for the final-step performance.

This demonstrates that the late-stage performance ceiling can be estimated very early in training, without running the full RL process.

#### Algorithm Independence of the Entropy-Performance Law (Section 2.5, Figure 6)

Figure 6 shows the fitted entropy-performance curves for Qwen2.5-7B trained on the same math data using four different RL algorithms: GRPO, RLOO, PRIME, and REINFORCE++. The curves "nearly coincide," with the paper stating that "although these algorithms apply distinct advantage estimation methods, they do not influence the fitted entropy-performance function." This is non-obvious because PRIME, in particular, uses a learned implicit process reward model for dense per-step rewards, which one might expect to produce different policy dynamics than the sparse outcome-based GRPO rewards. The result suggests the coefficients `a` and `b` are properties of the model-data pair, not the algorithm.

#### Scaling of Coefficients with Model Size (Section 2.5, Figure 7)

For the Qwen2.5 model family (0.5B through 32B), both `a` and `b` scale log-linearly with the parameter count (excluding embeddings). Figure 7 plots `log(parameters)` against `a` and `b` separately for math and coding:

- **Coefficient `a` (math):** Increases from ~0.03 (0.5B) to ~0.28 (32B) — the rate at which the model converts entropy into performance increases roughly 9× over this size range.
- **Coefficient `b` (math):** Increases from ~0.18 (0.5B) to ~0.65 (32B) — the sum of ceiling plus efficiency increases ~3.6×.
- **Coefficient `a` (code):** Increases from ~0.03 (0.5B) to ~0.28 (32B).
- **Coefficient `b` (code):** Increases from ~0.15 (0.5B) to ~0.58 (32B).

The log-linear fit is described as "smooth," enabling extrapolation: the coefficients of larger models can be predicted from training dynamics of smaller models in the same family. The paper does not report an analytical form for this log-linear relationship (e.g., `a = c_a · log(N) + d_a`), relying instead on the visual fit in Figure 7.

#### Training Data Dependence of Coefficients (Appendix C, Figure 13)

Training the same model (Qwen2.5-7B) on different datasets shifts the entropy-performance curve. Figure 13 shows curves for DAPO-MATH, GSM8K, Eurus-RL-Data, and Eurus-RL-Data-Difficult. The curves are distinct, with GSM8K (the easiest dataset) producing the highest final accuracy (~37%), and Eurus-RL-Data-Difficult producing the lowest (~20%). This confirms that `a` and `b` are data-dependent as well as model-dependent.

#### Validity of the Law for Instruct Models (Appendix D, Figure 14)

The exponential fitting law also holds when starting RL from instruct-tuned models rather than base models. Figure 14 shows Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct, 3B-Instruct, and 7B-Instruct trained on math. The curves remain well-fit by `R = -a exp(H) + b`, though the starting entropy values are lower (reflecting the narrower output distribution of instruct models).

#### Empirical Validation of the Covariance-Entropy Theory (Section 3.3, Figure 8)

The theoretical derivation (Theorem 1) predicts that the step-wise entropy change should be proportional to the negative covariance between log-probability and `πθ · advantage`. Figure 8 (left) tests this by plotting both the covariance term `Cov(log πθ, πθ · A)` and the negative entropy difference `-(H^{k+1} - H^k)` over 2000 steps of on-policy GRPO training on Qwen2.5-7B.

The key observations:

- **The two curves exhibit highly similar dynamics**, tracking each other in shape, timing of peaks, and overall magnitude. The paper does not report a quantitative correlation coefficient but states the curves "match exactly" in the caption and "exhibit highly similar dynamics" in the text.

- **Covariance is positive throughout training**, oscillating between ~0.002 and ~0.018 in the early phase, then decaying to ~0.0–0.004 after step 750. The persistent positivity explains the monotonic entropy decrease — covariance never crosses into negative territory where entropy would increase.

- **Entropy difference tracks covariance**: `-d(H)` (the negative step-wise entropy difference) starts at ~0.04 at step 0, drops rapidly to ~0.01 by step 250, and oscillates around ~0.0–0.005 for the remainder of training. The sharp initial decay in both curves matches the rapid entropy loss documented in Figure 2.

Figure 8 (right) stratifies covariance by problem difficulty. Training prompts are binned by per-prompt accuracy (computed from the 8 sampled responses): accuracy ~0.125 (hardest), ~0.5 (medium), ~0.875 (easiest). The findings:

- **Easiest prompts (accuracy ~0.875):** Covariance is highest, starting at ~0.008, rising to ~0.01, then gradually decaying. These prompts drive the majority of entropy collapse because the model is confident and correct — high-probability actions truly have high advantages.

- **Medium prompts (accuracy ~0.5):** Covariance is moderate, starting near ~0.004 and decaying to near-zero by step 1000.

- **Hardest prompts (accuracy ~0.125):** Covariance is near-zero throughout, oscillating around ~0.0. On these prompts, the model's high-probability actions are not reliably associated with positive advantages, so they contribute little to entropy dynamics.

This difficulty-dependence reveals that entropy collapse is primarily driven by the *easy* portion of the training distribution, where the model can safely exploit its existing knowledge. The exploration needed for harder problems is indirectly extinguished because the same policy parameters are shared across all prompts.

Table 1 provides statistics on the token-wise covariance distribution at training step 1 on Qwen2.5-7B:

| Top 0.2% | 3.112 |

The top 0.02% of tokens have a mean covariance ~1885× the global mean. The distribution is extremely heavy-tailed, with a small number of outlier tokens dominating the batch-level covariance (and thus, per the theory, dominating entropy decrease). The paper sets its intervention thresholds based on this: Clip-Cov selects from tokens with covariance in `[1, 5]` (well above the 0.003 mean), and KL-Cov targets the top 0.02%–0.2% of tokens.

#### Effect of Standard Entropy Regularization (Section 4.1, Figures 9-10)

The paper tests two standard entropy management approaches and finds them ineffective:

**Entropy loss (Figure 9):** Adding an entropy bonus `L_ent = L - αH(πθ)` to the GRPO objective with coefficients `α ∈ {0.0001, 0.001, 0.005, 0.01}`. With `α = 0.0001` or `α = 0.001`, the policy entropy dynamics are essentially unchanged from the baseline (`α = 0`) — entropy still collapses to near-zero. With `α = 0.01`, entropy *explodes* upward (exceeding 5.0 nats, compared to the baseline's <0.5 nats), indicating training destabilization. At the intermediate `α = 0.005`, entropy is successfully stabilized at ~1.0–2.0 nats throughout training, but **validation accuracy does not outperform the baseline** — the stabilized-entropy curve (green, Figure 9, right panel) ends at approximately the same accuracy (~37.5%) as the baseline that collapsed to near-zero entropy. The paper concludes: "entropy loss is highly sensitive to coefficients."

**KL penalty to reference model (Figure 10):** Adding a KL divergence penalty `L_KL = L + β D_KL(πθ || π_ref)` with the reference being the initial (pretrained) policy. With `β = 0.001` or `β = 0.01`, entropy still collapses (though slightly slower). With `β = 0.05` or `β = 0.1`, entropy is stabilized at ~0.2–0.4 nats, but **validation accuracy is uniformly worse** than the unregularized baseline — the `β = 0.1` curve (green) converges to ~33% accuracy versus ~37.5% for the baseline. The paper states: "Despite the reference KL achieves stable entropy values, it fails to improve policy and instead leads to a degradation in performance."

#### Entropy Controllability (Section 4.4, Figure 12)

Figure 12 demonstrates that both methods allow explicit control over policy entropy through their hyperparameters:

- **Clip-Cov (left panel):** Varying the clip ratio `r` from 2 to 7 (the paper uses `ratio` to denote a parameter controlling how many tokens are detached — the units and exact mapping to the `r` in Equation 11 are not precisely specified, but higher values correspond to more aggressive clipping). At `ratio=2`, entropy stabilizes near ~0.15 nats; at `ratio=7`, it stabilizes near ~0.55 nats. The relationship is monotonic: more clipped tokens → higher maintained entropy.

- **KL-Cov (right panel):** Varying the KL coefficient `β` from 0 to 1. At `β=0` (no penalty), entropy collapses to near-zero. At `β=0.05`, entropy stabilizes at ~0.05 nats. At `β=1`, entropy stabilizes at ~0.45 nats. KL-Cov produces "stabler entropy curves than Clip-Cov" per the paper, with less oscillation.

The key practical takeaway is that entropy can be tuned to a desired level by adjusting a single hyperparameter, without the instability seen with entropy loss coefficients. However, the paper notes in Section 4.5 that "we don't observe a relationship between the intervened entropy and model performance. It still remains open whether there exists an optimal entropy value to balance the exploration and training stability."

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

- **Entropy loss coefficient sensitivity (Figure 9):** Entropy loss is extremely brittle — `α = 0.0001` and `α = 0.001` have virtually no effect on entropy dynamics; `α = 0.01` causes entropy explosion; only `α = 0.005` stabilizes entropy, and even then it does not improve accuracy over the baseline. This ablation directly motivates the need for more targeted entropy control methods.

- **KL penalty to reference model (Figure 10):** All tested `β` values either fail to prevent entropy collapse (`β ≤ 0.01`) or degrade performance (`β ≥ 0.05`). This is a negative result showing that global KL regularization is too blunt — it constrains all tokens uniformly, preventing both harmful overconfidence and beneficial specialization.

- **Algorithm invariance of entropy-performance law (Figure 6):** Training Qwen2.5-7B with GRPO, RLOO, PRIME, and REINFORCE++ on the same data produces nearly identical `R = -a exp(H) + b` curves, validating that the coefficients are model-and-data properties, not algorithm artifacts.

- **Training data dependence of coefficients (Figure 13):** Different training datasets (DAPO-MATH, GSM8K, Eurus-RL-Data, Eurus-RL-Data-Difficult) produce distinct fitted curves for the same model (Qwen2.5-7B), confirming that `a` and `b` are data-dependent as well as model-dependent.

- **Extension to instruct models (Figure 14):** The exponential fitting holds when RL is applied to instruct-tuned Qwen2.5 variants (1.5B-Instruct, 3B-Instruct, 7B-Instruct), showing the phenomenon is not specific to base models.

- **Covariance dynamics by difficulty (Figure 8, right):** Stratifying by per-prompt accuracy reveals that easy prompts (accuracy ~0.875) drive the highest covariance, medium prompts (~0.5) show moderate covariance, and hard prompts (~0.125) show near-zero covariance. This explains why entropy collapse disproportionately arises from the easy portion of the training distribution.

- **Model size scaling of coefficients (Figure 7):** Within the Qwen2.5 family, `a` and `b` scale log-linearly with parameter count on both math and coding. This enables extrapolation of RL training outcomes from small to large models.

- **Prediction from early training (Figure 5):** Fitting the exponential law using only the first 15% of training steps predicts late-stage performance with RMSE 0.5–1.9%, an important practical capability for early stopping decisions.

- **Response length as an exploration proxy (Figure 11, middle panels):** Both Clip-Cov and KL-Cov increase average response length substantially (7B: ~800 → 1500-1800 tokens; 32B: ~1500 → 3000-4000 tokens), suggesting that entropy control enables exploration of more elaborate reasoning chains.

Notable missing ablations:

- **No sensitivity analysis for Clip-Cov's `ω_low` and `ω_high` thresholds:** The paper uses `ω_low = 1` and `ω_high = 5` for all experiments, but does not show what happens when these bounds are tightened (e.g., `[3, 5]`) or widened (`[0.5, 10]`). Given the heavy-tailed covariance distribution (Table 1), the choice of these thresholds likely matters.

- **No comparison of Clip-Cov with deterministic (non-random) selection:** Clip-Cov randomly selects tokens from the `[1, 5]` interval. The paper does not test a variant that always selects the top-`r` tokens by covariance (deterministic), making it unclear whether the random selection is important or if deterministic top-k would work equally well or better.

- **No combination of Clip-Cov and KL-Cov:** The two methods are presented as alternatives. The paper does not test whether applying both simultaneously (e.g., Clip-Cov to detach the most extreme outliers, KL-Cov to penalize the next tier) yields further gains.

- **No experiment on coding tasks with Clip-Cov/KL-Cov:** The intervention experiments are only run on math (DAPO-MATH); coding task results are absent. Given that the entropy-performance law holds for coding (Figure 4), this is a natural extension.

- **No multi-seed results:** The paper does not report standard deviations across multiple random seeds for any experiment. This is a limitation given that RL training can be noisy, and the reported gains (especially on AIME, where absolute scores are in the 10–30% range) could be sensitive to seed variation.

- **No test on model families beyond Qwen2.5 for interventions:** While the entropy-performance law is validated on Mistral, LLaMA, and DeepSeek families, the Clip-Cov and KL-Cov experiments are run only on Qwen2.5-7B and Qwen2.5-32B. Cross-family validation of the interventions is absent.

### Critical Assessment

**Claim: "Policy entropy encounters a sharp drop at the very beginning of training, and it keeps declining monotonically to near zero — a universal pattern."** The evidence for universality comes from 11 models across 4 families (Figure 2, Figures 3-4). However, the paper acknowledges in Section 2.6 that "other works that adopt different policy models or use off-policy data observed distinct entropy patterns." This is an honest admission that the "universality" claim is qualified: it holds under the specific training regime studied (on-policy RL from base models with verifiable rewards, the "Zero" setting). Under off-policy training, multi-stage curricula, or when starting from instruct-tuned models with different entropy priors, the pattern may differ. The paper's own Appendix D shows that instruct models still follow the exponential law, but their entropy starting points and trajectories differ from base models. So the more precise claim is: **under standard on-policy GRPO/PPO training from base models on verifiable reasoning tasks, entropy collapse is consistent and severe.**

**Claim: "The downstream performance is fully predictable from policy entropy, and the fitted curve is a simple exponential function `R = -a exp(H) + b`."** The evidence is strong — the exponential fits all 11 models × 2 tasks (Figures 3-4) with only two parameters across 200+ data points per run. However, "fully predictable" should be tempered: the paper reports RMSE of 0.5–1.9% for final-step prediction from early training (Figure 5), which is good but not perfect. The prediction of individual benchmark scores (as opposed to aggregate accuracy) is not shown. Moreover, the paper does not provide goodness-of-fit statistics (`R²`, residual plots) for the exponential fits — the assessment is purely visual. A skeptic could ask whether other functional forms (e.g., a logistic curve, a power law) would fit equally well; the paper does not compare alternative forms.

**Claim: "The coefficients `a` and `b` reflect internal characteristics of the policy and data, and are algorithm-independent."** Figure 6 strongly supports algorithm independence, with four algorithms producing nearly overlapping curves. The dependence on training data is shown in Figure 13 (different datasets → different curves) and on model size in Figure 7 (log-linear scaling). However, the claim that `a` represents "the rate at which the model converts entropy into downstream performance" and `b - a` is the "maximum validation score" deserves scrutiny. This interpretation comes from differentiating the fitted equation, not from an independent measurement. The paper does not verify that models trained to different entropy levels (via the proposed interventions) actually achieve the `b - a` ceiling predicted by their vanilla-training fit — this would be a strong test of the interpretation. The intervention experiments show that Clip-Cov and KL-Cov raise performance above the vanilla ceiling, but whether the new performance still follows the same `a` and `b` values (or new ones) is not analyzed.

**Claim: "The change of policy entropy is driven by the covariance between action probability and the change in logits, which is proportional to its advantage when using Policy Gradient-like algorithms."** The theoretical derivation (Lemma 1, Proposition 1, Theorem 1) is mathematically sound given the tabular softmax assumption. The empirical validation in Figure 8 (left) — showing that the covariance term and negative entropy difference closely track — is strong evidence. However, the theoretical derivation assumes a tabular softmax policy where each `(s, a)` pair has an independent parameter. Real LLMs use shared parameters across states, so the actual gradient dynamics are more complex. The paper acknowledges this implicitly by using a bandit approximation (entire sequence as one action) for the validation experiments, but does not discuss how parameter sharing might alter the covariance-entropy relationship. The fact that the tabular theory matches empirical dynamics as well as it does (Figure 8) is itself an interesting finding that the paper could have explored more.

**Claim: "Clip-Cov and KL-Cov encourage exploration, thus helping policy escape entropy collapse and achieve better downstream performance."** Table 2 and Figure 11 provide solid evidence that these methods increase entropy, response length, and accuracy relative to GRPO and clip-higher baselines. However, several aspects of the claim require scrutiny:

First, **the gains are not uniform across benchmarks**. On MATH500, the 7B model improves from 78.8% to 80.4% (Clip-Cov) or 80.8% (KL-Cov) — just 1.6–2.0 percentage points. On AIME25, the 7B model goes from 9.6% to 15.8% (Clip-Cov, +6.2 points). The method disproportionately helps on the hardest benchmarks. This is consistent with the paper's narrative (entropy preservation enables exploration that solves harder problems) but also means the average gain is largely driven by a few benchmarks with low baselines and high variance. Without error bars, it is unclear whether the AIME25 improvement from 9.6% to 15.8% is statistically significant or could be explained by the high variance of evaluating on a small number of hard problems (AIME25 has only 30 questions, so 9.6% = ~3 correct, 15.8% = ~5 correct — a difference of just 2 problems).

Second, **the 32B gains are substantially larger than the 7B gains** (6.4 vs. 2.0 percentage points average). The paper interprets this as larger models having "greater potential from pretraining" that entropy collapse otherwise prevents them from accessing. This is plausible, but the paper only tests two model sizes within one family. A test across, say, 0.5B, 1.5B, 3B, 7B, 32B with Clip-Cov/KL-Cov would test whether the intervention benefit scales smoothly with model size.

Third, **the comparison to clip-higher is nuanced**. On 7B, clip-higher achieves 38.8% average vs. 38.6% for GRPO — a marginal 0.2 point gain — while Clip-Cov and KL-Cov achieve 40.4–40.6%. On 32B, clip-higher achieves 47.2% vs. 45.8% for GRPO — a 1.4 point gain — but is still outperformed by Clip-Cov (50.3%) and KL-Cov (52.2%). However, clip-higher also strongly degrades MATH500 on 32B (84.2% → 77.2%), which Clip-Cov and KL-Cov do not. This suggests the covariance-aware methods are more stable than clip-higher, which is a meaningful advantage.

Fourth, **the paper does not report the computational overhead of Clip-Cov/KL-Cov**. While the pseudocode (Listing 1) suggests minimal additional computation (element-wise operations on existing tensors), the practical wall-clock overhead relative to baseline GRPO is not quantified. This matters because if the overhead is non-trivial, the fair comparison would be at equal wall-clock time rather than equal gradient steps.

**Missing experiments that would strengthen the paper:**

- **Multiple random seeds** for intervention experiments, with error bars on Table 2. This is the single most important missing element for establishing reliability.
- **Intervention results on coding tasks** — the paper validates the entropy-performance law on coding (Figure 4) but tests interventions only on math.
- **Intervention results on non-Qwen model families** — Mistral, LLaMA, and DeepSeek are used for the entropy-performance law but not for Clip-Cov/KL-Cov.
- **Direct measurement of exploration diversity** — the paper uses response length and entropy as proxies for exploration, but does not measure the diversity of reasoning strategies (e.g., semantic diversity of solution approaches, not just token-level entropy).
- **Ablation comparing Clip-Cov's random selection vs. deterministic top-k** — to test whether the randomness matters.
- **Ablation testing whether intervening based on covariance is better than intervening based on other metrics** — e.g., selecting tokens by highest advantage, highest probability, or highest importance-sampling ratio, to establish that covariance specifically (rather than just "outlier tokens") is the right criterion.
- **Analysis of what the high-covariance tokens actually are** — are they function words, mathematical operators, specific numbers, or what? This would provide intuition about *why* they drive entropy collapse. The paper identifies them only statistically (Table 1), not qualitatively.

**Conclusion on experimental support:** The paper provides strong evidence for its empirical law (`R = -a exp(H) + b`) and theoretical mechanism (covariance drives entropy), with broad validation across models, sizes, and algorithms. The intervention results are encouraging but would benefit from multi-seed statistical validation, cross-task extension, and cross-family testing. The most confidently supported claim is that entropy collapse is a real, severe, and predictable phenomenon under standard training; the intervention results are solid but preliminary given the two-model, one-task, no-error-bars reporting.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Tabular Softmax Assumption Underlying the Theoretical Derivation

The theoretical analysis that identifies covariance as the driver of entropy dynamics (Lemma 1, Proposition 1, Theorem 1) assumes a **tabular softmax policy** where each state-action pair `(s, a)` has an independent, learnable logit parameter `θ_{s,a}`. The derivations in Appendices E.2–E.4 explicitly rely on this assumption — for instance, Lemma 1's proof uses the fact that the derivative `∂log πθ(a|s) / ∂θ_{s,a'}` is the sparse indicator `1{a = a'} - πθ(a'|s)`, which holds only when each logit is a free parameter not shared across states. Real LLMs violate this assumption in two critical ways. First, parameters are shared across all states (token prefixes) through the transformer architecture — updating the network to change the logit for one action at one state necessarily changes logits for many actions at many other states. Second, the state space in language generation is combinatorially large (all possible token prefixes), meaning the tabular representation is not just an approximation but a fundamentally different regime.

The consequence is that the theoretical guarantee that entropy change equals the negative covariance is approximate in ways the paper does not bound or characterize. Under parameter sharing, a gradient update that increases the logit of a high-probability, high-advantage token at one prefix position may simultaneously alter the logits of tokens at other positions in ways that are not captured by the per-state covariance formula. The paper's empirical validation (Figure 8, left) shows that the covariance and negative entropy difference curves track each other closely under the bandit approximation (treating the entire response as a single action, with the prompt as the single state), which sidesteps the per-token parameter-sharing issue by aggregating to the response level. However, the interventions (Clip-Cov and KL-Cov) operate at the individual token level (Equations 10–14), where the tabular theory's applicability is least certain. The paper does not provide a theoretical analysis of how parameter sharing affects token-level entropy dynamics, nor does it measure whether per-token covariance contributions remain faithful to the actual entropy change when updates are applied through shared transformer parameters rather than independently per logit.

The paper implicitly acknowledges this limitation by using the bandit reduction for validation (Section 3.3: "we adopt the bandit setting where the prompt `x` is the state, and whole response `y` is the action") and by citing Liu (2025) as the source of the tabular derivation, indicating awareness that the framework is simplified. However, Section 4.2 moves directly from Theorem 2 (proven only for tabular NPG) to token-wise covariance computation in PPO without discussing what new error terms parameter sharing introduces. The mitigation status is partial: the empirical match in Figure 8 provides some confidence that the tabular insight carries over, but there is no analysis of when it might break down (e.g., for very long sequences where parameter-sharing effects accumulate, or for architectures with different weight-tying schemes). A practitioner implementing these methods on a non-transformer architecture or with unusual tokenization would have no guidance on whether the covariance-entropy relationship still holds.

### Difficulty Estimation for Entropy Control Is Absent — No Way to Know When or How Much to Intervene

The paper demonstrates that Clip-Cov and KL-Cov can control policy entropy through hyperparameter tuning — Figure 12 shows that varying the clip ratio `r` or KL coefficient `β` produces monotonically different entropy levels. However, the paper provides **no method for selecting these hyperparameters in advance** for a new model, task, or dataset. Section 4.4 states this explicitly: "Although the optimal value of entropy under different scenarios remains an open question, our method demonstrates that we can simply tune the hyperparameters to control policy entropy." Section 4.5 reinforces this: "We don't observe a relationship between the intervened entropy and model performance. It still remains open whether there exists an optimal entropy value to balance the exploration and training stability."

This is a significant practical gap. A practitioner adopting Clip-Cov or KL-Cov faces a hyperparameter search problem — they must run multiple full RL training runs (each expensive at the scales involved, especially for 32B models) just to find a `β` or `r` that works well. The paper's empirical law `R = -a exp H + b` predicts the ceiling under entropy collapse but does not predict what happens when entropy is artificially maintained — the fitted `a` and `b` are derived from runs where entropy was allowed to collapse, and there is no evidence that the same coefficients predict performance under the interventions. In fact, the intervention results (Table 2, Figure 11) show that Clip-Cov and KL-Cov **raise performance above the vanilla ceiling** (since they maintain exploration and discover better strategies), meaning the `a` and `b` from the unmodified run do not bound what is achievable with entropy control. So the entropy-performance law, which is the paper's main predictive tool, does not help choose intervention hyperparameters.

The consequence is that the headline gains in Table 2 (e.g., 6.4 percentage point improvement on 32B) are the result of hyperparameter choices (`r = 2 × 10^{-4}`, `β = 1`, `k` differing between 7B and 32B) that were presumably found through experimentation but whose selection methodology is not described and whose sensitivity is only partially characterized. Figure 12 shows that different `β` and `r` values produce different entropy levels, but does not show the corresponding validation accuracy for each setting — we do not know whether the chosen values are near-optimal or whether performance degrades sharply away from them. The paper's finding that entropy loss is "highly sensitive to coefficients" (Figure 9) raises the concern that Clip-Cov and KL-Cov might also exhibit sensitive dependence, just with a wider stable region. Without a principled selection method or even a grid of accuracy-vs-hyperparameter results, a practitioner has no way to set these values without trial-and-error on their own setup.

The mitigation status is that the paper explicitly flags this as an open question (Section 4.5). The suggested future direction — finding a relationship between entropy level and performance to guide hyperparameter selection — is not pursued. The paper provides default values that worked for Qwen2.5 on DAPO-MATH (`r = 2 × 10^{-4}`, `β = 1`, `k = 2 × 10^{-3}` for 7B, `k = 2 × 10^{-4}` for 32B), but does not test whether these values transfer to other model families, other datasets, or other tasks (coding), making the current contribution closer to a proof-of-concept than a deployable recipe.

### Single-Task Validation (Math Only) for the Intervention Experiments

The entropy-performance law `R = -a exp H + b` is validated on both math (Figure 3) and coding (Figure 4), across multiple model families. However, **all intervention experiments (Clip-Cov and KL-Cov) are run exclusively on math** using the DAPO-MATH dataset (Section 4.3). Table 2 reports results on seven math benchmarks, and Figure 11 shows training dynamics only for math runs. The coding domain, despite being one of the two core task domains studied elsewhere in the paper, receives no intervention evaluation.

This matters because the two domains have different characteristics that could affect entropy dynamics and the effectiveness of covariance-based control. Math problems typically have short, deterministic correct answers (a number or expression) with long reasoning chains, while coding problems have longer outputs (full programs) where correctness is verified by unit tests and partial credit is impossible (a program either passes all tests or fails). The advantage structure for coding might differ: a program that is 90% correct but fails one test gets the same zero reward as a completely nonsensical program, meaning the advantage signal is coarser. This could change the covariance distribution — high-probability tokens in coding might be standard boilerplate (import statements, function signatures) whose probability is largely independent of correctness, potentially altering which tokens drive entropy collapse.

The consequence is that a practitioner interested in RL for code generation cannot rely on the paper's math results to estimate the benefit of Clip-Cov or KL-Cov on their domain. The entropy-performance law *does* hold for coding, so entropy collapse is presumably also a problem there, but whether the same hyperparameters (`r`, `β`, `k`) and the same degree of improvement (2.0 points for 7B, 6.4 for 32B on math) transfer is unknown. The paper provides zero evidence one way or the other. The mitigation status is that this is an acknowledged gap — the authors do not claim coding results — but it is a substantial omission given that coding and math are presented as parallel, equally important domains throughout the rest of the paper. Section 6 mentions "verifiable tasks, such as math and coding, to avoid reward hacking," and Section 2.2 states "we primarily focus on math and coding problems," making the absence of coding intervention results conspicuous.

### No Statistical Significance or Multi-Seed Reporting for Headline Intervention Results

Table 2 presents point estimates of accuracy for GRPO, GRPO + clip-higher, GRPO + Clip-Cov, and GRPO + KL-Cov on seven benchmarks and two model sizes — 56 accuracy numbers. **None of these numbers are accompanied by standard deviations, confidence intervals, or any indication of how many random seeds were used.** The paper similarly provides no measure of variance for any of the training dynamics curves (Figures 8, 11, 12), the entropy-performance fits (Figures 3–4), or the prediction experiments (Figure 5). In Section 2.2, the paper describes its experimental protocol in detail (learning rates, batch sizes, rollout counts, filtering rules) but never mentions random seeds or replication.

This is consequential because the benchmarks where the intervention gains are largest — AIME24 and AIME25 — have very small test sets. AIME (American Invitational Mathematics Examination) consists of 15 problems per year, so AIME24 and AIME25 together contain only 30 problems. An accuracy of 21.2% on AIME24 for the 7B GRPO baseline means approximately 3 correct answers out of 15; an improvement to 22.6% with KL-Cov means approximately 3.4 correct — differences of a fraction of a problem. With 15 test items per benchmark, the binomial confidence interval for a proportion is wide: at 20% accuracy, the 95% Wilson interval is roughly `[6%, 44%]` — so the difference between 21.2% and 22.6% is well within sampling error. The larger gains on 32B are more likely to be real (36.8% on AIME24 for KL-Cov vs. 21.8% for GRPO, or roughly 5.5 vs. 3.3 correct out of 15), but still rest on a handful of problems. The paper's averaging across seven benchmarks in the "Avg." column of Table 2 partially mitigates this by aggregating over more test items, but also obscures that gains are concentrated on the lowest-sample-size, highest-variance benchmarks (AIME, OlympiadBench) while being small on the largest benchmark (MATH500, 500 problems).

The missing statistical reporting also affects the comparison between Clip-Cov and KL-Cov. On 7B, Clip-Cov achieves 40.4% average versus KL-Cov's 40.6% — a 0.2 point difference. On 32B, KL-Cov (52.2%) outperforms Clip-Cov (50.3%) by 1.9 points. Is KL-Cov genuinely better than Clip-Cov, or are these differences explainable by seed variation? The paper does not say. Similarly, the claim that Clip-Cov and KL-Cov produce "stabler entropy curves" than clip-higher (Section 4.3) is based on visual inspection of Figure 11 — no quantitative stability metric (e.g., variance of entropy over a sliding window) is reported.

The mitigation status is that this limitation is entirely unaddressed. The paper does not acknowledge the absence of error bars or multi-seed replication as a limitation. For a methods paper proposing new training techniques, this is a substantial weakness — practitioners need to know whether the reported gains are reliable before adopting these methods in their own expensive training runs. The standard in the field for RL fine-tuning papers is to report at least 3 seeds with mean and standard deviation, particularly when claiming improvements on small benchmarks. The paper falls short of this standard.

### No Cross-Family Validation of the Proposed Interventions

The entropy-performance law is validated across four model families: Qwen2.5 (0.5B–32B), Mistral (7B–24B), LLaMA (3B–8B), and DeepSeek-Math (7B) — 11 models total, with Figures 3–4 showing the fitted curves for all of them. This is the paper's strongest evidence for universality. However, **Clip-Cov and KL-Cov are tested exclusively on the Qwen2.5 family** — specifically Qwen2.5-7B and Qwen2.5-32B (Table 2, Figure 11). The paper provides no evidence that the covariance-based interventions work on Mistral, LLaMA, or DeepSeek-Math models.

This gap matters because model families differ in ways that could affect the covariance mechanism. The Qwen2.5 family uses a specific tokenizer, architecture, and pretraining recipe that shapes the initial policy entropy distribution. Different families have different vocabulary sizes, different typical entropy levels at initialization, and different calibration properties (how well probability aligns with correctness before RL). The paper's own data shows these differences: in Figure 3, Qwen2.5-7B and Mistral-7B-v0.3 start at similar sizes but have different ceiling accuracies (~30% vs. ~10% on math), implying different `a` and `b` coefficients and potentially different covariance distributions during training. The clip-higher baseline, which the paper compares against, was originally developed and tested on Qwen2.5 models in the DAPO paper (Yu et al., 2025), and its transferability to other families is itself not established.

The consequence for a practitioner using, say, LLaMA-3.1-8B is that they cannot assume the default hyperparameters (`r = 2 × 10^{-4}`, `β = 1`, `k = 2 × 10^{-3}`) will work — the covariance distribution (Table 1) might look different for LLaMA's tokenizer and pretraining, requiring different thresholds. More fundamentally, they cannot assume that the `1-5` covariance interval for Clip-Cov or the `k = 2 × 10^{-3}` top-k fraction for KL-Cov are appropriate when the underlying covariance statistics are unknown. The paper provides no diagnostic for a practitioner to assess whether their model's covariance distribution resembles the Qwen2.5 one or how to adapt hyperparameters if it does not.

The mitigation status is that the paper does not acknowledge this as a limitation. The title and abstract frame the contributions in general terms ("The Entropy Mechanism of Reinforcement Learning for Reasoning Language Models"), and the Introduction states that the phenomena are observed "across vast RL runs," but the interventions — which are the actionable output of the paper — are validated on only one model family. Section 5 (Related Work) and Section 6 (Conclusion) maintain the general framing. A more accurate characterization of the contribution would be: the mechanism and empirical law are broadly validated; the interventions are validated on Qwen2.5 only, and their cross-family generalization remains to be tested.

### Open Question of Optimal Entropy Level — The Paper Provides Control but No Target

The paper demonstrates that Clip-Cov and KL-Cov can control policy entropy across a range of values (Figure 12), and that maintaining higher entropy than the baseline improves performance (Table 2, Figure 11). However, the paper also finds that **higher entropy does not monotonically improve performance**, and provides no method for identifying how much entropy is optimal. Section 4.5 states: "we don't observe a relationship between the intervened entropy and model performance." This is not a minor caveat — it means the paper has solved the *control* problem (how to prevent entropy from collapsing) but not the *optimization* problem (what entropy level maximizes final performance).

The evidence for this limitation is both explicit (the quoted admission) and implicit in the results. In Figure 12, different entropy levels are achieved by varying hyperparameters, but the corresponding test accuracies are not shown — we cannot tell whether the model with entropy stabilized at 0.5 nats outperforms the one at 0.3 nats or vice versa. In Table 2, KL-Cov achieves higher average accuracy than Clip-Cov on 32B (52.2% vs. 50.3%), but it is not clear whether this is because KL-Cov maintains a different entropy level, because it has a different entropy *trajectory* (Figure 11 shows KL-Cov's entropy curve is slightly higher and more stable than Clip-Cov's), or for some other reason unrelated to entropy. The paper also finds that clip-higher increases entropy (Figure 11, left) but eventually degrades performance (Figure 11, right), demonstrating that indiscriminately raising entropy does not guarantee improvement.

The practical consequence is that a practitioner cannot simply "set entropy to X and forget it." They must either run a hyperparameter sweep (expensive) or adopt the paper's default values and hope they generalize. Moreover, the optimal entropy level likely depends on the model, the training data, and the training stage — early training might benefit from one entropy level while late training benefits from another. The paper's methods use fixed hyperparameters throughout training, and there is no mechanism for adaptive entropy control that responds to the current training state. The mitigation status is again that this is explicitly flagged as an open question (Section 4.5: "It still remains open whether there exists an optimal entropy value to balance the exploration and training stability"), but no progress toward answering it is made. This is a fundamental tradeoff the paper exposes but does not resolve: entropy is necessary for exploration, but too much entropy wastes capacity on tokens that will never be useful, and the paper provides no principled way to find the sweet spot.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper shifts the RL-for-reasoning conversation from *what* RL achieves to *how* it achieves it — and, more critically, to *when it stops achieving anything at all*. Prior to this work, the dominant narrative around RL for LLM reasoning was one of success: o1 (OpenAI, 2024a), DeepSeek-R1 (DeepSeek-AI et al., 2025), and numerous open replications (Cui et al., 2025; Hu et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025) demonstrated that RL with verifiable rewards could dramatically improve mathematical and coding reasoning from base models. The research frontier was focused on algorithmic improvements — better advantage estimators, more efficient rollout collection, multi-stage curricula — implicitly assuming that more training compute would continue to yield returns. This paper demonstrates that this assumption is false in a precise, quantifiable way: under standard on-policy RL from base models, **performance improvements are governed by a predictable entropy budget that is exhausted long before training budgets are**, and the ceiling is fully determined by the base model and training data. The 93% of performance gains achieved in the first 800 of 2400 steps (Figure 2) means the remaining 1600 steps are largely wasted — and this is not a failure of tuning but a consequence of the policy gradient dynamics themselves.

The magnitude of this reframing is substantial. The paper does not merely propose a new method that improves upon existing ones — it provides a **diagnostic framework** that explains *why* existing methods plateau, *when* they will plateau, and *how to predict* the plateau from early training. The empirical law `R = -a exp(H) + b` functions as a scaling law for the RL phase, analogous to the Chinchilla laws (Hoffmann et al., 2022) for pretraining: it says that given a model and training data, there is a fixed "entropy budget" that determines achievable performance, and once that budget is spent, further training compute yields negligible returns. The coefficients `a` and `b` are shown to be algorithm-independent (Figure 6) and to scale log-linearly with model size within a family (Figure 7), making them intrinsic properties of the model-data pair — not hyperparameters to be optimized but **characteristics to be measured and predicted**.

This reframing resolves several tensions in the literature. First, it explains why some researchers observe that RL "just elicits existing capabilities" (Yue et al., 2025) while others find it creates genuinely novel reasoning patterns: the extent to which RL can go beyond elicitation depends on whether the training dynamics preserve enough entropy to discover compositions of pretrained knowledge that never appeared in the training data. When entropy collapses prematurely (the default under standard training), RL is limited to elicitation; when entropy is maintained (via interventions like Clip-Cov or KL-Cov), the ceiling can be broken. Second, it reconciles conflicting reports about whether entropy regularization helps or hurts: generic entropy bonuses (Figure 9) and KL penalties (Figure 10) fail because they are too blunt — they push on all tokens uniformly, fighting the natural gradient dynamics rather than selectively targeting the causal driver of collapse. Third, it clarifies why clip-higher (Yu et al., 2025) provides early gains but becomes unstable (Figure 11): by raising the upper clipping threshold, it allows more aggressive exploitation on high-probability, high-advantage tokens — exactly those that decrease entropy — leading to faster initial learning but eventual collapse and degradation.

The paper also redirects research attention toward several questions that now become central, and away from others that become less pressing:

- **More attractive:** Understanding the *composition* of high-covariance tokens (what linguistic or mathematical roles they play), developing adaptive entropy control policies that vary across training, estimating `a` and `b` coefficients without running full RL training, extending covariance-aware interventions to other RL paradigms (off-policy, multi-turn, multi-agent), and characterizing the entropy dynamics of other policy architectures beyond standard autoregressive transformers.

- **Less pressing:** Incremental improvements to advantage estimation (GRPO vs. RLOO vs. PRIME) — Figure 6 shows they all trace the same entropy-performance curve, so algorithmic refinements that don't address entropy dynamics will not change the fundamental ceiling. Hyperparameter tuning of PPO clipping thresholds — the paper shows that the entropy mechanism, not the clipping details, governs the training trajectory. Scaling training compute by simply running longer — the paper demonstrates that most compute after entropy collapse is wasted, so brute-force scaling without entropy management yields rapidly diminishing returns.

Perhaps the most significant conceptual shift is the introduction of **covariance as a first-class diagnostic for policy optimization**. In traditional RL, the policy gradient is typically analyzed through the lens of expected reward improvement or KL divergence from a reference policy. This paper shows that the decomposition of entropy change into per-token covariance contributions (Equation 10, Table 1) reveals structure that is invisible in aggregate metrics: a tiny fraction of tokens (top 0.02%, with mean covariance ~1800× the batch average) dominate the entropy dynamics. This has implications beyond the specific interventions proposed — it suggests that future policy optimization methods should monitor and potentially control not just the aggregate policy gradient but its *distribution* across tokens, identifying and modulating the outlier contributions that disproportionately shape the training trajectory.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Characterizing the semantic identity of high-covariance tokens on specific training distributions.** The paper identifies high-covariance tokens only statistically (Table 1: top 0.02% have mean covariance 5.654 vs. 0.003 average), but does not examine *what* these tokens are — function words, mathematical operators, numbers, specific variable names, punctuation, or something else. A natural follow-up would collect high-covariance tokens from training runs on DAPO-MATH, GSM8K, and Eurus-RL-Data, categorize them by part-of-speech and mathematical role, and measure whether the same token types drive collapse across datasets and model families. If high-covariance tokens consistently correspond to, say, equality signs, arithmetic operators, and logical connectives (tokens where the model is both confident and correct on easy problems), this would explain *why* collapse happens and suggest token-type-specific interventions (e.g., applying stronger KL penalties to operator tokens while leaving noun/number tokens unconstrained). The paper's theoretical framework (covariance = driver of entropy change) provides the mechanistic rationale; a semantic analysis would bridge the gap between the abstract covariance statistic and the concrete reasoning behavior it shapes.

**Testing whether the `a` and `b` coefficients predict performance under entropy-controlled training.** The paper fits `R = -a exp(H) + b` on runs where entropy is allowed to collapse naturally, then shows that Clip-Cov and KL-Cov maintain higher entropy and achieve higher final performance than the `b - a` ceiling (Table 2). This raises the question: does the entropy-controlled training trace a *new* exponential curve with different coefficients, or does it follow the same functional form with the same `a` and `b` but shifted to a higher-entropy operating point? The experiment would involve: (1) running multiple KL-Cov variants with different `β` values to produce different sustained entropy levels, (2) recording `(H, R)` pairs throughout each run, (3) testing whether all runs (including the baseline) collapse onto a single `R = -a exp(H) + b` curve or whether each `β` produces its own curve. If a single curve fits all runs, it would mean entropy-controlled training simply spends more time in the high-entropy, high-improvement regime of a fixed exploitation-exploration relationship — the ceiling is genuinely unchanged, just delayed. If each `β` produces a distinct curve with a higher `b - a` ceiling, it would mean entropy control fundamentally changes what the model can learn, not just when it learns it. The paper's current data (Figure 11, right panels) shows accuracy continuing to improve after the baseline saturates, consistent with the second hypothesis, but a systematic curve-fitting analysis is missing. This experiment would be straightforward to run on Qwen2.5-7B with ~5 `β` values at modest computational cost.

**Evaluating Clip-Cov and KL-Cov on coding tasks with unit-test-based verifiers.** The paper validates the entropy-performance law on coding (Figure 4) but tests interventions only on math (Table 2). Coding and math differ in ways that matter for entropy dynamics: coding outputs are longer (programs vs. answers + derivations), correctness is binary and coarse (all tests pass vs. at least one fails), and successful programs contain large blocks of boilerplate (imports, function signatures, API calls) whose token probabilities may be independent of whether the solution is correct. This changes the covariance structure — boilerplate tokens likely have high probability (they always appear) but zero covariance with advantage (they appear in both correct and incorrect solutions), while logic tokens (conditionals, loop structures) drive correctness. If this is the case, the default `[ω_low=1, ω_high=5]` covariance interval for Clip-Cov might select different token types for coding than for math, potentially altering the optimal intervention hyperparameters. The experiment would replicate the Table 2 setup on, say, Eurus-2-RL-Code with Qwen2.5-7B and Qwen2.5-32B, reporting pass@1 on LiveCodeBench or HumanEval+. A negative result (interventions don't help on code) would be informative — it would suggest that the benefit of entropy control depends on the reward signal's granularity or the output distribution's structure.

**Multi-family validation of Clip-Cov and KL-Cov with covariance distribution characterization per family.** The paper's interventions are tested exclusively on Qwen2.5 models. A substantial follow-up would test them on Mistral-7B-v0.3, LLaMA-3.1-8B, and DeepSeek-Math-7B — the same models for which the entropy-performance law was validated (Figure 3). Crucially, this follow-up should also report the per-family covariance distribution (the analog of Table 1) before and during training, to characterize whether the heavy-tailed covariance structure is universal or Qwen2.5-specific. If, for example, LLaMA models have a less heavy-tailed covariance distribution (meaning the top 0.02% of tokens don't dominate as strongly), then the intervention fraction `r = 2 × 10^{-4}` might need to be larger to achieve the same entropy uplift. If DeepSeek-Math-7B (which is math-pretrained) has higher initial covariance on easy problems (because it's better calibrated from the start), it might require *more* aggressive intervention to prevent premature collapse. This experiment would determine whether Clip-Cov and KL-Cov are broadly applicable tools or Qwen2.5-specific techniques, and would provide practitioners with diagnostics (covariance histograms) to adapt the methods to their own models without trial-and-error.

**Developing adaptive, training-phase-aware entropy control schedules.** The paper's interventions use fixed hyperparameters throughout training (`r`, `β`, `k` constant across all steps). Figure 8 (left) shows that covariance is high early in training and decays over time, suggesting that the appropriate level of entropy intervention should also change — strong suppression of high-covariance tokens early (to prevent initial collapse), tapering to weaker intervention later (to allow beneficial exploitation to proceed once exploration has discovered good strategies). An adaptive schedule could be based on the running average of the batch-level covariance: when covariance is high, increase the clip ratio or KL coefficient; when covariance naturally declines, reduce them. Alternatively, a difficulty-conditional schedule could vary the intervention strength by problem difficulty: strong entropy preservation on hard problems (where the model needs exploration), weak on easy problems (where the model can safely exploit). The paper provides all the necessary infrastructure — per-prompt accuracy from group sampling (Figure 8, right), token-wise covariance computation (Equation 10), controllable entropy levels (Figure 12) — but does not combine them into an adaptive policy. Designing and evaluating such a schedule on DAPO-MATH with Qwen2.5-7B would test whether adaptive control outperforms the fixed-parameter version and how much of the remaining performance gap between entropy-controlled and theoretically optimal training remains.

**Connecting entropy collapse to specific training data properties — dataset "entropy efficiency" as a selection criterion.** The paper shows (Figure 13, Appendix C) that different training datasets produce different `a` and `b` coefficients for the same model. GSM8K (easier, shorter problems) produces a higher final ceiling than Eurus-RL-Data-Difficult for Qwen2.5-7B. This raises a practical question: can one estimate `a` and `b` from a small pilot RL run (say, 100 steps) on a candidate training dataset, and use the predicted ceiling `b - a` to decide whether the dataset is worth training on at scale? The paper's prediction experiment (Figure 5: fitting from 36 steps, predicting 200 steps with 0.5–1.9% RMSE) suggests this is feasible. A systematic follow-up would take 5–10 candidate math reasoning datasets (varying in difficulty, length, domain), run short RL pilots on each with Qwen2.5-7B, fit `a` and `b`, and then run full training on the 2–3 datasets with the highest predicted ceilings to verify the predictions. If validated, this would provide a compute-efficient dataset selection method: rather than training to convergence on each candidate dataset, run a cheap pilot and use the predicted ceiling to filter. The connection to entropy makes this more principled than existing dataset ablation approaches (which typically just compare final accuracy) because it distinguishes between datasets that produce slow initial learning but a high ceiling (high `a`, high `b`) from those that plateau quickly but low (low `a`, low `b`).

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Early-stopping and training-budget allocation for large-scale RL runs.** The most immediate application of the paper's empirical law is predicting when continued RL training will yield marginal returns. Using the protocol from Figure 5, a team running a 32B-model RL training job can, after 15–20% of the planned training steps, fit `R = -a exp(H) + b` to the observed `(H, R)` pairs, compute the predicted ceiling `b - a`, and compare it to the current accuracy. If the predicted ceiling is only 1–2 percentage points above the current accuracy and the remaining training steps would consume 80% of the total compute budget, the run can be terminated early with high confidence that little is being left on the table. Conversely, if the predicted ceiling is substantially higher and the entropy curve still has room to drop, the training can be continued or even extended. This is a concrete decision-making tool that requires no modifications to the training pipeline — only logging policy entropy on training batches (already computed for the loss) and validation accuracy on a held-out set (already standard practice). For organizations spending millions on RL training runs, a 15%-of-budget early check that prevents wasting 80% of compute on plateaued training translates directly to cost savings.

**Hyperparameter-free deployment of covariance-aware interventions with model-family pilot scaling.** The paper shows that `a` and `b` scale log-linearly with model size within the Qwen2.5 family (Figure 7), and that Clip-Cov/KL-Cov hyperparameters differ between 7B (`k = 2 × 10^{-3}`) and 32B (`k = 2 × 10^{-4}`). A practical deployment strategy for a model family with sizes from, say, 1B to 100B would be: (1) run full Clip-Cov/KL-Cov sweeps on the smallest two models (1B, 3B) to find optimal `r`, `β`, and `k` for those sizes; (2) extrapolate the optimal hyperparameters to larger models using the same log-linear scaling observed for `a` and `b`; (3) deploy the extrapolated hyperparameters on the 30B and 100B models without running expensive sweeps. The paper's finding that the covariance distribution is heavy-tailed across all models (implied by the universality of entropy collapse in Figure 2) suggests the extrapolation is likely to transfer, though this exact procedure has not been validated. The benefit is avoiding the compute cost of hyperparameter optimization at the largest scales, where each trial run costs orders of magnitude more than the small-model pilots.

**Entropy monitoring as a real-time training-health diagnostic in production RL systems.** Beyond the specific interventions, the paper provides a diagnostic toolkit that any team running RL for LLMs can adopt immediately: log per-batch policy entropy, compute the step-wise entropy difference, and compute the per-batch covariance term from Equation 10. If entropy is dropping faster than expected (curvature change in the `H` vs. step curve) or covariance is spiking (indicating a batch of prompts where the model is suddenly very well-calibrated and about to over-exploit), the system can trigger an alert or automatically adjust intervention hyperparameters. This is analogous to gradient-norm monitoring or loss-spike detection already standard in large-scale training, but targeted at the specific failure mode (entropy collapse) that this paper identifies as the primary bottleneck for RL scaling. The computation is cheap — covariance requires only element-wise operations on existing tensors per Listing 1 — so the monitoring overhead is negligible relative to the cost of generating rollouts.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper does not explicitly frame Clip-Cov and KL-Cov against named alternative entropy-control methods as a crisp decision rule. It identifies the failure modes of entropy loss (brittle coefficient sensitivity; Figure 9), KL penalty to a reference model (performance degradation; Figure 10), and clip-higher (late-training instability; Figure 11), but does not provide a set of conditions under which a practitioner should select one over the others in a new setting. The paper's contribution is primarily mechanistic (explaining *why* entropy collapses and providing proof-of-concept interventions) rather than a systematic comparison of entropy-control methods across diverse regimes. A forced decision matrix ("Prefer Clip-Cov when... Prefer entropy loss when...") would therefore fabricate a tradeoff analysis the paper itself does not conduct and does not provide evidence for. The paper's own position (Section 4.5) is that the optimal entropy-control strategy — including the optimal entropy level — remains an open question, and the methods demonstrated are instantiations of a principle (suppress high-covariance tokens) rather than a fully characterized solution.
