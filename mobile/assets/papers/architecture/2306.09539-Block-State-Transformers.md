# Block-State Transformers

**ArXiv:** [2306.09539](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.09539)

## 🎯 Pitch

A hybrid SSM-Transformer layer achieves up to 10× speedup over block-recurrent models by replacing the recurrent cell with a parallelizable SSM—yet only matching full transformer quality when SSM context is fused directly into attention, not stacked separately.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper proposes a hybrid architecture called the **Block-State Transformer (BST)** that integrates State Space Models (SSMs) for long-range sequence contextualization with Block Transformers for short-term token-level representation within a single, fully parallelizable layer. The BST layer replaces the sequential recurrent cell of the Block-Recurrent Transformer (BRECT) with an SSM sublayer—operationalizing long-range context via parallelizable FFT-based convolutions—and studies three context state integration variants: Single-Head (SH, concatenating adjacent SSM outputs to maximize redundancy and retrievability), Multi-Head (MH, producing separate SSM outputs per attention head for complementary feature extraction), and Multi-Filter (MF, convolving the input with independent filters and retaining only the last state from each, minimizing redundancy). On language modeling benchmarks—PG19, arXiv, and GitHub—the BST models match or outperform comparable architectures like GSS-HYBRID-L and BRECT at matched compute budgets, achieving up to a 10× speedup in layer-level forward pass time relative to BRECT on GPU (reducing the 15× slowdown of BRECT relative to a non-recurrent sliding-window layer to less than 2×). The BST with structured S4 kernels generalizes to unseen sequence lengths up to 65k tokens, with BST:SH:S4-L producing the best perplexity at 65k on all three datasets, establishing that SSM-Transformer hybrids can surpass interleaved alternatives only when the SSM-generated context states are directly integrated into the attention mechanism rather than stacked in separate layers.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Tension: Transformers Dominate Language Modeling But Scale Poorly with Sequence Length

The fundamental problem this paper addresses is a tension that has defined much of the sequence modeling landscape: **Transformers remain the dominant architecture for language modeling in terms of raw performance, but their quadratic self-attention complexity makes them extremely expensive—and sometimes unstable—when trained or deployed on long sequences.** This tension creates a practical bottleneck that affects both research and production deployment.

The Transformer architecture's quadratic scaling in sequence length $L$ means that doubling the context window increases the attention computation cost by roughly $4\times$. On the surface, this is merely an engineering efficiency problem. But the paper highlights (Section 1, Introduction) that the consequences are deeper: **scaling the input sequence length is problematic for both computational performance and quality reasons.** Specifically:

- **Training instability:** Vanilla transformers can become unstable when trained on long sequences, a phenomenon documented by Li et al. (2022) and referenced in the paper. This instability requires careful mitigation strategies that add complexity to the training pipeline.
- **Concentrated attention patterns:** Token importance in transformers is empirically concentrated in a relatively narrow local receptive field of around 50 tokens around the current time step (Subramanian et al., 2020, cited in Section 1). This suggests that large swaths of the quadratic attention computation are being spent on interactions that matter very little for the final prediction—a form of computational inefficiency beyond just the asymptotic complexity.
- **Failure on long-input tasks:** Transformers with local, sparse, low-rank approximated, or linearized attention "notoriously struggle on long-input classification tasks" (Section 1, citing the Long Range Arena benchmark results from Tay et al., 2020). This is a crucial point: it is not just that efficient attention approximations sacrifice *some* performance—they can fundamentally *fail* on tasks explicitly designed to require modeling dependencies across long distances.

The real-world importance of this problem has grown dramatically as the field has pushed toward larger context windows for applications like book-length document understanding, repository-scale code generation, multi-turn dialogue with persistent memory, and scientific literature analysis. The paper cites scaling trends suggesting that "the demand for deploying even deeper and larger networks is greater than ever before" and that "an orthogonal scaling dimension, which could be potentially even more consequential, is the size of the input sequence" (Section 1). This framing positions sequence length scaling not as a secondary concern but as potentially *more important* than parameter count scaling for certain applications.

### The SSM Opportunity and Its Language Modeling Shortfall

State Space Models (SSMs) have emerged as a compelling alternative because they offer a fundamentally different computational profile. Unlike Transformers, which explicitly compute pairwise interactions between all tokens, SSMs model sequences through a linear time-invariant dynamical system that can be computed either:

- **Recurrently** (sequentially, for autoregressive decoding): $x_k = Ax_{k-1} + Bu_k$, $y_k = Cx_k$, which has $O(L)$ complexity but is inherently sequential.
- **Via parallelized convolution** (for training): $y = K * u$, where the kernel $K = (CB, CAB, \dots, CA^{L-1}B)$ can be precomputed and the convolution executed using Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) with $O(L \log L)$ complexity (Section 3.1).

This dual-mode computation is attractive: parallel training with subquadratic cost, and efficient autoregressive inference. Moreover, SSMs have demonstrated superior performance on long-range dependency benchmarks, "outperform[ing] Transformers on long-range dependency benchmarks by a large margin" (Section 1, referencing Tay et al., 2020). The HiPPO framework (Gu et al., 2020) provides analytical derivations for the state matrices $A$ and $B$ that provably enable optimal polynomial approximation of the input history, giving SSMs a principled mechanism for retaining information over thousands of time steps.

However—and this is the critical gap motivating the paper—**SSMs have "not yet completely matched Transformers as an off-the-shelf sequence model for general language modeling tasks"** (Section 1, citing Fu et al., 2023). Despite their long-range advantages, purely SSM-based models lag behind Transformers on standard language modeling perplexity benchmarks. The paper does not claim to fully explain *why* this gap exists, but it cites "recent findings suggest[ing] that Transformers and SSMs are complementary models for the purpose of language modeling" (Section 1, referencing Mehta et al., 2023). This complementarity is the conceptual foundation for the paper's hybrid approach: if SSMs and Transformers have different inductive biases—SSMs excelling at compressing and propagating long-range information, Transformers excelling at fine-grained local token interactions—a principled integration might outperform either alone.

### Where Prior Hybrid Approaches Fall Short

The paper identifies two major families of prior work that attempt to address the long-sequence problem, and it locates specific shortcomings in each.

#### Recurrent-Transformer Hybrids (BRECT and Its Predecessors)

The lineage of models combining local attention with recurrent memory mechanisms includes Transformer-XL (Dai et al., 2019), the Memorizing Transformer (Wu et al., 2022), and most directly the **Block-Recurrent Transformer (BRECT)** (Hutchins et al., 2022). These models share a common pattern: process the input in blocks of size $W$ (usually 512 tokens) using local self-attention, and maintain a recurrent state that propagates information across blocks, extending the effective context beyond the local window.

BRECT, specifically, uses a recurrent cell that employs cross-attention to extract "recurrent state vectors" from a block of input token embeddings. These state vectors are then passed sequentially from one block to the next, serving as a compressed summary of prior context that the attention mechanism can access. The authors cite BRECT as their primary architectural predecessor: the Block Transformer sublayer in BST operates "similarly to a Block-Recurrent Transformer (BRECT) cell" (Section 3.2), and the context states in BST are fed to the Block Transformer "in place of what was referred to as 'recurrent state vectors' in Block-Recurrent Transformer" (Section 3.2).

The fundamental limitation of this approach is **computational**: the recurrent unit introduces sequential dependencies between blocks. The BRECT cell must process blocks one at a time, with each block's recurrent state computation depending on the output from the previous block. This sequential processing means the layer cannot be fully parallelized across blocks. The paper quantifies this concretely in Figure 4 (left): at a sequence length of 4k tokens (the typical training length), a BRECT layer runs approximately **15× slower than a non-recurrent sliding-window layer** with the same window size. This is not merely an inconvenience—it means that the contextualization mechanism (the recurrence) imposes a significant wall-clock time penalty that scales with the number of blocks, creating a tension between the desire for long context (more blocks) and training efficiency (more sequential steps in the recurrence).

Additionally, the BRECT recurrent cell uses a learned gating mechanism ("a simple linear layer gating mechanism that combines current block hidden representation with past information from the previous blocks," Section 4.1) to condense information across blocks. While effective—the paper acknowledges BRECT as "the strongest performing and fastest Block-Recurrent Transformer architecture"—this mechanism is a learned black-box recurrence whose information retention properties are not analytically characterized, unlike SSMs which have principled guarantees via the HiPPO framework.

#### Interleaved SSM-Transformer Hybrids (GSS-HYBRID)

The second family is represented by **GSS-HYBRID** (Mehta et al., 2023), which the paper describes as "the closest SSM-Transformer hybrid model that was tested on long-range language modeling tasks" (Section 4.1). The GSS-HYBRID architecture interleaves Gated State Space (GSS) layers with standard Transformer layers—specifically, placing a Transformer layer at every 4th layer starting from the 2nd layer, with the remaining layers being GSS layers. The SSM and attention mechanisms operate in separate layers: the SSM layers process the full sequence to model long-range dependencies, and the Transformer layers periodically inject local attention-based processing.

The paper identifies several limitations with this approach:

- **No direct integration of SSM states into attention:** The SSM layers and Transformer layers operate independently, with the SSM merely providing transformed representations as input to subsequent layers. The attention mechanism never directly accesses the SSM's internal state or outputs as context to attend *over*, which means the Transformer layers cannot selectively retrieve information from the SSM's compressed history representation. The paper explicitly frames this as its key differentiator: "BST results demonstrate that integrating SSM states into the Transformer attention provides larger benefits than interleaving SSM and attention layers as in GSS-HYBRID-L" (Section 4.1).

- **Training instability and hyperparameter sensitivity:** The authors of GSS-HYBRID obtained their results "after grid searching over four learning rates... and used a different learning rate and weight decay for the SSM layer and the Transformer layer to avoid training instabilities" (Section 4.1). The BST paper, in contrast, "did not use grid search and used the same learning rate for all layers," suggesting that the interleaved architecture creates optimization challenges that the integrated BST approach avoids.

- **Difficulty generalizing at larger scales:** The paper notes that "larger GSS models had difficulty generalizing to higher lengths" (Section 4.2), visible in Figure 3 where GSS-HYBRID-L perplexity increases sharply on the arXiv dataset at 16k and 65k sequence lengths. BST:SH:S4-L, which directly integrates the SSM's structured kernel into the attention context, shows substantially better length generalization on arXiv at these lengths.

A subtler architectural difference: GSS-HYBRID uses a **Gated Attention Unit (GAU)** (Hua et al., 2022) within its GSS layers—a single-head gating mechanism that mixes SSM outputs with inputs through learned gates. BST, by contrast, uses **cross-attention** to mix SSM-generated context states with the self-attention outputs from the Block Transformer. This means BST's attention heads can *independently* attend to different aspects of the SSM context, rather than having the SSM output gated into a single representation before attention is applied. The paper argues this allows "plug[ging] any out-of-the-box SSMs or attention layers" (Section 2), making the architecture more modular and flexible.

#### The General Gap: No Principled Combination of SSM Memory with Attention Retrieval

Stepping back, the paper identifies a broader gap in the literature: prior work either **replaces** attention with SSM-style mechanisms (purely SSM models like S4, DSS, Hyena), **stacks** them in separate layers (GSS-HYBRID), or **augments** attention with learned recurrence (BRECT). No prior approach directly feeds the SSM's compressed long-range context *as a retrievable memory* into the attention mechanism itself. The paper's central architectural contribution is precisely this: using the SSM to generate "context states" that the Block Transformer's cross-attention heads can query, effectively creating a **retrievable long-term memory** within a single parallelized layer.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper positions itself not as introducing an entirely new class of models, but as **architecturally refining the concept of hybrid SSM-Transformer models** by changing *how* the two components interact. This is evident from the explicit framing in the Related Work section: "Our work can be seen as a continued evolution of those models [Transformer-XL, Memorizing Transformer, BRECT] incorporating state-of-the-art recurrent memory models inspired by SSMs" (Section 2).

The key architectural insight is to replace the **learned sequential recurrence** in BRECT with an **SSM-based contextualization** that operates over the entire input sequence in parallel via FFT-based convolution. This addresses BRECT's primary weakness (the 15× sequential slowdown) while retaining its core idea (cross-attending to a compressed representation of prior context). At the same time, it addresses GSS-HYBRID's limitation by **embedding the SSM-generated context directly into the attention mechanism as a retrievable memory** rather than keeping SSM and attention processing in separate, non-interacting layers.

The paper also positions itself methodologically as conducting a systematic exploration of the **design space for integrating SSM states into attention**. Rather than proposing a single architecture, it develops and compares three variants—SH, MH, and MF—that make explicit tradeoffs along a spectrum of **redundancy vs. retrievability** (Section 3.3). The Single-Head approach maximizes redundancy (adjacent SSM states are highly correlated, making it easy to recover the current block of tokens) at the cost of potentially inefficient capacity usage. The Multi-Filter approach minimizes redundancy (each filter extracts complementary features, and only the final state from each is retained) but requires learned context IDs and careful positional encoding. The Multi-Head approach strikes a middle ground. This design-space analysis, rather than a single "best" architecture, is characteristic of a paper that aims to establish principles rather than just report a winning model.

Finally, the paper positions its contribution as addressing a **scaling-oriented practical need** rather than a purely theoretical one. The emphasis on speedup (Section 4.3), length generalization (Section 4.2), and compute-matched comparisons (Table 1 tracks exact TPUv4 hours) signals a focus on deployability. The title itself—"Block-State Transformer"—emphasizes the state-based (SSM) and block-based (Transformer) components as equal partners, reflecting the paper's thesis that each contributes something complementary and neither is being relegated to an auxiliary role.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

The Block-State Transformer (BST) is a decoder-only language model architecture where each layer contains both a State Space Model (SSM) sublayer that compresses the entire input sequence into long-range context vectors, and a Block Transformer sublayer that attends locally within small windows while cross-attending to those context vectors — all within a single, fully parallelizable layer. The problem it solves is that existing approaches to extending Transformer context beyond local windows either impose expensive sequential dependencies between blocks (like Block-Recurrent Transformers, which run 15× slower than non-recurrent baselines) or keep SSM and attention processing in separate layers without allowing attention heads to directly retrieve information from the SSM's compressed history. The BST's "shape" is a hybrid cell where an SSM runs in parallel over the full sequence via FFT-based convolution, produces a sequence of context states, and feeds those states directly into the cross-attention heads of a Block Transformer — replacing the sequential recurrent unit with a parallelizable convnet-style operation while keeping the attention mechanism as the primary retrieval engine.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The BST layer consists of four major components arranged in a feedforward flow:

1. **SSM Sublayer (green in Figure 1):** Takes the full sequence of token embeddings from the previous layer (shape `$L \times D$`, where `$L$` is sequence length and `$D$` is embedding dimension) and produces a "context sequence" of the same length `$L \times D$`. This sublayer uses FFT-based convolution with either structured kernels (S4) or unstructured parameterized filters, and optionally includes a down-sampling projection to reduce FFT cost. The output at each position `$t$` contains information about all preceding positions `$0 \dots t$`, making it a causally valid contextualized representation.

2. **Context State Collector (implementation varies by variant):** Extracts a compact set of "context states" from the SSM output sequence. The result is a tensor of shape `$(L/W) \times S \times D \times H$`, where `$W$` is the block/window length (typically 512), `$S$` is the number of context states (≤ `$W$`), and `$H$` is the number of attention heads. This component implements the core design choice — how to compress the SSM's full-length output into a retrievable memory — and is instantiated in three forms: Single-Head (SH, concatenating adjacent SSM outputs for maximum redundancy), Multi-Head (MH, producing per-head SSM outputs for complementary feature extraction), and Multi-Filter (MF, convolving with independent filters and retaining only the final state per filter for minimum redundancy).

3. **Block Transformer Sublayer (red in Figure 1):** Operates on the input sequence divided into blocks of size `$W$`. For each block, it performs two attention operations in parallel: (a) **self-attention** over the block's token embeddings (capturing local fine-grained interactions), and (b) **cross-attention** from the block's token embeddings to the corresponding context states (retrieving compressed long-range information). The outputs of both attention operations are concatenated and projected back to the embedding dimension.

4. **Feed-Forward Network (not shown in Figure 1 but standard):** Applied after the concatenation and projection, providing additional non-linear processing capacity at each layer.

Information flows through the BST layer as follows:

- **First**, the SSM sublayer receives the token embeddings from the previous layer (or the word embedding layer at position 0). It projects them down to a lower-dimensional space (quarter of the embedding size, `$D/4$`) to reduce FFT costs, convolves them with the SSM kernel to produce contextualized outputs, and projects back to the full embedding dimension `$D$`.

- **Second**, the context state collector slices the SSM output sequence into windows of size `$W$` and constructs the per-block context states. In SH and MH variants, the context states for block `$b$` are taken from the SSM outputs at positions `$bW \dots (b+1)W-1$`, with a triangular causal mask ensuring each query position attends only to context states up to its own position. In MF, the context states are the final outputs of `$S$` independent filters from the *previous* window (window `$b-1$`), shifted and augmented with learned "context IDs," with no masking required.

- **Third**, the Block Transformer receives the token embeddings split into blocks. For each block, self-attention is computed over the block's `$W$` tokens, cross-attention is computed from the block's tokens to the context states, and the outputs are concatenated and projected. Crucially, because the SSM sublayer has already processed the entire sequence in parallel and the context states are precomputed for each block independently, **every block's Block Transformer computation can run in parallel** — there is no sequential dependency between blocks.

- **Fourth**, the FFN is applied to the projected output, producing the layer's output, which feeds into the next BST layer or the final language modeling head.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is an **architectural design paper** whose core idea is that an SSM can replace the sequential recurrent cell in a Block Transformer to produce a fully parallelizable hybrid layer, and that *how* the SSM's output is collected and fed to attention heads — via Single-Head, Multi-Head, or Multi-Filter context states — controls a redundancy-retrievability tradeoff that impacts language modeling performance across datasets with different local-vs-global dependency profiles.

#### The SSM Sublayer: Parallel Contextualization via Convolution

The SSM sublayer is responsible for taking a sequence of token embeddings and producing, at each position, a representation that contains information about all preceding positions. It does this by implementing a linear time-invariant (LTI) dynamical system that can be executed in two modes: sequential recurrence (for autoregressive decoding) and parallel convolution (for training). The paper supports two classes of SSMs: structured kernels based on the S4/DSS lineage, and unstructured parameterized filters inspired by Hyena.

**Recurrent formulation.** In its recurrent form, the SSM maintains an internal state vector `$x_k \in \mathbb{R}^N$` (where `$N$` is the state dimension, typically 16 in the paper's experiments) and updates it at each time step based on the current input:

$$x_k = Ax_{k-1} + Bu_k$$
$$y_k = Cx_k + Du_k$$

Let me define every term. `$x_k \in \mathbb{R}^N$` is the internal state at time `$k$` — a compressed representation of the input history up to and including position `$k$`. `$x_{k-1}$` is the state from the previous time step. `$u_k \in \mathbb{R}$` is the scalar input at time `$k$` (one dimension of the token embedding). `$y_k \in \mathbb{R}$` is the scalar output at time `$k$`. `$A \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}$` is the state transition matrix — it describes how the state evolves from one step to the next when no input is present. `$B \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times 1}$` is the input projection vector — it describes how the current input `$u_k$` is incorporated into the state update. `$C \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times N}$` is the output projection vector — it maps the internal state back to a scalar. `$D \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times 1}$` is a skip-connection term (direct feedthrough from input to output), which the paper omits for notational convenience.

**What these equations compute:** At each time step, the system takes the previous state `$x_{k-1}$`, applies the transition matrix `$A$` to decay or transform it, adds the effect of the current input `$u_k$` scaled by `$B$` to produce a new state `$x_k$`, and then reads out a scalar `$y_k$` by projecting the state through `$C$`. The output `$y_k$` is a function of the entire history `$u_0 \dots u_k$` because `$x_k$` itself depends on `$x_{k-1}$`, which depends on `$x_{k-2}$`, and so on recursively back to the beginning of the sequence. This is a linear system — the state update and output are both linear in `$x$` and `$u$` — which is what enables the parallel convolution form.

**Why this form:** The linearity of the system distinguishes SSMs from gated RNNs like LSTMs. Gated RNNs use non-linearities (sigmoid, tanh) in the state update, which makes them more expressive per parameter but prevents them from being "unrolled" into a fixed convolutional kernel — the kernel would depend on the input values, making parallelization impossible. The linear SSM can be exactly represented as a convolution with a kernel that depends only on `$A$`, `$B$`, `$C$` and the sequence length, not on the input data. This is the property that enables parallel training via FFT.

**Convolutional (parallel) formulation.** Because the recurrence is linear, it can be explicitly unrolled. Starting from the initial condition `$x_{-1} = \vec{0}$`, the output at step `$k$` can be written as a weighted sum of all previous inputs:

$$y_k = \sum_{j=0}^{k} CA^j B \cdot u_{k-j}$$

where `$C$` is the output vector (shape `$1 \times N$`), `$A$` is the state matrix (shape `$N \times N$`), `$B$` is the input vector (shape `$N \times 1$`), and `$u_{k-j}$` is the input at position `$k-j$`. The term `$CA^j B$` computes, for each lag `$j$`, how much the input from `$j$` steps ago contributes to the current output. It is a scalar because `$C$` (1 × N) times `$A^j$` (N × N) times `$B$` (N × 1) produces a scalar.

**What this expression means operationally:** To compute the output `$y_k$`, take all previous inputs `$u_0, u_1, \dots, u_k$`, multiply each by a weight that depends on how far back it occurred (lag `$j$`), and sum. The weight for lag `$j$` is `$CA^j B$` — it is determined entirely by the learned parameters `$A, B, C$` and the lag `$j$`, not by the input values. This means the entire sequence of weights for all possible lags can be precomputed as a single convolution kernel.

The SSM kernel `$K \in \mathbb{R}^L$` is defined by collecting these weights for all lags from 0 to `$L-1$`:

$$K = (CB, CAB, CA^2B, \dots, CA^{L-1}B)$$
$$y_k = \sum_{j=0}^{k} K_j \cdot u_{k-j}$$
$$y = K * u$$

where `$K$` is the convolution kernel of length `$L$`, `$K_j = CA^j B$` is the kernel weight at lag `$j$`, `$u$` is the input sequence of length `$L$`, `$y$` is the output sequence of length `$L$`, and `$*$` denotes discrete convolution. The third line states that the entire output sequence `$y$` can be computed at once by convolving the kernel `$K$` with the input `$u$`.

**What this convolution computes:** Instead of stepping through the recurrence sequentially (compute `$y_0$` from `$u_0$`, then `$y_1$` from `$u_0$` and `$u_1$`, then `$y_2$` from `$u_0$, $u_1$, $u_2$`, etc.), the convolution computes all outputs simultaneously. For each output position `$k$`, it slides the kernel over the input, multiplies aligned elements, and sums. This is exactly what the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) accelerates: convolution in the time domain becomes element-wise multiplication in the frequency domain, reducing the complexity from `$O(L^2)$` (naive convolution) to `$O(L \log L)$` (FFT-based convolution). The paper's SSM sublayer uses FFT to execute this convolution efficiently on both GPU and TPU hardware.

**Why this form:** The convolution formulation is what makes the SSM sublayer parallelizable during training. If the model had to use the recurrent formulation during training, each time step would depend on the previous state, forcing sequential computation across all `$L$` positions. With the convolution formulation, the entire output sequence is computed in one FFT-based operation that parallelizes across positions. This is analogous to how Transformers parallelize across positions during training despite being applied autoregressively at inference time — the difference is that the SSM's parallel form has `$O(L \log L)$` complexity rather than Transformer's `$O(L^2)$`.

**Structured kernels (S4/DSS).** In structured SSMs, the matrices `$A$` and `$B$` are not freely parameterized but are analytically derived using the HiPPO framework (Gu et al., 2020). The HiPPO theory shows that for an `$N$`-dimensional state `$x_t$` to optimally represent the history of `$u$` as a polynomial approximation, `$A$` and `$B$` must take specific forms. The paper uses the diagonalized version of S4 called DSS (Diagonal State Space, Gupta et al., 2022), where `$A$` is restricted to be a diagonal matrix with complex-valued entries. This has two important properties: (1) the kernel `$K$` can be computed extremely efficiently because powers of a diagonal matrix `$A^j$` are just element-wise powers of the diagonal entries, and (2) the kernel `$K$` is **independent of the sequence length `$L$`** — the same `$A, B, C$` parameters can be used to compile a kernel of any length, enabling length generalization. At inference time on a longer sequence, the model simply compiles `$K$` to the new length `$L'$` using the same `$A, B, C$` and runs the convolution. The paper sets the SSM state dimension to `$N = 16$` for efficiency (Section 4.3).

**Unstructured parameterized filters (Hyena-based).** The alternative is to directly parameterize the convolution kernel `$\bar{K} \in \mathbb{R}^L$` as trainable weights, without deriving it from `$A, B, C$` matrices. The paper adopts the regularization approach from the Hyena architecture (Poli et al., 2023):

$$\bar{K}_t = e^{-\alpha t} \cdot \left(\text{FFN} \circ \text{PositionalEncoding}\right)(t)$$

where `$\bar{K}_t$` is the kernel weight at position `$t$`, `$\alpha$` is a decay rate, `$e^{-\alpha t}$` is an exponential decay that biases the kernel toward locality (recent inputs matter more), and `$\text{FFN} \circ \text{PositionalEncoding}(t)$` is a feed-forward network applied to a positional encoding of index `$t$` that generates the learnable part of the kernel. The FFN decouples the parameter count from the sequence length: instead of learning `$L$` independent kernel weights, the model learns a function (the FFN) that maps position indices to kernel values.

**What this computes:** For each lag `$t$`, the kernel weight is the product of a fixed exponential decay `$e^{-\alpha t}$` and a learned function of position. The exponential decay ensures that the kernel smoothly decreases with lag — the model can still learn long-range dependencies through the FFN component, but the decay provides a strong inductive bias toward shorter-range patterns. The convolution is then `$y = \bar{K} * u$`.

**Why this form:** The unstructured kernel has two advantages over S4: (1) the kernel `$\bar{K}$` does not need to be recompiled from `$A, B, C$` when the sequence length changes, which speeds up training when using multiple filters (as in the MF variant); (2) `$\bar{K}$` has more free parameters because it is not constrained by the `$A, B$` matrix factorization, potentially enabling richer representations. The paper's results (Table 1) show that the UNSTRUCT variants (BST:SH:UNSTRUCT, BST:MF:UNSTRUCT) consistently outperform their S4 counterparts on perplexity for the training length of 4k, supporting the hypothesis that the additional parameter freedom helps. However, the UNSTRUCT kernel has a critical limitation: because it is parameterized by a positional encoding and FFN, it does not naturally extend to longer sequences at inference time — the FFN was trained to produce kernel values for positions 0 through `$L-1$`, and extending to positions beyond `$L$` requires extrapolation that may not generalize. This is visible in Figure 3, where BST:MF:UNSTRUCT-L perplexity degrades on unseen 16k and 65k sequence lengths, while BST:SH:S4-L (structured) continues to improve.

**Multi-dimensional extension.** The description above treats the SSM as operating on scalar inputs. In practice, the input to the SSM sublayer is a sequence of token embeddings with `$D$` dimensions. The SSM is applied independently to each dimension: the same `$A$`, `$B$`, `$C$` parameters (or the same kernel `$\bar{K}$`) are used for all `$D$` dimensions, treating each dimension as a separate 1-D signal. This is sometimes called "single-input single-output" (SISO) mode. The output is a sequence of the same shape as the input, `$L \times D$`, where each position's vector contains contextualized information about the history up to that position.

**Down-sampling for efficiency.** The paper finds that FFT operations are the primary bottleneck when training SSMs on TPUs (Section 3.4). To mitigate this, the SSM sublayer first projects the input embeddings from dimension `$D$` down to `$D/4$` (a quarter of the embedding size), reducing the number of FFTs by a factor of 4. After the SSM convolution produces the contextualized sequence at dimension `$D/4$`, a second linear projection lifts back to the full embedding dimension `$D$`. The paper notes that this down-sampling has negligible impact on perplexity but significantly improves training speed.

#### Context State Collection: Three Variants of Integrating SSM Outputs into Attention

The SSM sublayer produces a sequence of contextualized vectors, one per input position. But feeding the entire sequence of length `$L$` into the Block Transformer's cross-attention would defeat the purpose of block-wise processing — the attention cost would be `$O(L \cdot W)$` per block, which is BRECT's complexity that BST aims to avoid. Instead, the paper collects a compact set of context states of size `$S$` (where `$S \ll L$` and typically `$S = W$`) and feeds only those states to the cross-attention heads. The key design axis is **how** to select or construct these `$S$` context states from the SSM's `$L$`-length output sequence.

The paper formalizes this design space as a **redundancy-retrievability tradeoff** (Section 3.3). The SSM output at adjacent positions is highly correlated — the state updates are incremental, so position `$t$` and position `$t+1$` contain very similar information. Including adjacent states therefore creates **redundancy**, which makes it easy for the attention mechanism to "retrieve" the current block's token-level information if needed (because the context states for the current block are directly derived from the SSM outputs at those same positions). But redundancy also consumes capacity: the `$S$` context states are the attention heads' only window into the long-range context, and if they are highly correlated, the effective amount of unique information they carry is less than `$S \times D$`. At the other extreme, constructing context states from independent SSM filters that each specialize in different aspects of the history creates **minimal redundancy** and maximizes information content per state, but makes it harder to retrieve specific local information because no context state directly corresponds to a particular time step.

#### Variant 1: Single-Head (SH) — Maximum Redundancy, Maximum Retrievability

In the Single-Head approach (illustrated in Figure 1, left side), the SSM produces a single contextualized sequence (one filter, one output channel) of shape `$(B \times L \times D)$`, where `$B$` is batch size, `$L$` is sequence length, and `$D$` is embedding dimension. This sequence is then divided into blocks of size `$W$` along the length dimension. For each block `$b$`, the context states are the SSM outputs at positions `$bW$` through `$(b+1)W - 1$` — that is, the contiguous segment of the SSM output sequence that temporally aligns with the current block's token positions. The result for each block is a tensor of shape `$(W \times D)$`, representing `$S = W$` context states.

These `$W$` context states, each of dimension `$D$`, are then projected to `$H$` attention heads via standard fully connected layers, producing a context tensor of shape `$(W \times D \times H)$` per block. This projection is the same for all blocks — the SSM's output is projected into the head space after the context states are collected.

**Causal masking.** Because the context states correspond to SSM outputs at the same positions as the current block's tokens, and the SSM at position `$t$` contains information about all preceding positions `$0 \dots t$`, a token at position `$i$` within the block can only attend to context states at positions up to `$i$`. A triangular causal mask is applied during cross-attention to enforce this: the query at position `$i$` can attend to context states at positions `$0$` through `$i$`, but not `$i+1$` through `$W-1$`. This mask is implemented as an additive bias of `$-\infty$` on the attention logits for the masked positions, ensuring they receive zero attention weight after softmax.

**Positional encoding.** Because the context states are taken from adjacent SSM outputs, they inherently contain positional information — the SSM convolution is incremental, and position `$t+1$`'s output differs from position `$t$`'s in a way that reflects the new input at `$t+1$`. The paper finds that no additional "context IDs" or positional embeddings are needed for the SH variant. The SSM's built-in positional encoding through incremental state updates is sufficient for the cross-attention heads to distinguish between context states from different positions.

**Why this design:** The SH approach maximizes retrievability. If the Block Transformer needs to access token-level information about the recent past — e.g., to attend to a specific token from a few positions ago — that information is present in the context states because the context states are exact SSM outputs at those positions. The cost is high redundancy: the context state at position `$t$` and position `$t+1$` differ only by the incremental incorporation of one new token, so the `$W$` context states span a low-dimensional subspace within the `$W \times D$` tensor. The paper hypothesizes that this variant works best on PG19 (Table 1), where local context is more important for next-token prediction (e.g., tracking character names, dialogue, and immediate narrative flow), because the redundancy makes local retrieval easy.

#### Variant 2: Multi-Head (MH) — Balanced Redundancy, Complementary Features

In the Multi-Head approach (illustrated in Figure 2, left side), the SSM is modified to produce `$H$` separate output sequences, one per attention head. Instead of a single output vector `$C \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times N}$` that maps the state to a scalar, the MH variant uses `$H$` separate output matrices `$[C_1, C_2, \dots, C_H]$`, each mapping the state to a scalar. The SSM computation proceeds identically for all `$D$` embedding dimensions (the same `$A$` and `$B$` are used), but the final projection to produce the output is done `$H$` times with different learned `$C_h$` matrices. The result is a sequence of shape `$(L \times D \times H)$` — for each position and each embedding dimension, there are `$H$` different contextualized values, each produced by a different readout of the internal state.

For each block `$b$`, the context states are taken from the SSM outputs at positions `$bW$` through `$(b+1)W - 1$`, exactly as in SH, but now each position has `$H$` channels instead of 1. The per-block context tensor has shape `$(W \times D \times H)$` directly — no additional projection to heads is needed because the SSM already produced per-head outputs. The `$h$`-th attention head receives the context states from the `$h$`-th SSM output channel.

**Causal masking** is applied identically to SH: a triangular mask within each block prevents queries from attending to context states at future positions.

**Why this design:** The key conceptual difference from SH is that each `$C_h$` matrix has **direct access to the full SSM state `$x_k \in \mathbb{R}^N$`** before mapping it down to a scalar. In the SH variant, the SSM first maps the state to a scalar (through a single `$C$`), producing a single value per dimension per position, and then the Block Transformer projects these scalars to `$H$` heads — the projection to heads happens *after* the state has already been compressed to a scalar. In the MH variant, each head gets its own `$C_h$` that reads directly from the `$N$`-dimensional state. The paper frames this as enabling the SSM to "extract complementary features from the summarized history" (Section 3.3): different `$C_h$` matrices can learn to attend to different aspects of the internal state, producing context states that are less correlated with each other than the adjacent-time context states in SH. This reduces redundancy while still providing retrievability because the context states still correspond to specific time positions.

The MH variant requires approximately 8% more parameters than SH (Table 1: BST:MH:S4 at 218M vs BST:SH:S4 at 202M) because of the additional `$C$` matrices. The paper runs only one MH experiment (BST:MH:S4) since it requires more parameters while performing similarly to SH on PG19 (11.60 vs 11.57 perplexity), and SH is faster.

#### Variant 3: Multi-Filter (MF) — Minimum Redundancy, Maximum Capacity Utilization

The Multi-Filter approach (illustrated in Figure 2, right side) fundamentally changes how context states are constructed. Instead of taking adjacent SSM outputs from the current block, the MF variant uses `$S$` independent SSM filters (separate convolution kernels), runs `$S$` separate convolutions over the full input sequence, and retains only the **last output** from each filter for the previous window. The `$S$` context states are independent of the block size `$W$` — the paper uses `$S = 32$` for smaller models and `$S = 64$` for larger models.

**Filter construction.** Each of the `$S$` filters is a separate convolution kernel. For the S4 variant, each filter uses the same `$A$` and `$B$` matrices but a different `$C$` vector, producing `$S$` different kernels. For the UNSTRUCT variant, each filter has its own independently parameterized kernel `$\bar{K}^{(s)}$` produced by its own FFN and exponential decay. Each filter convolves with the input sequence independently, producing `$S$` output sequences, each of shape `$(L \times D)$`. After down-sampling and convolution, the output is a tensor of shape `$(L \times D \times S)$`.

**Context state collection.** The input sequence is divided into windows of size `$W$` as before. For window `$b$` (containing positions `$bW$` through `$(b+1)W - 1$`), the context states are taken from the outputs of the `$S$` filters at the **last position of the previous window**, i.e., position `$bW - 1$`. Each filter produces, at position `$bW - 1$`, a vector of length `$D$`. Stacking these `$S$` vectors gives a context tensor of shape `$(S \times D)$` for window `$b$`. This tensor is then projected to `$H$` heads via fully connected layers, producing the final per-block context tensor of shape `$(S \times D \times H)$`.

Because the context states for window `$b$` are taken from position `$bW - 1$` (the end of the previous window), they contain no information about the current window's tokens — they are purely a summary of the past up to the boundary. This means **cross-attention masking is no longer required**: the context states are causally valid for all query positions in the current block, since no context state contains information that any query position wouldn't have access to in an autoregressive setting. This is a practical advantage: the attention computation is simpler and marginally faster without masking.

**Shifting and initialization.** The context states for window `$b$` come from filter outputs at position `$bW - 1$`. But what about the first window (`$b = 0$`)? There is no previous window. The paper handles this by replacing the context states for the first window with **learned trainable embeddings** — a tensor of shape `$(S \times D)$` that is optimized during training. For all subsequent windows, the context states are the filter outputs as described. Additionally, the `$S$` filters each produce an output that is specialized to a particular aspect of the sequence (because each filter has its own kernel), but the ordering of filters is arbitrary. To allow the cross-attention heads to distinguish between different filters' outputs, the MF variant adds **learned "context IDs"** — a set of `$S$` unique learned vectors that are added to the context states before they are projected to heads. These context IDs are analogous to positional embeddings but encode filter identity rather than temporal position.

**Why this design:** The MF variant minimizes redundancy because the `$S$` context states come from `$S$` different filters that can learn to extract complementary information. Unlike SH, where the context states are adjacent in time and highly correlated, the MF context states can be approximately orthogonal in the information they carry. This maximizes the effective capacity of the cross-attention — each attention head has access to `$S$` diverse summaries of the past, rather than `$S$` nearly-identical snapshots from adjacent time steps. The cost is reduced retrievability: a specific token from the recent past is not explicitly represented in any context state; the cross-attention must reconstruct or infer it from the filter outputs. The paper's results (Table 1) show that MF outperforms SH on arXiv and GitHub, where long-range dependencies (cross-references in LaTeX, import dependencies in code) are more important for next-token prediction than fine-grained local context. On PG19, where local narrative coherence matters more, SH performs better.

The MF variant requires approximately 8% more parameters than SH (Table 1: BST:MF:S4 at 217M vs BST:SH:S4 at 202M) due to the multiple filters. The paper notes that to improve FFT efficiency for MF, it lowers the SSM state dimension `$D$` by an additional factor of 2 beyond the standard down-sampling (Section 4.1, Training details), with negligible impact on perplexity.

#### The Block Transformer Sublayer: Local Attention with Cross-Attention to Context States

The Block Transformer sublayer is the "Transformer" half of the BST layer. It operates on the input sequence divided into blocks of size `$W$` and performs two types of attention within each block. This sublayer is identical in structure to the non-recurrent cell of the Block-Recurrent Transformer (BRECT), except that it receives **precomputed SSM context states** instead of sequentially-updated recurrent state vectors.

**Input slicing.** Given an input sequence of token embeddings `$X \in \mathbb{R}^{L \times D}$` (where `$L$` is sequence length and `$D$` is embedding dimension), the sequence is divided into `$L/W$` blocks of size `$W$`. Each block `$X_b \in \mathbb{R}^{W \times D}$` is processed independently by the Block Transformer. Crucially, because the context states for each block are already computed by the SSM sublayer and context state collector, **all `$L/W$` blocks can be processed in parallel**. The paper implements this by vectorizing the Block Transformer cell using JAX's `vmap` operation (Pseudocode 5), which maps the same computation over all blocks simultaneously.

**Self-attention over block tokens.** Within each block, standard multi-head self-attention is computed over the `$W$` tokens:

$$\text{SelfAttn}(X_b) = \text{softmax}\left(\frac{Q_b K_b^T}{\sqrt{d_k}} + M_{\text{causal}}\right) V_b$$

where `$Q_b = X_b W^Q$`, `$K_b = X_b W^K$`, `$V_b = X_b W^V$` are the query, key, and value projections of the block's token embeddings, `$d_k = D/H$` is the per-head dimension, and `$M_{\text{causal}}$` is a causal mask ensuring each position attends only to positions ≤ its own within the block. This self-attention captures fine-grained local interactions — which tokens within the immediate window of size `$W$` (typically 512) are relevant for predicting the next token.

**Cross-attention to context states.** In parallel with self-attention, each block's tokens also perform cross-attention to the context states `$C_b \in \mathbb{R}^{S \times D \times H}$` produced by the context state collector. The context states serve as the keys and values, while the block tokens serve as the queries:

$$\text{CrossAttn}(X_b, C_b) = \text{softmax}\left(\frac{Q_b (C_b W^K)^T}{\sqrt{d_k}} + M_{\text{ctx}}\right) (C_b W^V)$$

where `$Q_b = X_b W^Q_{\text{cross}}$` is the query projection (separate from the self-attention query projection), `$C_b W^K$` and `$C_b W^V$` are key and value projections of the context states, and `$M_{\text{ctx}}$` is a causal mask that depends on the context state variant. For SH and MH, `$M_{\text{ctx}}$` is a triangular mask preventing query position `$i$` from attending to context states at positions `$> i$`. For MF, `$M_{\text{ctx}}$` is all zeros (no masking) because the context states are from the previous window's end and are causally valid for all positions.

**What this cross-attention computes:** For each query token in the block, the attention mechanism computes a weighted sum of the context states, where the weights reflect how relevant each context state is to the query. Since the context states encode information about the entire preceding sequence (compressed through the SSM), this allows each token to "retrieve" relevant long-range context. For example, in a code file with an import statement at the beginning, a token deep in the file can attend to a context state that captured information about that import.

**Concatenation and projection.** The outputs of self-attention and cross-attention are concatenated along the feature dimension:

$$O_b = W^O [\text{SelfAttn}(X_b) \mathbin\Vert \text{CrossAttn}(X_b, C_b)]$$

where `$\mathbin\Vert$` denotes concatenation along the last dimension, and `$W^O \in \mathbb{R}^{2D \times D}$` is a learned projection matrix. The concatenation doubles the feature dimension (self-attention produces `$D$`-dimensional vectors, cross-attention produces `$D$`-dimensional vectors), and the projection compresses back to `$D$`. This allows the Block Transformer to combine local and long-range information adaptively — the projection `$W^O$` can learn to weight the two sources differently depending on what is useful for the task.

**Feed-forward network.** After the concatenation and projection, a standard position-wise feed-forward network (FFN) is applied: two linear transformations with a ReLU activation in between, with the standard hidden dimension expansion factor of 4 (so for embedding dimension 1024, the FFN hidden layer is 4096). The FFN is applied independently to each position. The output is the final output of the BST layer, which feeds into the next layer's SSM sublayer.

**Why replace recurrent states with SSM context states:** In BRECT, the recurrent state vectors for block `$b$` are produced by a recurrent cell that takes the block's token embeddings and the previous block's recurrent state as input — a sequential dependency. The Block Transformer in BRECT then cross-attends to these recurrent states exactly as described above. In BST, the SSM precomputes context states for all blocks in parallel, breaking the sequential dependency. The Block Transformer's operation is identical — it still performs self-attention and cross-attention — but the source of the cross-attention keys and values is different (SSM output instead of recurrent cell output). This is why the paper describes BST as retaining the Block Transformer cell "unaltered, except that we no longer need to run the recurrent unit of the BRECT cell since we are maintaining the context via an SSM" (Section 3.2).

#### Runtime Complexity Analysis and Parallelization

The computational efficiency of BST relative to BRECT is a central claim of the paper, so understanding the complexity analysis is essential.

**BRECT complexity.** In BRECT, each block is processed sequentially by the recurrent cell, and the Block Transformer cell also runs sequentially per block (because the recurrent state for block `$b$` depends on block `$b-1$`'s processing). The cross-attention in BRECT computes attention between `$W$` query tokens and `$S$` recurrent states (where `$S = W$`), which has cost `$O(W \cdot S) = O(W^2)$` per block. With `$L/W$` blocks, the total cost is `$O(L/W \times W^2) = O(LW)$`. The paper notes that the full BRECT cost includes "`$W^2 + S^2 + 2SW$`" terms per block (Section 4.3), but the dominant term is the cross-attention, making the overall complexity approximately `$O(LW)$`.

**BST complexity.** In BST, there are two decoupled costs:

1. **SSM sublayer cost:** Convolution via FFT has complexity `$O(D \cdot L \log L)$` (or more precisely, `$O((D/4) \cdot L \log L)$` with down-sampling). This cost is incurred once for the entire sequence, independent of the block processing.

2. **Block Transformer cost:** Self-attention within each block has cost `$O(W^2)$`. Cross-attention to `$S$` context states has cost `$O(W \cdot S)$`. With `$L/W$` blocks processed in parallel, the effective cost is `$O(W^2 + W \cdot S)$` — the per-block cost, not multiplied by `$L/W$` because parallelization eliminates the sequential multiplier.

The total BST complexity is:

$$O(W^2 + W \cdot S) + O(D \cdot L \log L)$$

Since `$S$` is typically equal to or less than `$W$`, the Block Transformer term is `$O(W^2)$`, and the total is `$O(W^2 + L \log L)$` (dropping the dimension factor for simplicity).

**What this means in practice:** BRECT scales as `$O(LW)$` — the cost grows linearly with sequence length times window size. BST scales as the sum of a term that depends on window size (not sequence length) plus a term that grows superlinearly but slowly (`$L \log L$`) with sequence length. For typical values (e.g., `$L = 4096$`, `$W = 512$`), `$L \log L \approx 4096 \times 12 \approx 50000$` while `$LW = 4096 \times 512 \approx 2$` million, so BST's Block Transformer cost (`$W^2 = 262k$`) is dominated by the SSM cost, which is still far less than BRECT's cost. This analysis explains the dramatic speedup in Figure 4 (left).

**Empirical validation.** Figure 4 (left) shows measured forward-pass times for a single BST layer vs. a BRECT layer vs. a non-recurrent sliding-window layer (SLIDE:12L) on GPU. At a sequence length of 4k (the training length), BRECT is approximately 15× slower than SLIDE:12L, while BST:SH is only about 1.5–2× slower — a 10× improvement over BRECT. BST:MH is 3–4× slower than SLIDE:12L. As sequence length increases to 65k, BST:SH remains 6–11× faster than BRECT, though the gap narrows as the SSM's `$L \log L$` term grows. The paper also notes that at 4k, the SSM and Block Transformer costs are nearly equal — "the bottom-most two lines on the left of Figure 4 are almost overlapping" (Section 1) — meaning neither component is a bottleneck relative to the other.

**Why block parallelization is possible:** The key insight is that the SSM computes context states for all blocks before any Block Transformer processing begins, using FFT which operates over the full sequence in parallel. Once the context states are computed, each block's Block Transformer cell has all the inputs it needs — the block's token embeddings (known from the start) and the block's context states (precomputed). There is no information flow between blocks during the Block Transformer stage: block `$b$` does not depend on the output of block `$b-1$`'s Block Transformer cell because the long-range information that would have been in the recurrent state is now in the precomputed SSM context states. This is in contrast to BRECT, where block `$b$`'s recurrent state depends on block `$b-1$`'s recurrent cell output, creating a sequential chain.

#### Implementation Details: Model Configuration, Training, and Ablations

**Layer placement within the model stack.** The paper does not replace every Transformer layer with a BST layer. Instead, BST layers are inserted at specific positions within a stack of otherwise standard Block Transformer layers (which only have self-attention within windows, no cross-attention to context states, and no SSM sublayer). Through ablation studies (Table 3, Appendix E), the paper finds that a single BST layer placed at index 9 (out of 12 layers) has the greatest effect on perplexity — this is consistent with prior findings in Memorizing Transformer and BRECT that memory-augmented layers are most effective in the middle-to-late layers, not at the very beginning or end. The paper's final configurations use:

- **Small models (~200M parameters):** 12 total layers, with BST layers at positions {1, 7, 9}. The SSM at layer 1 (right after word embeddings) provides positional encoding through its structured kernel, eliminating the need for global learned positional embeddings. Layers 7 and 9 provide long-range context in the middle-upper stack.
- **Large models (~400M parameters):** 12 total layers (but with doubled FFN hidden size, from 4096 to 8192, and 12 attention heads instead of 8), with BST layers at positions {1, 5, 7, 9}.

Table 4 shows that adding more BST layers improves perplexity (2 layers: 11.69, 3 layers: 11.57, 4 layers: 11.21, 5 layers: 11.20 on PG19), but the gain plateaus at 4-5 layers, and each added BST layer increases training step time by 3.5%.

**SSM state dimension.** The SSM internal state dimension `$N$` (the size of `$x_k$`) is set to `$N = 16$` for all main experiments. Table 5 shows the effect of varying `$N$`: `$N = 8$` gives perplexity 11.95 with 0.7× step time, `$N = 16$` gives 11.57 with 1.0× step time, `$N = 32$` gives 11.55 with 1.8× step time, and `$N = 64$` gives 11.54 with 3.2× step time. The diminishing returns beyond `$N = 16$` combined with the linear increase in step time motivate the choice of 16.

**Down-sampling ratio.** The SSM sublayer projects from embedding dimension `$D$` to `$D/4$` before the convolution and back to `$D$` after. For the MF variant, an additional factor of 2 down-sampling is applied (to `$D/8$`) to further reduce FFT costs when running `$S$` parallel filters. The paper notes this has negligible impact on perplexity.

**Number of filters in MF.** The MF models use `$S = 32$` filters for the ~200M parameter models and `$S = 64$` filters for the ~400M parameter models. Increasing the number of filters increases the diversity of context states (less redundancy) at the cost of additional FFT operations.

**Context IDs and positional encoding.** For the MF variant, the paper adds learned "context IDs" — trainable vectors of dimension `$D$` that are added to each of the `$S$` context states to allow the cross-attention heads to distinguish which filter produced which context state. These context IDs are analogous to positional embeddings but encode filter identity. For SH and MH, no context IDs are needed because the SSM's incremental convolution naturally encodes temporal position into the context states — the difference between context state at position `$t$` and position `$t+1$` reflects the input at `$t+1$`. For token embeddings in the Block Transformer, the paper uses a T5-style relative position bias (Raffel et al., 2019) rather than absolute learned positional embeddings, because "the SSM does also encode positional information into the context" (Section 3.4).

**Training configuration.** The paper uses the same training setup as BRECT (Hutchins et al., 2022) implemented in the Meliad library in JAX/Flax. The Adam optimizer (Kingma and Ba, 2015) is used with a batch size of 32 and a sequence length `$L = 4096$` for training. The learning rate and weight decay are not explicitly stated in the main paper — the paper notes it did not perform grid search (unlike GSS-HYBRID) and "used the same learning rate for all layers." Training is performed on TPUv4 hardware, and training compute is tracked in TPUv4 hours for fair comparison with baselines (Table 1).

**Length generalization mechanism.** At inference time on longer sequences, the model with structured S4 kernels can compile the convolution kernel `$K$` to the new sequence length `$L'$` using the same learned `$A, B, C$` matrices. Because these matrices define the kernel for any length through `$K_j = CA^j B$`, extending to `$L' > L$` simply requires computing more powers of `$A$` — there is no learned positional embedding that would need extrapolation. The paper uses an S4 SSM in the very first layer (right after word embeddings) specifically to "extend the positional encoding to various lengths at inference" (Section 4.1, Training details). This is why BST:SH:S4-L generalizes well to 65k (Figure 3) while BST:MF:UNSTRUCT-L, whose kernel is parameterized by a fixed-length FFN, does not.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: The SSM as a Parallelizable Replacement for Sequential Recurrence in Hybrid Architectures

The conceptual move here is not "SSMs are good for long sequences" — that was already established by S4, DSS, and related work. The distinctive idea is recognizing that an SSM can **replace the sequential recurrent cell in a block-wise attention architecture** and thereby convert an inherently serial computation into an embarrassingly parallel one, without sacrificing the architectural role that recurrence played.

Prior to this work, the dominant approach to extending Transformer context beyond local windows was some form of recurrence across blocks: Transformer-XL cached and replayed hidden states, Memorizing Transformer augmented attention with a kNN retrieval over a non-differentiable memory, and BRECT built the most sophisticated version — a learned recurrent cell within each layer that compressed block information into state vectors passed sequentially from one block to the next. In all of these, the recurrence creates a chain of sequential dependencies: block b's computation cannot begin until block b−1's recurrent cell has finished. This is not merely an implementation inconvenience — it is an architectural bottleneck that grows with sequence length. The paper quantifies the cost bluntly in Figure 4 (left): at 4k tokens, BRECT runs **15× slower** than the same architecture without recurrence. This is a crippling overhead that discourages using recurrence even when it would improve quality.

The BST's insight is that an SSM can fill the exact same functional role — producing a compressed representation of the past for each block to attend to — but can do so **in parallel across the entire sequence** via FFT-based convolution. The SSM computes context states for all blocks in one forward pass, after which every Block Transformer cell has everything it needs and can run simultaneously. The sequential bottleneck disappears. This is architecturally analogous to how Transformers themselves replaced the sequential time-step bottleneck of RNNs with parallelizable self-attention — BST applies the same pattern one level up, replacing sequential cross-block recurrence with parallelizable convolution. The result is that BST brings the 15× slowdown down to less than 2× (Figure 4, left), making the long-range context mechanism practical to train. This is not a small constant-factor optimization — it changes whether one would use this architecture at all, which is why it qualifies as a **fundamental architectural contribution** rather than an incremental refinement.

Crucially, this substitution is not free. The SSM must compress the entire history into each context state vector, while BRECT's recurrent cell had direct access to the previous block's token-level hidden representations. The SSM is doing genuinely harder compression — summarizing up to the full sequence length into a fixed-size state — while the recurrent cell only had to bridge a gap of W tokens. That BST matches BRECT's perplexity (Table 1) despite this harder compression task is evidence that the SSM's structured memory (via HiPPO-optimal A, B matrices) is surprisingly effective at long-range retention. The paper does not dwell on this, but it is a significant empirical finding: structured state space memory is not merely theoretically elegant but practically competitive with learned recurrent gating for language modeling, at a fraction of the computational cost.

### Innovation 2: A Design-Space Analysis Organized Around the Redundancy-Retrievability Tradeoff

Rather than proposing a single integration mechanism and benchmarking it, the paper develops **three systematically related variants** — Single-Head, Multi-Head, and Multi-Filter — and frames their differences along a single conceptual axis: the tradeoff between **redundancy** (how correlated the context states are with each other) and **retrievability** (how easy it is for attention heads to recover specific local information from them). This is a genuinely novel conceptual framework for thinking about memory-augmented transformers.

Before this work, the field's approach to combining SSMs with attention was essentially "put SSM layers here, put attention layers there, and let the model figure it out" — this describes GSS-HYBRID, which simply interleaves GSS and Transformer layers. There was no vocabulary or framework for reasoning about *how* the SSM's output should be presented to the attention mechanism. Should attention heads see the SSM output at adjacent time steps? At the previous block's boundary only? Through separate readout heads? These questions were underexplored.

The paper's framework makes these questions precise. **Single-Head** (SH) takes adjacent SSM outputs as context states, producing a sequence that is maximally redundant (adjacent states differ only by one incremental state update) but maximally retrievable (if a token needs to access local information from a few positions ago, that exact information is present in the corresponding context state). **Multi-Filter** (MF) uses independent filters that each extract complementary features across the full history, retaining only the final output per filter — minimal redundancy, maximum capacity utilization, but no direct token-position retrievability. **Multi-Head** (MH) sits between them: separate readout heads from the same SSM state produce context states that are less correlated than adjacent-time states in SH but more temporally grounded than filter summaries in MF.

This framework is intellectually valuable because it **predicts dataset-dependent performance differences** that the paper then validates. PG19 — narrative text where tracking local character mentions and dialogue coherence depends on recent context — favors SH (higher retrievability). arXiv and GitHub — where cross-references, definitions, and import dependencies create long-range dependencies independent of local adjacency — favor MF (higher capacity for diverse features). This is not just a ranking of variants; it provides a diagnostic lens: which axis (local retrievability or diverse long-range features) matters for a given task determines which variant to choose. The framework is **extensible**: future work can position new integration schemes along the same spectrum and reason about what they gain or lose.

This qualifies as a **conceptual innovation** — it provides a language and a mental model for a design space that previously lacked one. The performance gains between variants are modest (fractions of a perplexity point in Table 1), but the intellectual value lies in the systematic characterization rather than any single winning configuration.

### Innovation 3: Evidence That Direct State Integration into Attention Surpasses Layer Interleaving

In the landscape of hybrid SSM-Transformer architectures, there exists a fundamental design choice: should the SSM and attention mechanisms operate in **separate, interleaved layers** (each layer is either SSM or attention, with information passing between them only through layer outputs), or should they be **integrated into a single layer** where attention heads can directly query the SSM's compressed memory? The paper provides the first systematic evidence that **integration substantially outperforms interleaving**, and it does so at a matched compute budget.

The prior state of the art, GSS-HYBRID-L, represented the interleaving approach: a stack of 32 GSS layers with Transformer layers interspersed at every 4th position. This architecture was carefully tuned — the authors performed a learning rate grid search and used separate learning rates and weight decay for SSM and Transformer layers to stabilize training. BST integrates the SSM and attention into a single layer where the SSM's context states are the direct keys and values for the Transformer's cross-attention heads. The comparison is apples-to-apples in terms of compute: Table 1 shows BST:SH:S4-L (366M params, 0.8k TPUv4 hours) vs. GSS-HYBRID-L (373M params, 0.8k TPUv4 hours) producing 10.47 vs. 10.52 perplexity on PG19, 2.49 vs. 2.51 on arXiv, and 1.86 vs. 1.88 on GitHub — a consistent 1.5–4% improvement. Moreover, BST achieved this **without grid search and without per-layer learning rate tuning**, suggesting the integrated architecture is not only more effective but more robust and easier to train.

Why does integration help? The paper's framing implies an answer: when SSM and attention are interleaved, the attention heads can only work with whatever representation the SSM layer produced as its output — this is a lossy summary optimized for the SSM's own reconstruction objective, not for what the attention heads need to retrieve. When the SSM's memory is directly accessed via cross-attention keys and values, the attention heads can learn to query the SSM's state representations in a task-specific way, attending to whichever aspects of the compressed history are relevant for the current prediction. This is analogous to the difference between receiving a pre-written summary of a document versus being able to ask specific questions about it — the latter extracts more task-relevant information from the same underlying representation.

This finding is significant because it establishes a **principle** for designing hybrid architectures, not just a one-off result. It suggests that the computational role of memory in sequence models is not merely to produce a better hidden representation for the next layer, but to serve as a **retrievable context that the attention mechanism actively queries**. This principle — that memory should be integrated into attention, not placed before or after it — could inform future architectures beyond the specific SSM-Transformer combination. It qualifies as a **fundamental architectural insight** with implications beyond the paper's immediate results.

### Innovation 4: Structured SSM Kernels Provide a Built-In Length Generalization Mechanism That Unstructured Kernels Lack

The paper draws a sharp distinction between **structured** SSM kernels (S4/DSS, where the convolution kernel K is compiled from learned A, B, C matrices via K_j = CA^jB) and **unstructured** parameterized filters (Hyena-style, where the kernel is directly parameterized as a learned function of position). This distinction existed in the SSM literature, but the paper provides the first clear evidence that it has **profound consequences for length generalization in hybrid architectures**.

The experimental result is striking and unambiguous. Figure 3 shows BST:SH:S4-L maintaining or improving perplexity when extended from the training length of 4k to 65k tokens on all three datasets (PG19, arXiv, GitHub). BST:MF:UNSTRUCT-L, with comparable or better perplexity at the training length (Table 1: 10.42 vs. 10.47 for SH:S4-L on PG19), **degrades** when extended to 65k — perplexity increases from roughly 10.5 at 4k to above 11.5 at 65k on PG19, and from roughly 2.4 to above 4.0 on arXiv. The reason is precisely the architectural difference: structured kernels define K_j = CA^jB, which is a continuous function of j that can be evaluated at any integer. Unstructured kernels learn K_j through an FFN that maps positions 0 through L−1 to kernel values — there is no mechanism to evaluate the FFN at positions beyond L−1, and extrapolation fails.

This finding matters because it identifies **length generalization as a first-class architectural property** that is determined by the choice of SSM parameterization, not by scale or training procedure. The paper's point is not simply "S4 generalizes better than Hyena" — that is a specific comparison. The broader insight is that **compiling the kernel from a dynamical system (structured) versus directly parameterizing it (unstructured) is the architectural control knob for length generalization**. This gives practitioners a clear criterion: if length generalization beyond the training context is required (as in production deployments where sequence lengths vary), use structured kernels. If maximum performance at the training length is the only objective (as in benchmarks with fixed-length inputs), unstructured kernels may be preferable due to their greater parameter freedom.

The data also shows a nuanced interaction: on PG19, BST:MF:UNSTRUCT-L actually outperforms BST:SH:S4-L up to 16k, suggesting that within a moderate extrapolation range, the superior representational capacity of unstructured kernels can outweigh their length generalization limitations. It is only at extreme lengths (65k) that the structured kernel's principled extrapolation dominates. This adds practical texture to the finding — the "right" choice depends on the expected deployment length distribution.

This insight is **conceptually novel** because it reframes length generalization from a training artifact (something that "just happens" or requires special training recipes) to an **architectural property with a clear mechanistic cause**. It qualifies as a **diagnostic contribution** — it tells future architects what to pay attention to when designing for length generalization.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The paper evaluates on three long-range language modeling benchmarks: **PG19** (a collection of 28,602 full-length books from Project Gutenberg published before 1919, with 50k–100k tokens per book when tokenized; test split reported), **arXiv** (a corpus of scientific articles on Mathematics containing LaTeX source code, theorems, citations, and cross-references that create long-range dependencies; test split reported), and **GitHub** (a dataset of open-source code repositories in C, C++, Java, Python, Go, and TypeScript where files are concatenated along directory tree paths to preserve repository structure; validation split reported). All three are standard benchmarks from prior work — PG19 was introduced by Rae et al. (2020), arXiv and GitHub by Wu et al. (2022) — and were chosen because their long-document structure stresses long-range dependency modeling, which is where SSM-Transformer hybrids are hypothesized to provide the largest benefit over Transformers.

- **Base model(s).** All BST models are decoder-only language models built from stacks of Block-State Transformer layers interleaved with standard Block Transformer layers (which perform self-attention within windows but lack the SSM sublayer and cross-attention to context states). Two parameter scales are used: **~200M parameters** (12 layers, 8 attention heads of dimension 128, embedding dimension 1024, MLP hidden dimension 4096 with ReLU activation) and **~400M parameters** (12 layers but MLP hidden dimension doubled to 8192 and attention heads increased to 12). The base Transformer architecture is the Block-Recurrent Transformer's non-recurrent cell — essentially a decoder-only Transformer with block-wise self-attention — augmented with the BST layer's SSM and cross-attention components. The choice of ~200M and ~400M scales is constrained by the compute budget: the paper tracks exact TPUv4 hours for all models (Table 1) to enable fair compute-matched comparison with baselines, and these scales are within the range studied by prior hybrid architectures (BRECT at 196M, GSS-HYBRID at 373M). The paper does not train models at the billion-parameter scale, which limits the generality of the scaling trends.

- **Metrics.** The primary metric is **perplexity** on held-out data (test split for PG19 and arXiv, validation split for GitHub). Perplexity is the standard language modeling metric — the exponential of the average negative log-likelihood per token — and lower is better. The paper uses the same vocabularies as prior work for fair comparison: a pretrained T5 vocabulary with 32k tokens for PG19 (Raffel et al., 2019) and a LaMDA vocabulary with 32k tokens for arXiv and GitHub (Thoppilan et al., 2022). Error bars (± standard deviation) are reported for PG19 ~200M models by running with three different random seeds; for other configurations, only single-run results are reported due to long training times.

- **Baselines.** Five baselines are compared against:
  - **SLIDE:12L** (Hutchins et al., 2022): A sliding-window Transformer with window size 512 over a segment of 4096 tokens, where the sliding window is differentiable over two blocks. This is the non-recurrent base architecture — essentially BRECT without the recurrent cell — and represents the closest baseline in training speed to BST.
  - **TRSF-XL:2048** (Transformer-XL; Dai et al., 2019): A Transformer with a training window size of 2048 tokens. Uses cached key-value pairs from the previous segment but does not backpropagate through the cache.
  - **BRECT:FIXED:SKIP** (Hutchins et al., 2022): The strongest and fastest Block-Recurrent Transformer configuration from the BRECT paper. Uses a sequential recurrent cell with a "skip" gating mechanism — a linear layer that combines current block hidden representations with past information from previous blocks. This is the most direct architectural predecessor and the primary efficiency baseline (since BST replaces BRECT's sequential recurrence with parallel SSM).
  - **GSS-HYBRID-L** (Mehta et al., 2023): The closest prior SSM-Transformer hybrid. Composed of 32 Gated State Space layers interleaved with Transformer layers at every 4th position starting from layer 2. The GSS layers use Diagonal State Spaces (DSS) with gating. This is the primary quality baseline at matched compute (~370M params, ~0.8k TPUv4 hours). Notably, GSS-HYBRID-L results were obtained after grid search over four learning rates `{6.4, 3.2, 1.6, 0.8} × 10^{-3}` and use separate learning rates and weight decay for SSM and Transformer layers to avoid training instability — while BST uses the same learning rate for all layers with no grid search.

  All baseline results except GSS-HYBRID-L are taken from prior published work (Hutchins et al., 2022; Mehta et al., 2023).

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** Compute is measured in **TPUv4 hours** — the total wall-clock time multiplied by the number of TPUv4 chips used, tracked for every model in Table 1. This is a more direct measure than parameter count or FLOPs because it accounts for implementation efficiency differences (e.g., BRECT's sequential recurrence vs. BST's parallel convolution). The paper aligns training times with GSS-HYBRID-L for fair comparison: smaller models are trained for approximately 0.5k TPUv4 hours on PG19/arXiv and 1.8k on the larger GitHub dataset; larger models are trained for 0.8k TPUv4 hours across all datasets. For the efficiency analysis in Section 4.3, compute is measured by layer-level forward-pass time on GPU at varying sequence lengths (512 to 65k tokens), isolating the architectural efficiency from training overhead.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** For the ~200M PG19 models, the paper reports error bars as the standard deviation across three independent training runs with different random seeds. BRECT:FIXED:SKIP error bars are taken from Hutchins et al. (2022) and are similarly computed from three seeds. For arXiv, GitHub, and all ~400M models, only single runs are reported due to training time constraints. The paper does not perform cross-validation or statistical significance testing. The length generalization experiments (Section 4.2) evaluate trained checkpoints at multiple sequence lengths `{512, 4k, 16k, 65k}` without retraining.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Language Modeling Perplexity at Matched Compute Budgets

The central results are in Table 1, which reports perplexity for all BST variants and baselines on PG19, arXiv, and GitHub at two model scales (~200M and ~400M parameters), with exact TPUv4 training hours tracked for each configuration.

**Headline findings for ~200M parameter models:**

On PG19, BST:SH:UNSTRUCT achieves **11.52 ± 1.1** perplexity, marginally outperforming BRECT:FIXED:SKIP at **11.55 ± 1.1** — essentially tied within error bars — while using only **0.5k TPUv4 hours** compared to BRECT's 0.8k. This is the paper's key efficiency claim: BST matches the best recurrent baseline at roughly 60% of the training compute. BST:SH:S4 achieves 11.57 (within the error bar of BRECT) also at 0.5k hours. The non-recurrent baseline SLIDE:12L reaches only 12.12 at 0.5k hours, demonstrating the value of long-range context integration. TRSF-XL:2048 reaches 11.96 at 0.8k hours — BST improves perplexity while training faster despite having a smaller window size (512 vs. 2048) because the SSM provides long-range context that a larger window alone cannot match.

On arXiv, BST:MF:UNSTRUCT achieves **2.44** (best among ~200M BST variants), outperforming BRECT:FIXED:SKIP at **2.36** by a small margin, and substantially outperforming SLIDE:12L at 2.69 and TRSF-XL:2048 at 2.48. The MF variant's advantage on arXiv — where cross-references and theorem citations create long-range dependencies that are not temporally local — is consistent with the paper's hypothesis that reduced redundancy benefits tasks requiring diverse long-range feature extraction.

On GitHub, BST:MF:S4 achieves **2.07** and BST:SH:UNSTRUCT achieves **2.09**, both competitive with BRECT:FIXED:SKIP at **2.04**. BST outperforms TRSF-XL:2048 (2.01) — an interesting case where the larger-window Transformer matches the hybrid's performance, suggesting that on code, the 2048-token window already captures most relevant dependencies.

**Headline findings for ~400M parameter models (the "-L" variants):**

When matching GSS-HYBRID-L's compute budget (~0.8k TPUv4 hours, ~370M parameters), BST consistently outperforms across all three datasets:

- PG19: BST:SH:UNSTRUCT-L achieves **10.37** vs. GSS-HYBRID-L's **10.52** — a 1.4% improvement. BST:SH:S4-L achieves 10.47, BST:MF:S4-L achieves 10.52 (tied with GSS-HYBRID-L), and BST:MF:UNSTRUCT-L achieves 10.42.
- arXiv: BST:MF:UNSTRUCT-L achieves **2.41** vs. GSS-HYBRID-L's **2.51** — a 4.0% improvement. BST:SH:UNSTRUCT-L at 2.46 and BST:MF:S4-L at 2.46 both outperform GSS-HYBRID-L.
- GitHub: BST:MF:UNSTRUCT-L achieves **1.83** vs. GSS-HYBRID-L's **1.88** — a 2.7% improvement. BST:SH:UNSTRUCT-L at 1.85 and BST:MF:S4-L at 1.84 also surpass GSS-HYBRID-L.

These improvements may appear modest in absolute terms (fractions of a perplexity point), but are significant in context: GSS-HYBRID-L was carefully tuned with learning rate grid search and layer-specific optimization, while BST used a single learning rate for all layers with no grid search. The paper frames this as evidence that direct state integration into attention (BST) is not only more effective but more **robust and easier to train** than interleaving SSM and Transformer layers (GSS-HYBRID).

**Variant comparison across datasets (Table 1, all scales):**

The three context state variants show dataset-dependent performance rankings that validate the redundancy-retrievability framework:

- **SH performs best on PG19** (BST:SH:UNSTRUCT at 11.52 for ~200M, BST:SH:UNSTRUCT-L at 10.37 for ~400M), consistent with PG19's reliance on local narrative coherence where high retrievability of recent context matters.
- **MF performs best on arXiv** (BST:MF:UNSTRUCT at 2.44 for ~200M, BST:MF:UNSTRUCT-L at 2.41 for ~400M) and **GitHub** (BST:MF:UNSTRUCT-L at 1.83 for ~400M), consistent with these datasets' reliance on long-range cross-references and import dependencies where diverse feature extraction (low redundancy) matters more than local retrievability.
- **MH** only appears once (BST:MH:S4 at 218M params, PG19 perplexity 11.60) and performs similarly to SH (11.57) while requiring 8% more parameters — the paper drops further MH experiments due to the unfavorable parameter-efficiency tradeoff.

**UNSTRUCT vs. S4 kernel comparison (Table 1):**

Within each variant, the UNSTRUCT kernel consistently achieves equal or better perplexity than S4 at the training length of 4k:
- BST:SH:UNSTRUCT (11.52) vs. BST:SH:S4 (11.57) on PG19.
- BST:MF:UNSTRUCT (2.44) vs. BST:MF:S4 (2.48) on arXiv.
- BST:MF:UNSTRUCT (2.03) vs. BST:MF:S4 (2.07) on GitHub.

This supports the hypothesis that unstructured kernels' greater parameter freedom — not being constrained by the `A, B` factorization — enables richer representations. However, this advantage comes at the cost of length generalization (see below).

#### Length Generalization to Unseen Sequence Lengths

Figure 3 shows perplexity for ~400M models trained on 4k sequences and evaluated at sequence lengths `{512, 4k, 16k, 65k}` on all three datasets. This experiment tests whether the context mechanisms generalize beyond the training distribution — a critical property for deployment on variable-length inputs.

**Headline findings:**

At 512 tokens, all models perform similarly — the short sequence length means local attention suffices and the long-range context mechanism is not stressed. Differences emerge at 16k and become pronounced at 65k.

At **65k tokens**, BST:SH:S4-L achieves the **best perplexity on all three datasets**:
- PG19: BST:SH:S4-L reaches approximately **9.5**, substantially better than GSS-HYBRID-L (~10.5), BST:MF:UNSTRUCT-L (~11.5), and BRECT:FIXED:SKIP-L (~10.3).
- arXiv: BST:SH:S4-L reaches approximately **2.5**, while GSS-HYBRID-L degrades sharply to ~4.0 and BST:MF:UNSTRUCT-L degrades similarly.
- GitHub: BST:SH:S4-L maintains strong performance (~1.6), while other models show varying degrees of degradation.

**Structured vs. unstructured kernel for length generalization:**

The comparison between BST:SH:S4-L and BST:MF:UNSTRUCT-L is the most informative for the paper's claims about kernel structure and length generalization:
- **BST:SH:S4-L** improves perplexity as sequence length increases on PG19 and GitHub (perplexity decreases from 4k to 65k), consistent with the idea that structured kernels can leverage additional context beyond the training length.
- **BST:MF:UNSTRUCT-L** achieves the best perplexity at the training length of 4k on PG19 and GitHub, but degrades at 16k and 65k — perplexity *increases* as the sequence gets longer, indicating the unstructured kernel fails to generalize its positional FFN beyond positions seen during training.

This validates the paper's central claim about kernel structure: compiling the kernel from a dynamical system (`K_j = CA^jB`) provides a built-in length generalization mechanism that directly parameterized kernels lack.

**GSS-HYBRID-L length generalization failure on arXiv:**

Figure 3 (middle) shows GSS-HYBRID-L perplexity increasing drastically on arXiv at 16k and 65k. The paper notes that the original GSS authors "also reported that larger GSS models had difficulty generalizing to higher lengths" (Section 4.2), suggesting this is a systematic issue with the interleaved architecture rather than a one-off training artifact. The arXiv dataset's structure — LaTeX with cross-references, equations, and theorem environments that create dependencies spanning the entire document — appears to stress length generalization more than PG19 (narrative text) or GitHub (code with import-based dependencies).

**BRECT:FIXED:SKIP-L on arXiv:**

An interesting exception: BRECT:FIXED:SKIP-L generalizes well on arXiv at 16k and 65k in Figure 3 (middle). The paper hypothesizes this is because BRECT's non-differentiable cache — which carries recurrent states from one training sequence to the next within a document — provides access to information beyond the 4k training window, effectively giving the model a "memory" of symbols and definitions from earlier parts of the document. BST does not have this cross-sequence cache because it must compute the full sequence to run the SSM convolution, and "history beyond L may be lost in the process" (Appendix B.1). This is a structural advantage of recurrent caching over SSM-based context that the paper acknowledges as a limitation.

#### Layer-Level Efficiency Comparison

Figure 4 (left) benchmarks the forward-pass time of a single BST layer against a BRECT layer and a non-recurrent sliding-window layer (SLIDE:12L) on GPU at sequence lengths from 512 to 65,536 tokens, using a fixed window size of 128, SSM state size of 16, and 16 attention heads with embedding dimension 512.

**Headline findings:**

At the training sequence length of 4,096 tokens:
- **BRECT** runs approximately **15× slower** than SLIDE:12L — the sequential recurrence adds a factor-of-15 overhead over pure block-wise attention.
- **BST:SH** reduces this gap to **less than 2×** — i.e., BST:SH is 6–11× faster than BRECT, bringing the long-range context mechanism within a factor of 2 of the non-recurrent baseline.
- **BST:MH** is 3–4× slower than SLIDE:12L, reflecting the overhead of multiple SSM output heads.

As sequence length increases:
- BST:SH remains **6–11× faster** than BRECT up to 65k tokens. The gap narrows slightly at extreme lengths as the SSM's `$O(L \log L)$` convolution cost grows, but the advantage remains substantial.
- The curves for the SSM sublayer alone and the Block Transformer sublayer alone are "almost overlapping" at shorter sequence lengths (Section 1), meaning neither component is a bottleneck — both run in comparable time. At longer lengths, the SSM's FFT cost eventually dominates.

**What this measures:** The benchmark isolates a single layer's forward pass, not a full model. It uses the same embedding dimension as input to the SSM (512, without the down-sampling to D/4 used in full models), so the reported times are an upper bound on what full models with down-sampling would achieve. The implementation uses "the vanilla implementation of FFT and inverse FFT operations provided by JAX" (Section 4.3) — the paper notes that specialized I/O-aware FFT implementations could provide further speedups.

#### Scaling Properties

Figure 5 (Appendix C) compares perplexity on PG19 as a function of model parameter count for three architectures: BST:SH:UNSTRUCT, BRECT:FIXED:SKIP, and TRSF-XL:2048. Models are scaled from 80M to 1.3B parameters (12 layers for BST and BRECT, 13 layers for TRSF-XL:2048 due to the addition of a final Transformer-XL layer).

**Headline findings:**

At lower scales (80M–200M parameters), BRECT and BST have "very similar performances." The gap emerges and widens with scale:
- At 200M parameters, BRECT achieves approximately the same perplexity as BST (consistent with Table 1).
- At 1.3B parameters, the perplexity performance percentage gap between BRECT and BST reaches **4.0%** — BST scales better than BRECT. The gap between BRECT and TRSF-XL:2048 reaches 10.6% at 1.3B parameters, showing that both recurrent and SSM-based hybrids substantially outperform pure local-attention Transformers at scale.
- BST consistently outperforms BRECT at every scale from 200M upward, with the gap widening.

This is notable because it suggests the SSM's structured memory may have better scaling properties than learned recurrent gating — an important finding for future work targeting billion-parameter hybrid models. However, the paper only reports these scaling curves for PG19 and only for the UNSTRUCT kernel, so the generality of the scaling advantage across datasets and kernel types is unverified.

#### Long Range Arena (LRA) Results

Table 2 (Appendix D) reports accuracy on the Long Range Arena benchmark, which tests classification performance on sequences up to 16k tokens across text, image, and mathematical expression modalities. BST:SH:S4 is compared against BRECT:FIXED:SKIP, MEGA-CHUNK, and several pure-SSM baselines (S4, S4D, S5).

**Headline findings:**

BST:SH:S4 achieves an average LRA score of **86.96**, outperforming MEGA-CHUNK (85.66) and BRECT:FIXED:SKIP (60.80). On individual tasks:
- BST outperforms MEGA-CHUNK on four of six tasks (ListOps: 61.49 vs. 58.76; Image: 91.07 vs. 85.80; Pathfinder: 95.75 vs. 94.41; Path-X: 95.28 vs. 93.81).
- MEGA-CHUNK outperforms BST on Text (90.19 vs. 87.63) and Retrieval (90.97 vs. 90.51).
- Both substantially outperform BRECT on every task except Retrieval.

The LRA results demonstrate that BST's hybrid architecture is effective beyond autoregressive language modeling — the SSM-Transformer integration generalizes to bidirectional classification tasks and multiple modalities. However, BST (86.96) still trails the non-chunked MEGA model (88.21), suggesting that block-wise processing imposes a ceiling on classification performance that full-sequence methods can exceed. The paper notes that "BST still needs to improve (perhaps by extending the block size) to catch up to MEGA (without chunks)" (Appendix D).

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

All ablations use the ~200M parameter BST:SH:S4 on PG19 and are reported in Appendix E (Tables 3, 4, 5).

**SSM layer placement (Table 3):** A single BST layer placed at different indices within a 12-layer Block Transformer stack shows that index 9 gives the best perplexity (11.88), followed by index 7 (11.92), index 12 (12.03), and index 3 (12.41). The U-shaped pattern — worst near the input, best in the middle-upper layers, slightly worse at the output — is consistent with findings from Memorizing Transformer and BRECT that memory-augmented layers are most useful in middle-to-late layers where features are more abstract and benefit more from long-range context.

**Number of BST layers (Table 4):** Adding more BST layers monotonically improves perplexity but with diminishing returns and increased computational cost: 2 layers at indices {1, 9} gives 11.69; 3 layers at {1, 7, 9} gives 11.57; 4 layers at {1, 5, 7, 9} gives 11.21 (the largest absolute improvement); 5 layers at {1, 5, 7, 9, 12} gives 11.20 — essentially no improvement over 4 layers. Each added BST layer increases training step time by approximately 3.5%. The paper uses 3 layers for ~200M models and 4 layers for ~400M models as a compute-performance tradeoff.

**SSM state dimension (Table 5):** Varying the internal SSM state size `$N$` from 8 to 64 shows a clear compute-perplexity tradeoff. State size 8 gives 11.95 perplexity at 0.7× step time; 16 gives 11.57 at 1.0×; 32 gives 11.55 at 1.8×; 64 gives 11.54 at 3.2×. The gain from 16 to 32 is marginal (0.02 perplexity points for 1.8× the step time), and from 32 to 64 is essentially zero (0.01 for 3.2×). The paper selects `$N = 16$` as the sweet spot, where most of the benefit is captured before the step-time cost becomes prohibitive.

**Down-sampling impact (Section 3.4 and 4.1, Training details):** The paper states that projecting inputs to D/4 before the SSM convolution reduces FFT operations by a factor of 4, and for MF variants an additional factor of 2 (to D/8), with "negligible impact on perplexity." No ablation table is provided for the down-sampling ratio — this is an implicit design claim rather than an experimentally validated one.

**BMF filter count (Section 4.1, Training details):** Small MF models use `$S = 32$` filters; large MF models use `$S = 64$`. No ablation is reported for varying `$S$`. The choice appears to be determined by computational budget rather than a systematic sweep — doubling filters doubles FFT cost in the MF variant.

### Critical Assessment

#### Claim 1: BST matches or outperforms comparable architectures (BRECT, GSS-HYBRID) at matched compute budgets.

**What the experiments demonstrate:** This claim is **supported with qualifications**. On PG19 at ~200M parameters (Table 1), BST:SH:UNSTRUCT achieves 11.52 vs. BRECT:FIXED:SKIP's 11.55 — within the ±1.1 error bar, so the difference is not statistically significant. BST does achieve this at 0.5k TPUv4 hours vs. BRECT's 0.8k, which is a genuine efficiency improvement (same performance at 60% of the training compute). On arXiv and GitHub, BRECT retains a small advantage at ~200M (2.36 vs. 2.44, 2.04 vs. 2.07), and BST does not clearly outperform. At ~400M parameters, BST consistently outperforms GSS-HYBRID-L across all three datasets (e.g., 10.37 vs. 10.52 on PG19), but these are single-run results — no error bars are provided for the ~400M models, so the statistical significance of a 0.15 perplexity improvement cannot be assessed.

**Qualification 1 — Missing error bars at scale:** The paper provides error bars only for PG19 ~200M models (three seeds). All ~400M results, all arXiv results, and all GitHub results are single runs. Given that perplexity differences between BST and BRECT at ~200M are within previously established error bars (~0.03 vs. ±1.1), it is plausible that many of the ~400M comparisons would also fall within error bars if multiple seeds were run. The paper's claim of "outperforming" GSS-HYBRID-L should be interpreted cautiously — the reported differences (0.05–0.15 perplexity) are small relative to typical language modeling variance.

**Qualification 2 — No GSS-HYBRID comparison at ~200M:** The paper only compares against GSS-HYBRID at ~370M parameters (the "-L" variants). At ~200M, the only SSM-hybrid baseline is BST itself (comparing variants against each other). A smaller GSS-HYBRID baseline at matched compute would have strengthened the claim that integration outperforms interleaving at all scales.

**Qualification 3 — Compute budgets are not perfectly matched:** Table 1 shows that ~200M BST models on PG19 use 0.5k TPUv4 hours while BRECT uses 0.8k — BST is cheaper. For the ~400M comparison, both BST and GSS-HYBRID-L use 0.8k hours, which is a fair comparison. However, BST:MH:S4 uses 0.8k while BST:SH:S4 uses 0.5k (at ~200M on PG19), and MH performs worse — the compute difference partially confounds the architectural comparison.

#### Claim 2: BST achieves a 10× speedup at the layer level compared to BRECT.

**What the experiments demonstrate:** This claim is **strongly supported for the specific GPU configuration tested**. Figure 4 (left) shows BST:SH is 6–11× faster than BRECT at sequence lengths from 512 to 65k on GPU with embedding dimension 512, window size 128, 16 heads, and SSM state size 16. The 10× figure is a reasonable round summary of these measurements.

**Qualification 1 — GPU-specific and configuration-dependent:** The benchmarks are run on GPU ("to demonstrate and exploit the parallelizability of BST layers"). The paper notes in Appendix A that "FFT operation is an important speed bottleneck on TPUs" and that "JAX FFT was 4× faster on GPUs." The 10× speedup may not hold on TPUs, which are the training hardware used for all perplexity results. The paper does not provide TPU layer-level benchmarks, so the speedup claim is hardware-dependent in a way that matters for the paper's own training setup.

**Qualification 2 — Layer-level, not full-model or end-to-end training time:** Figure 4 measures a single layer's forward pass, not the training step time of a full model. The training time comparisons in Table 1 (TPUv4 hours) do not show a 10× speedup — at ~200M on PG19, BST trains in 0.5k hours vs. BRECT's 0.8k, which is a 1.6× speedup, not 10×. The layer-level speedup is real but does not translate proportionally to end-to-end training time because (a) not all layers are BST layers (only 3 out of 12), (b) the FFN and other components are identical between architectures, and (c) the TPU FFT bottleneck reduces the SSM's parallel advantage.

**Qualification 3 — Single data point for the "more than tenfold" claim in the abstract:** The abstract states "more than tenfold increase in speed at the layer level compared to the Block-Recurrent Transformer." Figure 4 shows BST:SH is 6–11× faster than BRECT depending on sequence length — the 10× figure is the upper end of this range. At the typical training length of 4k, the speedup is approximately 7.5× (15× slowdown for BRECT reduced to ~2× for BST, i.e., 15/2 ≈ 7.5×). The abstract's "more than tenfold" is a stretch for the most relevant operational point.

#### Claim 3: BSTh with structured S4 kernels generalizes to unseen sequence lengths, and structured kernels provide length generalization that unstructured kernels lack.

**What the experiments demonstrate:** This claim is **strongly supported** by Figure 3. BST:SH:S4-L maintains or improves perplexity from 4k to 65k on all three datasets, while BST:MF:UNSTRUCT-L degrades sharply beyond 4k. The mechanism is clearly identified (structured kernel compilation from A, B, C vs. fixed-length FFN parameterization), and the contrast is unambiguous.

**Strengths:** Two variants with different kernel types are compared at the same model scale (~400M), training length (4k), and evaluation lengths (512–65k). The structured variant works; the unstructured variant fails. The paper identifies the mechanistic reason and it is consistent with the known properties of each kernel type.

**Qualification — Only one structured variant tested at length generalization:** The paper shows BST:SH:S4-L generalizes well, but does not show BST:MF:S4-L (structured MF) or BST:MH:S4-L (structured MH) at length generalization. The comparison is confounded by variant type: is it the structured kernel or the SH collection method that enables generalization? Since MF:UNSTRUCT fails but MF:S4 is not tested, one cannot isolate the kernel's contribution independent of the context collection method. It is plausible that S4-structured MF would also generalize well, but this is not demonstrated.

**Additional note — BRECT length generalization on arXiv:** Figure 3 (middle) shows BRECT:FIXED:SKIP-L generalizing well on arXiv while GSS-HYBRID-L and BST:MF:UNSTRUCT-L fail. This is a positive result for BRECT that the paper explains post-hoc (cross-sequence caching). The fact that BRECT, a non-SSM architecture, also generalizes on arXiv suggests length generalization is not an exclusive property of structured SSMs — recurrent caching with non-differentiable state passing can also achieve it, albeit through a different mechanism. The paper does not explore this alternative mechanism in depth.

#### Claim 4: Integrating SSM states into attention provides larger benefits than interleaving SSM and attention layers.

**What the experiments demonstrate:** This claim is **supported at the ~400M scale** where BST directly outperforms GSS-HYBRID-L without grid search. The consistent 1.5–4% improvement across all three datasets (Table 1) and the greater robustness (no per-layer learning rate tuning required) are convincing evidence.

**Qualification — Confounded by multiple architectural differences:** BST and GSS-HYBRID differ along many dimensions beyond integration vs. interleaving: BST uses block-wise attention with cross-attention to context states; GSS-HYBRID uses Gated Attention Units with gating mechanisms. BST uses S4 or unstructured kernels; GSS-HYBRID uses DSS with gating. BST uses a small number of SSM layers embedded in a Transformer stack; GSS-HYBRID is primarily SSM layers with a few interleaved Transformers. Any of these differences could contribute to the performance gap. The paper cannot isolate "direct state integration into attention" as the sole causal factor.

**What would strengthen the claim:** A controlled ablation comparing a BST layer with cross-attention to SSM context states against a variant where the SSM output is added to the Block Transformer's input (interleaving-style within a single layer) — holding all other architectural choices constant — would isolate the integration mechanism. This experiment is not conducted.

#### Missing experiments and analyses:

- **No error bars at ~400M scale or on arXiv/GitHub.** This is the most significant methodological weakness. The central "BST outperforms" claims rest on single-run results where the margin (0.05–0.15 perplexity) is small. Three-run error bars at the larger scale would substantially affect confidence in the conclusions.

- **No ablation on down-sampling ratio.** The paper claims projecting to D/4 has "negligible impact on perplexity" but provides no data. This is a crucial efficiency-quality tradeoff — if down-sampling to D/4 actually degrades perplexity by 0.3 points, the speed-quality tradeoff changes.

- **No comparison with a larger-window Transformer at matched parameters.** TRSF-XL:2048 uses a 2048-token window at 190M parameters (Table 1). A BST with a 512-token window plus SSM context matches or exceeds this. But what about a Transformer with a 4096-token window at matched parameters? The paper argues such a model would be much slower (quadratic attention), but no perplexity comparison is provided to quantify the quality gap.

- **No length generalization for structured MF.** BST:MF:S4-L is trained but only evaluated at 4k (Table 1). Testing it at 65k in Figure 3 would have cleanly separated the kernel structure effect from the context collection method effect.

- **No evaluation on standard short-context LM benchmarks (e.g., WikiText-103).** All evaluations are on long-range datasets. A comparison on a standard benchmark would show whether BST's SSM integration imposes any penalty on short-context performance — if BST degrades on WikiText-103 relative to a pure Transformer, the hybrid approach has a hidden cost.

- **No inference-time speed benchmarks.** All efficiency results are for training (forward pass). The recurrent mode of the SSM (used for autoregressive decoding) has different computational properties than the convolutional mode (used for training). The paper does not report inference latency or throughput, which matters for deployment.

- **No analysis of attention patterns.** The paper claims that the SH variant provides higher retrievability of local context, but provides no attention weight analysis to show that cross-attention heads actually attend differently in SH vs. MF. Visualization or entropy analysis of cross-attention weights would substantiate the conceptual framework.

#### Summary assessment:

The experimental evidence generally supports the paper's architectural claims, but with important caveats about statistical significance (single runs at scale), hardware dependence (GPU speedups may not hold on TPUs), and confounded comparisons (BST vs. GSS-HYBRID differ along multiple dimensions). The strongest and most robust finding is the length generalization result (Figure 3), where the contrast between structured and unstructured kernels is unambiguous and mechanistically explained. The efficiency result (Figure 4) is strong for the tested GPU configuration but does not translate to the reported training times (Table 1 shows 1.6× end-to-end speedup, not 10×). The performance claims (BST "outperforms" baselines) are directionally consistent across datasets and scales but rest on small margins from single-run experiments at the larger scale — the paper would benefit substantially from multi-seed results at ~400M to establish statistical reliability.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The FFT Bottleneck on TPUs Undermines the Primary Speedup Claim on the Paper's Own Training Hardware

**The assumption or constraint:** The paper's central efficiency claim — that BST achieves a 10× speedup over BRECT at the layer level and is "fully parallelizable" — is demonstrated and quantified exclusively on GPU hardware. However, all training experiments (Table 1) are conducted on TPUv4, and the paper explicitly acknowledges in Appendix A that:

> "We have found that the FFT operation is an important speed bottleneck on TPUs that needs to be resolved to better scale BST to many layers and larger models. While we are still investigating the reasons, we found that JAX FFT was 4× faster on GPUs."

This is not a minor implementation detail — it is a hardware-architecture mismatch that directly affects the paper's primary value proposition. The entire motivation for replacing BRECT's sequential recurrence with an SSM is that the SSM's FFT-based convolution enables parallelization, which in turn enables the speedup. But if FFT is an "important speed bottleneck" on the hardware used for all training runs, then the parallelization advantage is partially or fully eroded in the very setting where the paper reports its results.

**The consequence:** The layer-level speedup demonstrated in Figure 4 (left) — BST:SH running 6–11× faster than BRECT on GPU — likely does not hold on TPUv4, which is the hardware used for all perplexity comparisons in Table 1. The paper does not provide TPU layer-level benchmarks, so the actual training-time speed advantage on the paper's own hardware is unknown. The end-to-end training time data in Table 1 is consistent with a much smaller speedup: at ~200M parameters on PG19, BST trains in 0.5k TPUv4 hours vs. BRECT's 0.8k, which is a 1.6× speedup, not 10×. Part of this gap is explained by only 3 of 12 layers being BST layers, but even accounting for that, the per-BST-layer speedup on TPU is clearly nowhere near 10×.

More importantly, the FFT bottleneck means that scaling BST to more layers (the ablation in Table 4 shows perplexity continues improving with 4–5 BST layers) or larger models is disproportionately expensive on TPUs. The paper's own scaling experiments (Figure 5) only go to 1.3B parameters, and the authors acknowledge the FFT issue must be "resolved to better scale BST to many layers and larger models." This is a ceiling on the practical applicability of the approach using current TPU hardware.

**What evidence exists in the paper:** The FFT bottleneck is acknowledged in Appendix A but **not quantified** — there is no TPU benchmark comparing BST to BRECT at the layer level, no measurement of FFT vs. attention time on TPU, and no end-to-end training time comparison on identical TPU configurations. The training time data in Table 1 does show a smaller speedup than the GPU layer benchmark, but this is not analyzed or discussed in the main text. The paper's abstract and introduction emphasize the 10× speedup without mentioning the TPU caveat, which appears only in the appendix.

**Mitigation status:** The paper mentions two potential mitigation paths: (1) using newer SSM variants like S5 that "bypass FFT operations using a binary associative operator," and (2) noting that the implementation is "modular enough that we can simply plug in S5 or use other FFT implementations." Neither is implemented or evaluated. The limitation is acknowledged but not addressed — the paper's headline efficiency claim rests on GPU benchmarks that do not reflect the training hardware used for all other experiments. A practitioner deploying BST on TPUs would encounter substantially different performance characteristics than the paper's claims suggest.

---

### Cross-Sequence Information Loss Compared to Recurrent Caching Architectures

**The assumption or constraint:** BST computes SSM context states via convolution over a single training sequence of length `L` (4,096 tokens in all experiments). This means the SSM has no access to information from *before* the start of the current sequence — history beyond `L` tokens is invisible. BRECT, by contrast, uses a non-differentiable cache that carries recurrent states from one training sequence to the next within a document, providing "weak access to the whole past" (Appendix B.1). The paper is explicit about this:

> "We do not pass such a representation, since to compute the output of the convolution, we need access to the whole sequence. We believe that this is one advantage that BRECT has over our method, especially for very long examples that split into ordered sequences of length L, since the cache carried from one sequence to the next can provide very useful long-range information and (weak) access to the whole past. Since we need the whole sequence to compute SSM states, history beyond L may be lost in the process." (Appendix B.1)

This is a fundamental constraint imposed by the convolutional formulation: the FFT operates over a fixed-length sequence, and there is no natural mechanism to incorporate state from the previous sequence without recomputing the convolution over the concatenation of both sequences (which would defeat the efficiency purpose).

**The consequence:** On documents longer than the training sequence length `L` (which is the common case — PG19 books have 50k–100k tokens, arXiv papers can be similar length), BST processes the document in chunks of `L` tokens with no information flow between chunks. Information from positions `L` through `2L-1` cannot influence predictions at positions `0` through `L-1` (obviously), but crucially, predictions at position `L+1` also cannot access information from positions `0` through `L-1` except through whatever the SSM happened to retain in its final state at position `L-1` — and that final state was computed without knowing that positions `L` onward exist. BRECT's recurrent cache, while not differentiable through the sequence boundary, at least provides a warm-start state that carries information forward from the previous chunk.

The paper's length generalization results (Figure 3) partially obscure this issue because they evaluate on a single sequence of 65k tokens (i.e., the model processes the entire 65k-token sequence in one forward pass after compiling the kernel to length 65k). This tests the SSM's ability to retain information over 65k steps — which BST:SH:S4-L does well — but it does *not* test the cross-sequence information loss that occurs in actual training, where documents are split into 4k-token segments. The training setup and the length-generalization evaluation setup test different things, and the paper does not disentangle them.

**What evidence exists in the paper:** Figure 3 (middle) provides indirect evidence: BRECT:FIXED:SKIP-L generalizes well on arXiv at 16k and 65k, while GSS-HYBRID-L (which also lacks cross-sequence caching) degrades sharply. The paper hypothesizes that BRECT's advantage comes from its cross-sequence cache allowing it to "access past symbols, definitions, theorems or equations beyond the 4k training sequence length." This is a plausible explanation, but the paper does not run a controlled experiment — e.g., training BST with a similar non-differentiable SSM-state cache or training BRECT without the cache — to isolate the effect.

**Mitigation status:** The paper explicitly identifies this as a limitation and suggests that "BST can further be improved by adding non-differentiable sequence cache for very long documents" (Appendix B.1), but no implementation or evaluation of such a cache is provided. The limitation is acknowledged but unresolved. A practitioner working with documents longer than 4k tokens should expect BST to lose information at sequence boundaries during training in a way that BRECT does not, and the paper provides no guidance on how to mitigate this.

### The Redundancy-Retrievability Framework Is Not Directly Validated by Attention Analysis

**The assumption or constraint:** The paper's primary conceptual contribution — the redundancy-retrievability tradeoff that organizes the three context state variants (SH, MH, MF) — is a *postulated explanatory framework*, not an empirically verified mechanism. The paper claims that SH "maximizes retrievability" because adjacent SSM outputs are directly available as context states, that MF "minimizes redundancy" because independent filters extract complementary features, and that these properties explain the dataset-dependent performance differences (SH better on PG19 where local narrative coherence matters; MF better on arXiv and GitHub where long-range dependencies dominate). However, the paper provides **no direct evidence** that the cross-attention heads in SH actually retrieve local information more effectively, or that the MF filters actually learn complementary features. The framework is inferred from architectural properties and validated only through downstream perplexity, which is a coarse, aggregate signal.

**The consequence:** Without direct validation, the redundancy-retrievability framework remains a plausible hypothesis rather than an established design principle. Several alternative explanations could account for the dataset-dependent performance differences:

- **Parameter count differences:** MF variants have ~8% more parameters than SH variants (Table 1: 217M vs. 202M for S4, 221M vs. 206M for UNSTRUCT). The performance difference could simply reflect additional capacity rather than the structural properties of the context collection method.
- **Attention masking differences:** MF does not require causal masking in cross-attention (because context states come from the previous window), while SH and MH do. The simpler attention pattern might affect optimization dynamics rather than retrievability per se.
- **Positional encoding differences:** SH and MH rely on the SSM's incremental convolution for positional information; MF uses learned context IDs. These different positional encoding schemes could affect generalization in ways unrelated to redundancy or retrievability.
- **Filter count vs. window size:** MF uses `S = 32` or `S = 64` context states per block, which is substantially fewer than SH's `S = W = 512` context states. The reduced cross-attention cost in MF could allow the model to allocate more capacity elsewhere, independent of redundancy.

**What evidence exists in the paper:** None. The paper reports no analysis of cross-attention weights, no visualization of what the context states encode, no probing of whether SH context states are more redundant than MF context states (e.g., by measuring the rank or mutual information of context state tensors), and no measurement of whether attention heads in SH attend to temporally local context states more than those in MF. The framework is presented as a conceptual lens but is treated as self-evidently true based on architectural design choices rather than empirical validation.

**Mitigation status:** Not addressed. The paper does not acknowledge this as a limitation, does not propose experiments to validate the framework, and does not discuss alternative explanations for the variant-wise performance differences. A practitioner choosing between SH and MF for a new dataset or task has no validated principle to guide the decision — the paper's framework is suggestive but unproven.

---

### Training Setup Requires Disabling Standard Learned Positional Embeddings Without Ablation

**The assumption or constraint:** BST's architecture makes an implicit but consequential design choice about positional encoding: it removes standard learned absolute positional embeddings (used by both BRECT and Transformer-XL) and relies instead on the SSM in the first layer to encode positional information through its convolution kernel, combined with a T5-style relative position bias in the attention mechanism. The paper states:

> "We also realize that we do not need to add global positional bias to the token embeddings, and use a T5-style relative position bias instead, as the SSM does also encode positional information into the context." (Section 3.4)

This is a significant deviation from prior work — BRECT, Transformer-XL, and GSS-HYBRID all use some form of absolute positional encoding — but the paper provides no ablation comparing BST with and without learned positional embeddings. The claim that the SSM encodes sufficient positional information is asserted, not demonstrated.

**The consequence:** The removal of learned positional embeddings could affect BST's performance in ways that are not disentangled from the architectural contributions. If the SSM's positional encoding is weaker than learned embeddings for certain types of positional reasoning (e.g., tasks requiring precise counting of token positions, or relative offsets beyond the attention window), BST would underperform in those regimes without the paper detecting it — because all evaluations are aggregate perplexity on long-range datasets, which may not stress positional reasoning per se.

Additionally, this design choice creates a dependency on the first-layer SSM for positional encoding. If a practitioner wanted to use BST without an SSM in the first layer (e.g., the ablation in Table 3 shows a single BST layer works best at index 9), the positional encoding would be purely relative (T5 bias), which may not be sufficient. The paper's final configuration always places a BST layer at index 1, which provides the positional encoding SSM — but this means any deployment that moves or removes the first-layer SSM would need to reconsider the positional encoding scheme, a coupling that is not discussed.

**What evidence exists in the paper:** No ablation on positional encoding. The paper does not report a controlled comparison of BST with learned absolute positional embeddings vs. the SSM-only approach. The length generalization results (Figure 3) are partially attributed to the structured kernel's ability to extend positional information, but without an absolute-positional-embedding baseline, one cannot determine whether the structured kernel provides *better* positional encoding or merely *adequate* positional encoding for length generalization.

**Mitigation status:** Not addressed. The paper does not treat this as a design choice requiring validation — it is presented as a realization ("we realize that we do not need...") rather than a hypothesis. A practitioner implementing BST should be aware that the architecture depends on the SSM for positional information and that removing or relocating the first-layer SSM would require adding back absolute positional embeddings, a modification with unknown effects.

### Length Generalization Results for Structured Kernels Are Not Separated from Context Collection Method

**The assumption or constraint:** The paper's strongest result — BST:SH:S4-L generalizing to 65k tokens while BST:MF:UNSTRUCT-L degrades — is used to support the claim that structured kernels (S4) provide length generalization that unstructured kernels (Hyena-based) lack. However, the comparison is confounded: **BST:SH:S4-L differs from BST:MF:UNSTRUCT-L along two axes simultaneously** — kernel type (structured vs. unstructured) and context collection method (SH vs. MF). The paper does not evaluate BST:MF:S4-L (structured kernel with Multi-Filter context collection) or BST:SH:UNSTRUCT-L (unstructured kernel with Single-Head context collection) at length generalization. Without these variants, one cannot attribute the length generalization difference solely to the kernel structure — the context collection method could be a contributing factor.

**The consequence:** The paper's central claim about structured kernels enabling length generalization is plausible and consistent with known properties of S4 vs. Hyena, but it is not cleanly isolated from the context collection method. It is possible, for instance, that MF context collection interacts poorly with length generalization regardless of kernel type — because MF uses learned context IDs that are filter-count-dependent and fixed at training time, extending to longer sequences might require the attention heads to handle more blocks with the same set of context IDs, which could cause confusion. Alternatively, SH might generalize better regardless of kernel type because its adjacent-time context states provide a more robust local signal that does not depend on filter specialization. The confounded experiment cannot distinguish these hypotheses from the kernel-structure explanation.

**What evidence exists in the paper:** Figure 3 includes only BST:SH:S4-L (structured, SH) and BST:MF:UNSTRUCT-L (unstructured, MF). The ~400M BST:MF:S4-L model is trained and evaluated at 4k in Table 1 (10.52 on PG19) but is not evaluated at 16k or 65k. BST:SH:UNSTRUCT-L is also trained (10.37 on PG19) but not length-generalization tested. The missing data points would directly test the kernel-structure explanation.

**Mitigation status:** Not addressed. The paper does not acknowledge the confound, does not explain why the cross-variants were not evaluated for length generalization, and does not qualify the length generalization claim. Given that evaluating an already-trained checkpoint at multiple sequence lengths requires only forward passes (no retraining), the missing evaluations are a notable gap. A practitioner choosing between kernel types based on this paper should be aware that the evidence for the structured-kernel length generalization advantage, while consistent with prior work on S4/DSS, is not cleanly isolated from the context collection method in this particular architecture.

---

### Perplexity Improvements Over Baselines Are Small and Partially Within Variance at ~400M Scale

**The assumption or constraint:** The claim that BST "outperforms similar Transformer-based architectures on language modeling perplexity" (abstract) and that integrating SSM states into attention "provides larger benefits than interleaving SSM and attention layers" (Section 4.1) rests on perplexity differences of **0.05–0.15 points** between BST variants and GSS-HYBRID-L at the ~400M parameter scale (Table 1). These differences are reported from single training runs — no error bars are provided for any ~400M model, any arXiv model, or any GitHub model. The only error bars in the paper are for the ~200M PG19 models (three seeds), where the standard deviation is ±1.1 perplexity points for BST:SH:S4 and similar for BRECT. Within that variance, BST and BRECT are statistically indistinguishable at ~200M on PG19.

The paper does not discuss whether the variance at ~400M is likely to be smaller (as one might expect for larger models with more stable training) or similarly large relative to the reported differences. The single-run results are treated as point estimates without uncertainty quantification.

**The consequence:** The paper's headline performance claims — "outperforms," "provides larger benefits," "surpasses" — overstate the strength of the evidence. A ±1.0 perplexity error bar at ~200M implies that even if variance shrinks by half at ~400M (to ±0.5), the reported 0.05–0.15 improvements would fall well within one standard deviation. Without error bars, a practitioner cannot determine whether switching from GSS-HYBRID to BST would reliably improve perplexity or whether the reported differences reflect random seed variation.

This is particularly important because the GSS-HYBRID baseline was carefully tuned with learning rate grid search and per-layer optimization, while BST was not. If BST's training recipe is simpler but noisier, single-run comparisons could overstate BST's advantage (if GSS-HYBRID's reported number is a best-of-grid-search result while BST's is a single sample from a wider distribution). Conversely, if GSS-HYBRID's grid search compensated for training instability, BST's single-run result might underestimate its advantage. Without variance estimates, neither interpretation can be ruled out.

**What evidence exists in the paper:** The only multi-seed results are the ~200M PG19 comparisons (Table 1). The error bars (±1.1) are larger than the gap between BST and BRECT (11.52 vs. 11.55), meaning the two are statistically tied at this scale. The paper does not comment on this fact, instead focusing on the compute efficiency angle (0.5k vs. 0.8k TPUv4 hours) as the differentiator. For the ~400M comparisons against GSS-HYBRID-L, no error bars exist.

**Mitigation status:** The paper acknowledges in Table 1 that "Results with ± are average scores and error bars of runs with three different random seeds," implicitly limiting error-bar results to the ~200M PG19 models. The lack of multi-seed runs at larger scales or on other datasets is attributed to "long training times and large number of experiments." This is a practical constraint, but it means the paper's strongest comparative claims rest on single-run evidence with unknown variance. A practitioner making architectural decisions based on these results should treat the ~400M perplexity comparisons as suggestive rather than conclusive, and should expect to run their own multi-seed validation.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

The Block-State Transformer does not introduce a new class of models, nor does it propose a fundamentally different learning algorithm. Its contribution is **architectural integration** — showing that a State Space Model can replace the sequential recurrent cell in a Block Transformer to produce a fully parallelizable layer, and that *how* the SSM's output is presented to attention heads controls a redundancy-retrievability tradeoff with dataset-dependent performance consequences. The magnitude of this contribution is best characterized as a **significant architectural refinement** that changes the design priorities for hybrid sequence models, rather than a paradigm shift that renders prior approaches obsolete.

**Reframing the hybrid architecture design space.** Before this work, the dominant approaches to combining SSMs with Transformers were either stacking them in separate layers (GSS-HYBRID) or augmenting Transformers with learned recurrence across blocks (BRECT). The implicit design assumption in both cases was that the long-range mechanism should operate *alongside* or *before* the attention mechanism, not *inside* it. BST reframes this by treating the SSM's compressed memory as a **retrievable context that attention heads actively query**, analogous to how cross-attention retrieves from encoder outputs in sequence-to-sequence models. This is a conceptual shift: the SSM is not a preprocessing step or a parallel processing stream, but a **memory substrate** whose output is organized specifically to be searched by attention. The paper's evidence that this integration outperforms layer-interleaving at matched compute (Table 1: BST:SH:S4-L achieves 10.47 vs. GSS-HYBRID-L's 10.52 on PG19, with simpler training) provides empirical grounding for the reframing — direct access to SSM states through cross-attention is not merely architecturally convenient but quantitatively better.

**Reconciling contradictory signals about SSM-Transformer hybrids.** The paper resolves a tension that was implicit in prior literature. On one hand, SSMs had demonstrated superior long-range classification performance (LRA benchmarks) and compelling length generalization properties (S4, DSS). On the other hand, attempts to use SSMs in language modeling either underperformed Transformers (purely SSM models like H3) or required careful interleaving with attention and extensive hyperparameter tuning to approach Transformer performance (GSS-HYBRID). The pessimistic interpretation was that SSMs and Transformers might be fundamentally incompatible for language — that the attention mechanism's inductive bias was so well-suited to natural language that any hybridization would degrade it. BST provides evidence against this pessimism: by integrating the SSM into the attention mechanism itself rather than placing it in separate layers, the hybrid matches or exceeds both purely recurrent hybrids (BRECT) and interleaved hybrids (GSS-HYBRID) without requiring per-layer learning rate tuning or grid search. This resolution is practically important because it means that SSM-Transformer hybridization is not a dead end — the negative prior results reflected suboptimal integration strategies rather than fundamental incompatibility.

**Shifting research priorities toward memory integration mechanisms.** The paper's redundancy-retrievability framework implicitly argues that the field should focus less on *which* long-range mechanism to use (SSM vs. recurrence vs. retrieval) and more on *how to present the mechanism's output to attention heads*. The three BST variants — SH, MH, MF — all use the same underlying SSM computation; what differs is the strategy for collecting and formatting the SSM's output as attention keys and values. The fact that these variants show systematic, dataset-dependent performance differences (SH better on PG19 where local narrative coherence matters; MF better on arXiv and GitHub where long-range cross-references dominate) suggests that the presentation strategy is not a次要 detail but a first-class design axis. This reframes the research agenda: rather than asking "SSM or attention?", the productive question becomes "given an SSM's compressed memory, what is the optimal way to make it retrievable by attention for a given task?" This is a more nuanced and potentially more fruitful direction than the binary SSM-vs-Transformer debate that preceded this work.

**What becomes less attractive.** The paper's results diminish the case for layer-interleaving as the primary hybridization strategy. GSS-HYBRID's approach — separate GSS and Transformer layers with information passing only through layer outputs — required grid search over learning rates and per-layer optimization to achieve stability, and still underperformed BST's integrated approach at matched compute. Future hybrid architectures that keep SSM and attention in separate layers will now need to justify why they do not adopt the integration strategy that BST shows to be both more effective and easier to train. Similarly, the paper's evidence that structured kernels (S4/DSS) provide length generalization while unstructured kernels (Hyena-based) do not (Figure 3) makes purely unstructured approaches less attractive for applications requiring variable-length inference — the performance advantage of unstructured kernels at the training length (Table 1) must now be weighed against their failure to generalize.

**What becomes more attractive.** The paper makes several research directions newly compelling: (1) exploring the presentation strategy design space beyond the three variants studied here — e.g., learned sparsification of context states, top-k retrieval over SSM outputs (as suggested in Appendix A), or dynamic selection of context states based on input content; (2) investigating whether the integration principle generalizes to other long-range mechanisms (linear attention, retroactive memory, retrieval-augmented generation) — if cross-attention to a compressed memory is broadly beneficial, the SSM is just one possible implementation of that memory; (3) developing hardware-efficient implementations of the SSM sublayer on TPUs, since the FFT bottleneck (Appendix A) is the primary obstacle to scaling BST to more layers and larger models; (4) analyzing what cross-attention heads actually retrieve from context states, to validate or refine the redundancy-retrievability framework with direct evidence rather than architectural inference.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Attribution analysis of cross-attention patterns to validate the redundancy-retrievability framework.** The paper's central conceptual contribution — that SH, MH, and MF instantiate different positions on a redundancy-retrievability spectrum — is inferred from architectural properties but never directly validated. A critical follow-up would measure what cross-attention heads actually attend to in each variant. Concretely: train BST:SH, BST:MH, and BST:MF models on PG19 and GitHub (where the paper predicts different optimal variants), then measure (a) the entropy of cross-attention weight distributions (higher entropy in MF would suggest more distributed retrieval consistent with lower redundancy), (b) the average temporal distance between query positions and attended context states (SH should show stronger preference for temporally proximate context states), and (c) the effective rank of the context state matrix via singular value decomposition (SH should show lower rank due to higher redundancy). If these measurements align with the framework's predictions, the framework is validated as a design principle; if not, the dataset-dependent performance differences require alternative explanations (parameter count, masking differences, positional encoding). This experiment is low-cost — it requires no retraining, only forward passes with attention weight logging on existing checkpoints — and would transform the redundancy-retrievability concept from a plausible narrative into an empirically grounded theory.

**Controlled isolation of kernel structure from context collection method in length generalization.** The paper's length generalization results (Figure 3) show BST:SH:S4-L generalizing to 65k while BST:MF:UNSTRUCT-L degrades, but the comparison is confounded: SH vs. MF and S4 vs. UNSTRUCT vary simultaneously. The critical missing experiments are BST:MF:S4-L and BST:SH:UNSTRUCT-L evaluated at 16k and 65k on all three datasets. These checkpoints already exist (Table 1 reports their 4k perplexity), and evaluation requires only forward passes at longer sequence lengths — no retraining. If BST:MF:S4-L generalizes well, the length generalization property is attributable to the structured kernel independent of context collection method. If BST:SH:UNSTRUCT-L generalizes well, the SH collection method provides a length-generalization benefit that compensates for the unstructured kernel's limitations. The most informative negative result would be both variants failing — which would mean length generalization requires *both* a structured kernel *and* the SH collection method, revealing a previously unknown interaction. This experiment would directly resolve the paper's primary confound and provide actionable guidance for practitioners choosing kernel types and collection methods for variable-length deployment.

**Scaling BST to billion-parameter models with S5 or other FFT-free SSM variants to test whether the integration advantage persists at scale.** The paper's scaling experiment (Figure 5) shows BST pulling ahead of BRECT as parameter count increases from 200M to 1.3B, suggesting the SSM-based integration has favorable scaling properties. However, the FFT bottleneck on TPUs (Appendix A) makes scaling beyond 1.3B parameters using S4/DSS prohibitively expensive on the paper's training hardware. A natural follow-up would replace the S4 sublayer with S5 (Smith et al., 2023), which uses a parallel scan (associative operator) instead of FFT to compute SSM outputs, eliminating the FFT bottleneck. The experiment would train BST:SH:S5 models at 1.3B, 2.7B, and 6.7B parameters on PG19 and compare scaling curves against equivalently-sized BRECT and pure Transformer baselines. The key question is whether the BST integration advantage — the gap between BST and BRECT in Figure 5 — continues to widen with scale or plateaus. If it widens, BST-style integration becomes increasingly compelling for large-scale training; if it plateaus, the architectural benefit saturates and further scaling should focus on other components. This experiment also tests whether the SSM integration principle is robust to the choice of SSM implementation, which matters for practical adoption.

**Evaluating BST on few-shot downstream tasks to determine whether the architecture's long-range context improves task performance beyond perplexity.** The paper evaluates only on language modeling perplexity (and LRA classification, Appendix D), but perplexity improvements do not always translate to downstream task gains. A critical stress-test would fine-tune BST checkpoints on tasks that explicitly require long-range reasoning: (a) NarrativeQA or similar long-document question answering, where answering a question requires synthesizing information from distant parts of a book; (b) repository-level code completion or bug detection, where understanding cross-file dependencies is essential; (c) multi-document summarization or evidence synthesis. The null hypothesis is that BST's perplexity advantage over BRECT and GSS-HYBRID reflects better modeling of local statistical patterns rather than genuinely better long-range reasoning, and that downstream task performance would not differ significantly. Rejecting this null — showing that BST's SSM-integrated context states enable better task performance on long-range reasoning benchmarks — would substantially strengthen the paper's claims about the practical value of the architecture. The experiment is straightforward: fine-tune the existing ~400M checkpoints on downstream tasks and compare against BRECT and GSS-HYBRID baselines.

**Cross-sequence SSM state caching to address information loss at document boundaries.** The paper explicitly acknowledges that BST loses information at sequence boundaries during training because the SSM convolution operates over fixed-length sequences with no state carried forward (Appendix B.1), while BRECT's non-differentiable recurrent cache provides weak access to the full document past. A concrete extension would implement a **non-differentiable SSM state cache**: after processing sequence `i` of a document, store the SSM's final state vector `x_{L-1}` (of dimension `N = 16`), and at the start of sequence `i+1`, use this cached state as the initial condition `x_{-1}` for the SSM convolution (currently initialized to zero). The initial condition can be incorporated into the convolution by pre-pending a "state initialization" step or by modifying the kernel compilation to account for non-zero initial state. The experiment would measure whether this cache improves perplexity on documents longer than the training sequence length (where information loss at boundaries matters) without degrading training speed (since the cache is non-differentiable, it does not affect backpropagation). If successful, this would close the one identified advantage BRECT holds over BST, making the architecture strictly dominant. If the cache degrades performance (e.g., because stale cached states create distribution shift), it would reveal a fundamental tension between convolutional SSMs and cross-sequence information flow that practitioners need to navigate.

**Integration of the BST design pattern into encoder-decoder architectures for long-input tasks.** The paper studies decoder-only language modeling, but many practical long-range tasks use encoder-decoder architectures — long-document summarization, multi-document question answering, repository-scale code translation — where the encoder processes a long input and the decoder generates a shorter output. The BST design pattern — SSM contextualization over the full input, with block-wise attention cross-attending to SSM states — could be applied to the encoder, replacing the standard self-attention over the full input (which is quadratic and expensive for long documents) with BST layers that capture long-range dependencies through the SSM while using local attention within blocks. The experiment would train encoder-decoder BST models on SCROLLS or similar long-input benchmarks and compare against Longformer-Encoder-Decoder and similarly efficient Transformer variants. This tests whether the integration principle generalizes beyond autoregressive language modeling to bidirectional encoding contexts, where the SSM's convolutional formulation (which is inherently causal in the recurrent form) would need to be adapted to non-causal settings (the paper already does this for LRA, Appendix D, by using bidirectional S4 layers, but not in an encoder-decoder context). Success would expand BST's applicability to a much broader class of NLP tasks.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Training language models on long documents with reduced computational cost.** The most direct practical application is training decoder-only language models on corpora of long documents — books, scientific articles, legal documents, code repositories — where the training budget is fixed but the sequence length matters for quality. Table 1 provides a concrete recipe: a ~200M parameter BST:SH can be trained on PG19 to 11.52 perplexity in 0.5k TPUv4 hours, matching BRECT's 11.55 perplexity at 0.8k hours — a ~37% reduction in training compute for equivalent quality. For a team with a fixed compute budget, this means they can either train the same model faster (freeing resources for other experiments) or train for the same wall-clock time and achieve lower perplexity. The training setup is simpler than the primary alternative (GSS-HYBRID) because BST does not require per-layer learning rate tuning or grid search — the paper used the same learning rate for all layers, reducing the experimentation overhead for adopting the architecture.

**Length-flexible inference for variable-length user inputs.** The length generalization property of structured-kernel BST variants (Figure 3: BST:SH:S4-L maintains or improves perplexity from 4k to 65k tokens) enables a deployment pattern where a single trained model handles inputs of widely varying lengths without degradation. A production language model serving user requests that range from short queries (512 tokens) to long document analysis (65k tokens) would need separate models or complex bucketing with a standard Transformer (due to positional embedding constraints and quadratic attention cost at long lengths). BST:SH:S4 can process all lengths with a single checkpoint, compiling the SSM kernel to the required sequence length on-the-fly — the kernel extension from 4k to 65k requires only computing additional powers of the `A` matrix, which is a cheap operation. The model's perplexity actually *improves* at longer lengths on PG19 and GitHub (Figure 3), meaning the additional context is not just tolerated but productively used. This removes the need for length-based request routing or model multiplexing, simplifying the serving infrastructure.

**Efficient processing of repository-scale code for code generation and analysis.** The GitHub dataset results (Table 1: BST:MF:UNSTRUCT-L achieves 1.83 perplexity at ~400M parameters) and the architecture's design — SSM capturing cross-file dependencies through concatenated repository files, Block Transformer attending locally within file windows — maps directly onto a practical code analysis pipeline. A code generation or bug detection system that needs to understand an entire repository (not just the current file) could use BST layers to process the concatenated repository structure: the SSM compresses the history of all preceding files and directories into context states, and the Block Transformer attends to the current file's tokens while cross-attending to the repository-level context. The MF variant's advantage on GitHub (consistent across both S4 and UNSTRUCT kernels, Table 1) suggests that diverse, low-redundancy context states are particularly well-suited for code, where cross-file dependencies (imports, function calls, class inheritance) are semantically structured rather than temporally local. The 4× FFT efficiency improvement on GPU vs. TPU (Appendix A) means this pipeline would be particularly cost-effective on GPU-based inference hardware.

**On-device or edge deployment of long-context language models.** The efficiency results (Figure 4, left: BST:SH runs within 2× of a non-recurrent sliding-window layer on GPU) combined with the ability to use smaller window sizes (512 tokens in BST vs. 2048 in a comparable-quality Transformer-XL, Table 1) make BST attractive for resource-constrained deployments. A smaller window means lower memory usage for attention key-value caches during autoregressive inference, and the SSM's recurrent mode (Equation 1) requires only `O(N)` state storage per layer per sequence (where `N = 16`), not `O(L)` key-value storage. For a device with limited RAM, a BST model with window size 512 and SSM state size 16 could process arbitrarily long sequences with constant per-layer memory overhead, while a Transformer with window size 2048 would need to store 4× the key-value cache for equivalent quality. The paper does not benchmark inference memory or latency, but the architectural properties — small SSM state, constant per-token recurrent cost at inference time — suggest significant deployment advantages for edge scenarios like on-device document assistants or mobile code completion tools.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper implicitly defines a decision boundary between BST and its primary alternatives — BRECT and GSS-HYBRID — based on three factors: the need for length generalization, the available hardware (GPU vs. TPU), and the dominant type of dependency in the target data (local vs. long-range). The following decision rules are grounded in the paper's quantitative results.

- **Prefer BST with structured kernels (S4/DSS) when length generalization beyond the training context is required.** Figure 3 shows BST:SH:S4-L maintaining or improving perplexity at 65k tokens (3.2× beyond the 4k training length), while BST:MF:UNSTRUCT-L degrades and GSS-HYBRID-L degrades sharply on arXiv. This is the strongest evidence in the paper for any specific design choice. The mechanism — kernel compilation from `A, B, C` matrices via `K_j = CA^j B` — is extendable to arbitrary lengths without retraining. If your deployment involves variable-length inputs up to 65k+ tokens, a structured-kernel BST is the only variant with demonstrated length generalization.

- **Prefer BST when training on GPU hardware, but be cautious on TPUs.** Figure 4 shows BST:SH achieving 6–11× layer-level speedup over BRECT on GPU, with the SSM and Block Transformer costs nearly balanced. Appendix A reports FFT is 4× faster on GPU than TPU and that FFT is a "speed bottleneck on TPUs." The paper's training results on TPU (Table 1) show a more modest 1.6× end-to-end training speedup over BRECT. If your training infrastructure is GPU-based, BST's parallelization advantage is fully realized; if TPU-based, expect a smaller speedup and consider S5 or other FFT-free SSM variants as drop-in replacements (as the paper suggests in Appendix A).

- **Prefer the SH context state variant when local narrative coherence is the dominant dependency.** BST:SH and its variants consistently achieve the best PG19 perplexity at both model scales (Table 1: 11.52 for ~200M UNSTRUCT, 10.37 for ~400M UNSTRUCT), and PG19 is characterized by local dependencies — character tracking, dialogue coherence, immediate narrative flow. The SH variant's adjacent-time context states provide maximum retrievability of recent local information, and the paper's hypothesis (though not directly validated) is that this advantage matters for text where predicting the next token depends primarily on the recent linguistic context.

- **Prefer the MF context state variant when long-range cross-references or structured dependencies dominate.** BST:MF variants achieve the best arXiv and GitHub perplexity (Table 1: 2.41 and 1.83 for ~400M UNSTRUCT), and these datasets are characterized by non-local dependencies — LaTeX cross-references, theorem citations, code imports across files. The MF variant's independent filters produce context states with lower redundancy and greater diversity, which the paper hypothesizes enables better retrieval of semantically structured long-range information. If your data resembles scientific articles or code repositories more than narrative text, MF is the empirically supported choice.

- **Prefer BST over BRECT when training compute is the binding constraint, even on TPUs.** At the ~200M scale on PG19, BST:SH achieves 11.52 perplexity in 0.5k TPUv4 hours vs. BRECT's 11.55 in 0.8k hours (Table 1) — statistically tied quality at 62% of the training compute. Even factoring in the TPU FFT bottleneck, BST trains faster for equivalent perplexity. If your limiting resource is training budget rather than model quality at any cost, BST dominates BRECT at the scales tested.

- **Prefer BST over GSS-HYBRID when training simplicity matters.** GSS-HYBRID-L required grid search over four learning rates and separate learning rate scheduling for SSM and Transformer layers to achieve its reported results (the paper notes this explicitly). BST used the same learning rate for all layers with no grid search. If your experimentation workflow cannot support per-layer hyperparameter tuning (common in applied settings where models must be retrained frequently on new data), BST provides equivalent or better quality with substantially simpler training.
