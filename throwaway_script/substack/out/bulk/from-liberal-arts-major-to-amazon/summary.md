Here are the takeaways from this Steve Huynh interview (went from English major to Principal Engineer at Amazon, navigating a 19-year journey of promotions and five consecutive rejections):

**Liberal Arts Major to Amazon Support Engineer (2005-2007)**

Near the end of his English degree at the University of Washington, Steve asked his writing professor what jobs to target. The reply was blunt: “Steve, you can’t apply to the job called writer. There is a job called waiter, though. You need to find a way to make some money to survive while you ply your craft.” Panic set in—he had no interest in waiting tables. He then applied a skill that became a career-long habit: **set an aggressive target and work backward from it**. He created a Venn diagram of his skills, what was in demand, and what paid well, and landed on “software developer at a big tech company.” He had been programming since the late eighties (both parents were electrical engineers), learned BASIC in grade school, got his first C compiler around age 10, and took AP computer science in high school (Pascal and C++). He minored in math and applied math—so he had the raw ingredients to pass a tech interview. He spent months preparing and sending resumes, but hit the classic chicken-and-egg problem: nobody would interview someone with no experience.

Complaining about this to an old friend over beers, he learned Amazon was looking for support engineers. The position normally required technical experience, but his friend could vouch for him and land him an interview. He jumped on it even though it wasn’t a true software developer role. Because he lacked the degree and experience, they hired him as a temporary contractor with a path to full-time. Support engineers handled builds, deployment, and operations—work adjacent to software engineers—so he’d get exposure. He saw this as his big opportunity and went all in. Instead of just doing assigned work, he spent extra cycles learning the inner workings of production systems: how they break, how to fix them. He became an expert in metrics and monitoring, and learned to insert important work into the software development lifecycle. Because he knew the operations of features millions of customers used daily, he preempted issues that would have set back big launches like the Kindle and Unbox Video (the precursor to Prime Video). This work, requiring a lot of extra cycles, earned him positive visibility. Instead of just doing assigned tasks, he was converting ambient knowledge into proactive risk-prevention—and was converted to a full-time support engineer in 2007. Ryan notes the pattern: he didn’t wait for permission; he created his own surface area.

**Support Engineer to Software Development Engineer (2007-2008)**

A little under a year after becoming full-time, he felt he’d outgrown the support role. His share of oncall was increasing because departing team members weren't backfilled, and the combination of extra effort and increased oncall was burning him out. This, coupled with his deferred goal of becoming an SDE, drove him to get structured and disciplined about preparing for a role change. In 2007-2008, there were no high-quality interview-prep resources—no books, no YouTube, no LeetCode. So he became a collector of coding interview questions, scouring internal wikis, the internet, and asking everyone he knew what they asked during interviews. He got every SDE he knew well to give him mock interviews, insisting they stay in character so he could get used to performing while anxious and uncomfortable. Over time, coding in front of a whiteboard became second nature.

Once ready, he contacted three teams internally to schedule SDE interviews. He did this for redundancy and to give himself choices if he got multiple offers. Batching interviews meant he could take a break after the batch ended—he was pushing hard to do his job while preparing, and got through it by telling himself there was an end date. He circled the day after his last interviews on his calendar and scheduled a long Hawaii vacation. “No matter what, I was going to be in Hawaii, having a tropical drink by the pool, whether I was offered a software engineering job or not.” The preparation paid off: he passed all three. He reverse-interviewed each team, speaking with engineers about whether they enjoyed their work, work-life balance, coding time, and most importantly how they got along with their manager. He dodged a bullet: the team he was initially most interested in had a terrible oncall load and hadn’t delivered new code in over a year. Another team’s manager he didn’t click with. He joined the Payments organization, building a new product and guaranteed to write a lot of code. He had realized his goal.

**Mid-level to Senior SDE (2009-2012)**

By 2011 he’d been an SDE for over three years and at Amazon for over five, working on a new product with a team of about eight. Amazon decided to invest heavily in the line of business and needed to ramp hiring to well over 100. Interviewing at Amazon is volunteer time—training, conducting the interview, and attending debrief takes time away from delivery. He recognized the biggest risk to the business wasn’t immediate 0-3-month deliveries, but the big 9-12-month projects that required staffing an order of magnitude more engineers. The bottleneck to staffing was interviewing, so he decided to become an Amazon **bar-raiser**—a volunteer expert interviewer who leads debriefs and holds veto power over offers. That year he conducted over 150 interviews and was instrumental in staffing the organization with top talent.

Because they hired well, the business succeeded and pulled in significant revenue. But the increased revenue brought a flood of support calls from local business sellers. Support staff were drowning and reaching out to dev teams for help with payments, reporting, and operational issues—stealing dev cycles from new capabilities. Drawing on his support background, Steve recognized most issues could be resolved with a self-service seller portal similar to Amazon's Seller Central. He pitched the idea to his manager, who smiled and said senior management already had the same idea. But none of the senior engineers wanted to pick it up—seller payments, reporting, and operational ticket tracking weren’t considered exciting work. Steve volunteered to lead it from the engineering side. The scope was aligned with senior engineer expectations, but because he pitched it and other senior engineers weren't interested, he led it. Four months later the backend local business portal launched, registering tens of thousands of sellers. Operational tickets plummeted, and a business bottleneck was addressed. He was promoted to SDE-III (L6) in 2012 because of his prolific interviewing and the portal launch. Although he contributed a lot of high-quality code, it was the technical leadership—growing a team through hiring and leading a high-visibility project—that made him stand out. In both cases, he identified a bottleneck and took initiative. He wasn’t handed the work. And in both cases, he had to put in more hours because to succeed with next-level scope, he had to acquire skills he didn’t yet have.

**Senior SDE to Principal Engineer: The Five-Rejection Grind (2016-2020)**

Amazon lacks Staff and Senior Staff levels; the next jump is to Principal Engineer. The promotion is widely regarded as one of the hardest in big tech—many senior engineers who couldn’t secure it left for other companies and became L7, L8, even L9 at Google and Meta. By 2016, Steve had been an SDE-III for four years and thought he was ready. He had helped kickstart multiple Amazon lines of business, going from zero to one, designing and launching operationally excellent systems scaled globally to hundreds of millions of customers. He had led cross-functional projects with a large number of dependencies, high complexity, and thorny technical challenges. He had helped staff many teams and created training materials to ramp them up. He had filed and been granted several patents, often co-authoring them with principal and senior principal engineers. He acted as a mentor and advisor to engineers in his organization.

Then the managerial turbulence hit. Several middle managers left simultaneously, and his director didn’t want a senior engineer reporting directly to him, so Steve ended up reporting to a manager a level below him. After about six months he finally reported to a senior manager, but it was “one of the worst managers I’ve ever had.” He was a micromanager, not technically deep, a poor communicator, and constantly promised things without following through. An example: he needed to submit a formal request for Steve’s promotion. Without it, Steve would wait another six months. He reminded him constantly; the manager promised to do it, but the deadline passed and it was never submitted. “To this day I still wonder how he got to where he was and what he’s doing.”

About three years later, Steve reported to one of the best managers he’s ever had—not a micromanager, technically deep, great communicator, always followed through. He authored Steve’s promotion document and shared a draft. But when Steve was put up, the packet was rejected for a simple reason: he had stopped doing all the things that made him a good senior engineer and only focused on tasks demonstrating principal-level performance. He needed to get back to basics. He was devastated—this was the fourth time he’d been put up since 2016. To make matters worse, his great manager was leaving the company. But because he was such a good manager, he properly transferred the packet to the new manager, put in a good word, and gave Steve an unexpectedly large raise on his way out that put him close to next-level compensation. “What a guy!”

Steve spent the next six months being the best senior developer he could be. He wrote a ton of code, authored several lower-level design documents, put himself back on the oncall rotation, came back to daily standup and planning meetings. When his packet was rejected for the fifth time, the feedback was simple: he had only done the senior engineer tasks and hadn’t adequately demonstrated principal-level performance over the past six months. The rejection still stung, but he now understood exactly what the expectation was—he needed to be a great senior engineer *and* show he could handle principal scope, **simultaneously**. He made a plan to demonstrate both with his new manager, and was finally promoted to principal engineer in Q1 2020.

His through-line lesson: “the best feedback for promotion is a prior promotion rejection.” It tells you exactly what to work on for the next cycle. He advises building a specific plan to close your gaps from that feedback. On the earlier managerial bottleneck, he’s clear: your manager relationship is the most critical relationship for promotion. If it’s not great, strive to strengthen it, and consider moving teams or companies if you’ve tried for a while without success.

**The Underlying Rhythm**

Across nearly two decades, Steve returned to the same recipe: assess what’s going on around you, set aggressive targets, and choose specific moments to put in extra effort—not all the time. Putting in more hours at the right times makes a huge difference in career progression; doing so continuously leads to burnout. He never became a writer in the sense he imagined in college, but now as a full-time content creator he writes every day, and his English degree ended up being a large benefit. He’s open to setting another aggressive, ambitious target to become a more traditional creative writer if the situation presents itself—but for now, he’s happy to share his experience to help others grow their careers.

---

His entire arc is a live-fire demonstration of what he calls **setting an aggressive target and working backward from it**—applied first to breaking into tech, then to escaping support, then to senior and principal. The twin engines were identifying bottlenecks nobody else wanted to touch (the bar-raiser hiring surge, the unglamorous seller portal) and treating every rejection as diagnostic fuel, not defeat. His hard-won insistence that you must *simultaneously* nail your current level *and* the next one became the key that finally unlocked the principal promo on attempt number six. Underneath it all runs a pacing discipline: sprint at the right inflection points, then step back so you don’t burn out before the next climb.
