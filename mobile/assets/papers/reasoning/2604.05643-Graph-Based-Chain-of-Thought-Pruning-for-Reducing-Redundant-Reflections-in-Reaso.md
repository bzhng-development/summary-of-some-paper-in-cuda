# Graph-Based Chain-of-Thought Pruning for Reducing Redundant Reflections in Reasoning LLMs

**ArXiv:** [2604.05643](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.05643)

## 🎯 Pitch

Reasoning LLMs waste nearly half their tokens on repetitive self-checks and trivial verifications. This paper shows that by first parsing Chain-of-Thought into a dependency graph, then surgically removing reflection nodes that neither contribute new information nor affect the final answer, it cuts reasoning length by 42% and slightly boosts accuracy—while naive truncation collapses performance by over 20 points.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces a **graph-based CoT optimization framework** that reduces redundant intermediate reasoning in large reasoning models by identifying and pruning two specific inefficiency patterns: Indiscriminate Reflection (broad low-impact checks throughout reasoning, such as verifying every arithmetic step even when trivial) and Repetitive Reflection (repeatedly re-verifying already-established conclusions in later stages). Applying the method to DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B on five math benchmarks (AIME24, AIME25, AMC23, OlympiadBench, MATH500), the approach reduces average reasoning tokens by 42.7% (from 8134 to 4660) while improving average accuracy from 59.72% to 60.95%, and yields consistent gains at the 1.5B scale. The paper establishes that explicitly modeling reasoning as a directed acyclic graph with progress and review node types, then pruning via branch-level and depth-level redundancy criteria, recovers the core reasoning logic from redundant reflection only when the graph structure preserves essential dependencies — naive length truncation degrades accuracy by over 20 percentage points compared to the graph-based pruning approach.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Redundant Reflection as a Barrier to Efficient Reasoning

This paper tackles a specific, well-defined inefficiency in reasoning LLMs: **the generation of redundant reflection steps that consume substantial inference compute without contributing new information to the reasoning process**. The problem is not that reasoning models produce reflection—some level of self-checking and verification is essential for complex multi-step reasoning. Rather, the problem is that in models trained with reinforcement learning on sparse reward signals, this reflective behavior becomes **indiscriminate** (applied uniformly to both trivial and crucial steps) and **repetitive** (re-verified conclusions are checked again and again in later stages of the same trajectory). The result is a significant fraction of generated tokens that serve no meaningful role in reaching the correct answer.

This matters for several concrete reasons. First, **inference cost scales directly with output length**—every redundant token generated consumes GPU compute, memory bandwidth, and wall-clock time. For production deployments serving thousands or millions of queries, a 42.7% reduction in reasoning tokens (the paper's headline result on 7B-scale models) translates directly to equivalent cost savings or throughput gains. Second, **longer outputs degrade user experience in interactive applications**, where latency is already a pain point for reasoning models that may generate thousands of tokens before producing a final answer. Third, and perhaps most significantly from a research perspective, **redundant reflection represents a fundamental failure mode of current RL training recipes** for reasoning models. The sparse, delayed reward signals typical of mathematical reasoning tasks (where feedback comes only after the complete solution is generated) make credit assignment over long trajectories difficult (Tran et al., 2025). As a consequence, models discover during training that generating more tokens—additional checks, restatements, and verification loops—is a low-risk strategy that occasionally helps and rarely hurts final answer accuracy, even when most of those tokens are logically unnecessary. This creates a **systematic bias toward verbosity** that compounds as RL training progresses.

### The "Overthinking" Phenomenon as a Recognized Challenge

The paper situates itself within a growing body of work documenting "overthinking" in long reasoning models. Chen et al. (2025a) explicitly identified that long reasoning models generate excessive intermediate content even for simple problems, coining the term "overthinking" to describe the phenomenon. Sui et al. (2025) provided a comprehensive survey of efficient reasoning methods, cataloging the various ways in which chain-of-thought generation can become pathologically verbose. The key insight from this prior work is that **longer chains are not always more effective**—beyond a certain point, additional reasoning length contributes diminishing or even negative returns to accuracy while driving up inference cost. This is precisely the efficiency frontier that the paper's Figure 1 illustrates: methods that reduce reasoning length without sacrificing accuracy push the Pareto frontier toward the upper-left region of the accuracy-vs-tokens plot.

However, the paper argues that prior approaches to mitigating overthinking have been **insufficiently targeted**. They either apply blanket compression heuristics that risk discarding essential reasoning, or they rely on surface-level signals (token importance scores, attention patterns, perplexity thresholds) that cannot distinguish between redundant reflection and structurally necessary verification. This is where the paper's core contribution—explicit structural modeling of the reasoning dependency graph—becomes necessary.

### Where Existing Approaches Fall Short

The paper positions its approach against two broad categories of prior work on efficient reasoning:

**1. Length control and reasoning budget methods.** One line of work directly regulates the overall reasoning budget by either predicting or assigning a token budget per problem (Han et al., 2025: token-budget-aware LLM reasoning; Ma et al., 2025b: CoT-Valve, which performs length-compressible chain-of-thought tuning). Other approaches introduce length-aware training objectives in reinforcement learning (Luo et al., 2025b: O1-Pruner, a length-harmonizing fine-tuning approach; Arora and Zanette, 2025: EfficientReasoning, an RL-based objective that favors correct yet concise reasoning). The fundamental limitation of these methods is that they treat reasoning length as a **global quantity to be reduced** without understanding *which* parts of the reasoning are redundant and *why*. Enforcing a shorter length budget can force the model to omit genuinely necessary steps, degrading accuracy on problems that require careful multi-step derivation. The paper's pruning-what-matters experiment (Table 3) demonstrates precisely this failure mode: naive length truncation (Len-Trunc) that matches the token count of graph-pruned traces causes accuracy to plummet from 98.95% to 73.60% and consistency from 99.60% to 69.10%. Simply removing tokens without understanding their functional role in the reasoning structure destroys coherence.

**2. Content-based redundancy detection methods.** A second line of work identifies and removes redundant content within generated trajectories. TokenSkip (Xia et al., 2025) estimates token-level semantic importance and removes low-utility tokens for controllable compression. Think Clearly (Choi et al., 2025) and TRAAC (Singh et al., 2025) leverage attention-based criteria to detect redundant tokens. SPIRIT (Cui et al., 2025) and Step-Entropy (Li et al., 2025a) use perplexity- and entropy-related cues to identify and remove low-information steps or tokens. These methods are more granular than budget-based approaches, but they share a common weakness: **they operate on surface-level statistics of the generated text** (token attention weights, probability distributions, semantic similarity scores) without recovering the underlying **logical dependency structure** of the reasoning. This means they can:
- Mistake dense but essential reasoning (e.g., a compact algebraic derivation where many tokens carry critical information) for redundancy because the tokens are individually low-importance by attention or perplexity metrics.
- Fail to identify structurally redundant reflection because the reflected-upon content is semantically similar to earlier content (a review node that restates an earlier conclusion may look "important" by attention metrics precisely because it attends to the earlier conclusion).
- Lack a principled criterion for what constitutes redundancy—"low importance" is a relative, threshold-dependent concept that requires careful calibration.

The paper's central argument is that to reliably identify redundant reflection, one must first **recover the dependency relations among reasoning steps**. Reflection is redundant not because its tokens score low on some importance metric, but because it occupies a specific **structural position** in the reasoning graph: it forms a narrow side-branch with few descendants (branch-level redundancy) or it appears in the late stages of the reasoning trajectory after the core solution has already been established (depth-level redundancy). These criteria are graph-theoretic, not statistical—they depend on understanding which steps depend on which other steps, a question that cannot be answered by examining tokens in isolation.

### Why Existing Work Misses the Reflection-Specific Nature of Redundancy

Prior work has recognized that "reflective" behaviors are a significant source of verbosity in reasoning models. For example, Chen et al. (2025a) noted that long reasoning models over-check simple arithmetic, and Sui et al. (2025) cataloged patterns such as excessive self-verification and backtracking. However, the paper identifies a gap: **no prior method explicitly models reflection as a structural category of reasoning node with its own dependency properties, separate from content-generating ("progress") reasoning**. This distinction is crucial because the redundancy criteria for reflection nodes are fundamentally different from those for progress nodes:

- **Progress nodes** advance the reasoning frontier by producing new conclusions, introducing variables, setting up equations, or establishing frameworks. A progress node is rarely redundant by structure alone—it may be *incorrect*, but if it contributes to the final answer, removing it breaks the dependency chain.

- **Review nodes** verify, check, restate, or backtrack over existing material without advancing the reasoning frontier. A review node's contribution is measured by whether subsequent reasoning *uses* its output. If a review node has few or no descendants (branch-level redundancy), it effectively leads nowhere—the model checked something, concluded it was correct, and then moved on without using that verification in any meaningful way. If a review node appears in the late stages after the main solution is established (depth-level redundancy), it represents post-answer backtracking that cannot change the answer.

The paper's framing of "Indiscriminate Reflection" and "Repetitive Reflection" as the two specific failure modes provides a **taxonomy of redundancy** that guides both the graph construction (by requiring node-type labeling as progress vs. review) and the pruning criteria (by applying different structural thresholds to review nodes than would be appropriate for progress nodes). This taxonomy is what distinguishes the approach from generic "remove low-importance tokens" methods.

### The Conceptual Gap: Linear Text vs. Reasoning Structure

Underpinning all of these limitations is a deeper conceptual issue: **chain-of-thought reasoning is generated as linear text but has an inherently non-linear logical structure**. Real mathematical reasoning involves branching exploration ("let me try a different approach"), backtracking from dead ends ("this doesn't work, let me reconsider"), parallel verification of independent sub-problems, and cross-step dependencies where a conclusion established early in the trajectory is referenced much later. The linear text format obscures these structural relationships—a reader (or a pruning algorithm) examining the text sequentially cannot easily determine whether step 15 depends on step 7 or step 12, whether a "let me check this" at step 20 refers to something established at step 5 or step 18, or whether a reflection branch ultimately feeds back into the main reasoning or terminates without influence.

The paper's key methodological insight is that **explicitly constructing a directed acyclic graph (DAG) from the linear CoT recovers these dependency relations**, enabling structure-aware pruning that would be impossible from the text alone. This connects to a broader theme in reasoning research: the tension between the **sequential generation process** (autoregressive language models produce tokens left-to-right) and the **structured reasoning process** (human mathematical reasoning is fundamentally graph-structured, with sub-goals, dependencies, and parallel explorations). By converting the output format back into a graph, the paper effectively performs **reverse engineering** of the reasoning structure that the model implicitly followed when generating the linear text.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper positions its contribution at the intersection of two research threads: **efficient reasoning** (reducing inference cost for reasoning models) and **structural analysis of chain-of-thought** (understanding the logical organization of generated reasoning). It does not claim to be the first to observe that reasoning models over-reflect or that CoT can be compressed. Instead, its contribution is **methodological specificity**: by modeling CoT as a graph with typed nodes (progress vs. review), it provides a principled, interpretable framework for answering the question "which reflection steps are actually unnecessary?" rather than relying on heuristic importance scores or global length constraints.

The paper explicitly contrasts its approach with prior work in the opening of Section 1:

> "To precisely identify redundant reflection, we need to recover the dependency relations among reasoning steps. Since practical reasoning often involves non-linear behaviors such as branching exploration, backtracking, and cross-step repetition, we propose to explicitly model CoT with a graph structure."

This framing makes clear that the graph construction is not an optional representation choice—it is the **necessary first step** for any pruning method that claims to preserve essential reasoning while removing redundancy. The paper's experimental demonstration that graph-based pruning preserves 93.70% accuracy while length-matched naive truncation achieves only 73.60% (Table 3) is the empirical validation of this claim: the graph structure captures something essential about reasoning coherence that raw token counts and surface-level statistics miss.

Finally, the paper's three-stage training pipeline (SFT → DPO → GRPO with length penalty) positions the approach within the broader paradigm of post-training for reasoning models. Rather than proposing a fundamentally new training algorithm, it shows how **existing training recipes can be augmented with graph-based data preprocessing** to achieve both accuracy improvements and length reduction. This makes the method practically adoptable—it integrates with standard RLHF pipelines (DPO, GRPO) that the reasoning community already uses, with the graph construction and pruning applied as a data preprocessing step before training begins.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

The system being built is a **post-training pipeline** that takes an existing reasoning LLM (one that already generates long chain-of-thought solutions with embedded self-checking and verification) and produces a fine-tuned version that generates substantially shorter, more efficient reasoning traces while maintaining or improving answer accuracy. The problem it solves is **redundant reflection during inference**: the trained model learns to skip low-value self-verification steps that do not contribute meaningful new information to the reasoning process, without losing the ability to check its work when it genuinely matters. The "shape" of the solution is a three-stage training recipe that begins with **data preprocessing** (converting reasoning traces into dependency graphs, pruning structurally redundant reflection nodes, and relinearizing into concise CoT), then applies **preference alignment** (DPO on concise-vs-redundant trajectory pairs) and finally **reinforcement learning with length penalty** (GRPO that jointly optimizes answer correctness and reasoning efficiency).

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The system has five major components that operate sequentially:

1. **CoT Chunking Module** — Takes a raw linear chain-of-thought solution and splits it into step-level chunks at explicit reasoning transition keywords (e.g., "Wait", "Alternatively", "But wait", "Therefore"). These chunks serve as the atomic units for graph construction.

2. **Iterative Graph Constructor** — Processes chunks one at a time with an external LLM (e.g., qwen-turbo) prompted to maintain and update a directed acyclic graph (DAG). For each chunk, the constructor decides whether to insert a new node or merge the chunk into an existing node, assigns node types as either `progress` (advances the reasoning frontier) or `review` (verifies/restates/backtracks without advancing), and adds directed edges encoding logical dependencies between nodes.

3. **Graph Pruning Module** — Applies two structural redundancy criteria to the completed DAG: (a) branch-level pruning removes `review` nodes with fewer than `k = 2` descendants, representing narrow side-branches that never feed back into the main reasoning; (b) depth-level pruning removes `review` nodes appearing in the final 10% of the reasoning trajectory (relative depth > `m = 0.9`), representing late-stage post-answer re-verification. Surviving nodes are relinearized into a compressed CoT text.

4. **Three-Stage Training Pipeline** — (Stage 1) Supervised fine-tuning on pruned concise traces to initialize the model with efficient reasoning behavior; (Stage 2) DPO on preference pairs where low-redundancy correct trajectories are preferred over high-redundancy correct trajectories; (Stage 3) GRPO with a length penalty term that penalizes excess reasoning length relative to the shortest correct trajectory in each rollout group.

5. **Inference-Time Model** — The final fine-tuned policy model generates reasoning directly without any graph construction or pruning—the graph-based preprocessing is purely a training-time mechanism for teaching the model what efficient reasoning looks like.

Information flows as follows: raw CoT solutions from training data enter the chunking module → chunks feed into the iterative graph constructor → completed graphs enter the pruning module → pruned graphs are relinearized into concise CoT text → concise CoT pairs with original answers form SFT training data → SFT model generates rollouts for DPO preference pair construction → DPO model generates rollouts for GRPO → final model is used for inference.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **methodology paper** whose core idea is that redundant reflection in reasoning LLMs can be identified and removed by first recovering the dependency structure of the reasoning as a typed directed acyclic graph, applying structural pruning criteria to review nodes specifically, and then using the pruned traces to train the model to internalize efficient reasoning behavior through a three-stage pipeline of supervised fine-tuning, preference optimization, and reinforcement learning with length regularization.

---

#### Formal Problem Definition

The paper frames the problem as follows. Let `$M_\theta$` be a large reasoning model parameterized by `$\theta$`. For a given problem `$q$`, the model generates reasoning `$r$` and produces a final answer `$a$`, written as:

$$r, a \sim M_\theta(q)$$

where the reasoning part `$r$` is split into a sequence of chunks `$r = [c_1, c_2, \ldots, c_n]$` at special control tokens. Each chunk `$c_i$` represents an individual reasoning step generated by the model, and `$n$` denotes the total number of reasoning chunks.

The objective is to produce a method that **locates and removes redundant reflective segments** in `$r$`, yielding:

$$\tilde{r} = \text{Prune}(r) \subseteq r, \quad \tilde{r} = [\tilde{c}_1, \ldots, \tilde{c}_{\tilde{n}}]$$

where `$\tilde{r}$` is a subsequence of the original chunks with redundancies eliminated. The pruned trace is then used to fine-tune the model `$M_\theta$` on a dataset `$\mathcal{D} = \{(q, \tilde{r}, a)\}$`, so that the fine-tuned model learns to generate more efficient and concise reasoning paths at inference time while maintaining or improving overall accuracy.

**What this formulation establishes:** The pruning operation is a **subset selection** over chunks—it removes entire steps rather than modifying individual tokens within steps. This is a crucial design decision because it preserves the semantic integrity of surviving steps (a chunk that remains is exactly as the model originally generated it) while eliminating reflection that is structurally unnecessary. The alternative—token-level deletion within chunks—would risk creating syntactically or semantically malformed intermediate steps that the model might not learn to reproduce cleanly.

**Why this form matters:** By operating at the chunk level, the pruning criteria can be defined in terms of the **structural role** of each chunk in the reasoning graph (is this a verification step that leads nowhere? is this a late-stage re-check of an already-established conclusion?) rather than in terms of token-level statistics (is this particular word important?). This connects the pruning operation directly to the graph construction step—the chunks are exactly the nodes of the graph—and ensures that the surviving reasoning trace remains a coherent sequence of complete reasoning steps that the model can meaningfully learn to imitate during SFT.

#### CoT Chunking: Splitting Reasoning into Step-Level Units

Before any graph construction can occur, the raw linear chain-of-thought must be divided into discrete reasoning steps. The paper defines a set of **split tokens**—keywords and phrases that typically indicate a transition between reasoning operations in LLM-generated mathematical CoT. The complete list provided in Appendix B includes:

- **Reflection indicators:** `"Wait"`, `"But wait"`, `"Hold on"`, `"Hmm"`, `"Maybe"`, `"Looking back"`, `"Let me double-check"`, `"I don't see any errors"`, `"That's correct"`, `"That seems right"`, `"Seems solid"`, `"Correct"`, `"Good"`, `"Got it"`
- **Alternative exploration indicators:** `"Alternatively"`, `"Another angle"`, `"Another approach"`
- **Progression indicators:** `"First"`, `"Then"`, `"Now"`, `"So"`, `"Therefore"`, `"Thus"`, `"Similarly"`, `"Starting"`, `"Alright"`, `"Okay"`, `"Let me"`, `"Compute"`, `"I think"`, `"Let's see"`, `"Remember"`

**How chunking operates:** The raw CoT string is scanned for occurrences of any token in the split set. Each occurrence marks the boundary between consecutive chunks. A chunk consists of the split token itself (if it is a meaningful reasoning marker) plus all text until the next split token or the end of the CoT. For example, a CoT fragment like `"...Therefore, s = 2.5 km/h. Wait, let me verify this with equation 2..."` would split into two chunks: one ending before `"Wait"` (the conclusion chunk) and one starting at `"Wait"` (the verification chunk).

**Why chunk-level granularity:** The choice to split at explicit transition keywords rather than using fixed-length segments or sentence boundaries is deliberate. Reasoning models trained with RL on mathematical problems learn to use these specific discourse markers to organize their thinking—they signal shifts between advancing the solution, checking intermediate results, exploring alternatives, and backtracking. By aligning chunk boundaries with these discourse-level transitions, each chunk tends to represent a **functionally coherent reasoning operation** (set up an equation, verify a solution, try a different approach) rather than an arbitrary text segment. This coherence is essential for the subsequent graph construction step, where each chunk must be classified as either `progress` (advancing the reasoning frontier) or `review` (verifying/restating without advancing). A chunk that mixes both functions—for example, a segment that both derives a new equation and then immediately verifies it—would be ambiguous to classify and would complicate the dependency analysis.

**What the chunking enables:** Once the CoT is decomposed into chunks `$[c_1, \ldots, c_n]$`, the graph construction algorithm processes them sequentially, building the DAG incrementally. The sequential order of chunks preserves the original generation order, which is important because the graph must capture not only logical dependencies (which conclusion depends on which premise) but also the **exploration order** (which branches were pursued and when they were abandoned). The pruning module later uses this temporal information to identify late-stage reflection (depth-level redundancy) that occurs after the main solution is complete.

#### Iterative Graph Construction

This is the paper's central technical contribution: converting a linear CoT into a typed directed acyclic graph (DAG) where nodes represent abstract reasoning units with explicit dependency edges. The construction is performed **iteratively**, processing one chunk at a time, so that each new chunk is integrated into the partial graph built from all previous chunks.

**Graph state definition:** At step `$i$` (after processing chunks `$c_1$` through `$c_i$`), the graph is defined as:

$$G_i = (V_i, E_i, \ell_i)$$

where `$V_i$` is the set of nodes created so far, `$E_i \subseteq V_i \times V_i$` is the set of directed edges encoding logical dependencies (an edge `$u \to v$` means node `$v$` uses the reasoning product established by node `$u$`), and `$\ell_i: V_i \to \{\text{progress}, \text{review}\}$` is a labeling function that assigns each node one of two semantic types:

- **`progress`:** A node that advances the active reasoning frontier by producing new information that later steps depend on. Examples include introducing variables and translating problem conditions into equations, deriving a new intermediate conclusion, setting up a case split, establishing a lemma or invariant, or initiating a new solution approach. The key property is that a progress node's output is **used by subsequent reasoning**—it creates dependencies that later nodes build upon.

- **`review`:** A node that reads, checks, restates, deletes, or rewinds existing material without advancing the frontier. Examples include verifying an arithmetic calculation, confirming that a derived equation is consistent with problem constraints, restating an earlier conclusion in different words, backtracking from a dead end ("this approach doesn't work"), or double-checking that all conditions have been satisfied. The key property is that a review node **does not produce new information that subsequent reasoning structurally depends on**—it may confirm correctness and thus provide confidence, but the confirmed information already exists in the graph.

**Initialization:** The construction begins with an empty graph `$G_0 = (\emptyset, \emptyset, \emptyset)$`—no nodes, no edges, no labels.

**Iterative update:** At each step `$i$` (for `$i = 1, \ldots, n$`), conditioned on the current graph `$G_{i-1}$` and the current chunk `$c_i$`, the system prompts an external LLM (referred to as `$f_L$` and implemented as qwen-turbo) to predict an operation:

$$o_i \sim f_L(G_{i-1}, c_i)$$

where `$o_i \in \{\text{insert}, \text{merge}\}$` indicates how to incorporate chunk `$c_i$` into the graph.

**The insert operation:** When `$o_i = \text{insert}$`, the LLM determines that chunk `$c_i$` represents a distinct reasoning unit that should become its own node in the graph. The LLM then:

1. Creates a new node `$v_i = (s_i, l_i)$`, where `$s_i$` is a concise summary of the node's content (its reasoning product), and `$l_i \in \{\text{progress}, \text{review}\}$` is the node's semantic type.
2. Determines which existing nodes the new node logically depends on—that is, which previous reasoning products are used as inputs to this new step.
3. Adds directed edges from those dependency nodes to the new node `$v_i$`, with each edge labeled to indicate the nature of the dependency (e.g., "uses equation from", "verifies result of", "provides alternative to").

**The merge operation:** When `$o_i = \text{merge}$`, the LLM determines that chunk `$c_i$` does not represent a distinct new reasoning unit but rather continues, elaborates, or restates content that already belongs to an existing node. The LLM then:

1. Selects a target node `$v^* \in V_{i-1}$` into which the current chunk's content should be integrated.
2. Updates the target node's summary `$s^*$` to incorporate the new information while keeping the node semantically coherent and single-purpose.

**Constraints on merging:** The paper imposes two hard constraints on the merge operation. First, **review content cannot be merged into a progress node**—if the current chunk contains reflective verification and the target node is a progress-type node representing frontier advancement, merging would create a node that mixes "advance" and "reflect" as major functions, violating the semantic atomicity of the node type. Second, merging is prohibited **if it would cause one node to mix "advance" and "reflect" as major functions**—this is the same constraint stated more generally to cover cases where the target node might be review and the chunk is progress-oriented. These constraints ensure that the node type labels remain semantically pure, which is essential for the downstream pruning criteria (which only operate on review nodes and depend on clear type boundaries).

**Edge construction principles:** The dependency edges encode which nodes use the products of which other nodes. The prompt (shown in full in Appendix A, Figure 6) specifies several principles:
- **Dependency principle:** If node B uses the reasoning product established by node A, add edge `$A \to B$` with a descriptive label.
- **Branch origin principle:** When a new attempt or alternative approach is initiated, it must branch from still-valid ancestor products, not from negated or dead-end nodes. This ensures that exploration branches are correctly rooted in the reasoning state that motivated them.
- **Ordering constraints:** Node IDs increase lexicographically (A–Z, AA–AZ, BA–BZ, ...), edges must go from lexicographically smaller IDs to larger IDs, and the final node must be named `final answer`. These constraints enforce a topological ordering compatible with the DAG structure—no cycles are possible when edges only go from earlier to later IDs.

**Why iterative construction rather than batch:** Processing chunks one at a time, with each step conditioned on the graph constructed so far, mimics how a human would build a dependency graph while reading the CoT sequentially. The partial graph provides context for interpreting each new chunk—for example, if the graph already contains a node representing "derived equation (1)" and the current chunk says "plugging this back into equation (1)", the LLM can identify the dependency edge by matching the reference to the existing node. If the LLM instead processed all chunks simultaneously, it would need to resolve all cross-references at once, a harder reasoning task that is more prone to errors. The iterative approach also naturally handles the fact that later chunks often refer to or build upon the **graph structure itself**—for instance, a chunk that says "let me try a different approach" implicitly references a failure in the current main branch, which is represented in the graph structure at that point in the iteration.

**Prompt structure and output format:** The prompt (Appendix A, Figure 6) provides:
- The current graph in Mermaid code format (a text-based graph description language).
- The current chunk text.
- Detailed instructions on node definition (semantic completeness, abstraction level, dependability), independence criteria for when to insert vs. merge, constraints on merging, and edge construction rules.

The LLM responds with a strict JSON object containing the `decision` field (`"Insert"` or `"Merge"`), the target node ID (if merging), the new node specification (if inserting: ID, description, and type), the list of directed edges with labels, and the updated node description (if merging). The JSON format ensures deterministic parsing for downstream processing.

**Why an external LLM for graph construction:** The graph construction requires strong semantic understanding of the reasoning content—it must identify what each step accomplishes, whether it produces new information or merely verifies existing information, and which earlier steps it depends on. This is essentially a reasoning task in itself, requiring a model with strong language understanding and mathematical reasoning capabilities. Using an external strong LLM (qwen-turbo) for this task is pragmatic: the model being trained (DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B/7B) would likely not have sufficient capability to perform this meta-reasoning reliably, and even if it did, using it for graph construction during data preprocessing would be circular (the model would be constructing graphs for its own outputs, which could introduce systematic biases). The external LLM serves as an **oracle annotator** that produces high-quality graph representations for training data.

**Human evaluation of construction quality:** The paper validates the graph construction through human evaluation on 100 randomly sampled nodes (Appendix C). Nodes are assessed along two dimensions:
- **Node type correctness:** Whether the predicted `progress`/`review` label matches the node's functional role. Per-class results: Review nodes achieve precision 0.9048, recall 0.9661, F1 0.9344. Progress nodes achieve precision 0.8947, recall 0.8500, F1 0.8718. The high recall for review nodes (0.97) indicates that the construction rarely misses reflection behavior—it correctly identifies nearly all review instances, which is critical because the pruning module can only remove nodes it correctly labels as review.
- **Step atomicity:** Whether the node represents a single, semantically independent reasoning step without mixing multiple operations. The atomicity valid rate is 85.29%, meaning the vast majority of nodes are structurally clean and suitable for dependency analysis.

A node is considered fully valid only if it passes both criteria. The high F1 scores and atomicity rate indicate that the constructed graphs are both semantically faithful (type labels match actual function) and structurally well-formed (nodes are atomic reasoning units), providing a reliable foundation for the pruning module.

#### Graph-Based Pruning Criteria

Given a completed DAG `$G = (V, E, \ell)$` with `$n$` chunks processed, the pruning module identifies which review nodes to remove based on two structural redundancy criteria. Both criteria rely on graph-theoretic properties computed from the DAG topology, not on token-level statistics or model confidence scores.

**Preliminary definitions:** Before defining the pruning criteria, the paper establishes several graph-theoretic quantities:

- **Descendant set:** For a node `$v \in V$`, the (directed) descendant set is `$\text{Desc}(v) = \{u \in V \mid v \leadsto u, u \neq v\}$`, meaning all nodes reachable from `$v$` by following directed edges (excluding `$v$` itself). This captures the set of reasoning steps that, directly or transitively, depend on the product of node `$v$`.

- **Descendant count (branch size):** `$B(v) = |\text{Desc}(v)|$` is the number of nodes in the descendant set. This measures how much subsequent reasoning builds on node `$v$`—a high descendant count indicates that `$v$`'s output feeds into a substantial portion of the remaining reasoning, while a low descendant count indicates that few or no later steps depend on `$v$`.

- **Node depth:** The depth `$d(v)$` is the length of a shortest path from the source node (any node with in-degree 0, representing the initial interpretation of the problem) to `$v$`. This measures how far into the reasoning trajectory the node appears, with later verification steps having higher depth.

- **Maximum depth:** `$d_{\max} = d(t)$` where `$t$` is the terminal node (the `final answer` node). This is the total depth of the reasoning graph.

**Why these quantities matter for reflection pruning:** The insight is that a review node's **structural contribution** to the reasoning process is reflected in its position in the DAG. A review node that verifies an intermediate calculation that the subsequent reasoning actually uses will have downstream descendants—later progress nodes will depend on the verified result, creating edges from the review node or from the verified content to those later steps. A review node that performs a drive-by verification that the model then ignores will have few or no descendants—the model checked something, concluded it was fine, and moved on without integrating that verification into any further reasoning. Similarly, a review node that appears after the main solution is complete (at high relative depth) cannot affect the answer because the reasoning structure leading to the answer has already been fully constructed.

**Branch-Level Redundancy (targeting Indiscriminate Reflection):**

**Criterion:** A review node `$v$` is considered branch-level redundant if:

$$B(v) < k$$

where `$k = 2$` is the descendant count threshold. In words: if a review node has fewer than 2 descendants in the DAG, it is pruned.

**What this means operationally:** A review node with `$B(v) = 0$` has no descendants at all—the model performed a verification and then never referenced the verified result in any subsequent reasoning step. This is the purest case of Indiscriminate Reflection: a self-check that contributes nothing to the final answer. A review node with `$B(v) = 1$` has exactly one descendant, forming a narrow chain that terminates quickly without feeding back into the main reasoning. Such nodes represent verification that the model briefly acknowledges ("yes, that checks out") but then immediately moves past without building further reasoning on top of it.

The threshold `$k = 2$` means that a review node must have at least 2 descendants to be considered structurally meaningful. This is a conservative threshold—requiring only 2 descendants means that even modestly useful verification branches survive pruning. In practice, review nodes that genuinely contribute to the reasoning (e.g., verifying a derived equation that is subsequently used in multiple downstream derivations) will typically have many more than 2 descendants, so the threshold mainly eliminates review nodes that lead to dead ends or near-dead-ends.

**Why this targets Indiscriminate Reflection:** Indiscriminate Reflection is characterized by broad, low-impact checks applied throughout reasoning—the model verifies steps even when the verification is trivial (e.g., checking that 2+2=4) or when the verified content is never subsequently used. These checks create review nodes that, in the dependency graph, appear as small, isolated verification branches that do not feed into the main reasoning flow. By pruning review nodes with small descendant counts, the criterion removes these structurally peripheral verifications while preserving review nodes that serve as genuine decision points (which will have larger downstream reasoning trees depending on their outcome).

**Depth-Level Redundancy (targeting Repetitive Reflection):**

**Criterion:** A review node `$v$` is considered depth-level redundant if its relative depth exceeds a threshold `$m$`:

$$\frac{d(v)}{d_{\max}} > m$$

where `$m = 0.9$` is the relative depth threshold. In words: if a review node appears in the last 10% of the reasoning trajectory (by graph depth), it is pruned.

**What this means operationally:** The reasoning graph's maximum depth `$d_{\max}$` represents the length of the shortest path from the initial problem interpretation to the final answer. A review node at relative depth `$> 0.9$` appears after 90% of the reasoning structure has already been established. At this stage, the core solution has been derived and the remaining content is typically final verification, restatement, or backtracking that cannot change the answer because all dependencies leading to the answer node have already been satisfied earlier in the graph.

**Why this targets Repetitive Reflection:** Repetitive Reflection is characterized by repeatedly re-verifying conclusions that have already been validated earlier in the trajectory. In terms of graph structure, these re-verifications appear as review nodes at high depth—they occur after the main reasoning chain has reached the answer, representing a "double-check" or "let me make sure" phase that adds no new information. The model may have already verified the same conclusion at depth 0.3 (a review node there), but at depth 0.95 it performs the same verification again, creating a redundant review node that the depth-level criterion catches.

The threshold `$m = 0.9$` is set conservatively to prune only the tail-end verification that occurs after the answer is effectively determined. If the threshold were lower (e.g., 0.7), it would risk pruning review nodes that are genuinely part of the problem-solving process—many mathematical solutions involve a "check your work" phase that occurs in the final third of the reasoning but before the answer is fully locked in. The chosen threshold targets specifically the post-answer backtracking pattern where the model has already stated the answer and then continues to re-verify.

**Combined pruning and relinearization:** Both criteria are applied to the completed DAG. Any review node that satisfies either `$B(v) < 2$` (branch-level) or `$d(v)/d_{\max} > 0.9$` (depth-level) is marked for removal. Progress nodes are never pruned by these criteria—the pruning is exclusively applied to review-typed nodes. After pruning, the surviving nodes and edges form a subgraph `$\tilde{G}$` which is then **relinearized** back into a CoT text sequence `$\tilde{r}$`. Relinearization follows the topological order of the surviving DAG, producing a coherent linear narrative that preserves the logical dependencies of the original reasoning but with redundant reflection steps removed.

**Why progress nodes are never pruned:** The paper's taxonomy of redundancy is specific to reflection behavior. A progress node, by definition, advances the reasoning frontier—it produces new information that (by the construction criteria) other nodes depend on. Pruning a progress node would break the dependency chain for all its descendants, destroying the logical coherence of the reasoning. The paper's position is that progress nodes, even if they could be made more concise, are never structurally redundant in the way that reflection nodes can be. This is a simplifying assumption that enables clean pruning criteria at the cost of not addressing verbosity within progress steps (e.g., overly detailed algebraic expansions that could be compressed). The authors accept this limitation, focusing the method on the reflection-specific redundancy that they argue is the dominant source of overthinking.

#### Three-Stage Training Pipeline

The training pipeline transforms the base reasoning model through three sequential stages, each building on the output of the previous stage. Algorithm 1 in Appendix F provides pseudocode for the full procedure.

**Stage 1: Cold-Start Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)**

**Purpose:** To instill an explicit inductive bias toward concise, non-redundant reasoning before any reinforcement learning begins. The authors argue this is essential because preference optimization and RL in later stages work best when the policy already has some probability mass on efficient reasoning trajectories—starting from a base model that generates heavily redundant CoT would make the preference signal too weak (almost all samples would be high-redundancy).

**Data preparation:** For each problem `$q$` in the training set (the Light-R1 dataset, Wen et al., 2025a), the system takes the raw CoT annotation `$r$` and its corresponding correct answer `$a$`, constructs the reasoning graph `$G_n$` via the iterative chunk-by-chunk procedure, applies the dual pruning criteria to obtain the pruned graph `$\tilde{G}$`, relinearizes into concise CoT `$\tilde{r}$`, and adds the triplet `$(q, \tilde{r}, a)$` to the SFT dataset `$\mathcal{D}_{\text{SFT}}$`.

**Dataset statistics:** Table 2 reports that pruning reduces the average number of nodes per graph from 27.8 to 15.6 (a 44% reduction), average review nodes from 16.8 to 4.5 (a 73% reduction in reflection nodes specifically), and average tokens from 6468 to 4439 (a 31% reduction). The dataset contains 3335 training examples. The total data synthesis cost (running the external LLM for graph construction) is reported as $20, indicating the approach is computationally cheap relative to the downstream training.

**Training objective:** The SFT stage trains the policy model `$M_\theta$` to generate the combined output sequence `$y = (\tilde{r}, a)$`—the pruned reasoning followed by the final answer—conditioned on the problem `$q$`. The objective is the standard next-token negative log-likelihood:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{SFT}}(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{(q, \tilde{r}, a)} \sum_t \log \pi_\theta(y_t \mid q, y_{<t})$$

where `$\pi_\theta$` is the policy model parameterized by `$\theta$`, `$y_t$` denotes the `$t$`-th token in the target sequence `$y$`, and `$y_{<t}$` denotes the preceding token sequence.

**What this computes:** For each training example, the model predicts the next token at each position in the pruned CoT + answer sequence and incurs a loss proportional to the negative log probability assigned to the actual token. The expectation is over the training dataset—the loss is averaged across all examples and all token positions. The result is a scalar loss value that gradient descent minimizes by adjusting model parameters `$\theta$`.

**Why this form:** Next-token prediction with cross-entropy loss is the standard supervised fine-tuning objective for autoregressive language models. It directly shapes the model's output distribution to match the pruned reasoning traces—after training, the model should assign high probability to generating concise, non-redundant reasoning steps similar to those produced by the graph-based pruning. The objective does not involve any explicit length penalty or redundancy signal; it simply teaches the model to imitate the pruned traces. The inductive bias toward efficiency comes entirely from the **data**, not from the loss function.

**Training hyperparameters** (Appendix D.1, Table 5): 8 epochs, global batch size 64, maximum sequence length 8192 tokens, AdamW optimizer, learning rate `$1 \times 10^{-5}$`, LoRA rank 32 with adapters applied to all attention and linear layers. Training is conducted using the LLaMA-Factory framework with LoRA-based parameter-efficient tuning. The use of LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) means that only a small number of additional parameters are trained while the base model weights remain frozen, making the SFT stage computationally efficient and reducing the risk of catastrophic forgetting of the base model's general reasoning capabilities.

**Why SFT before RL:** The paper argues that starting RL from a base model that already generates reasoning would be inefficient because the model has no examples of what "good" (concise) reasoning looks like. The SFT stage provides a warm start by exposing the model to high-quality pruned traces, giving it an initial policy that generates reasoning with substantially reduced reflection. This makes the subsequent DPO and GRPO stages more effective because:
- The DPO preference pairs have a meaningful contrast (the SFT model's outputs already show some efficiency, so "better" and "worse" trajectories can be distinguished).
- The GRPO rollout distribution already includes some concise trajectories, providing a meaningful signal for the length penalty (if all rollouts were extremely long, the length penalty would have no variation to work with).

---

**Stage 2: Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)**

**Purpose:** To further align the policy toward concise reasoning by explicitly preferring correct trajectories with lower redundancy over correct trajectories with higher redundancy. The SFT model produces concise reasoning but may still occasionally generate redundant behavior—DPO provides a direct preference signal that penalizes such lapses.

**Rollout generation:** For each problem `$x$` sampled from AIME (pre-2024) and AMC (pre-2023) datasets, the SFT model `$M_{\text{SFT}}$` generates multiple trajectory rollouts `$Y = \{y^{(k)}\}$` by sampling from its output distribution `$\pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x)$`.

**Redundancy scoring:** For each trajectory `$y$`, the system computes a redundancy score:

$$R(y) = \frac{|\{v \in V : \ell(v) = \text{review}\}|}{|V|} + \frac{|y|}{|y|_x}$$

where `$|y|$` is the number of generated tokens in the trajectory, `$|y|_x$` is the mean length of all sampled trajectories for the same question `$x$`, and `$|\{v \in V : \ell(v) = \text{review}\}| / |V|$` is the fraction of nodes in the trajectory's graph that are review-typed.

**What this computes:** The redundancy score has two components. The first term measures the **proportion of reflection content** in the reasoning graph—if a trajectory's graph consists mostly of review nodes, this term will be high. The second term measures the **relative length** of the trajectory compared to other rollouts for the same question—trajectories that are longer than average for that problem will have a ratio `$|y| / |y|_x > 1$`, contributing positively to the redundancy score. The sum of these two terms captures both structural redundancy (lots of reflection relative to progress) and length-based redundancy (verbose even by the standards of that problem).

**Why this form:** Combining the reflection proportion and relative length ensures that the redundancy score captures both dimensions of inefficiency. A trajectory might be long but structurally efficient (lots of progress nodes with low reflection proportion)—it would have a low first term but high second term. Another trajectory might be short but structurally redundant (few tokens but mostly reflection)—it would have a high first term but low second term. By summing both terms, the score identifies trajectories that are redundant by either criterion, which is appropriate because both contribute to unnecessary inference cost. The normalization by mean length for the same question `$|y|_x$` is crucial—it makes the score **question-relative**, so a trajectory that is long because the problem inherently requires long reasoning is not unfairly penalized compared to a trajectory that is long due to unnecessary verbosity on a simple problem.

**Preference pair construction:** Among trajectories that yield **correct** final answers (verified against ground truth), the system constructs preference pairs `$(y^+, y^-)$` where `$y^+$` has lower redundancy than `$y^-$`. Only correct trajectories are considered for both the preferred and dispreferred sides—the DPO objective is to prefer concise *correct* reasoning over verbose *correct* reasoning, not to prefer correctness itself (which is handled by the GRPO stage). This design choice ensures that DPO shapes efficiency while preserving accuracy, rather than trading one off against the other.

**DPO loss:** The policy model is trained using the standard DPO objective:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y^+, y^-)} \log \sigma\left(\beta \left[ \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y^+ \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y^+ \mid x)} - \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y^- \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y^- \mid x)} \right]\right)$$

where `$\pi_\theta$` is the current policy being optimized, `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$` is a fixed reference policy (in this case, the SFT model from Stage 1), `$\beta > 0$` controls the sharpness of the preference (how strongly the model is pushed toward the preferred trajectory), and `$\sigma(\cdot)$` is the logistic sigmoid function.

**What this computes:** The DPO loss compares the policy's relative log-probability of the preferred trajectory `$y^+$` versus the dispreferred trajectory `$y^-$`, both normalized by the reference policy's log-probabilities. The difference inside the brackets is large and positive when the policy assigns much higher probability to `$y^+$` than to `$y^-$` (relative to the reference policy). The sigmoid `$\sigma(\cdot)$` squashes this difference to `$(0, 1)$`, and the negative log makes the loss small when the sigmoid output is close to 1 (policy strongly prefers `$y^+$`) and large when it is close to 0 (policy does not distinguish or prefers `$y^-$`). The result is a scalar loss that gradient descent minimizes by adjusting `$\theta$` to increase the relative probability of concise correct trajectories over verbose correct trajectories.

**Why this form:** DPO directly optimizes the policy to match human (or oracle) preferences without requiring an explicit reward model or reinforcement learning loop. It works by reparameterizing the RLHF objective in terms of the policy and reference policy, making it simpler and more stable than PPO-based approaches. The reference policy `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$` (the SFT model) serves as an anchor that prevents the policy from diverging too far from reasonable behavior—the DPO loss only cares about the *relative* preference between `$y^+$` and `$y^-$`, and if the policy deviates far from the reference in ways that don't affect preference, the KL penalty implicit in the DPO derivation keeps it in check.

**Training hyperparameters** (Appendix D.2, Table 6): 5 epochs, global batch size 64, maximum sequence length 8192 tokens, AdamW optimizer, learning rate `$1 \times 10^{-7}$`. The very low learning rate compared to SFT (`$10^{-7}$` vs. `$10^{-5}$`) reflects that DPO is fine-tuning an already-capable policy—large updates would risk destabilizing the model or causing it to forget the base reasoning capabilities learned during SFT and pretraining.

**Stage 3: GRPO with Length Penalty**

**Purpose:** To jointly optimize answer correctness and reasoning efficiency through reinforcement learning, using final-answer correctness as the primary reward signal and a length penalty term to discourage unnecessarily long reasoning. This stage further compresses redundant steps and improves robustness on complex tasks.

**Reward design:** For a given problem `$x$` and generated trajectory `$y$`, the reward is computed in two steps:

First, a correctness reward is assigned:

$$V(x, y) \in \{0, 1\}$$

where `$V = 1$` if the final answer extracted from `$y$` matches the ground-truth answer (verified using math-verify), and `$V = 0$` otherwise. This is a sparse, outcome-based reward that only depends on the final answer, not on intermediate reasoning quality.

Second, a length regularization term is applied **only to correct trajectories**:

$$R(x, y) = V(x, y) - \lambda \cdot \mathbb{1}_{V(x, y) = 1} \cdot R_{\text{length}}(x, y)$$

where `$\lambda$` controls the strength of the length penalty and `$\mathbb{1}_{V(x, y) = 1}$` is an indicator that is 1 for correct trajectories and 0 for incorrect ones.

**What this structure achieves:** Incorrect trajectories receive reward 0 regardless of their length—the length penalty is not applied to them because the priority is to first learn to produce correct answers. Once a trajectory is correct, the length penalty subtracts from the reward based on how much longer it is than the shortest correct trajectory. This creates a natural curriculum: the model first learns to solve problems correctly (motivated by the positive correctness reward), and then among correct solutions, it is pushed toward conciseness (motivated by minimizing the length penalty). The indicator function prevents the model from gaming the reward by producing extremely short but incorrect answers—an incorrect short answer still gets 0 reward, so there's no incentive to sacrifice correctness for brevity.

**Length penalty definition:** The length penalty is based on the normalized excess length relative to the shortest correct trajectory in the rollout group. First, define:

$$L(y) = \text{number of reasoning tokens in } y$$

Then, among all trajectories sampled for problem `$x$` in the current rollout batch, identify the shortest **correct** trajectory length:

$$L^\star(x) = \min_{y: V(x, y) = 1} L(y)$$

The normalized excess length is:

$$\delta(x, y) = \frac{[L(y) - L^\star(x) - \Delta]_+}{L^\star(x) + \Delta}$$

where `$[z]_+ = \max(z, 0)$` is the ReLU function (clipping negative values to zero), and `$\Delta$` is a small tolerance margin.

Finally, the length regularizer is:

$$R_{\text{length}}(x, y) = \delta(x, y)^\gamma$$

where `$\gamma \geq 1$` controls the sharpness of the penalty.

**What this computes step by step:**

1. `$L(y) - L^\star(x)$` measures how many more tokens trajectory `$y$` uses compared to the shortest correct trajectory for the same problem. If `$y$` is the shortest correct trajectory itself, this difference is zero.

2. The tolerance margin `$\Delta$` allows a small amount of excess without penalty—if `$L(y) - L^\star(x) \leq \Delta$`, then `$[L(y) - L^\star(x) - \Delta]_+ = 0$` and no penalty is applied. This prevents the model from being penalized for minor length variations that are within normal sampling noise.

3. The normalization by `$L^\star(x) + \Delta$` makes the penalty **relative to problem difficulty**. A 100-token excess on a problem whose shortest correct solution is 5000 tokens is a 2% excess—minor. A 100-token excess on a problem whose shortest correct solution is 200 tokens is a 50% excess—substantial. The normalized excess `$\delta(x, y)$` captures this relativity.

4. Raising to the power `$\gamma$` controls the penalty shape. With `$\gamma = 1$`, the penalty is linear in excess length—doubling the excess doubles the penalty. With `$\gamma > 1$`, the penalty is convex—small excesses incur mild penalties but large excesses are heavily penalized. The paper does not specify the exact value of `$\gamma$` used in experiments (it states `$\gamma \geq 1$` but the specific value is not reported in the main text or appendix). Setting `$\gamma > 1$` would strongly discourage very long trajectories while being relatively lenient on moderate-length ones, which aligns with the goal of eliminating severe overthinking while tolerating reasonable variation.

**Why this form:** The normalized excess length formulation has several desirable properties over simpler alternatives such as a flat per-token penalty:
- **Question-adaptive:** Problems that inherently require long reasoning (e.g., complex AIME problems) have large `$L^\star(x)$`, so the same absolute excess produces a smaller normalized penalty. This prevents the model from being unfairly penalized for generating long reasoning on inherently difficult problems.
- **Self-normalizing within rollouts:** `$L^\star(x)$` is computed from the rollout batch itself, not from an external reference. This means the penalty adapts to the model's current capability—as the model improves and the shortest correct trajectory becomes shorter, the baseline tightens and the model must become even more concise to avoid penalty.
- **Tolerance margin prevents pathological optimization:** Without `$\Delta$`, the model would be incentivized to exactly match `$L^\star(x)$` on every correct trajectory, which could lead to brittle, minimally-sufficient reasoning that fails under slight distribution shift. The tolerance margin creates a "good enough" region where further length reduction yields no additional reward, discouraging the model from cutting corners dangerously close to incorrectness.
- **GRPO compatibility:** The Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) algorithm compares rewards within groups of rollouts for the same prompt. By computing `$L^\star(x)$` per problem within the rollout group, the length penalty is naturally integrated into GRPO's group-relative reward normalization.

**GRPO training dynamics:** GRPO is a variant of policy gradient that normalizes advantages within each group of rollouts. For a batch of problems, the model generates multiple rollouts per problem, computes rewards using the formula above, normalizes rewards within each problem's rollout group to compute advantages, and updates the policy to increase the probability of high-advantage trajectories and decrease the probability of low-advantage trajectories. The KL penalty coefficient `$\beta = 1 \times 10^{-3}$` controls how far the policy can diverge from the reference policy (the DPO model from Stage 2).

**Training hyperparameters** (Appendix D.3, Table 7): KL coefficient `$\beta = 1 \times 10^{-3}$`, 8 rollouts per input, sampling temperature 1.0, maximum response length 12000 tokens, global batch size 64, AdamW optimizer, learning rate `$1 \times 10^{-6}$`, 220 training steps. Training is conducted using the verl framework on the dapo-17k dataset (Yu et al., 2025) with verifiable rewards. Experiments run on a single compute node with 4× NVIDIA A800 GPUs.

**RL training dynamics:** Appendix E, Figure 5 shows the evolution of mean reward and average response length across training steps for both 7B and 1.5B models, with exponential moving average smoothing. The mean reward increases despite fluctuations (typical for policy optimization with sparse rewards), while the response length does not grow monotonically with reward—indicating that the model learns to achieve higher rewards through better reasoning strategies rather than simply generating longer outputs.

#### Design Choices Summary

The paper makes several deliberate design choices that collectively define the approach:

- **Graph-based structural modeling over surface-level statistics:** The core methodological choice is to model reasoning as a typed DAG rather than analyzing token-level features (attention weights, perplexity, semantic similarity). This enables pruning criteria that are principled (based on graph-theoretic properties of dependency and reachability) rather than heuristic. The validation experiment in Table 3 provides the empirical justification: graph-pruned traces preserve 93.70% accuracy while length-matched naive truncation achieves only 73.60%, confirming that graph structure captures reasoning coherence that surface-level statistics miss.

- **Progress/review binary typing over continuous importance:** Nodes are classified into exactly two types rather than assigned continuous importance scores. This choice simplifies the pruning module (binary decisions: prune review nodes satisfying structural criteria, never prune progress nodes) and aligns with the paper's taxonomy of redundancy as a specifically reflective phenomenon. The trade-off is that the typing is coarse—fine-grained variations in review importance (some verifications are more valuable than others) are collapsed into a binary, and the pruning criteria are binary thresholds (`$k=2$`, `$m=0.9$`) rather than graded importance scores.

- **External LLM for graph construction over self-annotation:** Using an external strong model (qwen-turbo) for graph construction avoids potential biases from self-annotation (where the model being trained would construct graphs for its own outputs) and ensures high-quality annotations ($20 total cost for 3335 examples). The limitation is that the method depends on access to a stronger teacher model than the model being trained, and the graph construction quality is bounded by the external LLM's capability.

- **Three-stage training over end-to-end optimization:** The SFT → DPO → GRPO pipeline incrementally shapes the model's behavior: SFT provides the initial inductive bias toward concise reasoning via imitation, DPO refines the preference toward efficient trajectories while maintaining correctness, and GRPO jointly optimizes correctness and length with an explicit length penalty. This staged approach is more stable than attempting to train directly from the base model with a length-penalized reward, which would likely fail because the base model produces no concise trajectories and the length penalty would have no effective signal. Each stage builds on the previous stage's improved policy, creating a curriculum that gradually tightens the efficiency constraint.

- **Question-relative length penalty over absolute length targets:** The length penalty in GRPO uses `$L^\star(x)$` computed per-problem from the rollout group, making it adaptive to problem difficulty. An absolute length budget (e.g., "generate no more than 4000 tokens") would penalize long but necessary reasoning on hard problems and be too lenient on easy problems. The relative formulation ensures that the model is pushed toward the frontier of what is possible for each specific problem, not toward a one-size-fits-all length target.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Reasoning Redundancy Is Primarily Structural, Not Token-Level

The paper's most fundamental conceptual contribution is reframing the overthinking problem from a surface-level phenomenon (tokens that look unimportant by statistical metrics) to a **structural phenomenon** (reasoning steps that occupy specific, identifiable positions in a dependency graph). Prior work on efficient reasoning operated almost exclusively through token-level heuristics—attention scores (Choi et al., 2025; Singh et al., 2025), perplexity/entropy thresholds (Cui et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025a), token importance estimation (Xia et al., 2025), or global length budgets (Han et al., 2025; Ma et al., 2025b). These approaches share an implicit assumption: redundancy manifests as particular tokens or spans that are, by some computable metric, "less important" than others. Removing them is therefore a filtering problem—score each token, set a threshold, and discard those below it.

This paper argues that this assumption is fundamentally wrong for reasoning models. The problem is not that some tokens in a reflection are superfluous while others in the same reflection are essential. The problem is that **entire reflection steps are structurally disconnected from the reasoning that produces the answer**. A review node that verifies an intermediate calculation but produces no descendants in the dependency graph is not "partially redundant"—it is entirely redundant as a structural unit, regardless of the individual token importance scores within it. Conversely, a dense, high-entropy derivation step may have low per-token "importance" by attention-based metrics while being structurally essential because the entire downstream reasoning depends on its product.

The key diagnostic move is converting a **statistical filtering problem** into a **graph reachability problem**. Instead of asking "how important is each token?", the paper asks "does this reasoning step produce anything that subsequent steps depend on?" This transforms the pruning criterion from a continuous importance threshold (which requires careful calibration and generalizes poorly across tasks and models) to a discrete structural check (descendant count < k, relative depth > m). The structural criteria are interpretable, parameterizable with clear semantic meaning (k = 2 means "must feed into at least two downstream steps"), and crucially, they explicitly exclude progress nodes from pruning—something no token-level method can guarantee because token importance scores are agnostic to the semantic type of the reasoning step they belong to.

The empirical validation in Table 3 is the paper's strongest evidence for this reframing. The Graph-Pruned condition (structural pruning) preserves 93.70% accuracy and 90.69% consistency, while the Len-Trunc condition (matching the token length of pruned traces by naively truncating from the beginning) drops to 73.60% accuracy and 69.10% consistency. Both conditions remove similar numbers of tokens—the difference is **which** tokens are removed. The Len-Trunc condition destroys accuracy because it removes tokens without understanding their structural role, breaking essential dependency chains. The Graph-Pruned condition maintains accuracy because the graph structure identifies exactly which reasoning units are disconnected from the final answer, leaving the dependency chain intact. This result demonstrates that the dependency structure, not the token count, is what matters for preserving reasoning coherence—a finding that challenges the premise of budget-based and token-importance-based compression methods.

This is a **fundamental conceptual reframing** rather than an incremental refinement. It changes the question from "which tokens can we afford to lose?" to "which reasoning operations are structurally unnecessary?", and the methodology flows directly from this new question. The implication is that future work on reasoning efficiency should prioritize recovering the logical structure of CoT before attempting compression, rather than designing more sophisticated token-level importance estimators.

### Innovation 2: A Taxonomy of Redundant Reflection with Distinct Structural Signatures

Prior work on overthinking recognized that reasoning models engage in excessive self-checking and verification (Chen et al., 2025a; Sui et al., 2025), but treated this as a monolithic phenomenon—"the model reflects too much." The paper's second key contribution is decomposing redundant reflection into **two behaviorally and structurally distinct failure modes**, each with its own graph-theoretic signature that maps to a specific pruning criterion:

- **Indiscriminate Reflection** is the pattern of applying broad, low-impact verification throughout reasoning, including to trivial steps where verification is unnecessary and contributes nothing to subsequent reasoning. Its structural signature is **branch-level redundancy**: review nodes with small descendant counts (B(v) < k), indicating that the verification did not feed into any meaningful downstream reasoning. The pruning criterion (branch-level pruning) exploits this signature by removing review nodes that initiate only narrow side-branches.

- **Repetitive Reflection** is the pattern of re-verifying already-established conclusions in the later stages of the reasoning trajectory, after the core solution has been derived. Its structural signature is **depth-level redundancy**: review nodes appearing at high relative depth (d(v)/d_max > m), indicating post-answer backtracking that cannot affect the result because the dependency chain to the answer is already complete. The pruning criterion (depth-level pruning) exploits this signature by removing review nodes in the final portion of the graph.

This taxonomy is significant not merely as a classification exercise but because it establishes that **different types of redundancy require different structural detection mechanisms**. A single "is this step important?" criterion would fail to distinguish between the two: an Indiscriminate Reflection step that performs a trivial check might score moderately on some importance metric (the model does attend to the step it's checking), while a Repetitive Reflection step that re-verifies a critical conclusion might score highly on importance (the conclusion is genuinely important—it just doesn't need to be verified a third time). The graph-theoretic criteria succeed precisely because they target the **structural context** of reflection rather than the content of what is being reflected upon:

- Branch-level pruning asks: "Did this verification lead anywhere?"—a question about the verification's consequences, not its content.
- Depth-level pruning asks: "Is this verification happening after the answer is already determined?"—a question about the verification's position in the reasoning trajectory, not its content.

This dual-criterion approach also provides interpretability that a single continuous importance score cannot match. When a review node is pruned, the reason is explicit: it was either a dead-end branch (B(v) = 0 or 1) or a late-stage re-verification (d(v)/d_max > 0.9). This interpretability makes the method debuggable—if pruning removes too aggressively on certain problem types, the thresholds k and m can be adjusted with clear semantic justification, rather than retuning an opaque importance threshold.

The taxonomy represents a **fundamental diagnostic advance** rather than an incremental classification. It provides the field with a vocabulary and structural diagnostics for reasoning redundancy that did not exist before, enabling future work to analyze *which type of redundancy* a model exhibits and design targeted interventions accordingly. The quantitative evidence in Table 2 supports the prevalence of both types: the pruning reduces average review nodes from 16.8 to 4.5 (a 73% reduction) and average nodes from 27.8 to 15.6 (a 44% reduction), indicating that nearly half of the original reasoning graph consisted of structurally redundant reflection. The 73% reduction in review nodes specifically shows that most of the review behavior in the base model falls into one of the two redundancy categories—only 4.5 out of 16.8 review nodes on average survive pruning, meaning the base model's reflection is overwhelmingly structural noise.

### Innovation 3: Reasoning Dependency Structure Can Be Recovered Post-Hoc Through Iterative Graph Construction

The paper demonstrates a practical method for extracting the logical dependency structure of chain-of-thought reasoning **after generation**, using an external LLM as a graph construction oracle. This is significant because it sidesteps a fundamental tension in reasoning research: autoregressive language models generate reasoning left-to-right as a linear sequence, but mathematical reasoning is inherently structured as a dependency graph (with sub-goals, branching exploration, backtracking, and cross-step references). Prior work confronted this tension in one of two ways, both unsatisfying:

- **Ignore the structure** and operate directly on the linear text. This is what token-level compression and length budget methods do—they compress the surface form without recovering the underlying organization. The paper's Table 3 result shows the cost of this approach: removing tokens without understanding the dependency structure destroys coherence.

- **Force the model to generate structured output** during inference, such as requiring explicit step markers, structured reasoning formats, or programmatic reasoning traces. This constrains the generation process but risks reducing the model's flexibility and requires modifying the training objective—the model must learn to produce structured output, not just correct answers. It also cannot be applied retrospectively to existing reasoning traces.

The iterative graph construction method offers a third path: **recover structure from unstructured output**. By prompting an external LLM to incrementally build a DAG from the linear CoT, the method reverse-engineers the dependency relations that were implicit in the model's generation without requiring the model to have generated structured output in the first place. This is possible because the iterative prompt provides the graph constructor with the same sequential information that the original model had when generating each chunk—the graph state at step i represents the accumulated reasoning state at that point in the trajectory, and the constructor can identify dependencies by matching references in the current chunk to nodes in the partial graph.

What makes this approach distinctive is not the use of an external LLM (which is a pragmatic implementation choice) but the **iterative, stateful construction process** that mirrors the original generation order. The graph is not built by processing the entire CoT at once and inferring all dependencies simultaneously—that would be a much harder reasoning task for the external LLM, requiring it to resolve all cross-references in a single pass. Instead, the graph grows incrementally as each chunk is processed, with each new node connected to existing nodes based on immediate context. This design choice makes the construction task tractable for the external LLM and produces graphs that respect the **temporal unfolding** of the reasoning—the graph topology reflects not only logical dependencies but also the order in which reasoning steps were taken, which matters for identifying patterns like Repetitive Reflection (which is defined partly by when a step occurs relative to the answer).

The human evaluation results (Table 4) validate that this construction process is reliable: review node identification achieves F1 0.9344, progress node identification achieves F1 0.8718, and step atomicity is valid 85.29% of the time. The slightly lower recall for progress nodes (0.85 vs. 0.97 for review) suggests the constructor is more conservative about labeling nodes as progress (it may label some progress-like content as review when uncertain), but the high review recall means that the pruning module—which only operates on review nodes—has a very low miss rate. The $20 total cost for 3335 examples makes the approach computationally practical for dataset-scale preprocessing.

This contribution is **methodologically significant** rather than fundamental: the core idea of representing reasoning as a graph is not new (prior work has used tree search, graph-of-thought, and structured reasoning formats), but the specific method of iterative post-hoc graph construction with typed nodes is novel and provides a practical pipeline for transforming unstructured CoT into structured representations suitable for pruning. It opens the door to applying graph-theoretic analysis to any existing reasoning dataset, without requiring models to have been trained with structured output constraints.

### Innovation 4: Length Optimization as a Relative, Question-Adaptive Signal Rather Than an Absolute Constraint

The paper's GRPO length penalty design represents a conceptual advance in how length regularization is formulated for reasoning models. The dominant paradigm in prior work on length-controlled reasoning falls into two categories: **absolute length constraints** (e.g., "generate no more than N tokens," as in budget-aware methods like Han et al., 2025 and Ma et al., 2025b) and **flat per-token penalties** (e.g., subtract a small constant from the reward for each token generated, as in some RL-based approaches like Arora and Zanette, 2025). Both approaches share a common limitation: they apply a uniform length standard across all problems, penalizing long reasoning equally whether the problem is inherently simple (where 500 tokens is excessive) or inherently complex (where 5000 tokens is necessary).

The paper's length penalty formula `R_length(x, y) = δ(x, y)^γ` with `δ(x, y) = [L(y) - L*(x) - Δ]+ / (L*(x) + Δ)` introduces three conceptual shifts:

**First, question-adaptive baselining.** The penalty is computed relative to `L*(x)`, the shortest correct trajectory for that specific problem in the current rollout group. This means the model is not penalized for generating the *absolute* number of tokens, but for generating *more tokens than the current frontier of efficiency for that problem*. A 3000-token solution on an AIME problem where the shortest correct solution is 2800 tokens incurs a small penalty (7% excess). A 3000-token solution on a MATH500 problem where the shortest correct solution is 500 tokens incurs a large penalty (500% excess). This relativity ensures that the model is never forced to choose between solving a hard problem (which requires long reasoning) and avoiding length penalty—it can solve hard problems with long reasoning and incur only the excess relative to what is achievable for that problem.

This is fundamentally different from a flat per-token penalty, which creates a constant tension between completeness and conciseness that must be carefully balanced with the λ hyperparameter. With flat penalties, either λ is small (in which case the model ignores length on easy problems) or λ is large (in which case the model avoids long reasoning even on hard problems where it's necessary). The question-adaptive penalty resolves this tension by making the effective penalty per token a function of problem difficulty.

**Second, self-improving efficiency frontier.** Because `L*(x)` is computed from the rollout group generated by the current policy, the baseline for efficiency is always the model's own best performance. As training progresses and the model learns to solve problems more concisely, `L*(x)` decreases, which tightens the penalty for all correct trajectories. This creates a natural curriculum: early in training, when the model's solutions are long, `L*(x)` is relatively large and the penalty is lenient; as the model improves, `L*(x)` shrinks and the penalty becomes stricter. The model is constantly chasing its own efficiency frontier, preventing it from settling into a local optimum where it produces correct-but-verbose solutions.

**Third, tolerance margin for robustness.** The Δ parameter creates a "grace region" around the shortest correct trajectory where no penalty is applied. This is a crucial anti-fragility mechanism. Without Δ, the model would be incentivized to produce the absolute shortest possible correct solution for each problem, which could lead to brittle, minimally-sufficient reasoning that fails on edge cases or under distribution shift. The tolerance margin says: "as long as you're within Δ tokens of the current best, you're good enough." This prevents the pathological optimization where the model shaves off essential verification steps to get a slightly shorter trajectory, because those steps would need to be removed entirely (reducing L(y) by many tokens) to cross into a lower penalty region—and removing them entirely would likely make the answer incorrect, dropping the correctness reward to 0. The Δ margin thus creates a stable equilibrium where the model produces reasonably concise but robust reasoning.

The experimental evidence for the effectiveness of this formulation is indirect but compelling: Figure 1 shows that the method achieves the best accuracy-efficiency trade-off among all compared methods (pushing furthest toward the upper-left of the accuracy-vs-tokens Pareto frontier), and the stage-wise ablation (Figure 3) shows that GRPO with length penalty provides additional length reduction beyond SFT and DPO while maintaining or improving accuracy—precisely what a well-designed length penalty should achieve. The RL training dynamics (Appendix E, Figure 5) show that reward increases while response length remains stable or decreases slightly, indicating the penalty is successfully discouraging length inflation that often accompanies reward optimization in unregularized RL.

This contribution is **conceptually incremental but practically significant**. The idea of length regularization in RL is not new, and question-adaptive penalties have been explored in other contexts. However, the specific formulation of a relative penalty with tolerance margin, computed from the rollout group's own efficiency frontier, is a carefully engineered solution to a specific set of failure modes (uniform penalty tension, pathological shortening, lack of adaptive curriculum) that prior length regularization methods exhibit. Its significance lies in demonstrating that these design details matter substantially for reasoning models, where the relationship between length and correctness is highly problem-dependent.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The paper evaluates on five mathematical reasoning benchmarks spanning different difficulty levels: AIME24 and AIME25 (competition-level American Invitational Mathematics Examinations, from 2024 and 2025 respectively), AMC23 (moderately challenging American Mathematics Competitions problems from 2023), MATH500 (a curated 500-problem subset of the MATH benchmark covering algebra, number theory, geometry, and probability; Hendrycks et al., 2021), and OlympiadBench (advanced mathematical and scientific reasoning with bilingual Olympiad-level problems; He et al., 2024). For all datasets, 10 solutions are sampled per problem.

- **Base model(s).** All experiments use DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen models at two scales: 1.5B and 7B parameters (DeepSeek-AI et al., 2025). These are distilled reasoning models that already exhibit long chain-of-thought generation with embedded self-checking and verification—precisely the source of the redundant reflection behavior the method targets. The 7B model serves as the primary evaluation scale since it produces substantial reasoning traces (8134 tokens on average) and has headroom for improvement; the 1.5B model demonstrates that the approach scales down to smaller models (7442 average tokens, reduced by 36%).

- **Metrics.** Two primary metrics are reported: **Accuracy (%)** and **Average Reasoning Length (tokens)**. Accuracy is computed as the average correctness across 10 sampled solutions per problem, with answers verified using math-verify (a rule-based answer checking tool for mathematical expressions). Average reasoning length is the mean number of generated tokens across all samples, measuring the inference cost of the reasoning phase (excluding the final answer). The paper reports these metrics per-benchmark and as an average across all five benchmarks. The relationship between these two metrics is the key trade-off: the goal is to maintain or improve accuracy while substantially reducing length.

- **Baselines.** The paper compares against two categories of methods. **Efficiency-oriented training methods** include: (1) O1-Pruner (Luo et al., 2025b), a length-harmonizing fine-tuning approach that reduces long CoT traces while preserving performance; (2) TokenSkip (Xia et al., 2025), a controllable CoT compression method that estimates token-level importance and removes low-utility tokens; (3) EfficientReasoning (Arora and Zanette, 2025), an RL-based objective that favors correct yet concise reasoning; and (4) AdaptThink (Zhang et al., 2025), an RL method that trains models to adaptively decide whether to generate explicit reasoning or respond directly based on input difficulty. Additionally, **open-source R1-style reasoning models** include Skywork-OR1-7B (He et al., 2025), OREAL-7B (Lyu et al., 2025), AReaL-boba-RL-7B (Mei et al., 2025), and Light-R1-DS-7B (Wen et al., 2025a). The base (unmodified) DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen models serve as the reference point for both accuracy and length. Note that O1-Pruner and TokenSkip were adapted by the authors to the DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen models (indicated by `*` in Table 1), since their original implementations were designed for different base models.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper does not use a formal FLOPs-based compute budget for fair comparison between methods. Instead, efficiency is compared directly through the reported average reasoning token counts—methods that achieve similar or better accuracy with fewer tokens are considered more efficient. This is a practical metric (tokens directly translate to inference cost and latency) but does not account for differences in training cost across methods. All methods in Table 1 are compared at inference time; the training pipelines differ substantially but are not part of the reported efficiency comparison.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not apply cross-validation for the main benchmark results—Table 1 reports direct evaluation on the test sets of each benchmark, with 10 samples per problem. The cross-validation mentioned in the technical approach (Section 3) applies to the training pipeline (SFT data preparation from the Light-R1 dataset, DPO rollout generation from AIME/AMC pre-2023/2024 splits) but not to the final evaluation. For the pruning-preservation experiment (Table 3), 1000 examples are randomly sampled from the training set with N=8 generation passes per question to assess robustness.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Headline Accuracy-Efficiency Trade-off Across All Benchmarks

The paper's central result is presented in Table 1. On DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B, the proposed method achieves the highest average accuracy (60.95%) while producing the shortest average reasoning length (4660 tokens) among all compared methods. This represents a 42.7% reduction from the base model's 8134 tokens and a 1.23 percentage point accuracy improvement from the base model's 59.72%. The method dominates the accuracy-efficiency Pareto frontier visualized in Figure 1: it sits in the upper-left region (higher accuracy, lower tokens) relative to all baselines.

Compared against the strongest-efficiency baselines at the 7B scale:
- **EfficientReasoning**: 59.36% accuracy at 5515 tokens. The proposed method achieves +1.59 percentage points higher accuracy with 855 fewer tokens (15.5% less).
- **AdaptThink**: 60.15% accuracy at 5972 tokens. The proposed method achieves +0.80 percentage points higher accuracy with 1312 fewer tokens (22.0% less).
- **Light-R1-DS-7B**: 58.25% accuracy at 7787 tokens. The proposed method achieves +2.70 percentage points higher accuracy with 3127 fewer tokens (40.2% less).

#### Per-Benchmark Performance at the 7B Scale

The paper reports results per benchmark in Table 1, revealing that the gains are not uniform across difficulty levels:

**Hardest benchmarks (AIME24, AIME25):** On AIME24, the proposed method achieves 42.08% accuracy at 7244 tokens, compared to the base model's 42.67% at 12723 tokens—a slight accuracy decrease of 0.59 percentage points but with a 43.1% length reduction. Compared to EfficientReasoning (43.75% at 9568 tokens), the proposed method is 1.67 points lower in accuracy but uses 2324 fewer tokens (24.3% less). On AIME25, the proposed method achieves 31.67% at 6977 tokens, improving over the base model's 29.00% at 12779 tokens (+2.67 points accuracy, 45.4% length reduction) and over EfficientReasoning's 30.83% at 9293 tokens (+0.84 points, 2316 fewer tokens).

**Moderate benchmarks (AMC23, OlympiadBench):** On AMC23, the proposed method achieves 82.34% at 3211 tokens, improving over the base model's 81.00% at 6849 tokens (+1.34 points, 53.1% length reduction). On OlympiadBench, the proposed method achieves 59.85% at 3786 tokens, improving over the base model's 56.77% at 5252 tokens (+3.08 points, 27.9% length reduction). These are the strongest accuracy gains, suggesting that the method is most effective at recovering accuracy on moderate-complexity problems where the base model's reasoning is partially correct but burdened by excessive reflection.

**Easiest benchmark (MATH500):** On MATH500, the proposed method achieves 88.80% at 2080 tokens, essentially matching the base model's 89.16% at 3065 tokens (-0.36 points, 32.1% length reduction). This near-parity on easier problems with substantial length reduction is consistent with the paper's claim that redundancy is most removable when the underlying reasoning is already correct and reflection is largely unnecessary.

#### Per-Benchmark Performance at the 1.5B Scale

To demonstrate scalability, the paper reports results on DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B in the upper half of Table 1. The proposed method improves average accuracy from 46.68% to 49.91% (+3.23 points) while reducing average length from 7442 to 4762 tokens (-36.0%). This is a larger relative accuracy gain than at the 7B scale, suggesting that smaller models benefit more from the removal of redundant reflection—possibly because their base reasoning is less reliable, making unnecessary reflection both more prevalent and more damaging (incorrect verification can overwrite correct intermediate results).

The strongest improvements at 1.5B are on AMC23 (63.12% → 69.38%, +6.26 points) and MATH500 (72.65% → 80.40%, +7.75 points). These large gains on benchmarks where the 1.5B base model has substantial room for improvement indicate that the method's graph-based pruning successfully removes reflection that was actively harmful (not just wasted computation) in the smaller model's reasoning.

Compared to baselines at 1.5B: the proposed method's 49.91% accuracy at 4762 tokens substantially outperforms EfficientReasoning (48.78% at 5574 tokens), AdaptThink (46.09% at 2806 tokens—lower accuracy despite even shorter length), and TokenSkip (41.56% at 8110 tokens). The method also achieves competitive accuracy with open-source R1-style models (Skywork-OR1-7B: 54.52% at 7958 tokens; Light-R1-DS-7B: 58.25% at 7787 tokens) while using substantially fewer tokens, though these are 7B-scale models and the comparison is not scale-matched.

#### Accuracy-Efficiency Frontier Visualization

Figure 1 plots the relationship between average reasoning tokens (x-axis) and accuracy (y-axis) averaged across all five benchmarks for each method. The proposed method ("Ours") appears in the upper-left region: approximately 60% accuracy at approximately 4800 tokens for the 7B variant. The closest competitors in the high-accuracy region are AdaptThink (~5960 tokens, ~60% accuracy) and EfficientReasoning (~5500 tokens, ~59.4% accuracy). The proposed method achieves both higher accuracy and lower token count than both, with the gap in the token dimension being more pronounced (roughly 700-1200 fewer tokens at similar accuracy levels). The Pareto frontier drawn by the set of methods shows a trade-off where longer reasoning generally correlates with higher accuracy, but the proposed method shifts the frontier outward—achieving accuracy previously requiring 6000+ tokens at under 5000 tokens.

#### Stage-Wise Ablation: Contribution of Each Training Component

Figure 3 presents a stage-wise ablation that disentangles the contributions of SFT, DPO, and GRPO by cumulatively adding each training stage to the base model. The results are reported as both accuracy (Figure 3a) and relative reasoning length (Figure 3b, normalized to Base=1.0):

**Base → SFT**: Adding SFT on pruned traces reduces token ratio from 1.0 to approximately 0.55-0.65 across benchmarks—a 35-45% length reduction from cold-start fine-tuning alone. Accuracy changes are benchmark-dependent: AIME24 and AIME25 show slight accuracy decreases (the SFT model is slightly less accurate than the base model on the hardest problems), while AMC23, OlympiadBench, and MATH500 show accuracy improvements. This pattern suggests that SFT on pruned traces effectively teaches concise reasoning but may lose some of the base model's verification behavior that was genuinely useful on the hardest problems.

**SFT → +DPO**: Adding DPO further reduces token ratio by roughly 0.05-0.10 (an additional 5-10% length reduction beyond SFT) while recovering or improving accuracy across most benchmarks. On AIME24 and AIME25, the DPO stage restores accuracy to near or above the base model level while maintaining the SFT stage's length reduction. This is consistent with DPO's design: by preferring correct concise trajectories over correct verbose ones, it teaches the model to preserve correctness while further compressing.

**DPO → +GRPO**: Adding GRPO with length penalty provides the final efficiency push. Token ratio drops further (to approximately 0.45-0.55 of the base model across benchmarks) while accuracy stabilizes or improves slightly. On MATH500, the final model's token ratio is approximately 0.55 while accuracy is slightly above the base model. On AIME24, the final model's token ratio is approximately 0.57 with accuracy slightly above the base model. The cumulative effect across all three stages produces the final 42.7% length reduction at the 7B scale reported in Table 1.

A critical observation from Figure 3: the GRPO stage does not produce a monotonic length decrease with a flat or decreasing accuracy—both metrics improve simultaneously, indicating that the length penalty is not simply trading accuracy for efficiency but is genuinely teaching the model to reason more effectively (fewer tokens, higher correctness).

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Pruning preservation of essential reasoning (Table 3):** This experiment tests whether graph-based pruning preserves reasoning coherence compared to naive length truncation. Three CoT variants are used as conditioning context for DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B: Full-CoT (original traces), Graph-Pruned (traces processed by the graph-based pruning), and Len-Trunc (traces naively truncated from the beginning to match the token length of the Graph-Pruned variants). On 1000 randomly sampled training examples with N=8 generation passes, Graph-Pruned achieves 93.70% accuracy and 90.69% consistency, compared to Full-CoT's 98.95% accuracy and 99.60% consistency. Len-Trunc drops dramatically to 73.60% accuracy and 69.10% consistency—a 20.1 percentage point accuracy gap between graph-based and naive pruning at matched token lengths. This large gap demonstrates that the graph structure captures essential reasoning dependencies that token-count-based truncation destroys. The consistency metric (defined as C = Σ(n_y/N)^2 where n_y is the frequency of answer y across N generations) measures answer stability—graph-based pruning preserves much more generation consistency (90.69% vs. 69.10%), indicating that pruned traces produce more reliable, less divergent model outputs.

**Reasoning length distribution shift (Figure 4, left):** Before training, the probability density of generated token length on AIME24 shows a broad distribution with a substantial long tail extending beyond 1.2× the normalized mean. After training, the distribution shifts left and sharpens—the peak moves to lower normalized lengths, and the long tail is substantially suppressed. This demonstrates that the training pipeline reduces not just average length but also eliminates the extreme-length trajectories (the "overthinking" tail) that contribute disproportionately to worst-case inference cost.

**Reasoning token frequency changes (Figure 4, right):** The bar plot shows counts per response of representative discourse markers on AIME24. Reflection-oriented tokens decrease after training: "Wait" drops by roughly 55%, "But" by roughly 40%, "Hmm" by roughly 60%, "Maybe" by roughly 50%, and "Check" by roughly 45%. Progress-oriented connectives increase: "Therefore" roughly doubles in frequency. "Since" and "Similarly" show modest increases or remain stable. This is direct behavioral evidence that the model shifts from reflective verbosity toward more direct, forward-driven reasoning—precisely the behavioral change the method aims to produce.

**Dataset and graph statistics before and after pruning (Table 2):** On the 3335-sample SFT training set, graph-based pruning reduces average nodes per graph from 27.8 to 15.6 (44.2% reduction), average review nodes from 16.8 to 4.5 (73.2% reduction), and average tokens from 6468 to 4439 (31.4% reduction). The 73.2% reduction in review nodes is substantially larger than the 31.4% reduction in tokens, confirming that the pruning specifically targets reflection content rather than uniformly compressing all reasoning. Since review nodes are removed while progress nodes are preserved, the proportion of progress nodes in the surviving graph increases substantially—the concise traces consist primarily of frontier-advancing reasoning with only the structurally justified review nodes remaining. The total data synthesis cost of $20 for 3335 examples is negligible compared to the downstream training cost.

**Human evaluation of graph construction quality (Table 4):** On 100 randomly sampled nodes, the graph construction achieves strong performance: Review nodes—precision 0.9048, recall 0.9661, F1 0.9344; Progress nodes—precision 0.8947, recall 0.8500, F1 0.8718. The step atomicity valid rate is 85.29%. The high recall for review nodes (0.9661) is particularly important—it means the construction rarely fails to identify reflection content, minimizing the risk that redundant reflection survives pruning because it was misclassified as progress. The slightly lower precision for progress nodes (0.8947) and lower recall (0.8500) indicate that the construction is somewhat conservative: it occasionally labels progress content as review (false positives for review, false negatives for progress). Since the pruning module operates only on review nodes, this conservative bias is safe—it errs on the side of treating potentially useful verification as review and then applying structural criteria to determine whether to prune it, rather than misclassifying reflection as progress and letting it survive.

**RL training dynamics (Appendix E, Figure 5):** The mean reward curve for the 7B model (Figure 5a) shows an overall increasing trend from approximately 0.55 to 0.80 over 220 training steps, with noticeable fluctuations smoothed by exponential moving average. The response length fluctuates in the 4000-5500 token range without a clear upward or downward trend—it oscillates rather than monotonically decreasing. This is important because it shows that the increasing reward is not achieved by simply generating longer responses (a common failure mode in unregularized RL for reasoning). Instead, the model learns more effective reasoning strategies within a relatively stable length regime. For the 1.5B model (Figure 5b), the mean reward increases from approximately 0.35 to 0.60, again with fluctuating response length in the 4000-6000 token range. The lower absolute rewards for the 1.5B model reflect its lower base accuracy, but the training dynamics are qualitatively similar—reward improves without length inflation.

**Qualitative case study (Figure 7):** The paper provides a detailed example comparing the base model's CoT and the trained model's CoT for an algebra word problem. The base model's CoT contains multiple redundant reflection segments (highlighted in red in the figure), including restating the coffee shop time relationship multiple times ("the coffee shop time is fixed at t = 24 minutes regardless of her walking speed"), checking consistency with equation 2 after already confirming correctness, questioning whether the answer should be in hours or minutes ("Wait... let me make sure..."), and proposing an alternative reasoning approach after the answer is already derived ("Alternatively, maybe I can think of it another way..."). The trained model's CoT shows the same core derivation (setting up equations, solving the quadratic, computing the final time) but omits the repeated restatements, the redundant consistency check, and the post-answer alternative approach. The graph visualization in the left column maps each CoT segment to progress nodes (blue) and review nodes (red) in the DAG structure, showing how the base model's graph contains review nodes that initiate small verification branches, while the trained model's graph is predominantly progress-driven with minimal review.

### Critical Assessment

**Claim 1: The method reduces average reasoning tokens by 42% while maintaining or improving accuracy.** This claim is supported by Table 1, with important nuance. The 42.7% reduction is the headline figure for the 7B model across all five benchmarks (8134 → 4660 tokens), and accuracy improves from 59.72% to 60.95% (+1.23 points). However, the accuracy story is not uniform: on AIME24, accuracy decreases slightly from 42.67% to 42.08% (-0.59 points), while on OlympiadBench it increases substantially from 56.77% to 59.85% (+3.08 points). The claim of "maintaining or improving" accuracy thus holds on average but admits one case of slight degradation on the hardest benchmark. The paper is transparent about this—Table 1 reports all numbers—but the abstract's claim of "maintaining or improving accuracy" could be read as claiming no degradation on any benchmark, which is not exactly true.

A deeper concern: the accuracy metric averages 10 samples per problem. For competition-level problems with only 30 questions (AIME24, AIME25), the accuracy numbers have high variance—a change of 1-2 percentage points corresponds to less than one problem's worth of difference. The paper does not report confidence intervals or standard errors, making it impossible to assess whether the -0.59 point drop on AIME24 is statistically significant or within sampling noise. This is a genuine weakness for the hard-benchmark comparisons.

**Claim 2: The approach outperforms existing efficient reasoning methods.** This is supported by Table 1 and visualized in Figure 1. The proposed method achieves both higher accuracy and lower token count than all baselines at both model scales, with the gap being largest against budget-control methods (TokenSkip, O1-Pruner) and smaller but still meaningful against RL-based methods (EfficientReasoning, AdaptThink). The comparison is fair in that all methods are evaluated on the same benchmarks with the same metric. However, the implementation details matter: O1-Pruner and TokenSkip were adapted by the authors to the DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen models (marked with `*` in Table 1). The paper does not detail how these adaptations were performed, and it's possible that the adaptations are suboptimal compared to the original implementations on their intended base models. The performance of these adapted baselines is notably low (O1-Pruner at 7B: 54.11% accuracy vs. base 59.72%), which could indicate adaptation difficulties rather than inherent method limitations. A fairer comparison would include these baselines on their originally reported model configurations.

Additionally, the comparison against open-source R1-style models (Skywork-OR1-7B, OREAL-7B, Light-R1-DS-7B) is somewhat misleading. These models were trained with different data, different RL recipes, and different compute budgets. They are not efficiency-optimized variants of the same base model—they are entirely separate model checkpoints. The proposed method improves upon the DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B base, and outperforming other 7B reasoning models in the accuracy-efficiency trade-off is a valid comparison, but it conflates the method's efficiency gains with the base model's capability. A controlled comparison would apply each efficiency method to the same base model, which is only done for O1-Pruner, TokenSkip, EfficientReasoning, and AdaptThink.

**Claim 3: Graph-based pruning preserves essential reasoning, unlike naive length truncation.** This claim is strongly supported by Table 3. The 20.1 percentage point accuracy gap between Graph-Pruned (93.70%) and Len-Trunc (73.60%) at matched token lengths is a compelling demonstration. However, several caveats apply:

First, the Len-Trunc baseline is extremely naive—truncating from the beginning of the CoT. Most practical length-control methods would not simply chop off the first N tokens; they would use some form of content-aware selection. The paper does not compare against a stronger length-matching baseline such as truncating from the end, selecting spans based on attention scores, or using a summarization model to compress. The 20-point gap establishes that graph structure beats the worst possible truncation strategy, but it does not establish that graph structure beats all possible truncation strategies.

Second, Table 3 evaluates accuracy when the DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B model is *conditioned on* different CoT types (Full-CoT, Graph-Pruned, Len-Trunc) and asked to generate answers. This is a meta-evaluation: given a reasoning trace as context, can the model produce correct answers? It does not directly measure whether the pruned traces, when used as *training data*, produce a model that reasons accurately. The training results (Table 1) provide that evidence, but the Table 3 experiment measures something different—it measures how much information the pruned traces contain, not how effectively the model learns from them.

Third, the 1000 examples are drawn from the training set, not from the evaluation benchmarks. The characteristics of training-set CoT (which may be higher-quality or more systematically annotated) may differ from the evaluation benchmarks' model-generated CoT.

**Claim 4: The three-stage training pipeline progressively improves both accuracy and efficiency.** Supported by Figure 3. The cumulative addition of SFT, DPO, and GRPO shows monotonic token ratio reduction and generally improving accuracy. The stage that contributes the most length reduction is SFT (roughly 40-45% reduction from base), while DPO and GRPO contribute smaller additional reductions (5-10% each). Accuracy follows a different pattern: SFT reduces accuracy on the hardest benchmarks (AIME24, AIME25) but improves it on easier ones; DPO restores accuracy on hard benchmarks; GRPO provides small additional accuracy gains. This is a nuanced and believable pattern—SFT on pruned traces teaches efficient reasoning but may lose some useful verification on hard problems; DPO's preference for correct efficient traces restores that verification where it matters; GRPO's length-penalized RL fine-tunes the balance. However, the ablation does not test alternative orderings (e.g., DPO before SFT, or GRPO directly from SFT without DPO), so the claim that this specific three-stage ordering is optimal is not established.

**Claim 5: Redundancy stems from Indiscriminate Reflection and Repetitive Reflection.** This is the paper's taxonomic claim, and the supporting evidence is largely qualitative (the example in Figure 7) and structural (the pruning criteria are designed to target these patterns). The paper does not provide a quantitative breakdown of how much pruned content falls into each category—the Table 2 statistics show that 73.2% of review nodes are pruned, but they do not report what fraction of pruned nodes were removed by branch-level vs. depth-level criteria. Without this breakdown, the claim that both patterns are prevalent and both pruning criteria are necessary remains asserted rather than demonstrated. An ablation that applies only branch-level pruning or only depth-level pruning and reports the resulting accuracy-efficiency trade-off would address this gap.

**Missing experiments that would strengthen the paper:**

- **Difficulty-stratified results**: The main results (Table 1) report per-benchmark performance, and the benchmarks naturally vary in difficulty (AIME > OlympiadBench > AMC > MATH500). However, the paper does not break down performance within each benchmark by problem difficulty. In the prior example paper, difficulty-stratified analysis revealed that test-time compute helps on easy-to-medium problems but not on hard ones—a similar stratification here could reveal whether the method's accuracy gains are concentrated on easier problems (where redundant reflection is most removable without consequence) and whether accuracy on the hardest problems is genuinely maintained or slightly degraded.

- **Pruning threshold sensitivity**: The paper sets k=2 and m=0.9 for all experiments but does not explore sensitivity to these hyperparameters. How does accuracy change if k=1 (lenient, only prune leaf review nodes) or k=3 (aggressive, require review nodes to have substantial downstream impact)? How does length reduction change if m=0.7 (prune more late-stage reflection) or m=1.0 (no depth-level pruning)? Understanding these sensitivities would reveal how robust the approach is to threshold choice and whether the specific values were tuned on the evaluation benchmarks.

- **Comparison to a "review-only pruning" baseline**: The paper's key claim is that structural graph-based pruning is superior to token-level and budget-level methods. A natural ablation would be: what if you simply remove all review-type nodes without applying the structural criteria (branch-level and depth-level thresholds)? How much worse is this "prune all review" baseline compared to the selective structural pruning? If pruning all review nodes already achieves similar accuracy-efficiency, then the structural criteria add little. If it substantially degrades accuracy, then the structural criteria are doing important work. This ablation is not present.

- **Direct comparison to SPIRIT, Think Clearly, or TRAAC**: The paper critiques token-level and attention-based redundancy detection methods (Section 2.2) but does not include any of them as baselines in Table 1. Including at least one representative from this category (e.g., Think Clearly's attention-based pruning or TokenSkip, which is included but is an adapted version) on the same base model would directly test the paper's central claim that structural pruning outperforms content-based pruning.

- **Generalization beyond mathematical reasoning**: All five benchmarks are mathematical reasoning tasks. The paper acknowledges this limitation explicitly in the Limitations section: "while effective for mathematical reasoning, it remains unclear how well the method generalizes to more open-ended domains." Testing on code generation (HumanEval, MBPP) or logical reasoning (ARC, FOLIO) would test whether the structural reflection taxonomy and graph construction generalize beyond the domain where they were developed.

- **Computational cost comparison of training**: The paper claims the method is efficient, but it involves three training stages (SFT, DPO, GRPO) plus an external LLM for graph construction. The total training FLOPs are not reported, and no comparison is made to the training cost of baseline methods like EfficientReasoning or AdaptThink. If the proposed method's training pipeline is substantially more expensive, the inference-time efficiency gains must be weighed against the training cost, especially for deployment scenarios with limited training budgets.

**Overall assessment:** The experimental section provides solid evidence for the method's effectiveness on mathematical reasoning benchmarks at two model scales. The headline 42.7% length reduction with maintained accuracy is a strong result that the experiments consistently support. The pruning-preservation experiment (Table 3) convincingly demonstrates the value of structural over naive compression. The stage-wise ablation (Figure 3) cleanly decomposes the contributions of each training stage. The primary weaknesses are: (1) lack of statistical rigor (no confidence intervals, 10 samples per problem on small test sets for AIME), (2) incomplete ablation of the structural pruning criteria (no sensitivity analysis, no "prune all review" baseline, no breakdown of branch-level vs. depth-level contributions), (3) baseline comparisons that are not fully controlled (adapted implementations of O1-Pruner and TokenSkip, no content-based redundancy detection baselines), and (4) confinement to mathematical reasoning with no evidence of domain generalization. These weaknesses do not undermine the central findings but limit the strength of the paper's broader claims about the superiority of structural over content-based approaches and the generality of the reflection taxonomy.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### 1. The Method Has Not Been Demonstrated Beyond Mathematical Reasoning

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper evaluates its method exclusively on five mathematical reasoning benchmarks (AIME24, AIME25, AMC23, OlympiadBench, MATH500), all of which involve problems with verifiable, closed-form answers that can be checked via math-verify. The graph construction prompt (Appendix A, Figure 6), the node typing taxonomy (progress vs. review), and the pruning criteria were all developed and validated in this domain. The authors explicitly acknowledge this constraint:

> "while effective for mathematical reasoning, it remains unclear how well the method generalizes to more open-ended domains."

**The consequence.** The entire framework depends on the ability to reliably distinguish progress from review in reasoning traces, which in turn depends on the discourse patterns that mathematical reasoning models learn during RL training (e.g., "Wait, let me verify," "Let me double-check," "Therefore"). In other domains—code generation, legal reasoning, creative writing, multi-step planning—the linguistic markers of reflection may be qualitatively different or entirely absent. A model reasoning about code might not use explicit self-verification keywords but instead revise the code inline or test it mentally without discourse markers. In such domains, both the chunking procedure (which relies on specific split tokens from Appendix B) and the node-type classification (which depends on recognizing verification from its textual form) could fail, either by producing inaccurate graphs or by failing to detect domain-specific forms of redundant reasoning. Moreover, domains without clean correctness signals for answer verification cannot use the GRPO stage's length penalty as designed, since the correctness reward V(x,y) ∈ {0,1} requires ground-truth answers.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None—the paper contains zero experiments outside mathematical reasoning. The Limitations section acknowledges this as a scope constraint but provides no analysis of how the method's components might transfer or fail in other domains. The graph construction validation (Table 4) was performed only on mathematical reasoning traces. The dataset statistics (Table 2) are from the Light-R1 math training set. All five evaluation benchmarks in Table 1 are math-specific.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not attempt to address this. The Limitations section states that "future work may explore... broader domain evaluations" but offers no hypotheses about which components would generalize and which would require redesign. A practitioner considering this method for a non-math domain would need to independently validate the graph construction quality and the pruning criteria's effectiveness, with no guidance from the paper on expected failure modes.

---

### 2. The Graph Construction Depends on an External Strong LLM Oracle

**The assumption or constraint.** The iterative graph construction procedure (Section 3.2.1) requires a capable external LLM (qwen-turbo) to build typed DAGs from linear CoT. This model serves as an oracle annotator—it must be able to (a) understand the mathematical content of each reasoning chunk deeply enough to classify it as progress or review, (b) identify logical dependencies between chunks to construct correct edges, and (c) follow a detailed prompt with structural constraints (node IDs, edge ordering, JSON output format). The paper reports that this external LLM achieves high quality on mathematical reasoning (Table 4: review F1 0.9344, progress F1 0.8718), with a total data synthesis cost of $20 for 3335 examples.

**The consequence.** This creates a dependency that is underappreciated in the paper's framing. The method's core value proposition—structural pruning of redundant reflection without relying on surface-level heuristics—is only realizable if a sufficiently capable graph constructor is available. If the graph constructor's node-typing accuracy degrades on harder problems, on out-of-domain tasks, or when applied to a base model whose reasoning patterns differ from the training distribution, the pruning quality degrades correspondingly. A practitioner working with a base model that the external LLM does not understand well (e.g., a domain-specific reasoning model for medical diagnosis or legal analysis) might get lower-quality graphs, leading to either under-pruning (review nodes misclassified as progress and left in) or over-pruning (progress nodes misclassified as review and removed, breaking essential dependencies). The paper does not study how much the downstream task performance depends on graph construction quality, so there is no guidance on what level of construction accuracy is "good enough."

Moreover, the approach is somewhat circular in a way that limits scalability: the method requires a model *at least as capable* as the one being trained to construct the training data. This means the technique cannot be used to train a model that exceeds the graph constructor's capability—the constructor must understand the reasoning to classify it correctly. For frontier models that push the boundary of reasoning capability, there may be no external model that can reliably construct graphs for their outputs, making the method inapplicable without human annotation (which would be far more expensive than the reported $20 for 3335 examples).

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 4 provides the only quality assessment: on 100 randomly sampled nodes from mathematical reasoning graphs, review node classification achieves F1 0.9344 and progress classification achieves F1 0.8718. The $20 cost figure in Table 2 demonstrates practical feasibility at the current scale. However, the paper does not report how graph construction quality varies by problem difficulty—the 100 sampled nodes may be biased toward easier problems where the constructor's task is simpler. There is no ablation where lower-quality graphs (e.g., from a weaker constructor) are used to train the model and downstream performance is measured, which would quantify the method's sensitivity to construction quality. There is also no experiment showing that a model other than qwen-turbo can serve as the constructor—if qwen-turbo has specific properties that make the construction work well (e.g., its training data or architecture), practitioners using a different external LLM may get different results.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this dependency as a limitation. The $20 cost is presented as evidence of practicality rather than as a constraint that might not generalize. The human evaluation (Table 4) partially validates construction quality but does not establish a lower bound on acceptable quality or analyze failure cases that would inform practitioners about when the approach might break. The paper also does not discuss whether the graph constructor could be the same model being trained (self-annotation), which would eliminate the external-model dependency but potentially introduce systematic biases (a model that over-reflects might also misclassify its own reflection when acting as graph constructor).

---

### 3. No Sensitivity Analysis for the Critical Pruning Thresholds k and m

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper sets the pruning hyperparameters to fixed values—k = 2 for branch-level pruning (Section 3.2.2: review nodes with fewer than 2 descendants are pruned) and m = 0.9 for depth-level pruning (review nodes at relative depth > 0.9 are pruned)—and uses these values throughout all experiments. The paper states these values once:

> "we set m = 0.9 and k = 2 throughout all experiments."

No justification is provided for these specific choices, and no experiments explore how performance varies if these thresholds are changed.

**The consequence.** These thresholds encode fundamental trade-offs that determine the method's behavior. The branch-level threshold k controls how aggressively narrow reflection branches are pruned: k = 1 would only prune leaf review nodes with zero descendants (the most conservative setting), while k = 5 would require review nodes to have at least 5 downstream dependencies to survive (highly aggressive, potentially removing verification that genuinely feeds into the main reasoning). The depth-level threshold m controls how much late-stage reflection is pruned: m = 0.7 would prune all review in the final 30% of the reasoning trajectory (more aggressive), while m = 1.0 would disable depth-level pruning entirely (no pruning of late-stage reflection). The choice of k and m effectively determines the accuracy-efficiency trade-off, and without sensitivity analysis, a practitioner cannot:
- Determine whether the reported results are robust to threshold variation or heavily dependent on these specific values.
- Tune the method for a different accuracy-efficiency preference (e.g., if a practitioner is willing to accept slightly lower accuracy for substantially greater length reduction, they would want to know whether lowering m to 0.7 or raising k to 3 achieves that trade-off gracefully).
- Assess whether the thresholds were implicitly tuned on the evaluation benchmarks (since the paper does not describe a validation procedure for selecting k and m, it's possible that these values were chosen based on performance on the same benchmarks used for reporting, which would inflate reported performance).

Furthermore, the two pruning criteria interact: a review node at depth 0.85 with only 1 descendant would be pruned by the branch-level criterion even if the depth-level criterion would keep it (since 0.85 < 0.9). Changing m changes which nodes are eligible for branch-level pruning by altering the set of nodes that survive to be evaluated by the descendant-count criterion. Without understanding this interaction, it's unclear whether both criteria are necessary or whether one dominates.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. The paper does not include any ablation varying k or m. Table 2 reports aggregate statistics (73.2% of review nodes pruned) but does not break down what fraction of pruned nodes were removed by each criterion. There is no experiment showing, for example, performance with only branch-level pruning (m = 1.0, effectively disabling depth-level pruning) or only depth-level pruning (k = 0, effectively disabling branch-level pruning). The paper also does not report the distribution of descendant counts for review nodes, which would help readers assess whether the k = 2 threshold sits at a natural breakpoint in the data or is an arbitrary choice.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The fixed threshold values are stated as definitive without discussion of sensitivity, validation, or alternative settings. This is a significant gap because the thresholds are the operational interface of the method's core contribution (the structural pruning criteria)—the paper demonstrates that structural pruning works, but does not characterize the sensitivity of that "working" to the specific structural parameters chosen.

### 4. The Training Pipeline's Cumulative Cost Is Not Compared to Simpler Alternatives

**The assumption or constraint.** The proposed method uses a three-stage training pipeline: SFT on graph-pruned traces, DPO on redundancy-ranked preference pairs, and GRPO with length penalty. Each stage requires data preparation, model training, and hyperparameter tuning. The paper reports training hyperparameters (Tables 5, 6, 7) and the graph construction cost ($20), but does not report the total training FLOPs, GPU-hours, or wall-clock time for the full pipeline. The baseline methods in Table 1 (O1-Pruner, TokenSkip, EfficientReasoning, AdaptThink) have their own training pipelines of unspecified cost, but the paper does not attempt a training-cost-normalized comparison.

**The consequence.** The paper's headline result—42.7% length reduction with maintained accuracy—is an **inference-time** efficiency gain. A practitioner deciding whether to adopt this method needs to know the **training-time** cost to achieve that inference-time gain, because the two must be traded off against each other. If the three-stage pipeline costs substantially more to train than, say, EfficientReasoning (which uses a single-stage RL objective), then the inference-time savings must be amortized over many queries before the method breaks even. For a deployment that serves a small number of queries (e.g., a research evaluation), the training cost might dominate and the method would be net-negative in total resource consumption even if inference is more efficient.

This is particularly relevant because the GRPO stage uses 8 rollouts per input (Table 7), 220 training steps, and a maximum response length of 12000 tokens—this is a computationally intensive RL training procedure. The DPO stage requires generating multiple rollouts per problem and constructing preference pairs. The SFT stage uses LoRA (which is parameter-efficient) but still requires 8 epochs over 3335 examples. The cumulative cost of all three stages is non-trivial, but the paper provides no numbers to contextualize it.

Furthermore, the paper does not compare against a simpler alternative: what if you only perform SFT on graph-pruned traces without DPO and GRPO? Figure 3 shows that SFT alone achieves roughly 35-45% length reduction from the base model (compared to the final ~43% from all three stages), but the accuracy on hard benchmarks (AIME24, AIME25) drops below the base model. A practitioner might reasonably ask: is the additional accuracy recovery and length reduction from DPO + GRPO worth the additional training cost, or would SFT alone provide most of the benefit at a fraction of the cost? The ablation shows the **performance** contribution of each stage but not the **cost** of each stage, making it impossible to answer this question from the paper.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figure 3 provides stage-wise performance decomposition but no cost data. Table 2 reports $20 for graph construction (external LLM API cost) but not the GPU cost of training. Appendices D.1-D.3 provide hyperparameters (epochs, batch sizes, learning rates, training steps) from which approximate FLOPs could be estimated, but the paper does not do this estimation. The paper states that all experiments run on "a single compute node with 4× NVIDIA A800 GPUs" (Section 4.1) but does not report the total training time.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address training cost as a consideration. The focus is entirely on inference-time efficiency, which is the most natural metric for deployment scenarios with high query volumes, but the omission of training cost makes it difficult for practitioners with limited training budgets to assess whether the method is appropriate for their setting. The paper's claim in the abstract—"experiments show that our approach reduces the average reasoning tokens by 42% while maintaining or improving accuracy"—describes inference-time performance and does not claim training efficiency, so this is an omission rather than an overclaim. However, for a paper proposing a new training recipe, the absence of any training-cost analysis is a meaningful gap.

---

### 5. The Accuracy Gains Are Non-Uniform and May Reverse on the Hardest Problems

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's central claim is that the method "maintains or improves accuracy" while reducing reasoning length. The abstract states this without qualification, and the conclusion repeats it. However, the method is applied uniformly—the same pruning thresholds, the same training pipeline, and the same model are used for all problems regardless of difficulty. There is no difficulty-adaptive component that adjusts pruning aggressiveness or training emphasis based on whether a problem is easy (where most reflection is likely redundant) or hard (where some reflection may be genuinely necessary for correctness).

**The consequence.** On the hardest benchmark (AIME24), the 7B model's accuracy drops from 42.67% (base) to 42.08% (proposed), a decrease of 0.59 percentage points. While this is a small absolute decrease, it matters disproportionately because AIME problems are exactly the kind of high-stakes reasoning tasks where users are least willing to trade accuracy for efficiency. A practitioner deploying the method for competition-level math assistance would need to know whether the method systematically degrades performance on the hardest problems in their distribution. The paper's average accuracy improvement of +1.23 points (from 59.72% to 60.95%) is driven by larger gains on moderate benchmarks (OlympiadBench: +3.08 points; AMC23: +1.34 points) that compensate for the slight degradation on AIME24. A deployment where the problem distribution skews toward the hardest tier (many AIME-level problems, few MATH500-level problems) might see net accuracy degradation rather than improvement.

This non-uniformity is consistent with the paper's own reasoning: on harder problems, some of the reflection that the base model produces may be genuinely useful—verifying an intermediate algebraic manipulation on an AIME problem might catch errors that would otherwise propagate, and removing that verification could cause incorrect answers. The pruning criteria do not distinguish between "reflection that happens to be structurally redundant on this particular (correct) trace" and "reflection that, in expectation across possible reasoning paths, prevents errors." A reflection node that has few descendants in a particular correct CoT might still be important for robustness—if the model had made a different error earlier, that verification might have caught it. The pruning criteria are purely structural and operate on single correct traces, so they cannot capture this counterfactual value of verification.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 1 shows the per-benchmark breakdown, with AIME24 accuracy decreasing slightly at both model scales (7B: 42.67% → 42.08%; 1.5B: 30.83% → 26.67%, a more substantial 4.16-point drop). The 1.5B model also degrades on AIME25 (22.92% → 23.33%, essentially flat) while gaining substantially on AMC23 (63.12% → 69.38%) and MATH500 (72.65% → 80.40%). The paper does not break down performance within each benchmark by problem difficulty, so it's impossible to determine whether the accuracy degradation is concentrated on the hardest problems or spread across the benchmark.

**Mitigation status.** The paper reports the per-benchmark numbers transparently in Table 1, so the non-uniformity is visible to careful readers, but the text does not discuss it. The abstract and conclusion both state "maintaining or improving accuracy" without the nuance that this holds on average but not on the single hardest benchmark. The paper does not propose any difficulty-adaptive mechanism that would apply less aggressive pruning or different training to hard problems. This is a missed opportunity, especially given prior work (AdaptThink, Zhang et al., 2025, which is included as a baseline) that explicitly learns difficulty-adaptive reasoning strategies. Combining the graph-based pruning framework with a difficulty estimator could produce a method that only prunes reflection on problems where it is genuinely redundant, but this direction is not explored.

### 6. Latency Characteristics of Reduced-Length Reasoning Are Not Analyzed

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper measures efficiency exclusively through average reasoning token count, which is a proxy for total inference FLOPs. The claim is that reducing token count from 8134 to 4660 (42.7% reduction) translates to proportional inference cost savings. This assumes that inference cost is dominated by total FLOPs and that per-token latency is constant regardless of what those tokens represent.

**The consequence.** In autoregressive transformer inference, the per-token computational cost is roughly uniform (each token requires one forward pass through the model), so the 42.7% token reduction does indeed correspond to roughly 42.7% fewer FLOPs for the reasoning phase. However, the user-facing metric that matters for interactive applications is **wall-clock latency** (time from query to answer), not total FLOPs. The paper does not report generation latency for the trained model, which could differ from what the token-count reduction would naively suggest for several reasons:

First, the trained model produces fewer reflection tokens but potentially **higher-quality progress tokens** that require more computation to generate (e.g., denser mathematical derivations with more complex token interactions). If the model shifts from generating simple reflection phrases ("Wait, let me check this") to generating compressed mathematical notation, the per-token generation time could increase due to more complex attention patterns, even though there are fewer tokens.

Second, autoregressive generation latency is dominated by the sequential dependency—each token must be generated before the next one can begin, and the total latency is roughly (number of tokens × time per token). If the time per token is constant, the latency reduction matches the token reduction (42.7%). But if the trained model's tokens are slightly more expensive (e.g., because the model is computing more complex internal representations for denser reasoning), the latency reduction could be less than 42.7%. Conversely, if shorter sequences improve KV-cache memory locality and reduce memory bandwidth pressure, the per-token time could decrease, making the latency reduction greater than 42.7%. The paper provides no data to distinguish these scenarios.

Third, for batch inference (processing many queries simultaneously), the total throughput depends on both per-query token count and how efficiently queries can be batched. Shorter reasoning traces mean less memory pressure from KV-caches, potentially enabling larger batch sizes and higher throughput. But the paper does not analyze this.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. The paper reports token counts (Table 1, Figure 3, Figure 4) but never reports generation time, tokens-per-second, or latency. The RL training dynamics (Figure 5) show response length fluctuating during training but not generation speed.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper treats token count as a sufficient proxy for efficiency, which is reasonable for a first-order analysis but insufficient for deployment planning where latency guarantees matter. A practitioner considering this method for a latency-sensitive application (e.g., an interactive math tutoring system) cannot estimate from the paper whether the token reduction translates fully to latency reduction. This is a common omission in efficient-reasoning papers—most baselines in Table 1 also report only token counts—but it limits the practical interpretability of the results for real-time applications.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper introduces a **structural diagnostic toolkit** for reasoning efficiency that shifts the conversation from "can we compress CoT?" to "can we understand *why* certain reasoning steps are unnecessary and *where* they live in the reasoning structure?" The magnitude of the shift is **methodological rather than paradigmatic**: the paper does not propose a fundamentally new training algorithm or model architecture, but it provides a concrete, validated framework for reasoning about redundancy that was previously missing from the field. The key conceptual change is the reframing of overthinking from a surface-level token phenomenon to a **structural property of the reasoning dependency graph**—a shift that makes redundancy analysis precise, interpretable, and principled in a way that token-importance scores and attention-based heuristics cannot match.

Prior to this work, the efficient reasoning literature operated largely through two lenses: **global budget constraints** (limit total tokens, either through learned or assigned budgets, as in Han et al., 2025 and Ma et al., 2025b) and **local importance estimation** (score each token or span by attention weight, entropy, or perplexity, and discard low-scoring ones, as in Choi et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025a; Xia et al., 2025). Both lenses treat redundancy as a property of individual tokens or their statistical signatures. This paper demonstrates empirically that these lenses miss the essential structure: when tokens are removed by naive truncation (matching the token count of structurally pruned traces), accuracy collapses by more than 20 percentage points compared to structural pruning (Table 3: Len-Trunc 73.60% vs. Graph-Pruned 93.70%). The implication is clear—**reasoning coherence is encoded in the dependency graph, not in the token sequence**. Any compression method that does not recover this graph operates blindly, and the paper provides both the conceptual argument and the experimental evidence for why blindness is costly.

The paper also partially **reconciles a tension** in the literature between those who observe that verification and self-checking improve reasoning (the RL-for-reasoning paradigm that incentivizes long CoT with embedded reflection) and those who observe that much of this reflection is wasteful (the overthinking literature that documents redundant self-verification). The structural taxonomy of Indiscriminate and Repetitive Reflection provides a **diagnostic vocabulary** for distinguishing between reflection that feeds into the reasoning (structurally justified review nodes with substantial descendant trees) and reflection that does not (narrow branches with few descendants, post-answer backtracking). This suggests that the apparent contradiction—"reflection helps" versus "reflection is wasteful"—is resolved by recognizing that reflection is heterogeneous: some of it is structurally integrated into the reasoning and genuinely useful, while much of it (73.2% of review nodes in the paper's training data, per Table 2) is structurally peripheral. The field can now ask not "should models reflect?" but "which reflection patterns are structurally productive and which are not?"—a much more nuanced and productive question.

The paper makes certain research directions more attractive while tempering enthusiasm for others:

**More attractive directions:**
- **Structural analysis of reasoning** as a prerequisite for compression. The paper's core finding is that understanding the dependency graph yields substantial efficiency gains with preserved accuracy, while ignoring the graph degrades performance. This elevates graph construction from a niche representation choice to a central methodological step for any serious effort to reduce reasoning verbosity.
- **Typed reasoning analysis** that goes beyond binary progress/review. The paper demonstrates that semantic typing of reasoning nodes (progress vs. review) enables targeted pruning criteria that would be impossible with untyped graphs. Extending this to richer type systems (e.g., hypothesis generation, constraint application, error detection, sub-goal completion) could enable even more precise redundancy analysis.
- **Training data preprocessing as a efficiency intervention**. The paper shows that applying structural pruning to *training data* (not inference-time outputs) effectively teaches the model to internalize efficient reasoning patterns. This is a fundamentally different strategy from inference-time compression or post-hoc pruning, and it suggests that data preprocessing (graph construction → pruning → relinearization → SFT) is a viable alternative to modifying the RL objective.

**Less attractive directions:**
- **Solely improving token-level importance estimators.** If the dependency structure—not token-level statistics—determines which content is redundant, then making attention-based or entropy-based importance scores more accurate may yield diminishing returns. The paper's Table 3 result implies a ceiling on token-level methods: even a perfect token importance estimator would not know that a low-importance token in a structurally essential derivation must be preserved, while high-importance tokens in a dead-end verification branch should be removed.
- **Uniform length budgets without structural awareness.** The paper's comparison against length-control methods (O1-Pruner, AdaptThink) shows that structural pruning achieves better accuracy-efficiency trade-offs, suggesting that global length constraints without knowledge of *what* to shorten are fundamentally limited. A budget-aware method that incorporates graph structure (e.g., allocating budget to progress nodes while restricting or eliminating review budget) would be more promising than further refining flat per-problem token budgets.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Sensitivity analysis of pruning thresholds k and m across difficulty strata.** The paper sets k=2 and m=0.9 for all experiments without exploring how these thresholds interact with problem difficulty. A direct follow-up would systematically vary k (e.g., 0, 1, 2, 3, 5) and m (e.g., 0.7, 0.8, 0.9, 1.0) and measure accuracy and length reduction on each of the five benchmarks in Table 1, reporting separate curves for easy, medium, and hard problems. The key hypothesis to test is whether harder problems require more conservative thresholds (lower k, higher m, preserving more review nodes) to maintain accuracy, while easier problems tolerate aggressive pruning (higher k, lower m) without degradation. If confirmed, this would motivate a difficulty-adaptive pruning policy that adjusts thresholds per problem based on estimated difficulty—analogous to the compute-optimal test-time scaling approach in the reference example paper. The experiment would also reveal whether the specific values k=2 and m=0.9 sit at natural breakpoints in the accuracy-efficiency frontier or were tuned to the evaluation benchmarks, addressing a key limitation of the current paper.

**Direct comparison between structural pruning and content-based redundancy detection on the same base model.** The paper critiques attention-based and perplexity-based redundancy methods (Think Clearly, Choi et al., 2025; TRAAC, Singh et al., 2025; SPIRIT, Cui et al., 2025) but includes none as baselines. A rigorous follow-up would implement at least two representative content-based methods (e.g., attention-based token pruning à la Think Clearly and entropy-based step removal à la Step-Entropy) on the DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B base model, apply them at compression ratios matched to the paper's 42.7% token reduction, and report accuracy on the same five benchmarks. The key question is whether content-based methods, when tuned for equivalent compression, can match structural pruning's accuracy preservation. If they can, the paper's central claim about the necessity of graph structure is weakened. If—as the Len-Trunc result in Table 3 suggests—content-based methods substantially underperform structural pruning, the paper's thesis is strengthened and the field would have clear evidence that structural modeling is not merely helpful but necessary. This experiment also addresses the paper's omission of this baseline category.

**Extension to code generation: does the reflection taxonomy transfer?** All experiments are on mathematical reasoning, where reflection takes predictable linguistic forms (verification keywords, restating conclusions, checking arithmetic). Code generation introduces different reflection patterns: the model might write a function, mentally simulate its execution, add debug print statements, or rewrite a solution approach with inline comments. A follow-up would apply the graph construction pipeline to chain-of-thought traces from a code generation model (e.g., on HumanEval or MBPP), with two specific investigations: (1) whether the progress/review node typing, as currently prompted, correctly classifies code-specific reflection (e.g., "let me trace through this with an example input" vs. "this loop correctly iterates over the array"), and (2) whether the branch-level and depth-level pruning criteria identify structurally redundant code verification steps. A strong result would show that with minimal prompt adaptation, the graph construction achieves similar node-typing F1 scores to the math domain (Table 4: review F1 0.9344). A negative result—where the constructor frequently misclassifies code reasoning steps—would define the domain boundary of the approach and motivate domain-specific adaptations to the chunking tokens (Appendix B) and graph construction prompt (Appendix A, Figure 6).

**Self-annotation: can the base model construct its own reasoning graphs?** The paper uses an external LLM (qwen-turbo) as a graph construction oracle, which introduces a capability dependency (the constructor must understand the reasoning to classify it). A natural extension is to train the base model itself to perform graph construction on its own outputs—effectively, adding a graph construction head or fine-tuning the model to produce structured reasoning traces with explicit node types and dependency edges during generation. This would eliminate the external-model dependency and enable fully self-contained training pipelines. A concrete experiment: fine-tune DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B to produce CoT in a structured format (e.g., JSON with typed steps and explicit "depends_on" fields), using the external-LLM-constructed graphs as training targets, then evaluate whether the self-annotated graphs achieve comparable node-typing accuracy to the external LLM's graphs (Table 4). If self-annotation works, the method becomes scalable to larger models and new domains without requiring a stronger external annotator. If self-annotation degrades graph quality (e.g., because the model's overthinking biases its own graph construction), it would reveal a fundamental limitation of bootstrapping approaches for reasoning analysis.

**Ablation of individual pruning criteria to decompose their contributions.** The paper applies both branch-level and depth-level pruning simultaneously but never reports their individual effects. A critical ablation would train three separate SFT models using: (a) only branch-level pruned traces (m=1.0, disabling depth-level pruning), (b) only depth-level pruned traces (k=0, disabling branch-level pruning), and (c) both criteria (the paper's current approach). Evaluating all three on the five benchmarks would reveal: (1) what fraction of the total length reduction comes from each criterion, (2) whether one criterion dominates the accuracy preservation (e.g., depth-level pruning might account for most length reduction on MATH500 while branch-level pruning drives gains on AIME), and (3) whether the two criteria are complementary (each removing different types of redundancy with minimal overlap) or redundant (largely removing the same nodes). This decomposition is essential for practitioners who want to understand *why* the pruning works and for researchers who want to refine the criteria—if branch-level pruning accounts for 80% of the benefit, future work should focus on improving descendant-count thresholds rather than depth-level heuristics.

**Difficulty-adaptive pruning: combining graph structure with difficulty estimation.** The paper applies uniform pruning thresholds regardless of problem difficulty, yet the results show accuracy degradation on the hardest benchmarks (AIME24: -0.59 points at 7B, -4.16 points at 1.5B). A natural extension is to estimate problem difficulty (following the approach from the reference example paper: sample a few answers from the base model and use pass@1 rate or verifier confidence as a difficulty signal) and apply difficulty-conditioned pruning thresholds—conservative thresholds (low k, high m) for hard problems where verification may be essential, aggressive thresholds (high k, low m) for easy problems where reflection is almost certainly redundant. A concrete experiment: on the AIME24 test set, bin problems by difficulty (based on the base model's pass@1 from 2048 samples), apply separate k and m per bin, and measure whether accuracy on the hardest bin can be preserved or improved while still achieving substantial length reduction on easier bins. If difficulty-adaptive pruning recovers the lost accuracy on hard problems while maintaining the paper's headline length reduction on easy-to-medium problems, it addresses the most significant limitation of the current approach and directly connects to the broader literature on difficulty-aware inference optimization.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Cost reduction for high-volume mathematical reasoning APIs.** Any service that processes large numbers of mathematical reasoning queries—homework help platforms, automated grading systems, competition training tools—currently pays inference costs proportional to the full CoT length that reasoning models generate. Applying this paper's method to the serving model would reduce per-query reasoning tokens by approximately 42.7% (from 8134 to 4660 tokens at the 7B scale, per Table 1) while maintaining or slightly improving answer accuracy (+1.23 points on average). For a service processing 1 million queries per month, with typical cloud inference pricing of roughly $0.50–1.00 per million output tokens for 7B-scale models, the token reduction translates to direct cost savings of roughly $2,000–4,000 per month—not transformative for a single deployment, but meaningful at scale and compounding across model sizes and query volumes. The training cost to achieve this (three-stage pipeline on 4× A800 GPUs, plus $20 for graph construction) amortizes quickly over production query volumes.

**Training data generation for self-improvement loops with reduced redundancy bias.** When reasoning models are used to generate training data for self-improvement (e.g., in STaR, ReST^EM, or rejection sampling fine-tuning pipelines), the generated CoT solutions inherit the base model's overthinking patterns—the training data is contaminated with redundant reflection that the student model then learns to reproduce. Applying graph-based pruning to the generated CoT *before* using it as training data would produce cleaner supervision signals that teach efficient reasoning from the start. The paper's Table 2 shows that pruning reduces average tokens from 6468 to 4439 in the SFT dataset while preserving reasoning structure (Table 3: 93.70% accuracy when conditioning on pruned traces). This means self-improvement pipelines could generate higher-quality training data at lower token cost: the base model generates long, redundant CoT (expensive), the graph-based pruning compresses it to the essential reasoning (cheap), and the student model trains on concise, structurally clean traces—potentially avoiding the verbosity inflation that often accompanies iterative self-training.

**Latency-sensitive interactive tutoring systems.** In applications where a student is waiting for a step-by-step solution explanation—such as an interactive math tutor that shows reasoning in real-time—every additional token of reflection increases the perceived delay before the answer arrives. The paper's qualitative case study (Figure 7) illustrates the practical impact: the base model's solution to a word problem contains multiple rounds of re-verification ("the coffee shop time is fixed at t = 24 minutes regardless...", "let me verify with equation 2...", "Wait... let me make sure...", "Alternatively, maybe I can think of it another way...") that the trained model omits entirely while preserving the core derivation. For a student reading the solution, the trained model's output arrives approximately 42.7% faster (assuming latency scales linearly with token count) and is substantially easier to follow because it presents a clean logical flow without distracting re-verification loops. This improves both the objective experience (less waiting) and the pedagogical quality (clearer exposition) without sacrificing solution correctness.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper positions its method against both efficiency-oriented training methods (O1-Pruner, TokenSkip, EfficientReasoning, AdaptThink) and open-source R1-style reasoning models (Skywork-OR1, OREAL, Light-R1). Based on the experimental evidence, the following decision guidance emerges:

- **Prefer graph-based pruning when** the base model produces long CoT with substantial explicit reflection (high frequency of "wait," "check," "verify," and other keywords from Appendix B), and the deployment requires both high accuracy (matching or exceeding the base model) and substantially lower inference cost. The method is strongest when the target domain is mathematical reasoning, where the graph construction has been validated (Table 4: review F1 0.9344) and the chunking tokens are well-matched to the discourse patterns of RL-trained reasoning models. The method also applies cleanly when a strong external LLM is available for graph construction during data preprocessing—the $20 cost (Table 2) is negligible relative to downstream training, but the dependency on the external model's capability is real and unquantified for non-math domains.

- **Prefer simpler length-penalty methods (EfficientReasoning, AdaptThink) when** the base model's reflection is less structured or the domain lacks the clear progress/review discourse markers that the graph construction relies on. AdaptThink's ability to skip reasoning entirely on easy problems is a feature the graph-based method does not offer—the pruned traces still contain (concise) reasoning for all problems. If the deployment values zero-reasoning shortcut answers for trivial queries, AdaptThink's learned "think or not" gate is a better fit. EfficientReasoning's single-stage RL objective with length penalty is simpler to implement and tune than the three-stage SFT → DPO → GRPO pipeline, making it preferable when training budget is constrained and the additional accuracy from the full pipeline (+1.59 points over EfficientReasoning at 7B, per Table 1) does not justify the extra training stages.

- **Avoid graph-based pruning when** the problem distribution is heavily skewed toward competition-level difficulty (AIME and harder). The method shows slight accuracy degradation on AIME24 at 7B (-0.59 points) and more substantial degradation at 1.5B (-4.16 points). If maintaining the absolute highest accuracy on the hardest tier is non-negotiable (e.g., in formal competition settings where every point matters), the base model's full reasoning—including its redundant but sometimes useful verification—may be preferable, or the pruning thresholds need to be tuned conservatively (lower k, higher m) specifically for hard problems, which the current paper does not explore. The method is also not validated for non-math domains, so practitioners in code generation, legal reasoning, or open-ended generation should not assume the graph construction or pruning criteria transfer without independent validation of node-typing accuracy and pruning effectiveness in their domain.
