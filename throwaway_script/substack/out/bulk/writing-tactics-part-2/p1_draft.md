Here are the takeaways from this Ryan Peterman interview (author of The Developing Dev newsletter, sharing concrete writing tactics for engineers):

**Too Long, Didn’t Read (TL;DR)**

Ryan opens with the core problem: you spend a week on a long planning doc, hit send, and "hardly anyone reads it." The solution is to treat the top of any long post as your most valuable real estate. You must put what your audience absolutely needs to know in a few lines right at the beginning. He makes this explicit by literally labeling it "tl;dr" — a pattern he uses in his own launch post template. "Most people who read my launch posts probably just read the 'tl;dr' and skim the rest," he admits. Only the people directly involved in the details read the whole thing, so you should design for that reality rather than fighting it.

**Optimize For Skimming**

The longer the post, the more each reader will only care about certain parts. People naturally skim in an **F-shaped pattern** to find what's relevant to them, so your job is to help them get more out of your writing before they abandon it entirely. Ryan offers five concrete skimmability tactics:

- **Add section headings** — These give people a quick sense of relevance as their eyes scan down the page. Rather than abandoning a wall of text, they can peek at headings and dive deeper into only the sections that seem relevant.
- **Add an index with anchor links** — For very long posts like giant planning docs, take this a step further with a table of contents. A dense list of headings lets people jump directly to what they care about.
- **Use bullets and lists** — Don't shy away from bullets when you have a list of points. They're much easier to skim than paragraphs, and their spacing separates ideas clearly, helping readers extract key points quickly.
- **Bold the main points** — Bolding draws attention to what matters most. He cautions that it's not always appropriate since "it can come off as aggressive," so use your judgment.
- **Break up long paragraphs** — "Big walls of text are difficult to skim since the words blend together." The longer the paragraph, the more likely the reader will abandon it entirely. Add line breaks where it makes sense and don't let paragraphs get too long.

To illustrate how dramatically these tactics change readability, he had ChatGPT produce a before-and-after transformation of a sample post using the techniques from the article.

His closing principle for writing growth is experiential: "The best way to get better at writing is to learn by doing. There are so many little things you only get through experience." The cycle is simple: write, reflect on how readers receive it, and iterate. He adds that it's even better if you can find someone who writes well to give you feedback — a nod to the apprenticeship model of skill-building that runs through his broader work.
