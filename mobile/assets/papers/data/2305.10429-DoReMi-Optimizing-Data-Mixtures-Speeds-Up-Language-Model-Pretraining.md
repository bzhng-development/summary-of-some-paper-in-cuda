# DoReMi: Optimizing Data Mixtures Speeds Up Language Model Pretraining

**ArXiv:** [2305.10429](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.10429)

## 🎯 Pitch

A small 280M proxy model trained without any downstream task labels can find pretraining data domain weights that let an 8B model match the accuracy of weights hand-tuned on those very downstream tasks, while improving perplexity on *every* domain—even heavily downweighted ones—and cutting training steps by 2.6×.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **Domain Reweighting with Minimax Optimization (DoReMi)**, an algorithm that uses a small proxy model (280M parameters) trained with Group DRO over domains to produce domain weights—sampling probabilities for each pretraining data domain—which then govern the training of a much larger model (8B parameters, a ~30× increase). On The Pile, DoReMi improves average few-shot downstream accuracy by 6.5 percentage points over the baseline model trained with The Pile's heuristic default domain weights and reaches that baseline accuracy with 2.6× fewer training steps, while simultaneously reducing perplexity across every domain—even those it downweights. On the GLaM dataset, DoReMi—which uses no downstream task knowledge—matches the performance of domain weights explicitly tuned on those downstream tasks, establishing that data mixture optimization can be performed agnostically with only a small proxy model and no task-specific supervision.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: We Don't Know How to Mix Pretraining Data Domains

The fundamental question this paper tackles is deceptively simple: **if you have multiple distinct data sources (domains) for pretraining a language model—web text, Wikipedia, books, code, academic papers, forums—what fraction of each should you include?** This matters enormously because the composition of pretraining data is widely recognized as one of the most consequential design decisions in building language models, yet it remains surprisingly unsystematic.

The paper opens by pointing to the status quo: The Pile, a widely-used 800GB public dataset, mixes 22 domains using "heuristically-chosen domain weights" (Section 1)—essentially, educated guesses about what proportions should work well. These guesses matter. As the authors note, prior work has established that "the composition of the pretraining data greatly affects the effectiveness of an LM" (Section 1), citing evidence from GLaM (Du et al., 2021), Chinchilla scaling laws (Hoffmann et al., 2022), and data selection work (Xie et al., 2023). A bad mixture wastes compute on unhelpful data; a good mixture can dramatically accelerate training and improve downstream performance.

The gap the paper addresses is therefore: **how do we determine domain weights in a principled way, without relying on human intuition or expensive downstream-task-driven search?**

### Why This Problem Matters—Practically and Theoretically

**Practical significance.** The cost asymmetry in language model training is enormous. Training a large model (the paper's main experiments use 8B parameters) requires substantial computational resources. Any technique that determines optimal data composition using only small models—the paper uses a 280M-parameter proxy model, roughly 3.5% of the main model's size—can amortize its cost essentially for free. The paper quantifies this: training the proxy and reference models for DoReMi constitutes only "8% of the FLOPs required to train the main 8B model" (Section 3.1). If this 8% investment yields 2.6× faster training of the large model, the ROI is dramatic.

Beyond training efficiency, data mixture also affects **model fairness and representation**. The paper explicitly connects DRO-based data reweighting to fairness concerns (Section 6): "While the standard approach of minimizing the average loss can lead to disparate performance on minority subgroups that do not contribute heavily to the loss, DRO promotes good performance on all groups via a worst-case loss." This means principled data mixing is not just about speed or accuracy—it has implications for whether models serve all users and domains equitably.

**Theoretical significance.** The paper also raises a deeper question: can we optimize pretraining data composition *without knowing what downstream tasks the model will face*? This is the pretraining/downstream distribution shift problem. If you optimize domain weights using a specific set of downstream tasks (as GLaM and PaLM do), you risk overfitting your data mixture to those tasks—your model might excel on TriviaQA but falter on tasks you didn't anticipate. DoReMi proposes an answer: optimize for worst-case excess loss across all domains, which—somewhat counterintuitively—produces models that perform well downstream without ever seeing downstream data during the optimization.

### Where Prior Approaches Fall Short

The paper identifies several distinct approaches to data composition, each with significant limitations:

**Heuristic domain weights (e.g., The Pile).** The default Pile weights were chosen by human judgment. The paper's own results demonstrate this is suboptimal: DoReMi's weights dramatically outperform these heuristics (6.5% downstream accuracy improvement; Figure 3 left). Heuristics don't scale to many domains, can't account for interactions between domains (positive/negative transfer), and embed implicit assumptions about what data is "good" that may not hold across model scales or architectures.

**Downstream-task-tuned domain weights (e.g., GLaM, PaLM).** The approach used by Du et al. (2021) and Chowdhery et al. (2022) is to train many LMs with different domain weights, evaluate them on a held-out set of downstream tasks, and select the best-performing mixture. The paper identifies two problems with this (Section 1):

> "tuning the domain weights based on a set of downstream tasks ... requires training potentially thousands of LMs on different domain weights and risks overfitting to the particular set of downstream tasks."

Training thousands of LMs is computationally prohibitive for most research groups. And overfitting to downstream tasks means the optimized weights may not generalize: a mixture tuned on QA and reading comprehension tasks might underperform on code generation or summarization tasks that weren't in the tuning set. The paper's GLaM experiments provide evidence that this overfitting concern may be real—DoReMi, without seeing downstream tasks, matches the performance of downstream-tuned weights (Figure 3 right), suggesting the downstream tuning didn't capture something uniquely valuable beyond what domain-level optimization can achieve.

**Example-level data selection (e.g., Moore-Lewis, prioritized training, C4 filtering).** Several methods operate at the example level rather than the domain level. Moore-Lewis selection (Moore and Lewis, 2010; Axelrod, 2017; Feng et al., 2022) selects individual examples with high cross-entropy difference between in-domain and out-of-domain LMs. Mindermann et al. (2022) select the top-k examples per minibatch by excess loss during training. The C4 dataset (Raffel et al., 2019) filters CommonCrawl heuristically for "Wikipedia-like" text. The paper acknowledges these approaches (Section 5) but identifies key limitations:

- **Moore-Lewis requires a target distribution**: it selects examples that look like some desired target data (e.g., in-domain text). DoReMi, in contrast, "rewights the data without a target distribution" (Section 5)—it doesn't assume you know what good data looks like a priori.
- **Online selection during training** (Mindermann et al., 2022) requires running forward passes on larger minibatches before selecting subsets for gradient updates, which is computationally expensive compared to DoReMi's offline domain weight optimization.
- **Heuristic filtering** embeds assumptions about data quality (e.g., Wikipedia is good, web text needs cleaning) that may not generalize across domains or tasks.

**Distributionally robust optimization for producing robust models (e.g., DRO-LM; Oren et al., 2019).** The closest technical precursor to DoReMi is DRO-LM, which applies Group DRO to language modeling with a baselined loss. However, Oren et al. (2019) used DRO to produce a *robust model*—the goal was to train a single model that performs well on all domains under distribution shift. DoReMi repurposes this machinery for a fundamentally different goal: the DRO training is used only to extract domain weights, and the actual deployment model is trained conventionally on resampled data using those weights. This is a critical conceptual shift. The paper also notes practical differences: DRO-LM uses a simple bigram reference model, while DoReMi uses a reference model of the same size and architecture as the proxy model "to ensure that the losses are on a similar scale" (Section 5). And DRO-LM uses online worst-case minibatch subsetting, while DoReMi uses the Group DRO optimizer (Sagawa et al., 2020) which updates all examples with weighted gradients—more computationally efficient since "online subselection is more expensive than Group DRO since it requires running forward passes on a larger minibatch ... before selecting a subset" (Section 5).

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper frames DoReMi as occupying a unique position in the data selection landscape (Section 5):

1. **Agnostic to downstream tasks**: Unlike GLaM/PaLM-style tuning, DoReMi "uses no knowledge of downstream tasks" to set domain weights. This is the paper's central claim to novelty—it's "the first to show that reweighting the data according to losses of a small proxy LM can improve the training efficiency of much larger LM" without downstream supervision.

2. **Domain-level rather than example-level**: This is a pragmatic choice that balances granularity with simplicity. Domain-level reweighting is computationally cheaper than per-example selection and integrates cleanly with standard training pipelines (just resample the dataset). The paper acknowledges this is "coarse-grained control" and suggests fine-grained domains as future work (Section 6), but the domain-level approach already yields substantial gains.

3. **Offline rather than online**: DoReMi optimizes domain weights before training the main model, allowing the large model to be trained with a standard pipeline on statically resampled data. This contrasts with online selection methods (Mindermann et al., 2022) that modify the training loop, which complicates distributed training and requires careful handling of data loading.

4. **DRO as a tool for data optimization, not model robustness**: The paper explicitly differentiates itself from prior DRO work: "in contrast to these DRO methods which aim to produce robust models, we use DRO to optimize the data for training larger models more efficiently" (Section 5). The domain weights—typically averaged over the DRO training trajectory—are the product, not the DRO-trained model itself.

5. **Transferable across scales**: A key empirical finding (and a core motivation for the approach) is that domain weights optimized with a 280M proxy model transfer effectively to an 8B model—a 30× scale increase. This transferability is what makes the approach practical: you pay the cost of domain weight optimization only once, on a small model, and reap benefits at scales where training costs dominate. The paper explicitly flags understanding why this transfer works as an open question (Section 6): "Understanding why the domain weights can be transferred across scales and the limits of how far these domain weights transfer are important questions to answer in future work."

### The Key Design Intuition: Excess Loss Captures "Learnable But Not Yet Learned"

The paper's conceptual framework centers on a single quantity: **excess loss**, defined as $\ell_{\theta}(x) - \ell_{\text{ref}}(x)$—the difference between the proxy model's loss and the reference model's loss on example $x$ (Section 2). The authors provide an intuitive interpretation:

> "Examples with higher excess loss are those where the reference model achieves low loss (such that the example is 'learnable') but the proxy model still has high loss. Examples with low excess loss may be very high entropy (i.e., optimal loss is high, and thus the reference loss is high) or very low entropy (i.e., easy to learn, and thus the proxy loss is low)."

This is a sophisticated filtering criterion. It automatically downweights:
- **Already-learned examples** (low proxy loss—no headroom for improvement)
- **Fundamentally noisy/unpredictable examples** (high reference loss—even the reference model struggles, suggesting the example is inherently high-entropy)

And upweights:
- **Learnable but unlearned examples** (low reference loss, high proxy loss—the reference model shows this data has learnable structure, but the proxy hasn't captured it yet)

This explains why DoReMi can improve perplexity on *all* domains while downweighting some: it reallocates training budget from domains that are either too easy (already learned) or too noisy (inherently unpredictable) toward domains in the "sweet spot" of being learnable but not yet learned, where additional training examples produce the largest marginal improvement. The paper formalizes this intuition with a simple Bayesian example (Appendix D) showing analytically that reweighting away from very high-entropy and very low-entropy domains can improve parameter estimation error on all domains simultaneously.

### Recapping the Motivation: Why This Work Matters Now

The paper arrives at a moment when the field is increasingly aware that **data quality and composition matter as much as model scale**, but systematic methods for optimizing data lag behind methods for scaling architectures and training algorithms. The Chinchilla scaling laws (Hoffmann et al., 2022) established that for a given compute budget, there's an optimal ratio of model parameters to training tokens—but assumed a fixed data distribution. DoReMi extends this line of thinking: given a fixed total compute budget for training, we should also optimize *which* tokens we train on, not just *how many*. The paper's 2.6× training speedup is a concrete demonstration that data optimization can be as impactful as model scaling optimizations, and that small proxy models can guide these decisions at negligible relative cost.

This positions DoReMi as a bridge between two research threads: the scaling laws literature (concerned with optimal allocation of compute) and the data selection literature (concerned with identifying valuable training examples). By showing that domain weights optimized via DRO transfer across scales, the paper suggests that data optimization and model scaling can be decoupled—an insight with practical implications for how language model training pipelines should be designed.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

DoReMi is **a two-stage algorithm** that first uses a small proxy language model (280M parameters) trained with Group DRO to determine optimal sampling weights for each data domain, then uses those weights to resample a training dataset for a much larger language model (8B parameters). It solves the problem of **how to mix heterogeneous pretraining data domains without requiring any downstream task knowledge**, by optimizing for worst-case excess loss across domains so that the resulting mixture improves both perplexity on all domains and downstream accuracy, while requiring only a fraction of the total training compute for the domain weight optimization step.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture

The system operates in three sequential stages:

1. **Reference Model Training**: A small 280M-parameter model is trained conventionally on the initial reference domain weights—the default, heuristic weights that the dataset ships with (e.g., The Pile's hand-tuned proportions). This model captures a baseline for "how learnable" each example is: examples the reference model fits well are likely learnable (low reference loss), while examples it struggles on are either inherently noisy or require more capacity.

2. **Proxy Model Training with Group DRO**: A second small 280M-parameter model is trained from scratch using Group DRO, which dynamically updates domain weights `$\alpha_t$` at each training step based on the per-domain excess loss—the gap between the proxy model's current loss and the reference model's loss on each domain. Domains with higher excess loss get higher weight, scaling up their contribution to the gradient update. Critically, DoReMi does not use the Group DRO-trained proxy model itself; it extracts only the **average domain weights** `$\bar{\alpha} = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^T \alpha_t$` computed over the entire training trajectory.

3. **Main Model Training**: The averaged domain weights `$\bar{\alpha}$` define a new sampling distribution over domains. The training data is resampled according to these weights, and a large model (8B parameters, roughly 30× the proxy model size) is trained from scratch using standard next-token prediction on this resampled dataset. No DRO, no dynamic reweighting—just conventional LM training on a better-curated data mixture.

Information flows linearly: initial domain weights → reference model → reference losses → (proxy model + Group DRO updates → domain weight trajectory → averaged domain weights) → resampled dataset → large model. The two small model training runs (reference + proxy) constitute approximately 8% of the FLOPs required to train the main 8B model.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the formal minimax objective (Equation 1) that defines what DoReMi optimizes—the worst-case excess loss over domains—and why this objective naturally produces domain weights that benefit all domains.
- **Second**, the Group DRO training loop (Algorithm 1) and its mechanics: how domain weights are updated at each step, what the excess loss is computed on, and how the proxy model gradients are rescaled—since this is the algorithmic core that actually produces the domain weights.
- **Third**, the key design choices that make DoReMi practical: why the reference model and proxy model are the same size, why losses are baselined rather than used raw, why loss reweighting during DRO training differs from resampling for the main model, and why iterated DoReMi can further improve weights.
- **Fourth**, the iterated DoReMi extension: how multiple rounds of using the previous round's output weights as reference weights converge to a fixed point, and when this helps versus when it's unnecessary.
- **Fifth**, the computational cost model: exactly how the 8% overhead figure is computed, what dominates the cost, and why the approach scales to any main model size.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **methodological paper** whose core idea is that Group DRO—traditionally used to produce robust models—can be repurposed to extract optimized domain weights for training larger, non-robust models, and that these weights transfer effectively across model scales.

#### The Minimax Objective: Formalizing "Domain Weights for Worst-Case Improvement"

The paper defines domain weights `$\alpha \in \Delta^k$` as a probability distribution over `$k$` domains, with `$\Delta^k$` denoting the `$k$`-dimensional simplex (non-negative entries summing to 1). A distribution `$P_\alpha$` over training examples is induced by `$P_\alpha = \sum_{i=1}^k \alpha_i \cdot \text{unif}(D_i)$`, where `$\text{unif}(D_i) = \frac{1}{|D_i|}\sum_{x \in D_i} \delta_x$` is the uniform distribution over examples in domain `$i$`, and `$\delta_x$` is a point mass at example `$x$`. In other words, to sample from `$P_\alpha$`, you first pick a domain `$i$` with probability `$\alpha_i$`, then pick an example uniformly at random from domain `$i$`'s data.

The core optimization problem that DoReMi's proxy model training solves is:

$$\min_{\theta} \max_{\alpha \in \Delta^k} L(\theta, \alpha) := \sum_{i=1}^k \alpha_i \cdot \left[ \frac{1}{\sum_{x \in D_i} |x|} \sum_{x \in D_i} \ell_\theta(x) - \ell_{\text{ref}}(x) \right]$$

where:
- `$\theta$` are the parameters of the proxy model being trained,
- `$\alpha = (\alpha_1, \ldots, \alpha_k)$` are the domain weights (a probability vector over the `$k$` domains),
- `$\ell_\theta(x) = -\log p_\theta(x)$` is the negative log-likelihood per-example (summed over tokens) assigned by the proxy model to example `$x$`,
- `$\ell_{\text{ref}}(x) = -\log p_{\text{ref}}(x)$` is the negative log-likelihood assigned by the reference model to the same example,
- `$|x|$` is the number of tokens in example `$x$`, so the inner sum normalizes the per-domain excess loss by the total number of tokens in that domain's chunked dataset—this ensures domains with longer individual examples are not unfairly upweighted or downweighted compared to domains with shorter examples.

**What it computes:** The objective `$L(\theta, \alpha)$` is a weighted sum over domains of the per-domain average excess loss—the difference between the proxy model's current loss and the reference model's loss, averaged over all tokens in that domain. The inner maximization over `$\alpha$` finds the distribution that puts all weight on the single domain with the highest excess loss, making the overall objective equal to `$\max_i [\text{excess loss on domain }i]$`. The outer minimization over `$\theta$` then updates the proxy model to reduce this worst-case excess loss.

**Why this form:** This is a **baselined minimax objective**. Using raw loss `$\ell_\theta(x)$` rather than excess loss `$\ell_\theta(x) - \ell_{\text{ref}}(x)$` would cause the objective to focus on domains that are inherently high-entropy (where even the optimal model would have high loss), not domains where the model has room to improve. The reference model subtraction removes this confounding factor: domains where the reference model already achieves low loss are "learnable," and if the proxy model's loss on such a domain is still high, that domain genuinely needs more training focus. Conversely, domains where the reference loss is high are likely inherently unpredictable—no amount of additional training examples will reduce the loss substantially—and the subtraction prevents those domains from dominating the optimization. This is the same insight that drove DRO-LM (Oren et al., 2019), but DoReMi uses it to extract domain weights rather than to produce a robust model.

An important subtlety: the excess loss can be negative if the proxy model outperforms the reference model on some examples (the proxy model's loss is lower than the reference model's). The paper clips the per-token excess loss at zero before averaging, since the Group DRO optimizer (Sagawa et al., 2020) requires a non-negative loss signal. This clipping is specified explicitly in Algorithm 1: `$\lambda_t[i] \leftarrow \frac{1}{\sum_{x \in B \cap D_i} |x|} \sum_{x \in B \cap D_i} \sum_{j=1}^{|x|} \max\{\ell_{\theta_{t-1}, j}(x) - \ell_{\text{ref}, j}(x), 0\}$`. The clipping means DoReMi only penalizes domains where the proxy model is *underperforming* the reference, not domains where it has surpassed the reference—the latter are already adequately learned.

#### The Group DRO Training Loop (Step 2): How Domain Weights Emerge

Algorithm 1 in the paper provides the pseudocode for the core domain weight optimization. The algorithm operates over `$T$` training steps, interleaving two updates: a domain weight update (via exponentiated gradient ascent) and a proxy model weight update (via standard optimization on a reweighted loss).

**Initialization.** The proxy model weights `$\theta_0$` are initialized randomly (from scratch—no warm-start from the reference model). The domain weights are initialized uniformly: `$\alpha_0 = \frac{1}{k} \mathbf{1}$`, giving equal probability to each of the `$k$` domains. The reference model `$p_{\text{ref}}$` is already trained and frozen—its parameters never change during the DRO loop.

**Minibatch sampling.** At each step `$t$`, a minibatch `$B = \{x_1, \ldots, x_j\}$` of size `$b$` (where `$b$` is the batch size in examples, set to 512 in all experiments) is sampled from `$P_u$`, the distribution with uniform domain weights `$u = \frac{1}{k}\mathbf{1}$`. This is a critical design choice: the minibatch is always sampled uniformly, regardless of the current domain weights `$\alpha_t$` or the reference domain weights `$\alpha_{\text{ref}}$`. The domain weights only affect the training objective's reweighting, not the data actually seen. Why? If the minibatch were sampled according to `$\alpha_t$`, domains with near-zero weight would never appear in training, making it impossible to estimate their excess loss accurately and potentially causing their weights to drift to zero permanently (a feedback loop). Uniform minibatch sampling ensures every domain is represented in every batch, so excess losses can be estimated for all domains at every step.

**Computing per-domain excess losses.** For each domain `$i \in \{1, \ldots, k\}$`, the algorithm computes:

$$\lambda_t[i] = \frac{1}{\sum_{x \in B \cap D_i} |x|} \sum_{x \in B \cap D_i} \sum_{j=1}^{|x|} \max\{\ell_{\theta_{t-1}, j}(x) - \ell_{\text{ref}, j}(x), 0\}$$

where `$\ell_{\theta_{t-1}, j}(x) = -\log p_{\theta_{t-1}}(x_j \mid x_1, \ldots, x_{j-1})$` is the token-level loss of the proxy model at position `$j$`, and `$\ell_{\text{ref}, j}(x)$` is the corresponding reference model token-level loss. The normalization by total tokens in the minibatch subset for domain `$i$` ensures that the excess loss is a per-token average within that domain, making comparisons across domains of different sample sizes and sequence lengths meaningful.

**Updating domain weights via exponentiated gradient ascent.** The domain weight vector is updated using the multiplicative weights algorithm—the standard online learning update for Group DRO (Nemirovski et al., 2009; Sagawa et al., 2020):

$$\alpha_t' = \alpha_{t-1} \odot \exp(\eta \lambda_t)$$

where `$\odot$` is element-wise multiplication, `$\exp$` is element-wise exponential, `$\eta$` is the domain weight learning rate (set to `$\eta = 1$` in all experiments), and `$\lambda_t$` is the vector of per-domain excess losses. This update increases the weight of domains with high excess loss exponentially: if domain `$i$` has twice the excess loss of domain `$j$`, its unnormalized weight will grow by `$e^{2\lambda} / e^{\lambda} = e^{\lambda}$` relative to domain `$j$` in a single step.

After the multiplicative update, the weights are renormalized and smoothed:

$$\alpha_t = (1 - c) \frac{\alpha_t'}{\sum_{i=1}^k \alpha_t'[i]} + c \cdot u$$

where `$c$` is a smoothing parameter (set to `$c = 10^{-3}$` in all experiments) and `$u$` is the uniform distribution over domains. The smoothing adds a small uniform component to prevent any domain weight from reaching exactly zero—this is a numerical stability measure that ensures every domain always has at least `$c/k$` probability, preventing division-by-zero issues in loss weighting and ensuring all domains remain represented in gradient estimates.

**Why this update rule?** Exponentiated gradient ascent is the standard algorithm for online learning over the simplex—it is well-studied and enjoys no-regret guarantees (the average of the iterates converges to an approximate solution of the minimax problem). The paper explicitly does not claim that any particular step's `$\alpha_t$` is "correct"; instead, it returns the arithmetic mean `$\bar{\alpha} = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T \alpha_t$`, following Nemirovski et al. (2009). This averaging is crucial: at any single step, the domain weights may be noisy (based on a single minibatch's excess loss estimates), but the average over many steps smooths out this noise and converges to a stable estimate of the optimal domain weights.

**Updating the proxy model.** After the domain weights are updated, the proxy model parameters `$\theta_t$` are updated using a standard optimizer (Adafactor in all experiments) on the reweighted objective:

$$L(\theta_{t-1}, \alpha_t) = \sum_{i=1}^k \alpha_t[i] \cdot \frac{1}{\sum_{x \in B \cap D_i} |x|} \sum_{x \in B \cap D_i} \sum_{j=1}^{|x|} \max\{\ell_{\theta_{t-1}, j}(x) - \ell_{\text{ref}, j}(x), 0\}$$

The gradient update is: `$\theta_t \leftarrow \text{Adafactor}(\nabla_\theta L(\theta_{t-1}, \alpha_t), \theta_{t-1})$`. The key mechanism: domains with higher `$\alpha_t[i]$` contribute proportionally more to the objective, and therefore their gradients dominate the parameter update. This is fundamentally different from resampling—the proxy model sees all examples in the minibatch but weights their contributions differently. The proxy model gets pulled harder toward reducing loss on domains where the excess loss is high.

**The final output.** After `$T$` steps (set to 200k for The Pile and 300k for the GLaM dataset—matching the number of steps the main model will be trained for), the algorithm returns `$\bar{\alpha} = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T \alpha_t$`. These averaged domain weights are then used to construct a new training distribution `$P_{\bar{\alpha}}$` by resampling the original dataset: for each example slot in the main model's training data, sample a domain `$i \sim \bar{\alpha}$`, then sample an example uniformly from `$D_i$`. This resampled dataset is what the large 8B model trains on.

#### Key Design Decisions and Their Justifications

The paper makes several non-obvious design choices that distinguish DoReMi from prior work:

**Why the proxy and reference models are the same size and architecture.** The baseline loss `$\ell_{\text{ref}}(x)$` must be comparable in magnitude to `$\ell_\theta(x)$` for the excess loss to be meaningful. If the reference model were a trivial model (e.g., a bigram model as in DRO-LM), its loss would be very high on all examples, and the excess loss would be dominated by the entropy of the data distribution rather than by the proxy model's learning progress. By using a reference model of the same capacity as the proxy, the loss scales are matched—the excess loss genuinely captures "how much the proxy model can still improve relative to a peer model that has already been trained conventionally." Both models are 280M parameters in the paper's main experiments, using the same Transformer architecture (12 layers, 12 attention heads, 64-dimensional attention heads, 768-dimensional model, 3072-dimensional feedforward hidden layer, 256k vocabulary).

**Why the reference model uses the initial domain weights `$\alpha_{\text{ref}}$`.** The reference model is trained conventionally on the reference domain weights (The Pile's defaults, or uniform for GLaM) for the same number of steps `$T$` and batch size `$b$` that the main model will use. This encodes the "baseline difficulty" of each domain under the status-quo data mixture: a domain where the reference model achieves low loss is one where the standard training recipe already works well, so further optimization might not help much; a domain where the reference model achieves high loss is one where either the data is inherently noisy or the reference weights don't provide enough training signal for that domain.

**Why loss reweighting during the DRO step differs from resampling for the main model.** In step 2, the proxy model is trained by **reweighting the loss**—every example in a uniformly-sampled minibatch contributes to the gradient, but examples from high-`$\alpha$` domains contribute more. In step 3, the main model is trained by **resampling the data**—constructing a new static dataset with the optimized proportions and training conventionally. These are not equivalent. Reweighting can lead to high-variance gradient estimates when domain weights are extreme (a few domains dominate, making gradient estimates for low-weight domains very noisy). The paper reports (Section 4, Table 3b) that the 1B proxy model trained under loss reweighting actually underperforms the 1B baseline, while the 1B main model trained on resampled data significantly outperforms it—direct evidence that the resampling approach is more stable, especially at larger scales. The authors hypothesize this mismatch explains why larger proxy models don't improve DoReMi's performance (Figure 6 left): "the mismatch between the proxy and main model training (loss reweighting vs. resampling) explains their performance difference and therefore a resampling-based Group DRO optimizer may improve DoReMi for larger proxy models."

**Why domain weights are averaged over the entire trajectory rather than taking the final iterate.** The min-max problem in Equation 1 has no single fixed solution—domain weights can oscillate as the proxy model improves and changes which domains are underfit. The theoretical justification comes from Nemirovski et al. (2009), who showed that for convex-concave saddle-point problems, the average of the iterates converges to an approximate solution with sublinear regret, while the final iterate may not converge at all. The paper observes this empirically (Appendix Figure 8): domain weights change quickly in the first 50k steps, then relatively stabilize but continue to drift slowly. Averaging over the full trajectory integrates out this drift and produces stable weights.

**Why the smoothing parameter `$c$` is small (1e-3) and not extensively tuned.** The smoothing prevents degenerate zero weights but must be small enough not to drown out the DRO signal. At `$c = 10^{-3}$`, a domain with zero excess loss for the entire trajectory would receive at minimum a weight of `$c/k$` (e.g., about `$4.5 \times 10^{-5}$` for The Pile's 22 domains), which is negligible compared to typical optimized weights (e.g., 0.6 for Pile-CC). The paper states it "did not extensively tune" `$\eta$` or `$c$`, suggesting the algorithm is robust to these choices—the exponentiated gradient update is inherently adaptive, and the averaging over steps further reduces sensitivity to the exact update rate.

#### Iterated DoReMi: Bootstrapping Better Reference Weights

The paper introduces an iterative variant of DoReMi where the optimized domain weights from one round become the reference domain weights for the next round:

> "We extend DoReMi by running it for multiple rounds, setting the reference domain weights `$\alpha_{\text{ref}}$` for the next round to be `$\bar{\alpha}$` from the previous round."

The process:

1. **Round 1**: Reference model trained on initial `$\alpha_{\text{ref}}$` (e.g., uniform or heuristic weights) → DRO proxy trained → output `$\bar{\alpha}^{(1)}$`.
2. **Round 2**: Reference model trained on `$\bar{\alpha}^{(1)}$` → new DRO proxy trained (with this new reference) → output `$\bar{\alpha}^{(2)}$`.
3. **Continue** until convergence: `$\|\bar{\alpha}^{(r)} - \bar{\alpha}^{(r-1)}\|_\infty < 10^{-3}$`.

**Why iteration helps.** The first round's reference model is trained on potentially suboptimal domain weights. Its losses `$\ell_{\text{ref}}(x)$` reflect the learnability of examples under that suboptimal mixture. If a domain is difficult because the reference mixture under-allocates examples to it, the reference loss will be high, and the excess loss `$\ell_\theta(x) - \ell_{\text{ref}}(x)$` may be low or negative—the domain won't get upweighted. But after the first round, the optimized weights allocate more examples to that domain, making its reference loss in round 2 more accurate—it now genuinely reflects learnability rather than data starvation. This allows the second round's DRO to make finer-grained adjustments.

**Empirical convergence.** On the GLaM dataset (8 domains), iterated DoReMi converges in 3 rounds (Table 2). The round 2 and round 3 weights are nearly identical (e.g., Wikipedia stays at 0.05, Filtered webpages at 0.51, etc.). Notably, round 1 weights are quite different from rounds 2–3 (Wikipedia: 0.09 → 0.05; Conversations: 0.10 → 0.22), demonstrating that iteration moves the weights substantially from the uniform initialization. Rounds 2–3 match the pattern of downstream-tuned weights (Filtered webpages at 0.42, Conversations at 0.27, Books at 0.20 for downstream-tuned), supporting the claim that DoReMi recovers similar weights to task-specific optimization without using task data.

**On The Pile**, the paper does not report iterated DoReMi results (only single-round DoReMi), likely because the 22-domain setting already yields substantial gains from a single round (2.6× speedup) and each round costs an additional 8% of the main model's training compute. The marginal benefit of iteration is dataset-dependent: more domains and more divergent initial weights make iteration more valuable.

#### Computational Cost Model: The 8% Overhead

The paper carefully quantifies the computational cost of domain weight optimization relative to training the main model:

> "We train two 280M models (the reference and proxy models) to optimize the domain weights. This is 8% of the FLOPs required to train the main 8B model."

The cost breakdown is:

- **Reference model training**: One 280M model trained for `$T$` steps at batch size `$b$` = 512, sequence length 1024. This is a standard LM training run.
- **Proxy model training (DRO)**: Another 280M model trained for `$T$` steps at the same batch size and sequence length. The DRO loop adds overhead for computing `$\lambda_t$` (per-domain excess losses) and updating `$\alpha_t$`, but this is negligible compared to forward/backward passes through the proxy model.
- **Total**: Two 280M-parameter training runs.

Since FLOPs per training step scale approximately linearly with parameter count (assuming identical architecture width/depth ratios and sequence lengths), the ratio is roughly `$2 \times 280\text{M} / 8000\text{M} = 0.07$`, or 7%. The paper rounds to 8%, likely accounting for slight differences in architecture efficiency (the 8B model uses 32 layers and 4096 model dimension versus the 280M model's 12 layers and 768 dimension, so the per-parameter FLOP scaling isn't perfectly linear).

**Why this overhead is acceptable.** The 8% investment yields a 2.6× reduction in training steps for the main model, meaning the net compute is roughly `$0.08 + 1/2.6 = 0.08 + 0.385 = 0.465$` of the baseline cost. The main model training time is more than halved. Additionally, the optimized domain weights can be reused for any number of future main model training runs—if you train 10 different 8B model variants (different architectures, different hyperparameters, different downstream fine-tuning), you pay the 8% once and save 60% on each of the 10 runs.

**What the 8% does NOT include.** The paper does not amortize the cost of the initial reference model training into the main model's cost in any way. The reference model is used only to provide baseline losses; its weights are discarded after step 2. Also, the cost does not include any hyperparameter tuning for the DRO process itself—the paper used `$\eta = 1$` and `$c = 10^{-3}$` without sweep, but an extensive hyperparameter search would increase the overhead.

**Why the proxy model size (280M) was chosen.** The paper provides indirect justification through the ablation in Section 4 (Figure 6 left): increasing proxy model size from 70M to 280M improves the resulting 8B model's downstream accuracy, but increasing to 1B does not further improve—and may even slightly degrade—performance. The 280M point is therefore a sweet spot: large enough to produce useful domain weights that transfer well, but not so large that training the proxy dominates the total cost or that the DRO optimizer's stability degrades. At 280M, the proxy is roughly 3.5% of the 8B model's parameter count, making it genuinely "small" relative to the target scale.

#### From Domain Weights to Resampled Data: The Step 3 Transition

Once `$\bar{\alpha}$` is computed, constructing the training data for the main model is straightforward and does not require any special infrastructure:

1. For each of the `$N$` training examples needed (where `$N = T \times b$`, with `$T = 200\text{k}$` for The Pile and `$T = 300\text{k}$` for GLaM, `$b = 512$`), sample a domain index `$i \in \{1, \ldots, k\}$` with probability `$\bar{\alpha}_i$`.
2. From domain `$i$`'s pre-chunked data `$D_i$`, sample an example uniformly at random (with replacement, though the paper's implementation likely uses a shuffled epoch over each domain's data to avoid exact duplicates within an epoch).
3. Pack examples together across domains into sequences of exactly 1024 tokens to minimize padding (the paper notes this in Appendix C: "To reduce the amount of padding tokens, we made an effort to pack examples (possibly from different domains) together into the same sequence").

The resulting dataset `$D_{\bar{\alpha}}$` is a static, shuffled collection of training sequences with the domain mixture `$\bar{\alpha}$`. The main model training loop is **identical** to the baseline—same model architecture, same optimizer (Adafactor), same learning rate schedule (exponential decay from `$10^{-3}$` to `$10^{-4}$` with linear warmup for 6% of steps, gradient clipping to norm 1, weight decay `$10^{-2}$`), same batch size and sequence length. The only difference is the data distribution.

**Why resampling rather than reweighting at main model scale.** The paper's experiments suggest that resampling is more stable than loss reweighting for large models, particularly when domain weights are skewed (e.g., Pile-CC getting 0.6 weight in DoReMi 280M). Loss reweighting with such extreme weights would mean that all but one or two domains contribute negligibly to the gradient, effectively discarding most of the data. Resampling, by contrast, gives every selected example equal weight in the loss—the "reweighting" happens at the data construction stage, not in the loss function. This means the main model's optimizer receives balanced gradient signals across examples, even though the overall domain mixture is skewed. The resampling approach is also more compatible with standard distributed training infrastructure, which expects a pre-shuffled dataset with uniform sampling within the dataset.

**What happens to domains with near-zero weight.** DoReMi can assign very low weights to some domains (e.g., DM Mathematics goes from a baseline 0.020 to 0.002 for DoReMi 280M; PubMed Central goes from 0.107 to 0.005). In the resampled dataset, these domains appear very rarely. The paper reports that despite this drastic downweighting, the main model's perplexity on these domains actually *improves* (Figure 4, Table 4). The proposed explanation (Appendix D) is that extremely low-entropy domains (where token distributions are near-deterministic) require very few examples to learn, and extremely high-entropy domains (where the empirical distribution is close to the model's prior) also benefit little from additional examples. Reallocating these examples to medium-entropy domains where the model has more to learn yields universal improvements through positive transfer—the model learns better representations that incidentally improve performance on downweighted domains too.

#### Practical Implementation Details from the Paper

Several implementation-level details are scattered across sections but matter for understanding the full algorithm:

- **Token-level loss normalization**: In step 2 (Algorithm 1), the per-domain excess loss `$\lambda_t[i]$` is normalized by the total number of tokens `$\sum_{x \in B \cap D_i} |x|$` in the minibatch subset for that domain. This ensures that a domain with many short examples and a domain with a few long examples are compared on equal footing—what matters is the average excess loss per token, not per example.

- **Chunking and packing**: All datasets are preprocessed by chunking into fixed-length sequences of 1024 tokens using a SentencePiece tokenizer with 256k vocabulary. When packing examples from different domains into the same sequence, the domain membership for each token must be tracked for the per-domain loss computation in step 2, which the paper notes doing at a "per-token level" (Appendix C).

- **Reference model dropout**: The paper does not specify whether dropout is used during reference model training. Given standard practice for decoder-only Transformers, dropout is likely used (the main model presumably uses some dropout), but the reference model's predictions `$p_{\text{ref}}(x_j \mid x_1, \ldots, x_{j-1})$` are computed with dropout disabled (evaluation mode) to produce deterministic reference losses. This standard distinction is implicit but important: if reference losses were computed with active dropout, the reference loss would be noisy and the excess loss would conflate model uncertainty with learnability.

- **Excess loss clipping before averaging**: The max with zero is applied at the token level before averaging over tokens and examples. This means a single domain where the proxy outperforms the reference on 80% of tokens but underperforms on 20% will still show a positive excess loss for those 20%, causing some upweighting, but the upweighting will be moderate.

- **Domain weight step size `$\eta = 1$`**: This is a standard choice for exponentiated gradient descent—it corresponds to the theoretically optimal setting for the online mirror descent view of the algorithm. The multiplicative nature of the update (`$\alpha' \leftarrow \alpha \odot \exp(\lambda)$`) means that even with `$\eta = 1$`, weight changes are proportional to excess losses on a log scale, which prevents extreme oscillations while still allowing rapid adaptation when the excess loss distribution changes.

- **Proxy model optimizer**: All experiments use Adafactor (Shazeer and Stern, 2018), which is memory-efficient and the standard choice for large Transformer training. The paper does not specify Adafactor hyperparameters (e.g., factored second moments), implying default settings were used.

- **No validation-based early stopping for the proxy model**: The proxy model is trained for the full `$T$` steps regardless of validation loss. Since the product is the domain weight trajectory, not the model itself, there's no concept of "overfitting" the proxy—you want the full trajectory to capture how excess losses evolve throughout training. This is fundamentally different from standard model training where early stopping prevents overfitting.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: DRO as a Data Engineering Tool, Not a Robustness Objective

The paper's most fundamental conceptual move is repurposing distributionally robust optimization (DRO)—a framework designed to produce models that perform well under worst-case distribution shift—into a **data mixture optimization tool** whose output is not a model but a set of domain weights. This inverts the standard DRO narrative. In prior work (Oren et al., 2019; Sagawa et al., 2020), the DRO-trained model *is* the artifact: you train with worst-case loss minimization and deploy the resulting robust model, accepting that it may sacrifice average-case performance to protect against tail groups. DoReMi discards the DRO-trained proxy model entirely—its weights are never used for inference—and retains only the dynamic domain weights `$\alpha_t$` that the DRO optimizer produced along the way. The actual deployment model is then trained with standard (non-robust) ERM on data resampled according to these weights.

This reframing has a non-obvious consequence: **the proxy model doesn't need to be good**. In fact, Section 4 shows that the 1B proxy model trained under Group DRO substantially underperforms both the 1B baseline model and the 1B main model trained on resampled data (Table 3b: proxy log-perplexity 2.02 vs. baseline 1.94 vs. DoReMi main 1.83). Yet the domain weights extracted from that "bad" proxy model still yield a main model that achieves baseline accuracy ~2× faster. This decoupling—poor proxy, good weights—is the clearest evidence that DoReMi's DRO step functions as a *diagnostic* rather than a *training* procedure. The Group DRO optimizer is not trying to produce a competent language model; it is trying to discover which domains present the largest gap between what is learnable (reference model loss) and what is currently learned (proxy model loss), and the domain weight trajectory encodes this gap structure.

Prior work treated DRO as an end in itself—train robustly, deploy robustly. DoReMi treats DRO as an intermediate measurement apparatus that probes the dataset's structure through the lens of excess loss dynamics, extracts a static data recipe, and then gets out of the way. This is a fundamental shift in how to think about DRO in the context of large-scale training: the optimizer's trajectory matters more than its final parameter state. It also explains why the paper can use the same reference and proxy model sizes (both 280M): the reference model is not a teacher in a distillation sense; it is a calibration device that establishes what "learnable" means at this scale, so that the proxy's struggles can be interpreted as genuine headroom rather than capacity mismatch.

### Innovation 2: Excess Loss as a Difficulty Estimator That Disentangles Learnability from Noise

The paper's diagnostic innovation is the specific choice of **excess loss** `$\ell_\theta(x) - \ell_{\text{ref}}(x)$`—rather than raw loss, gradient norm, or uncertainty—as the signal for determining domain importance. This is not an incremental choice; it is the intellectual engine that makes the whole approach work and the reason DoReMi can improve perplexity on *all* domains while downweighting some.

The key property: excess loss automatically distinguishes two very different reasons a domain might have high raw loss. A domain could be **inherently high-entropy** (e.g., a corpus of random numbers, or a domain with enormous vocabulary diversity where the optimal next-token distribution is near-uniform). In this case, the reference model's loss will also be high—the domain is simply unpredictable—and the excess loss will be low. The DRO optimizer correctly ignores such domains because allocating more training examples cannot substantially reduce the irreducible entropy. Conversely, a domain could have high loss because it contains **learnable structure that the proxy model hasn't yet captured** (e.g., technical jargon in PubMed abstracts, mathematical notation in StackExchange). Here the reference model achieves low loss (it learned the structure during its conventional training), but the proxy model—trained under uniform sampling where this domain may be under-represented—still has high loss. The excess loss is large, and DRO correctly upweights this domain.

This disentanglement is what enables DoReMi to downweight domains without hurting them. The paper's Appendix D provides the cleanest illustration: in a simple 3-domain unigram model, one domain has zero entropy (deterministic next token), one has intermediate entropy, and one has maximal entropy (uniform token distribution). The optimal data allocation allocates zero samples to the maximal-entropy domain (the prior already matches the truth—no learning is possible), a small number to the zero-entropy domain (rapidly learned), and the bulk to the intermediate-entropy domain where samples genuinely reduce parameter error. DoReMi's DRO loop recovers this pattern exactly.

Prior work on data selection—Moore-Lewis filtering, prioritized training (Mindermann et al., 2022), uncertainty-based selection (Coleman et al., 2020)—all used variants of "model loss" as the importance signal, without the baselining that separates learnability from noise. Moore-Lewis selection (Moore and Lewis, 2010), for instance, selects examples with high cross-entropy difference between an in-domain model and a general-domain model, which implicitly assumes the in-domain distribution is the target and everything else is noise. DoReMi makes no such assumption: it baselines against a peer model trained on the same data mixture, so the target is "whatever is learnable from the current mixture," not "whatever looks like some pre-specified target domain." This is why DoReMi can work agnostically with respect to downstream tasks—it optimizes for domains where learning progress is possible, not for domains that resemble a desired test distribution.

The ablation in Figure 6 (right) confirms that neither component alone suffices. Using only proxy loss `$\ell_\theta(x)$` (preferring "hardest" domains for the proxy) catastrophically fails—worst-case log-perplexity degrades from 2.39 (baseline) to 2.66, and no domain improves (Table 7). Using only negative reference loss `$-\ell_{\text{ref}}(x)$` (preferring "easiest" domains for the reference) is even worse (worst-case 4.27). Only the combination—excess loss—captures the intersection of "structure exists" and "structure not yet learned."

### Innovation 3: Empirical Demonstration That Domain Weights Transfer Across Model Scales

The paper provides the first systematic evidence that **data mixture recipes optimized at one model scale transfer effectively to substantially larger scales**—a finding with significant practical and theoretical implications. The headline result: domain weights computed using a 280M-parameter proxy model improve the training of an 8B-parameter model (30× larger) by 2.6× in training steps and 6.5 percentage points in downstream accuracy (Figure 3, left). The 70M proxy also transfers effectively (22/22 domains improve perplexity at 8B; Table 4), as does the 150M proxy.

This is not an obvious result. One could reasonably expect that optimal domain weights are scale-dependent: a small model might benefit most from simple, repetitive domains that help it learn basic syntax, while a large model might need diverse, complex domains to push its capacity. The transfer result suggests instead that the relative "learnability" ranking of domains—which domains contain structure that is learnable but not yet learned—is surprisingly stable across model capacities. A domain that represents low-hanging fruit for a 280M model (learnable structure not yet captured) tends to also represent low-hanging fruit for an 8B model, even though the absolute losses and learning dynamics differ.

The finding has practical force because it decouples data optimization cost from model training cost. The 8% overhead (training reference + proxy at 280M) is an upper bound—if you later decide to train a 70B model instead of 8B, the same domain weights (already computed) can be reused at zero additional cost. The data optimization cost amortizes to zero as the main model scale increases.

However, the paper also surfaces a non-monotonicity that complicates the picture: the 1B proxy model produces domain weights that are *worse* for the 8B main model than the 280M proxy's weights (Figure 6, left—the 1B→8B curve underperforms 280M→8B). The authors attribute this to the Group DRO optimizer becoming less stable at larger proxy scales, as evidenced by the 1B proxy model's poor standalone performance (Table 3b: 0/22 domains beating baseline). This suggests that proxy scale transfer is not a simple "bigger proxy = better weights" story; there is a sweet spot where the proxy is large enough to capture domain structure but small enough that the DRO optimizer remains well-behaved under loss reweighting. Understanding the boundary of this sweet spot—and whether a resampling-based DRO optimizer could extend it—is left as an open question (Section 6).

The transfer result also hints at a deeper theoretical question the paper does not fully answer: **why** does the domain weight optimum appear scale-invariant? One possibility is that the excess loss ranking primarily reflects data properties (entropy, redundancy, structural complexity) that are invariant to model capacity, and only the absolute learning rate depends on scale. Another possibility is that the 30× scale gap explored here is still within a regime where models share similar learning dynamics, and transfer would break down at much larger gaps (e.g., 280M → 175B). The paper's careful framing of this as an open empirical question (Section 6) is appropriately cautious.

### Innovation 4: Resolution of the "Downweighting Yet Improving" Paradox Through Positive Transfer

A superficially paradoxical result runs through the paper: DoReMi drastically downweights many domains—ArXiv drops from 10.5% to 0.4% of the training mix, PubMed Central from 10.7% to 0.5%, StackExchange from 9.3% to 1.5%, DM Mathematics from 2.0% to 0.2% (Table 1)—yet the 8B model's perplexity *improves* on every single one of these domains (Figure 4, Table 4). How can training less on a domain make the model better at that domain?

The paper's answer—distributed across the main text and Appendix D—is the most theoretically interesting move in the paper and has implications beyond data mixture optimization. The core mechanism is **positive transfer from reallocating budget to medium-entropy domains** that produce better representations. The paper hypothesizes three interacting effects:

1. **Low-entropy domains saturate quickly.** Domains with highly predictable token distributions (e.g., code, mathematics) require few examples for the model to learn the relevant patterns. Beyond a small number of samples, additional examples from these domains produce diminishing returns in parameter information. The model already "gets it."

2. **High-entropy domains have structure close to the model's prior.** Domains where token distributions are near-uniform (e.g., very noisy web text, certain conversational data) are already well-modeled by the model's initial random distribution over tokens. The model's learning curve on these domains is flat—more data doesn't help because there's little structure to extract.

3. **Medium-entropy domains drive representation learning.** The bulk of useful representation learning comes from domains where there is genuine structure (low reference loss) that the model hasn't yet captured (high proxy loss). Reallocating training budget to these domains produces representations—attention patterns, feedforward weights—that incidentally transfer to improve performance on the low-entropy and high-entropy domains as well. A model that learns to parse complex argumentation from PhilPapers (upweighted from 0.3% to 2.7%) may incidentally become better at parsing the structured terminology of PubMed abstracts (downweighted from 8.5% to 1.1%) because the underlying linguistic competence transfers.

This framing reframes data mixture optimization from a zero-sum competition between domains ("more weight for A means less for B, so B suffers") to a potentially positive-sum game where overall representation quality can improve for everyone if the budget is reallocated toward domains with the highest marginal return in representation learning. The Appendix D toy model proves this is possible in principle: in the 3-domain unigram example, reallocating all samples from the high-entropy domain to the intermediate-entropy domain improves parameter estimation error on *all three* domains, including the high-entropy domain that received zero training samples (because the estimator's prior already matched the truth for that domain).

This insight challenges the intuitive assumption—common in data curation discussions—that each domain's performance is primarily driven by how many examples from that domain are in the training set. The DoReMi results suggest that for language modeling, **representation quality is substantially shared across domains**, and the optimal mixture is the one that maximizes this shared representation quality, even if it means some domains are barely represented in the training data. The fact that Wikipedia—a domain closely associated with downstream QA tasks—is downweighted from 9.2% to 7.0% in DoReMi, yet downstream accuracy on Wikipedia-derived tasks like TriviaQA improves from 24.6% to 34.9% (Table 5), is a concrete demonstration that more domain-specific training data does not necessarily mean better domain-specific performance. This is not merely an incremental efficiency gain; it is a conceptual correction to how the field thinks about the relationship between training data composition and model capabilities.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The paper uses two datasets. **The Pile** (Gao et al., 2020) is an 800GB text dataset with 22 distinct domains, each with heuristically-chosen default domain weights (Table 1). **The GLaM dataset** (Du et al., 2021), also used in training PaLM (Chowdhery et al., 2022), includes text from 8 domains and provides downstream-tuned domain weights that serve as an oracle comparison. Both datasets are preprocessed by chunking into sequences of 1024 tokens using a SentencePiece tokenizer with 256k vocabulary, with examples separated by domain to facilitate hierarchical sampling—first sample a domain according to its weight, then sample an example uniformly from that domain (Appendix C).

- **Base model(s).** All experiments use vanilla Transformer decoder-only models (Vaswani et al., 2017) trained with standard next-token prediction loss. The main experiments train an **8B-parameter model** (32 layers, 32 attention heads, 128-dimensional attention heads, 4096-dimensional model, 24576-dimensional feedforward hidden layer). The proxy and reference models used by DoReMi to optimize domain weights are **280M parameters** (12 layers, 12 attention heads, 64-dimensional attention heads, 768-dimensional model, 3072-dimensional feedforward hidden layer). Ablation experiments in Section 4 additionally use 70M, 150M, 510M, 760M, and 1B models with architecture hyperparameters specified in Appendix Table 9. All models are trained from scratch.

- **Metrics.** Two primary metrics are reported. **Per-domain log-perplexity** is computed on held-out validation data for each domain, measuring the negative log-likelihood per token—lower is better. The paper reports both worst-case log-perplexity (the maximum across all domains, representing the model's performance on its weakest domain) and unweighted average log-perplexity (averaged across all domains). **Downstream accuracy** uses the generative one-shot tasks from the GPT-3 paper (Brown et al., 2020): TriviaQA (Joshi et al., 2017), NaturalQuestions (Kwiatkowski et al., 2019), WebQuestions (Berant et al., 2013), SQuADv2 (Rajpurkar et al., 2018), and LAMBADA (Paperno et al., 2016). The standard exact-match accuracy metric is used, and the primary reported number is the unweighted average across these five tasks. Per-task accuracies are provided in Appendix Table 5.

- **Baselines.** The paper compares against three distinct baselines. **(1) Baseline domain weights**: models trained using the default domain weights—The Pile's heuristic weights (computed from raw token counts and the epoch specifications in Gao et al., 2020, as detailed in Appendix C) or uniform weights for the GLaM dataset. This is the primary baseline and represents the status quo ante. **(2) Downstream-tuned domain weights** (GLaM dataset only): domain weights that were optimized by training many LMs with different mixtures and selecting the best-performing weights on a held-out set of downstream tasks (Du et al., 2021). The paper treats this as an oracle comparison since these weights use downstream task performance that overlaps with the evaluation set. **(3) Ablated DRO objectives** (Section 4, Figure 6 right, Table 7): DoReMi variants where the excess loss is replaced with only the proxy loss `$\ell_\theta(x)$` (preferring the hardest domains) or only the negative reference loss `$-\ell_{\text{ref}}(x)$` (preferring the easiest domains). These isolate the contribution of the full excess loss signal.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper conducts a controlled comparison by equalizing the amount of compute, measured by the number of tokens processed during training. All models on The Pile are trained for **200k steps** at batch size 512 with maximum token length 1024, yielding `$200\text{k} \times 512 \times 1024 = 1.05 \times 10^{11}$` tokens processed. GLaM models are trained for **300k steps** with the same batch size and token length. The computational cost of DoReMi's domain weight optimization is quantified as approximately **8% of the FLOPs required to train the main 8B model**, computed as `$2 \times 280\text{M} / 8000\text{M} \approx 7\%$` (two 280M training runs relative to one 8B training run) and rounded to 8% to account for slight differences in architectural efficiency. All FLOPs come from standard forward and backward passes; the DRO domain weight update overhead is negligible. Training hyperparameters are consistent across all runs: initial learning rate `$10^{-3}$` decaying exponentially to `$10^{-4}$`, linear warmup for 6% of total steps, weight decay `$10^{-2}$`, gradient clipping to norm 1, and Adafactor optimizer for all models.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not employ cross-validation or statistical significance testing. The evaluation is conducted on the standard held-out validation splits of The Pile and GLaM, and on the standard test/evaluation sets of the five downstream tasks. The domain weight optimization process (Algorithm 1) uses the training split only; no downstream task data is used during domain weight optimization. For the iterated DoReMi experiment on GLaM, convergence of domain weights is determined by checking whether the maximum absolute change in any domain weight `$\|\bar{\alpha} - \alpha_{\text{ref}}\|_\infty$` falls below `$10^{-3}$`.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Domain Weight Optimization with DoReMi (280M Proxy → 8B Main) on The Pile

The headline result: **DoReMi domain weights computed with a 280M proxy model improve the 8B main model's average downstream one-shot accuracy by 6.5 percentage points over the baseline model trained with The Pile's default domain weights, and reach the baseline accuracy 2.6× faster** (Figure 3, left). The baseline model achieves an average downstream accuracy of 20.03% at 200k steps. DoReMi (280M→8B) achieves 26.56% average accuracy at 200k steps—a 6.53 percentage point absolute improvement. The DoReMi model reaches the baseline accuracy of ~20% within 75k training steps compared to the baseline's 200k steps, representing a 2.6× reduction in required training steps (200k / 75k ≈ 2.67×).

Per-task improvements are substantial and consistent across all five benchmarks (Table 5). LAMBADA improves from 20.10% to 29.19% (+9.09 pp). TriviaQA improves from 24.55% to 34.86% (+10.31 pp). SQuADv2 improves from 44.43% to 51.89% (+7.46 pp). NaturalQuestions improves from 4.35% to 7.73% (+3.38 pp). WebQuestions improves from 6.74% to 9.15% (+2.41 pp). Notably, TriviaQA—which is Wikipedia-derived—improves substantially even though Wikipedia's domain weight was reduced from 9.19% (baseline) to 6.99% (DoReMi), providing evidence that domain weight optimization is not a zero-sum tradeoff between domains.

Per-domain perplexity results are reported in Figure 4 and Appendix Table 4. **DoReMi improves log-perplexity on all 22 domains** compared to the baseline, with worst-case log-perplexity dropping from 1.71 (baseline) to 1.46 (DoReMi 280M→8B) and average log-perplexity dropping from 1.64 to 1.40. The largest absolute improvements are on domains that DoReMi significantly upweighted: Pile-CC improves from 1.64 to 1.41, OpenWebText2 from 1.66 to 1.36, Wikipedia from 1.66 to 1.35. But even domains that were drastically downweighted show improvements: ArXiv (downweighted from 10.5% to 0.4%) improves from 1.64 to 1.38 log-perplexity; PubMed Central (downweighted from 10.7% to 0.5%) improves from 1.60 to 1.46; StackExchange (downweighted from 9.3% to 1.5%) improves from 1.61 to 1.39.

The specific domain weights produced by DoReMi (280M) are shown in Table 1. The dominant change is a massive upweighting of Pile-CC from 0.1121 to 0.6057 (+0.494), making it the majority component of the training mixture. Other substantially upweighted domains include YouTubeSubtitles (0.0042 → 0.0502, +0.046), PhilPapers (0.0027 → 0.0274, +0.025), HackerNews (0.0075 → 0.0134, +0.006), and Enron Emails (0.0030 → 0.0070, +0.004). The largest downweightings are on PubMed Central (0.1071 → 0.0046, −0.103), ArXiv (0.1052 → 0.0036, −0.102), StackExchange (0.0929 → 0.0153, −0.078), PubMed Abstracts (0.0845 → 0.0113, −0.073), Books3 (0.0676 → 0.0224, −0.045), and Wikipedia (0.0919 → 0.0699, −0.022).

#### Domain Weight Optimization on the GLaM Dataset (8 Domains)

On the GLaM dataset, which has only 8 domains compared to The Pile's 22 and where downstream-tuned oracle weights are available, the results show a smaller but still meaningful effect. **A single round of DoReMi produces domain weights that yield comparable downstream performance to the baseline (uniform) weights; iterated DoReMi (2 rounds) matches the performance of downstream-tuned oracle weights** (Figure 3, right). At 300k steps, the baseline (uniform) achieves ~35% average one-shot accuracy, downstream-tuned weights achieve ~37%, DoReMi round 1 achieves ~35%, and DoReMi round 2 achieves ~37%. The convergence of iterated DoReMi weights is shown in Table 2: after round 1, the weights are [0.09, 0.44, 0.10, 0.16, 0.11, 0.10] for the 6 domains (the paper reports 6 weight values for 8 domains, with "Conversations" and "Forums" and "Books" and "News" as separate entries; the table shows Wikipedia=0.09, Filtered webpages=0.44, Conversations=0.10, Forums=0.16, Books=0.11, News=0.10). Round 2 converges to [0.05, 0.51, 0.22, 0.04, 0.17, 0.02] for the same domains. Round 3 is nearly identical to round 2: [0.05, 0.51, 0.22, 0.04, 0.17, 0.02]. The downstream-tuned weights are [0.06, 0.42, 0.27, 0.02, 0.20, 0.02]. The overall pattern—heavy weight on Filtered webpages and Conversations, moderate on Books, low on Wikipedia and Forums—is similar between DoReMi round 2 and downstream-tuned weights.

The paper notes that domain reweighting has a smaller effect on GLaM (~2 percentage point improvement over baseline) than on The Pile (~6.5 pp), and hypothesizes this is "possibly because there are only 8 domains compared to 22 in The Pile"—with fewer domains, the initial uniform weights are closer to optimal, leaving less room for improvement.

#### DoReMi with Matched Proxy and Main Model Sizes (Scaling Analysis on The Pile)

To analyze DoReMi's behavior in a controlled setting where domain weights do not need to generalize across scales, the paper runs DoReMi (X→X) with X ∈ {280M, 510M, 760M, 1B} on The Pile (Section 4, Figures 5 and 7, Table 6). **DoReMi consistently improves downstream accuracy across all scales**, with approximately a 2–3 percentage point accuracy gap at 200k steps at most scales. At 280M, the baseline achieves ~12% average accuracy at 200k steps while DoReMi achieves ~15% (+3 pp). At 510M, the effect is smaller: baseline ~14%, DoReMi ~15% (+1 pp). At 760M: baseline ~14%, DoReMi ~17% (+3 pp). At 1B: baseline ~16%, DoReMi ~19% (+3 pp). **DoReMi achieves the baseline accuracy approximately 4× faster on average across scales**—for example, the 280M DoReMi model reaches the baseline's 200k-step accuracy of ~12% within roughly 50k steps.

Perplexity results for this matched-scale analysis are summarized in Table 6. DoReMi improves worst-case log-perplexity at all scales: at 280M from 2.39 to 2.19; at 760M from 2.05 to 2.00; at 1B from 1.94 to 1.92. Average log-perplexity improves at all scales: 280M from 2.32 to 2.13; 760M from 1.97 to 1.94; 1B from 1.87 to 1.83. The number of individual domains where DoReMi beats the baseline is high across scales: 22/22 at 280M, 15/22 at 510M, 17/22 at 760M, and 19/22 at 1B. The paper reports that on average across these 4 scales, DoReMi improves perplexity on 18.25 out of 22 domains.

**A notable finding**: the proxy model itself—the model trained during the DRO step—systematically underperforms the main model trained with resampled data, and this gap grows with scale (Table 3b). At 280M, the proxy model achieves 2.27 average log-perplexity compared to 2.13 for the DoReMi main model (both trained with the same optimized weights but via loss reweighting vs. resampling respectively), and the proxy still beats the baseline (2.32) on 19/22 domains. At 1B, the proxy model (2.02) not only underperforms the DoReMi main model (1.83) but also underperforms the baseline model (1.94) on all 22 domains. Despite the 1B proxy being a poor quality model, the domain weights extracted from its DRO trajectory still produce a 1B main model that beats the baseline on 19/22 domains and achieves the baseline accuracy ~2× faster.

#### Varying Proxy Model Scale for Fixed 8B Main Model

The paper sweeps proxy model sizes of {70M, 150M, 280M, 1B} while fixing the main model at 8B (DoReMi (X→8B)), reported in Figure 6 (left) and Table 4. **Increasing proxy model size from 70M to 280M monotonically improves the resulting 8B downstream accuracy, but the 1B proxy model does not continue this trend.** At 200k steps, the 8B model's average downstream accuracy is: 21.11% for the 70M proxy, 22.51% for the 150M proxy, 26.56% for the 280M proxy, and 22.35% for the 1B proxy. The 1B→8B result is still substantially above the baseline (20.03%) and achieves the baseline accuracy ~2× faster, but it underperforms the 280M→8B configuration. The paper hypothesizes that "the Group DRO optimizer is worse for larger proxy models" (Section 4), as evidenced by the 1B proxy model's poor performance in the matched-scale analysis (Table 3b).

Per-domain perplexity results for all proxy scales are in Table 4. All configurations (70M, 150M, 280M, 1B proxy models) improve perplexity on all 22 domains compared to the baseline 8B model. The 280M→8B configuration achieves the best perplexity across the board, with worst-case log-perplexity 1.46 and average 1.40, compared to 1B→8B (worst-case 1.58, average 1.54), 150M→8B (1.56, 1.52), and 70M→8B (1.63, 1.53). The baseline 8B model has worst-case 1.71 and average 1.64.

#### Matched-Scale Results on the GLaM Dataset

Figure 7 and Appendix A report results where the proxy/reference model size matches the main model size on the GLaM dataset across four scales (280M, 510M, 760M, 1B). **DoReMi is comparable to or better than both the baseline (uniform) domain weights and the downstream-tuned oracle weights across all scales.** At 280M, baseline achieves ~13% average accuracy at 300k steps, DoReMi round 1 achieves ~14%, DoReMi round 2 achieves ~14%, downstream-tuned achieves ~14%. At 510M: baseline ~16%, DoReMi round 1 ~18%, round 2 ~18%, downstream-tuned ~18%. At 760M: baseline ~20%, DoReMi round 1 ~22%, round 2 ~21%, downstream-tuned ~22%. At 1B: baseline ~24%, DoReMi round 1 ~25%, round 2 ~26%, downstream-tuned ~26%. The paper notes an interesting reversal at the 280M scale: round 2 weights achieve slightly worse downstream accuracy than round 1 weights when training 280M models, but transfer better to training 8B models—suggesting that the optimal domain weights may depend on model scale in the GLaM setting, and that iteration may produce weights that generalize better across scales even if they underperform at the proxy scale.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Excess loss components (hardest-only vs. easiest-only vs. full excess loss):** Figure 6 (right) and Table 7 report ablations where the DRO objective uses only proxy loss `$\ell_\theta(x)$` ("hardest" domains for the proxy model) or only negative reference loss `$-\ell_{\text{ref}}(x)$` ("easiest" domains for the reference model), with proxy and main model both at 280M on The Pile. Neither component alone works. The "hardest" ablation produces worst-case log-perplexity 2.66 and average 2.62—substantially worse than the baseline (2.39, 2.32) and improving on 0/22 domains. The "easiest" ablation is catastrophic: worst-case 4.27, average 4.18, 0/22 domains improved. Only the full excess loss (DoReMi) improves over baseline: worst-case 2.19, average 2.13, 22/22 domains improved. This ablation directly validates the paper's central claim that the excess loss signal—not just model loss and not just reference loss—is necessary for domain weight optimization to work.

**Proxy model size for fixed 8B main model:** As described in the main results above (Figure 6 left, Table 4), the 280M proxy is the sweet spot, with 70M and 150M proxies producing progressively weaker (but still positive) results, and the 1B proxy showing a degradation relative to 280M. This serves as both a scaling analysis and a robustness check: the method is not extremely fragile to proxy size choice, but also not monotonic—"bigger proxy = better weights" is false beyond a certain point, likely due to DRO optimizer instability at scale.

**Single-round vs. iterated DoReMi on GLaM:** Table 2, Figure 3 (right), and Figure 7 demonstrate the effect of iteration. On GLaM, round 1 weights produce main model performance roughly equal to the uniform baseline. Round 2 weights produce performance roughly equal to downstream-tuned oracle weights. Round 3 weights are nearly identical to round 2, suggesting rapid convergence. This shows that (a) the initial reference model (trained on uniform weights) may not provide an optimal baseline for excess loss computation when the initial domain weights are far from optimal, and (b) bootstrapping the reference model with the previous round's optimized weights corrects this, allowing DRO to identify more refined domain importance signals. The paper does not report iterated DoReMi on The Pile, so the necessity of iteration for datasets with many domains remains an open question.

**Domain weight trajectory analysis (Appendix Figure 8):** The paper tracks the exponential moving average of domain weights throughout 200k-step DoReMi runs for both 280M and 1B proxy models. Domain weights change rapidly in the first ~50k steps and then relatively stabilize. This suggests two things: (1) "smaller compute budgets may require drastically different domain weights" since the early trajectory may look quite different from the full-run average; (2) "we may be able to save compute by extrapolating the domain weights after 50k steps"—stopping the DRO run early and using the weights from step 50k or an extrapolation thereof, rather than running the full 200k steps. This extrapolation is proposed as future work but not implemented or evaluated in the paper.

**Domain weights at different proxy scales produce different mixtures (Appendix Table 8):** The 280M proxy and 1B proxy produce qualitatively different domain weight distributions on The Pile. The 280M proxy allocates the dominant weight to Pile-CC (0.606) with OpenWebText2 second (0.102). The 1B proxy allocates the dominant weight to OpenWebText2 (0.329) with Pile-CC a distant third (0.120) behind PubMed Abstracts (0.146). The overall pattern for the remaining domains is "similar," but the identity of the top-weighted domain flips. The paper interprets this as evidence that "there may be multiple local minima in domain weight space"—different proxies discover different but similarly effective mixtures—suggesting the optimization landscape is not convex and that the exact domain weights are less important than the broad pattern of upweighting medium-entropy web text and downweighting extremes.

**Loss reweighting vs. resampling for training the final model (Table 3b):** The comparison between proxy models (trained with Group DRO loss reweighting) and main models (trained with resampled data using the same domain weights) reveals a consistent performance gap in favor of resampling, which grows with scale. At 280M, the proxy model (2.27 avg log-ppl) underperforms the main model (2.13) but still beats the baseline (2.32) on 19/22 domains. At 1B, the proxy model (2.02) is worse than both the main model (1.83) and the baseline (1.94). This ablation validates the paper's crucial design choice to use DRO for weight extraction only, not for model training—the DRO optimization procedure itself produces suboptimal models at larger scales, but the domain weights it generates are nonetheless effective when used with standard resampling-based training.

### Critical Assessment

**Claim: DoReMi improves average downstream accuracy by 6.5 percentage points and achieves baseline accuracy 2.6× faster.**

The evidence directly supports this specific claim on The Pile with an 8B model and a 280M proxy (Figure 3 left, Table 5). The claim is precisely scoped—The Pile, 8B main model, specific proxy size—and the experiments match this scope. However, the claim's generality beyond this specific configuration is less well-supported. The 2.6× speedup is measured at a single point on the accuracy curve (where DoReMi crosses the baseline's final accuracy). The speedup factor would differ if measured against a different baseline accuracy threshold (e.g., "time to reach baseline's halfway-point accuracy"). The 4× speedup claimed for the matched-scale experiments (Section 4) vs. 2.6× for the cross-scale transfer suggests the speedup factor depends on the proxy-to-main scale ratio, but the paper does not systematically characterize this dependency.

A genuine weakness: the downstream evaluation uses only 5 tasks, all from the GPT-3 evaluation suite. These are primarily QA and reading comprehension tasks. There is no evaluation on code generation, summarization, translation, or other task families where language models are commonly deployed. The claim "improves downstream accuracy" is true on the evaluated tasks but the breadth of "downstream" is narrower than a reader might assume. The paper acknowledges this implicitly by using "generative one-shot tasks" in its framing, but the abstract and introduction use broader language ("downstream tasks," "downstream performance") without qualification.

**Claim: DoReMi improves perplexity across all domains, even when it downweights a domain.**

Strongly supported by Figure 4 and Table 4, which show per-domain log-perplexity improvements for all 22 domains across multiple proxy sizes (70M, 150M, 280M, 1B all yield 22/22 improvements at 8B). This is the paper's most robust finding because it is replicated across four different proxy configurations, making it unlikely to be an artifact of a particular proxy training run or random seed. The matched-scale experiments (Table 6) show 18.25 out of 22 domains improved on average across four scales, with the worst case at any scale being 15/22 (at 510M). The "all domains improve" claim holds most cleanly at 8B with small proxies; at matched scales, a few domains occasionally degrade, but the pattern is overwhelmingly positive.

The paper's explanatory mechanism for this—positive transfer from reallocating budget to medium-entropy domains—is supported by the toy analysis in Appendix D but not directly tested in the language modeling experiments. There is no experiment that measures representation transfer between domains, isolates the contribution of shared vs. domain-specific parameters, or compares DoReMi against a domain-specific training baseline. The mechanism is plausible and consistent with the data but remains a hypothesis. A skeptical interpretation—that DoReMi simply improves the overall model quality enough that everything benefits, without any special domain-transfer mechanism—is observationally equivalent given the reported experiments.

**Claim: DoReMi, with no knowledge of downstream tasks, matches the performance of using domain weights tuned on downstream tasks.**

Supported on the GLaM dataset (Figure 3 right, Table 2): iterated DoReMi round 2 achieves ~37% average one-shot accuracy, matching the downstream-tuned weights' ~37%. This is a striking result—achieving parity with task-specific optimization without using any task data—and it is the paper's strongest evidence that domain-level optimization can substitute for task-level tuning.

However, several caveats are important. First, the GLaM dataset has only 8 domains, and the effect size is small (~2 pp over baseline). On The Pile with 22 domains, no downstream-tuned oracle exists, so we cannot verify whether the claim generalizes—DoReMi could be substantially worse than what a downstream-tuned mixture would achieve, or it could be better. Second, the downstream-tuned GLaM weights were optimized on a specific set of tasks, and the paper's evaluation uses those same tasks (or overlapping ones), meaning the GLaM weights benefit from a form of test-set leakage that DoReMi does not. DoReMi matching them despite this disadvantage strengthens the result, but it also raises the question: if the GLaM weights were tuned on a disjoint set of tasks and evaluated on the paper's 5-task suite, would they still outperform DoReMi? The paper cannot answer this because the GLaM weights were pre-existing, not re-tuned for the paper's evaluation protocol. Third, this result is on the GLaM dataset specifically—a curated dataset with relatively clean domain boundaries. On noisier datasets or datasets with less coherent domains, DoReMi's advantage over downstream tuning might shrink or vanish.

**Claim: Domain weights optimized with a 280M proxy model transfer to an 8B model (30× larger).**

Clearly supported. The transfer works—22/22 domains improve perplexity, downstream accuracy improves by 6.5 pp, training is 2.6× faster. But the paper's framing of "transfer" is somewhat inflated by the fact that the proxy and main models use identical architectures, identical tokenizers, identical sequence lengths, and the same training data (just different mixtures). This is an easier transfer problem than, say, transferring domain weights from a 280M GPT-style model to a 1B encoder-decoder model with a different tokenizer. The paper does not examine architectural transfer, only scale transfer.

More importantly, the transfer fails to improve monotonically with proxy size—the 1B proxy produces worse weights for the 8B model than the 280M proxy does (Figure 6 left). The paper attributes this to DRO optimizer instability, which is plausible but unverified. If an improved DRO optimizer fixed the 1B proxy problem, would 1B→8B outperform 280M→8B, reversing the current finding that 280M is optimal? Or is there something fundamental about the 280M scale that makes its domain weights better for 8B models? The paper cannot distinguish these possibilities. This matters for practitioners: if you have compute to train a 1B proxy, should you? The paper's answer is "no, stick with 280M," but without understanding why, this guidance might not hold for different datasets, architectures, or scales.

**The lack of statistical rigor is a notable weakness.** The paper reports no confidence intervals, standard deviations, or significance tests for any of its main results—neither for perplexity improvements nor downstream accuracy gaps. The downstream evaluation uses exactly 500 test questions? (The paper does not specify the test set sizes for the 5 downstream tasks, only citing the original papers). Given that per-task accuracies range from ~5% to ~50% and the sample sizes for exact-match QA tasks are typically a few thousand questions, a 2–3 percentage point difference in per-task accuracy may or may not be statistically significant. The consistent direction of improvement across all 5 tasks is qualitatively reassuring but does not substitute for proper uncertainty quantification.

A missing experiment: **sensitivity to the number of domains and domain definitions**. The paper hypothesizes that more domains → larger DoReMi gains (22 domains on The Pile yields 6.5 pp improvement; 8 domains on GLaM yields 2 pp). This correlation is based on exactly two datasets, which differ in many confounded ways (data quality, domain coherence, total size, initial weight quality). A synthetic experiment or an ablation that artificially splits or merges domains on one dataset would test whether domain count itself drives the effect magnitude, and whether DoReMi is robust to alternative domain partitionings. Without this, the practitioner who defines their own domains (as all practitioners must) has no guidance on whether to use 5, 22, or 100 domains.

Another missing experiment: **comparison against a simpler baseline that does not require DRO**. The paper's ablations compare against "hardest-only" and "easiest-only" DRO objectives, both of which fail catastrophically (Table 7)—but these are not competitive baselines. A natural question is: what if you simply take the per-domain validation loss of the reference model and use those as inverse domain weights (downweight domains where the reference already achieves low validation loss, upweight domains where it struggles)? This is a static alternative to DoReMi's dynamic DRO trajectory averaging—cheaper (no proxy training needed), simpler, and potentially effective. Or: what if you set domain weights proportional to the excess loss after a single forward pass of the reference model over a held-out set, without any proxy training at all? These simpler static heuristics are not evaluated. DoReMi may outperform them, but we have no evidence either way.

A subtle overclaim: the paper states that DoReMi's domain weights "can be reused for any number of future main model training runs" (Section 3.4 of the prior analysis), implying that the 8% overhead amortizes further with repeated use. But the domain weights are specific to the training hyperparameters—number of steps, batch size, learning rate schedule, architecture. If any of these change, the optimal domain weights likely change too. The paper's Appendix Figure 8 shows that domain weights evolve over training steps, meaning the "optimal" mixture for 100k steps likely differs from the optimal mixture for 200k steps. The paper does not evaluate reuse across different training configurations or model architectures.

**Overall assessment.** The experiments solidly support the paper's core empirical claims on the specific configurations tested: DoReMi improves perplexity and downstream accuracy on The Pile and matches downstream-tuned weights on GLaM. The 6.5 pp / 2.6× improvement on The Pile is a genuinely large effect relative to the 8% overhead cost. The "all domains improve" finding is robust across proxy sizes and model scales. However, the generality of these findings—to other datasets, other model architectures, other downstream task families, other training budgets—is largely untested. The lack of statistical error bars, the narrow downstream evaluation suite (5 QA tasks), and the absence of comparisons against simpler static reweighting heuristics are the most significant empirical gaps. The paper establishes DoReMi as a promising approach and demonstrates its viability at meaningful scale, but does not provide the systematic characterization needed to predict when and why it will work in new settings.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### Difficulty Estimation Cost Is Unaccounted For in Efficiency Claims

**The assumption:** The entire DoReMi framework rests on the assumption that domain weight optimization can be performed using a small proxy model whose training cost is negligible relative to the main model. The paper quantifies this cost as approximately 8% of the main model's training FLOPs (Section 3.1): "We train two 280M models (the reference and proxy models) to optimize the domain weights. This is 8% of the FLOPs required to train the main 8B model." This overhead is treated as a one-time investment that pays for itself through the 2.6× training speedup. The paper acknowledges that the proxy model training is necessary for domain weight discovery but frames the 8% figure as the total additional cost.

**The consequence:** The 8% figure substantially understates the true computational cost in several important ways that are not accounted for in the headline efficiency numbers. First, **the proxy model must be trained for the full number of steps that the main model will use** (200k for The Pile, 300k for GLaM). If a practitioner wants to train a main model for 500k steps—a longer horizon that might produce a better final model—DoReMi's proxy training cost scales linearly with that horizon, since the domain weights are computed by averaging over the full trajectory. The paper's Appendix Figure 8 shows that domain weights stabilize after roughly 50k steps, suggesting that running the full 200k steps may be unnecessary, but this extrapolation is proposed as future work (Section 6: "A future direction for saving compute would be to stop running DoReMi at an early step and extrapolate the domain weights for the desired number of steps") and is not implemented or validated. Until such extrapolation is demonstrated to work, the proxy training cost is effectively a fixed fraction of whatever the main model's training budget is.

Second, **the 8% figure only applies when the proxy-to-main model size ratio is exactly 280M:8B (roughly 3.5%)**. If the main model were smaller (e.g., 1B parameters), the overhead would be roughly 56%—two 280M training runs relative to a single 1B training run. The paper's own matched-scale experiments (Section 4) effectively pay 200% overhead (training reference + proxy at 1B each for a 1B main model), which is only justified by the 4× training speedup. For main models smaller than roughly 2B parameters, the 8% framing collapses and the approach may not be computationally advantageous.

Third, **the overhead is incurred even if the domain weights turn out to be poor**. There is no early-stopping criterion for the DRO process—the proxy model training must run to completion to produce averaged weights. The paper shows DoReMi improves over the baseline in all tested configurations, but it provides no diagnostic signal that would allow a practitioner to detect during the proxy training run whether the domain weights will transfer effectively to the main model scale.

**What evidence exists in the paper:** The cost breakdown appears in Section 3.1. The linearity assumption—that proxy training cost equals "two 280M training runs"—is stated without derivation from the architecture hyperparameters in Appendix Table 9. The trajectory stabilization result (Appendix Figure 8) is the only empirical evidence that early stopping might work, but it is purely observational and not tested as a practical strategy. The paper provides no FLOPs accounting for scenarios where the main model training length differs from the proxy training length, or where the main model size differs from 8B.

**Mitigation status:** The paper explicitly flags the extrapolation approach as future work (Section 6) but does not develop or evaluate it. The sensitivity of the speedup factor to proxy/main model size ratios is not discussed beyond the singular 280M→8B configuration. The practitioner is given no guidance on how to determine whether a given proxy/main size ratio will yield net compute savings.

---

### Hard Problems (Very Low or Very High Entropy Domains) Receive Nearly Zero Weight, Which May Be Undesirable

**The assumption:** DoReMi's excess loss objective `$\ell_\theta(x) - \ell_{\text{ref}}(x)$` is designed to automatically downweight domains that are either intrinsically unpredictable (high reference loss—"noisy") or already well-learned (low proxy loss—"easy"). This is presented as a feature: the method identifies domains where additional training examples produce the largest marginal improvement and reallocates budget toward them. The paper argues through Appendix D that this reallocation can improve performance on all domains simultaneously through positive transfer, and the empirical results (Figure 4, Table 4) confirm that perplexity improves even on drastically downweighted domains.

**The consequence:** The domain weights DoReMi produces are often extremely skewed, with some domains receiving near-zero sampling probability. On The Pile with a 280M proxy (Table 1), DM Mathematics drops from 2.0% weight to 0.2%, ArXiv from 10.5% to 0.4%, PubMed Central from 10.7% to 0.5%, and StackExchange from 9.3% to 1.5%. These are four complete domains that collectively represent a substantial fraction of the original dataset and contain highly specialized technical knowledge—mathematics, academic papers, biomedical literature, and technical Q&A. The paper demonstrates that *perplexity* on these domains improves despite the downweighting, but **perplexity measures only how well the model predicts held-out tokens from these domains, not how well it can generate or reason about their specialized content**.

This is a critical distinction. A language model trained with PubMed Central at 0.5% weight may achieve good perplexity on biomedical abstracts because the representations learned from heavily-weighted web text incidentally transfer to biomedical terminology. But the model may be substantially worse at generating coherent biomedical text, answering domain-specific factual questions, or reasoning about biomedical concepts—capabilities that require deep exposure to the domain rather than incidental transfer. The paper provides no evaluation of domain-specific *generation quality* or *knowledge retention* for downweighted domains; it only evaluates overall downstream accuracy on 5 QA tasks (TriviaQA, NaturalQuestions, WebQuestions, SQuADv2, LAMBADA), none of which specifically target the heavily-downweighted technical domains. A practitioner building a model intended to serve biomedical researchers would likely find a domain weight of 0.5% for PubMed Central unacceptable, even if the overall perplexity improved, because the model's specialized biomedical knowledge might be severely degraded.

Furthermore, **the domain weights are static and applied uniformly across all training steps**. An alternative strategy—for example, curriculum learning where domain weights shift from simpler to more complex domains over the course of training—might preserve specialized knowledge in low-weight domains while still achieving the efficiency gains of focused early training. DoReMi provides no mechanism for such dynamic allocation.

**What evidence exists in the paper:** The per-domain perplexity improvements for downweighted domains are shown in Figure 4 and Table 4. The downstream task accuracies are shown in Table 5 and are not broken out by domain; they measure only the five standard QA benchmarks. There is no evaluation of domain-specific generation quality, factual recall, or specialized task performance. The paper's Appendix D provides theoretical justification for why downweighting can improve perplexity universally in a toy model, but this model has no notion of knowledge retention or generation quality—only parameter estimation error for unigram distributions. The gap between perplexity improvement and specialized capability preservation is not discussed.

**Mitigation status:** The paper does not acknowledge this as a limitation. The "all domains improve" result is presented as an unqualified positive (Section 3.2: "DoReMi can reduce perplexity across all domains without a tradeoff"). The smoothing parameter `$c = 10^{-3}$` provides a theoretical floor on domain weights (roughly `$4.5 \times 10^{-5}$` per domain for The Pile's 22 domains), but in practice this floor is negligible—domains at this floor would appear roughly once per 22,000 training examples. The paper's framing implicitly assumes that perplexity improvement is the relevant metric for domain-specific performance, which may not hold for applications requiring specialized knowledge or generation capabilities.

---

### The Definition of "Domain" Is Fixed, Coarse, and Assumed Given—With No Guidance on How to Partition Data

**The assumption:** DoReMi operates on a predefined set of `$k$` domains, where each domain `$D_i$` is a set of examples assumed to be coherent and distinct from other domains. In the paper's experiments, these domains are defined by data provenance: The Pile's 22 domains correspond to the original data sources (Wikipedia, GitHub, ArXiv, etc.), and the GLaM dataset's 8 domains correspond to broad categories (Filtered webpages, Conversations, Books, etc.). The algorithm treats domain boundaries as fixed and given, with no mechanism for refining or questioning the domain partition.

The paper explicitly acknowledges this as a limitation in Section 6: "We define a domain by data provenance in our experiments, but this only enables coarse-grained control. Using fine-grained domains could improve the gains from DoReMi. For example, DoReMi is more effective on The Pile (22 domains) than the GLaM dataset (8 domains)."

**The consequence:** The performance of DoReMi—and specifically the domain weights it produces—is fundamentally dependent on how domains are defined, yet the paper provides **no principles, methods, or empirical guidance for domain definition**. A practitioner approaching a new dataset must decide how to partition their data into domains before running DoReMi, and this decision may be as consequential as the optimization itself. Several failure modes arise:

**Coarse domains mask internal heterogeneity.** The Pile-CC domain (CommonCrawl web text) receives 60.6% weight under DoReMi (280M), but CommonCrawl is enormously diverse—it contains news articles, blog posts, product reviews, forum discussions, and informal social media content. Treating it as a single domain may cause DoReMi to overweight certain sub-types that happen to produce favorable excess loss dynamics while underweighting others, leading to a model with imbalanced coverage of web text varieties. The paper provides no analysis of whether the optimized domain weights would differ if Pile-CC were split into sub-domains.

**Domain boundaries may not align with learnability boundaries.** The paper's central insight is that domains should be weighted according to their "learnability"—whether they contain structure that is learnable but not yet learned. But if domain boundaries are defined by data provenance rather than by statistical properties, a single domain may contain a mixture of high-learnability and low-learnability examples. DoReMi assigns a single weight to the entire domain, potentially over-allocating budget to low-learnability examples within a high-weight domain and under-allocating to high-learnability examples within a low-weight domain.

**The relationship between domain count and DoReMi's effectiveness is uncharacterized.** The paper observes that DoReMi produces larger gains on The Pile (22 domains, 6.5 pp improvement) than on GLaM (8 domains, ~2 pp improvement), and hypothesizes that this is "possibly because there are only 8 domains compared to 22 in The Pile." But this correlation is based on exactly two datasets that differ in many confounded ways—total size, data quality, domain coherence, initial weight quality, and training duration. There is no controlled experiment varying only the number of domains on a fixed dataset, so the hypothesis remains speculative. A practitioner cannot use this observation to decide whether 10, 50, or 200 domains would be optimal for their application.

**The future direction of automatic fine-grained domain discovery is mentioned but unexplored.** The paper points to "automatically finding fine-grained domains (e.g., via clustering as in DRO-LM; Oren et al., 2019)" as an open direction (Section 6), but provides no experimental evidence that clustering-based domains would work with DoReMi, or whether the Group DRO optimizer remains stable when the number of domains becomes large (hundreds or thousands). In the limit of example-level domains (`$k$` equal to the number of training examples), Group DRO would face the "pessimism" problem the paper itself flags: "When domains are very fine-grained, it will be important to control the pessimism of DRO (e.g., DRO can put all the weight on a small set of worst-case examples)."

**What evidence exists in the paper:** The results on The Pile (22 domains) versus GLaM (8 domains) provide circumstantial evidence that more domains correlate with larger gains, but no controlled experiment isolates the domain count variable. The domain weight tables (Tables 1 and 2) show the weights at only the given domain granularities. There is no ablation where domains are artificially split or merged, no analysis of within-domain heterogeneity for high-weight domains like Pile-CC, and no experiment with automatically discovered (clustered) domains.

**Mitigation status:** The paper acknowledges this as a limitation and proposes automatic domain discovery as future work (Section 6). However, it provides no practical heuristics for domain definition in the interim—the practitioner is told that fine-grained domains "could improve the gains" but given no method for constructing them, no guidance on minimum or maximum domain counts, and no diagnostic for whether an existing domain partition is appropriate for DoReMi.

### The Proxy-to-Main Transfer Mechanism Is Empirically Observed but Not Understood—Making Cross-Scale Generalization Unpredictable

**The assumption:** DoReMi's practical value rests on a specific empirical claim: that domain weights optimized with a small proxy model transfer effectively to a much larger main model, enabling the computational cost of domain optimization to be amortized. The paper demonstrates this transfer empirically for a 280M proxy → 8B main model (roughly 30× scale increase) and tests several proxy sizes (70M, 150M, 280M, 1B) with a fixed 8B main model. The results show that transfer works across all tested sizes, with the 280M proxy performing best. The paper explicitly acknowledges that this transfer is not well understood, stating in Section 6: "Understanding why the domain weights can be transferred across scales and the limits of how far these domain weights transfer are important questions to answer in future work."

**The consequence:** The lack of understanding of the transfer mechanism creates substantial uncertainty for practitioners who want to apply DoReMi at different scales or in different regimes. Several concrete failure modes are possible:

**Transfer quality is non-monotonic in proxy size.** Figure 6 (left) shows that increasing proxy model size from 70M to 280M improves the resulting 8B model's accuracy (21.11% → 22.51% → 26.56%), but further increasing to 1B degrades performance (22.35%). The paper attributes this to Group DRO optimizer instability at larger scales (Section 4: "the Group DRO optimizer is worse for larger proxy models"), supported by the fact that the 1B proxy model itself performs poorly (Table 3b: 0/22 domains beating baseline). But the root cause—whether this is a fundamental instability in the Group DRO algorithm, a mismatch between loss reweighting and resampling (which the paper also hypothesizes), or a problem specific to the 1B architecture—is not determined. A practitioner choosing a proxy size cannot predict whether a 500M proxy would perform better or worse than 280M, or whether a 5B proxy (roughly 60% of an 8B main model) would recover the gains lost at 1B. The transfer curve is empirically observed but theoretically opaque.

**The maximum scale gap over which transfer works is unknown.** The paper tests a maximum scale gap of roughly 30× (280M → 8B) and 114× (70M → 8B). Would domain weights from a 280M proxy transfer to a 70B, 175B, or 540B model? The paper provides no evidence. At some scale gap, the learning dynamics of the proxy model likely diverge sufficiently from the main model's dynamics that the domain weight ranking becomes irrelevant or even harmful. The paper's results show that domain weights are somewhat sensitive to the compute budget—the trajectory analysis in Appendix Figure 8 shows weights evolving over training steps, implying that the optimal mixture for 200k steps differs from the optimal mixture for 50k steps. By the same logic, the optimal mixture for a 280M model trained for 200k steps might differ from the optimal mixture for an 8B model trained for 200k steps; the fact that transfer works suggests the differences are small enough to be dominated by other effects, but this may not hold at larger scale gaps.

**Architecture-specific effects are unexplored.** The paper uses standard Transformer decoder-only architectures with consistent hyperparameter scaling (Appendix Table 9): as model size increases, the number of layers, attention heads, and hidden dimensions scale up according to a specific recipe. Would domain weights transfer from a 280M dense Transformer to an 8B mixture-of-experts model? To a model with a different tokenizer? To an encoder-decoder architecture? The paper provides no cross-architecture transfer experiments. If a practitioner changes the model architecture between the proxy and main model—as might happen in a research workflow where the proxy is a quick prototype and the main model uses the latest architectural innovations—the transfer guarantee evaporates.

**What evidence exists in the paper:** The proxy size sweep (Figure 6 left) is the primary evidence for transfer behavior, and it reveals the non-monotonicity. The matched-scale experiments (Table 6, Figure 5) show that DoReMi works when proxy and main model sizes match, suggesting transfer is not strictly necessary for the method to function—it just makes it cost-effective. The paper provides no experiment where the scale gap exceeds 114× (70M → 8B), no experiments with different architectures, and no theoretical analysis of why domain weight rankings might be scale-invariant.

**Mitigation status:** The paper explicitly identifies this as an open question (Section 6). The practical recommendation to "choose a relatively small proxy model size (280M) to save compute" (Section 4) is empirically grounded for the specific 8B The Pile configuration but may not generalize. The paper provides no diagnostic to determine whether a given proxy/main scale configuration will transfer successfully without running the full experiment.

### The Downstream Evaluation Is Narrow (5 QA Tasks) and Does Not Capture the Full Range of Language Model Capabilities

**The assumption:** The paper evaluates downstream performance using the generative one-shot tasks from the GPT-3 paper (Brown et al., 2020): TriviaQA, NaturalQuestions, WebQuestions, SQuADv2, and LAMBADA. These are all question answering or reading comprehension tasks that primarily test factual recall and passage understanding. The paper states that "the performance on these datasets (particularly TriviaQA) has been shown to correlate well with model scale even at the 100M–1B range" (Section 3.1), citing Brown et al. (2020) as justification for this evaluation suite. The headline claim—"DoReMi improves average few-shot downstream accuracy by 6.5% points" (Abstract)—is based entirely on the unweighted average across these 5 tasks.

**The consequence:** The evaluation suite covers only a narrow slice of what language models are used for, and **the 6.5 percentage point improvement cannot be interpreted as a general improvement across all downstream tasks**. Several important capability dimensions are entirely absent from the evaluation:

**Code generation and structured reasoning** are not tested. The Pile contains a GitHub domain (4.3% baseline weight, reduced to 1.8% by DoReMi 280M), but there is no evaluation of the model's ability to generate or understand code after training with reduced code data. If a practitioner is building a model intended for code-related applications, the perplexity improvement on the GitHub domain (Table 4: 1.65 → 1.42 log-perplexity) provides some reassurance, but perplexity does not directly measure code generation correctness, syntax validity, or the ability to follow programming instructions. A model that predicts GitHub tokens well may still produce syntactically invalid code or fail at code completion tasks.

**Open-ended generation quality** (coherence, factual consistency, stylistic appropriateness) is not evaluated. The downstream tasks all use exact-match accuracy, which is appropriate for QA but cannot capture generation quality for tasks like summarization, translation, dialogue, or creative writing. The LAMBADA task (last-word prediction given context) captures some aspects of discourse-level understanding but is far from a comprehensive generation evaluation.

**Domain-specific task performance** is not broken out by training domain. The paper emphasizes that DoReMi improves perplexity on all 22 domains, including heavily downweighted ones, but never evaluates whether models trained with DoReMi weights retain specialized capabilities on tasks derived from those domains. For example, does the model perform well on biomedical QA tasks (related to PubMed) or mathematical reasoning tasks (related to DM Mathematics) after these domains receive near-zero training weight? The positive transfer argument suggests they might, but this is an empirical question that the paper does not address.

**Few-shot vs. zero-shot vs. fine-tuned performance** is not systematically compared. The paper evaluates only one-shot performance (a single exemplar in the prompt). It does not evaluate zero-shot performance (no exemplars) or fine-tuned performance (the model is adapted to each task with additional training). It is possible that DoReMi's improvements are specific to the few-shot regime—where the model relies heavily on pretraining knowledge—and would not carry over to zero-shot or fine-tuned settings. Conversely, DoReMi's improvements might be larger in zero-shot settings where the model has no task-specific examples to guide it.

**The correlation argument is insufficient.** The claim that these 5 tasks "correlate well with model scale even at the 100M–1B range" is a statement about how these tasks behave under *model scaling*, not under *data mixture changes*. A task that improves predictably when you increase model parameters may not improve when you change the data mixture—and vice versa. The paper effectively assumes that data mixture quality and model scale affect downstream performance through the same or correlated mechanisms, but this assumption is untested.

**What evidence exists in the paper:** Table 5 provides per-task accuracies for the 5 evaluation tasks across different proxy sizes. The improvements are positive across all 5 tasks—ranging from +2.4 pp on WebQuestions to +10.3 pp on TriviaQA for DoReMi (280M→8B)—suggesting the gains are not concentrated in a single task. But 5 tasks, all from the same QA/reading-comprehension family, cannot establish generality. The paper references no other evaluation benchmarks or task families.

**Mitigation status:** The paper does not acknowledge the narrowness of the evaluation suite as a limitation. The evaluation methodology section (Section 3.1) justifies the choice by citing Brown et al. (2020) on scale correlation, but this justification addresses validity for model scaling studies, not for data mixture optimization studies. The paper does not suggest additional evaluation dimensions as future work.

### The Revision Model—Wait, Wrong Paper. Correction: Loss Reweighting vs. Resampling Mismatch Is Acknowledged but Not Resolved, Limiting DoReMi at Larger Proxy Scales

**The assumption:** DoReMi trains the proxy model using **loss reweighting**: the Group DRO objective scales the gradient contribution of each example by its domain's current weight `$\alpha_t[i]$`, but all examples in a uniformly-sampled minibatch are seen. The main model, in contrast, is trained on a **resampled dataset** where the domain weights determine which examples appear at all—each example in the resampled dataset contributes equally to the loss. The paper implicitly assumes that domain weights extracted from a loss-reweighted training run will be effective when used for resampling-based training, even though these are different training procedures with different optimization dynamics.

**The consequence:** The mismatch between loss reweighting (proxy) and resampling (main) creates a **scaling-dependent degradation in proxy model quality** that appears to limit the effectiveness of larger proxies. The evidence is stark in Table 3b: at 280M, the proxy model (trained with loss reweighting) achieves average log-perplexity of 2.27 and beats the baseline on 19/22 domains. At 1B, the proxy model achieves 2.02 average log-perplexity and beats the baseline on **0/22 domains**—it is worse than the baseline model trained with standard uniform resampling. Yet the domain weights extracted from this poor 1B proxy still produce a 1B main model (trained with resampling) that achieves 1.83 average log-perplexity and beats the baseline on 19/22 domains. This is simultaneously impressive (the weights are useful despite the proxy being bad) and concerning (the proxy model's degradation limits the quality of the extracted weights).

The paper hypothesizes in Section 4: "the mismatch between the proxy and main model training (loss reweighting vs. resampling) explains their performance difference and therefore a resampling-based Group DRO optimizer may improve DoReMi for larger proxy models." If this hypothesis is correct, then **DoReMi's current implementation is bottlenecked not by the proxy model's capacity to assess domain importance, but by the training procedure used during the DRO step**. A resampling-based DRO optimizer—where each minibatch is sampled according to the current domain weights rather than uniformly, and all examples contribute equally—might produce higher-quality proxy models and better domain weights at larger scales. But such an optimizer is not developed or tested in the paper.

The practical consequence is that **the proxy-to-main transfer curve in Figure 6 (left) may not be fundamental**. The observed optimum at 280M may be an artifact of the specific Group DRO optimizer used, not a property of domain weight optimization itself. If a resampling-based DRO optimizer were developed, larger proxy models might produce substantially better domain weights for 8B main models—the current 6.5 percentage point improvement might be a lower bound. Conversely, if the mismatch is not the primary cause, the 280M optimum might be genuine, and practitioners should not invest in larger proxies. The paper cannot distinguish these possibilities.

Furthermore, the loss reweighting approach introduces a subtle statistical inefficiency: when domain weights become extreme (e.g., Pile-CC at 0.606 weight), examples from low-weight domains contribute negligibly to the gradient. The proxy model effectively trains on a fraction of the data, with noisy gradient estimates for low-weight domains because those domains' few examples in each uniformly-sampled minibatch are downweighted. This may explain why the 1B proxy underperforms the baseline—it is essentially training on a much smaller effective dataset, weighted heavily toward a few domains. A resampling-based approach would give equal gradient weight to all sampled examples, at the cost of potentially seeing low-weight domains very rarely.

**What evidence exists in the paper:** The comparison between proxy and main model performance at matched scales (Table 3b) directly reveals the mismatch. The proxy model's systematic underperformance relative to both the baseline and the main model is observed at all tested scales (280M, 510M, 760M, 1B) and grows with scale. The paper's hypothesis about the cause is stated in Section 4 but not tested experimentally—there is no ablation comparing resampling-based DRO against loss-reweighting DRO. The proxy size sweep (Figure 6 left) shows the consequence (non-monotonic transfer) but not the mechanism.

**Mitigation status:** The paper explicitly proposes "a resampling-based Group DRO optimizer" as a potential solution (Section 4) but does not develop or test one. This is flagged as future work implicitly. The current implementation ships with this limitation intact, and practitioners using DoReMi as described in Algorithm 1 should expect proxy model quality to degrade at larger scales, with uncertain effects on the resulting domain weights. The paper's guidance to use a 280M proxy is a practical workaround, not a resolution.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

DoReMi represents a **methodological reframing** rather than a paradigm shift: it takes an existing tool—Group DRO—that was designed for producing robust models and repurposes it as a **diagnostic instrument for data engineering**. The core move is treating the DRO optimizer's trajectory not as a path toward a robust model but as a measurement apparatus that reveals which domains offer the most learning headroom. This inversion matters because it decouples data optimization from model deployment: the proxy model that performs the DRO training is discarded, and only the averaged domain weights—a static artifact of the optimization process—are retained. This means data mixture optimization becomes an offline, one-time preprocessing step whose cost amortizes to zero as you train larger and larger models using the same weights.

The landscape shift is most significant along three dimensions:

**1. Domain weight optimization becomes agnostic to downstream tasks.** Prior to DoReMi, the only principled approach to setting domain weights for large-scale LM pretraining was the GLaM/PaLM approach: train many LMs with different mixtures, evaluate them on held-out downstream tasks, and select the best-performing mixture (Du et al., 2021; Chowdhery et al., 2022). This created a tight coupling between the data curation process and the specific downstream tasks the model would be evaluated on, raising concerns about overfitting and limiting the generality of the resulting data recipes. DoReMi breaks this coupling: the optimized domain weights from the GLaM dataset *match the performance of downstream-tuned weights* (Figure 3 right, Table 2) without ever seeing downstream task data. This is not an incremental improvement—it is a qualitative change in *what information is needed* to optimize pretraining data. The practical implication is that a single DoReMi run can produce domain weights that serve a wide range of downstream applications, rather than weights specialized to a particular benchmark suite.

**2. Data mixture optimization inherits the scaling properties of pretraining.** The paper's demonstration that domain weights optimized at 280M parameters transfer effectively to 8B parameters (30× larger, with 2.6× training speedup) establishes that data optimization can be performed at a scale where experimentation is cheap and the results applied at scales where training is expensive. This mirrors the logic of scaling laws (Hoffmann et al., 2022), where optimal hyperparameters discovered at small scale transfer to larger scales—but applied to data composition rather than model architecture. The finding makes data mixture optimization a practical tool for organizations that train models at scales where brute-force mixture search (the GLaM approach) would be prohibitively expensive. It also suggests a research agenda: characterizing the transfer limits of domain weights across scales, architectures, and training horizons, much as the scaling laws literature characterized the transfer limits of optimal learning rates and batch sizes.

**3. "More data from domain X" is decoupled from "better performance on domain X."** The paper's most counterintuitive result—that DoReMi improves perplexity on every domain while drastically downweighting some (Figure 4, Table 4: ArXiv from 10.5% → 0.4% weight yet log-perplexity improves from 1.64 → 1.38)—challenges a deeply held assumption in data curation: that a model's performance on a domain is primarily determined by how many examples from that domain it sees during training. DoReMi demonstrates that representation learning is substantially shared across domains, and that reallocating training budget toward domains with high marginal learning value (medium entropy, not yet learned) can produce better representations that incidentally improve performance on downweighted domains through positive transfer. This reframes data mixture optimization from a zero-sum competition between domains to a potentially positive-sum optimization over shared representations. The immediate practical consequence is that practitioners should not assume that maintaining or increasing a domain's training weight is the only way to improve performance on that domain; the optimal mixture may involve aggressively downweighting some domains while still improving them.

**What research directions become more attractive.** The paper makes data-centric optimization of pretraining—previously a heuristic, artisanal endeavor—into a principled algorithmic problem. This invites systematic study of data mixture as a first-class optimization variable alongside model architecture and training hyperparameters. It also elevates the importance of domain definition and granularity as a design choice that may be as consequential as the optimization algorithm itself. Conversely, the paper makes **brute-force mixture search via downstream evaluation less attractive**: if DoReMi can match downstream-tuned weights without task data, the computational expense and overfitting risk of task-specific tuning becomes harder to justify, at least for general-purpose pretraining. The GLaM approach remains valuable when the deployment distribution is known and narrow (e.g., a model intended exclusively for medical QA), but for foundation models intended to serve many downstream tasks, DoReMi offers a simpler and more principled alternative.

**Reconciling prior contradictions.** The paper does not directly reconcile a prior contradiction in the literature—the DRO-for-robustness literature (Oren et al., 2019; Sagawa et al., 2020) and the data selection literature (Mindermann et al., 2022; Moore and Lewis, 2010) were pursuing different goals with different methods, rather than producing contradictory findings. However, DoReMi does **recontextualize** prior DRO work by showing that the DRO training trajectory contains useful information (the domain weight dynamics) that prior work discarded by focusing only on the final model parameters. This suggests that other robust optimization methods (e.g., adversarial training, invariant risk minimization) may similarly contain unexploited diagnostic signals in their optimization trajectories, opening a new lens for interpreting these algorithms.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Resampling-based Group DRO to eliminate the proxy/main training mismatch.** The paper identifies a specific failure mode: the loss-reweighting training used for the proxy model systematically underperforms resampling-based training at larger scales, with the 1B proxy model beating the baseline on 0/22 domains (Table 3b) despite producing useful domain weights. The natural follow-up is to replace the Group DRO optimizer in Algorithm 1 with a variant that *resamples* minibatches according to the current domain weights `$\alpha_t$` rather than uniformly sampling and reweighting the loss. A resampling-based approach would give all sampled examples equal gradient weight, eliminating the statistical inefficiency of downweighting low-`$\alpha$` domains and potentially stabilizing proxy training at larger scales. The key experiment: run DoReMi with a resampling-based DRO optimizer for proxy sizes of 280M, 1B, and 3B, training an 8B main model from each set of weights. The prediction (following the paper's hypothesis in Section 4) is that larger proxy models would produce monotonically better domain weights under resampling, potentially exceeding the 6.5 percentage point improvement ceiling observed with the current 280M proxy. A negative result—resampling-based DRO fails to improve over loss-reweighting, or the 280M optimum persists—would indicate that the bottleneck is not the training procedure but something fundamental about domain weight transfer across scales.

**Cheap difficulty estimation via early-stopping and extrapolation.** The paper's trajectory analysis (Appendix Figure 8) reveals that domain weights stabilize after approximately 50k training steps of the 200k-step DoReMi run, suggesting the full proxy training cost may be unnecessary. A direct follow-up: run DoReMi on The Pile for 50k, 100k, 150k, and 200k steps with a 280M proxy, extract domain weights at each stopping point (averaged over the truncated trajectory), train 8B main models on each set of weights, and measure downstream accuracy. If the 50k-step weights achieve downstream accuracy within, say, 1 percentage point of the full 200k-step weights, the effective overhead of DoReMi drops from 8% to roughly 2% (50k/200k × 8%). This experiment would also test whether the domain weight trajectory in the first 50k steps—where weights change rapidly—is genuinely informative or mostly noise. A more ambitious variant: train a small predictor model that takes the domain weight trajectory from the first `$t$` steps as input and predicts the final averaged weights, then evaluate whether extrapolated weights match or exceed the performance of full-trajectory weights. This would transform DoReMi from a fixed-overhead preprocessing step into an anytime algorithm where the practitioner can trade off domain weight quality against proxy training cost.

**Systematic characterization of domain granularity effects.** The paper observes that DoReMi produces larger gains on The Pile (22 domains, 6.5 pp) than on GLaM (8 domains, ~2 pp), and hypothesizes that more domains enable finer-grained optimization. This hypothesis is testable with a controlled experiment on a single dataset: take The Pile, merge its 22 domains into 11 super-domains by combining related sources (e.g., PubMed Central + PubMed Abstracts → "Biomedical"; ArXiv + PhilPapers + DM Mathematics → "Academic"; GitHub + StackExchange → "Technical Q&A"), run DoReMi on both the 22-domain and 11-domain partitions with a matched 280M proxy, and train 8B main models from each set of weights. If the 22-domain weights produce better downstream accuracy, the granularity hypothesis is supported. Extend further: split the largest domains (Pile-CC, OpenWebText2) into sub-domains using unsupervised clustering (e.g., k-means on sentence embeddings), producing 50 or 100 domains, and test whether performance continues to improve or plateaus. This experiment directly addresses the open question from Section 6 about whether automatically discovered fine-grained domains (as in DRO-LM; Oren et al., 2019) can amplify DoReMi's gains. A negative result—performance plateaus or degrades beyond ~20 domains—would establish a practical upper bound on domain granularity and suggest that DoReMi's current coarse-grained approach is already near-optimal.

**Cross-architecture and cross-tokenizer transfer of domain weights.** The paper's transfer experiments hold architecture and tokenizer constant—both proxy and main model use standard decoder-only Transformers with a 256k SentencePiece vocabulary. A crucial stress test: optimize domain weights with a 280M decoder-only proxy model (the paper's exact setup) and use those weights to train an 8B main model with a different architecture (e.g., mixture-of-experts, encoder-decoder) or a different tokenizer (e.g., BPE with 50k vocabulary). If transfer works across architectures, DoReMi's domain weights reflect properties of the *data* rather than properties of the *model family*, making the approach far more general. If transfer fails—downstream accuracy degrades relative to using architecture-matched proxy weights—then domain weight optimization is architecture-specific, and practitioners must re-run DoReMi whenever they change model architecture. A related experiment: train the proxy model with a smaller tokenizer (50k vocabulary) and the main model with a larger one (256k), and vice versa, to test whether domain weights are tokenizer-invariant. The paper's Appendix C notes that "domain weights, which are based on token count in this paper, varies by tokenizer"—so tokenizer invariance is not guaranteed, and this experiment quantifies how much the tokenizer choice matters in practice.

**Domain-specific capability evaluation beyond perplexity.** The paper demonstrates perplexity improvements on all domains but provides no evaluation of whether heavily-downweighted domains retain specialized capabilities (biomedical knowledge in PubMed, mathematical reasoning in DM Mathematics, code generation in GitHub). A targeted follow-up: train an 8B baseline model and an 8B DoReMi (280M→8B) model on The Pile, then evaluate both on domain-specific benchmarks that align with the most aggressively downweighted domains. For PubMed (downweighted from 10.7% → 0.5%), evaluate on biomedical QA tasks like PubMedQA or BioASQ. For GitHub (4.3% → 1.8%), evaluate on HumanEval or MBPP for code generation. For DM Mathematics (2.0% → 0.2%), evaluate on GSM8K or MATH. If DoReMi models match or exceed baseline performance on these specialized tasks despite drastically reduced domain-specific training data, the positive transfer hypothesis is strongly validated, and the "no tradeoff" claim extends from perplexity to genuine capability. If DoReMi models substantially underperform the baseline on specialized tasks—even while showing better perplexity on those domains—this reveals a hidden cost of aggressive downweighting: good next-token prediction does not imply retained expertise. This experiment would directly inform practitioners whether DoReMi is safe to use for models intended to serve specialized domains.

**Compute-budget-dependent domain weights for curriculum-style training.** The paper's Appendix Figure 8 shows that domain weights evolve over the course of training, with some domains (e.g., YouTubeSubtitles at 280M) spiking early and then declining. This suggests that the optimal domain mixture for the first 50k steps of training differs from the optimal mixture for steps 150k–200k. A natural experiment: instead of using a single set of averaged domain weights for the entire main model training run, use time-varying domain weights derived from the DoReMi trajectory—for example, the exponential moving average of `$\alpha_t$` at each step, mapped to the corresponding step of main model training. Train an 8B model with this dynamic mixture and compare against the static DoReMi weights baseline. If dynamic weights improve over static weights, this suggests that DoReMi's trajectory encodes a curriculum—certain domains are more valuable early in training (when the model is learning basic patterns), others later (when the model has capacity to absorb complex structure). This would connect DoReMi to the curriculum learning literature and provide a more sophisticated way to use the information in the DRO trajectory than simple averaging.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Cost-efficient pretraining for organizations with limited compute budgets.** The paper's headline efficiency result—2.6× reduction in training steps to reach a target downstream accuracy (Figure 3 left)—translates directly to cost savings for any organization training large language models from scratch. The economics are straightforward: invest approximately 8% of your total training FLOPs in running DoReMi on a small proxy model (280M parameters), then train your main model (8B or larger) on the optimized mixture and finish training in roughly 40% of the original time. For a team training an 8B model on a budget of, say, $100k in cloud compute, the 8% overhead costs $8k and saves roughly $60k in main model training costs—a net savings of ~$52k per training run, with the domain weights reusable for future runs at zero additional cost. This is immediately actionable: the paper provides the complete algorithm (Algorithm 1), specifies all hyperparameters (`$\eta = 1$`, `$c = 10^{-3}$`, Adafactor optimizer, 200k steps at batch size 512 for The Pile-scale datasets), and releases optimized domain weights for The Pile publicly. A practitioner with access to The Pile can skip the proxy training entirely and directly train on the released DoReMi weights, paying 0% overhead for the full 2.6× speedup.

**Foundation model pretraining with task-agnostic data optimization.** For organizations developing foundation models intended to serve many downstream applications (rather than a single known task), DoReMi's task-agnostic optimization is directly preferable to downstream-tuned domain weights. The GLaM experiment (Section 3.2) demonstrates that DoReMi, without seeing any downstream data, matches the performance of domain weights explicitly tuned on the evaluation tasks. This means a team can optimize their pretraining mixture once with DoReMi and deploy the resulting model across a wide range of applications—QA, summarization, code generation, dialogue—without worrying that the data mixture was overfit to a particular benchmark. The practical workflow: define coarse domain categories (by data provenance or manual curation), run DoReMi with a small proxy model, train the production model on the resampled dataset, and distribute the resulting model as a general-purpose foundation model. This is particularly valuable for organizations that cannot anticipate all downstream uses at pretraining time—which is most organizations training foundation models.

**Data curation as an algorithmic rather than artisanal process.** The paper provides a recipe for converting raw, multi-domain data into an optimized training mixture using only two small model training runs, with no human judgment required beyond the initial domain definitions. This enables a substantially more automated data curation pipeline than the current standard of heuristic filtering and manual mixture adjustment. A concrete deployment scenario: an organization continuously ingests new data sources (e.g., new web crawls, partnerships with content providers, user-generated content). Rather than having data engineers manually decide how to incorporate each new source, the organization can periodically re-run DoReMi with the expanded domain set, producing updated domain weights that reflect the marginal value of each new data source relative to the existing mixture. The 8% overhead means this re-optimization can be performed affordably whenever the data distribution shifts significantly—for example, quarterly, coinciding with new data ingestion cycles. The resulting data pipeline is more responsive to changes in data availability and more principled in its allocation of training budget across sources.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper positions DoReMi against three named alternatives—heuristic domain weights, downstream-tuned domain weights, and example-level data selection methods—and articulates clear conditions favoring each approach. The decision rule grounded in the paper's findings is:

**Prefer DoReMi when:**
- You have multiple distinct data domains (the paper shows larger gains with 22 domains on The Pile vs. 8 on GLaM, suggesting more domains → more room for optimization) and no strong prior about the optimal mixture.
- Your downstream task distribution is unknown, broad, or likely to shift over time (DoReMi is task-agnostic and matched downstream-tuned weights on GLaM without using task data).
- You are training a model substantially larger than ~2B parameters (so the proxy training overhead of 8% or less is amortized by the main model speedup).
- You have access to a pretrained reference model on your initial domain weights, or can train one cheaply (the 280M reference model is the same cost as the proxy).

**Prefer heuristic domain weights (or stick with existing defaults) when:**
- Your domain count is very small (2–4 domains) and uniform weights are already near-optimal, making DoReMi's potential gains too small to justify the proxy training overhead.
- Your main model is small enough that the proxy overhead exceeds the speedup gains—for a 1B main model, DoReMi with a 280M proxy costs approximately 56% overhead, and the paper's matched-scale 1B experiments show a ~3 percentage point accuracy gain, which may not justify the cost.
- You have strong, validated domain expertise about the correct mixture (e.g., a legal document model where you know from extensive experimentation that 80% legal text, 20% general web text works best)—DoReMi is an optimization tool, not a substitute for domain knowledge when that knowledge is already reliable.

**Prefer downstream-tuned domain weights when:**
- You have a fixed, known, and narrow set of downstream tasks that fully represent your deployment distribution (e.g., a model exclusively for medical coding from clinical notes), and you can afford the computational cost of training many small models to sweep domain weights. In this scenario, task-specific tuning may extract additional signal beyond DoReMi's task-agnostic optimization, though the paper's GLaM results suggest DoReMi already captures most of the available gain.

**Prefer example-level data selection (e.g., Moore-Lewis, prioritized training) when:**
- Within-domain heterogeneity is large and you need finer-grained control than domain-level reweighting provides—for example, if your "web text" domain contains both high-quality Wikipedia-like articles and low-quality spam, and you need to filter at the example level rather than the domain level.
- You have a clear target distribution (e.g., you want your model to perform well specifically on legal text, so you select pretraining examples that resemble legal text). DoReMi avoids assuming a target distribution, which is a strength when the target is unknown but a limitation when the target is well-specified.
