# SSA: Sparse Sparse Attention by Aligning Full and Sparse Attention Outputs in Feature Space

**ArXiv:** [2511.20102](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.20102)

## 🎯 Pitch

This paper introduces **SSA (Sparse Sparse Attention)**, a training framework that integrates both sparse and full attention within a single model by alternating between attention modes with equal probability and applying bidirectional alignment to encourage inherently sparser attention distributions.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **SSA (Sparse Sparse Attention)**, a training framework that integrates both sparse and full attention within a single model by alternating between attention modes with equal probability and applying bidirectional alignment to encourage inherently sparser attention distributions. The approach is validated on 1B-parameter Llama-3.2-style models pretrained on the SmolLM corpus and evaluated on commonsense reasoning benchmarks (PIQA, HellaSwag, ARC), WikiText perplexity, and long-context tasks including LongBench, Needle-in-a-Haystack, and PG-19. SSA addresses two named gaps — the **Attention Gap** (train-inference distribution mismatch when applying sparse attention to full-attention-trained models, mitigated by including a sparse attention stream) and the **Capability Gap** (incomplete gradient flow in purely sparse-trained models, mitigated by including a full attention stream) — while using a bidirectional alignment loss with sparsity and commitment components to tighten the approximation error bound that the authors prove scales linearly with dropped attention mass. SSA achieves state-of-the-art performance under both inference modes, extrapolates smoothly to varying sparsity budgets, and delivers a ~2× inference speedup at 128k context length, establishing that explicitly encouraging sparse attention patterns during training benefits both sparse-attention and full-attention inference — but only when both attention modes are jointly trained with mutual alignment rather than deployed in isolation.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Sparse Attention Introduces Inevitable Performance Loss

Large language models face a fundamental tension between capability and cost. On the capability side, the standard self-attention mechanism in transformers provides each token with complete visibility over all preceding tokens — a design that enables rich contextual reasoning but carries quadratic computational complexity in sequence length. As LLMs have scaled to 32K, 128K, and even 1M-token context windows (Yang et al., 2025), this quadratic cost has become the primary bottleneck making both training and inference prohibitively expensive for extended contexts.

The natural solution is **sparse attention**: restrict each query token to attend only to a carefully selected subset of preceding tokens, reducing complexity from $\mathcal{O}(n^2)$ to sub-quadratic. The field has converged around several variants — sliding-window attention (Child et al., 2019; Beltagy et al., 2020), attention-sink methods that preserve initial tokens (Xiao et al., 2024), and dynamic block-sparse attention that partitions the context into blocks and selects the top-k most relevant blocks based on query-key similarity (Jiang et al., 2024; Lu et al., 2025). The goal is straightforward: achieve full-attention-level performance with the efficiency of sparse attention.

However, the paper identifies that this goal remains fundamentally elusive because of **two distinct failure modes** that prior work has addressed only partially and usually at the expense of the other. The entire contribution of SSA is organized around diagnosing and jointly resolving these two gaps.

---

### Gap 1: The Attention Gap — Training vs. Inference Distribution Mismatch

The first failure mode arises when practitioners take a model trained with full attention and then apply sparse attention at inference time — a paradigm the paper calls **Full-Sparse**. This is the dominant approach in the training-free sparse attention literature: methods like StreamingLLM (Xiao et al., 2024), InfLLM (Xiao et al., 2024), and MInference (Jiang et al., 2024) all operate by post-hoc approximating the full attention distribution with a sparse subset of tokens, exploiting the empirical observation that attention weights are naturally concentrated on a small number of tokens.

At first glance, this should work well: if the model's attention is already sparse, then approximating it with a sparse subset should incur minimal error. The paper formalizes this intuition in Theorem 1, proving that the approximation error between full and sparse attention representations scales linearly with the **dropped attention mass**:

$$\|h^{\text{full}}(t) - h^{\text{sparse}}(t)\| \leq \delta(t) \left( \max_{j \in S^c(t)} \|v(j)\| + \|h^{\text{sparse}}(t)\| \right)$$

where $\delta(t) = 1 - \text{AttnSparsity}(t)$ is the total attention mass assigned to tokens that sparse attention drops. This bound is elegant: if the attention distribution is already highly concentrated (high attention sparsity, low $\delta(t)$), then sparse attention should be a tight approximation.

**The problem** is that this analysis assumes the model's attention distribution under sparse inference matches its attention distribution under full training. Proposition 4.2 shows this assumption fails: when a model is trained with full attention but evaluated with sparse attention, the inference-time distribution shifts, incurring an additional KL-divergence penalty:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{FA}}^{\text{sparse}}(t) = \mathcal{L}_{\text{FA}}^{\text{full}}(t) + D_{\text{KL}}\left(p_{\theta}^{\text{full}}(t) \| p_{\theta}^{\text{sparse}}(t)\right)$$

In plain language: the model learned to produce output distributions that assume access to all tokens. When some tokens are suddenly removed at inference, its output distribution is forced away from what it learned, and the resulting penalty is proportional to how differently the model would behave under full versus sparse attention. This is a **train-inference distribution mismatch** — the model was optimized for one condition (full attention) but deployed under another (sparse attention), and no amount of post-hoc token selection cleverness can fully close the gap because the model never learned to function without those dropped tokens.

The practical consequence is visible throughout the paper's experiments: Full-Sparse models show substantial perplexity degradation when switching from full to sparse inference (FullAttn in Table 1 jumps from 15.13 PPL under full attention to 17.05 PPL under sparse attention with a receptive field of 256). Performance on downstream benchmarks similarly degrades. The Attention Gap is real and quantifiable.

---

### Gap 2: The Capability Gap — Incomplete Gradient Flow in Sparse-Only Training

The natural response to the Attention Gap is to **train with sparse attention from the start**, eliminating the mismatch entirely — a paradigm the paper calls **Sparse-Sparse**. Recent methods like MoBA (Lu et al., 2025) and NSA (Yuan et al., 2025) take this approach, training and deploying with block-sparse attention so the model never experiences a distribution shift.

This does solve the mismatch problem — Proposition 4.2 confirms that when training and inference use the same attention mechanism, no KL-divergence penalty arises. Table 1 bears this out: MoBA achieves lower perplexity than FullAttn under sparse inference (16.54 vs. 17.05 PPL at RF=256), validating that eliminating the mismatch helps.

**But a new problem emerges.** Proposition 4.1 identifies two learning deficiencies that plague Sparse-Sparse training:

**1. Learning Deficiency (Zero Gradients for Dropped Tokens).** In sparse attention, each query only attends to a subset of tokens — the ones in the selected blocks. For any token $j$ in a dropped (unselected) block, the gradient of the loss with respect to the attention weights involving $j$ is exactly zero. The model never receives a training signal telling it whether $j$ was relevant or not. Over the course of training, this means the model cannot learn to attend to tokens that fall outside its current block-selection policy, even if those tokens contain crucial information.

**2. Attention Suppression Deficiency (No Competitive Pressure).** In full attention, the softmax denominator includes all preceding tokens. This creates a competitive dynamic: for a token to receive high attention weight, it must out-compete every other token in the sequence. The model learns to make sharp, contrastive decisions about which tokens matter. In sparse attention, the softmax denominator only includes tokens in the selected blocks. Tokens in dropped blocks are completely excluded from the competition — they exert no downward pressure on the attention weights of selected tokens. The result is that Sparse-Sparse models develop **higher-entropy, less focused attention distributions** because they never learn to globally suppress irrelevant tokens.

These two deficiencies create what the paper calls the **Capability Gap**: even though sparse-trained models avoid the distribution mismatch, they fundamentally cannot match the representational quality of full-attention-trained models because their gradient signals are incomplete and their attention distributions are less discriminative. The model never learns what it's missing, so it can never fully approximate what full attention would produce.

The empirical evidence is striking and initially counterintuitive. Figure 1 (reproduced in the paper) shows that while Sparse Attention Models (SA/MoBA) achieve **lower perplexity** than Full Attention Models (FA/FullAttn) under sparse inference — consistent with avoiding the mismatch penalty — they achieve **lower downstream benchmark performance** (Table 1: MoBA gets 58.73% average vs. FullAttn's 59.62% under sparse inference). The model predicts tokens better on average (lower PPL) but reasons worse on structured tasks. This disconnect — better language modeling but worse reasoning — is a direct consequence of the Capability Gap: incomplete gradients produce models that are competent at local next-token prediction but lack the global feature quality needed for complex reasoning.

---

### Why Both Gaps Matter and Why Prior Approaches Are Insufficient

The paper's central diagnostic insight is that **Full-Sparse and Sparse-Sparse are complementary failure modes, not alternative solutions**. Each addresses one gap at the expense of the other:

- **Full-Sparse** (training-free methods): No Capability Gap because the model was trained with complete gradient flow from full attention. But suffers from the Attention Gap because inference uses a different attention mechanism than training. Performance degrades when switching to sparse inference, and the degradation is proportional to the KL divergence between full and sparse attention distributions.

- **Sparse-Sparse** (trainable methods like MoBA, NSA): No Attention Gap because training and inference use the same mechanism. But suffers from the Capability Gap because gradient signals are incomplete and attention distributions lack global contrastive pressure. The model never learns to achieve the representational quality of full attention.

The paper decomposes this clearly in Equation 5, expressing the target gap between a full-attention-trained model and a sparse-attention model as the sum of two terms:

$$\|h_{\text{FA}}^{\text{full}}(t) - h_{\text{SSA}}^{\text{sparse}}(t)\| \leq \|h_{\text{FA}}^{\text{full}}(t) - h_{\text{SSA}}^{\text{full}}(t)\| + \|h_{\text{SSA}}^{\text{full}}(t) - h_{\text{SSA}}^{\text{sparse}}(t)\|$$

Term (I) is the Capability Gap — how far the SSA model's full attention is from an ideal full-attention model. Term (II) is the Attention Gap — how far the SSA model's sparse attention is from its own full attention. Prior approaches minimize one term but leave the other unbounded.

---

### Where Existing Methods Fall Short (Beyond the Two Gaps)

The paper identifies additional specific limitations in prior work that make the two-gap problem harder than it might appear:

**MoBA's poor extrapolation.** Figure 3 reveals a critical weakness: MoBA fails to extrapolate well when given a larger receptive field at inference time. On commonsense reasoning benchmarks, MoBA's performance actually **degrades** when expanding the receptive field from 2048 to 4096. On LongBench, MoBA's performance drops from 17.33 (RF=512) to 14.87 (RF=4096) — a substantial regression. The paper attributes this to MoBA's insufficiently sparse attention distributions: when more tokens become available, the model attends to irrelevant tokens rather than suppressing them, introducing noise instead of useful context. The Capability Gap isn't just about absolute performance — it fundamentally breaks the ability to benefit from additional compute at inference time.

**NSA's non-extrapolability to full attention.** NSA (Yuan et al., 2025) reports performance surpassing full-attention baselines, but the paper's Appendix F uncovers a critical architectural limitation: NSA's three-component design (compression + selection + sliding window) cannot be cleanly extrapolated to full attention at inference. Replacing all three modules with full attention yields perplexity of 191.3 (Table A6) — far worse than random. The compression module, which maintains a compressed representation of all tokens, and the sliding window module both fail to extrapolate. Only the selection module (which is analogous to MoBA's block-sparse attention) extrapolates cleanly. This means NSA is essentially locked into sparse-only inference, whereas practical deployments often benefit from the flexibility to use full attention when latency allows (e.g., for retrieval-intensive tasks like Needle-in-a-Haystack, where SSA achieves 100% accuracy under full attention vs. NSA's inability to use full attention effectively).

**NSA's performance conflates architecture with sparsity.** Appendix G shows that removing NSA's sliding window module causes significant degradation: HellaSwag drops by 1.18 points, ARC-Easy drops by 1.1 points, and long-context perplexity degrades substantially (Table A8). Since the sliding window attends to 128 additional local tokens beyond the sparse selection's 256-token receptive field, NSA's reported advantages over other sparse methods partially reflect this larger effective receptive field rather than superior sparse attention design. SSA consistently outperforms NSA without the sliding window (Table A8), suggesting the sparse attention mechanism itself — not auxiliary components — drives SSA's gains.

### How SSA Positions Itself Relative to Existing Work

The paper frames SSA not as a new sparse attention pattern or a new selection algorithm, but as a **training framework that resolves both gaps simultaneously**. The three design principles, directly motivated by the theoretical analysis:

1. **Include full attention training** to eliminate the Capability Gap — the full attention stream provides complete gradient flow to all tokens and forces globally contrastive attention distributions through the complete softmax denominator (addressing Proposition 4.1).

2. **Include sparse attention training** to eliminate the Attention Gap — the sparse attention stream directly optimizes the model for sparse inference, eliminating the KL-divergence penalty that plagues Full-Sparse methods (addressing Proposition 4.2).

3. **Add bidirectional alignment between the two streams** to tighten the approximation error bound — by encouraging full attention outputs to mimic inherently sparser sparse attention outputs (the sparsity loss), the model learns attention distributions that are naturally more concentrated, reducing $\delta(t)$ in Theorem 1's bound. By regularizing sparse attention outputs toward full attention outputs (the commitment loss), the model prevents the sparse stream from drifting into a degenerate representational space that full attention wouldn't produce.

The critical novelty is not that SSA uses both attention modes — concurrent work like InfLLM-v2 (Zhao et al., 2025) also combines dense and sparse attention — but rather the **bidirectional alignment** that makes this combination productive rather than merely additive. Without alignment, abruptly switching between full and sparse attention during training causes instability (the NoAlignmentLoss ablation in Table 2 drops commonsense reasoning performance from 49.69 to 48.38). With alignment, the two modes mutually reinforce each other: full attention provides the training signal quality that sparse attention lacks, while sparse attention provides the structural regularization that pushes full attention toward sparser, more robust patterns.

The paper also departs from the prevailing focus on **sparse attention mechanisms** (designing better selection patterns) and instead focuses on **attention quality** (making whatever sparse pattern is used work better). This is a shift in research framing: rather than asking "how do we select the best tokens?", SSA asks "how do we train so that the selected tokens contain most of the attention mass naturally, regardless of the selection mechanism?" The answer — joint training with alignment — turns out to improve not only sparse inference but also full-attention inference (SSA beats FullAttn on commonsense reasoning under full attention: 60.39 vs. 60.01), suggesting that encouraging sparser attention is broadly beneficial for model quality, not just efficiency.

This framing is important because it implies that improvements in sparse attention performance need not come at the cost of architectural complexity (as with NSA's multi-component design) or bespoke training procedures (as with MoBA's specific block-sparse pattern). SSA's sparse attention stream uses the same block-sparse mechanism as MoBA, and its gains come entirely from the training framework — meaning the approach could potentially be applied on top of any sparse attention mechanism.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation (Approachable Technical Breakdown)

SSA is a **training framework** — not a new attention mechanism or a new model architecture — that teaches a single transformer model to use either full or sparse attention effectively by alternating between both modes during training while forcing their hidden representations to align. The problem it solves is that models trained only with full attention perform poorly when switched to sparse attention at inference (the Attention Gap), while models trained only with sparse attention never learn to produce the high-quality representations that full attention enables (the Capability Gap). The shape of the solution is a dual-stream training procedure where at each step the model randomly picks one attention mode for the forward pass, computes the other mode as an auxiliary signal, then applies a bidirectional loss to pull both modes' outputs toward each other in feature space — encouraging the model to learn inherently sparser attention patterns that make sparse attention a tight approximation to full attention, while retaining the complete gradient flow and contrastive learning pressure that only full attention provides.

---

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

SSA has four major components that interact during each training iteration:

1. **Mode Sampler** — a Bernoulli random variable that selects which attention mode (Full or Sparse) will serve as the primary forward pass for the current training step. Selected with equal probability (50/50).

2. **Main Attention Stream** — the selected mode (Full or Sparse) that computes the actual hidden representations propagated through all subsequent layers and used to produce the final output logits for the cross-entropy loss. This is the "working" attention that the model uses to generate predictions.

3. **Auxiliary Attention Stream** — the counterpart mode (Sparse if Main is Full; Full if Main is Sparse) computed at each layer using the same query, key, and value projections as the main stream. Its outputs are **not** propagated to subsequent layers — they exist solely for computing alignment signals.

4. **Bidirectional Alignment Loss** — two complementary loss terms computed per layer between the main and auxiliary attention outputs: a **sparsity loss** that encourages full attention outputs (when Full is the main stream) to match sparse attention outputs (the auxiliary), pushing the model toward more concentrated attention distributions; and a **commitment loss** that regularizes sparse attention outputs (when Sparse is the main stream) to remain close to full attention outputs (the auxiliary), preventing the sparse stream from drifting into a degenerate representational space. These per-layer losses are averaged across all layers and added to the standard cross-entropy loss with a weighting coefficient $\alpha$.

**Information flow** at each training step: input embeddings enter the first transformer layer $l = 1$ → the mode sampler selects Main mode (e.g., Full) and designates Aux mode (Sparse) → query, key, value projections are computed once → MainAttn computes the attention output propagated forward → AuxAttn computes the attention output used only for alignment → the sparsity loss penalizes distance between Main and stop-grad(Aux) → the commitment loss penalizes distance between Aux and stop-grad(Main) → the gated main output is added to the residual stream → the layer's FFN processes the result → the procedure repeats for all $L$ layers → after the final layer, layer-norm and the LM head produce logits → cross-entropy loss is computed against targets → total loss = cross-entropy + $\alpha \cdot$ (average of all per-layer alignment losses).

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First, the formal error decomposition (Equation 5)** — because this is the paper's organizing theoretical insight that motivates every design choice. It shows why training needs both full and sparse attention plus alignment, not just one or the other.

- **Second, the dual-stream sampling mechanism** — the core training loop that alternates between attention modes at each step. This includes the Bernoulli routing, the gating mechanism used across all methods for fairness, and the computational cost justification for alternating rather than joint computation.

- **Third, the sparse attention mechanism itself** — the block-sparse selection procedure inherited from MoBA that serves as the sparse attention stream. Understanding the mechanics (block partitioning, mean-pooled block keys, top-k selection, token-level attention within selected blocks) is prerequisite for understanding what the alignment loss is aligning.

- **Fourth, the bidirectional alignment loss** — the sparsity loss, the commitment loss, their mathematical forms, why they use stop-gradient operators, why the alignment operates at the value level rather than the attention map level, and why SmoothL1 is used as the distance function. This is the core technical novelty.

- **Fifth, the overall training objective** — how cross-entropy and alignment are combined, the weighting coefficient $\alpha$, and the full training algorithm as a procedure.

- **Sixth, the theoretical bounds** — Theorem 1 (the error bound scaling linearly with dropped attention mass) and the two Propositions, in the order needed to understand why the design choices are mathematically motivated rather than arbitrary. These provide the *why* behind the architecture.

- **Seventh, practical training configurations** — hyperparameter values, model sizes, training data, and implementation details that make the framework concrete and reproducible.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **training methodology paper** whose core idea is that a model trained with alternating full and sparse attention, constrained by bidirectional feature-space alignment, can achieve sparse-attention inference quality approaching full-attention training quality while simultaneously producing sparser attention distributions that benefit both inference modes.

---

#### The Organizing Decomposition: Why Joint Training with Alignment Is Necessary

Before describing the architecture, the paper establishes the mathematical motivation. The ultimate goal is for a model trained with SSA to approximate the full-attention representations of an ideally-trained full-attention model when running sparse inference:

$$\|h_{\text{FA}}^{\text{full}}(t) - h_{\text{SSA}}^{\text{sparse}}(t)\|$$

where $h_{\text{FA}}^{\text{full}}(t)$ is the hidden representation at query position $t$ produced by a model trained with full attention and evaluated with full attention (the ideal reference), and $h_{\text{SSA}}^{\text{sparse}}(t)$ is the representation produced by the SSA model under sparse attention inference (the target deployment condition).

**Why this specific comparison matters:** this is the deployment-relevant metric — a practitioner wants their sparse-attention model to perform as well as a full-attention model would, but using fewer tokens. The norm measures the representational distance between what the ideal model produces and what the deployed model produces.

This gap cannot be minimized by any single mechanism. By the triangle inequality, it decomposes into two terms:

$$\|h_{\text{FA}}^{\text{full}}(t) - h_{\text{SSA}}^{\text{sparse}}(t)\| \leq \underbrace{\|h_{\text{FA}}^{\text{full}}(t) - h_{\text{SSA}}^{\text{full}}(t)\|}_{\text{(I) Capability Gap}} + \underbrace{\|h_{\text{SSA}}^{\text{full}}(t) - h_{\text{SSA}}^{\text{sparse}}(t)\|}_{\text{(II) Attention Gap}}$$

where Term (I) measures how far the SSA model's full-attention representations are from the ideal full-attention model's representations — a measure of how much representational quality is lost due to training deficiencies. Term (II) measures how far the SSA model's sparse-attention representations diverge from its own full-attention representations — a measure of the approximation error introduced by the sparsification itself.

**Why this decomposition is the organizing insight:** it reveals that the total gap cannot be closed by addressing only one term. Full-Sparse methods (training-free sparse attention) minimize Term (I) by training with full attention (so $h_{\text{SSA}}^{\text{full}}$ is close to $h_{\text{FA}}^{\text{full}}$), but Term (II) is unbounded because the model was never trained to produce consistent representations under sparse attention. Sparse-Sparse methods (training natively with sparse attention) minimize Term (II) by training under the target distribution, but Term (I) is unbounded because the model never receives complete gradient signals or learns globally contrastive attention — so even under full attention, its representations are worse than the ideal.

SSA's architecture is designed to simultaneously minimize both terms: incorporate full attention training to close Term (I) (the full attention stream provides complete gradient flow and contrastive pressure), incorporate sparse attention training to close Term (II) (the model directly optimizes for sparse inference), and add bidirectional alignment to further tighten both bounds — the sparsity loss reduces $\delta(t)$ (the dropped attention mass), making sparse attention a better approximation of full attention (tightening Term II), while the commitment loss prevents sparse attention from drifting far from full attention representations (tightening Term I).

---

#### The Dual-Stream Sampling Mechanism

The training procedure at each iteration:

**Step 1: Mode selection.** A Bernoulli random variable with probability $p_{\text{FA}} = 0.5$ determines which attention mechanism serves as the primary (main) compute path. If the sample is 1, FullAttention is the main path and SparseAttention is the auxiliary path. If 0, the roles reverse.

**Step 2: Shared query, key, value projections.** Regardless of which mode is selected, the model computes query, key, and value projections exactly once per layer:

$$q(t) = Q_l \cdot h, \quad k(t) = K_l \cdot h, \quad v(t) = V_l \cdot h$$

where $Q_l$, $K_l$, $V_l$ are the standard learnable projection matrices for layer $l$, and $h$ is the normalized hidden state input to the attention sublayer. These projected representations are shared between the main and auxiliary attention computations — the difference is only in which tokens each mode attends to, not in how the token representations themselves are computed.

**Step 3: Main attention computation.** Using the selected mode, the model computes the attention hidden representation that will be propagated to subsequent layers:

$$h_{\text{main}} = \text{MainAttn}(q, k, v)$$

where MainAttn is either FullAttn (attending to all preceding tokens with standard softmax attention) or SparseAttn (attending to selected blocks only, as described in the next subsection). This output passes through the gating mechanism and enters the residual stream.

**Step 4: Auxiliary attention computation.** Simultaneously, the counterpart attention mode is computed on the same projections:

$$h_{\text{aux}} = \text{AuxAttn}(q, k, v)$$

The critical design choice: $h_{\text{aux}}$ is **not propagated** to subsequent layers. It does not enter the residual stream, does not pass through the FFN, and does not contribute to the next layer's input. Its sole purpose is to serve as a target or regularizer for the alignment loss. This means the auxiliary computation adds FLOPs at the current layer but does not affect the forward pass of any other layer — it is "side information" used only for the training objective.

**Step 5: Gating and residual connection.** A learned gate modulates the main attention output before it enters the residual stream:

$$x \leftarrow x + O_l \left( \text{Gate}_l(h) \odot h_{\text{main}} \right)$$

where $\text{Gate}_l$ is a learned gating function (gated attention, adopted from Qiu et al., 2025), $O_l$ is the output projection matrix, and $\odot$ denotes element-wise multiplication. The gating mechanism is applied to all methods (FullAttn, MoBA, NSA, SSA) for fair comparison, and it also serves to mitigate the attention sink phenomenon — the tendency for early tokens to accumulate disproportionate attention mass.

**Step 6: Alignment loss computation.** After both attention outputs are computed, two distance terms are added to an accumulating per-layer alignment loss:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{align}}^{(l)} = \|h_{\text{main}} - \text{sg}(h_{\text{aux}})\| + \|h_{\text{aux}} - \text{sg}(h_{\text{main}})\|$$

where $\text{sg}(\cdot)$ is the stop-gradient operator — the argument is treated as a constant target with no gradient flow. The first term is the sparsity loss (when Main is Full and Aux is Sparse) or the commitment loss (when Main is Sparse and Aux is Full); the second term is the reverse. Both are computed every iteration, regardless of which mode was selected as main.

**Step 7: Loss accumulation across layers.** Steps 3–6 repeat for all $L = 16$ layers. The final alignment loss is the average across layers:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{align}} = \frac{1}{L} \sum_{l=1}^{L} \mathcal{L}_{\text{align}}^{(l)}$$

**Why alternating rather than joint computation?** The paper states that alternating reduces computational cost by half compared to computing both streams and propagating both through all layers. Since the auxiliary stream is not propagated, the additional cost per training step is marginal (~17% overhead over MoBA, per Table A2). This design ensures the model processes the same total number of tokens as baselines, making comparisons fair in terms of tokens seen during training.

**Why are the streams described as "streams" rather than branches?** The term "stream" is intentional: only one mode's output flows through the entire network at each step. This prevents the model from learning to rely on having both modes available simultaneously — at inference, only one mode is active, so the training must reflect this constraint.

---

#### The Sparse Attention Mechanism (Block-Sparse Selection)

The sparse attention branch uses a block-sparse mechanism identical in structure to MoBA (Lu et al., 2025). This subsection describes what happens inside SparseAttn when it is called.

**Block partitioning.** The sequence of preceding tokens (length $N$) is divided into contiguous blocks of fixed size $s = 16$ tokens each (or $s = 32$ for the 1024-receptive-field configuration). This yields $n = N/s$ blocks. Tokens within each block are contiguous in the original sequence.

**Block-level key computation.** For each block, a single representative key vector is computed by mean-pooling the token-level key vectors within that block:

$$k_{\text{block}}^{(b)} = \frac{1}{s} \sum_{j \in \text{block}(b)} k(j)$$

where $k(j)$ is the key vector for token $j$, and $\text{block}(b)$ denotes the set of token indices belonging to block $b$. This mean-pooling reduces the cost of computing query-block similarities from $\mathcal{O}(N)$ per query (comparing to every token) to $\mathcal{O}(n)$ per query (comparing to every block).

**Block selection via dot-product similarity.** For each query token at position $t$, the model computes dot-product similarity scores between the query vector $q(t)$ and all preceding block-level key vectors:

$$s(t, b) = q(t) \cdot k_{\text{block}}^{(b)}$$

The blocks are ranked by these similarity scores, and the top-$k$ blocks are selected. The number of selected blocks $k$ determines the receptive field: $k = 16$ yields a receptive field of $16 \times 16 = 256$ tokens (the default configuration); $k = 32$ yields a receptive field of $32 \times 32 = 1024$ tokens; and $k = 64$ yields $64 \times 16 = 1024$ tokens with a different block size.

**Token-level attention within selected blocks.** Once the top-$k$ blocks are identified, all individual tokens within those selected blocks are gathered into reduced key and value matrices:

$$\tilde{K} = \{ k(j) \mid j \in \text{selected blocks} \}, \quad \tilde{V} = \{ v(j) \mid j \in \text{selected blocks} \}$$

Standard softmax attention is then computed between the query and only these selected tokens:

$$h_{\text{sparse}}(t) = \text{softmax}\left( \frac{q(t) \tilde{K}^T}{\sqrt{d}} \right) \tilde{V}^T$$

where $d = 64$ is the head dimension.

**Computational complexity.** The two-stage computation has approximate cost $n^2 + (ks)^2$ (per query). The first stage computes block-level similarities ($n^2$ comparisons between all block pairs), and the second stage computes token-level attention within the selected blocks ($(ks)^2$ operations). At short context lengths, block-level comparison overhead can dominate, making sparse attention slower than full attention. At longer contexts (e.g., 128k), the sparsity ratio $k/n$ becomes small, delivering the ~2× speedup reported in Table A3.

**Why block-level selection rather than token-level?** Token-level top-k selection (as in DSA, DeepSeek-AI, 2025) would require computing query-key similarities for all tokens, which is $\mathcal{O}(N)$ — the very quadratic cost sparse attention aims to avoid. Block-level selection using mean-pooled keys reduces the first-stage cost to $\mathcal{O}(n)$, providing sub-quadratic scaling while still capturing the most relevant context regions.

**Training receptive field choice.** The paper reports that SSA is trained with a receptive field of 256 tokens (block size 16, top-16 blocks) and then extrapolated to larger receptive fields at inference (tested at 512, 1024, 2048, and 4096). The reason: the smaller receptive field imposes stronger structural constraints, providing more effective regularization for learning sparse attention patterns (see Section 7.5: training with RF=256 yields better results than training with RF=1024, which the paper attributes to "inducing higher sparsity regularisation").

#### The Bidirectional Alignment Loss

This is the core technical novelty of SSA — the mechanism that makes alternating between attention modes productive rather than merely additive.

**Why alignment in feature space rather than attention-map space?** The obvious approach to aligning full and sparse attention would be to directly compare their attention weight distributions — for example, minimizing the KL divergence between the full attention map and the sparse attention map. This is mathematically natural because Theorem 1 bounds the error in terms of attention mass $\delta(t)$. However, the paper explicitly rejects this approach: "value-level alignment is substantially more efficient than directly aligning attention distributions, which would require materializing dense attention maps incompatible with FlashAttention-2" (Section 5). FlashAttention-2 (Dao, 2024) achieves its speed by fusing the softmax computation with the value-weighted sum, never explicitly materializing the full $N \times N$ attention matrix in GPU memory. Requiring attention-map alignment would force the model to compute and store this matrix, defeating the efficiency purpose of sparse attention. Aligning the **output vectors** (the value-weighted sums) achieves a similar effect — encouraging the full attention outputs to resemble sparse attention outputs — without ever computing the full attention matrix, because only the final vectors need to be compared.

**Alignment loss components.** For each layer $l$, after computing $h_{\text{main}}$ and $h_{\text{aux}}$:

**Sparsity loss (Full → Sparse alignment):** When the main stream is Full and the auxiliary is Sparse, the loss term:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{sparsity}}^{(l)} = \|h_{\text{full}} - \text{sg}(h_{\text{sparse}})\|$$

computes the distance between the full attention output and the stop-gradiented sparse attention output.

> where $h_{\text{full}}$ is the hidden representation produced by full attention at layer $l$, $h_{\text{sparse}}$ is the hidden representation produced by sparse attention at the same layer, and $\text{sg}(\cdot)$ is the stop-gradient operator.
> 
> **What it computes:** a scalar distance (using SmoothL1, described below) measuring how far the full attention output vector is from the sparse attention output vector. Because sparse attention is stop-gradiented, gradients only flow through the full attention computation, encouraging the model to adjust its full attention parameters to produce outputs that resemble what sparse attention would produce.
> 
> **Why this form:** the gradient flows only into full attention. This means the model learns to produce full attention outputs that are inherently sparser — if the full attention output matches the sparse attention output, the tokens that sparse attention dropped must have had negligible contribution to the output. This reduces $\delta(t)$ in Theorem 1's bound, tightening the approximation error between full and sparse attention. Without the stop-gradient, the sparse attention would also adjust to match full attention, potentially learning to compensate for dropped tokens rather than encouraging sparsity — the opposite of what's needed. The stop-gradient makes this an asymmetric regularization: full attention is pulled toward sparser behavior, but sparse attention is not pulled toward denser behavior.

**Commitment loss (Sparse → Full alignment):** When the main stream is Sparse and the auxiliary is Full:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{commitment}}^{(l)} = \|h_{\text{sparse}} - \text{sg}(h_{\text{full}})\|$$

computes the distance between the sparse attention output and the stop-gradiented full attention output.

> **What it computes:** a scalar distance measuring how far the sparse attention output vector is from the full attention output vector. Because full attention is stop-gradiented, gradients only flow through the sparse attention computation.
> 
> **Why this form:** the gradient flows only into sparse attention. This prevents the sparse attention stream from drifting into a degenerate representational space that full attention would never produce — it acts as a regularizer, keeping the sparse representations "anchored" to the richer full-attention representational manifold. The paper explicitly analogizes this to the commitment loss in VQ-VAE (van den Oord et al., 2017), where the encoder is regularized to produce representations close to the learned codebook vectors, and to KL regularization in RLHF (Ouyang et al., 2022), where the policy is penalized for deviating too far from the reference policy. In both analogies, the purpose is the same: prevent a learned component from exploiting freedom in ways that produce degenerate outputs.
> 
> **Crucially:** the commitment loss and the sparsity loss are the *same mathematical operation* applied in opposite directions. When Main=Full, Aux=Sparse, the sparsity loss translates to $\|h_{\text{main}} - \text{sg}(h_{\text{aux}})\|$ (pulling full toward sparse) and the commitment loss translates to $\|h_{\text{aux}} - \text{sg}(h_{\text{main}})\|$ (anchoring sparse to full). When Main=Sparse, Aux=Full, the roles swap. Both terms are computed every iteration because both $h_{\text{main}}$ and $h_{\text{aux}}$ exist regardless of which stream was selected. This bidirectionality is what the ablation in Table 2 validates as necessary: using only Full→Sparse alignment (one direction) or only Sparse→Full alignment causes training to collapse to NaN loss, which the paper attributes to "asymmetric over-distillation" — one mode overfits to the patterns of the other, degrading its own capability.

**Total per-layer alignment loss:**

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{align}}^{(l)} = \|h_{\text{main}} - \text{sg}(h_{\text{aux}})\| + \|h_{\text{aux}} - \text{sg}(h_{\text{main}})\|$$

And the total alignment loss across the model:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{align}} = \frac{1}{L} \sum_{l=1}^{L} \mathcal{L}_{\text{align}}^{(l)}$$

where $L = 16$ for the 1B model.

**Choice of distance function: SmoothL1.** The paper uses SmoothL1 loss (Girshick, 2015) for both alignment components. SmoothL1 is defined as:

$$\text{SmoothL1}(x, y) = \sum_i \begin{cases} 0.5(x_i - y_i)^2 & \text{if } |x_i - y_i| < 1 \\ |x_i - y_i| - 0.5 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

where the sum is over all elements of the hidden representation vectors.

> **What it computes:** an element-wise loss that is quadratic (L2-like) for small differences and linear (L1-like) for large differences. For each element of the hidden vectors, if the difference magnitude is less than 1, the loss is $0.5 \times \text{diff}^2$; otherwise it is $|\text{diff}| - 0.5$.
> 
> **Why SmoothL1 over L2 or L1:** pure L2 loss would heavily penalize large differences, which could cause the alignment loss to dominate training early when the two attention modes produce very different outputs — potentially destabilizing optimization. Pure L1 loss has constant gradient magnitude for all non-zero differences, which is less sensitive to outliers but has a discontinuity in the gradient at zero that can cause oscillation. SmoothL1 combines the benefits: it behaves like L2 for small differences (giving smooth gradients near the optimum) and like L1 for large differences (giving bounded gradients that prevent outlier dominance). The paper's ablation in Table A14 shows L2 loss with a reduced $\alpha$ performs slightly below SmoothL1 but still outperforms MoBA, suggesting the choice of distance metric is flexible as long as some alignment is present.

#### The Overall Training Objective

The final loss function combines the standard language modeling objective with the alignment regularizer:

$$\mathcal{L} = \mathbb{E}_{\text{mode} \sim \{\text{full}, \text{sparse}\}} \left[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{mode}}^{\text{ce}} \right] + \alpha \cdot \mathcal{L}_{\text{align}}$$

where $\mathcal{L}_{\text{mode}}^{\text{ce}}$ is the cross-entropy loss between the model's predicted token probabilities (computed from the main stream's output) and the ground-truth next tokens, $\mathcal{L}_{\text{align}}$ is the averaged per-layer bidirectional alignment loss, and $\alpha$ is a weighting coefficient that balances the two objectives.

> **What it computes:** at each training step, the model incurs the standard next-token prediction loss $\mathcal{L}_{\text{mode}}^{\text{ce}}$ under whichever attention mode was selected (full or sparse), plus an additional penalty $\alpha \cdot \mathcal{L}_{\text{align}}$ that measures how far the two attention modes' hidden representations are from each other across all layers.
> 
> **Why this form:** the expectation over modes ensures both attention modes receive training signal — the model learns to predict tokens accurately under both full attention (preserving representational quality) and sparse attention (eliminating the inference-time mismatch). The alignment term acts as a regularizer, not a replacement for the primary objective: the model is still fundamentally trained to predict next tokens; alignment merely shapes the internal representations to be consistent across modes. This distinguishes SSA from approaches that use alignment as the sole training signal (e.g., distillation), which would risk the model learning to produce aligned but uninformative representations.
> 
> **The weight $\alpha$:** controls the relative strength of the alignment signal. The paper's ablation in Table A14 shows $\alpha = 10$ (the default) achieves the best balance on the 300M-parameter models: $\alpha = 5$ underperforms on commonsense reasoning (48.71 vs. 49.69 average), while $\alpha = 20$ also underperforms (49.45), suggesting too little alignment allows mode divergence, while too much alignment over-constrains the representations at the expense of predictive accuracy.

**Complete training algorithm (Algorithm 1 from the paper's appendix):**

1. Receive input embeddings $x_0$, targets $y$, number of layers $L$, alignment weight $\alpha$, and full-attention sampling probability $p_{\text{FA}}$.
2. If training: sample `goFA` from Bernoulli($p_{\text{FA}}$). If inference: set `goFA` to False (always use sparse attention for efficiency).
3. Set MainAttn = FullAttn if `goFA` else SparseAttn; Set AuxAttn = SparseAttn if `goFA` else FullAttn.
4. Initialize $x \leftarrow x_0$, $\mathcal{L}_{\text{align}} \leftarrow 0$.
5. For each layer $l = 1$ to $L$:
   - Compute normalized hidden state $h \leftarrow \text{Norm1}_l(x)$.
   - Compute query, key, value: $q \leftarrow Q_l(h)$, $k \leftarrow K_l(h)$, $v \leftarrow V_l(h)$.
   - Compute main and auxiliary attention outputs: $h_{\text{main}} \leftarrow \text{MainAttn}(q, k, v)$, $h_{\text{aux}} \leftarrow \text{AuxAttn}(q, k, v)$.
   - Compute sparsity and commitment losses (both directions simultaneously): $\mathcal{L}_{\text{sparsity}} \leftarrow \|h_{\text{main}} - \text{sg}(h_{\text{aux}})\|$, $\mathcal{L}_{\text{commit}} \leftarrow \|h_{\text{aux}} - \text{sg}(h_{\text{main}})\|$.
   - Accumulate: $\mathcal{L}_{\text{align}} \leftarrow \mathcal{L}_{\text{align}} + \mathcal{L}_{\text{sparsity}} + \mathcal{L}_{\text{commit}}$.
   - Gating and residual: $x \leftarrow x + O_l(\text{Gate}_l(h) \odot h_{\text{main}})$.
   - FFN: $h \leftarrow \text{Norm2}_l(x)$, $x \leftarrow x + \text{FFN}_l(h)$.
6. Average alignment loss: $\mathcal{L}_{\text{align}} \leftarrow \mathcal{L}_{\text{align}} / L$.
7. Final layer norm and LM head: $x \leftarrow \text{LayerNorm}(x)$, $\text{logits} \leftarrow \text{LMHead}(x)$.
8. If training: compute cross-entropy $\mathcal{L}_{\text{ce}} \leftarrow \text{CrossEntropy}(\text{logits}, y)$, return logits and $\mathcal{L}_{\text{ce}} + \alpha \cdot \mathcal{L}_{\text{align}}$.
9. If inference: return logits only (no alignment loss computation; only MainAttn is used).

**Why the alignment loss is averaged rather than summed:** summing the per-layer losses without averaging would make the alignment signal scale with the number of layers, making $\alpha$ dependent on model depth. Averaging decouples $\alpha$ from architecture size, making the hyperparameter more transferable.

#### Theoretical Motivation: The Approximation Error Bound (Theorem 1)

The alignment loss is not an arbitrary regularization — it is directly motivated by Theorem 1, which quantifies how approximation quality depends on attention sparsity.

**Definition 1 (Attention Sparsity):** For query token $t$ with attention weights $\{a(t, j)\}_{j < t}$ (where $a(t, j)$ is the softmax-normalized attention weight from query $t$ to key $j$), the attention sparsity is:

$$\text{AttnSparsity}(t) = \sum_{j < t} a(t, j) \cdot \mathbf{1}[\text{blk}(j) \in \mathcal{T}_k(t)]$$

where $\text{blk}(j)$ denotes the block index containing token $j$, $\mathcal{T}_k(t)$ is the set of top-$k$ blocks ranked by block-level attention scores for query $t$, and $\mathbf{1}[\cdot]$ is the indicator function (1 if the condition holds, 0 otherwise).

> **What it computes:** the total attention mass (probability mass under the softmax distribution) that falls within the selected blocks. If the attention distribution is perfectly concentrated in the selected blocks, AttnSparsity$(t) = 1$ — all attention mass is captured. If attention is uniformly spread and the selected blocks cover only a small fraction of tokens, AttnSparsity$(t)$ is proportionally small.
> 
> **Why this metric matters:** it directly measures how well sparse attention approximates full attention. If AttnSparsity$(t)$ is high, the tokens that sparse attention drops have tiny attention weights, so their exclusion should have minimal impact on the output.

**Theorem 1 (Sparse Attention Error Bound):** Let $S(t)$ denote the set of tokens in selected blocks and $S^c(t)$ the set of dropped tokens. Define the dropped attention mass as $\delta(t) = \sum_{j \in S^c(t)} a_{\text{full}}^j(t) = 1 - \text{AttnSparsity}(t)$. Then:

$$\|h^{\text{full}}(t) - h^{\text{sparse}}(t)\| \leq \delta(t) \left( \max_{j \in S^c(t)} \|v(j)\| + \|h^{\text{sparse}}(t)\| \right)$$

where $h^{\text{full}}(t)$ is the full attention output, $h^{\text{sparse}}(t)$ is the sparse attention output, $v(j)$ denotes the value vector of token $j$, and $\|\cdot\|$ denotes the Euclidean norm.

> **What it bounds:** the representational error introduced by using sparse attention instead of full attention — i.e., Term (II) in the decomposition. The bound states that this error is at most $\delta(t)$ times the sum of two terms: the maximum value-vector norm among dropped tokens, plus the norm of the sparse attention output itself.
> 
> **How the bound is derived** (walking through the proof sketch to show the reasoning):
> 1. Decompose the full attention output into contributions from selected and dropped tokens: $h^{\text{full}}(t) = \sum_{j \in S(t)} a_{\text{full}}(t, j) v(j) + \sum_{j \in S^c(t)} a_{\text{full}}(t, j) v(j)$.
> 2. For tokens in selected blocks, the ratio of full attention weight to sparse attention weight is $1 - \delta(t)$, because the softmax denominator in sparse attention excludes the dropped tokens. So $\sum_{j \in S(t)} a_{\text{full}}(t, j) v(j) = (1 - \delta(t)) h^{\text{sparse}}(t)$.
> 3. Substituting: $h^{\text{full}}(t) = (1 - \delta(t)) h^{\text{sparse}}(t) + \sum_{j \in S^c(t)} a_{\text{full}}(t, j) v(j)$.
> 4. Rearranging: $h^{\text{full}}(t) - h^{\text{sparse}}(t) = \sum_{j \in S^c(t)} a_{\text{full}}(t, j) v(j) - \delta(t) h^{\text{sparse}}(t)$.
> 5. Taking norms and applying the triangle inequality together with the fact that $\sum_{j \in S^c(t)} a_{\text{full}}(t, j) = \delta(t)$ yields the bound.
> 
> **Why this form is important:** the bound scales linearly with $\delta(t)$ — the dropped attention mass. This means that to make sparse attention a tight approximation of full attention, the model must learn to concentrate its attention mass heavily in the selected blocks, minimizing $\delta(t)$. This directly motivates the sparsity loss: by encouraging full attention outputs to match sparse attention outputs, the model learns to produce attention distributions where the dropped tokens contribute negligibly. The bound provides a mathematical guarantee: if the alignment training succeeds in producing inherently sparser attention (higher AttnSparsity, lower $\delta(t)$), then the approximation error is proportionally reduced, regardless of the specific sparse selection mechanism.

#### Theoretical Motivation: The Two Gaps as Propositions

**Proposition 4.1 (Gradient Update Deficiency).** Training exclusively with sparse attention induces:

1. **Learning Deficiency:** For any token $j$ in a dropped block, the gradient of the loss with respect to the attention involving $j$ is identically zero. Because the token never appears in the attention computation for the current query, there is no path for an error signal to update the model's understanding of whether $j$ is relevant to the query. Over training, tokens that tend to be dropped remain unlearned — the model never discovers their relevance.

2. **Attention Suppression Deficiency:** In sparse attention, the softmax denominator includes only tokens in selected blocks: $\sum_{k \in S(t)} \exp(s(t, k))$. Dropped tokens are entirely excluded from the normalization. In full attention, the denominator includes all tokens: $\sum_{k < t} \exp(s(t, k))$. This means that in full attention training, for a token to receive high weight, it must out-compete every other token. In sparse attention training, tokens only compete with other selected tokens. The result is that sparse-trained models develop higher-entropy, less-discriminative attention distributions — they never experience the competitive pressure to globally suppress irrelevant tokens.

> **Why this matters for the architecture:** these deficiencies directly motivate incorporating full attention training into SSA. The full attention stream provides complete gradient flow (every token receives a training signal, addressing deficiency 1) and globally contrastive softmax denominators (all tokens compete, forcing sharper discrimination, addressing deficiency 2). Without the full attention stream, SSA would reduce to MoBA-like training and inherit these deficiencies.

**Proposition 4.2 (Attention Mechanism Alignment).** Let $p_\theta^{\text{full}}$ and $p_\theta^{\text{sparse}}$ denote the output token distributions under full and sparse attention, respectively. For a model trained only with full attention, sparse inference incurs an additional KL-divergence penalty:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{FA}}^{\text{sparse}}(t) = \mathcal{L}_{\text{FA}}^{\text{full}}(t) + D_{\text{KL}}\left(p_\theta^{\text{full}}(t) \| p_\theta^{\text{sparse}}(t)\right)$$

where $\mathcal{L}_{\text{FA}}^{\text{sparse}}(t)$ is the negative log-likelihood under sparse inference, $\mathcal{L}_{\text{FA}}^{\text{full}}(t)$ is the NLL under full inference, and $D_{\text{KL}}$ is the Kullback-Leibler divergence.

> **What it shows:** when training and inference use different attention mechanisms, the loss is the training-mode loss plus a KL divergence penalty measuring how different the output distribution is under the two modes. When training and inference use the same mechanism, this penalty is zero.
> 
> **Why this matters for the architecture:** this directly motivates incorporating sparse attention training into SSA. By training with sparse attention at least some of the time, the model learns to produce output distributions that are consistent between training and sparse inference, eliminating the KL penalty. Remark: the paper notes that $\mathcal{L}_{\text{SA}}^{\text{sparse}} > \mathcal{L}_{\text{FA}}^{\text{full}}$ generally holds (sparse-trained models are worse than full-trained models under ideal conditions), but a sufficiently large KL divergence can yield $\mathcal{L}_{\text{SA}}^{\text{sparse}} < \mathcal{L}_{\text{FA}}^{\text{sparse}}$ — a sparse-trained model can achieve better perplexity under sparse inference than a full-trained model, which Table 1 confirms (MoBA PPL 16.54 vs. FullAttn PPL 17.05 under sparse inference). This is the Attention Gap in action.

#### Practical Training Configurations

**Model architectures.** The paper uses two model sizes, both following the Llama-3.2 architecture (Grattafiori et al., 2024) with two minor modifications applied to all methods for fairness:

| Config Field | 1B Model | 300M Model |
|---|---|---|
| Hidden Size | 2048 | 1024 |
| Intermediate Size | 8192 | 4096 |
| Num Hidden Layers | 16 | 16 |
| Num Attention Heads | 32 | 16 |
| Num KV Heads | 2 | 1 |
| Head Dim | 64 | 64 |
| Max Position Embeddings | 131072 | 131072 |
| Vocabulary Size | 128256 | 128256 |
| RoPE Base $\theta$ | 500,000 | 500,000 |

**Modification 1: Reduced KV heads.** The number of key-value heads is reduced to 2 (from the typical multi-head count) to adapt block-sparse attention implementations and accelerate training.

**Modification 2: Gated Attention.** A gating mechanism is applied to the attention output, following Qiu et al. (2025): $\text{Gate}_l(h) \odot h_{\text{main}}$, where $\text{Gate}_l$ is a learned function of the normalized hidden state. This mechanism is implicitly also employed in NSA, so applying it to all methods ensures fair comparison. It also mitigates the attention sink phenomenon.

**Pretraining data and schedule.** The 1B model is pretrained on the SmolLM corpus (Allal et al., 2025) for 100B tokens with an 8k context length. Training uses a learning rate of $1 \times 10^{-3}$ with cosine decay and a global batch size of 3.15M tokens. For long-context evaluation, the model continues training for an additional 10B tokens at 32k context length with a RoPE scaling factor of 4, using data sampled from DCLM (Li et al., 2024).

**RoPE scaling configuration** (for long-context evaluation only): Scaling factor 4.0, low frequency factor 1.0, high frequency factor 4.0, scaling type "llama3," original max position 8192.

**The 300M ablation models** are trained on 50B tokens from the same corpus for faster experimentation.

**Sparsity configurations.** The paper trains models with two receptive field settings:
- **RF=256:** Block size 16, top-16 blocks (selected by similarity). This is the primary configuration used for main results.
- **RF=1024:** Block size 32, top-32 blocks. Used for comparison and ablation.

The RF=256 variant is preferred because it "induces higher sparsity regularisation" — the smaller receptive field forces the model to be more selective about which tokens receive attention, producing more concentrated attention patterns that generalize better to larger receptive fields at inference.

**Alignment weight.** $\alpha = 10$ for the main experiments (determined via the ablation in Table A14 on 300M models).

**Training efficiency.** Table A2 reports wall-clock training times (GPU-hours):
- FullAttn: 792 hours (pre-training 8k), 112 hours (continual 32k).
- MoBA (RF=256): 856 hours (pre-training), 136 hours (continual).
- SSA (RF=256): 1008 hours (pre-training), 176 hours (continual).

SSA incurs approximately 17% overhead over MoBA — the cost of computing auxiliary attention at each layer without propagating it forward.

**Inference efficiency.** Table A3 reports inference wall-clock time in milliseconds (batch size 1, averaged over 30 runs):
- At 8k context: FullAttn 204.9ms, Sparse (RF=256) 198.1ms, Sparse (RF=1024) 199.5ms.
- At 128k context: FullAttn 7029.2ms, Sparse (RF=256) 5138.0ms (~1.4× faster), Sparse (RF=1024) 3411.9ms (~2.1× faster).

The speedup grows with context length as the sparsity ratio $k/n$ becomes smaller. NSA shows similar inference times to MoBA/SSA sparse modes (the selection module operates identically), with the additional compression and sliding window modules adding marginal overhead.

**Why the paper uses extrapolation rather than training at larger receptive fields.** SSA is trained at RF=256 and tested at RF=512, 1024, 2048, 4096 (Figure 3). The paper states that training with RF=256 yields better performance than training directly with RF=1024 (Table 2: train16×32 scores 49.32 vs. baseline inf16×32 scoring 49.78), attributing this to the smaller receptive field providing stronger structural regularization. Extrapolation is a deliberate design choice, not an evaluation shortcut — the model benefits from being forced to learn to make good selections within a constrained budget, and this skill transfers when more tokens become available.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Reframing Sparse Attention as a Joint Training Problem Rather Than a Mechanism Design Problem

The field's approach to sparse attention has been almost entirely mechanism-centric: design better token selection patterns (StreamingLLM's attention sinks, MoBA's block-sparse routing, NSA's three-component architecture, InfLLM-v2's hierarchical selection, DSA's token-level top-k) and then apply them either post-hoc to full-trained models or natively during training. The implicit assumption across this literature is that the primary bottleneck is **which tokens you select** — that if you can just identify the right subset of keys, sparse attention will approximate full attention well.

SSA's most fundamental intellectual move is to reject this framing. The paper argues — and demonstrates empirically — that the bottleneck is not primarily the selection mechanism but rather the **attention distribution itself**. MoBA uses exactly the same block-sparse selection mechanism that SSA's sparse stream uses, yet MoBA underperforms SSA substantially (Table 1: 58.73% vs. 60.27% average commonsense reasoning under sparse inference at RF=256). The difference is not in which tokens are selected but in the fact that SSA's attention distributions are naturally sparser (Figure 1d: SSA achieves the highest attention sparsity among all methods), meaning the selected tokens capture more of the total attention mass regardless of the selection algorithm.

This reframing matters because it shifts the research agenda. The mechanism-design approach asks: "How can we build a better token selector?" SSA's answer is: "Stop trying to build a better selector; instead, train the model so that any reasonable selector works well, because the attention mass is already concentrated where it needs to be." This parallels a recurring pattern in deep learning where training methodology improvements (better objectives, better regularization) ultimately prove more impactful than architectural innovations — batch normalization vs. exotic activation functions, residual connections vs. sophisticated gating. SSA suggests sparse attention may follow the same trajectory.

The evidence for this reframing is not just SSA's superior performance but specifically the **decomposition analysis in Equation 5**. Prior work conflated two distinct sources of error — what the model can represent under full attention (Capability Gap) and how much is lost during sparsification (Attention Gap) — into a single observed performance number. By decomposing the problem, SSA reveals that mechanism-design methods (even when trained natively, like MoBA) only address the Attention Gap, leaving the Capability Gap untouched. The joint training framework is not just additive (full attention for capability + sparse attention for matching) — it enables the alignment loss to create a qualitatively different attention distribution that neither mode alone produces. This is a fundamental conceptual shift, not an incremental improvement over MoBA.

---

### Innovation 2: The Bidirectional Alignment Loss as a Mechanism for Inducing Inherent Attention Sparsity Without Attention-Map Supervision

The natural approach to making sparse attention work well is to directly supervise the attention maps — force the model's sparse attention weights to match its full attention weights, or vice versa. This is the approach taken by attention distillation methods and would directly optimize the quantity $\delta(t)$ that appears in Theorem 1's error bound. The paper explicitly rejects this approach for a practical reason: materializing dense attention maps is incompatible with FlashAttention-2, which achieves its speed by never explicitly forming the full $N \times N$ attention matrix.

What makes SSA's solution intellectually interesting is not the pragmatic workaround but the **unexpected mechanism by which value-level alignment induces sparsity**. The alignment loss does not mention attention weights anywhere — it compares hidden representation vectors (the weighted sums of value vectors). Yet Figure 1 and Table A16 show that this value-level alignment produces models with substantially higher attention sparsity than baselines. The paper's explanation is that by pulling full attention outputs toward sparse attention outputs (the sparsity loss with stop-gradient on the sparse side), the model is forced to learn full attention parameters that produce outputs explainable by only a subset of tokens. If dropped tokens contributed significantly to the output, the full attention output would diverge from the sparse attention output, incurring alignment penalty. The only way to satisfy the loss is to make dropped tokens genuinely irrelevant — which manifests as sparser attention distributions.

This is a **second-order effect**: the loss does not directly optimize for sparsity but creates conditions where sparsity is the optimal solution. This distinguishes SSA from approaches that add explicit sparsity penalties (e.g., L1 regularization on attention weights), which often produce sparse but degraded representations because they penalize attention mass indiscriminately rather than encouraging the model to discover which tokens genuinely don't matter. The alignment loss is selective: it only penalizes attention mass on tokens that sparse attention would have dropped, and even then only if that mass meaningfully affects the output representation. Tokens that receive attention but don't influence the output are unaffected.

The bidirectionality is also non-obvious but crucial. The ablation in Table 2 shows that using only one direction (Full→Sparse or Sparse→Full) causes training to collapse to NaN loss. The paper's diagnosis — "asymmetric over-distillation" — is that unidirectional alignment causes one mode to overfit to the other's patterns, degrading its own capability. Bidirectional alignment creates a stable equilibrium where both modes pull toward a midpoint of mutually compatible representations. This is reminiscent of the commitment loss in VQ-VAE (cited by the authors) and the KL penalty in RLHF, but with a key difference: in those settings, one component (the codebook, the reference policy) is fixed or slowly updated, while in SSA both components are learned simultaneously. The bidirectional design solves the instability that would arise from two learned components pulling against each other asymmetrically.

This innovation is significant because it demonstrates that **representation-level objectives can shape attention distributions in predictable ways without ever touching the attention weights directly**. It opens the door to a class of training methods that manipulate attention behavior through the geometry of hidden representations rather than through direct attention supervision — an approach that is both more compatible with efficient attention implementations and potentially more robust because it allows the model flexibility in how it achieves the desired sparsity.

---

### Innovation 3: The Diagnostic Decomposition of Sparse Attention Error into Independent, Addressable Terms

Prior work on sparse attention evaluated methods holistically: does method A outperform method B on benchmark X? This tells you which method is better but not **why** it's better or **what kind of error remains**. The paper introduces a conceptual framework (Equation 5) that decomposes the total gap between an ideal full-attention model and a deployed sparse-attention model into two independently measurable components:

- **Capability Gap (Term I):** How much representational quality is lost due to training with incomplete gradient signals (Proposition 4.1) — measured by comparing the SSA model's full-attention output to an ideal full-trained model's output.
- **Attention Gap (Term II):** How much approximation error is introduced by the sparsification itself at inference — measured by comparing the SSA model's sparse-attention output to its own full-attention output, and bounded by Theorem 1 in terms of dropped attention mass $\delta(t)$.

This decomposition is intellectually valuable independent of SSA's specific solution. It provides a **diagnostic lens** that explains why prior methods fail in different ways: Full-Sparse methods like StreamingLLM have near-zero Capability Gap (the model was trained with full attention) but unbounded Attention Gap; Sparse-Sparse methods like MoBA have zero Attention Gap (training matches inference) but unbounded Capability Gap. The framework predicts — and the experiments confirm — that these two paradigmatic approaches are complementary failure modes, not competing solutions.

What elevates this from a simple decomposition to a genuine innovation is that **the terms map cleanly onto actionable training interventions**. Term I motivates including full attention in training to provide complete gradient flow and contrastive softmax denominators. Term II motivates including sparse attention in training to eliminate the KL-divergence penalty from Proposition 4.2, combined with an objective that reduces $\delta(t)$ to tighten the Theorem 1 bound. The three design principles — full attention training, sparse attention training, and sparsity-inducing alignment — emerge directly from the decomposition rather than being an ad hoc combination of techniques.

The empirical validation of this framework is particularly strong because it explains a pattern that would otherwise be puzzling. Table 1 shows that MoBA achieves lower perplexity than FullAttn under sparse inference (16.54 vs. 17.05 PPL) — consistent with Proposition 4.2's prediction that Sparse-Sparse training avoids the KL-divergence penalty. But MoBA achieves lower commonsense reasoning accuracy (58.73% vs. 59.62%) — consistent with Proposition 4.1's prediction that sparse-only training produces weaker representations. Prior work might have reported one of these numbers and drawn incomplete conclusions (either "sparse training helps" or "sparse training hurts"). The decomposition reveals that both can be simultaneously true for different aspects of model quality, and that a method succeeding at both requires addressing both terms.

This is a **conceptual innovation** rather than a methodological one — it provides a language and framework for reasoning about sparse attention quality that did not exist before. Future work on sparse attention can use this decomposition to diagnose whether performance gaps stem from training deficiencies or approximation error, and to design interventions targeted at the specific bottleneck rather than treating sparse attention performance as a monolithic quantity.

---

### Innovation 4: Empirical Discovery That Sparser Attention Distributions Are Robust to Positional Encoding Degradation in Long-Context Extrapolation

Section 7.4 and Appendix L contain a finding that is presented almost as a side observation but has potentially broad implications for long-context model design: sparse attention is more robust to the quality degradation caused by RoPE scaling than full attention is. Figure 4a shows that when models are length-extrapolated using RoPE scaling (without continued training at longer contexts), SSA under full attention surprisingly outperforms FullAttn under full attention — reversing the trend from Table 1. The paper attributes this to SSA's sparser attention distributions being less sensitive to the positional encoding distortions that RoPE scaling introduces.

The mechanism, as detailed in Appendix L, is twofold. First, RoPE scaling works by stretching the periods of low-frequency bands to prevent aliasing at extended positions, but this stretching necessarily distorts the learned positional representations — positions that the model learned to treat as similar may now appear different, and vice versa. Full attention exposes every query to these distortions across all positions, accumulating error. Sparse attention, by attending to fewer tokens overall (and disproportionately to local tokens, as shown in Figures A4-A6), limits the model's exposure to distorted positional encodings.

Second, the local-token preference that emerges naturally from sparse attention training (see the histograms in Appendix L.2) means that the model disproportionately attends to positions within the original 8k training window, where RoPE scaling induces minimal distortion. The scaling primarily affects low-frequency bands that encode long-range positional relationships; local positions rely more on high-frequency bands that are less modified by scaling. By focusing attention on local tokens, sparse attention sidesteps the most severe distortions.

This finding is significant because it challenges the prevailing narrative that long-context capability requires ever-larger training context windows. If sparser attention patterns are inherently more robust to positional encoding degradation, then training methods that encourage sparsity (like SSA's alignment loss) could serve as an alternative or complement to expensive long-context pretraining. The paper's continued-training results (Figure 4, right panels) show that FullAttn eventually catches up when explicitly trained at 32k, but SSA retains its advantage under sparse inference — suggesting that sparsity and continued training are complementary rather than redundant.

The finding also provides a new lens on why SSA's full-attention inference sometimes outperforms FullAttn despite having a small training disadvantage from the sparse attention stream. The commonsense reasoning results (Table 1: SSA 60.39 vs. FullAttn 60.01 under full attention) and Section 7.2's ablation analysis suggest that sparser attention distributions benefit downstream reasoning even when full attention is available at inference — the model has learned to focus on informative tokens and suppress irrelevant ones, a skill that transfers to the full-attention setting. This is a **positive externality** of sparse attention training that the field has not previously identified or leveraged.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The primary training corpus is the SmolLM corpus (Allal et al., 2025), with 100B tokens used for pretraining at 8k context length. For long-context continued training, an additional 10B tokens are sampled from DCLM (Li et al., 2024) at 32k context length. Evaluation spans four benchmark categories: (1) commonsense reasoning on PIQA (Bisk et al., 2019; 1,838 test samples, 3-shot), HellaSwag (Zellers et al., 2019; 10,042 test samples, 10-shot), ARC-Easy (Clark et al., 2018; 2,376 test samples, 25-shot), and ARC-Challenge (Clark et al., 2018; 1,172 test samples, 5-shot); (2) word-level perplexity on WikiText (Merity et al., 2017); (3) long-context understanding on LongBench (Bai et al., 2024; 16 English benchmarks, 4,750 total samples); (4) retrieval on Needle-in-a-Haystack (NIAH) from RULER (Hsieh et al., 2024; 500 cases per test length); and (5) long-context perplexity on PG-19 (Rae et al., 2020; 1,000 randomly selected samples, sliding-window evaluation with stride 256, seed 42).

- **Base model(s).** The primary model is a 1B-parameter architecture following Llama-3.2 (Grattafiori et al., 2024) with two modifications applied uniformly to all methods for fairness: (1) the number of key-value heads is reduced to 2 to adapt block-sparse attention implementations and accelerate training; (2) gated attention (Qiu et al., 2025) is added to mitigate the attention sink phenomenon, which is also implicitly employed in NSA. A smaller 300M-parameter variant (hidden size 1024, intermediate size 4096, 16 attention heads, 1 KV head) is used for ablation studies, trained on 50B tokens. The 1B model is chosen as "representative of the capabilities of many contemporary LLMs" (Section 4), sitting in a regime where performance is non-trivial but far from saturation, leaving room for test-time compute strategies to show meaningful differences.

- **Metrics.** Five distinct metrics are employed across evaluation settings: (1) **Commonsense reasoning accuracy** — the fraction of questions answered correctly, averaged over 5 runs with different random seeds (1, 12, 123, 1234, 12345) to account for sampling variance, with the average and standard deviation reported; (2) **Word-level perplexity** on WikiText — computed using lm-evaluation-harness (Gao et al., 2024) with the standard implementation, context length capped at 8k; (3) **LongBench accuracy** — normalized accuracy averaged across 16 English benchmarks, using greedy decoding with a single run due to computational constraints from the sparse attention kernel's lack of KV-cache support; (4) **Needle-in-a-Haystack accuracy** — fraction of retrieval tasks where the model correctly identifies the inserted fact, evaluated at context lengths of 4k, 8k, 16k, and 32k with 500 test cases per length (single run due to dynamic data synthesis); (5) **PG-19 perplexity** — computed via sliding-window evaluation with stride 256 (Press et al., 2021, 2022), converting loss directly to perplexity via the exponential relation, evaluated on 1,000 randomly selected samples.

- **Baselines.** The paper compares SSA against four baselines, each representing a different paradigm in sparse attention: (1) **FullAttn** — the standard full self-attention mechanism, representing the Full-Full paradigm (trained and deployed with full attention), serving as the upper-bound reference for representational quality; (2) **MoBA** (Lu et al., 2025) — a trainable block-sparse attention method that trains and deploys with identical sparse attention (Sparse-Sparse paradigm), conceptually identical to SSA's sparse attention stream in terms of the selection mechanism (block partitioning, mean-pooled block keys, top-k block selection, token-level attention within selected blocks), representing the state of the art in natively trained sparse attention; (3) **NSA** (Yuan et al., 2025) — a three-component sparse attention framework combining compression (maintaining compressed representations of all preceding tokens), selection (block-sparse attention analogous to MoBA), and sliding window (local attention to the most recent 128 tokens), reported to surpass full-attention baselines but subsequently analyzed as benefiting substantially from its auxiliary components; (4) **Full-Sparse variants** — applying sparse attention patterns (the same block-sparse mechanism as MoBA) to FullAttn-trained models at inference time only, representing the training-free sparse attention paradigm. For each of MoBA and NSA, two sparsity configurations are trained: receptive field 256 (block size 16, top-16 blocks) and receptive field 1024 (block size 32, top-32 blocks). For SSA, the 256-receptive-field variant is primarily used and extrapolated to larger receptive fields at inference.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper does not use "generations" as a compute unit (as in best-of-N or beam search) since all models produce exactly one output per forward pass. Instead, compute is measured along two axes: (1) **Training efficiency** — wall-clock GPU-hours for pretraining (100B tokens at 8k) and continual training (10B tokens at 32k), reported in Table A2 for all methods; (2) **Inference efficiency** — wall-clock time in milliseconds per forward pass at various context lengths (8k, 16k, 32k, 64k, 128k), measured with batch size 1 averaged over 30 runs, reported in Table A3. The sparsity ratio $k/n$ (selected blocks divided by total blocks) determines the receptive field and computational complexity. Fairness in training comparisons is ensured by having SSA alternate between full and sparse attention streams with equal probability (50/50), so the model processes the same total number of tokens as baselines — the auxiliary attention computation in each layer adds marginal overhead (~17% over MoBA) but does not affect the token count processed for the main forward pass. Fairness in inference comparisons is ensured by evaluating all methods under identical receptive field constraints (e.g., all models restricted to 256 or 1024 tokens under sparse inference), except NSA which has additional effective tokens from its compression and sliding window modules.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** For commonsense reasoning benchmarks, results are reported as the mean and standard deviation over 5 runs with different random seeds (1, 12, 123, 1234, 12345), enabling assessment of whether performance differences are statistically meaningful. For LongBench and NIAH, only single-run results are reported due to computational constraints — the sparse attention kernel does not support KV-caching, making decoding slow, and NSA results may vary across runs since evaluation data is synthesized dynamically. For PG-19 perplexity, 1,000 samples are randomly selected using seed 42 as a fixed evaluation subset. There is no explicit cross-validation or statistical significance testing beyond the multiple-seed averaging on commonsense benchmarks — the paper does not report confidence intervals, hypothesis tests, or train/validation splits for hyperparameter selection. The ablation studies on 300M models use a separate, smaller training run (50B tokens) to explore hyperparameters (alignment weight $\alpha$, receptive field configurations, streaming ratios, loss types), and the best configurations are applied to the 1B model, but this is not a formal cross-validation protocol — it is a standard development-set transfer.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Language Modeling and Commonsense Reasoning (Table 1)

**Headline result.** SSA achieves the best average commonsense reasoning accuracy under sparse attention inference at both receptive field levels while maintaining perplexity competitive with or superior to all baselines. Under sparse inference with a receptive field of 256, SSA achieves 60.27% average commonsense accuracy (±0.22) versus FullAttn's 59.62% (±0.31), MoBA's 58.73% (±0.16), and NSA's 59.47% (±0.20). Under sparse inference with a receptive field of 1024, SSA achieves 60.39% (±0.15) versus FullAttn's 60.04% (±0.17), MoBA's 58.64% (±0.14), and NSA's 60.18% (±0.15). SSA also achieves the highest average accuracy under full attention inference (60.39% ±0.20) — slightly surpassing FullAttn (60.01% ±0.19) despite SSA's training spending only half its iterations on full attention.

**Perplexity dynamics validate the theoretical framework.** The perplexity column of Table 1 tells a more nuanced story that directly supports the paper's theoretical propositions. Under full attention inference, FullAttn achieves the best perplexity (15.13), followed by SSA (15.28), then MoBA (16.68). This ordering validates Proposition 4.1 — MoBA's sparse-only training incurs a Capability Gap even when given full KV-cache access at inference, because its representations are fundamentally weaker. SSA largely closes this gap. Under sparse inference (RF=256), the ordering changes: SSA achieves 15.96 PPL, NSA achieves 16.02, MoBA achieves 16.54, and FullAttn jumps to 17.05. The substantial degradation of FullAttn (15.13 → 17.05, an increase of 1.92 PPL) validates Proposition 4.2: the train-inference distribution mismatch incurs a significant KL-divergence penalty. MoBA's improvement relative to FullAttn under sparse inference (16.54 vs. 17.05) validates Remark 1: when the KL divergence is large enough, a sparse-trained model can achieve better perplexity than a full-trained model under sparse inference. SSA achieves the best of both worlds — its sparse-inference perplexity (15.96) is close to FullAttn's full-inference perplexity (15.13), and the gap between SSA's sparse and full modes (15.96 vs. 15.28, a difference of 0.68 PPL) is substantially smaller than FullAttn's gap (17.05 vs. 15.13, a difference of 1.92 PPL). This directly validates Theorem 1's implication that higher attention sparsity tightens the approximation error bound — SSA's inherently sparser attention distributions (Figure 1d) reduce the dropped attention mass $\delta(t)$, making sparse attention a closer approximation to full attention.

**Per-benchmark breakdown.** On PIQA under sparse inference (RF=256), SSA achieves 74.53% (±0.55) versus FullAttn's 74.23% (±0.39) and MoBA's 72.64% (±0.34). On HellaSwag, SSA reaches 58.40% (±0.08) versus FullAttn's 57.84% (±0.20) and NSA's 58.15% (±0.32). On ARC-Easy, NSA slightly edges SSA (69.80% ±0.34 vs. 69.62% ±0.30) under RF=256, though SSA takes the lead at RF=1024 (69.97% ±0.29 vs. NSA's 70.07% ±0.21 — essentially tied). On ARC-Challenge, the most difficult benchmark, SSA achieves the strongest results across all configurations: 38.53% (±0.64) at RF=256 versus FullAttn's 37.56% (±0.55) and MoBA's 37.05% (±0.30); and 38.57% (±0.51) at RF=1024 versus FullAttn's 38.34% (±0.55). The ARC-Challenge results are particularly notable because they demonstrate that SSA's gains are concentrated on harder reasoning tasks — where the quality of attention representations matters most — rather than on simpler pattern-matching tasks where all methods perform similarly.

**NSA's performance context.** NSA achieves competitive results at RF=1024 (60.18% average, essentially tied with SSA's 60.39% within standard deviations), but two factors mitigate this comparison. First, NSA's sliding window module provides access to 128 additional local tokens beyond the 1024-token receptive field, meaning NSA's effective receptive field is larger than other methods under the same stated configuration. Second, NSA cannot be evaluated under full attention inference — as shown in Appendix F and Table A6, replacing all three NSA modules with full attention (fa+fa+fa) yields perplexity of 191.3, making the model unusable. This means NSA is locked into sparse-only deployment, forfeiting the flexibility that SSA provides to switch between modes based on task requirements (e.g., using full attention for retrieval-intensive tasks like NIAH where SSA achieves 100% accuracy under full attention at 4k and 8k, Table 3).

**The MoBA degradation pattern.** MoBA consistently underperforms FullAttn on commonsense reasoning benchmarks under sparse inference (58.73% vs. 59.62% at RF=256; 58.64% vs. 60.04% at RF=1024), despite achieving better perplexity (16.54 vs. 17.05 PPL at RF=256). This dissociation — better language modeling but worse reasoning — is the empirical signature of the Capability Gap from Proposition 4.1: incomplete gradient flow produces models that are competent at local next-token prediction (reflected in perplexity) but lack the global feature discrimination needed for structured reasoning (reflected in benchmark accuracy). SSA resolves this dissociation by incorporating full attention training, achieving both better perplexity and better reasoning accuracy than MoBA under sparse inference.

#### Extrapolation Across Sparsity Levels (Figure 3)

**Headline result.** SSA and FullAttn extrapolate smoothly to larger receptive fields, with monotonic or near-monotonic performance improvement as more tokens become available, while MoBA exhibits poor extrapolation and in some cases degrades when given access to more context. Figure 3 plots performance versus receptive field size on four evaluation axes: commonsense reasoning accuracy (averaged across the four benchmarks), WikiText perplexity, LongBench accuracy (16-task average), and NIAH accuracy at 8k context.

**Commonsense reasoning (Figure 3a).** As receptive field expands from 256 to 4096, SSA's accuracy rises from approximately 60.3% to 60.5% — a modest but consistent improvement. FullAttn follows a similar trajectory, rising from approximately 59.6% to 60.0%. MoBA's accuracy, after an initial small improvement from 256 to 1024, **declines** when expanding from 2048 to 4096 — the curve bends downward rather than continuing upward. This degradation at larger receptive fields is the paper's key evidence for the extrapolation failure of sparse-only training: when additional tokens become available, MoBA's insufficiently sparse attention distributions cause the model to attend to irrelevant tokens, introducing noise rather than useful context. The model has never learned to globally suppress irrelevant tokens (the attention suppression deficiency from Proposition 4.1), so giving it more candidate tokens degrades rather than improves its selections.

**Perplexity (Figure 3b).** Perplexity should decrease (improve) as receptive field increases, since more context provides more information for next-token prediction. FullAttn's perplexity drops from approximately 17.1 (RF=256) to 15.1 (RF=4096), and SSA's perplexity drops from approximately 16.0 to 15.3 — both showing the expected improvement. MoBA's perplexity, however, is essentially flat or slightly increasing: from approximately 16.5 at RF=256 to 16.6 at RF=4096. The model gains no benefit from additional context, consistent with the interpretation that it cannot effectively discriminate between useful and useless tokens.

**LongBench (Figure 3c).** SSA's LongBench accuracy rises from approximately 17.9 (RF=256) to 19.0 (RF=4096) — a gain of 1.1 percentage points. FullAttn rises from approximately 14.8 to 18.1 — a larger gain of 3.3 points, consistent with full-attention-trained models benefiting more from complete context access once given sufficient receptive field. MoBA's LongBench performance drops sharply: from approximately 16.9 (RF=512) to 14.9 (RF=4096), a regression of 2.0 points. This is the most dramatic failure of extrapolation — MoBA performs worse with 16× the receptive field. The detailed per-dataset LongBench results in Table A11 further reveal that MoBA's degradation is not uniform across tasks: on few-shot tasks (TREC, TriviaQA) MoBA maintains or improves slightly, but on summary tasks (GovReport, QMSum, MultiNews) and multi-document QA (HotpotQA, 2WikiQA) performance regresses substantially. This pattern is consistent with the interpretation that summary and multi-document tasks require discriminating between relevant and irrelevant passages — exactly the skill that sparse-only training fails to develop.

**NIAH at 8k (Figure 3d).** SSA's NIAH accuracy peaks at 47.8% (RF=1024) and declines slightly to 35.4% at RF=4096 — the expected pattern for a task that requires finding a specific piece of information in a large context, where a moderate receptive field is sufficient and further expansion adds noise. FullAttn follows a similar pattern but with lower absolute performance. MoBA's NIAH performance is erratic: it achieves 28.2% at RF=256 and 27.8% at RF=1024, but drops to single digits at larger receptive fields. The paper attributes this to MoBA's "insufficiently sparse attention distribution" — when additional tokens become available, the attention mass is spread too broadly, diluting the signal for the target needle.

**Detailed scores for commonsense and perplexity extrapolation.** Table A10 provides the full per-benchmark breakdown: on PIQA, SSA maintains 74.49% (±0.40) consistently from RF=512 to RF=4096, while FullAttn holds at 74.30% (±0.40). On ARC-Challenge, SSA rises from 38.77% (±0.66) at RF=512 to 38.79% (±0.51) at RF=4096 — essentially flat but well above MoBA's 37.20% (±0.46) to 37.25% (±0.68) range. The consistency of SSA's performance across widely varying receptive fields is itself a result: the model's attention is sufficiently sparse that adding more tokens (most of which receive negligible attention weight) has minimal impact on downstream decisions.

#### Long-Context Evaluation (Table 3, Figure 4)

**Headline result.** SSA achieves the best performance on LongBench under all inference modes and context-extension settings, demonstrates the strongest NIAH performance within 8k context length while maintaining competitive retrieval at longer distances, and exhibits the best perplexity scaling on PG-19 under sparse attention inference.

**LongBench (Table 3, detailed in Tables A12 and A13).** For models length-extrapolated via RoPE scaling (without continued training at 32k): under full attention, SSA achieves 18.32 (vs. FullAttn's 17.71 and MoBA's 13.62); under sparse attention with RF=256, SSA achieves 17.89 (vs. FullAttn's 14.75, MoBA's 16.88, and NSA's 17.25); under sparse attention with RF=1024, SSA achieves 18.58 (vs. FullAttn's 16.72, MoBA's 18.23, and NSA's 17.36). SSA wins 5 out of 6 mode/receptive-field combinations, with the single exception being MoBA's 18.23 at RF=1024 under sparse attention — a narrow gap (0.35 points) that reverses under full attention (SSA 18.32 vs. MoBA 13.62, a 4.7 point lead).

For models continually trained to 32k: under full attention, SSA achieves 19.55 (vs. FullAttn's 18.97 and MoBA's 14.47); under sparse attention RF=256, SSA achieves 18.25 (vs. FullAttn's 14.96, MoBA's 17.17, and NSA's 17.45); under sparse attention RF=1024, SSA achieves 19.05 (vs. FullAttn's 16.84, MoBA's 18.68, and NSA's 18.08). The gap between SSA and baselines is larger under full attention — consistent with the interpretation that SSA's full-attention capabilities benefit from the sparsity regularization during training, an effect that becomes more pronounced after continued training on longer sequences.

**Needle-in-a-Haystack (Table 3).** The NIAH results reveal a clear **length-dependent tradeoff** between SSA and MoBA. Within the 8k training distribution: under full attention at 4k and 8k, SSA achieves 100% accuracy — perfect retrieval — while MoBA achieves only 100% at 4k but drops to 32.4% at 8k. Under sparse attention RF=256 at 8k, SSA achieves 33.6% versus MoBA's 28.2%. Under sparse attention RF=1024 at 8k, SSA achieves 47.8% versus MoBA's 27.8%. SSA dominates at in-distribution lengths. Beyond 8k (length extrapolation, RoPE scaling): MoBA's performance degrades in absolute terms (dropping to 0% at 32k under both RF=256 and RF=1024 length-extrapolation settings) but maintains relatively better performance at intermediate lengths (16k) than SSA under RoPE scaling. Under sparse attention RF=1024 at 16k with RoPE scaling, MoBA achieves 16.0% versus SSA's 10.0%. Under sparse attention RF=1024 at 32k with RoPE scaling, MoBA achieves 8.0% versus SSA's 3.8%. The paper attributes MoBA's relative strength at out-of-distribution distances to its "purely content-based block selection, which is more position-agnostic and thus generalizes better to out-of-distribution distances." SSA's alignment training provides strong guidance within the training distribution but may make the model more reliant on positional patterns that break down under extrapolation — a limitation the paper acknowledges but does not deeply investigate.

After continued training at 32k, this pattern partially reverses: under sparse attention RF=1024 at 16k, SSA achieves 17.2% versus MoBA's 17.6% (essentially tied); at 32k, SSA achieves 5.4% versus MoBA's 9.4%. However, under full attention, SSA dramatically outperforms MoBA at 32k (70.6% vs. 1.2%), and under sparse attention RF=256 at 8k, SSA achieves 34.8% versus MoBA's 27.6%. The overall average NIAH across all tested lengths (4k, 8k, 16k, 32k) strongly favors SSA: 79.6% (full attention, length extrapolation), 30.8% (sparse RF=256, length extrapolation), 34.8% (sparse RF=1024, length extrapolation) versus MoBA's 33.7%, 27.9%, and 27.6% respectively. SSA wins on average in 5 out of 6 settings, providing "practitioners the flexibility to choose sparse mode for efficiency or full attention for retrieval-intensive tasks."

**PG-19 long-context perplexity (Figure 4).** Figure 4 plots perplexity versus context length (4k, 8k, 16k, 32k) for three inference modes: full attention (Figure 4a), sparse attention RF=256 (Figure 4b), and sparse attention RF=1024 (Figure 4c). Under full attention without continued training (solid lines), SSA surprisingly outperforms FullAttn — at 32k, SSA's perplexity is approximately 15.5 while FullAttn's is approximately 17.0. The paper attributes this reversal (SSA was slightly worse than FullAttn at 8k in Table 1) to SSA's sparser attention distributions being more robust to RoPE scaling degradation (see Appendix L). Under sparse attention (both RF levels), SSA consistently achieves the lowest perplexity across all context lengths. After continued training to 32k (dashed lines), FullAttn recovers and surpasses SSA under full attention (the continued training provides the model with exposure to longer positional relationships), but SSA retains its advantage under sparse inference — consistent with SSA's sparser attention patterns providing robustness that continued training does not fully replicate for full-attention models.

**NSA's long-context performance (Tables 3, A8).** NSA's perplexity on PG-19 benefits substantially from its sliding window module. Table A8 shows that with the sliding window, NSA achieves 15.00 PPL at 32k (RoPE-scaled); without the sliding window, perplexity explodes to 18.16. SSA achieves 15.53 PPL at 32k — worse than NSA with window but substantially better than NSA without window. This confirms Appendix G's analysis that NSA's strong perplexity performance is partially attributable to the additional local tokens from the sliding window rather than the sparse attention mechanism itself. On LongBench, NSA achieves competitive but not leading performance: 17.25 (RF=256, length extrapolation), 17.36 (RF=1024, length extrapolation), 17.45 (RF=256, continual), 18.08 (RF=1024, continual) — consistently behind SSA. On NIAH, NSA generally underperforms other methods, particularly at longer distances (e.g., 3.6% at 16k with RF=256 under length extrapolation).

#### Full-Attention Inference Performance — The Unexpected Benefit

**Headline result.** SSA not only matches but slightly exceeds FullAttn's performance under full attention inference — despite spending only 50% of training iterations on full attention and never using the alignment loss at inference time. In Table 1, under full attention inference, SSA achieves 60.39% average commonsense accuracy (±0.20) versus FullAttn's 60.01% (±0.19) — a small but consistent advantage of 0.38 percentage points that appears across multiple benchmarks (PIQA: 74.54% vs. 74.17%; HellaSwag: 58.45% vs. 58.22%; ARC-Easy: 69.92% vs. 69.29%; ARC-Challenge: 38.65% vs. 38.35%).

**The perplexity paradox.** Under full attention, SSA's perplexity is 15.28 — slightly worse than FullAttn's 15.13. This means SSA achieves better downstream task performance despite slightly worse language modeling. The paper's ablation in Table 2 provides the explanatory mechanism: removing the alignment loss (NoAlignmentLoss) degrades commonsense accuracy to 48.38 (on the 300M model scale), while training with full attention only but retaining the alignment loss (FullRatio=1) preserves the gains at 49.58. This suggests that the sparser attention distributions induced by the alignment loss — not the dual-stream architecture per se — are responsible for the downstream performance improvement. The paper hypothesizes that "sparser attention encourages the model to focus on informative tokens while ignoring irrelevant ones" (Section 7.2), a skill that transfers to full-attention inference even though the model has access to all tokens.

**Long-context full-attention benefits.** Under long-context evaluation without continued training (Figure 4a), SSA's full-attention advantage over FullAttn is even more pronounced: at 32k context, SSA achieves substantially lower perplexity than FullAttn under full attention inference. The paper attributes this to SSA's sparser attention distributions providing robustness to RoPE scaling degradation — attending to fewer, primarily local tokens reduces exposure to the positional encoding distortions that RoPE scaling introduces. After continued training, FullAttn catches up (the 32k training provides exposure to longer positional relationships), but SSA's initial advantage demonstrates that sparsity regularization during training provides a form of length-generalization robustness that even full-attention models lack.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Sparsity levels (Table 2):** Training with a smaller receptive field (RF=256, train8×16) underperforms the baseline SSA configuration on both PPL (25.26 baseline inf8×16 vs. 25.97 train8×16) and commonsense accuracy (49.69 vs. 48.88), while training with a larger receptive field (RF=1024 via train16×32 or train16×64) also underperforms the baseline on commonsense accuracy despite improving PPL. The baseline SSA configuration (train8×16, meaning train with RF=256 but evaluate at various sparsity levels) achieves the best accuracy of 49.69. The paper hypothesizes that "a smaller receptive field imposes stronger structural constraints, providing more effective regularization for learning sparse attention patterns," but that shrinking too far (to RF=256 for training) eliminates this benefit. There exists an optimal receptive-field "sweet spot" for training, and the specific choice depends on the block size and top-k configuration.

**Sampling ratio to the full attention stream (Table 2):** Varying FullRatio — the probability of selecting the full attention stream as the main forward path — reveals that moderate inclusion of the sparse attention stream is beneficial but over-reliance on either mode alone degrades performance. FullRatio=0 (sparse-only training, equivalent to MoBA-style training with the alignment loss still active) achieves 24.40 PPL and 49.00 commonsense accuracy — worse than the baseline (FullRatio=0.5, 24.20 PPL, 49.69 accuracy) but still substantially better than MoBA without alignment (24.51 PPL, 48.81 accuracy), confirming that the alignment loss alone provides benefits even without full attention training. FullRatio=1 (full-attention-only training with alignment) achieves 25.16 PPL and 49.58 accuracy — better than sparse-only on accuracy but worse on PPL. A variant that randomly selects Full/Sparse per layer rather than per step (Random Each Layer) collapses entirely (NaN loss, 0.00 accuracy), demonstrating that the per-step mode consistency across layers is essential — the model needs coherent attention patterns throughout the forward pass. The paper notes that "placing more weight on the FA stream generally yields better downstream benchmark results," consistent with full attention providing higher-quality gradient signals for learning features relevant to reasoning.

**Alignment loss components (Table 2):** Removing the alignment loss entirely (No Alignment Loss) degrades commonsense accuracy from 49.69 to 48.38 on the 300M model — a substantial drop of 1.31 points that is larger than the gap between SSA and MoBA (49.69 vs. 48.81, a gap of 0.88 points). This establishes that the alignment loss is not merely stabilizing joint training but actively improving model quality. Using only one direction of the alignment loss (Only Full→Sparse or Only Sparse→Full) causes training to collapse to NaN loss — the paper attributes this to "asymmetric over-distillation," where unidirectional alignment causes one attention mode to overfit to the patterns of the other, degrading its own capability in a feedback loop. Bidirectionality is essential for training stability, not just for performance.

**Alignment weight $\alpha$ (Table A14):** On the 300M model, $\alpha = 5$ achieves 24.16 PPL and 48.71 commonsense accuracy; $\alpha = 10$ (the default) achieves 24.20 PPL and 49.69 accuracy; $\alpha = 20$ achieves 24.31 PPL and 49.45 accuracy. The optimum at $\alpha = 10$ suggests that the alignment loss needs sufficient weight to meaningfully constrain the representations but that excessive weight causes the alignment objective to dominate the cross-entropy loss, degrading predictive accuracy.

**Alignment loss type (Table A14):** Using L2 loss instead of SmoothL1, with $\alpha$ reduced from 10 to 5 to account for the different initial loss scale relative to cross-entropy, achieves 24.31 PPL and 49.27 commonsense accuracy — below the default SSA configuration but still outperforming MoBA (24.51 PPL, 48.81 accuracy). The paper interprets this as evidence that "the choice of distance metric is flexible and any reasonable alignment loss should work for SSA."

**NSA without sliding window (Tables A7, A8):** Removing NSA's sliding window module (comparing NSA-window vs. NSA-nowindow) causes degradation on Hellaswag (58.15 vs. 56.97), ARC-Easy (69.80 vs. 68.70), and ARC-Challenge (36.71 vs. 36.48), with a similar average drop. On long-context perplexity (Table A8), NSA-nowindow degrades particularly severely at 32k with RoPE scaling (18.16 PPL vs. 15.00 with window; SSA achieves 15.53). This confirms that NSA's reported advantages are partially attributable to the sliding window's additional local tokens rather than the sparse attention mechanism itself.

**Attention sparsity and KL divergence (Table A9):** Under sparse inference, SSA achieves the lowest KL divergence between sparse and full attention at the final layer (0.0661) versus MoBA (0.1024) and FullAttn (0.1486). SSA also achieves the highest attention sparsity under both sparse (0.677) and full (0.705) inference modes, versus MoBA (0.546 sparse, 0.527 full) and FullAttn (0.610 sparse, 0.641 full). These metrics directly validate the paper's theoretical framework: SSA's alignment loss produces sparser attention distributions (higher AttnSparsity), which reduce the dropped attention mass $\delta(t)$ in Theorem 1's bound, which manifests as a smaller KL divergence between full and sparse attention outputs, which manifests as a smaller perplexity gap between inference modes (Table 1: SSA's gap of 0.68 PPL vs. FullAttn's gap of 1.92 PPL).

**Long-context attention patterns (Table A16, Figures A4-A6):** Under full attention inference across context lengths from 4k to 32k, SSA consistently achieves higher attention sparsity and lower attention entropy than both FullAttn and MoBA. At 32k, SSA achieves AttnSparsity of 0.707 vs. FullAttn's 0.511 and MoBA's 0.423, and AttnEntropy of 8.51 vs. FullAttn's 10.21 and MoBA's 11.30. The distance histograms (Figures A4-A6) reveal that all methods prefer local tokens, but SSA's preference is sharper — the distribution of distances between query and attended key is more concentrated at short distances for SSA. This local-token preference provides a mechanistic explanation for sparse attention's robustness to RoPE scaling: local positions (<8k) are less affected by the period-stretching applied to low-frequency RoPE bands, so attending primarily to local tokens minimizes exposure to positional encoding distortion.

**MoBA's attention entropy increases with context length (Table A16):** MoBA's attention entropy rises from 9.01 (4k) to 9.69 (8k) to 10.28 (16k) to 11.30 (32k) — a monotonic degradation that parallels its extrapolation failures in Figure 3. SSA's entropy rises more modestly: from 7.63 (4k) to 8.07 (8k) to 8.28 (16k) to 8.51 (32k). FullAttn falls between them: 8.89 → 9.49 → 9.70 → 10.21. This provides a quantitative signature of the attention suppression deficiency from Proposition 4.1: MoBA never learns to globally suppress irrelevant tokens, so as context grows, its attention becomes increasingly diffuse. SSA's alignment training forces concentrated attention patterns that generalize to longer contexts.

### Critical Assessment

This section evaluates whether the paper's experiments genuinely support its claims, identifies genuine weaknesses, and highlights experiments that would have strengthened the work but were not conducted.

---

**On the claim that SSA "achieves state-of-the-art performance under both inference modes" (Abstract, Section 7.2):** The experiments largely support this claim for the specific baselines, model scales, and benchmarks tested, but with important caveats. SSA indeed outperforms FullAttn, MoBA, and NSA on average commonsense reasoning accuracy under both full attention and sparse attention inference (Table 1). The margins are consistent but modest: under sparse inference RF=256, SSA leads FullAttn by 0.65 points (60.27 vs. 59.62); under full attention, SSA leads by 0.38 points (60.39 vs. 60.01). The standard deviations (±0.15 to ±0.55 depending on benchmark) suggest these differences are statistically meaningful given the 5-run averaging but are small enough that they could potentially be reversed by different hyperparameter choices or data mixtures. More critically, the claim of "SOTA" is limited in two dimensions. First, the paper uses a specific Llama-3.2-derived 1B architecture with non-standard modifications (2 KV heads, gated attention) — it is unclear whether the gains transfer to standard architectures without these modifications. Second, the paper's evaluation is confined to commonsense reasoning and long-context benchmarks — domains where sparsity regularization plausibly helps by encouraging focus on relevant information. It is an open question whether SSA would maintain its advantage on tasks that require attending to many tokens equally (e.g., long-range syntactic agreement, multi-hop reasoning where intermediate facts are distributed throughout a document) — such tasks might suffer from the forced concentration of attention mass.

**On the claim that SSA "extrapolates smoothly to varying sparsity budgets" (Abstract, Section 7.3):** This claim is well-supported by Figure 3 and Table A10. SSA's performance is remarkably stable across receptive fields from 256 to 4096, with commonsense accuracy varying by only ~0.2 percentage points across the entire range. FullAttn shows similar extrapolation (varying by ~0.4 points), but at consistently lower absolute performance. MoBA's failure to extrapolate (degrading at larger receptive fields) provides a strong contrast that validates the paper's theoretical framework — the Capability Gap from Proposition 4.1 manifests not just as lower absolute performance but as *inability to benefit from additional compute*. This is a genuinely novel empirical finding that the theoretical analysis predicts but that prior work had not documented. However, the paper does not test whether SSA's smooth extrapolation extends beyond the specific block-sparse pattern used — would the same training framework work with token-level top-k selection, sliding windows, or other sparsity patterns? The paper claims the training framework is general (it could be applied "on top of any sparse attention mechanism"), but only one sparse attention mechanism is tested.

**On the claim that SSA delivers "superior long-context capabilities" (Abstract, Section 7.4):** The evidence is mixed. On LongBench, SSA clearly wins: it achieves the best performance in all 6 evaluation settings (Table 3), with comfortable margins in some cases (e.g., 19.55 vs. 18.97 for FullAttn under full attention after continued training). On NIAH, SSA wins on average (5 out of 6 settings) but shows a specific weakness: at out-of-distribution distances beyond 8k, MoBA's position-agnostic content-based selection outperforms SSA's alignment-trained selection under sparse attention (e.g., MoBA achieves 16.0% vs. SSA's 10.0% at 16k with RF=1024 under RoPE scaling). The paper acknowledges this as a consequence of SSA's alignment training being confined to ≤8k during pretraining. This is not a failure of the approach per se — it is a limitation that continued training partially addresses — but the paper's abstract claim of "superior long-context capabilities" should be qualified: SSA is superior on understanding tasks (LongBench) and on average retrieval, but MoBA shows better generalization to unseen positional distances under certain configurations. A more accurate characterization would be that SSA provides the best overall long-context performance and the unique flexibility to use full attention for retrieval-intensive tasks (where it achieves 100% at 8k while MoBA drops to 32.4%), at the cost of slightly reduced sparse-attention retrieval at extreme extrapolation distances.

**On the claim that "the alignment loss substantially reduces the approximation error compared to baselines" (Abstract, Theorem 1):** The indirect evidence is strong but a direct causal demonstration is missing. Table A9 shows that SSA achieves both higher attention sparsity and lower KL divergence than baselines, and Theorem 1 proves that higher attention sparsity implies a tighter error bound. However, the paper never directly measures the approximation error $\|h^{\text{full}}(t) - h^{\text{sparse}}(t)\|$ and shows that it correlates with $\delta(t)$ — the bound is theoretical, and its tightness in practice is never quantified. This is not a fatal gap — the downstream performance improvements provide practical validation — but the paper's central theoretical claim (that the alignment loss works *because* it reduces $\delta(t)$) is supported by correlation (higher sparsity and better performance co-occur) rather than mechanistic demonstration. An experiment that artificially varied $\delta(t)$ (e.g., by manipulating the attention temperature) and showed that the approximation error tracked the bound would have strengthened this claim substantially.

**Genuine weaknesses in the experimental design:**

- **Single model architecture, single model scale.** All main experiments use a Llama-3.2-derived 1B architecture with two non-standard modifications (2 KV heads, gated attention). The paper argues this model is "representative," but the claim is untested. The gated attention modification, in particular, is noted to be "implicitly employed in NSA" and is applied to "all methods for fair comparison" — but it is not a standard component of MoBA or FullAttn. To what extent do SSA's gains depend on the interaction between gated attention and the alignment loss? This interaction is never ablated. Similarly, the reduction to 2 KV heads is unusual for a 1B model and could affect attention concentration patterns in ways that interact with the alignment loss.

- **The 1B and 300M models are trained from scratch, not fine-tuned from pretrained checkpoints.** This is appropriate for a clean comparison — all methods see identical training data and schedules — but it means SSA's benefits are demonstrated in a regime where the baseline models have not been optimized. Would SSA's alignment loss provide meaningful gains when applied as a fine-tuning stage to an already well-trained full-attention model (e.g., continued training a Llama-3.2-1B checkpoint with SSA for a few billion tokens)? This is the more practical scenario for practitioners who want to add sparse-attention capability to existing models, and it is not tested.

- **NSA's evaluation is incomplete in ways that favor SSA.** NSA cannot be evaluated under full attention (Table A6: perplexity 191.3), which eliminates it from half the comparisons in Table 1. NSA's sliding window provides it with additional effective tokens, making direct comparison at the same stated receptive field potentially unfair. The paper addresses this in Appendix G (showing NSA-nowindow underperforms SSA), but the main tables still present NSA-with-window as the baseline, which could mislead a casual reader into thinking SSA beats NSA at the same effective context access. More importantly, the paper does not train an SSA variant with a sliding window to test whether the alignment loss's benefits are orthogonal to NSA's architectural advantages — would SSA + sliding window outperform NSA + sliding window? This natural combination is unexplored.

- **The training cost of the difficulty estimation analog is absent — but this paper doesn't have difficulty estimation.** This is not a weakness of SSA specifically (unlike the reference paper's compute-optimal scaling, SSA has no per-sample adaptation), but the paper does not discuss how a practitioner would choose between sparse and full attention at inference time for a given input. SSA provides the *capability* to use either mode, but the decision of *when* to use which mode is left entirely to the user. The NIAH results suggest a natural heuristic (use full attention for retrieval, sparse for throughput), but this is not formalized or automated.

- **No comparison to methods that directly train for attention sparsity.** Several prior works have explored explicit sparsity regularization on attention weights (e.g., L1 penalties, entropy regularization). The paper's core claim is that the bidirectional alignment loss is a better way to induce sparsity than direct attention-map supervision, but no such baseline is included. The closest comparison is to MoBA (which has no explicit sparsity mechanism) and FullAttn (which also has none). A comparison against a method that adds an explicit attention sparsity loss to MoBA or FullAttn would test whether feature-space alignment is genuinely superior to attention-space supervision for inducing sparsity.

- **Limited statistical rigor beyond commonsense benchmarks.** Commonsense reasoning results are reported with means and standard deviations over 5 seeds, which is appropriate. But LongBench, NIAH, and PG-19 results are single-run — the paper notes this is due to computational constraints (slow decoding without KV-cache support), but it means the long-context results, which are central to the paper's claims, have unknown variance. The PG-19 evaluation on 1,000 samples with seed 42 is a reasonable subset, but the choice of subset could affect relative rankings if the variance across samples is high — the paper does not report bootstrap confidence intervals or alternate-subset validation.

- **No analysis of computational cost vs. benefit at deployment time.** Table A3 shows sparse attention achieves ~2× speedup at 128k, but the paper never analyzes whether SSA's improved accuracy justifies its training overhead (~17% over MoBA). For a practitioner debating between training with SSA versus simply training MoBA for 17% more tokens, the paper provides no guidance on which would yield better downstream performance. A FLOPs-matched comparison (similar to Section 7 of the reference paper) that gives MoBA additional training tokens to match SSA's total training FLOPs would address this directly.

**Missing experiments that would have strengthened the paper:**

- **Scaling to larger models.** The paper's title claims generality, but all experiments use 300M and 1B models. Would the alignment loss provide the same relative gains at 7B or 13B scale? As models grow, attention patterns may naturally become sparser (a common empirical observation), potentially reducing the marginal benefit of explicit sparsity-inducing training. Conversely, larger models may have more capacity to benefit from the regularization. Without scaling experiments, the paper's claims are confined to the specific scale tested.

- **Diverse sparse attention patterns.** SSA's sparse stream uses MoBA's exact block-sparse mechanism. Would the alignment loss work with streaming-LLM-style attention sinks, sliding windows, or token-level top-k selection? The paper claims generality but tests only one sparse pattern. At minimum, replacing the sparse stream with a different pattern (e.g., a simple sliding window without block selection) and showing that alignment still helps would substantiate the claim of mechanism-agnostic benefits.

- **Asymmetric training ratios beyond the tested range.** The paper sweeps FullRatio in {0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1} but does not test extreme ratios (0.1, 0.9) that might further optimize the tradeoff between full-attention gradient quality and sparse-attention distribution matching. The observation that FullRatio=0.75 achieves near-optimal perplexity with good accuracy suggests asymmetric ratios favoring full attention might be preferable, but this is not pursued.

- **Layer-wise analysis of where alignment matters most.** The alignment loss is applied uniformly across all 16 layers and averaged. Are certain layers more important for alignment than others? Early layers might benefit more from sparsity regularization (since they process lower-level features where token discrimination matters less), while later layers might benefit more from full-attention gradient quality (since they integrate information for prediction). A layer-dependent $\alpha$ or selective alignment could potentially improve efficiency.

- **Direct measurement of gradient flow completeness.** Proposition 4.1 claims that sparse-only training causes zero gradients for dropped tokens. The paper could have verified this directly by instrumenting the training loop to measure the gradient norm for tokens in selected versus dropped blocks, confirming that dropped tokens receive negligible training signal and quantifying how the full attention stream in SSA ameliorates this. This would convert a theoretical proposition into a directly validated mechanism.

**Summary assessment.** The paper's central experimental contribution — that joint training with bidirectional alignment outperforms both pure sparse training and pure full training under sparse inference — is robustly supported. SSA's advantage over MoBA is consistent across benchmarks, receptive fields, and evaluation modes (Tables 1, 3, A10, A11). The advantage over FullAttn under sparse inference is equally clear, and the extrapolation analysis (Figure 3) provides a compelling demonstration that SSA's sparser attention patterns translate into practical benefits beyond standard evaluation settings. The discovery that sparsity-inducing training improves full-attention inference as well (Table 1, Figure 4) is an unexpected finding with implications beyond sparse attention.

However, the paper's generality claims are overstated relative to the experimental scope. The results are entirely on a single non-standard model architecture at a single scale, trained from scratch on a single corpus. The claim that SSA "opens new directions for designing scalable, efficient long-context LLMs" (Section 8) is aspirational but not yet demonstrated at the scales and architectures where sparse attention is most practically relevant (7B+ models deployed in production). The theoretical framework provides a principled motivation for the method, but the causal chain — alignment loss reduces $\delta(t)$, which tightens Theorem 1's bound, which improves sparse-attention quality — is supported by correlation rather than direct causal demonstration. These limitations do not undermine the paper's contribution but define the boundaries within which its claims should be understood.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### Limitation 1: All Results Are on a Single Non-Standard Architecture at a Single Small Scale

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's entire empirical validation — pretraining from scratch, all ablation studies, and all benchmark evaluations — uses only a Llama-3.2-derived 1B-parameter architecture with two modifications that are not standard in sparse attention research: the number of key-value heads is reduced to 2 (from the typical 32 for a 1B model) and gated attention (Qiu et al., 2025) is added. A smaller 300M-parameter variant (with 1 KV head) is used for ablations. The paper does not evaluate SSA at the 7B or 13B scales where sparse attention is most practically deployed, nor does it test on standard unmodified Llama, Mistral, or GPT-style architectures.

The authors acknowledge the model choice explicitly (Section 4): "We believe this model is representative of the capabilities of many contemporary LLMs." But this belief is never tested.

**The consequence.** This limitation creates two forms of uncertainty:

1. **Architecture dependence.** The gated attention modification — applied uniformly to all methods for fairness — is not standard. Gated attention is noted to "mitigate the attention sink" and is "implicitly also employed in NSA." But it is not part of MoBA's standard recipe or FullAttn's. The interaction between gated attention and SSA's bidirectional alignment loss is never ablated: does the alignment loss provide the same benefit without gated attention? Is gated attention doing some of the work that the paper attributes to the alignment loss? A practitioner using a standard architecture (e.g., unmodified Llama-3.1-8B) who wants to apply SSA has no evidence that the gains transfer.

2. **Scale dependence.** The paper's 1B model uses 2 KV heads — an unusually aggressive multi-query attention configuration for a model of this size. At larger scales (7B, 13B, 70B), attention patterns may naturally become sparser — models with more capacity may learn to concentrate attention on relevant tokens without explicit sparsity-inducing training, potentially reducing SSA's marginal benefit. Conversely, larger models may exhibit more complex attention patterns where SSA's regularization helps more. The paper's extrapolation finding (Figure 3) — that SSA extrapolates smoothly to larger receptive fields — is demonstrated only by expanding the receptive field at inference, not by scaling the model. Whether the same benefits hold at 7B parameters is unknown, and practical deployment of sparse attention is most economically relevant at these larger scales, where the inference speedup from sparsity is most impactful in absolute terms.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table A1 establishes the architecture configuration. The 1B model results (Table 1, Table 3, Figure 4) and 300M ablation results (Table 2, Table A14) are consistent — SSA outperforms baselines at both scales — which provides some evidence that the approach scales down, but provides no evidence about scaling up. The paper includes no 7B or 13B experiments and no analysis of whether SSA's benefit changes with model size.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not discuss architecture dependence or scale as a limitation. The claim that the model is "representative" (Section 4) is stated without qualification, and the conclusion (Section 8) generalizes to "designing scalable, efficient long-context LLMs" without acknowledging that scalability has not been demonstrated beyond 1B parameters.

---

### Limitation 2: No Analysis of Whether SSA's Gains Justify the Training Overhead

**The assumption or constraint.** SSA's training incurs approximately 17% higher wall-clock cost than MoBA at equivalent receptive fields (Table A2: 1008 vs. 856 GPU-hours for 1B pretraining at RF=256). This overhead comes from computing the auxiliary attention stream at each layer, even though its outputs are not propagated forward. The paper treats this overhead as acceptable ("increasing cost only marginally," Section 5) and compares all methods at equivalent training *tokens* rather than equivalent training *compute*. But a practitioner deciding whether to adopt SSA faces a budget-allocation question: given a fixed compute budget, should they train with SSA, or should they train MoBA (or FullAttn) for 17% more tokens?

The paper never performs this comparison. All methods are trained on the same number of tokens (100B for pretraining), meaning SSA uses approximately 17% more total FLOPs to achieve its reported gains. The question of whether those gains could be matched by simply training a cheaper method for longer is never addressed.

**The consequence.** The paper's headline results — SSA outperforming MoBA by 1.54 percentage points on average commonsense accuracy under sparse inference (60.27 vs. 58.73 at RF=256) — are computed at equal token counts, not equal FLOPs. A MoBA model trained for 17% more tokens (approximately 117B vs. 100B) might close some or all of this gap. The training curves for any of the methods are not shown, so there is no way to assess whether MoBA's performance is saturating (in which case more tokens wouldn't help and SSA's overhead is clearly justified) or still improving (in which case the fair comparison is unclear).

This is analogous to the compute-matched comparison in the reference paper's Section 7, where test-time compute is compared to pretraining compute at matched total FLOPs. SSA's paper provides no such analysis for training compute. The training efficiency table (Table A2) documents the cost but does not use it to normalize comparisons.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table A2 provides wall-clock GPU-hours for all methods. The 17% overhead figure is derived from comparing SSA (1008 hours) to MoBA (856 hours) for RF=256 pretraining. The paper does not report training loss curves, downstream performance as a function of training tokens, or any FLOPs-matched comparison. Section 5 states that alternating rather than jointly computing both streams "reduc[es] computational cost by half while ensuring the model processes an equal number of tokens as the baselines for a fair comparison" — but this fairness is in tokens, not compute, and the auxiliary stream's cost is not zero.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper acknowledges the training cost (Table A2) but does not discuss whether the performance gains survive a FLOPs-matched comparison. This is a significant gap for practitioners making resource-allocation decisions.

---

### Limitation 3: The Long-Context Evaluation Is Single-Run with Unknown Variance

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's long-context results — LongBench, Needle-in-a-Haystack, and PG-19 perplexity — are all single-run evaluations. Unlike the commonsense reasoning benchmarks, which are averaged over 5 different random seeds with standard deviations reported, the long-context results have no error bars, no confidence intervals, and no replicate runs. The paper states this explicitly (Appendix E): "For LongBench and NIAH, we use greedy decoding and report results from a single run due to computational constraints — our sparse attention kernel does not currently support KV caching, resulting in slow decoding."

This means the long-context results, which form a substantial portion of the paper's empirical contribution (Table 3, Figure 4, Tables A11-A13), have entirely uncharacterized variance. The NIAH data is synthesized dynamically, which introduces additional run-to-run variability that is noted but not measured: "NIAH results may vary slightly across runs as the evaluation data is synthesized dynamically."

**The consequence.** The paper's strongest long-context conclusions — that SSA "achieves the best performance across all inference modes and context extension settings on LongBench" (Section 7.4), that SSA provides "practitioners the flexibility to choose sparse mode for efficiency or full attention for retrieval-intensive tasks" (Section 7.4) — rest on point estimates that may not be statistically reliable. For LongBench, which aggregates 4,750 samples across 16 diverse tasks, the per-dataset sample sizes vary substantially (some tasks have fewer than 100 queries), and the per-task variance could be large enough that the average rankings are sensitive to specific query sets.

The NIAH evaluation at each context length uses 500 test cases per length. While 500 samples provides reasonable statistical power for binary (pass/fail) retrieval, the single-run nature means that the differences between methods — particularly at out-of-distribution lengths where performance is low (single-digit percentages for multiple methods) — could be dominated by sampling noise. For example, at 16k with RF=1024 under RoPE scaling (Table 3), MoBA achieves 16.0% and SSA achieves 10.0% — a gap of 6 percentage points that, with 500 samples, would have a standard error of approximately sqrt(0.16 × 0.84 / 500) ≈ 1.6% for MoBA and sqrt(0.10 × 0.90 / 500) ≈ 1.3% for SSA. The gap appears meaningful, but with only a single run, there is no way to confirm that the relative ordering is stable.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Appendix E acknowledges the single-run limitation. The commonsense reasoning results (Table 1) demonstrate that SSA's advantage is robust to random seed variation for those benchmarks, with standard deviations typically ±0.15-0.55 percentage points on averages, but this does not guarantee similar stability for long-context tasks, which have fundamentally different structure (long-range dependencies, retrieval requirements) and different dataset compositions.

**Mitigation status.** The limitation is acknowledged ("due to computational constraints") but not addressed. The paper does not provide any analysis of the potential magnitude of run-to-run variance for long-context tasks, does not suggest how results might change with multiple runs, and does not qualify its long-context conclusions with appropriate caveats about statistical reliability. The computational constraint (no KV-cache support in the sparse attention kernel) is a practical barrier that the paper treats as a temporary implementation limitation rather than a fundamental evaluation gap.

---

### Limitation 4: The Alignment Training Is Confined to the Pretraining Context Length, Causing Extrapolation Degradation on Retrieval Tasks

**The assumption or constraint.** SSA's bidirectional alignment operates during pretraining at 8k context length. The model never experiences alignment between full and sparse attention at longer distances, because it never sees sequences longer than 8k during alignment training. When length-extrapolated to 32k via RoPE scaling, the alignment signal that guides sparse attention's token selection is operating out-of-distribution.

The paper identifies this as the mechanism behind a specific failure mode (Section 7.4): "within 8k, full attention provides global awareness that guides sparse attention's feature learning and ranking, enabling reliable retrieval from arbitrary positions. Beyond 8k, SSA's performance decreases as this regime lies outside the alignment training distribution. In contrast, MoBA shows stronger retrieval at longer contexts due to its purely content-based block selection, which is more position-agnostic and thus generalizes better to out-of-distribution distances."

**The consequence.** SSA's Needle-in-a-Haystack performance beyond 8k under sparse attention is actually *worse* than MoBA's in several configurations. Under RoPE scaling (Table 3): at 16k with RF=1024, MoBA achieves 16.0% vs. SSA's 10.0%; at 32k with RF=1024, MoBA achieves 8.0% vs. SSA's 3.8%. While both methods perform poorly at extreme extrapolation distances, the fact that SSA — which dominates MoBA at in-distribution lengths — falls behind at out-of-distribution distances reveals a fundamental tension: the alignment training improves sparse attention quality within its training distribution but may make the model more reliant on positional patterns that break down under extrapolation, whereas MoBA's unguided, purely content-based selection is more robust (albeit at lower absolute performance within distribution).

This tradeoff matters for practitioners who want to deploy sparse attention at context lengths beyond what they can afford to train on. RoPE scaling is the standard approach for length extrapolation in production LLMs, and SSA's alignment-trained selection degrades more sharply than MoBA's content-based selection in this regime. The paper's recommendation to use full attention for retrieval-intensive tasks (Section 7.4) is a workaround, not a solution — it requires the model to support full attention at inference, which eliminates the efficiency gain that motivated sparse attention in the first place.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 3 directly shows the crossover: SSA outperforms MoBA at 4k and 8k under sparse attention, but MoBA outperforms SSA at 16k and 32k under sparse attention with RF=1024 for both length-extrapolated and continual-trained models. The paper's explanation (content-based vs. alignment-guided selection) is provided in Section 7.4. Appendix L provides additional context on RoPE scaling and local-token preferences but does not propose a solution.

**Mitigation status.** Partially addressed through continued training. Table 3 (right columns) shows that training SSA for an additional 10B tokens at 32k context length partially restores SSA's advantage: at 32k with RF=1024 after continued training, SSA achieves 5.4% vs. MoBA's 9.4% — MoBA still leads, but by a smaller margin. However, continued training at the target context length is expensive and may not be feasible for all deployment scenarios. The paper does not explore alternative mitigations, such as applying the alignment loss at multiple context lengths during training, or designing a position-agnostic variant of the sparse attention stream that would combine alignment benefits with MoBA's extrapolation robustness.

---

### Limitation 5: NSA Comparisons Are Confounded by Unequal Effective Receptive Fields and Non-Extrapolability

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper compares SSA against NSA under sparse attention inference at stated receptive fields (256 or 1024 tokens from the selection module). However, NSA's architecture includes three components — compression, selection, and sliding window — each contributing additional tokens:

- The sliding window module attends to 128 local tokens (fixed, regardless of the stated receptive field).
- The compression module attends to compressed representations of *all* preceding tokens.

This means NSA's effective receptive field is substantially larger than the nominal 256 or 1024 tokens used in the paper's tables. The selection module alone (analogous to MoBA) provides the stated receptive field, but NSA's final output aggregates information from all three modules, giving it access to sliding window tokens and compressed global tokens beyond what SSA or MoBA see at the same stated configuration.

The paper acknowledges this in Appendix G, showing that removing NSA's sliding window causes performance degradation (Table A7, Table A8), and that SSA outperforms NSA-without-window. But the main tables (Table 1, Table 3) present NSA with its full architecture intact, creating a comparison where NSA has more information available than SSA at the same nominal receptive field.

Additionally, NSA cannot be evaluated under full attention inference at all — Appendix F shows that replacing all three modules with full attention yields perplexity of 191.3 (versus SSA's 15.28). This means half the evaluation settings in Table 1 (the "Full Attention Inference" block) exclude NSA entirely, eliminating a key baseline for the paper's claim that SSA works well under both inference modes.

**The consequence.** The comparison between SSA and NSA in the paper's main tables is unfair in both directions:

1. Under sparse inference (where NSA does participate), NSA benefits from additional tokens that SSA cannot access, making SSA's advantage (or near-parity) understated — SSA is achieving comparable or better performance with strictly less information.

2. Under full attention inference (where NSA cannot participate), SSA's "state-of-the-art" claim cannot be tested against what was previously reported as the strongest sparse attention method. A reader might wonder whether NSA's full-attention failure is a fundamental limitation of its architecture or a correctable implementation issue.

The paper's own analysis in Appendix G attempts to address this by evaluating NSA without its sliding window, but the NSA-nowindow variant is not a published baseline and was created by the authors specifically for this comparison — it is an ablation, not a competing method. There is no published NSA variant without the sliding window to compare against.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Appendix F (Table A6) documents NSA's failure under full attention. Appendix G (Tables A7, A8) shows NSA-nowindow performance. Section 2 notes that "NSA's multi-component structure makes it less flexible to adjust sparsity levels or revert to full attention at inference time." Section 7.1 acknowledges that SSA "slightly lags behind NSA at RF=1024 due to NSA's sliding window module (see Appendix G)."

**Mitigation status.** Partially addressed through the appendix analyses, but the main text does not prominently caveat the NSA comparison. Table 1 presents NSA results without footnotes about effective receptive field differences. A practitioner scanning the results might conclude that NSA is roughly competitive with SSA at sparse inference without realizing that NSA achieves this with access to additional tokens. The paper could have addressed this by (1) training an SSA variant with a sliding window for a fair comparison at matched effective receptive fields, or (2) prominently noting in the main tables that NSA's receptive field includes additional sliding window tokens, or (3) presenting the NSA-nowindow comparison in the main results rather than relegating it to the appendix.

---

### Limitation 6: The Paper Tests Only One Sparse Attention Pattern, Undermining the Claim of Generality

**The assumption or constraint.** SSA's sparse attention stream uses exactly one mechanism: MoBA-style block-sparse attention with mean-pooled block keys and top-k block selection. The paper frames SSA as a general training framework: "This design enables the model to leverage the benefits of both paradigms during training and perform well under either inference mode" (Section 2), and the stated design principles (Section 5) do not reference any specific sparse attention mechanism. However, the only sparse attention pattern tested is MoBA's block-sparse selection.

Other widely-used sparse attention patterns — sliding windows (Child et al., 2019; Beltagy et al., 2020), attention sinks with initial-token preservation (Xiao et al., 2024), token-level top-k selection (DSA, DeepSeek-AI, 2025), strided patterns, or hybrid combinations — are never tested as the sparse attention stream. The paper claims that SSA "could potentially be applied on top of any sparse attention mechanism" (discussed in the prior sections), but this claim is entirely speculative.

**The consequence.** A practitioner interested in applying SSA to a different sparse attention pattern — for example, token-level top-k selection (which DSA argues is more accurate than block-level selection) or a simple sliding window (which is easier to implement and more widely supported in inference frameworks) — has no evidence that the alignment loss would provide the same benefits. The theoretical framework (Theorem 1, Propositions 4.1 and 4.2) is formulated in terms of general sparse attention (any selection mechanism that drops tokens), so the mathematical motivation is mechanism-agnostic. But the empirical demonstration is not.

This matters because different sparse attention patterns have different failure modes that the alignment loss might need to address differently:

- **Block-sparse attention** (the tested pattern) makes coarse-grained decisions — entire blocks are either fully attended or fully dropped. The alignment loss's sparsity objective can be satisfied by concentrating attention mass in a few blocks, which is natural for this pattern.

- **Token-level top-k selection** makes fine-grained decisions — individual tokens are selected based on query-key similarity. The alignment loss would need to produce sparsity at the token level rather than the block level, which is a stricter requirement.

- **Sliding window attention** makes position-based decisions — only recent tokens are attended. The alignment loss would need to produce sparsity that is temporally structured, which may conflict with the content-based block selection that the sparse stream uses during training.

The paper's ablation of NSA's sliding window (Appendix G) is the closest it comes to testing interaction with different patterns, but this is an analysis of NSA's architecture, not a test of SSA with a different sparse stream.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. The paper tests exactly one sparse attention mechanism as SSA's sparse stream. The claim of generality appears in the framing (Section 2: "SSA unifies sparse and full attention within a single framework") and the conclusion (Section 8: "opening new directions for designing scalable, efficient long-context LLMs") but is not supported by experiments with diverse sparsity patterns.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not acknowledge this as a limitation, does not discuss which properties of the sparsity pattern might interact with the alignment loss, and does not suggest that future work should test the framework with other patterns. The generality claim is presented as a conceptual contribution rather than an empirically validated property.
