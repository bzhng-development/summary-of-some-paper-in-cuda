Here are the takeaways from this edition of The Peterman Post (Ryan Peterman on the mechanics of setting up cross-team workstreams at the L4/L5 level, framed around his own L6 Staff promotion):

**Influencing across teams is the baseline for Staff**

Ryan opens with a crisp calibration: influencing across teams is a "baseline expectation for Staff Engineers (L6)." For L4/L5 engineers, the challenge is demonstrating this *before* the promo. The workstream requires two distinct phases—first, finding a problem large enough to need multiple teams; second, convincing those teams the problem is worth solving and that your direction is correct. This post is the playbook for the second phase, drawn directly from the cross-team workstream that anchored his own promotion.

**The concentric-circles alignment model**

The alignment strategy is methodical, expanding outward from a small nucleus: **team alignment**, then **org alignment**, then **partner alignment**. Ryan first discussed the opportunity with a specialist and a tech lead on his own team—getting them on board and using their feedback to refine the direction. With that coalition formed, he wrote publicly about the problem and why it needed solving, which brought managers in his org into alignment and "made staffing discussions easier." Finally, he booked dedicated discussions with relevant tech leads in the partner org to secure their buy-in for collaboration. He notes your specific sequence might differ, but this concentric pattern works for most cases, and consulting your manager on who to align with is a useful shortcut since "your manager should know the org structure well."

**The blunt diagnosis when buy-in fails**

For engineers who struggle to get others to prioritize work they've identified as impactful, Ryan offers a straightforward diagnosis: "either the work isn't that impactful or you're not explaining its impact well." If communication is the root cause, two correctives apply. First, **speak in terms of impact your audience already knows**—tying your workstream to a metric people already care about makes persuasion dramatically easier. Second, **be concise**—people are busy, and the goal is to "maximize how much of your message you can get across with limited time." He notes this is why strong engineers emphasize communication as a force multiplier: the better you communicate, the more natural alignment becomes.

**Roadmapping as the second act**

Once people agree the problem is worth solving, you need a concrete plan. Ryan's sequence: **leverage domain expertise** (he already had high-level ideas and began hashing out designs to map what needed to get done), **align on project plans** (leading a few meetings to agree on solutions, divide ownership, and set rough timelines), then **communicate alignment** broadly—sharing an update for each cross-team project covering what was planned, why, who was doing what, and rough timelines. The pattern is consistent: communication quality correlates directly with how smoothly alignment lands.

**Two tangible investments in communication skill**

Ryan offers a pair of specific, actionable suggestions. For speaking, **Toastmasters**—an international public-speaking club he's participated in since college—provides a supportive environment to practice articulation; he credits it with helping him "better articulate myself during meetings." For writing, the prescription is straightforward: "write more and solicit feedback to become a better writer." That's one of his top reasons for writing this Substack, and he can already see measurable improvement compared to earlier articles.

**On ownership and the coming funnel post**

The final piece of the original question—how to maintain ownership and leadership through the workstream—he intentionally defers. It's a topic that "comes up a lot" and is "worth its own post since there are a lot of misconceptions." He closes with a teaser aimed at engineers who feel more senior colleagues take opportunities from them, promising the next article will address that dynamic directly.

---

Ryan's **concentric-circles alignment model** does double duty here—it's both a tactical sequencing play and a communication forcing function, since each expanding ring demands you articulate impact in terms that specific audience already cares about. His blunt diagnosis ("either the work isn't that impactful or you're not explaining its impact well") cuts through a common L4/L5 frustration by recasting buy-in failure as a signal to sharpen either the problem selection or the message, not to push harder. The roadmapping phase then makes the same point in reverse: when you've done the communication work upfront, getting time with relevant engineers and aligning on concrete plans becomes "easy"—a direct instantiation of his broader stance that **communication quality correlates directly with how smoothly alignment lands**. The deferred ownership question teases what may become his next named concept, but even here the preview is consistent with the rest of the framework: misconceptions about senior engineers "taking opportunities" are common enough to warrant their own post, implying there's a reframe coming that will tie back to the same skill of making impact legible to others.
