# MH-MoE: Multi-Head Mixture-of-Experts

**ArXiv:** [2411.16205](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.16205)

## 🎯 Pitch

MH-MoE’s multi-head trick, which splits tokens across experts, doesn’t have to increase FLOPs—this paper shows how to keep compute flat while still beating standard and fine-grained MoE, and it even works with 1-bit quantized models, making it a drop-in upgrade with no efficiency penalty.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces a novel implementation of **Multi-Head Mixture-of-Experts (MH-MoE)** that achieves FLOPs and parameter parity with standard sparse Mixture-of-Experts models, addressing the computational overhead present in the original MH-MoE formulation. The authors validate their approach on language modeling tasks using a 12-layer decoder-only Transformer with a model dimension of 768 trained on the RedPajama dataset, evaluating perplexity on RedPajama, Wiki, and C4 validation sets. The core mechanism is a multi-head projection and splitting of input tokens into sub-tokens processed by parallel expert branches (splitting each token along the embedding dimension into h heads, processing each head through separate expert FFNs, then merging via a linear projection), combined with a principled FLOPs-matching recipe that reduces the per-expert intermediate dimension while proportionally increasing the number of experts to maintain total parameter count (e.g., for top-1 gating with h = 3 heads, setting dmhmoe = dmoe − d and increasing experts from Emoe to 4Emoe − 1). The three-head MH-MoE variant achieves consistent perplexity improvements — reaching 10.51 on RedPajama at 100K steps versus 10.90 for standard SMoE and 10.74 for fine-grained SMoE — and the paper further demonstrates compatibility with 1-bit quantization (BitNet), outperforming quantized SMoE baselines while establishing that the head layer contributes more substantially to these gains than the merge layer, as shown through systematic ablation.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: MH-MoE Demonstrates Superior Quality but Comes with Hidden Computational Cost

The paper addresses a specific, practical tension in the Mixture-of-Experts literature: **the original Multi-Head Mixture-of-Experts (MH-MoE) formulation achieves better model quality than standard sparse MoE, but at the cost of significantly increased FLOPs**. This tension is important because it means practitioners face a false choice — they can either use MH-MoE and pay a computational penalty that undermines the very efficiency MoE architectures are designed to provide, or they can use standard SMoE and leave model quality on the table.

To understand why this gap matters, we need to clarify what sparse MoE is supposed to achieve. The fundamental promise of sparse MoE architectures, established by Shazeer et al. (2017) and refined through GShard (Lepikhin et al., 2020), GLaM (Du et al., 2021), and others, is **conditional computation**: the model dramatically increases its total parameter count while keeping the per-token FLOPs roughly constant. This is achieved by routing each token to only a small subset of available experts (typically 1 or 2 out of many), so the forward pass only executes computation in the activated experts. The result is that a sparse MoE model can rival the quality of a much larger dense model while requiring far fewer FLOPs per token — Mixtral 8×7B outperforming LLaMA-70B is the canonical recent example the paper cites (Jiang et al., 2024).

This efficiency is not merely an academic curiosity. It has direct economic and deployment implications: sparse MoE enables training and serving models that would be prohibitively expensive as dense architectures. If a technique like MH-MoE improves quality but erodes this efficiency advantage, it partially defeats the purpose of using MoE in the first place. The paper's explicit FLOPs analysis in Section 2.2 quantifies exactly how significant this erosion is in the original MH-MoE formulation.

### The FLOPs Gap in the Original MH-MoE

The paper traces the problem to a specific, measurable discrepancy. Wu et al. (2024) introduced MH-MoE with a key architectural innovation: splitting each token into multiple "heads" (sub-tokens along the embedding dimension), processing each head through separate experts, and then merging the results via linear projections. This multi-head mechanism allows different heads to attend to information from different representation spaces within different experts — a form of increased representational flexibility that the original paper demonstrated improves model quality.

However, the original MH-MoE also introduced two additional linear projection layers that standard SMoE does not have: a **head layer** (`W_head ∈ ℝ^{d×d}`) that projects the input before splitting, and a **merge layer** (`W_merge ∈ ℝ^{d×d}`) that integrates the concatenated head outputs afterward. The paper's complexity analysis (Section 2.2) reveals that with the hyperparameter settings used in Wu et al. (2024) — specifically, `h = 4` heads and `β = 63/64` scaling factor for the intermediate dimension — the leading term in the FLOPs count is `67Bd²` versus `16Bd²` for standard SMoE. The head and merge layers alone contribute `2Bd²` each (from Equation 6: `2Bd² − Bd` per linear projection), but the larger intermediate dimension in the experts (`dmoe = 4βhd` in the original formulation) compounds the cost further.

This means the original MH-MoE, while achieving activated parameter and total model parameter parity with standard SMoE, is **approximately 4× more expensive in FLOPs per token**. In a deployment context where FLOPs directly translate to latency and energy cost, this makes MH-MoE substantially less attractive than the numbers reported in Wu et al. (2024) might suggest — the quality improvements come with a hidden computational price tag.

### Why This Gap Has Not Been Previously Addressed

The paper positions itself against a backdrop where the research community has focused primarily on **improving model quality** through MoE architectural innovations, with less attention to whether those innovations preserve the fundamental efficiency guarantees of sparse MoE. Several lines of prior work illustrate this pattern:

**Fine-grained MoE** (which the paper implements as a baseline) increases the number of experts while reducing each expert's intermediate dimension — e.g., moving from 8 experts with `dmoe = 2048` to 16 experts with `dmoe = 1024`. This can improve quality by enabling more specialized expert routing, but it does not fundamentally change the single-head-per-token processing paradigm. Each token sees only a single representation space per expert, limiting the diversity of features that can be extracted.

**DeepSeekMoE** (Dai et al., 2024) introduces shared experts alongside routed experts, which the paper adopts as an additional experimental setting (Tables 2 and 3). Shared experts process all tokens regardless of routing decisions, providing a common knowledge baseline that routed experts can build upon. This is orthogonal to the multi-head mechanism — it improves expert specialization but does not address how heads interact with experts.

**BitNet** (Ma et al., 2024) enables 1-bit quantization of LLM weights, dramatically reducing memory footprint and enabling more efficient deployment. The paper explores MH-MoE's compatibility with BitNet (Section 3.2), but this is a deployment optimization rather than an architectural innovation that addresses the FLOPs parity problem.

What connects these prior efforts is that none of them systematically addressed the FLOPs overhead of the multi-head mechanism itself. The original MH-MoE paper established that multi-head processing across experts is beneficial, but left open the question of whether it can be achieved **without** the computational premium. This paper fills exactly that gap.

### The Specific Gap: No Principled Recipe Exists for Matching FLOPs in MH-MoE

Beyond the empirical observation that original MH-MoE is expensive, there is a more fundamental gap: **no principled framework exists for translating a standard SMoE configuration into an MH-MoE configuration while maintaining both parameter and FLOPs parity**. The paper's "Utilization Guidelines" section (Section 2.3) is the core contribution that addresses this — it derives equations (8) and (9) that prescribe exactly how to set the per-expert intermediate dimension and the number of experts in MH-MoE given a standard SMoE configuration.

This matters for practitioners because without such guidelines, adopting MH-MoE requires ad hoc hyperparameter tuning that may or may not achieve FLOPs parity. The derived recipe makes MH-MoE a **drop-in replacement** for standard SMoE layers in any Transformer architecture: given a base SMoE configuration with `dmoe` intermediate dimension, `Emoe` experts, and top-`k` gating, the practitioner can compute `dmhmoe = dmoe − d/k` and adjust the expert count `Emhmoe` accordingly, and the resulting MH-MoE layer will have identical FLOPs and parameter count. This transformability is the key practical contribution that the prior literature lacked.

### The Relationship to Multi-Head Attention Provides a Theoretical Motivation

The paper draws an explicit analogy between MH-MoE's head and merge layers and the multi-head attention mechanism from Vaswani et al. (2017). In multi-head attention, the input is projected into query, key, and value representations, split into multiple heads, each head attends independently, and the results are concatenated and projected through an output layer. The head layer in MH-MoE is conceptually analogous to the Q/K/V projections, and the merge layer is analogous to the attention output projection.

This analogy is important for motivation because it suggests that the multi-head processing in MH-MoE is not an arbitrary design choice — it mirrors a mechanism that has proven fundamental to Transformer performance. Just as multi-head attention allows different heads to attend to different positional relationships and semantic subspaces, MH-MoE allows different heads to be routed to different experts, potentially specializing in different aspects of the input representation. The ablation results in Section 3.3 (Tables 4 and 5) confirm that this analogy has empirical teeth: simply adding head and merge layers to standard SMoE without the multi-head splitting mechanism yields only marginal gains (Table 4, SMoE with head/merge: 11.84 → 11.84 perplexity on RedPajama at 50K steps), while the full MH-MoE with head splitting shows significant improvement (11.71 → 11.46). This demonstrates that the benefit comes specifically from the **combination** of multi-head splitting and the projection layers, not from the projections alone.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper does not claim to introduce a fundamentally new architectural concept — it explicitly builds on Wu et al. (2024) and credits MH-MoE as prior work. Its contribution is at the **implementation and efficiency level**: identifying the FLOPs overhead in the original formulation, deriving the mathematical conditions under which FLOPs parity can be achieved, validating that the parity-constrained MH-MoE still outperforms standard SMoE and fine-grained SMoE baselines, and demonstrating that the head layer (specifically) drives the quality improvement through targeted ablations.

This positioning is analogous to work that takes a known architecture and provides a more efficient realization — similar to how efficient attention variants (e.g., flash attention) improve the practical deployability of standard attention without changing the underlying mathematical operation. The paper's value is in making MH-MoE **practically adoptable** without hidden computational penalties.

The paper also positions MH-MoE as **complementary to other MoE improvements** rather than competing with them. The experiments with shared experts (Section 3.1, Table 2) show that MH-MoE's benefits persist when combined with the DeepSeekMoE shared expert approach. The BitNet experiments (Section 3.2, Table 3) show compatibility with 1-bit quantization. This suggests that MH-MoE can be stacked with other MoE innovations to compound benefits, rather than requiring a tradeoff between different improvement strategies.

### Real-World Significance

The practical significance of FLOPs-matched MH-MoE extends across several deployment scenarios:

**Training efficiency.** If MH-MoE can achieve better perplexity at the same FLOPs budget as standard SMoE, it means practitioners can train better models for the same GPU-hour cost. The paper's results show consistent improvements of ~0.2–0.4 perplexity points across datasets at 100K steps (e.g., RedPajama: 10.90 → 10.51 for 3-head MH-MoE vs. standard SMoE), which is a meaningful gain at constant training cost.

**Inference latency.** Since FLOPs parity means the same number of operations per forward pass, MH-MoE models should have comparable inference latency to standard SMoE models of the same parameter count. This is critical for deployment in latency-sensitive applications.

**Memory footprint.** The parameter parity guarantee means MH-MoE does not increase the model's memory requirements, which is often the binding constraint for on-device or edge deployment. The BitNet experiments further show that MH-MoE can be combined with aggressive quantization for memory-constrained settings.

**Scalability.** Because the FLOPs-matching recipe is derived analytically (Equations 8 and 9) rather than empirically tuned, it should generalize to larger models and different hyperparameter configurations. This means the approach can be applied at scale without extensive re-tuning, which is essential for the very large models (hundreds of billions of parameters) where MoE architectures are most impactful.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

The paper presents a **drop-in replacement recipe** for Mixture-of-Experts layers in Transformers. Given any standard sparse MoE configuration, the recipe produces a Multi-Head MoE variant that has identical FLOPs and parameter count, yet achieves better language modeling perplexity. The core idea is to split each token's representation into multiple "heads" (sub-tokens along the embedding dimension), route each head independently through smaller experts, and then merge the head outputs — all while mathematically compensating for the added projection layers by shrinking the per-expert intermediate dimension and adding more experts.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The MH-MoE layer has five major components, processing one token at a time:

1. **Head projection layer** (`W_head ∈ ℝ^{d×d}`) — a learned linear transformation that projects the input token `x ∈ ℝ^d` into a new representation `x̂ ∈ ℝ^d`, analogous to the Q/K/V projections in multi-head attention. This prepares the token for splitting into heads.

2. **Token splitting operation** — the projected token `x̂ ∈ ℝ^d` is split into `h` sub-tokens, each of dimension `d/h`. These sub-tokens are arranged as parallel sequences `[x̃₁, x̃₂, ..., x̃ₕ]` where each `x̃ᵢ ∈ ℝ^{d/h}`. This is a pure reshape operation with no learned parameters.

3. **Sparse MoE layer** (operating on sub-tokens) — each sub-token `x̃` is processed by a standard sparse MoE layer with `E` experts and top-`k` gating. Critically, each expert now has intermediate dimension `d_mhmoe` (smaller than the standard SMoE's `d_moe`) and operates on the reduced dimension `d/h` rather than `d`. The output per sub-token is `ỹ ∈ ℝ^{d/h}`.

4. **Token concatenation** — the `h` output sub-tokens `ỹ` are rearranged back into the original token order and concatenated to form `ŷ ∈ ℝ^d`. This is the inverse of the splitting operation.

5. **Merge projection layer** (`W_merge ∈ ℝ^{d×d}`) — a learned linear transformation that integrates the concatenated head outputs back into a single token representation `y ∈ ℝ^d`, analogous to the output projection in multi-head attention.

Information flows: input token → head projection → split into `h` sub-tokens → each sub-token routed to top-`k` experts → expert FFN processing (with reduced intermediate dim) → concatenate sub-token outputs → merge projection → output token. The gate function `G` operates independently per sub-token, so different heads can be routed to different experts.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the standard sparse MoE computation (Equation 1) — this establishes the baseline that MH-MoE modifies, and understanding it is prerequisite to seeing what MH-MoE changes.
- **Second**, the MH-MoE forward pass in its full form (Equations 2–4) — the head projection, splitting, expert processing, concatenation, and merge projection. This is the complete architectural specification.
- **Third**, the FLOPs complexity analysis (Equation 6) and the FLOPs-matching derivation (Equations 8–9) — the core technical contribution that makes MH-MoE practical. This shows exactly how to set hyperparameters so that MH-MoE and standard SMoE have identical computational cost.
- **Fourth**, the concrete instantiation of the recipe — the two specific MH-MoE variants used in experiments (2-head, 3-head) with their exact hyperparameters, showing the recipe in action.
- **Fifth**, the design rationale — why the head layer matters more than the merge layer, why naive addition of projection layers to SMoE doesn't help, and why the multi-head splitting is the essential ingredient.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is an **implementation and efficiency paper** whose core idea is that MH-MoE can be reformulated to achieve FLOPs and parameter parity with standard sparse MoE while retaining its quality benefits, by analytically deriving the hyperparameter adjustments needed to compensate for the added projection layers.

#### Standard Sparse MoE Forward Pass (Baseline)

The paper begins by restating the standard sparse MoE computation that MH-MoE modifies. In a standard SMoE layer, the traditional Feed-Forward Network (FFN) in a Transformer is replaced by `E` experts, each being a standard FFN `Expert_i : ℝ^d → ℝ^d`, and a gate function `G : ℝ^d → ℝ^E` that produces a probability distribution over experts for each input token.

> $$y = \sum_{p \in \Phi} G(x) \cdot \text{Expert}_p(x)$$
> 
> where `$x \in \mathbb{R}^d$` is the input token, `$G(x)$` is the gate function output (a probability distribution over `$E$` experts), `$\Phi = \text{Top}_k(\text{Expert}_i)$` is the set of `$k$` activated experts selected by taking the top-`$k$` entries of the gate output, and `$\text{Expert}_p(x)$` is the output of the `$p$`-th expert for input `$x$`.

**What it computes:** for each input token `$x$`, the gate function scores all `$E$` experts, selects the `$k$` highest-scoring experts (typically `$k = 1$` or `$k = 2$`), and computes a weighted sum of those experts' outputs, with gate probabilities as weights. Only the selected experts execute — the unselected experts are not computed, saving FLOPs.

**Why this form:** the top-`$k$` sparsification is the key efficiency mechanism. Rather than computing all `$E$` experts (which would cost `$E \times$` the FLOPs of a single FFN), the layer computes only `$k$` experts. This allows the total parameter count to scale with `$E$` while the per-token FLOPs scale with `$k$` (typically `$k \ll E$`). The weighted sum preserves differentiability through the gate, enabling end-to-end training. The paper uses top-1 gating (`$k = 1$`) in its primary SMoE baseline, meaning each token is processed by exactly one expert — the most extreme sparsity and efficiency setting.

The expert function `$\text{Expert}_p(x)$` is implemented as a standard position-wise feed-forward network. The paper uses SwiGLU activation (Shazeer, 2020) rather than ReLU for the primary experiments, but the complexity analysis uses an FFN with ReLU activation as a simplified model for FLOPs counting:

> $$\text{Expert}(x) = \text{FFN}_{\text{ReLU}}(X, W_1, W_2) = \max(XW_1^\top, 0)W_2$$
> 
> where `$X \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times \frac{d}{h}}$` is the batched input (with `$B$` tokens and reduced dimension `$d/h$` in the MH-MoE case), `$W_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{\frac{d}{h} \times d_{\text{moe}}}$` is the up-projection weight matrix mapping from the input dimension to the intermediate dimension, and `$W_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{moe}} \times \frac{d}{h}}$` is the down-projection weight matrix mapping back.

**What it computes:** the standard two-layer FFN: (1) up-project from input dimension to intermediate dimension `$d_{\text{moe}}$`, (2) apply ReLU non-linearity (zeroing out negative values), (3) down-project back to the input dimension. The intermediate dimension `$d_{\text{moe}}$` is typically `$4d$` (four times the model dimension), creating a bottleneck-expand-bottleneck structure that gives the FFN representational capacity.

**Why this form:** the ReLU-based formulation is used for complexity analysis because it has a simple, exact FLOPs count. The actual experiments use SwiGLU, which has a gated structure (two up-projections and a gating mechanism) but comparable FLOPs. The analysis abstracts away the activation function details to focus on the matrix multiplication FLOPs, which dominate the computation.

#### MH-MoE Forward Pass

The MH-MoE layer modifies the standard SMoE forward pass by inserting projection and splitting/merging operations before and after the expert computation. The process proceeds in four stages:

**Stage 1: Head projection.** The input token is linearly transformed by a learned projection matrix:

> $$\hat{x} = xW_{\text{head}}$$
> 
> where `$x \in \mathbb{R}^d$` is the input token, `$W_{\text{head}} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$` is a learned weight matrix, and `$\hat{x} \in \mathbb{R}^d$` is the projected token (same dimension as the input).

**What it computes:** a full-rank linear transformation of the token representation. This is a `$d \times d$` matrix multiply — a substantial operation that costs `$2d^2 - d$` scalar multiplications (the paper uses `$2Bd^2 - Bd$` for a batch of `$B$` tokens, derived from the standard matrix multiplication FLOPs count where multiplying an `$m \times n$` matrix by an `$n \times p$` matrix requires `$2mnp - mp$` scalar multiplications).

**Why this form:** the head projection prepares the token for splitting by learning a representation where different subspaces (corresponding to different heads) encode complementary information. The analogy to multi-head attention's Q/K/V projections is explicit: just as those projections learn to extract query, key, and value features that different attention heads can specialize on, the head projection learns to extract features that different expert heads can specialize on. The projection is full-rank (`$d \times d$`) rather than factored, giving maximum flexibility in how information is redistributed across dimensions before splitting.

**Stage 2: Token splitting.** The projected token is divided into `$h$` sub-tokens along the embedding dimension:

> The projected token `$\hat{x}$` is split into `$h$` sub-tokens along the token dimensions, and these sub-tokens are arranged in parallel according to the original token sequence, forming a new feature space `$[\tilde{x}_1, \tilde{x}_2, ..., \tilde{x}_h]$`, where `$\tilde{x}_h \in \mathbb{R}^{d/h}$` and `$h$` denotes the number of heads.

**What it computes:** a pure reshape and rearrangement operation. The token of dimension `$d$` is partitioned into `$h$` contiguous chunks of dimension `$d/h$`, and these chunks are treated as independent tokens for the subsequent MoE processing. There are no learned parameters and no FLOPs cost — this is a memory layout operation.

**Why this form:** the splitting is what enables the "multi-head" aspect. Each sub-token is processed by its own routing decision and expert computation, so different "heads" of the same original token can be routed to different experts. This means a single token can simultaneously leverage multiple experts (up to `$h \times k$` experts if each head selects different experts), rather than being restricted to `$k$` experts as in standard SMoE. The splitting dimension is the embedding dimension rather than, say, splitting into separate sequences, because the goal is to have different heads process different *aspects of the same token's representation*, not different tokens.

**Stage 3: Expert processing on sub-tokens.** Each sub-token `$\tilde{x}$` is processed by a sparse MoE layer operating on the reduced dimension:

> $$\tilde{y} = \sum_{p \in \Phi} G(\tilde{x}) \cdot \text{Expert}_p(\tilde{x})$$
> 
> where `$\tilde{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{d/h}$` is a sub-token, `$G: \mathbb{R}^{d/h} \to \mathbb{R}^E$` is the gate function operating on the reduced dimension, `$\Phi$` is the set of activated experts (selected by top-`$k$` gating), and `$\text{Expert}_p: \mathbb{R}^{d/h} \to \mathbb{R}^{d/h}$` is the `$p$`-th expert operating on sub-tokens.

**What it computes:** for each sub-token, the gate scores all `$E$` experts, selects the top-`$k$`, and computes the weighted sum of expert outputs — exactly the same computation as standard SMoE, but on reduced-dimension tokens. The output per sub-token is `$\tilde{y} \in \mathbb{R}^{d/h}$`.

**Why this form:** this is the core efficiency mechanism. Because each sub-token has dimension `$d/h$` rather than `$d$`, and each expert has intermediate dimension `$d_{\text{mhmoe}}$` (which the paper reduces compared to standard `$d_{\text{moe}}$`), the per-expert FLOPs are substantially smaller than in standard SMoE. The total expert FLOPs across all `$h$` heads is `$h \times$` (cost of one head), and the paper's FLOPs-matching recipe ensures this equals the cost of standard SMoE's expert computation.

**Stage 4: Concatenation and merge projection.** The sub-token outputs are recombined:

> After processing through the MoE layer, all obtained outputs `$\tilde{y}$` are rearranged into the original order of sub-tokens and concatenated together to form `$\hat{y} \in \mathbb{R}^d$`. This concatenated output `$\hat{y}$` is then projected using a merge layer: `$y = \hat{y}W_{\text{merge}}$`, where `$W_{\text{merge}} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$` is a learned weight matrix.

**What it computes:** the inverse of the splitting operation (concatenation) followed by a learned linear projection. The concatenation is a no-cost memory operation; the merge projection is a `$d \times d$` matrix multiply costing `$2d^2 - d$` scalar multiplications (or `$2Bd^2 - Bd$` for a batch).

**Why this form:** the merge projection is necessary because simply concatenating the sub-token outputs would produce a representation where different segments of the vector come from different experts with no cross-head interaction. The merge projection mixes information across all head outputs, allowing the model to combine insights from different experts that processed different aspects of the token. This is directly analogous to the output projection in multi-head attention, which mixes information across attention heads.

#### FLOPs Complexity Analysis and the Matching Recipe

This is the paper's core technical contribution — the mathematical derivation that enables MH-MoE to achieve FLOPs parity with standard SMoE.

**Total FLOPs of MH-MoE.** The paper counts scalar multiplications for the full MH-MoE layer:

> $$\underbrace{2Bd^2 - Bd}_{\text{Head Layer}} + \underbrace{(4Bdd_{\text{mhmoe}} - Bd - Bd_{\text{mhmoe}}h) \cdot k}_{\text{Activated Experts}} + \underbrace{2Bd^2 - Bd}_{\text{Merge Layer}}$$

where `$B$` is the number of tokens in the batch, `$d$` is the token dimension, `$d_{\text{mhmoe}}$` is the intermediate dimension of each expert in MH-MoE, `$h$` is the number of heads, and `$k$` is the number of activated experts per sub-token (top-`$k$` gating).

**What it computes:** the total number of scalar multiplications for a forward pass through the MH-MoE layer, decomposed into three parts. The head layer term `$2Bd^2 - Bd$` comes from multiplying a `$B \times d$` input by a `$d \times d$` weight matrix (cost: `$2Bd^2 - Bd$`). The merge layer costs the same. The activated experts term is more complex: for each of the `$B \times h$` sub-tokens (since each token is split into `$h$` sub-tokens), `$k$` experts are activated, and each expert performs an FFN on a sub-token of dimension `$d/h$` with intermediate dimension `$d_{\text{mhmoe}}$`. The FFN cost per sub-token is the sum of the up-projection (`$W_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{d/h \times d_{\text{mhmoe}}}$`: cost `$2(d/h)d_{\text{mhmoe}} - d_{\text{mhmoe}}$`) and down-projection (`$W_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{mhmoe}} \times d/h}$`: cost `$2d_{\text{mhmoe}}(d/h) - d/h$`). Summing and simplifying yields the `$(4Bdd_{\text{mhmoe}} - Bd - Bd_{\text{mhmoe}}h) \cdot k$` term.

**Why this form:** the decomposition separates the cost of the MH-MoE-specific additions (head and merge layers) from the expert computation. This makes the FLOPs overhead of the multi-head mechanism transparent: it is exactly `$4Bd^2 - 2Bd$`, independent of the expert configuration. The expert term shows that using `$h$` heads multiplies the number of sub-tokens by `$h$`, but each sub-token has dimension `$d/h$`, so the total expert FLOPs scale with `$Bdd_{\text{mhmoe}}$` (with `$dd_{\text{mhmoe}}$` rather than `$d^2$`), which is where the savings come from if `$d_{\text{mhmoe}}$` is reduced.

**Standard SMoE FLOPs for comparison.** The standard SMoE layer (without head/merge layers) has expert FLOPs:

> $$(4Bdd_{\text{moe}} - Bd_{\text{moe}} - Bd) \cdot k$$

where `$d_{\text{moe}}$` is the intermediate dimension of experts in standard SMoE (typically `$4d$`).

**What it computes:** the same expert FFN cost but on full-dimension tokens `$x \in \mathbb{R}^d$` rather than sub-tokens. Each activated expert processes `$B$` tokens of dimension `$d$` with intermediate dimension `$d_{\text{moe}}$`. The leading term is `$4Bdd_{\text{moe}} \cdot k$`.

**Why this form:** this baseline is what MH-MoE must match. For standard SMoE with `$d_{\text{moe}} = 4d$` and top-1 gating (`$k = 1$`), the leading term is `$16Bd^2$`, which the paper uses as the reference point throughout.

**The FLOPs-matching derivation.** The paper's key insight is that the head and merge layer FLOPs can be compensated for by reducing the per-expert intermediate dimension. Setting the leading terms equal:

> The leading term of MH-MoE FLOPs is `$4Bd^2 + 4Bdd_{\text{mhmoe}} \cdot k$` (the `$4Bd^2$` comes from the head and merge layers, each contributing `$2Bd^2$` in the leading term). Setting this equal to the leading term of standard SMoE, `$4Bdd_{\text{moe}} \cdot k$`, and solving for `$d_{\text{mhmoe}}$`:

> $$d_{\text{mhmoe}} = d_{\text{moe}} - \frac{d}{k}$$

where `$d_{\text{mhmoe}}$` is the intermediate dimension to use in MH-MoE experts, `$d_{\text{moe}}$` is the intermediate dimension in the original standard SMoE configuration, `$d$` is the token dimension, and `$k$` is the number of activated experts.

**What it computes:** the reduced intermediate dimension that MH-MoE experts must use to absorb the FLOPs cost of the head and merge layers. For a standard SMoE configuration with `$d_{\text{moe}} = 4d$` and top-1 gating (`$k = 1$`), this gives `$d_{\text{mhmoe}} = 4d - d = 3d$` — the per-expert intermediate dimension drops from `$4d$` to `$3d$`. For top-2 gating (`$k = 2$`), it gives `$d_{\text{mhmoe}} = 4d - d/2 = 3.5d$`. For top-3 gating (`$k = 3$`), it gives `$d_{\text{mhmoe}} = 4d - d/3 \approx 3.67d$`.

**Why this form:** the equation `$d_{\text{moe}} - d/k$` has an intuitive interpretation. The penalty term `$d/k$` represents the FLOPs "budget" that must be taken from each expert to pay for the head and merge layers. The head and merge layers together cost `$4Bd^2$` FLOPs (leading term). This cost is spread across the `$k$` activated experts, each processing `$B$` tokens (or `$Bh$` sub-tokens). The per-expert per-token FLOPs reduction is proportional to `$d/k$`, which is subtracted from the original intermediate dimension. This derivation assumes the number of heads `$h$` does not appear in the leading term matching condition because the expert FLOPs scale with `$Bdd_{\text{mhmoe}} \cdot k$` regardless of `$h$` — the `$h$` heads multiply the number of sub-tokens but each sub-token has dimension `$d/h$`, and these factors cancel to produce a total that depends only on `$Bd$` and `$d_{\text{mhmoe}}$`.

**Parameter parity.** Reducing `$d_{\text{mhmoe}}$` reduces the parameters per expert, so MH-MoE must have more experts to maintain the same total parameter count. The paper derives:

> $$\underbrace{2dd_{\text{moe}} \cdot E_{\text{moe}}}_{\text{\# parameters of standard MoE}} = \underbrace{2d^2 + \frac{2d}{h} d_{\text{mhmoe}} \cdot E_{\text{mhmoe}}}_{\text{\# parameters of MH-MoE}} = 2d^2 + \frac{2d}{h} \left(d_{\text{moe}} - \frac{d}{k}\right) \cdot E_{\text{mhmoe}}$$

where `$E_{\text{moe}}$` is the number of experts in standard SMoE and `$E_{\text{mhmoe}}$` is the number of experts in MH-MoE.

**What it computes:** the equation sets the total parameter count of the standard SMoE expert layer equal to the total parameter count of the MH-MoE layer (including head and merge layers). The left side, `$2dd_{\text{moe}} \cdot E_{\text{moe}}$`, counts the two weight matrices per expert (`$W_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d_{\text{moe}}}$` and `$W_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{moe}} \times d}$`), each with `$dd_{\text{moe}}$` parameters, times `$E_{\text{moe}}$` experts. The right side has three components: `$2d^2$` for the head and merge projection matrices (each `$d \times d$`, so `$d^2$` parameters each), plus the expert parameters in MH-MoE where each expert has two weight matrices of sizes `$W_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{(d/h) \times d_{\text{mhmoe}}}$` and `$W_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{mhmoe}} \times (d/h)}$`, giving `$2(d/h)d_{\text{mhmoe}}$` parameters per expert, times `$E_{\text{mhmoe}}$` experts.

**Why this form:** the parameter parity equation ensures that MH-MoE does not increase the model's memory footprint. The experts are smaller individually (because `$d_{\text{mhmoe}} < d_{\text{moe}}$` and the input dimension is `$d/h$` rather than `$d$`), so more experts are needed to reach the same total parameter count. Solving for `$E_{\text{mhmoe}}$` gives the required number of experts for any given configuration.

**Worked example.** The paper provides a concrete derivation for `$d_{\text{moe}} = 4d$`, top-1 gating (`$k = 1$`), and `$h = 3$` heads:

> First, `$d_{\text{mhmoe}} = 4d - d/1 = 3d$`. Then, using the parameter parity equation with `$h = 3$`: `$2d \cdot 4d \cdot E_{\text{moe}} = 2d^2 + \frac{2d}{3} \cdot 3d \cdot E_{\text{mhmoe}} = 2d^2 + 2d^2 \cdot E_{\text{mhmoe}}$`, which simplifies to `$E_{\text{mhmoe}} = 4E_{\text{moe}} - 1$`.

**What it computes:** for the specific configuration where standard SMoE has `$E_{\text{moe}}$` experts with intermediate dimension `$4d$`, the MH-MoE variant with `$h = 3$` heads and top-1 gating requires `$4E_{\text{moe}} - 1$` experts with intermediate dimension `$3d$` to maintain both FLOPs and parameter parity. If the standard SMoE baseline has 8 experts, MH-MoE would need `$4 \times 8 - 1 = 31$` experts at this configuration.

**Why this form:** the `$-1$` term accounts for the head and merge layer parameters (`$2d^2$` total), which contribute the equivalent of one expert's worth of parameters (`$2d^2$` is the parameter count of one MH-MoE expert at this configuration). So the expert count formula is "four times as many experts as standard, minus one to offset the projection layers." This demonstrates the tradeoff concretely: the projection layers cost the equivalent of one expert in parameters, and the per-expert intermediate dimension reduction from `$4d$` to `$3d$` costs the equivalent of three additional experts to compensate.

---

#### Concrete Model Instantiations

The paper implements two MH-MoE variants in its experiments, both derived from a standard SMoE baseline with `$d = 768$`, `$d_{\text{moe}} = 2048$` (which is `$4d \times 2/3 \approx 2.67d$` due to SwiGLU having a different parameter count than ReLU FFN), `$E_{\text{moe}} = 8$`, top-1 gating, and MoE layers placed every two Transformer layers.

**2-head MH-MoE variant.** For `$h = 2$` heads with top-2 gating (`$k = 2$`):
- Intermediate dimension: the paper explicitly states `$d_{\text{mhmoe}} = 768$`. While this doesn't directly follow from the simplified `$d_{\text{mhmoe}} = d_{\text{moe}} - d/k = 2048 - 768/2 = 1664$` formula (which assumes ReLU FFN), the discrepancy arises because the actual experiments use SwiGLU activation. The paper's stated number of `$768$` is the empirically configured value that achieves FLOPs parity under the SwiGLU FFN structure.
- Number of experts: `$E_{\text{mhmoe}} = 40$`. This is approximately 5× the baseline 8 experts, compensating for both the reduced per-expert intermediate dimension and the head/merge layer parameters.
- Gating: top-2 (`$k = 2$`), meaning each sub-token activates 2 experts, and with 2 heads, each original token can potentially activate up to 4 distinct experts (2 per head).

**3-head MH-MoE variant.** For `$h = 3$` heads with top-3 gating (`$k = 3$`):
- Intermediate dimension: `$d_{\text{mhmoe}} = 512$`.
- Number of experts: `$E_{\text{mhmoe}} = 96$`. This is 12× the baseline 8 experts, reflecting the more aggressive intermediate dimension reduction.
- Gating: top-3 (`$k = 3$`), meaning each sub-token activates 3 experts, and with 3 heads, each original token can potentially activate up to 9 distinct experts.

**Why these configurations.** The paper chooses these specific configurations to explore the tradeoff space. The 2-head variant uses more moderate expert size reduction (768 vs. 2048 intermediate dim) and top-2 gating, while the 3-head variant uses more aggressive reduction (512 intermediate dim) and top-3 gating. Both configurations satisfy the FLOPs and parameter parity constraints derived in Section 2.3 while varying the degree of multi-head specialization. The paper notes that using higher numbers of heads with higher `$k$` values allows the intermediate dimension to remain larger (since the `$d/k$` penalty is smaller for larger `$k$`), which may help preserve per-expert representational capacity even as the number of experts grows.

**Shared expert variants.** Following DeepSeekMoE (Dai et al., 2024), the paper also experiments with adding a shared expert — an additional FFN with intermediate dimension 2048 that processes all tokens regardless of routing — to all MoE models. The shared expert is identical across SMoE, fine-grained SMoE, and both MH-MoE variants, making it an orthogonal addition that tests whether MH-MoE's benefits persist when combined with other MoE improvements.

**Fine-grained SMoE baseline.** For comparison, the paper implements a fine-grained SMoE with `$d_{\text{moe}} = 1024$` (half the standard intermediate dimension) and `$E_{\text{moe}} = 16$` (double the experts), keeping top-1 gating. This tests whether simply using more, smaller experts — without the multi-head mechanism — can achieve similar improvements to MH-MoE.

#### Design Rationale and Ablation Insights

The paper's ablation experiments (Section 3.3) provide critical evidence for *why* MH-MoE works, not just *that* it works. These experiments test three hypotheses about the mechanism of improvement.

**The head layer matters more than the merge layer.** Table 5 shows a systematic ablation of the head and merge layers in the 2-head MH-MoE configuration at 50K steps on RedPajama:

| Configuration | RedPajama PPL |
|---|---|
| No head, no merge (baseline) | 11.97 |
| Head only, no merge | 11.74 |
| No head, merge only | 11.84 |
| Both head and merge | 11.60 |

The head-only configuration improves perplexity by 0.23 over the baseline, while the merge-only configuration improves by only 0.13. The full configuration achieves 0.37 improvement, which is roughly the sum of the individual contributions (0.23 + 0.13 ≈ 0.36), suggesting the head and merge layers contribute approximately additively rather than synergistically.

**Why the head layer matters more:** the head projection learns to redistribute information across the embedding dimensions *before* splitting, determining which information goes to which head. This is directly analogous to the query/key/value projections in multi-head attention, which are known to be critical because they control what each attention head can "see." The merge layer, by contrast, only recombines information that has already been processed — it can mix but not create new specialization. The larger impact of the head layer suggests that the MH-MoE's benefit primarily comes from *better input feature partitioning* before expert processing, not from better output integration.

**Naive addition of projection layers to SMoE does not help.** Table 4 provides a crucial control experiment: adding head and merge layers to standard SMoE *without* the multi-head splitting mechanism. The results at 50K steps on RedPajama:

| Model | w/ head & merge | RedPajama PPL |
|---|---|---|
| SMoE | No | 11.87 |
| SMoE | Yes | 11.84 |
| Fine-grained SMoE | No | 11.68 |
| Fine-grained SMoE | Yes | 11.67 |
| MH-MoE (head=2) | No | 11.71 |
| MH-MoE (head=2) | Yes | 11.46 |

For standard SMoE, adding the projection layers provides a marginal 0.03 perplexity improvement. For fine-grained SMoE, the improvement is 0.01. These are negligible compared to the 0.25 improvement that MH-MoE gains from having the projection layers (11.71 → 11.46). Critically, when MH-MoE is stripped of its head and merge layers (the "MH-MoE w/o head & merge" row, which retains the multi-head splitting and expert structure but without the learned projections), its perplexity of 11.71 is *worse* than the fine-grained SMoE baseline of 11.68.

**What this proves:** the multi-head splitting mechanism alone (without learned projections) is not beneficial — it may even be slightly harmful, likely because splitting the token into fixed-size chunks without learned redistribution disrupts the representational structure. The projection layers alone (without splitting) provide minimal benefit because a single monolithic token processed by multiple experts in sequence doesn't create genuine multi-head specialization. It is the **combination** — learned redistribution via the head projection, splitting into independent sub-tokens, independent expert routing per sub-token, and learned recombination via the merge projection — that yields the improvement. This confirms that MH-MoE's benefit is specifically from enabling different heads to attend to information from different representation spaces within different experts, as the original MH-MoE paper claimed.

**The "heads" dimension enables expert diversity.** The standard SMoE with head/merge layers still processes each token as a single entity through the MoE layer — the head and merge layers are just additional linear transformations before and after the experts, but the experts still see the full token representation. In MH-MoE, each expert sees only a `$d/h$`-dimensional slice of the token, which forces the experts to specialize in different aspects of the representation. Combined with independent routing per head, this means different experts naturally develop complementary specializations — one head's expert might focus on syntactic patterns while another head's expert focuses on semantic content, for example. The ablation evidence supports this interpretation: the quality improvement requires both the projection layers (to learn meaningful splits) and the multi-head structure (to create independent expert processing paths).

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: FLOPs Parity as a First-Class Architectural Constraint, Not an Afterthought

The dominant paradigm in MoE research has been to propose architectural innovations (fine-grained experts, shared experts, novel routing mechanisms) and evaluate them primarily on quality metrics, with computational cost treated as a secondary concern to be reported but not systematically controlled. The original MH-MoE paper (Wu et al., 2024) followed exactly this pattern: it demonstrated perplexity improvements from the multi-head mechanism while achieving parameter parity with standard SMoE, but the ~4× FLOPs overhead (leading term 67Bd² vs. 16Bd²) was documented rather than addressed.

This paper makes a fundamental conceptual move: it **elevates FLOPs parity to a design requirement equal in status to parameter parity**. This reframing matters because it changes what counts as a valid architectural contribution. In the paper's framework, an MoE variant that improves quality but increases per-token FLOPs is not a Pareto improvement — it's a tradeoff that must be justified against the alternative of simply using a larger standard SMoE model. The paper implicitly argues that the correct baseline for any MoE innovation is not "same parameters, whatever FLOPs," but "same parameters AND same FLOPs" — a stricter standard that few prior MoE papers have met.

The significance of this reframing extends beyond MH-MoE. It establishes a template for how future MoE architectural proposals should be evaluated. The paper's "Utilization Guidelines" (Section 2.3, Equations 8–9) are not merely a recipe for MH-MoE — they are a **framework for thinking about architectural modifications as constrained optimization problems**: given a base architecture with known FLOPs, any added component (like projection layers) must be compensated for by reducing computation elsewhere (like shrinking expert intermediate dimensions). This is a conceptual contribution to MoE methodology, not just an implementation detail for MH-MoE.

The paper validates this reframing empirically through its matched-FLOPs comparisons. The fact that MH-MoE with FLOPs parity still outperforms standard SMoE (Table 1: 10.51 vs. 10.90 perplexity on RedPajama at 100K steps) demonstrates that the multi-head mechanism provides genuine efficiency gains — more model quality per FLOP — rather than simply buying quality with extra computation. This transforms MH-MoE from an interesting but expensive architectural novelty into a **strictly preferable alternative** to standard SMoE (at least for the configurations tested), since it dominates on quality at identical computational cost.

### Innovation 2: The Head-and-Split Operation as a Distinct Mechanism from Multi-Head Attention

The paper draws an explicit architectural analogy between MH-MoE's head/merge layers and multi-head attention's Q/K/V and output projections, but its ablation experiments reveal that the **mechanism of benefit is fundamentally different**. This is a diagnostic contribution: the paper doesn't just show that MH-MoE works, it identifies *which part* of the design produces the gains and *why naive attempts to replicate it fail*.

In multi-head attention, the benefit of multiple heads comes from attending to different positional relationships — different heads learn to focus on different parts of the sequence. The projection layers (Q/K/V) are necessary to create the query, key, and value representations, but the heads themselves operate on the same token dimension, merely attending to different context positions.

In MH-MoE, the benefit mechanism is different: it comes from **splitting the token's representational space and routing different subspaces to different experts**. The ablation in Table 4 is the critical evidence. Adding head and merge projections to standard SMoE *without* splitting yields negligible improvement (SMoE: 11.87 → 11.84 perplexity). Adding splitting *without* projections also fails (MH-MoE without head/merge: 11.71, which is worse than the fine-grained SMoE baseline of 11.68). Only the combination — learned redistribution via projections AND independent expert routing per subspace — produces substantial gains (11.71 → 11.46).

This diagnostic result has implications beyond MH-MoE. It demonstrates that **token-splitting for expert routing is a distinct architectural primitive** that cannot be approximated by other mechanisms. Fine-grained MoE (more, smaller experts) achieves some specialization but still processes the full token representation through each expert — it doesn't achieve subspace-level routing. Adding extra linear layers around standard SMoE doesn't help because the experts still see the full token. The head-and-split mechanism creates a fundamentally different information flow where individual experts never see the complete token, forcing complementary specialization across experts in a way that neither more experts nor more linear layers can replicate.

The asymmetry between head and merge layer contributions (Table 5: head-only gives -0.23 perplexity improvement, merge-only gives -0.13) further refines this diagnostic. The head layer, which controls *how the token is partitioned into subspaces*, matters roughly 1.8× more than the merge layer, which controls *how the subspace outputs are recombined*. This suggests that the primary value of MH-MoE is in **better input decomposition** (learning which dimensions should be processed together by the same expert) rather than better output integration. This is a non-obvious finding — one might have expected the merge layer to be equally important since it's the only place where cross-head information mixing occurs — and it provides guidance for future architectural designs: invest more capacity in the pre-expert projection than the post-expert projection.

### Innovation 3: A Principled Scaling Recipe That Generalizes Across Hyperparameters

The paper's derivation of the FLOPs-matching equations (Section 2.3) is more than a convenience for these specific experiments — it is a **formal specification of a hyperparameter transformation** that maps any standard SMoE configuration to an equivalent-cost MH-MoE configuration. This is a conceptual contribution to the MoE design space: it shows that the relationship between number of heads, expert intermediate dimension, number of experts, and top-k gating is not a space to be explored by brute-force hyperparameter search, but a **constrained algebraic system** with a unique solution for any given FLOPs and parameter budget.

The key insight is captured in Equation 8: $d_{\text{mhmoe}} = d_{\text{moe}} - d/k$. This equation reveals an economically intuitive tradeoff: the head and merge layers cost a fixed FLOPs budget (the $4Bd^2$ leading term), and this budget is "paid for" by reducing the per-expert intermediate dimension. The per-expert reduction is $d/k$, which means that using more activated experts per head (higher $k$) allows each expert to remain larger (the $d/k$ penalty shrinks). This creates a design tension that the paper's recipe automatically resolves: for a given number of heads and top-k gating, there is exactly one intermediate dimension that achieves FLOPs parity, and exactly one expert count that achieves parameter parity.

The generality of this recipe distinguishes it from ad hoc architecture modifications in prior MoE work. Fine-grained MoE (e.g., reducing $d_{\text{moe}}$ from 4d to 2d and doubling experts) is a specific point in the design space without a principled relationship to the base configuration. DeepSeekMoE's shared experts are an orthogonal addition that doesn't modify the routed expert dimensions. In contrast, the MH-MoE recipe defines a **transformation function** $f: (\text{SMoE config}) \rightarrow (\text{MH-MoE config})$ that is parameterized by the architectural choices ($h$, $k$) but deterministic given those choices. This means the recipe can be applied to any base SMoE model — at any scale, with any activation function, with any expert placement pattern — without re-deriving the FLOPs matching conditions. The paper demonstrates this portability implicitly by applying the recipe to both standard SMoE and shared-expert SMoE (Tables 1 and 2), and by showing compatibility with BitNet quantization (Table 3), but the underlying claim is stronger: the recipe should work at scales far beyond the 768-dimensional, 12-layer models used in these experiments.

### Innovation 4: The Multi-Head Mechanism Compounds with Orthogonal MoE Improvements

The paper makes a methodological contribution by demonstrating that MH-MoE benefits are **additive with other architectural improvements** rather than overlapping or redundant. This is shown through two separate experiments: the shared expert setting (Table 2) and the BitNet quantization setting (Table 3).

In the shared expert setting, MH-MoE (head=3) achieves 10.28 perplexity on RedPajama at 100K steps versus 10.66 for shared-expert SMoE — a 0.38 improvement. In the non-shared setting (Table 1), the corresponding improvement is 10.51 versus 10.90, a 0.39 improvement. The near-identical delta suggests that the shared expert and multi-head mechanisms provide **independent, non-interacting benefits**. If they were redundant (i.e., both mechanisms improved the same underlying limitation), the improvement from adding MH-MoE to an already-shared-expert model would be smaller than the improvement from adding it to a standard model. The fact that the deltas are essentially identical indicates they address different bottlenecks.

Similarly, in the BitNet setting (Table 3), MH-MoE (head=3) achieves 26.47 on RedPajama at 100K steps versus 26.78 for quantized SMoE — MH-MoE still provides a benefit even under aggressive 1-bit quantization, where one might expect the quantization noise to wash out the finer-grained representational benefits of multi-head processing. The consistency of the MH-MoE advantage across both settings (standard, shared expert, BitNet) suggests the multi-head mechanism provides a **fundamentally different form of model capacity** than what is gained from either expert specialization improvements or quantization — it is not merely a better way to achieve what other techniques already achieve.

This additivity finding has practical significance for practitioners: it means MH-MoE can be adopted **without giving up other MoE advances**. A model builder can take the best known MoE configuration (fine-grained experts, shared experts, load-balanced routing, etc.) and apply the MH-MoE recipe to convert it to a multi-head variant, expecting the MH-MoE improvement to stack on top of the existing improvements. This is not true of all architectural modifications — some techniques saturate the same performance ceiling — and establishing additivity is an important empirical contribution that the field often neglects.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** All experiments use the RedPajama dataset (Together Computer, 2023), described as "an open source recipe to reproduce llama training dataset." The paper does not specify the exact subset or size beyond the computational scale: pre-training runs for 100,000 steps with each batch consisting of 0.5 million tokens, totaling 50 billion tokens of training data. Perplexity is evaluated on three separate validation sets: RedPajama (in-domain), Wiki, and C4 (out-of-domain). The paper does not specify the size of these validation sets.

- **Base model(s).** All experiments use a 12-layer decoder-only Transformer with a model dimension of 768. No model family name is given (e.g., it is not referred to as GPT-2 scale or LLaMA scale), but the 768-dimensional, 12-layer configuration places it in the small-to-medium model regime by contemporary LLM standards. The choice is motivated implicitly by the need to train multiple configurations from scratch for 100K steps within a reasonable compute budget while being large enough to exhibit meaningful expert routing behavior. No pretrained weights are used — all models are trained from random initialization on RedPajama.

- **Metrics.** The primary metric is **validation set perplexity**, reported at two training checkpoints: 50,000 steps and 100,000 steps. Perplexity is the standard language modeling metric: the exponentiated average negative log-likelihood of the validation tokens under the model. Lower perplexity indicates better language modeling performance. The paper reports perplexity on three held-out datasets (RedPajama validation, Wiki, C4) to assess both in-domain and out-of-domain generalization, though it does not specify whether the validation sets are used for early stopping or purely for evaluation.

- **Baselines.** The paper compares against four baselines:
  1. **Dense Transformer**: a standard 12-layer decoder-only Transformer with no MoE layers, using SwiGLU FFNs with intermediate dimension 2048 in every layer (since there are no experts, there is no routing sparsity).
  2. **Sparse MoE (SMoE)**: standard top-1 gating with 8 experts, MoE layers placed every two layers, SwiGLU FFN with intermediate dimension $d_{\text{moe}} = 2048$ per expert. This is the canonical sparse MoE configuration following Shazeer et al. (2017) and subsequent work.
  3. **Fine-grained SMoE**: a variant of SMoE with reduced intermediate dimension $d_{\text{moe}} = 1024$ (half the standard) and doubled experts $E_{\text{moe}} = 16$, maintaining top-1 gating. This tests whether simply using more, smaller experts — without the multi-head mechanism — can match MH-MoE's performance.
  4. **Shared expert variants** (Tables 2 and 3): all MoE models are additionally evaluated with a shared expert (hidden dimension 2048) added, following DeepSeekMoE (Dai et al., 2024). The shared expert processes all tokens regardless of routing, providing a common knowledge baseline.
  
  Among these, the standard SMoE is the primary baseline against which MH-MoE's improvements are measured. The fine-grained SMoE tests whether MH-MoE provides benefits beyond simple expert granularity increases. The dense baseline establishes the lower bound — how much standard SMoE already improves over a dense model of comparable per-token FLOPs.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** All models are matched in terms of both **parameters** and **computation** (scalar multiplications / FLOPs). The paper uses scalar multiplication count as the unit of computational cost, derived from the standard matrix multiplication FLOPs formula (multiplying an $m \times n$ matrix by an $n \times p$ matrix requires $2mnp - mp$ scalar multiplications). The matching is achieved through the analytical recipe in Section 2.3 (Equations 8 and 9), which reduces the per-expert intermediate dimension $d_{\text{mhmoe}}$ and increases the number of experts $E_{\text{mhmoe}}$ to compensate for the added head and merge projection layers. All models are trained with identical batch size (0.5M tokens), number of steps (100K), and optimizer settings, ensuring a fair comparison of per-step and total training cost.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not employ cross-validation or report confidence intervals. Validation perplexity is reported at two fixed checkpoints (50K and 100K steps) rather than selecting the best checkpoint per model via held-out validation performance. This is a weakness — different architectures may converge at different rates, and reporting only fixed-step perplexities could advantage models that converge faster rather than those with better asymptotic performance. No statistical significance tests are reported; the consistency of MH-MoE's advantage across three validation sets and two training horizons provides informal robustness evidence but no formal statistical guarantees.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Standard Language Modeling (No Shared Expert)

Table 1 reports the primary results comparing all architectures without a shared expert. The headline findings, organized by training horizon and model configuration:

**At 50,000 steps**, MH-MoE consistently outperforms all baselines across all three validation sets:
- On RedPajama (in-domain): Dense achieves 13.01, SMoE improves to 11.87, fine-grained SMoE further improves to 11.68, and MH-MoE achieves the best results: 11.60 (head=2) and 11.45 (head=3). The three-head MH-MoE provides a 0.42 perplexity improvement over standard SMoE and a 0.23 improvement over fine-grained SMoE.
- On Wiki: Dense 12.95, SMoE 10.51, fine-grained SMoE 10.18, MH-MoE (head=2) 10.11, MH-MoE (head=3) 10.00. The three-head variant improves over standard SMoE by 0.51 perplexity.
- On C4: Dense 17.41, SMoE 15.63, fine-grained SMoE 15.21, MH-MoE (head=2) 15.11, MH-MoE (head=3) 14.90. The three-head variant improves over standard SMoE by 0.73 perplexity.

**At 100,000 steps**, the performance ordering remains stable and the gaps persist:
- On RedPajama: Dense 12.13, SMoE 10.90, fine-grained SMoE 10.74, MH-MoE (head=2) 10.70, MH-MoE (head=3) 10.51. The three-head MH-MoE achieves a 0.39 improvement over standard SMoE and 0.23 over fine-grained SMoE.
- On Wiki: Dense 11.58, SMoE 9.68, fine-grained SMoE 9.38, MH-MoE (head=2) 9.26, MH-MoE (head=3) 9.18. Improvement of 0.50 over standard SMoE.
- On C4: Dense 16.21, SMoE 14.35, fine-grained SMoE 13.97, MH-MoE (head=2) 13.80, MH-MoE (head=3) 13.63. Improvement of 0.72 over standard SMoE.

**Key observations from Table 1:**

The absolute perplexity improvements from MH-MoE (head=3) over standard SMoE range from 0.39 (RedPajama at 100K) to 0.73 (C4 at 50K), representing roughly 3.6% to 4.8% relative improvement. These are meaningful but not dramatic gains — they suggest the multi-head mechanism provides a consistent, moderate benefit rather than a breakthrough.

The three-head configuration consistently outperforms the two-head configuration across all datasets and both training horizons, with the gap being typically 0.10–0.20 perplexity points (e.g., RedPajama at 100K: 10.70 vs. 10.51). This suggests that more heads are beneficial, at least up to $h = 3$, though the paper does not explore $h = 4$ or higher values to determine where diminishing returns set in.

The fine-grained SMoE baseline provides a critical comparison: it already improves over standard SMoE (e.g., RedPajama at 100K: 10.74 vs. 10.90), but MH-MoE further improves over fine-grained SMoE (10.51 vs. 10.74). This means the multi-head mechanism provides benefits **beyond simply using more, smaller experts** — the head-and-split operation creates a form of specialization that expert granularity alone does not capture.

The improvements on out-of-domain validation sets (Wiki, C4) are consistently larger in absolute terms than on the in-domain RedPajama set. For the three-head variant at 100K steps: +0.39 on RedPajama, +0.50 on Wiki, +0.72 on C4. This pattern is not explicitly discussed by the authors, but it suggests that MH-MoE's multi-head mechanism may provide better generalization — possibly because processing tokens through multiple representation subspaces creates more robust features that transfer better to out-of-distribution text.

#### Language Modeling with Shared Experts

Table 2 reports results when all MoE models incorporate a shared expert (hidden dimension 2048) following DeepSeekMoE. The shared expert processes all tokens, with routed experts providing additional specialized computation on top of this shared foundation.

**At 50,000 steps:**
- On RedPajama: SMoE 11.76, fine-grained SMoE 11.51, MH-MoE (head=2) 11.48, MH-MoE (head=3) 11.26. The three-head MH-MoE improves over shared SMoE by 0.50 perplexity.
- On Wiki: SMoE 10.33, fine-grained SMoE 10.06, MH-MoE (head=2) 9.91, MH-MoE (head=3) 9.74. Improvement of 0.59.
- On C4: SMoE 15.19, fine-grained SMoE 15.01, MH-MoE (head=2) 14.87, MH-MoE (head=3) 14.82. Improvement of 0.37.

**At 100,000 steps:**
- On RedPajama: SMoE 10.66, fine-grained SMoE 10.41, MH-MoE (head=2) 10.36, MH-MoE (head=3) 10.28. Improvement of 0.38 over shared SMoE.
- On Wiki: SMoE 9.44, fine-grained SMoE 9.15, MH-MoE (head=2) 8.79, MH-MoE (head=3) 8.72. Improvement of 0.72.
- On C4: SMoE 14.30, fine-grained SMoE 13.78, MH-MoE (head=2) 13.66, MH-MoE (head=3) 13.49. Improvement of 0.81.

**Key observations from Table 2:**

Adding a shared expert improves all models compared to their non-shared counterparts. For standard SMoE at 100K on RedPajama: 10.90 → 10.66 (0.24 improvement). For MH-MoE (head=3): 10.51 → 10.28 (0.23 improvement). The improvement from adding a shared expert is nearly identical across architectures, providing evidence that the shared expert and multi-head mechanisms provide **independent, additive benefits**. If they addressed the same underlying limitation, we would expect diminishing returns — the shared expert would provide less benefit when added to an already-improved MH-MoE model. The near-constant delta (~0.23–0.24) suggests they improve different aspects of model capacity.

The MH-MoE advantage over standard SMoE is preserved under the shared expert setting. Comparing MH-MoE (head=3) to SMoE with shared experts: improvements of 0.38 (RedPajama), 0.72 (Wiki), 0.81 (C4) at 100K steps. These are comparable to the non-shared improvements (0.39, 0.50, 0.72 respectively), further supporting the additivity hypothesis.

The two-head vs. three-head gap narrows slightly in the shared expert setting: on RedPajama at 100K, the gap is 0.08 (10.36 vs. 10.28) compared to 0.19 in the non-shared setting (10.70 vs. 10.51). This might suggest that the shared expert partially compensates for having fewer heads, though the paper does not analyze this interaction.

#### 1-Bit MH-MoE (BitNet Compatibility)

Table 3 reports results when all models are quantized and trained using BitNet (Ma et al., 2024), a 1-bit quantization scheme that represents weights using ternary values {-1, 0, +1}. All models in this setting use the same architecture and training recipe as in Sections 3.1, except with BitNet quantization applied throughout.

**At 50,000 steps:**
- On RedPajama: Dense 32.17, SMoE 29.18, fine-grained SMoE 29.04, MH-MoE (head=2) 28.84, MH-MoE (head=3) 28.77. The three-head MH-MoE improves over SMoE by 0.41 perplexity.
- On Wiki: Dense 27.56, SMoE 24.70, fine-grained SMoE 24.51, MH-MoE (head=2) 24.27, MH-MoE (head=3) 24.13. Improvement of 0.57.
- On C4: Dense 35.85, SMoE 32.34, fine-grained SMoE 32.03, MH-MoE (head=2) 31.86, MH-MoE (head=3) 31.81. Improvement of 0.53.

**At 100,000 steps:**
- On RedPajama: Dense 30.04, SMoE 26.78, fine-grained SMoE 26.68, MH-MoE (head=2) 26.59, MH-MoE (head=3) 26.47. Improvement of 0.31 over SMoE.
- On Wiki: Dense 24.75, SMoE 21.54, fine-grained SMoE 21.42, MH-MoE (head=2) 21.11, MH-MoE (head=3) 21.06. Improvement of 0.48.
- On C4: Dense 33.55, SMoE 29.73, fine-grained SMoE 29.50, MH-MoE (head=2) 29.27, MH-MoE (head=3) 29.14. Improvement of 0.59.

**Key observations from Table 3:**

MH-MoE maintains its advantage over SMoE and fine-grained SMoE even under extreme 1-bit quantization. The absolute improvements are comparable to the non-quantized setting: for RedPajama at 100K, the MH-MoE (head=3) improvement over SMoE is 0.31 in the BitNet setting vs. 0.39 in the standard setting — a small reduction but still clearly positive.

All models show substantially worse perplexity under BitNet quantization compared to their full-precision counterparts. For SMoE on RedPajama at 100K: 26.78 (BitNet) vs. 10.90 (full precision) — a degradation of ~15.9 perplexity points. The authors acknowledge this, attributing it to the small model scale: "when the model size is relatively small, BitNet tends to degrade performance, a finding that aligns with the conclusions reported in the original BitNet paper." This is a known characteristic of extreme quantization — the information loss from 1-bit weights is more damaging for smaller models where redundancy is lower.

The performance ordering (MH-MoE > fine-grained SMoE > SMoE > Dense) is preserved under quantization, suggesting the architectural benefits are robust to the representation precision. The three-head MH-MoE under BitNet even slightly outperforms the fine-grained SMoE under BitNet, with gaps of 0.21 (RedPajama at 100K), 0.36 (Wiki), and 0.36 (C4).

The two-head vs. three-head gap narrows considerably in the BitNet setting. On RedPajama at 100K: 26.59 vs. 26.47 (gap of 0.12) compared to 10.70 vs. 10.51 (gap of 0.19) in full precision. This might indicate that the finer-grained representational benefits of additional heads are partially washed out by quantization noise, though with only two head configurations tested, this interpretation is tentative.

#### Training Dynamics

Although the paper does not plot training curves, the two-checkpoint reporting (50K and 100K steps) provides limited training dynamics information. Across all configurations in Tables 1-3, the relative ordering of models is stable between 50K and 100K steps — no model that is worse at 50K overtakes a better model at 100K. This suggests that the benefit of MH-MoE is not merely faster convergence but a genuine improvement in the model's representational capacity that persists through training.

The absolute perplexity improvements from 50K to 100K steps (i.e., the training progress in the second half) show that all models continue to improve meaningfully:
- SMoE on RedPajama: 11.87 → 10.90 (0.97 improvement)
- MH-MoE (head=3) on RedPajama: 11.45 → 10.51 (0.94 improvement)

The similar rate of improvement suggests that MH-MoE does not change the fundamental training dynamics — it provides a roughly constant offset in model quality throughout training, rather than accelerating or decelerating learning.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Head and merge layers added to standard SMoE without multi-head splitting (Table 4):** Adding the head and merge projection layers to standard SMoE and fine-grained SMoE *without* the multi-head token-splitting mechanism yields negligible improvements. On RedPajama at 50K steps: SMoE goes from 11.87 (without) to 11.84 (with) — a 0.03 improvement. Fine-grained SMoE goes from 11.68 to 11.67 — a 0.01 improvement. In contrast, MH-MoE (head=2) gains substantially from its head and merge layers: 11.71 (MH-MoE without head/merge) to 11.46 (with) — a 0.25 improvement. This demonstrates that the projection layers alone, without the multi-head splitting that creates independent expert routing per subspace, provide essentially no benefit. The benefit of MH-MoE specifically requires the combination of learned redistribution (projection layers) and independent per-subspace expert processing (splitting).

Also notable: the "MH-MoE (head=2) without head & merge" row achieves 11.71 perplexity, which is actually *worse* than the fine-grained SMoE baseline of 11.68 at the same step. This means the raw token-splitting operation, without learned projections to determine *how* to split, is slightly harmful — it disrupts the representational structure without providing compensating benefits. Only when the head projection learns to redistribute information optimally across subspaces does splitting become beneficial.

**Relative importance of head vs. merge layers (Table 5):** A granular ablation of the two projection layers in the MH-MoE (head=2) configuration, evaluated on RedPajama at 50K steps with all models having matched FLOPs:
- Neither head nor merge: 11.97 (baseline — note this differs from the 11.71 in Table 4 because the expert/intermediate dimension configurations may differ to maintain FLOPs parity across all four conditions)
- Head only, no merge: 11.74 (improvement of 0.23)
- Merge only, no head: 11.84 (improvement of 0.13)
- Both head and merge: 11.60 (improvement of 0.37)

The head layer alone provides roughly 1.8× the benefit of the merge layer alone (0.23 vs. 0.13). The combined benefit (0.37) is approximately additive (0.23 + 0.13 = 0.36), suggesting the two layers contribute through complementary rather than synergistic mechanisms. The head layer's larger impact implies that the primary value of MH-MoE comes from **learning how to partition the token representation into subspaces** before expert processing, rather than from learning how to recombine subspace outputs afterward.

**Note on baseline discrepancy:** The "neither" baseline in Table 5 (11.97) differs from the "MH-MoE without head & merge" baseline in Table 4 (11.71). The authors note that "all models without the head and merge layers maintain the same number of scalar multiplications, and similarly, all models with the head and merge layers also maintain an equivalent number of scalar multiplications." This means the four configurations in Table 5 are all FLOPs-matched *within the table* but may use different expert configurations than in Table 4 to achieve this matching under the four different head/merge combinations. The specific hyperparameter adjustments are not provided, making it difficult to fully interpret the exact 11.97 vs. 11.71 discrepancy.

**Three datasets for robustness:** All results are reported on three validation sets (RedPajama, Wiki, C4), providing a basic cross-domain robustness check. The MH-MoE advantage is consistent across all three, though the absolute magnitude varies (larger on C4, smaller on RedPajama). This consistency across in-domain and out-of-domain text suggests the benefit is not dataset-specific.

**Two training horizons for convergence robustness:** Results at both 50K and 100K steps show that the relative ordering of models is stable — MH-MoE is not merely faster-converging but achieves better final performance. However, without training curves or multiple seeds, it is impossible to determine whether the 0.1–0.2 perplexity gaps between configurations are statistically reliable or within the noise of training stochasticity.

### Critical Assessment

#### Does MH-MoE Actually Outperform SMoE at Matched Compute?

The paper's central empirical claim is that MH-MoE, when configured for FLOPs and parameter parity, achieves lower perplexity than standard SMoE. The evidence in Tables 1-3 supports this claim **for the specific model scale and configuration tested** (d=768, 12 layers, trained for 100K steps on 50B tokens). The improvements are consistent across datasets, training horizons, shared-expert settings, and quantization settings.

However, several factors limit the strength and generality of this evidence:

**1. Single scale, small model.** All experiments use a single model configuration: 12 layers, d=768, which is a small model by contemporary LLM standards. The total parameter counts are not reported, but a rough estimate — 12 layers × (4 × 768² for attention + 2 × 768 × 2048 for FFN) ≈ 85M parameters for the dense model, with MoE variants having comparable activated parameters but more total parameters — places these in the sub-100M parameter range. Contemporary MoE models operate at the 1B–100B+ parameter scale. The paper provides no evidence that the 0.3–0.7 perplexity advantages observed at this small scale translate to larger models, where different training dynamics (loss scaling laws, expert specialization patterns, overfitting behavior) may apply. The MH-MoE improvements are small enough in absolute terms (3–5% relative) that they could easily be noise at larger scales or could be swamped by other effects not present at this scale.

**2. No statistical uncertainty reported.** No standard deviations, confidence intervals, or multiple training seeds are reported. All numbers in Tables 1-5 are single-run point estimates. For a paper whose primary contribution is demonstrating a perplexity improvement of ~0.2–0.7 points, the absence of any uncertainty quantification is a significant weakness. It is impossible to know whether the 0.19 gap between MH-MoE (head=3) and MH-MoE (head=2) on RedPajama at 100K (Table 1: 10.51 vs. 10.70) is a real effect or training noise. Language model training is known to have non-trivial run-to-run variance, especially at smaller scales, and without multiple seeds, the reported ranking could be partially an artifact of lucky initialization.

**3. The FLOPs matching is approximate, not exact.** The paper's FLOPs matching derivation (Section 2.3) works with leading terms only. For standard SMoE with top-1 gating and $d_{\text{moe}} = 4d$, the leading term is $16Bd^2$. The full expression is $(16Bd^2 - 5Bd)$ for the standard SMoE expert FLOPs (from Equation 6 with appropriate substitutions). The MH-MoE full expression from Equation 6 is $(4Bd^2 - 2Bd)$ for head/merge layers plus $(4Bdd_{\text{mhmoe}} - Bd - Bd_{\text{mhmoe}}h) \cdot k$ for experts. When the leading terms are matched, the lower-order terms (involving $-Bd$, $-Bd_{\text{mhmoe}}h$, etc.) may not match, producing small FLOPs discrepancies. The paper does not analyze whether these lower-order mismatches systematically advantage or disadvantage either architecture. For the model size tested (d=768), the $-Bd$ terms are small relative to the leading terms ($-Bd$ vs. $16Bd^2$ with $d=768$ is a factor of ~12,000× smaller), making this concern primarily academic, but for completeness the paper should acknowledge that the matching is approximate.

**4. The hyperparameter configurations for MH-MoE appear empirically chosen, not analytically derived from the recipe.** The paper's Section 2.3 derives a clean recipe: $d_{\text{mhmoe}} = d_{\text{moe}} - d/k$. For the standard SMoE baseline with $d_{\text{moe}} = 2048$, $d = 768$, and top-1 gating ($k = 1$), this formula gives $d_{\text{mhmoe}} = 2048 - 768/1 = 1280$. Yet the actual MH-MoE configurations in Section 3 use $d_{\text{mhmoe}} = 768$ (head=2) and $d_{\text{mhmoe}} = 512$ (head=3), which are substantially smaller than 1280. The paper acknowledges this implicitly by noting that the baseline uses SwiGLU rather than ReLU FFN, which changes the parameter count computation, but it does not provide the SwiGLU-adjusted FLOPs matching derivation or justify the specific chosen values of 768 and 512. The expert counts (40 and 96) also do not obviously follow from Equation 9 with the stated intermediate dimensions. The reader is left unsure whether the reported configurations truly achieve FLOPs parity with the baseline or whether they represent empirically chosen points that the paper retrofits to the analytical framework. A table showing the computed FLOPs for each configuration (using the exact implementation, not the simplified analytical model) would resolve this ambiguity.

**5. The dense model baseline is very weak.** In Table 1 at 100K steps on RedPajama, the dense model achieves 12.13 perplexity while standard SMoE achieves 10.90 — a 1.23 improvement (10.1% relative). This is a very large gap that dwarfs the SMoE-to-MH-MoE improvement of 0.39 (3.6%). The dense baseline demonstrates that MoE provides a large benefit at this scale, but it does not help distinguish whether MH-MoE's incremental improvement over SMoE is practically meaningful. A more informative baseline would be a standard SMoE with increased total parameters (more layers, wider dimension) at matched total FLOPs — this would test whether MH-MoE's multi-head mechanism is more efficient than simply making the standard SMoE larger.

**6. No comparison to the original (non-FLOPs-matched) MH-MoE.** The paper positions itself as improving on Wu et al. (2024) by providing a FLOPs-efficient implementation, but it never directly compares its FLOPs-matched MH-MoE against the original (higher-FLOPs) MH-MoE from that work. Such a comparison would quantify the quality cost of the FLOPs reduction. If the original MH-MoE at 4× the FLOPs achieved, say, 10.30 perplexity versus 10.51 for the FLOPs-matched version, the tradeoff would be clear. Without this comparison, the reader cannot assess whether the recipe achieves a good quality-per-FLOPs Pareto point or whether it sacrifices too much quality to reach FLOPs parity.

**7. The training data (RedPajama) is a reproduction of LLaMA's training data, but only 50B tokens are used.** The paper pre-trains for 100K steps at 0.5M tokens per batch, totaling 50B tokens. For a model with d=768 (~85M parameters for the dense version), this is roughly 588 tokens per parameter — far below the Chinchilla-optimal ratio of ~20 tokens per parameter, meaning the models are significantly undertrained by contemporary standards. The paper does not discuss whether the relative performance of different architectures might change with more training data. It is possible that MH-MoE provides a larger benefit in the undertrained regime (because the multi-head mechanism helps with data efficiency) or that the gap narrows with more training (because standard SMoE eventually catches up given enough data).

**8. The BitNet results are confounded with model size.** The paper reports that BitNet degrades performance at this model scale, which aligns with the original BitNet paper's findings. This means the BitNet experiments in Table 3 are testing two things simultaneously: MH-MoE's compatibility with quantization AND whether architectural improvements can partially compensate for quantization-induced degradation. The 0.31 perplexity improvement of MH-MoE (head=3) over SMoE in the BitNet setting (RedPajama at 100K: 26.47 vs. 26.78) is proportionally similar to the 0.39 improvement in the full-precision setting (10.51 vs. 10.90), suggesting MH-MoE provides roughly the same *relative* benefit regardless of quantization. But the absolute perplexity gap is smaller (0.31 vs. 0.39), which could indicate that quantization noise partially masks the multi-head benefit or simply that perplexity differences compress at higher absolute values.

#### Do the Ablations Support the Claim That the Head/Split Mechanism Is the Key Innovation?

The ablation experiments in Tables 4 and 5 are the strongest part of the paper's empirical contribution, but they have important limitations:

**The "head and merge layers added to SMoE" ablation (Table 4) is genuinely informative.** It cleanly demonstrates that the projection layers alone (without token splitting) provide negligible benefit to standard SMoE. This rules out the hypothesis that MH-MoE's gains come simply from having additional learned linear transformations in the MoE layer. The combination of projection + splitting is necessary.

**The head vs. merge ablation (Table 5) provides useful directional insight** — the head layer matters more than the merge layer. However, the absolute numbers should be interpreted cautiously given the single-run nature of the experiments. A difference of 0.10 perplexity (11.74 vs. 11.84) could easily be noise. Multiple seeds would be needed to confidently claim the head layer is more important.

**A missing ablation: varying the number of heads at fixed total parameters and FLOPs.** The paper tests $h=2$ and $h=3$, but does not test $h=1$ (which would effectively reduce MH-MoE to standard SMoE with extra projection layers — the Table 4 "SMoE w/ head & merge" configuration) or $h=4$ (the configuration used in the original MH-MoE paper). A sweep over $h \in \{1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8\}$ at matched FLOPs would reveal:
- Whether the benefit increases monotonically with number of heads or saturates
- Whether the optimal number of heads depends on model scale
- Whether the $h=4$ configuration from Wu et al. (2024), when corrected for FLOPs parity, outperforms or underperforms the $h=2$ and $h=3$ configurations

**A missing ablation: comparing the head-and-split mechanism to alternative multi-expert-per-token mechanisms.** The paper compares MH-MoE to top-1 gating SMoE and fine-grained SMoE, but not to top-2 or top-3 gating SMoE at matched parameters and FLOPs. Standard SMoE with top-2 gating allows each token to be processed by two experts, which provides a form of multi-expert processing without token splitting. A comparison between MH-MoE (head=2, top-2 gating per head) and standard SMoE (top-2 gating) at matched FLOPs would isolate whether the benefit comes from the multi-head splitting mechanism specifically or from simply allowing each token to leverage multiple experts. The paper's current baselines (all top-1 gating SMoE) leave this ambiguity unresolved.

**A missing ablation: head dimension vs. number of heads.** In the paper's formulation, $h$ heads means each head processes a $d/h$-dimensional subspace. The paper does not test whether the benefit of more heads comes from having more independent routing decisions (more heads) or from each expert operating on a smaller subspace (lower $d/h$). These are confounded in the current experiments. Varying $h$ while keeping expert input dimension constant (e.g., by projecting to a different dimension before splitting) could disentangle these effects.

#### Summary: What the Experiments Do and Do Not Demonstrate

**What they demonstrate convincingly:**
- At a small scale (d=768, 12 layers) trained on 50B tokens, the FLOPs-matched MH-MoE implementation achieves better perplexity than standard SMoE and fine-grained SMoE across three validation sets.
- The benefit persists when combined with shared experts and 1-bit quantization.
- The benefit requires both the token-splitting mechanism and the projection layers — neither alone is sufficient.

**What they do not demonstrate:**
- That the benefit scales to larger models (1B+ parameters) or longer training (Chinchilla-optimal token budgets).
- That the benefit is statistically significant (no error bars, no multiple seeds).
- That the benefit exceeds what could be achieved by other architectural modifications (e.g., more experts with top-2 gating instead of top-1) at the same FLOPs budget.
- That the FLOPs matching is exact rather than approximate (lower-order terms not accounted for).
- That the three-head configuration is superior to two-head with statistical confidence.
- That MH-MoE outperforms the original non-FLOPs-matched MH-MoE when both are evaluated at their respective FLOPs budgets.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### 6.1 Single-Scale Validation with No Evidence of Scaling Behavior

**The assumption or constraint.** All experiments in the paper use a single model configuration: a 12-layer decoder-only Transformer with model dimension `d = 768`, trained on 50B tokens from the RedPajama dataset. The paper does not specify the total parameter count of any model, but a rough estimate places the dense baseline at approximately 85M parameters, with MoE variants having comparable activated parameters but larger total parameter counts. The paper makes no attempt to validate whether the observed perplexity improvements persist at larger scales.

The authors present their FLOPs-matching recipe (Equations 8 and 9, Section 2.3) as a general transformation applicable to "any standard SMoE configuration," and cite large-scale MoE successes like Mixtral 8×7B in the introduction to motivate the importance of MoE architectures. However, they provide no empirical evidence that the recipe scales.

**The consequence.** The behavior of architectural modifications at small scale does not reliably predict behavior at large scale, a phenomenon well-documented in the scaling laws literature. Several specific failure modes are possible at scale that the paper's experiments cannot detect:

The relative contribution of the head and merge layers (`4Bd²` FLOPs, constant relative to the expert FLOPs) may become negligible at large model dimensions. If the expert FLOPs grow with model size while the head/merge FLOPs remain proportional to `d²`, the multi-head mechanism might provide diminishing returns — the quality benefit of token splitting may not compound with scale the way the paper's small-scale results suggest it should.

Expert specialization patterns may differ at scale. At `d = 768` with 40–96 experts, each expert processes a very low-dimensional subspace (e.g., `d/h = 384` for 2-head or `256` for 3-head). At `d = 4096` or `d = 8192` (representative of production MoE models), these subspaces would be proportionally larger (`2048` or `2731` for 3-head at `d = 8192`), and the nature of what constitutes a "different representation space" may change qualitatively. The paper's finding that more heads help (3 > 2) might reverse or saturate at larger scales.

Training dynamics at 50B tokens (roughly 588 tokens per parameter for the dense model) represent a severely undertrained regime by Chinchilla-optimal standards (~20 tokens per parameter). Architectural innovations that help in the undertrained regime (e.g., by improving data efficiency through better inductive biases) may provide smaller benefits at Chinchilla-optimal training durations where all architectures approach their irreducible loss.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides no scaling evidence whatsoever. There are no experiments varying model depth, model width, training tokens, or total parameter count. The three-head advantage over two-head (e.g., 10.51 vs. 10.70 perplexity on RedPajama at 100K steps, Table 1) is the only within-paper indication of how the mechanism responds to increased capacity, and this is a single data point with a gap small enough (0.19 perplexity) to potentially be training noise in a single-run experiment.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not acknowledge the single-scale limitation, does not discuss whether the FLOPs-matching recipe is expected to produce proportional benefits at larger scales, and does not suggest scaling experiments as future work. The introduction's reference to large-scale MoE models (Mixtral 8×7B) implicitly claims relevance to those scales, but the experimental validation provides no support for this claim. A practitioner considering MH-MoE for a production-scale model (1B+ parameters) must extrapolate from a ~85M parameter experiment with no guidance on whether the benefits scale, saturate, or reverse.

### 6.2 No Statistical Validation or Multiple Training Runs

**The assumption or constraint.** Every number in every table (Tables 1–5) is a single-run point estimate. The paper reports no standard deviations, no confidence intervals, and no indication that any experiment was repeated with different random seeds. Language model training has well-documented run-to-run variance from random initialization, data ordering, and dropout stochasticity, particularly at small model scales where individual training runs can differ by several tenths of a perplexity point.

**The consequence.** The paper's headline findings rest on perplexity differences as small as 0.08 (MH-MoE head=3 vs. head=2 with shared experts on RedPajama at 100K steps, Table 2: 10.28 vs. 10.36) and 0.03 (SMoE with head/merge vs. without, Table 4: 11.84 vs. 11.87). Without any measure of statistical variability, it is impossible to determine which of these differences represent genuine architectural improvements and which are within the expected noise of a single training run.

This is particularly acute for the ablation experiments in Section 3.3, which the paper uses to make specific mechanistic claims:
- The claim that "the head layer provides a more substantial gain compared to the merge layer" (Table 5: head-only 11.74 vs. merge-only 11.84, a gap of 0.10) could be an artifact of seed variance.
- The claim that MH-MoE without head/merge layers (11.71) underperforms fine-grained SMoE (11.68) in Table 4 — a gap of 0.03 — could easily reverse in a different run.
- The claim that three-head MH-MoE consistently outperforms two-head (gaps of 0.10–0.19 across Tables 1–3) cannot be distinguished from run-to-run noise.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. The consistency of rankings across three validation datasets (RedPajama, Wiki, C4) provides informal but weak robustness evidence — since the datasets share the same training seed, correlated errors across datasets are possible. The consistency across two training horizons (50K and 100K steps) similarly provides informal evidence that the benefits are not transient, but does not address seed variance.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper neither acknowledges the single-run limitation nor suggests that future work should validate with multiple seeds. Standard practice in language modeling research is to report either multiple seeds (3–5 runs) or to note that observed differences exceed known training variance. This paper does neither. A practitioner cannot determine, from the reported evidence, whether the MH-MoE advantage over fine-grained SMoE is a reliable finding or a lucky initialization.

### 6.3 The FLOPs-Matching Recipe Is Approximate and the Actual Configurations Are Not Transparently Derived

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's FLOPs-matching derivation (Section 2.3) operates on leading terms of the scalar multiplication counts and uses a simplified ReLU FFN model. The actual experiments use SwiGLU activations (Shazeer, 2020), which have a different parameter and FLOPs profile — SwiGLU uses three weight matrices (two up-projections and a gating mechanism) rather than two, with the intermediate dimension conventionally set to `2/3 × 4d` to maintain comparable parameter counts. The paper acknowledges this discrepancy implicitly when listing the baseline configuration: the standard SMoE uses `d_moe = 2048` rather than `4d = 3072` or the SwiGLU-adjusted `8d/3 ≈ 2048`.

The paper then reports MH-MoE configurations with intermediate dimensions of 768 (head=2) and 512 (head=3). These values do not follow from the derived formula `d_mhmoe = d_moe − d/k`. For `d = 768`, `d_moe = 2048`, and `k = 1` (top-1 gating), the formula gives `d_mhmoe = 2048 − 768 = 1280` — substantially larger than either 768 or 512. The expert counts (40 for head=2, 96 for head=3) similarly do not follow transparently from Equation 9. The paper does not provide a SwiGLU-adjusted derivation, does not show the actual FLOPs calculations for the chosen configurations, and does not explain how the specific hyperparameters were selected.

**The consequence.** A practitioner attempting to apply the MH-MoE recipe to their own model cannot confidently compute the correct intermediate dimension and expert count. The derived equations (8 and 9) are presented as general, but the actual experiments use hyperparameters that do not match the derivation. This creates an implementation barrier: either the practitioner trusts the formula and arrives at different configurations than the paper tested, or the practitioner must empirically search for FLOPs-matched configurations, which defeats the purpose of having a principled recipe.

Furthermore, without an exact FLOPs accounting (including lower-order terms and correct SwiGLU arithmetic), the claimed FLOPs parity cannot be verified. The paper states the goal is to ensure "the leading term is on par with sparse MoE" (Section 2.2), but leading-term matching can hide non-trivial discrepancies in lower-order terms that accumulate across layers and training steps. For a production training run consuming millions of GPU-hours, even a 2–3% FLOPs discrepancy between the reported MH-MoE configuration and the baseline SMoE would mean the comparison is not truly compute-matched, and the reported perplexity improvements may partially reflect additional compute rather than architectural efficiency.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The complexity analysis in Section 2.2 derives scalar multiplication counts for a ReLU FFN and identifies leading-term matching conditions. Section 3 lists the actual experimental configurations but does not show the FLOPs calculation that verifies these configurations satisfy the matching conditions under SwiGLU. Lower-order terms (the `−Bd`, `−Bd_mhmoe h`, etc. in Equation 6) are not accounted for. The paper does not contain a table comparing the actual computed FLOPs (not just leading terms) of each configuration.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The discrepancy between the derived formula and the actual configurations is never acknowledged or explained. The paper does not provide the SwiGLU-adjusted derivation, does not report the exact FLOPs of each model configuration, and does not discuss the sensitivity of results to small FLOPs mismatches. A practitioner must either reverse-engineer the SwiGLU arithmetic or accept on faith that the reported configurations achieve parity.

### 6.4 No Comparison to Top-K SMoE Baselines at Matched FLOPs

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's standard SMoE baselines all use top-1 gating (`k = 1`): each token is routed to exactly one expert per MoE layer. The MH-MoE configurations use multi-head splitting combined with higher top-k per head: the 2-head variant uses top-2 gating per head (so each token potentially activates up to 4 experts — 2 per head), and the 3-head variant uses top-3 gating per head (potentially up to 9 experts per token).

This means the paper is comparing architectures that differ along two axes simultaneously: (1) the multi-head splitting mechanism and (2) the number of activated experts per token. A standard SMoE with top-2 or top-4 gating (at appropriately adjusted intermediate dimensions and expert counts for FLOPs parity) would also allow each token to benefit from multiple experts. The paper provides no such baselines.

**The consequence.** The experimental design cannot distinguish whether MH-MoE's improvement comes from the multi-head splitting mechanism specifically or from the simpler fact that each token is processed by more experts. This is a critical confound because allowing top-k > 1 gating in standard SMoE is a well-known technique for improving MoE performance — it increases the model's capacity per token by letting multiple experts contribute, at the cost of additional FLOPs. The paper's FLOPs-matching recipe could potentially be applied to produce a top-2 or top-4 SMoE baseline: reduce the per-expert intermediate dimension to compensate for the increased number of activated experts, and add more experts to maintain total parameters. Such a baseline would test whether the head-and-split mechanism provides benefits beyond simple multi-expert aggregation.

The ablation in Table 4 partially addresses this concern by showing that adding head and merge layers to top-1 SMoE without splitting provides negligible benefit — but this only shows that projection layers alone don't help top-1 SMoE. It does not test whether a top-4 SMoE with reduced per-expert dimensions (and matched total FLOPs) would perform comparably to MH-MoE. If it did, the multi-head mechanism would be revealed as an unnecessarily complex way to achieve multi-expert processing per token, and a simpler top-k gating approach would be preferable.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None directly. The paper provides fine-grained SMoE (more experts, smaller per-expert dimension, but still top-1 gating) as an alternative baseline, but fine-grained SMoE still only routes each token to a single expert. The performance ordering (MH-MoE > fine-grained SMoE > SMoE) suggests that multi-expert processing per token is beneficial, but cannot distinguish multi-head splitting from top-k gating as the mechanism.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not acknowledge the confounding of multi-head splitting with multi-expert activation, does not discuss top-k gating as an alternative, and does not provide or suggest top-k SMoE baselines. The "Utilization Guidelines" (Section 2.3) derive the recipe only for converting standard SMoE to MH-MoE, not for producing matched-FLOPs top-k SMoE variants. A practitioner considering whether to adopt MH-MoE versus simply increasing k in their existing SMoE architecture receives no guidance.

### 6.5 Latency and Implementation Complexity Tradeoffs Are Not Analyzed

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper measures computational cost exclusively in terms of scalar multiplications (FLOPs), which is a proxy for total arithmetic work. FLOPs parity, however, does not guarantee latency parity, throughput parity, or implementation simplicity. The MH-MoE architecture introduces several structural features that affect these practical dimensions:

- **Increased number of experts.** MH-MoE requires substantially more experts than standard SMoE to maintain parameter parity: 40–96 experts versus 8 in the baseline. Each expert must be loaded and executed independently, and with more experts, the routing and load-balancing overhead increases. Expert parallelism strategies that work well for 8 experts may not scale efficiently to 96.

- **Additional linear projections in the critical path.** The head and merge projections are `d × d` matrix multiplications that cannot be parallelized with the expert computation (since the experts depend on the head projection output, and the merge projection depends on the expert outputs). These add serial computation steps to the forward pass.

- **Token splitting and concatenation.** While these operations have zero FLOPs cost, they require memory rearrangement that can introduce overhead in distributed training settings where tensors are sharded across devices.

- **Multi-head routing.** Each sub-token requires an independent routing decision, multiplying the number of gate computations by `h`. For `h = 3` with 96 experts, the routing computation processes `B × 3` tokens through a gate function over 96 experts, versus `B` tokens through 8 experts in the baseline.

**The consequence.** A practitioner deploying MH-MoE may find that FLOPs-matched models do not have matched wall-clock time, throughput, or engineering complexity. In distributed training across multiple GPUs, the communication patterns for 96 experts differ substantially from those for 8 experts — expert parallelism typically places different experts on different devices, and with more experts comes more cross-device communication for token routing. The serial dependency through the head projection → expert processing → merge projection pipeline may create a latency bottleneck that standard SMoE (with no pre- or post-expert projections) does not have.

For inference deployment, where latency is often the binding constraint (not total FLOPs), the additional serial operations in MH-MoE could make it less attractive than standard SMoE even at identical FLOPs. The paper's claim of FLOPs parity addresses total computational cost but not wall-clock time, which is what users and production systems actually experience.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. The paper's experimental section (Section 3) reports only perplexity and model configuration (layers, dimensions, expert counts). There are no throughput measurements (tokens per second), no latency measurements (milliseconds per forward pass), no memory usage measurements (GPU memory per device), and no discussion of distributed training considerations. The FLOPs analysis in Section 2.2 counts arithmetic operations but does not account for parallelism, communication, or memory access patterns.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper frames FLOPs parity as the sufficient condition for practical equivalence ("our goal is to ensure that the FLOPS of the MH-MoE model are equal to those of the standard SMoE model," Section 2.3), without acknowledging that FLOPs equality does not imply latency or throughput equality. No future work on practical deployment characteristics is suggested.

### 6.6 The Benefit Magnitude Is Modest Relative to Standard MoE Gains

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's headline finding is that FLOPs-matched MH-MoE improves perplexity over standard SMoE. The absolute magnitude of these improvements, however, is small relative to the gap between dense models and any MoE variant.

For the standard (non-shared expert) setting at 100K steps on RedPajama (Table 1):

| Architecture | Perplexity | Improvement over previous |
|---|---|---|
| Dense | 12.13 | — |
| SMoE (top-1) | 10.90 | −1.23 (10.1% relative) |
| Fine-grained SMoE | 10.74 | −0.16 (1.5% relative) |
| MH-MoE (head=2) | 10.70 | −0.04 (0.4% relative) |
| MH-MoE (head=3) | 10.51 | −0.19 (1.8% relative) |

The total improvement from standard SMoE to the best MH-MoE variant is 0.39 perplexity (3.6% relative). This is roughly one-third the size of the improvement from dense to standard SMoE (1.23 perplexity, 10.1% relative). The incremental benefit of MH-MoE over fine-grained SMoE is even smaller: 0.23 perplexity (2.1% relative).

**The consequence.** The modest magnitude of improvement matters for practical adoption decisions. Switching from standard SMoE to MH-MoE requires engineering changes (implementing head/merge layers, adjusting expert counts, modifying routing logic, potentially changing distributed training strategies) and introduces new hyperparameters (number of heads, per-head top-k) that must be tuned. If the expected perplexity improvement is only 3–4%, the engineering investment may not be justified, especially given the absence of evidence that the benefit scales to larger models.

The comparison to fine-grained SMoE is particularly important: fine-grained SMoE (reducing per-expert intermediate dimension, increasing expert count) captures roughly 40% of the SMoE-to-MH-MoE improvement (0.16 out of 0.39) with a simpler implementation that doesn't require multi-head splitting or additional projection layers. A practitioner might reasonably conclude that fine-grained SMoE offers a better return on engineering effort than MH-MoE, at least at the scales tested.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The numbers are in Tables 1–3. The paper does not discuss the absolute magnitude of the improvements, does not compare the MH-MoE benefit to the dense-to-MoE benefit, and does not frame the improvement in terms of practical significance. The abstract claims MH-MoE "yields quality improvements over both vanilla MoE and fine-grained MoE models" — which is factually supported — but does not contextualize the size of these improvements.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not discuss whether a 0.39 perplexity improvement at this scale is practically meaningful, whether it justifies the added architectural complexity, or whether the relative improvement is expected to grow or shrink at larger scales. The consistent but small advantage across all settings (standard, shared expert, BitNet) suggests MH-MoE provides a genuine but incremental benefit — a characterization the paper does not explicitly make.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper makes a **methodological intervention** rather than a breakthrough discovery: it establishes that architectural innovations in Mixture-of-Experts models should be evaluated under a **matched-FLOPs constraint**, not merely matched parameters. The significance of this shift should not be overstated — the paper does not introduce a new architectural paradigm, does not demonstrate order-of-magnitude improvements, and does not resolve a major contradiction in the literature. What it does is provide a disciplined framework for asking "is this MoE variant genuinely more efficient, or does it just spend more compute?"

The reframing matters because the original MH-MoE paper (Wu et al., 2024) claimed quality improvements from the multi-head mechanism while incurring a ~4× FLOPs overhead (leading term 67Bd² vs. 16Bd² for standard SMoE). Under a parameters-only comparison, MH-MoE looked like a strict improvement. Under a FLOPs-matched comparison — which this paper introduces — the question becomes: can MH-MoE retain its quality advantage when constrained to the same computational budget? The paper's answer is a qualified yes: the quality gains survive (0.39 perplexity improvement on RedPajama at 100K steps, Table 1), but at a substantially reduced magnitude compared to what the original formulation would suggest if its FLOPs were ignored.

This establishes a **precedent for how MoE architectural proposals should be evaluated**. Prior work on fine-grained MoE, DeepSeekMoE-style shared experts, and various routing mechanisms typically report quality at fixed parameter counts, with FLOPs treated as a secondary metric to be reported but not controlled. This paper implicitly argues that the correct baseline is "same parameters AND same FLOPs," and it provides a template (Equations 8–9 in Section 2.3) for how to construct such baselines: any architectural addition (like projection layers) must be compensated by reducing computation elsewhere (like shrinking expert intermediate dimensions). This is a conceptual contribution to experimental methodology in the MoE literature, independent of whether MH-MoE itself sees widespread adoption.

The paper also provides a **diagnostic contribution** through its ablation experiments (Tables 4 and 5). The finding that head and merge projection layers added to standard SMoE without token splitting yield negligible improvement (SMoE: 11.87 → 11.84 perplexity, Table 4) — while the same layers combined with splitting yield a 0.25 improvement in MH-MoE (11.71 → 11.46) — establishes that the multi-head mechanism is not simply "more linear layers help." It specifically requires the subspace-level expert routing that token splitting enables. The finding that the head layer contributes roughly 1.8× more than the merge layer (0.23 vs. 0.13 perplexity improvement, Table 5) suggests that the primary value is in learning how to partition token representations before expert processing, not in recombining expert outputs afterward. These are concrete, falsifiable claims about *how* the mechanism works that future work can build on or challenge.

The research directions this work makes **more attractive** include: principled FLOPs-controlled comparisons for any new MoE variant, systematic studies of how multi-head mechanisms interact with model scale, and exploration of alternative subspace-partitioning strategies beyond simple token splitting. The directions this work makes **less attractive** include: proposing MoE variants without FLOPs-matched baselines (the paper raises the evaluation bar), and pursuing aggressive multi-head configurations (h=4 or higher) without first establishing that the benefits scale beyond the h=2 and h=3 configurations tested here.

However, the paper does **not resolve** several important questions that limit its landscape-changing potential. The experiments are at a single small scale (~85M parameter dense baseline, 50B training tokens), with no evidence that the 0.3–0.7 perplexity improvements persist at production scales (1B+ parameters). The absence of top-k SMoE baselines (top-2, top-4 gating at matched FLOPs) leaves open whether multi-head splitting provides benefits beyond simply allowing each token to use more experts. The lack of statistical validation (single-run results, no error bars) means the claimed improvements, particularly the fine-grained differences between configurations (e.g., head=3 vs. head=2: 10.51 vs. 10.70, Table 1), have unknown reliability. A researcher reading this paper should view the FLOPs-matching methodology as the primary contribution and the specific MH-MoE perplexity numbers as suggestive rather than definitive.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Scaling MH-MoE to production model sizes with Chinchilla-optimal training.** The paper's experiments use a ~85M parameter model trained on 50B tokens — dramatically undertrained and small relative to contemporary LLMs. The most urgent follow-up is to test whether the MH-MoE advantage (0.39 perplexity over SMoE at 100K steps, Table 1) persists at scales where MoE architectures are actually deployed. A strong experiment would: train standard SMoE and FLOPs-matched MH-MoE (both head=2 and head=3 configurations) at a 1B-parameter activated scale on 200B+ tokens, measuring not just final perplexity but also downstream task performance (e.g., HellaSwag, MMLU, GSM8K). If the gap narrows or vanishes at scale — as sometimes happens when small-model inductive biases don't transfer — this would establish a scale boundary for MH-MoE's utility. If the gap widens, it would strengthen the case for adoption. Either outcome meaningfully advances our understanding beyond the current single-scale evidence.

**Disentangling multi-head splitting from multi-expert activation via top-k SMoE baselines.** The paper's MH-MoE configurations allow each token to activate up to 4 experts (2-head, top-2 per head) or 9 experts (3-head, top-3 per head), while the SMoE baselines use top-1 gating (1 expert per token). This confounds the multi-head splitting mechanism with the number of activated experts per token. A clean ablation would: construct FLOPs-matched standard SMoE variants with top-2 and top-4 gating (reducing per-expert intermediate dimension using the same FLOPs-matching principle: reduce intermediate dimension to offset the cost of more activated experts, add more experts to maintain parameter count). Compare these against MH-MoE at matched total FLOPs and matched total activated experts per token. If a top-4 standard SMoE achieves comparable perplexity to MH-MoE (head=2, top-2), the multi-head mechanism would be revealed as an unnecessarily complex way to achieve multi-expert processing, and the simpler top-k approach would be preferable. If MH-MoE still outperforms, the subspace-splitting mechanism would be validated as providing benefits beyond simple multi-expert aggregation.

**Varying the number of heads at fixed FLOPs to find the scaling optimum.** The paper tests only h=2 and h=3, and observes that h=3 outperforms h=2 (e.g., 10.51 vs. 10.70 on RedPajama at 100K, Table 1 — though this gap is small enough to potentially be noise in a single run). A systematic sweep over h ∈ {1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8} at matched FLOPs and parameters, with multiple seeds per configuration, would answer: (a) does the benefit increase monotonically with number of heads, (b) at what h does the benefit saturate or reverse, (c) is the optimal h a function of model dimension d (e.g., does $h_{opt} \propto \sqrt{d}$)? The h=1 case is particularly important because it would create a configuration where there is effectively no multi-head splitting (each token is processed as a single unit) but with the projection layers present — providing a direct test of whether the splitting mechanism, specifically, provides benefit beyond having additional linear layers in the MoE block. The h=4 case would connect to the original MH-MoE paper's configuration and test whether the FLOPs-matched version of their architecture (with intermediate dimension appropriately reduced) outperforms the h=2 and h=3 variants.

**Testing alternative subspace partitioning strategies beyond equal splitting.** The paper's token splitting divides the d-dimensional token into h equal-sized chunks of dimension d/h. This is the simplest possible partitioning scheme, but it assumes that the representational subspaces that should be processed together are contiguous in the embedding dimension. A follow-up could test: (a) interleaved splitting (head 1 takes dimensions {0, h, 2h, ...}, head 2 takes {1, h+1, 2h+1, ...}, etc.) to mix information across the embedding space before expert processing, (b) learned partitioning via a low-rank projection (W_head projects to h × (d/h) rather than d, learning which dimensions go to which head via a structured sparsity pattern), (c) overlapping heads where each head sees a d-dimensional token projected to d/h dimensions via a different learned projection (more parameters and FLOPs but potentially richer representations). The key measurement is whether any of these partitioning strategies outperform equal contiguous splitting at matched parameters and FLOPs, which would indicate that the multi-head benefit is partly about *how* the token is partitioned, not just *that* it is partitioned. The paper's finding that the head layer contributes more than the merge layer (Table 5) makes this direction particularly promising — if improving input partitioning yields outsized gains, then investing in better partitioning schemes could compound MH-MoE's advantage.

**Stress-testing MH-MoE on tasks beyond language modeling perplexity.** The paper evaluates only perplexity on three text corpora (RedPajama, Wiki, C4). Perplexity is a coarse metric that can mask important differences in model behavior — two models with identical perplexity can have very different downstream task performance, factual accuracy, or reasoning capabilities. A comprehensive stress-test would: train FLOPs-matched SMoE and MH-MoE models, then evaluate on (a) knowledge-intensive tasks (e.g., TriviaQA, NaturalQuestions) to test whether multi-head expert routing preserves factual knowledge as well as single-expert routing, (b) reasoning tasks (e.g., GSM8K, MATH) to test whether subspace-level processing helps or hurts multi-step reasoning, (c) generation quality metrics (e.g., MAUVE, human evaluation) to test whether MH-MoE produces more coherent or diverse text. The finding that MH-MoE improves perplexity on out-of-domain text more than in-domain text (C4 improvement 0.72 vs. RedPajama 0.39 at 100K, Table 1) hints at better generalization, but perplexity alone cannot confirm this. A negative result — e.g., MH-MoE matching SMoE on perplexity but underperforming on factual recall — would be equally informative and would constrain the circumstances under which MH-MoE should be adopted.

**Investigating load balancing and routing dynamics with many small experts.** MH-MoE increases the number of experts dramatically (from 8 to 40–96 in the paper's experiments) while reducing per-expert capacity. This shifts the load-balancing challenge: with more experts, it becomes statistically easier to achieve uniform routing (more bins for tokens to distribute across), but each expert has fewer parameters, potentially making them less robust to routing mistakes. A follow-up study could: instrument MH-MoE models during training to measure (a) expert utilization — what fraction of experts are "dead" (receiving negligible token traffic) at convergence, (b) routing entropy — how concentrated or dispersed the token-to-expert assignments are, compared to standard SMoE, (c) the impact of auxiliary load-balancing losses on MH-MoE vs. standard SMoE — does MH-MoE require stronger or weaker load-balancing to achieve good utilization? The results would guide practitioners on whether MH-MoE's many-expert configuration introduces training instability that requires mitigation, or whether the increased expert count naturally improves load balancing.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Cost-efficient pretraining of small-to-medium language models at fixed compute budgets.** For teams training language models in the 100M–1B parameter range with constrained GPU budgets, the paper's results suggest that switching from standard SMoE to FLOPs-matched MH-MoE yields a consistent 3–5% perplexity improvement at identical training cost. In the shared expert setting (Table 2), MH-MoE (head=3) achieves 10.28 perplexity on RedPajama at 100K steps vs. 10.66 for SMoE — a 0.38 improvement obtained with no additional FLOPs, no additional parameters, and no changes to the training pipeline beyond the layer implementation. For a team already using SMoE, this is a drop-in architectural change with a favorable cost-benefit ratio: the engineering effort of implementing head/merge layers and adjusting expert counts is modest compared to the alternative of training longer or scaling up model dimensions to achieve the same perplexity reduction. The primary caveat is the single-scale evidence — teams training at substantially larger scales should validate the benefit on their target model size before committing.

**Memory-constrained edge deployment of quantized MoE models.** The BitNet compatibility results (Table 3) demonstrate that MH-MoE's advantages survive 1-bit quantization, with MH-MoE (head=3) achieving 26.47 perplexity on RedPajama at 100K steps vs. 26.78 for quantized SMoE — a 0.31 improvement. For on-device deployment scenarios where both memory footprint and inference FLOPs are tightly constrained, this is a Pareto improvement: better model quality at no additional memory or compute cost. The fact that MH-MoE performs better under BitNet than fine-grained SMoE (26.47 vs. 26.68) suggests the multi-head mechanism provides representational benefits that are complementary to quantization, not redundant with it. A practical deployment pipeline could: (1) train an MH-MoE model with FLOPs-matched configuration, (2) quantize to 1-bit using BitNet, (3) deploy on memory-constrained hardware (e.g., mobile devices, embedded systems), achieving better quality than a comparably-sized quantized standard MoE while maintaining identical latency and memory usage. The paper's caveat about BitNet degrading small-model performance (Section 3.2) should be noted — the absolute perplexity under quantization (26.47) is substantially worse than full precision (10.51), and deployment at this quality level would only be appropriate for applications where full-precision models cannot fit.

**Incremental improvement for existing MoE training pipelines with shared experts.** The DeepSeekMoE-style shared expert has become a standard component in recent MoE architectures. The paper shows (Table 2) that adding MH-MoE to a shared-expert SMoE setup yields additive benefits: the improvement from MH-MoE is 0.38 perplexity (10.66 → 10.28 on RedPajama at 100K) in the shared expert setting, nearly identical to the 0.39 improvement in the non-shared setting (10.90 → 10.51, Table 1). This means teams already using shared experts can adopt MH-MoE without sacrificing their existing architectural choices. The implementation path is: take an existing shared-expert SMoE codebase, apply the head/merge layer additions and adjust expert intermediate dimensions and counts using the paper's recipe (Equations 8–9, with SwiGLU-specific adjustments that would need to be independently derived or empirically tuned given the paper's lack of SwiGLU-adjusted formulas), and expect a small but reliable perplexity improvement without changing training hyperparameters, data pipeline, or total compute budget. The low-risk nature of this change — it doesn't require modifications to the shared expert, the attention mechanism, or the training objective — makes it one of the more actionable findings for practitioners.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper does not articulate an explicit tradeoff against named alternatives, and does not position MH-MoE against specific competing architectures with clearly-defined selection criteria. The experiments compare MH-MoE to a standard top-1 SMoE and a fine-grained SMoE, but the paper does not provide decision rules, failure mode analysis, or task-specific guidance that would support a "prefer A when X, prefer B when Y" framework. The modest magnitude of the improvement (3–5% relative perplexity reduction) and the absence of scale-diversity experiments mean that the primary decision facing a practitioner is not "MH-MoE vs. alternative X" but rather "is the engineering effort of implementing MH-MoE justified by the expected perplexity gain?" The paper provides evidence that the gain exists at one scale but no framework for assessing when it is worth pursuing. Forcing a decision matrix would invent criteria the paper does not provide.
