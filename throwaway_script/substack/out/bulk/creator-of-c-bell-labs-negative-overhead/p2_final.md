Here are the takeaways from this Bjarne Stroustrup interview (creator of C++, former Bell Labs researcher, and steward of the language's decades-long evolution):

**The origin of C++**

Stroustrup arrived at Bell Labs in the late 1970s, looked around at the people who built Unix, and realized "I had to do something important; otherwise, it didn't belong." His first ambition was to build a distributed Unix, but he immediately hit the problem that would define his career: no existing language could do what he needed. He required low-level hardware access (memory managers, process schedulers, device drivers) *and* high-level module and communication abstractions in a single tool. C was the obvious choice for the low level—Dennis Ritchie and Brian Kernighan were down the hall—and Simula, which he'd learned from its inventors Kristen Nygaard and Ole-Johan Dahl, provided the class concept he admired. His insight was to merge Simula's classes into C, making the type system more regular so user-defined types behaved like built-in types, and eventually generalizing overloading—"you can add two integers, you can add two floating-point numbers, you can add a floating-point number to an integer with a plus; a single name"—to work uniformly across both. That lineage, sparked by a distributed-systems problem he never actually solved, became C++.

But the deeper seed was planted even earlier, during his PhD at Cambridge. He wrote a distributed-systems simulator in Simula that worked beautifully on small tests, then attempted a full-scale run on the department's mainframe. "The chemists and the astrophysicists would never accept it," so he was kicked off. The CAP computer—an unusual experimental machine with hardware protection that the astrophysicists couldn't use—was available, but Simula had never been ported to it. He rewrote the entire simulator in BCPL, a language "that will make C look like a high-level language" with only one data type: the word. It was a painful exercise, but the program ran roughly 50 times faster, and he got his PhD. He emerged determined never again to tackle a problem with such inadequate tools, and carried a mental list of what his ideal language needed. C came closest. (Ryan notes that Stroustrup has joked he lost half his hair debugging that BCPL project, and the other half getting C++ going.)

**What Bell Labs was like**

Bell Labs, in Stroustrup's telling, was "easily the best" practical engineering institution in the world—"probably twice as many computer scientists as MIT at the time." He got in through a chance connection: a Bell Labs researcher visiting Cambridge told him at the pub, "When you need a job, give us a buzz." He flew to New Jersey on his own tab, only to be told by his future boss Sandy Fraser that they had no jobs. The next day he gave a talk to a development group, and they changed their minds and brought him into the research group. There was no formal interview process—"they hadn't actually hired anybody new for five years." He just talked to people, including a long chat with Dennis Ritchie, and a week later the director called to offer him a position.

The research philosophy was radically hands-off: **hire the best people and don't tell them what to do**. His job description was "do something interesting in a year's time, tell us what you did, and if we like it, we'll extend." The reporting mechanism was famously sparse—write one sheet of paper, nine-point font or larger, because "if you can't say what you did fairly briefly, you probably haven't done something interesting enough." This anarchic model, Stroustrup argues, outperformed the more structured approach that IBM and others used; most of what people remember from Bell Labs—Unix, fiber optics, wireless technology, charge-coupled devices for cameras, the cellular system—emerged from that culture. He describes a fluid, cross-disciplinary environment: he helped build network-overload simulators and was once asked to simulate all of Manhattan's computer traffic, which he declined because even then "we don't have the compute power to do that."

When Ryan asks whether the density of talent produced imposter syndrome, Stroustrup is emphatic: "Definitely, maybe I still have a bit of it." Seeing the names on the doors—people whose papers had created the fields he wanted to work in—convinced him he had to "up my game." But the openness was deliberate; doors were always open as a policy, and the lunchtime conversations were a constant education. Cambridge had a similar effect on him. His advice to himself at the time was simple: "If it worked, it was the best. And if it didn't work, so what? You can't succeed at everything."

**Dennis Ritchie and the C relationship**

Stroustrup had lunch with Dennis Ritchie roughly once a week for 16 years. He describes Ritchie as a great guy who never said anything rude or negative about C++, and who in his HOPL paper explicitly called C++ "the obvious successor to C." The C-versus-C++ language wars, Stroustrup insists, "are ridiculous. They should never have happened, and they certainly didn't happen because, well, I knew Dennis. We were not fighting." Ritchie helped him design `const` (originally called "Read Only" and "Write Only," but "the C guys couldn't handle two words and they were too long") and was initially worried about overloading because you'd need to look at declarations before understanding a call. But Stroustrup points out that modern C syntax for function definitions, declarations, and call semantics actually came out of his early C++ and C-with-classes work—"when people start ranting, they should remember that they're actually using my handiwork every day."

One notable shared idea that took decades to arrive: buffer overflow. Ritchie proposed **fat pointers**—a pointer bundled with the number of elements it points to—but at the time they had 48 kilobytes of memory, and a two-word pointer was too expensive. Stroustrup revisited the concept years later during the C++ Core Guidelines work and it became `span`, now in the standard. "Some of these ideas are very old."

**When to build a programming language**

Stroustrup pushes back sharply on the "how do I build a language?" question most people ask. "What you need is a problem that needs a solution." Most would-be language designers just want something better for what they're currently doing, which can usually be achieved with existing languages. A specialized domain-specific language is fine; a general-purpose language demands a much broader set of users. C++ grew large and complex for two reasons: historical constraints (he couldn't build the language he wanted in the 1980s—insufficient technology, insufficient knowledge) and a deliberate engineering cycle: "do the best you can, then see what works and what doesn't work and try to fix the problems. Then you repeat."

His initial technical decisions were shaped by pragmatic constraints. He refused to touch linkers because Bell Labs alone used about 25 different ones, and "nobody wants you to touch their linker because if you make a mistake, everything breaks." The same logic applied to optimizers—every computer had a different one. He realized every code generator shared a common interface: C. So he used C as an assembler, which gave him C compatibility both as an implementation technique and a cultural bridge. His framing at the time: "We can have Dennis Ritchie's mistakes, which we know, and we can have my mistakes, which we don't know yet, so we'll take Dennis's."

On bootstrapping: he started with a C preprocessor that handled classes and overloading, then used that to write a simple compiler for a subset of C++, then used that to write the next version—a standard technique he was taught as an undergraduate.

**C++ is not object-oriented**

Stroustrup never called C++ an object-oriented language. The first edition of *The C++ Programming Language* came closest to saying "some people call these techniques object-based." The language supports object orientation well, following Simula's model of class hierarchies, "but that was never all it was." His canonical example: "I do not want object-oriented complex numbers. I don't want to say `2.dot()`; I really want to say `2 + Z`." Math has developed its notation over 300 years, and he wanted it preserved. He also wanted things that didn't need inheritance or runtime resolution to simply not use them. Another motivation: he wanted to reuse *existing* software—Fortran libraries, C systems code, and Simula—rather than build a closed world that only enabled new reuse. "This is the real world; this is the real set of problems. You have to respect the constraints."

**Type systems**

The weak C type system was an obvious source of errors and endless debugging—"I hate debugging. I would much rather do design." A stronger type system moved effort from runtime to compile time. Overloading, in particular, turned out to be essential for generic programming: when you write `vector<T>`, you need the type system to resolve operations on `T` at compile time. He cites an experiment at Bell Labs's Chicago facility where groups switching from C to C++ used roughly the same amount of compilation time overall even though C++ compiled ~2× slower, because the C++ programmers compiled twice as often—they were getting faster feedback.

On static versus dynamic typing: the choice followed from the problems. A Smalltalk runtime error drops you into the debugger, which makes sense if a programmer is sitting at a screen, but "it doesn't make any sense if a telephone switch finds a runtime error." He wanted to fit into small memories—120K, 256K, 1 megabyte—and static typing eliminates the runtime metadata overhead. Embedded systems (which he estimates are 99% of all computers) remain memory-constrained. He acknowledges Python's popularity but notes that raw Python runs about 70× slower than raw C++; it's viable only because "a lot of key Python libraries are written in C or C++ to get the performance"—exactly the two-language split he was trying to eliminate.

**Memory safety**

Stroustrup visibly bristles at C++'s reputation for memory unsafety. "I'm so tired of that. I haven't had those problems for years." He points to studies showing over 90% of buffer-overflow issues come from code written in a C style—raw pointers without element counts, no spans, no vectors. Modern C++ has hardened libraries at Apple, Google, and Microsoft; C++26 standardizes a hardened option, and his current work on **profiles** will provide compiler-enforced guarantees against the "stupid things." The problem was solved years ago in theory; people just keep doing what they've always done and getting the same problems. "That makes me sad."

Ryan asks whether the compiler could simply prevent these patterns by default. Stroustrup says it should be the default and he's proposing it for C++29, with simpler versions that should have been in C++26. But "there are still a lot of people, even in the C++ Standards Committee, that are very devoted to their old code and their old ways."

**Standards committee anecdotes**

C++ was never a dictatorship, but Stroustrup didn't exactly choose standardization either. Two representatives from IBM, HP, and another major vendor came to his office and told him their organizations couldn't use a non-standardized language, nor one owned by a single corporation they sometimes competed with. They twisted his arm for an hour, and he agreed to standardize under ANSI rules. "If I had said no," he believes, "C++ would have faded into becoming an academic cube language." He cites Java's early ads promising to "absolutely kill C++ in two years"—today there are 10–12× more C++ developers than when those claims were made, and C++ had essentially zero marketing budget ($5,000 over three years) compared to Sun's massive Java campaigns.

The committee now has about 527 members and operates on **consensus, not simple majority**—he prefers 90% agreement, worries at 80%, and notes that the convener determines what consensus means, not a fixed threshold. A 95/5 vote where all major implementers (Google, Apple, Microsoft) are in the 5% would never be called consensus. "Not everybody cares for the whole language. Not everybody understands what's going on. You can vote at your third meeting."

When Ryan asks about the infamously controversial `auto` proposal, Stroustrup explains that it was seen as weakening the type system, even though it's actually the simplest form of concepts—the constraint that something must be a type. It eventually passed, but later he observed industrial overuse: programmers were writing `auto` and then adding comments like "must be an input channel" because they kept having to look up what `auto` resolved to. The solution was concepts, allowing `input_channel auto`.

He also recounts a memorable piece of **shuttle diplomacy** between IBM (PowerPC) and Intel (x86) representatives who were deadlocked over hardware memory models. Days of direct negotiation had failed, so Stroustrup spent a couple of hours walking between two corners of a large room, translating and asking questions. They reached an agreement that made it into C++; a few years later, both sides were using a hybrid of their original approaches. The IBM representative, Brian McKnight, later did much of the synchronization work for the Linux kernel.

**The Vasa cautionary tale**

During a standards meeting in Stockholm, Stroustrup discovered the Vasa—a 17th-century battleship ordered by the king to be the most beautiful and powerful ever built. After construction began, the king learned a rival was building two-gun-deck ships and ordered a second gun deck added to the existing hull; more cannons and ornate statues were piled on top. Testing for stability involved the crew running side to side; they stopped at seven passes instead of the required 14 because it looked dangerous. The ship sailed halfway across Stockholm harbor, a gust of wind hit, and it sank. Stroustrup tells the committee: don't add features without strengthening the foundation, don't compromise testing, and when high bosses demand something dangerous, "the professional thing is to say, no, we are not doing this." Build the one-gun-deck version, then design a two-gun-deck ship from scratch next year.

**Garbage collection in C++**

In 1995, the committee, driven by expert users and Hans Boehm's conservative garbage collector, added a standard garbage-collection interface. Stroustrup was always focused on general resource management (constructors/destructors, later known as **RAII—"probably my worst naming ever"**), but he listened to the community. What happened over the next decade: usage of garbage collection decreased as RAII became better understood, and the people who still used garbage collectors didn't use the standard interface because they'd found better ways. The standard interface was eventually removed.

**Template instantiation is Turing complete**

When Erwin Unruh demonstrated that C++ template instantiation was Turing complete—calculating prime numbers at compile time and reporting results via error messages—he thought Stroustrup should ban it. Stroustrup's reaction: "This looks useful. Great." The theoreticians' concern about infinite loops is a category error: a real compile-time Turing machine would require infinite tape, and real machines run out of resources long before that becomes a practical problem. The real issue was that people were misusing templates for compile-time calculations (factorials, sieves), which was "so awful, so expensive, it uses up so much memory." That drove him and Gabriel Dos Reis to build `constexpr`, which allows ordinary code to execute at compile time—simpler, faster to compile, and producing faster code. `constexpr` displaced the template-metaprogramming abuse.

**Abstraction and performance**

Ryan raises the common intuition that C, being closer to the machine, should outperform C++. Stroustrup flatly disagrees: C++ shares exactly the same machine model (C adopted the C++11 memory model), so identical code produces identical results. But the optimizer in a C++ compiler has *more* information because the type system and abstractions encode programmer intent, so it can do a better job. This leads to his signature framing: **zero overhead abstraction**—abstractions that compile away entirely—and, provocatively, **negative overhead abstraction**, where the abstraction enables optimizations that wouldn't be possible in lower-level code. If you were brilliant and had infinite time, hand-written assembly could theoretically beat the compiler, but "we are not as smart as the optimizers anymore, and we don't have infinite time."

His talk titled *"Don't Be (Too) Clever"* to a group of finance-industry performance engineers made the case that modern C++ is fast enough for 98% of your code, freeing your time for the small fraction that genuinely needs optimization. "Clever optimizations these days tend to be machine dependent. If you get a new computer or a new version of the compiler, you might actually have pessimized your code." His technique: throw away the 1990s clever stuff first, then measure. In one performance-test-suite experiment with a fluid-dynamics colleague, stripping cleverness produced a 20% speedup while reducing code volume to 80% of the original. He follows Knuth's advice about premature optimization literally: profile, identify the 2–3% of code that matters, and only optimize there.

**AI writing code**

Stroustrup is skeptical about LLMs in the domains he cares about—performance-critical, safety-critical systems. Current AI-generated code in this space produces more bugs, more security holes, and bloated code that pessimizes. The deeper problem is validation: when an AI regenerates code after a prompt tweak, large swaths change, and *all* of it must be re-validated. A human makes localized changes with predictable impact. Moreover, regulatory bodies increasingly require validation of safety-critical software. Senior developers, he's observed, are starting to retire rather than deal with constantly re-validating AI output. AI trained on old code imitates old code, bringing "old performance and old bugs again," while his job is to get people writing new things with new techniques. He's not saying AI is useless—it helps with documentation and routine web apps—but for the 10–20% of the world's code he focuses on, "it's not there. And I don't see it coming with the LLM model." The economic equation also puzzles him: if you need a $100 million data center to replace junior developers, "how many junior developers does it take to be cheaper?"

**What keeps him going**

Two forces: the fun of building the future, and an obligation to move C++ forward. He knew from the start that he couldn't build his ideal language immediately—limited resources, limited knowledge, and being essentially a solo effort meant iterating: "build what you can, see what works, improve it." That required designing for evolution from the beginning, which is why C++ was never a single-paradigm language. "I could see in the world there were things that didn't seem to fit that paradigm." The other half is applications—visiting JPL to talk to the Mars rover team, going to CERN to watch high-energy physicists, chatting with the engineer whose job is to open and close multi-ton lead doors without sending them through the wall. "Just learning is fun."

**On quotes, books, and advice**

Several of his enduring quotes trace to specific moments. *"C++ makes it easy to shoot yourself in the foot; C makes it harder, but when you do, it blows your whole leg off"* was an off-the-cuff answer at a Boston talk in the 1980s. The deeper idea came from Nobel laureate Arno Penzias, who told Bell Labs managers, "You can't have a power tool without knowing how to use it." *"Nobody should call themselves a professional if they only know one language"* is not about which second language you learn, but about absorbing the different ideas embedded in different paradigms—a scripting language, a functional language, something unlike yours. The multilingual joke ("One language? American") applies equally to programming.

His recommended learning path is *Programming: Principles and Practice Using C++* (second edition, with a thinner third edition coming because the language has improved). "Learn the modern way first. Don't start learning all the bad ways of writing C as the starter."

His advice to his younger self has three parts. First, fight harder against implicit narrowing conversions between built-in types—he tried and was overruled by more senior Bell Labs people, but considers it a fundamental type-system flaw ("I even wrote a paper about how to get rid of them last year in a library"). Second, delay the release of C++ until template-like mechanisms existed so a decent standard library could ship from the start; they were saved by Alex Stepanov's STL, but that was "real luck because I made a mistake." Third, knowing what he now knows about bloated standards committees, he would push for a small steering group of 5–6 vastly experienced people who care about the whole language, making decisions based on input from a 500-person community rather than giving everyone a vote. He deliberately does *not* mention tools, because C++'s tooling weakness is a product of when it grew up, and "a lot of languages designed to be perfect for the future computers and the future programmers—most of them die because by the time ten years later they get the language, the world has changed."

---

His career arc echoes his own engineering philosophy: identify the real problem first, build what you can, then iterate based on what works. The **Vasa cautionary tale** and the **negative overhead abstraction** concept both stem from the same conviction—that abstractions fail when foundations aren't strengthened, and succeed when they encode intent the compiler can exploit. His frustration with memory-safety reputations mirrors his template-Turing story: a theoretically scary property becomes a non-issue in finite machines, just as modern types and spans have solved the practical buffer-overflow problem years ago. The thread connecting Bell Labs's hands-off management, his "don't tell them what to do" research culture, and his stance against making C++ an object-only language is a consistent refusal to let a single paradigm over-constrain a system meant to evolve for decades.
