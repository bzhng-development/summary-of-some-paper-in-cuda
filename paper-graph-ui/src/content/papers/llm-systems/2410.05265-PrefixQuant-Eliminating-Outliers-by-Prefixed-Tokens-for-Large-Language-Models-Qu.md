# PrefixQuant: Eliminating Outliers by Prefixed Tokens for Large Language Models Quantization

**ArXiv:** [2410.05265](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.05265)

## 🎯 Pitch

Just two outlier tokens in a 2048-token context can cause 94.7% of all quantization error in 4-bit LLMs. PrefixQuant eliminates this by prepending a handful of high-frequency outlier tokens to the KV cache—a training-free fix taking under one minute for Llama-3-70B—outperforming even dynamic quantization methods while enabling up to 2.74× faster inference.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

PrefixQuant proposes a novel quantization method that isolates **token-wise outliers** — activation values orders of magnitude larger than normal tokens that cause disproportionate quantization error — by prefixing high-frequency outlier tokens (e.g., `. \n` for Llama-2-7B) directly into the KV cache, a training-free process that completes in under one minute for Llama-3-70B. Combining this prefixing mechanism with block-wise fine-tuning that introduces trainable activation clipping factors (for dynamic quantization) or trainable scaling parameters (for static quantization), PrefixQuant achieves state-of-the-art accuracy across W4A4KV4 and W4A8KV4 settings — improving over SpinQuant by +3.08 and +2.85 average accuracy points on zero-shot reasoning tasks under dynamic and static quantization respectively on Llama-3-8B — while delivering up to 2.74× prefilling and 2.16× decoding speedup over FP16 models, establishing that prefixing as few as 1–4 tokens can eliminate token-wise outliers without requiring the costly grid searches or retraining of prior outlier-mitigation approaches.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Token-Wise Outliers Are the Unaddressed Bottleneck in LLM Activation Quantization

The fundamental problem PrefixQuant tackles is that **a tiny fraction of tokens — as few as 2 out of 2048 — can contribute the overwhelming majority of quantization error** in LLM activations. Figure 1 in the paper crystallizes this: for a 4-bit per-token dynamic quantization of a Llama-2-7B transformer block output, two outlier tokens account for 94.7% of the total quantization error (54.6 MSE contribution out of 54.6 total), while the remaining 2046 tokens contribute just 5.3%. This is not a marginal inefficiency — it means that the entire quantization scheme is being dominated by rare, extreme activation values, and addressing those few tokens could dramatically improve overall quantized model quality without touching the vast majority of the computation.

This problem matters for two tightly coupled reasons. First, **practical deployment**: LLMs consume enormous memory and compute resources at inference time. Quantization — reducing weight and activation precision from 16-bit floating point to 4-bit or 8-bit integers — is the primary technique for shrinking memory footprints and enabling fast integer matrix multiplication. Weight-only quantization (e.g., W4A16) helps with memory-bound operations but leaves the activations in high precision, so compute-bound operations remain bottlenecked. **Weight-activation quantization** (W4A4, W4A8) unlocks low-bit matrix manipulation that accelerates both the prefilling (computation-bound) and decoding (memory-bound) phases of inference. As the paper demonstrates in Table 6, W4A4 quantization yields 2.74× prefilling speedup and 2.16× decoding speedup over FP16 — gains that directly translate to lower latency, higher throughput, and reduced hardware requirements for serving LLMs. However, these gains are only realizable if quantization accuracy is preserved. Token-wise outliers are the primary obstacle to achieving that accuracy at low bit-widths.

Second, **the core LLM architecture creates these outliers inherently**, making them a universal problem rather than a model-specific quirk. The SoftMax function in self-attention prevents any attention score from being exactly zero — there is always some probability mass assigned to every key. For special tokens (delimiters, punctuation, the beginning-of-sequence marker) and initial positions that must attend broadly, this creates a feedback loop: these tokens receive non-trivial attention scores from all query positions, which produces large activations flowing through the network, which in turn makes these positions more prominent in subsequent layers' attention patterns. This phenomenon, identified by Sun et al. (2024) as "massive activations" and by Xiao et al. (2023b) as "attention sinks," means that token-wise outliers are not an accident of training — they are a structural consequence of the transformer architecture itself. Any deployment of any LLM on any task will encounter them.

### The Existing Focus on Channel-Wise Outliers Leaves Token-Wise Outliers Unresolved

The LLM quantization literature has been overwhelmingly focused on **channel-wise outliers** — activation values that are consistently large in specific channel dimensions across many tokens. This is a different phenomenon from token-wise outliers, and the distinction is critical for understanding what PrefixQuant contributes.

**What channel-wise outliers look like:** In a tensor $X \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times C}$ (tokens × channels), a channel-wise outlier appears as a column (channel index) where *most tokens* have abnormally large activation values. This pattern is predictable: the same channel indices are outliers regardless of the specific input sequence. The solutions developed for this pattern exploit that predictability:

- **Mixed-precision quantization** (Dettmers et al., 2022; Zhao et al., 2023; Ashkboos et al., 2023) isolates outlier channels and keeps them in 16-bit precision while quantizing the rest. This works because the outlier channels are fixed and known ahead of time.
- **Channel-wise scaling** (Xiao et al., 2023a; Shao et al., 2023; Wei et al., 2023a) scales down outlier channels before quantization and compensates in the weights, exploiting the fact that the same scaling factor works across all tokens.
- **Hadamard rotation** (Ashkboos et al., 2024b; Liu et al., 2024b) applies random orthogonal transformations to redistribute outlier values from outlier channels across all channels, making the distribution more uniform within each token. QuaRot (Ashkboos et al., 2024b) demonstrated that this approach can achieve W4A4 quantization with reasonable accuracy, and SpinQuant (Liu et al., 2024b) improved on it by learning the rotation matrices rather than using random ones.

These approaches have been remarkably successful at addressing the channel-wise dimension of the outlier problem. However, **none of them directly address token-wise outliers.**

**What token-wise outliers look like:** In the same tensor $X \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times C}$, a token-wise outlier appears as a *row* (token index) where some or all channels have abnormally large (or abnormally small) activation values. These are not predictable by channel index — their *position* depends on the specific input sequence, and their *content* depends on token semantics (delimiters, punctuation marks) and sequence position (early tokens tend to be outliers regardless of content). Figure 4 in the paper shows this clearly: in Llama-2-7B, outlier tokens occur at position 0 (the initial token) 50% of the time and at some other early but *unpredictable* position the rest of the time, and their content is consistently low-semantic tokens like "." or "\n" when not in the initial position.

**Why channel-wise methods fall short for token-wise outliers:** Hadamard rotation — the current state-of-the-art — redistributes values across channels within each token, but it cannot eliminate the fact that some tokens have *overall magnitudes* hundreds or thousands of times larger than normal tokens. Figure 2(b) in the paper quantifies this limitation: after applying Hadamard rotation to a Llama-2-7B activation distribution, the maximum value in outlier tokens drops from over 1,500 to approximately 15, but normal tokens have values around 0.1–0.5, so the outlier tokens are still ~30–150× larger. The resulting quantization error only improves from 54.63 to 7.88 — better, but still an order of magnitude worse than what could be achieved if token-wise outliers were eliminated entirely (0.04, as shown in Figure 2c with PrefixQuant).

The fundamental issue is that **per-token dynamic quantization computes quantization parameters separately for each token** (the $s$ and $z$ in Equations 1–2), so outlier tokens with extreme values force these parameters to accommodate a much wider range than normal tokens. This increases the step size $s$ for outlier tokens, which increases rounding error for all values within that token. And because these tokens are rare and input-dependent, there is no fixed channel pattern to exploit.

### Prior Approaches to Token-Wise Outliers Are Expensive, Brittle, or Incomplete

The paper identifies a handful of prior works that have explicitly targeted token-wise outliers, and it is worth understanding what they did and why they are insufficient:

**Training-based approaches that modify the attention mechanism.** Bondarenko et al. (2024) and Sun et al. (2024) address the root cause of token-wise outliers by changing the SoftMax behavior or adding attention biases during training. The idea is to allow the attention mechanism to truly attend to nothing (producing near-zero attention scores) or to dampen the feedback loop that creates "massive activations." These methods are effective at reducing outliers, but they require **retraining the model** — which is computationally expensive, requires access to the original training pipeline and data, and produces a modified model that may deviate from the well-tested behavior of the original pretrained weights.

**KV-cache prefixing methods with expensive search.** The approaches most similar to PrefixQuant are QFeP (Yang et al., 2024) and CushionCache (Son et al., 2024), which also prefix tokens in the KV cache to mitigate activation outliers. However, both rely on **grid search over token combinations** to find effective prefix tokens. The paper reports that this grid search takes **12 hours for Llama-3-8B** — making these methods impractical for large-scale deployment or rapid experimentation. PrefixQuant, by contrast, identifies prefix tokens through a simple frequency analysis of outlier tokens on a small calibration dataset, completing in **12 seconds for Llama-3-8B** and **1 minute for Llama-3-70B** (Table 6).

**StreamingLLM and LM-Infinite for long-context generation.** Prior work on attention sinks (Xiao et al., 2023b; Han et al., 2023) observed that retaining initial tokens in the KV cache prevents perplexity degradation in long-context generation. These methods *keep* the initial tokens for streaming purposes, but they do not *leverage* them to improve quantization — the prefixed tokens in StreamingLLM serve a different purpose (maintaining the attention score distribution for long sequences) and are not selected or analyzed for their outlier-absorbing properties.

**Limited scope: focusing only on large-value outliers.** The paper makes an important distinction that prior works missed: token-wise outliers include not only **upper outliers** (tokens with extremely large values) but also **lower outliers** in the queries (Q) and keys (K) of self-attention — tokens with extremely small values relative to the median. Figure 3(III) shows that in Llama-2-7B's K activations, the ratio $\text{median} / \text{min-1}$ exceeds 9, meaning some tokens have values an order of magnitude *smaller* than the median. Hadamard rotation does nothing to mitigate these lower outliers (Figure 3(III.b)), because rotating small values across channels doesn't make them larger. Prior works on token-wise outliers (Sun et al., 2024; Son et al., 2024) focused exclusively on large-value outliers, leaving this lower-outlier problem unaddressed.

### How PrefixQuant Positions Itself

PrefixQuant is positioned as a **simple, training-free, and computationally cheap method** that directly addresses token-wise outliers in a way that is orthogonal and complementary to channel-wise methods. The key design choice is to **work with existing outlier structures rather than trying to eliminate them**.

The core observation is that token-wise outliers have three properties that make them amenable to isolation rather than suppression:

1. **Scarcity:** Only a small number of tokens (1–4 depending on the model) in any sequence are outliers. Figure 4(a) shows that in Llama-2-7B, outlier tokens occupy only ~2 positions in the input sequence regardless of its total length.

2. **Predictability of content, not position:** While the *positions* of outlier tokens vary with the input, the *content* of outlier tokens is highly predictable — they are initial tokens (across all models) and a small set of low-semantic tokens (delimiters like ".", "\n", "," that are consistent for each model). Figure 8 in the appendix shows the top outlier token contents for various models, with "\n" accounting for 64.34% of non-initial outlier occurrences in Mistral-7B-v0.3 and "." accounting for 71.4% in Llama-2-7B.

3. **Consistency across layers:** Outlier tokens appear at the same positions across transformer layers. The paper computes the average number of outlier tokens $O \in \mathbb{R}^b$ for each of $b$ transformer blocks and finds them to be "nearly consistent across layers," so a single count $o = \lceil \max(O) \rceil$ suffices to characterize the model's outlier behavior.

These properties enable PrefixQuant's central idea: **prefix the top-$o$ most frequent outlier tokens (plus a [BOS] token for consistency) at the beginning of every input sequence**. Because outlier tokens are then constrained to these prefixed positions, which are identical for all inputs, their KV cache representations can be precomputed once offline in full precision and reused across all inference runs. This transforms an input-dependent problem into a fixed, precomputable one.

Crucially, PrefixQuant integrates with — rather than replaces — the Hadamard rotation backbone. The paper builds on QuaRot's rotation scheme (absorbable rotations for q/k/v/gate/up projections and online rotations for down projections and post-RoPE representations) and adds the prefixing mechanism as an orthogonal improvement. The ablation in Table 5 confirms this complementarity: starting from the rotated baseline, adding prefixed outliers reduces perplexity from 11.70 to 7.53 for W4A4KV4 dynamic quantization (PrefixQuant-O1) and from 141.02 to 7.93 for static quantization (PrefixQuant-O2). The rotation handles channel-wise outliers; the prefixed tokens handle token-wise outliers.

The paper also introduces a block-wise fine-tuning stage with trainable activation quantization parameters. This serves a different purpose from the prefixing mechanism: prefixing removes the *catastrophic* errors from outlier tokens, but there remain *distributed* quantization errors from rounding and clipping across all activations. The block-wise fine-tuning minimizes mean squared error between full-precision and quantized block outputs, jointly optimizing weights and quantization parameters (clipping factors for dynamic quantization, scaling factors and zero-points for static quantization) to compensate for these residual errors. This is in line with prior work (Shao et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2024a) but adapted to PrefixQuant's specific needs — notably introducing trainable clipping factors for dynamic activation quantization, which previously lacked trainable parameters for block-wise optimization.

The paper's **two operating points** (PrefixQuant-O1 and PrefixQuant-O2, detailed in Table 1) reflect a deliberate design choice about the precision-efficiency tradeoff: O1 uses per-token dynamic quantization (matching prior work for fair comparison) while O2 pushes to per-tensor static quantization, which is more efficient (lower latency, simpler operator fusion) but historically less accurate. The paper's key claim is that by eliminating token-wise outliers, PrefixQuant makes static quantization competitive with or superior to prior dynamic quantization methods — a significant practical advance because static quantization enables ahead-of-time calibration and simpler deployment.

Finally, the paper explicitly positions its approach as requiring **no retraining and minimal calibration cost**. The detection of prefixed tokens uses only a small calibration dataset (the paper's experiments use 8 Pile samples) and completes in seconds. This contrasts sharply with training-based outlier mitigation (Sun et al., 2024; Bondarenko et al., 2024) and with the expensive grid search of QFeP and CushionCache. The speed comparison is stark: PrefixQuant's 12-second token detection versus CushionCache's 12-hour grid search for the same model — a **3600× speedup** in the preparation step — making PrefixQuant practical for rapid iteration and large-scale deployment where reprocessing every model variant would be prohibitive.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader orientation (approachable technical breakdown)

PrefixQuant is a **quantization system for LLM inference** that reduces activation and weight precision from 16-bit floating point to 4-bit or 8-bit integers while preserving model accuracy. It solves the problem that a tiny number of "outlier tokens" in the activation tensors — typically 1–4 tokens out of thousands — have values hundreds or thousands of times larger than normal tokens, and these extreme values dominate the quantization error budget, forcing the quantizer to accommodate a much wider range than necessary for the vast majority of tokens. The "shape" of the solution is elegantly simple: instead of trying to suppress or redistribute these outliers (which existing methods attempt with partial success), PrefixQuant **isolates them into predictable, precomputable positions** by prepending a small fixed set of tokens to every input sequence, then precomputes and caches their full-precision representations offline so they never pass through the quantization pipeline at inference time.

### 3.2 Big-picture architecture (diagram in words)

The PrefixQuant system consists of five major components that operate in sequence, with the first three being offline preprocessing steps and the last two being runtime modifications:

1. **Outlier Token Detector** — analyzes a small calibration dataset (8 samples from the Pile) to identify which tokens are responsible for outlier activations across all transformer layers. This component counts how many distinct outlier tokens exist in the model, what text they correspond to (e.g., ".\n" for Llama-2-7B), and verifies that the count is small and consistent.

2. **Prefixed Token KV Cache Precomputer** — takes the identified high-frequency outlier tokens, prepends them to a dummy input, runs them through the full-precision model once in a "prefilling" pass, and stores their key-value representations (`k'`, `v'` in Equation 4) for all layers. This cached KV pair is reused across all subsequent inference runs without recomputation.

3. **Quantization Parameter Initializer** — performs grid search over clipping factors (for dynamic quantization) or scaling factors and zero-points (for static quantization) on a per-layer or per-block basis, using 8 calibration samples and minimizing layer or block output MSE. This step produces the initial quantization parameters (step sizes, zero-points) before fine-tuning.

4. **Quantized Forward Pass with Prefixed KV** — at inference time, every input sequence is automatically prepended with the precomputed prefix tokens in the KV cache (via Equation 4). The self-attention mechanism attends to both the actual input tokens and the prefixed tokens, with the prefixed tokens absorbing the outlier-inducing attention patterns. The actual input tokens (now free of outliers) pass through quantized linear layers using per-token (O1) or per-tensor (O2) activation quantization.

5. **Block-wise Fine-Tuning Optimizer** — after the prefixing mechanism is in place, a block-wise training procedure minimizes the mean squared error between full-precision and quantized transformer block outputs on 512 Pile samples. This introduces trainable parameters for activation quantization — clipping factors for dynamic quantization (PrefixQuant-O1) and scaling factors/zero-points for static quantization (PrefixQuant-O2) — and trains them jointly with all weights and weight quantization parameters for 10–20 epochs.

Information flows through the system as follows: calibration data → token frequency analysis → outlier token selection → full-precision prefix KV precomputation → grid search initialization → block-wise fine-tuning → deployment with prefixed KV cache injection at every forward pass. The key property is that the prefix KV cache is computed once per model, is identical across all inputs, and operates in full precision even when the rest of the model is quantized.

#### 3.4.1 Formal Definition of Outlier Tokens and Detection

The paper defines an outlier token through a quantitative framework that compares each token's maximum activation magnitude to the median token's maximum activation magnitude across the sequence. This is a relative definition — a token is an outlier not because its absolute value exceeds some fixed threshold, but because it deviates dramatically from what is "normal" for that particular sequence and layer.

**Step 1: Compute the token-wise maximum magnitude.** For each activation tensor $X \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times C}$ — where $T$ is the number of tokens in the sequence and $C$ is the number of channels (hidden dimension size) — the paper first takes the absolute value of every element, then computes the maximum across channels for each token:

$$M_i = \max_{j \in \{1, \ldots, C\}} |X_{ij}|$$

where $M \in \mathbb{R}^T$ is a vector of length $T$, and each element $M_i$ is the maximum absolute activation value of the $i$-th token across all its channel dimensions.

**Step 2: Compare each token's maximum to the median token's maximum.** The outlier degree $R_i$ of token $i$ is then defined as:

$$R_i = \frac{M_i}{\text{median}(M)}$$

where $\text{median}(M)$ is the median of the token-wise maximum values across all $T$ tokens.

**What it computes:** $R_i$ measures how many times larger (or smaller) a given token's peak activation is compared to the "typical" peak activation in the sequence. If $R_i = 1$, the token is perfectly normal — its maximum value equals the median token's maximum. If $R_i = 100$, the token's maximum is 100 times the median maximum, indicating a severe upper outlier. If $R_i = 0.1$, so $R_i^{-1} = 10$, the token's maximum is one-tenth the median, indicating a lower outlier.

**Why this form:** using the median rather than the mean for the baseline is critical because the mean would itself be heavily influenced by extreme outliers. If the sequence has tokens with maximum values of $[1500, 0.5, 0.5, \ldots, 0.5]$, the mean maximum would be dominated by the 1500 value, making it an unreliable baseline for identifying which tokens are unusual. The median is robust to extreme values — no matter how large the outlier, the median of 2048 tokens remains anchored at a value representing the "typical" token. The ratio formulation also makes the definition independent of the absolute scale of activations, which varies across layers and models.

**Step 3: Classify a token as an upper or lower outlier.** A token is classified as an **upper outlier** if its ratio $R_i$ exceeds a threshold $\eta_1$, and as a **lower outlier** if its inverse ratio $R_i^{-1}$ exceeds a threshold $\eta_2$:

- Upper outlier: $R_i > \eta_1$
- Lower outlier: $R_i^{-1} > \eta_2$

The paper sets $\eta_1 = 64$ and $\eta_2 = 8$ in its experiments. These thresholds are deliberately conservative — they capture only tokens that are extremely deviant from the median, ensuring that the prefix set is small and that normal tokens with slightly above-average values are not incorrectly classified.

**Practical consequence of these thresholds:** For a token to be classified as an upper outlier, its maximum activation value must be at least 64 times larger than the median token's maximum. In Llama-2-7B's down projection input (Figure 3 I.a), the top token's maximum is 4,161 times the median — far exceeding the 64× threshold. For a token to be classified as a lower outlier, the median token's maximum must be at least 8 times larger than the token's maximum. In Llama-2-7B's K activations (Figure 3 III.a), the median-to-minimum ratio exceeds 9 — again exceeding the threshold.

**Global characterization across layers.** To visualize the overall severity of outliers across all transformer blocks, the paper defines two aggregate metrics:

$$\max\left(\frac{\text{top-1}}{\text{median}}\right) \quad \text{and} \quad \max\left(\frac{\text{median}}{\text{min-1}}\right)$$

where "top-1" is the largest token-wise maximum across all tokens in a layer, and "min-1" is the smallest token-wise maximum. The maximum over layers captures the worst-case outlier severity anywhere in the model. If any layer has an extreme upper outlier, $\max(\text{top-1}/\text{median})$ will be large even if most layers are well-behaved.

**The detection procedure in practice.** To determine the number of outlier tokens $o$ for a given model, PrefixQuant runs a small calibration dataset (8 Pile samples) through the full-precision model. For each transformer block, it computes the average number of tokens that exceed the $\eta_1$ threshold (for upper outliers) using Equation 3. This yields a vector $O \in \mathbb{R}^b$ where $b$ is the number of transformer blocks. The final count is $o = \lceil\max(O)\rceil$ — the ceiling of the maximum average count across layers. The ceiling operator ensures an integer count, and taking the maximum across layers ensures that the prefix set is large enough to handle the worst-case layer. The paper reports that "outlier tokens are nearly consistent across layers that contain them," so the per-block counts are similar, and a single $o$ suffices.

#### 3.4.2 Empirical Characterization of Outlier Tokens

Before designing the prefixing solution, the paper conducts a detailed empirical analysis of outlier token properties across multiple model families. This analysis reveals three key characteristics that make prefixing feasible.

**Characteristic 1: Outlier tokens are extremely scarce.** The paper computes the fraction of tokens in a sequence that are classified as outliers. For Llama-2-7B, Figure 4a shows the distribution: 96.8% of sequences have exactly 2 outlier tokens, 1.6% have 1 outlier token, and 1.6% have 3 outlier tokens. The total number of outlier tokens per sequence is effectively 2 for this model. Across all models studied, the number ranges from 1 to 4. This scarcity is what makes the prefixing approach viable — if hundreds of tokens were outliers, prefixing them all would create unacceptable overhead in the KV cache and would distort the input distribution too severely.

**Characteristic 2: Outlier tokens have predictable content but unpredictable positions.** When inspecting *which* tokens are outliers (excluding position 0, which is always an outlier regardless of content as discussed below), the paper finds a striking pattern: the content of outlier tokens is drawn from a tiny vocabulary of low-semantic tokens. For Llama-2-7B (Figure 4b), non-initial outlier tokens are either "." (71.4% of occurrences) or "\n" (28.6%). For Llama-2-13B (Figure 8a), the top outlier tokens are "the" (47.6%), "." (24.7%), "," (16.3%), and "\n" (7.3%). For Mistral-7B-v0.3 (Figure 8d), "\n" alone accounts for 64.3% of non-initial outlier occurrences. These are delimiter tokens, whitespace, or common function words — tokens that carry minimal semantic content but appear frequently.

However, the **positions** of these outlier tokens vary unpredictably across different input sequences. Figure 4c shows the distribution of outlier token positions in Llama-2-7B: position 0 accounts for 50%, positions 1–20 account for 30.5%, positions 21–40 account for 10.2%, and other positions account for 9.3%. The non-initial outliers are concentrated near the beginning of the sequence but at varying indices. This position-dependence means that one cannot simply designate certain sequence indices as "always keep in high precision" — the same index might contain a normal token in one input and an outlier token in another.

**Characteristic 3: The initial token is universally an outlier regardless of content.** Across all models studied (Llama-2, Llama-3, Llama-3-Instruct, Mistral, Qwen-2), the token at position 0 (the beginning-of-sequence marker, such as `<s>` for Llama-2 or `|begin_of_text|` for Llama-3) is consistently an outlier token. This finding aligns with prior work on attention sinks (Xiao et al., 2023b) — the initial token receives attention from all subsequent tokens because the causal attention mask allows it, and this accumulated attention produces large activations. Crucially, this is a structural property, not a content-dependent one: the initial token is an outlier regardless of what text it represents, because its position in the sequence dictates its attention pattern.

**Characteristic 4: Outlier tokens exhibit both upper and lower extremes.** The paper identifies two distinct outlier patterns in different parts of the transformer. Upper outliers (tokens with $R_i \gg 1$) appear in the inputs to the down-projection linear layers and in the outputs of entire transformer blocks. Figure 3(I.a) shows that the down-projection input in Llama-2-7B has $\max(\text{top-1}/\text{median}) = 4161$, meaning the most extreme token's maximum value is over 4000 times the median token's maximum. Lower outliers (tokens with $R_i^{-1} \gg 1$) appear in the queries (Q) and keys (K) of self-attention. Figure 3(III.a) shows that in K, $\max(\text{median}/\text{min-1}) > 9$, meaning some tokens have maximum values less than one-ninth of the median maximum. These lower outliers are particularly insidious because Hadamard rotation — which redistributes values across channels to reduce upper outliers — does nothing to help with tokens whose magnitudes are too small (rotating small values produces small values in other channels). Figure 3(III.b) confirms that the $\max(\text{median}/\text{min-1})$ ratio remains above 9 after rotation.

**Characteristic 5: The same token can be an outlier at one position but not at another.** The paper notes that "low-semantic tokens may behave as outlier tokens at the front of the sequence but appear as normal tokens in other positions." This suggests that outlier status is primarily a function of position (and the attention patterns that position creates) rather than token identity per se. However, because certain low-semantic tokens disproportionately occur early in sequences (delimiters, whitespace near the beginning), they are the tokens most likely to land at outlier positions. This distinction is important for the prefixing strategy: prefixing the *content* of outlier tokens works because that content tends to produce outlier behavior at the beginning of sequences, which is exactly where the prefixed tokens are placed.

**Characteristic 6: Some models only have initial-token outliers.** Llama-3-8B and Qwen-2-7B are special cases where "outlier tokens only appear as initial tokens" (Section 4.2). For these models, no delimiter or function word tokens exhibit outlier behavior beyond the initial position. This simplifies the prefixing strategy — only a single `[BOS]` token needs to be prefixed — and it explains why PrefixQuant works well even with just one prefixed token for these models (as shown in Table 5, the prefixed token for Llama-3-8B is simply `[BOS]`).

#### 3.4.3 The Prefixing Mechanism: Selecting, Inserting, and Caching Prefixed Tokens

The prefixing mechanism is the core technical innovation of PrefixQuant. It transforms token-wise outliers from an input-dependent problem that cannot be handled at quantization time into a fixed, precomputable cost that can be absorbed by the full-precision KV cache.

**Step 1: Determine the number of prefixed tokens.** Using the detection procedure from Section 3.4.1, PrefixQuant computes $o$, the maximum average number of upper outlier tokens across all transformer blocks. The number of prefixed tokens is then set to $o + 1$: the $o$ high-frequency outlier tokens plus an additional `[BOS]` token (the beginning-of-sequence marker for that model). The paper justifies this $+1$ by noting that "for consistency, we also include `[BOS]` as the last prefixed token for all models," and that in special cases where outlier tokens only appear as initial tokens, "we set the prefix token to `[BOS]`."

Table 5 in the paper shows the concrete prefixed tokens for each model:

- **Llama-2-7B:** 3 prefixed tokens: `. \n [BOS]` (the high-frequency outliers ".\n" plus the beginning-of-sequence marker)
- **Llama-2-13B:** 3 prefixed tokens: `the . [BOS]`
- **Llama-2-70B:** 4 prefixed tokens: `\n " [BOS]`
- **Llama-3-8B(-Instruct):** 1 prefixed token: `[BOS]` (outlier tokens only at initial position)
- **Llama-3-70B(-Instruct):** 3 prefixed tokens: `, [BOS]` (the comma and space before the BOS marker)
- **Mistral-v0.3-7B:** 4 prefixed tokens: `\n . to [BOS]`
- **Qwen-2-7B:** 1 prefixed token: `[BOS]` (outlier tokens only at initial position)

**Step 2: Compute the full-precision KV cache for the prefixed tokens.** The prefixed tokens are run through the full-precision (FP16) model in a one-time "prefilling" pass with no other input. This produces key vectors $k' \in \mathbb{R}^{o \times C}$ and value vectors $v' \in \mathbb{R}^{o \times C}$ for each transformer layer, where $o$ is the number of prefixed tokens and $C$ is the per-head dimension (or the full hidden dimension, depending on the KV cache organization). These $k'$ and $v'$ are stored and reused for all subsequent inference runs. The critical property is that $k'$ and $v'$ remain in **full precision** (FP16) even when used with quantized models — they are never passed through the activation quantizer.

**Step 3: Modify the self-attention computation at inference time.** The standard self-attention with causal masking is:

$$\text{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \text{Softmax}\left(\frac{Q K^T}{\sqrt{d}}\right) V$$

where $Q, K, V \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times C}$ are the query, key, and value matrices for the actual input tokens, $T$ is the input sequence length, $C$ is the per-head dimension, and $d = C$ is the scaling factor.

PrefixQuant modifies this to incorporate the prefixed KV cache:

$$\text{Attention}(Q, K, V; k', v') = \text{Softmax}\left(\frac{Q \begin{bmatrix} K^T & k' \end{bmatrix}}{\sqrt{d}}\right) \begin{bmatrix} V \\ v'^T \end{bmatrix}$$

where $k', v' \in \mathbb{R}^{o \times C}$ are the prefixed tokens' key and value representations.

**What this equation computes:** the query vectors $Q$ attend not only to the keys of the actual input tokens $K$, but also to the keys of the prefixed tokens $k'$. The concatenated key matrix $\begin{bmatrix} K^T & k' \end{bmatrix}$ has dimensions $d \times (T + o)$, meaning each query position can attend to the $T$ input key positions plus the $o$ prefixed key positions. The attention weights are computed via the SoftMax over this expanded set of keys, and the output is a weighted sum of the corresponding values $\begin{bmatrix} V \\ v'^T \end{bmatrix}$ from both input and prefixed tokens. The prefixed tokens are treated as preceding the input sequence — their keys are concatenated to the right of $K^T$, and their values are stacked below $V$ — which means in the causal attention ordering they effectively appear at positions before the input tokens.

**Why this works:** the outlier-inducing attention mechanism (SoftMax preventing zero scores) now directs its unavoidable non-zero attention mass to the prefixed tokens, which are present in every input and are designed to accept this attention. The actual input tokens no longer need to serve as attention sinks for the initial positions, because the prefixed tokens have taken over that role. This means that the actual input tokens' activation values no longer get amplified by the outlier feedback loop — the outliers are constrained to the prefixed positions.

**Why this is efficient:** the prefixed KV cache is precomputed once per model and reused across all inference runs. It adds only $o$ additional key-value pairs to the attention computation (where $o \leq 4$ for all models studied), which is a negligible overhead compared to typical sequence lengths of 2048–8192 tokens. The prefixed tokens do not need to be quantized because they are stored in full precision, and the input tokens — now free of outliers — can be quantized much more aggressively without catastrophic error.

**Step 4: Verify that outlier tokens are now constrained to prefixed positions.** Figure 4d shows the position distribution of outlier tokens after prefixing in Llama-2-7B: 50% occur at position 0 (the initial prefixed token) and 50% occur at position 1 (the second prefixed token), with zero outlier tokens elsewhere in the sequence. This confirms that the prefixing strategy successfully constrains outlier behavior to the prefixed positions. The mechanism is self-enforcing: because the prefixed tokens are always present at the beginning of the sequence and are the first tokens in the causal ordering, they absorb the attention patterns that would otherwise create outliers at variable positions in the input.

**A subtle design choice: the ordering of prefixed tokens.** The paper places the `[BOS]` token as the *last* prefixed token (not the first). This means the actual sequence begins with the high-frequency outlier tokens (e.g., `. \n`), followed by `[BOS]`, followed by the original input. This ordering ensures that the `[BOS]` token is the one that immediately precedes the user's actual input, which may be important for some models where `[BOS]` has special semantic significance. The paper does not ablate this ordering choice, but the consistent performance across models suggests it is not highly sensitive.

#### 3.4.4 Effect of Prefixing on Activation Distributions and Quantization Error

The paper provides extensive quantitative evidence that prefixing dramatically improves activation distributions for quantization.

**Quantization error improvement (Figure 2).** The headline result is the reduction in 4-bit per-token dynamic quantization error on Llama-2-7B's second transformer block output, measured via mean squared error (MSE):

- **Original (no rotation, no prefixing):** MSE = 54.63. The distribution (Figure 2a) shows extreme values exceeding 1,500, with a few outlier tokens dominating the range.
- **With Hadamard rotation (QuaRot):** MSE = 7.88. The distribution (Figure 2b) is more uniform, with outliers reduced to approximately 15, but still much larger than normal tokens (values ~0.1–0.5).
- **With PrefixQuant (rotation + prefixed tokens):** MSE = 0.04. The distribution (Figure 2c) shows all values below 0.07, with no visible outliers — a uniform, well-behaved range.

This is a **~197× error reduction** over Hadamard rotation alone (7.88 → 0.04). While this is a single-layer measurement, it illustrates the mechanism's fundamental effectiveness: removing the outlier tokens from the quantization process eliminates the dominant source of error.

**Token-wise maximum ratio improvement (Figure 3).** Across all models studied, prefixing dramatically reduces the $\max(\text{top-1}/\text{median})$ and $\max(\text{median}/\text{min-1})$ ratios:

- **Down projection inputs (upper outliers):** In Llama-2-7B, the $\max(\text{top-1}/\text{median})$ ratio drops from 476 (with rotation only) to 2.7 (with PrefixQuant). This means the most extreme token is only 2.7× the median, versus 476× before — a ~176× improvement in uniformity. Similar improvements appear across models: Llama-2-13B drops from 90.4 to 3.1, Llama-3-8B from 150 to 3.9, Llama-3-70B from 236 to 3.3, Mistral-7B-v0.3 from 486 to 5.3, and Qwen-2-7B from 137 to 4.3.

- **Self-attention K (lower outliers):** In Llama-2-7B, the $\max(\text{median}/\text{min-1})$ ratio drops from >9 (with rotation only, unchanged from original) to 3.3 (with PrefixQuant). This is particularly significant because rotation alone did nothing for these lower outliers — the prefixing mechanism is the first approach to address them.

- **Self-attention Q (lower outliers):** Similar improvements in Q across all models. In Llama-2-7B, $\max(\text{median}/\text{min-1})$ drops from >9 to 2.2.

**Ablation evidence for the interaction with rotation (Table 5).** The progressive ablation in Table 5 on Llama-3-8B W4A4KV4 quantifies the contribution of each component:

- **Base RTN (round-to-nearest with no optimization):** PPL = 1282.34 — essentially broken.
- **+ Hadamard rotation:** PPL = 24.98 — rotation alone makes quantization barely functional.
- **+ Grid search initialization:** PPL = 11.70 — optimizing clipping factors helps significantly, but outliers still limit performance.
- **+ Prefixed outliers (PrefixQuant-O1 without fine-tuning):** PPL = 7.53 — a 4.17 PPL improvement from prefixing alone, confirming that outlier elimination is the primary driver.
- **+ Block-wise fine-tuning:** PPL = 7.41 (O1) — further modest improvement from residual error compensation.

For **static quantization (PrefixQuant-O2)**, the pattern is even more dramatic:

- **+ Rotation + grid search with static quantization:** PPL = 141.02 — static quantization is nearly unusable with outliers present, because a single per-tensor scaling factor cannot accommodate the extreme range.
- **+ Prefixed outliers (without fine-tuning):** PPL = 7.93 — a **133 PPL improvement** from prefixing alone, demonstrating that eliminating token-wise outliers is what makes per-tensor static quantization viable.
- **+ Block-wise fine-tuning:** PPL = 7.41 — further improvement to near dynamic quantization levels.

This ablation is the clearest evidence that token-wise outliers — not channel-wise outliers, not quantization scheme granularity — are the fundamental bottleneck for low-bit activation quantization. Rotation handles channel-wise uniformity; prefixing handles token-wise uniformity; both are necessary, and neither suffices alone.

**Why static quantization benefits disproportionately.** Static quantization computes a single scaling factor $s$ and zero-point $z$ for an entire tensor (all tokens in all sequences that will be seen at inference time). If even one token in the calibration data has extreme values, the scaling factor must accommodate it, which means the step size $s$ becomes large, which means rounding error for all normal tokens increases. By constraining outliers to prefixed tokens that never pass through the activation quantizer, PrefixQuant allows static quantization to calibrate on a distribution that is uniformly well-behaved, recovering accuracy competitive with dynamic per-token quantization — a result the paper claims is the first of its kind.

#### 3.4.5 Block-wise Fine-Tuning with Trainable Activation Quantization Parameters

After the prefixing mechanism eliminates the catastrophic errors from outlier tokens, residual quantization errors remain from rounding and clipping across all activations and weights. Block-wise fine-tuning compensates for these residual errors by jointly optimizing the full-precision weights, weight quantization parameters, and — crucially — newly introduced trainable activation quantization parameters using a mean squared error (MSE) loss at the block output level.

**Block-wise training architecture.** The fine-tuning operates on each transformer block sequentially, from the first block to the last. For each block, the training objective is:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{block}} = \| \text{Block}_{\text{FP}}(X) - \text{Block}_{\text{Quant}}(X) \|_2^2$$

where $\text{Block}_{\text{FP}}(X)$ is the output of the full-precision transformer block given input $X$, and $\text{Block}_{\text{Quant}}(X)$ is the output of the same block with quantized weights and activations. The input $X$ to each block comes from the preceding block's full-precision output (not the quantized output), following the standard block reconstruction paradigm (Li et al., 2021; Shao et al., 2023).

**What this loss computes:** the element-wise squared difference between the full-precision and quantized block outputs, summed over all elements in the output tensor. This is a per-block reconstruction loss that encourages the quantized block to produce outputs that are numerically similar to the full-precision block's outputs, without requiring end-to-end training or access to task-specific labels.

**Why block-wise rather than end-to-end:** block-wise training is substantially more memory-efficient because only one transformer block's parameters need to be in GPU memory at a time. It also avoids the compounding of quantization errors across layers — each block is optimized to match its own full-precision output, so errors in earlier blocks don't propagate to later blocks' training objectives. Prior work (Shao et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2024a) established that block-wise reconstruction is effective for LLM quantization, and PrefixQuant follows this established paradigm.

**Trainable parameters for weight quantization.** Following EfficientQAT (Chen et al., 2024a), PrefixQuant makes all full-precision weights and all weight quantization parameters trainable during block-wise fine-tuning. The weight quantization parameters include the per-channel scaling factors and zero-points (for per-channel weight quantization, which PrefixQuant uses in both O1 and O2, as shown in Table 1). The full-precision weights are updated via gradient descent and periodically re-quantized — this is the standard quantization-aware training (QAT) approach.

**Trainable parameters for dynamic activation quantization (PrefixQuant-O1).** Dynamic quantization computes scaling factors and zero-points on-the-fly during inference from the actual activation statistics, so there are no fixed quantization parameters to train. To enable block-wise optimization of the dynamic quantizer, PrefixQuant introduces **trainable tensor-wise clipping factors** $\gamma$ and $\beta$:

$$s = \frac{\gamma \max(X) - \beta \min(X)}{2^N - 1}, \quad z = -\left\lfloor \frac{\beta \min(X)}{s} \right\rfloor$$

where $X$ is the activation tensor for a single token, $\max(X)$ and $\min(X)$ are computed dynamically per token, $N$ is the target bit-width (4 or 8), and $\gamma, \beta \in [0, 1]$ are learnable clipping factors shared across all tokens in the layer. During fine-tuning, $\gamma$ and $\beta$ are trained via gradient descent (the straight-through estimator handles the rounding operation), while $\max(X)$ and $\min(X)$ continue to be computed per-token from the actual activations.

**What these clipping factors do:** $\gamma$ controls what fraction of the maximum value to use as the upper bound of the quantization range, and $\beta$ controls what fraction of the minimum value to use as the lower bound. If $\gamma = 1$ and $\beta = 1$, the quantizer uses the full min-max range. If $\gamma = 0.9$, the quantizer clips the top 10% of the activation range, trading larger clipping error for smaller rounding error (because the step size $s$ can be smaller). The optimal balance between clipping and rounding error depends on the activation distribution and the target bit-width, and making $\gamma$ and $\beta$ trainable allows the block-wise optimizer to find this balance automatically.

**Why the clipping factors cannot be token-wise:** the paper explicitly notes that "the clipping factors cannot be token-wise, as long-context scenarios introduce excessive storage overhead with token-wise clipping factors." If clipping factors were per-token, a sequence of length 8192 would require storing 8192 separate $\gamma$ and $\beta$ values per layer, which would defeat the memory savings of quantization. Per-token dynamic quantization already adapts to each token's range via the online $\max(X)$ and $\min(X)$ computation; the trainable $\gamma$ and $\beta$ provide a per-layer adjustment to that adaptation.

**Trainable parameters for static activation quantization (PrefixQuant-O2).** For static (per-tensor) quantization, the scaling factor $s$ and zero-point $z$ are fixed after calibration and used for all inputs at inference time. These are inherently trainable during block-wise fine-tuning — the optimizer can directly adjust $s$ and $z$ to minimize reconstruction error. The paper notes that "for per-tensor static quantization, we directly perform a grid search for the quantization parameters $s$ and $z$ instead of optimizing the clipping factors" during initialization, and then these $s$ and $z$ become trainable during fine-tuning.

**Grid search initialization.** Before block-wise fine-tuning, PrefixQuant initializes the quantization parameters via grid search on 8 Pile samples with sequence length 1024. The objective is to minimize MSE between the original and quantized outputs:

- For per-channel and per-head quantization (weight and KV cache): minimize the layer output MSE.
- For per-tensor quantization (static activations in PrefixQuant-O2): minimize the block output MSE.

The grid search iterates over candidate clipping factors (for per-token dynamic) or candidate scaling factors and zero-points (for per-tensor static) and selects the values that produce the lowest MSE. This initialization provides a much better starting point than min-max initialization (where $\gamma = \beta = 1$), which would produce large step sizes dominated by outlier values.

**Training hyperparameters.** The paper specifies the following configuration:

- **Training data:** 512 samples from the Pile dataset, each with 1024-token sequence length. Total training tokens: $512 \times 1024 = 524,288$.
- **Learning rates:** $5 \times 10^{-5}$ for quantization parameters (step sizes, clipping factors), $5 \times 10^{-6}$ for full-precision weights. For Llama-3-70B(-Instruct), both rates are reduced to $2 \times 10^{-5}$ and $2 \times 10^{-6}$, respectively — a sensible reduction for the larger model where gradient magnitudes are naturally larger.
- **Batch size:** 4 sequences per batch.
- **Training epochs:** 10 epochs for W4A8KV4, 20 epochs for W4A4KV4. The lower precision requires more training because the quantization error surface is more complex and requires more iterations to converge. Table 8 confirms that 10 epochs suffice for W4A8KV4 (PPL plateaus at 10 epochs) and 20 for W4A4KV4 (PPL plateaus after 20 epochs for O2).

**Why Pile dataset for calibration and fine-tuning:** the paper follows prior work (QoQ, Lin et al., 2024b) in using the Pile dataset rather than task-specific data to avoid overfitting to evaluation benchmarks. The ablation in Table 7a confirms that Pile outperforms C4 (PPL 7.60) and RedPajama (PPL 7.49) as the fine-tuning dataset. Table 7b shows that a sequence length of 1024 outperforms both longer (2048) and shorter (512) lengths, likely because 1024 provides sufficient context for the block-wise reconstruction while keeping training samples diverse. Table 7c shows diminishing returns beyond 512 samples — the paper notes that "lower precision generally requires more training data," citing EfficientQAT's use of $4096 \times 2048$ tokens for W2A16, while PrefixQuant needs only $512 \times 1024$ for W4A4 — a practical efficiency advantage.

#### 3.4.6 The Two Operating Modes: PrefixQuant-O1 and PrefixQuant-O2

PrefixQuant is presented in two configurations (Table 1), which represent different points on the accuracy-vs-efficiency Pareto frontier. Both share the same prefixing mechanism and block-wise fine-tuning, but differ in activation and KV cache quantization granularity.

**PrefixQuant-O1: dynamic quantization for fair comparison.** This mode uses:
- **Weight quantization:** per-channel (the standard for most quantization methods)
- **Activation quantization:** per-token dynamic
- **KV cache quantization:** group-wise dynamic with group size 128

This configuration matches the quantization granularities used by QuaRot, SpinQuant, QoQ, and other baselines, enabling direct apples-to-apples comparison. Per-token dynamic quantization computes scaling factors $s$ and zero-points $z$ online for each token during inference — this adapts to the varying statistics of different tokens but requires additional computation at runtime.

**PrefixQuant-O2: static quantization for maximum efficiency.** This mode uses:
- **Weight quantization:** per-channel (unchanged)
- **Activation quantization:** per-tensor static
- **KV cache quantization:** per-head static

This is coarser than O1 (and coarser than all baselines): per-tensor static activation quantization uses a single scaling factor for all tokens in all sequences, and per-head static KV cache quantization uses a single scaling factor per attention head. Static quantization is more efficient because:
1. No online computation of $\max(X)$ and $\min(X)$ is needed — $s$ and $z$ are precomputed.
2. Operator fusion is more feasible — the quantize-dequantize operations can be fused with the preceding layer normalization.
3. Less memory is needed for storing quantization parameters (one scalar per tensor vs. one scalar per token).

**The paper's key claim about O2:** "PrefixQuant is the first method to surpass prior per-token dynamic quantization methods using the coarser per-tensor static quantization." This is significant because it means PrefixQuant's outlier elimination is so effective that it enables a cheaper quantization scheme to outperform more expensive schemes from prior work. In Table 2 (W4A4KV4), PrefixQuant-O2 (static) achieves 66.37 average accuracy vs. QuaRot's 64.69 (dynamic) on Llama-2-7B, and 71.08 vs. DuQuant's 67.13 on Llama-3-8B. In Table 3 (W4A8KV4), PrefixQuant-O2 achieves 72.46 accuracy on Llama-3-8B, surpassing QoQ's 71.35 (both are within margin, but the static O2 is matching or exceeding the dynamic baseline).

**Why static quantization works after prefixing but not before.** The dramatic difference between pre-prefix and post-prefix static quantization performance (PPL 141.02 → 7.93 in Table 5, W4A4KV4) can be understood through the quantization error formula. Per-tensor static quantization computes:

$$s = \frac{\gamma \max(\tilde{X}) - \beta \min(\tilde{X})}{2^N - 1}$$

where $\max(\tilde{X})$ and $\min(\tilde{X})$ are the maximum and minimum values observed over the entire calibration dataset for that tensor. Without prefixing, the calibration dataset includes outlier tokens with values 100–4000× larger than normal tokens, so $\max(\tilde{X})$ is enormous, making $s$ enormous, resulting in coarse quantization that loses most of the precision for normal tokens. With prefixing, the outlier tokens never appear in the quantized activations, so $\max(\tilde{X})$ reflects only the well-behaved normal token distribution, making $s$ appropriately small and enabling fine-grained quantization for the tokens that actually pass through the quantizer.

**KV cache quantization granularity.** Both O1 and O2 quantize the KV cache to 4 bits (KV4), but at different granularities: group-wise (O1) vs. per-head (O2). Since the prefixed tokens' KV representations are stored in full precision and never quantized, the KV cache quantization only affects the actual input tokens' keys and values. The paper's results show that per-head static KV quantization is sufficient when token-wise outliers are eliminated, because the remaining KV values are well-behaved enough for coarse quantization to work.

#### 3.4.7 Summary of Design Choices and Their Justifications

**Why prefix tokens instead of pruning or ignoring outlier positions:** The SoftMax attention mechanism *requires* that every position attends to every preceding position with non-zero weight. There is no architectural mechanism for a token to decide "I will pay zero attention to that delimiter token." The outlier tokens are structural consequences of the attention pattern, not noise that can be filtered. Prefixing works *with* this constraint: it provides designated "attention sink" tokens that are always present, absorbing the forced non-zero attention and protecting the actual input tokens from outlier amplification.

**Why detect prefix tokens via frequency analysis rather than grid search:** The grid search used by QFeP and CushionCache would need to test combinations of candidate tokens to find which set best absorbs outliers, which is combinatorially expensive (12 hours for Llama-3-8B). Frequency analysis leverages the empirical observation that the tokens causing outliers are consistently the same high-frequency low-semantic tokens across inputs — they are outliers precisely because they appear often in positions that receive broad attention. A simple frequency count on a small calibration set identifies these tokens in seconds. The paper's ablation (Table 10) confirms that the top-N high-frequency approach significantly outperforms random selection (PPL 6.22 vs. 66.51 for Llama-2-7B without fine-tuning), validating that frequency is the right selection criterion.

**Why block-wise fine-tuning rather than end-to-end training:** Block-wise training is standard in the post-training quantization literature because it is far more memory-efficient (one transformer block at a time vs. the entire model), requires less data (512 samples suffice vs. the massive datasets needed for end-to-end QAT), and avoids the well-known instability of end-to-end QAT for LLMs. The sequential nature (training block 1, then using its outputs to train block 2, etc.) also mirrors the forward pass, so reconstruction at each block approximates end-to-end reconstruction.

**Why trainable clipping factors for dynamic quantization:** Without trainable parameters in the activation quantizer, block-wise fine-tuning can only optimize weights and weight quantization parameters, leaving the activation quantizer's behavior fixed after grid search initialization. By introducing trainable $\gamma$ and $\beta$, the optimizer can learn per-layer tradeoffs between clipping error (from narrowing the quantization range) and rounding error (from having too coarse a step size). This is particularly important at W4A4, where the quantization error surface is complex and sensitive to the clipping range.

#### 3.4.8 Edge Cases: When Outlier Tokens Vary by Model

The paper's handling of model-specific variation in outlier patterns is worth examining, as it reveals how general the prefixing approach is and where it might need adaptation.

**Models where outlier tokens are only initial tokens (Llama-3-8B, Qwen-2-7B).** For these models, the prefix set is simply `[BOS]`. This is the minimum possible prefix (1 token) and adds essentially zero overhead. The fact that these models have no delimiter/filler outliers suggests that their attention patterns are more concentrated on the initial token, perhaps due to differences in pretraining data or architecture. The prefixing mechanism still works because the `[BOS]` token serves the attention sink role.

**Models where outlier tokens are highly varied in content (Mistral-7B-v0.3).** Mistral-7B-v0.3 has four prefix tokens: `\n . to [BOS]`, with `\n` accounting for 64.3% of non-initial outlier occurrences. The inclusion of `to` (an actual word rather than pure delimiter/punctuation) is interesting — it suggests that in Mistral's pretraining distribution, the word "to" frequently appears in positions that receive broad attention (perhaps as an infinitive marker in instructional or procedural text). This doesn't affect the mechanism; the prefix tokens are still determined by frequency analysis, and the KV cache precomputation works identically regardless of token semantics.

**Models with instruction tuning (Llama-3-8B-Instruct, Llama-3-70B-Instruct).** The instruct-tuned models use the same prefix tokens as their base counterparts (Table 15 shows results on instruct models). This suggests that instruction tuning does not fundamentally change the outlier token patterns — the structural attention properties of the transformer architecture dominate over the fine-tuning objective. This is an important practical property: PrefixQuant can be applied to instruction-tuned models without re-analyzing outlier patterns if they are known for the base model.

**What happens if a new outlier token appears that wasn't in the calibration set:** the paper does not directly address this, but the mechanism suggests it would be handled gracefully. If an unprefixed token in a novel input happens to have outlier-scale activations, it would cause larger quantization error for that specific token (and potentially for its layer's outputs). However, because PrefixQuant's two operating modes both use per-token dynamic (O1) or per-tensor static (O2) quantization that adapts to the tensor's (or token's) actual range, the effect would be localized to that token rather than propagating to the entire sequence. The risk is higher for O2 (static), where a single unexpected outlier could exceed the calibrated range, causing clipping — but the paper's strong results on diverse benchmarks (WikiText2, zero-shot tasks, MMLU) suggest this is rare in practice.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Token-Wise Outliers Are a Diagnostically Distinct — Not Just a Secondary — Problem from Channel-Wise Outliers

The dominant conceptual framework in the LLM quantization literature, crystallized through a progression of increasingly sophisticated channel-wise methods (SmoothQuant, OmniQuant, QuaRot, SpinQuant), treats activation outliers as fundamentally a *per-channel* phenomenon: the problem is that certain feature dimensions consistently produce large values across many tokens. The solution space — mixed-precision isolation of outlier channels, channel-wise scaling, Hadamard rotation — all follow from this framing. Token-wise outliers were either treated as a side effect that channel-wise methods would incidentally address, or were conflated with the broader "massive activations" problem studied primarily for its impact on long-context generation rather than quantization.

PrefixQuant's first conceptual contribution is to establish that token-wise outliers are a **diagnostically distinct failure mode** that survives even when channel-wise outliers are fully resolved, and that treating them as such requires a fundamentally different intervention strategy. The diagnostic evidence is Figure 2(b): after Hadamard rotation (the most aggressive channel-wise method available) has reduced channel-wise variation to near-uniformity, the token-wise maximums still span a ~30–150× range between outlier and normal tokens, producing a quantization error of 7.88 MSE. This residual error is not a refinement problem — it is a *different kind of problem* that channel-wise methods are architecturally incapable of addressing because they operate within tokens (redistributing across channels) rather than between tokens.

The distinction matters because it reframes what counts as "solving" the outlier problem. Prior work that reported success with rotation-based methods implicitly treated the residual token-wise variation as acceptable noise. PrefixQuant's authors demonstrate that this residual is not noise — it accounts for 94.7% of quantization error in the unrotated case (Figure 1) and remains the dominant error source after rotation — and that eliminating it produces a qualitatively different quantization regime where even coarse per-tensor static quantization becomes viable (PPL dropping from 141.02 to 7.93 in Table 5). This is a **reframing contribution**: the paper argues that the field's conceptual model of what constitutes an "outlier" and what it means to "handle" outliers was incomplete, and that token-wise outliers are not a secondary concern to be cleaned up after channel-wise methods but a co-equal problem requiring its own dedicated mechanism.

The paper also identifies that token-wise outliers manifest in **two distinct patterns** — upper outliers in feed-forward layers and lower outliers in attention queries/keys — and that rotation is ineffective against the latter (Figure 3 III.b). This diagnostic nuance was absent from prior work, which focused exclusively on large-value spikes. By showing that the $\max(\text{median}/\text{min-1})$ ratio in K activations exceeds 9 and is unaffected by rotation, the paper expands the definition of what constitutes a quantization-relevant outlier beyond the obvious large-value case, which has practical consequences: lower outliers in Q/K can cause information loss in the attention computation that propagates through the model even if the feed-forward activations are well-behaved.

---

### Innovation 2: Isolation as a Strategy — Working With Outliers Rather Than Eliminating Them

The prior approaches to token-wise outliers can be classified into two strategies: **suppression** (modify the model's attention mechanism during training to prevent outliers from forming — Bondarenko et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2024) and **redistribution** (apply transformations that spread outlier values across channels or dimensions — which is the channel-wise approach and doesn't address the token-wise case). Both strategies share an implicit assumption: the goal is to *make the outliers go away* — to produce activation distributions that are uniformly well-behaved so that quantization works cleanly.

PrefixQuant makes a fundamentally different strategic move: **isolation rather than elimination**. The paper's key observation is that token-wise outliers are structurally inevitable given the SoftMax attention mechanism (which cannot produce zero attention scores), so trying to eliminate them entirely is fighting the architecture. Instead, PrefixQuant accepts that outliers will exist but **confines them to known, precomputable positions** — the prefixed tokens in the KV cache — where they never pass through the activation quantizer and therefore do not affect quantization error.

This strategic shift has several non-obvious advantages that go beyond the immediate performance improvement:

**Training-free deployment with zero architectural modification.** Suppression-based methods require retraining the model to change the attention mechanism, which demands access to training pipelines and data, produces a modified model whose behavior may deviate from the well-characterized original, and is computationally expensive. PrefixQuant requires no model modification whatsoever — the prefix tokens are injected externally through the KV cache, and the base model's weights and architecture remain untouched. This means PrefixQuant can be applied to any pretrained model immediately, without retraining, and the resulting quantized model benefits from all the validation and safety testing done on the original.

**Computational cost of outlier handling shifts from per-input to one-time.** Grid-search-based prefixing methods (QFeP, CushionCache) treat the selection of prefix tokens as an optimization problem to be solved, which is expensive (12 hours for Llama-3-8B) but produces a reusable solution. PrefixQuant's frequency-analysis approach treats it as a *detection* problem, which is cheap (12 seconds). But the deeper strategic point is that even the expensive grid search methods share PrefixQuant's isolation philosophy — they differ only in the efficiency of finding the isolation targets, not in the core strategy. PrefixQuant's contribution here is demonstrating that the isolation targets can be identified through simple empirical analysis rather than combinatorial search, making the isolation strategy practical at scale — a 3600× speedup in the preparation step transforms the approach from a research curiosity to a deployable technique.

**Full-precision KV cache for outlier tokens as a form of implicit mixed-precision.** The prefixed tokens are stored in full precision (FP16) in the KV cache and never quantized, while the actual input tokens use 4-bit representations. This is effectively a mixed-precision strategy, but one that is applied at the token level rather than the channel level. Unlike channel-wise mixed precision (Dettmers et al., 2022), which requires custom kernels to handle the 16-bit/8-bit split within matrix multiplications, PrefixQuant's token-level mixed precision is architecturally clean: the full-precision prefixed KV cache participates in attention normally, while the quantized input tokens flow through quantized linear layers. There is no need for custom mixed-precision matrix multiplication kernels — the boundary between high and low precision is at the attention-KV boundary, not inside the matmul.

**The isolation strategy scales with sequence length.** As the input sequence grows longer, the number of outlier tokens stays constant (1–4), so the overhead of the prefixed KV cache becomes an increasingly negligible fraction of the total computation. For a 2048-token sequence, 3 prefixed tokens add 0.15% overhead; for an 8192-token sequence, it drops to 0.04%. This is in contrast to per-token dynamic quantization methods, where every token incurs the cost of online min-max computation, and static quantization with outlier-sensitive calibration, where extreme values from even one token affect the quantization range for all tokens.

### Innovation 3: Static Quantization Can Match or Exceed Dynamic Quantization When Token-Wise Outliers Are Eliminated

The LLM quantization field has operated under a well-established hierarchy: per-tensor static quantization (cheapest, least accurate) < per-token dynamic quantization (moderate cost, better accuracy) < group-wise dynamic quantization (highest cost, best accuracy). This hierarchy is not merely empirical — it follows from the quantization error model: when activation distributions vary significantly across tokens, a single per-tensor scaling factor cannot represent all tokens well, so adaptive per-token scaling is necessary. Prior to PrefixQuant, no method had demonstrated that static per-tensor quantization could be competitive with dynamic per-token quantization for low-bit (4-bit) LLM activations — the accuracy gap was simply too large.

PrefixQuant's results fundamentally challenge this hierarchy. The paper claims — and provides evidence for — being "the first method to surpass prior per-token dynamic quantization methods using the coarser per-tensor static quantization." The evidence:

- In W4A4KV4 on Llama-3-8B (Table 2), PrefixQuant-O2 (static) achieves 71.08 average zero-shot accuracy vs. DuQuant's 67.13 (dynamic) and QuaRot's 65.15 (dynamic). It even surpasses SpinQuant's 68.23 (dynamic) by 2.85 points.
- In W4A4KV4 on Llama-2-13B (Table 2), PrefixQuant-O2 achieves 70.36 vs. DuQuant's 69.13 (dynamic) and QuaRot's 69.01 (dynamic).
- In W4A8KV4 on Llama-3-8B (Table 3), PrefixQuant-O2 achieves 72.46 vs. QoQ's 71.35 (dynamic).

What makes this intellectually significant — beyond the raw numbers — is that it reveals the **true bottleneck** for static quantization accuracy was not the coarseness of the quantization granularity per se, but the presence of token-wise outliers that forced the per-tensor scaling factor to accommodate an enormous range. When those outliers are removed via prefixing, the remaining activation distribution is uniform enough that a single per-tensor scaling factor works well — the per-token adaptation that dynamic quantization provides is largely compensating for outlier tokens, not for inherent variability in normal token distributions. This is a **conceptual reframing**: the hierarchy of quantization granularities is not absolute but is contingent on the presence of outliers. Eliminate the outliers, and the hierarchy collapses.

The practical implications are substantial. Static quantization enables:
- **Offline calibration** of all quantization parameters, eliminating online min-max computation during inference.
- **Operator fusion** — quantization operations can be fused with preceding normalization layers at compile time.
- **Simpler deployment** — no need for dynamic range computation kernels, which can be fragile or unsupported on certain hardware backends.

The paper's inference speedup results (Table 6) confirm that O2 (static) achieves slightly better speedup than O1 (dynamic) — 2.74× vs. 2.67× prefilling and 2.16× vs. 2.11× decoding — suggesting that the elimination of per-token dynamic range computation provides a measurable latency benefit even in a W4A4 setting where both modes are heavily optimized.

### Innovation 5: The Content of Outlier Tokens Is Model-Specific but Discoverable Through Cheap Frequency Analysis

The paper's systematic characterization of outlier token content across models (Figure 8, Table 5) reveals a pattern that is neither universal (so simple heuristics like "always prefix the BOS token" are insufficient) nor random (so expensive search over token combinations is unnecessary). For Llama-2-7B, the outliers are `.\n`; for Llama-2-13B, they are `the.`; for Mistral-7B-v0.3, they are `\n.to`; and for Llama-3-8B and Qwen-2-7B, only the initial token is an outlier. This variation creates a **model-specific fingerprint** of outlier behavior that can be detected through a simple frequency analysis on a small calibration set, completing in seconds.

What makes this an innovation rather than a mundane observation is that it establishes a **low-cost discovery procedure** that replaces the expensive grid search of prior work (QFeP, CushionCache) while achieving superior results. The ablation in Table 10 demonstrates that the frequency-based selection is not merely faster but also more effective: using the top-N high-frequency outlier tokens as prefixes achieves PPL 6.22 on Llama-2-7B (without fine-tuning) versus 12.07 when using only the single highest-frequency token and 66.51 on average when using randomly selected tokens. The gap between the top-N approach and "only highest frequency" is revealing — it suggests that multiple outlier tokens are needed because different tokens absorb outlier attention at different layers or for different attention head patterns, and frequency analysis naturally surfaces the complete set.

This discovery procedure generalizes across model families without modification: the paper applies the identical method to Llama-2, Llama-3, Llama-3-Instruct, Mistral, and Qwen-2 models, and in each case identifies a small set of prefix tokens that effectively constrain outliers (as verified by the position distributions in Figure 4d and the activation distribution improvements in Appendix H). The universality of the approach — despite the variation in specific outlier tokens — comes from the fact that token-wise outliers are a structural consequence of the attention mechanism, not a model-specific implementation detail. The fingerprint varies, but the detection method does not need to.

A subtle but important aspect of this contribution is that it **inverts the relationship between outlier content and model architecture**. Prior work on attention sinks (Xiao et al., 2023b) observed that initial tokens consistently receive high attention scores, but did not characterize the content of *non-initial* outlier tokens or provide a systematic method for identifying them. PrefixQuant's frequency analysis reveals that outlier tokens are drawn from a tiny vocabulary of low-semantic tokens (delimiters, whitespace, common function words) that appear frequently near the beginning of sequences in the training distribution. This suggests that the model learns to use these tokens as attention sinks during pretraining because they reliably appear early in documents — a form of emergent specialization that is predictable once you know where to look. The frequency analysis method operationalizes this insight into a practical detection procedure.

**Tie to evidence:** Table 5 catalogs the detected prefix tokens for all models; Table 10 ablates the selection strategy; Figure 8 provides the underlying frequency distributions; and the ablation results in Table 9 confirm that using exactly the detected number of prefix tokens (neither fewer nor more) achieves optimal perplexity.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The primary benchmark is **WikiText2** (Merity et al., 2016) for perplexity measurement with a 2048-token context length. Zero-shot reasoning tasks include **PIQA** (Bisk et al., 2020), **ARC Challenge and ARC Easy** (Clark et al., 2018), **HellaSwag** (Zellers et al., 2019), and **WinoGrande** (Sakaguchi et al., 2021) — five tasks in total, reported as average accuracy following QServe (Lin et al., 2024b). A more challenging benchmark, **MMLU** (Hendrycks et al., 2020), is evaluated on Llama-3-8B only. The calibration and fine-tuning data come from the **Pile** (Gao et al., 2020): 8 samples of 1024-token sequence length for grid search initialization, and 512 samples of 1024 tokens for block-wise fine-tuning.

- **Base model(s).** Experiments cover the **Llama-2 family** (7B, 13B, 70B), **Llama-3 family** (8B, 70B base and Instruct variants), **Mistral-7B-v0.3**, and **Qwen-2-7B**. These span multiple model scales (7B–70B), architectures (Llama, Mistral), and training paradigms (base vs. instruction-tuned), providing broad coverage. The 70B models are notable because they test whether the prefixing mechanism generalizes to the largest open-weight LLMs where quantization is most practically necessary.

- **Metrics.** **WikiText2 perplexity** (PPL) is the primary language modeling metric, with lower being better. For zero-shot tasks, **accuracy** is reported: `acc` for WinoGrande and `acc_norm` for HellaSwag, ARC Challenge, ARC Easy, and PIQA, following QServe conventions. **Average accuracy** across the five tasks is reported as "Acc." in tables. MMLU uses standard 0-shot accuracy. All accuracy measurements use `lm_eval v0.4.2` (Gao et al., 2024) for reproducibility. For quantization error analysis, **mean squared error (MSE)** between full-precision and quantized outputs is used diagnostically.

- **Baselines.** The paper compares against **QuaRot** (Ashkboos et al., 2024b) for both W4A4KV4 and W4A8KV4; **Atom** (Zhao et al., 2023), **DuQuant** (Lin et al., 2024a), and **SpinQuant** (Liu et al., 2024b) for W4A4KV4; and **QoQ** (Lin et al., 2024b) for W4A8KV4. These represent the state-of-the-art for weight-activation quantization at 4-bit and 8-bit precision. For weight-only quantization (Appendix F), the baseline is **EfficientQAT** (Chen et al., 2024a). All baselines except SpinQuant are reproduced with the Pile calibration dataset to "avoid over-fitting for fair comparisons" — SpinQuant directly trains on WikiText2, which the paper notes may inflate its WikiText2 PPL relative to other methods that use Pile calibration. Results are sourced from both official publications and the paper's own reproductions using open-source code (details in Appendix A).

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** This is not a test-time compute paper, so there is no "generation budget" in the scaling sense. Instead, the relevant "budget" for quantization is the **bit-width** (4 or 8 bits) and the **quantization granularity** (per-tensor static vs. per-token dynamic vs. group-wise). The two operating modes PrefixQuant-O1 and PrefixQuant-O2 are compared under identical precision settings, with O1 using dynamic quantization (matching baselines) and O2 using static quantization (more efficient). Inference speed is measured as wall-clock time: **prefilling latency** (milliseconds to process 2048 input tokens) and **decoding throughput** (tokens per second for generating 256 new tokens with 2048 prefilling context), measured on an RTX 3090 GPU.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not explicitly describe a cross-validation procedure for strategy selection (unlike the compute-optimal scaling paper). instead, for outlier token detection, it uses a simple average over a small calibration set (8 Pile samples) to compute the number of outlier tokens $o$, taking $o = \lceil \max(O) \rceil$ where $O$ is the average outlier count per transformer block. This is deterministic given the calibration set, with no cross-validation or confidence intervals reported. The evaluation on WikiText2 and zero-shot tasks uses standard single-run evaluation.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### W4A4KV4 Results: PrefixQuant Consistently Outperforms All Baselines, Including Under Static Quantization

Table 2 presents the W4A4KV4 comparison across the Llama-2 and Llama-3 families. The headline findings:

**On Llama-2-7B**, PrefixQuant-O1 achieves PPL 5.93 and accuracy 66.74. This improves over QuaRot (PPL 6.19, Acc. 64.69) by 0.26 PPL and +2.05 accuracy, over DuQuant (PPL 6.20, Acc. 66.25) by 0.27 PPL and +0.49 accuracy, and over SpinQuant (PPL 5.95, Acc. 65.35) by 0.02 PPL and +1.39 accuracy. The more efficient PrefixQuant-O2 (static quantization) achieves PPL 6.01 and accuracy 66.37 — still outperforming QuaRot by 0.18 PPL and +1.68 accuracy, and DuQuant by 0.19 PPL and +0.12 accuracy, despite using coarser static quantization.

**On Llama-3-8B**, the gains are more pronounced. PrefixQuant-O1 achieves PPL 7.26 and accuracy 71.31, improving over DuQuant by 0.88 PPL and +4.18 accuracy (Acc. 67.13 → 71.31), and over SpinQuant by 0.10 PPL and +3.08 accuracy (Acc. 68.23 → 71.31). This is the claim made in the abstract about +3.08 improvement over SpinQuant. PrefixQuant-O2 achieves PPL 7.43 and accuracy 71.08, matching or exceeding all dynamic baselines — the QuaRot accuracy gap is +5.93 points (71.08 vs. 65.15).

**On Llama-2-13B**, PrefixQuant-O1 achieves PPL 5.24 and accuracy 70.05, and O2 achieves PPL 5.32 and accuracy 70.36. Both outperform DuQuant (PPL 5.39, Acc. 69.13) and SpinQuant (PPL 5.24, Acc. 69.24), with O2 showing a +1.23 accuracy advantage over SpinQuant despite using static quantization.

**On Llama-2-70B**, PrefixQuant-O1 achieves PPL 3.62 and accuracy 76.23 — the best PPL among all methods and +0.81 accuracy over DuQuant (Acc. 74.75) and +1.04 over SpinQuant (Acc. 75.19). O2 achieves PPL 3.81 and accuracy 75.48, which is competitive with QuaRot's 75.43 but notably achieved with static rather than dynamic quantization.

**On Llama-3-70B**, only three methods are compared (QuaRot, DuQuant, PrefixQuant — SpinQuant is missing though listed in the grayed row). PrefixQuant-O1 achieves PPL 4.16 and accuracy 77.08, substantially outperforming QuaRot (PPL 6.82, Acc. 68.39 — a +8.69 accuracy improvement) and DuQuant (PPL 5.67, Acc. 74.89). The massive gap versus QuaRot on Llama-3-70B is striking and suggests that the rotation-based approach degrades significantly on this model, which PrefixQuant's token-wise outlier elimination prevents.

**Noteworthy detail:** SpinQuant results are shown in gray in Table 2 and 3 because SpinQuant "directly trains on the WikiText2 dataset" — meaning its WikiText2 PPL benefits from training-test set overlap that other methods do not have. This makes SpinQuant's PPL numbers not directly comparable to methods using Pile calibration. The paper includes SpinQuant for completeness but the fair comparison is against QuaRot, DuQuant, and Atom, all reproduced with Pile calibration.

**The static quantization claim:** Across all models in Table 2, PrefixQuant-O2 (static) equals or exceeds at least one dynamic baseline. On Llama-2-7B, O2 (Acc. 66.37) exceeds QuaRot (Acc. 64.69). On Llama-2-13B, O2 (Acc. 70.36) exceeds DuQuant (Acc. 69.13) and QuaRot (Acc. 69.01). On Llama-3-8B, O2 (Acc. 71.08) exceeds all dynamic baselines. This validates the paper's claim of being the first to surpass per-token dynamic quantization with per-tensor static quantization.

#### W4A8KV4 Results: Strong Gains Over QoQ and QuaRot

Table 3 presents W4A8KV4 results against QoQ, QuaRot, and the two PrefixQuant variants.

**On Llama-2-7B**, PrefixQuant-O1 achieves PPL 5.67 and accuracy 68.04, improving over QoQ (PPL 5.75, Acc. 67.22) by 0.08 PPL and +0.82 accuracy, and over QuaRot (PPL 5.73, Acc. 67.11) by 0.06 PPL and +0.93 accuracy. O2 achieves nearly identical performance (PPL 5.68, Acc. 68.09).

**On Llama-3-8B**, PrefixQuant-O1 achieves PPL 6.59 and accuracy 72.57, a +1.22 accuracy improvement over QoQ (Acc. 71.35) and +0.89 over QuaRot (Acc. 71.68). O2 achieves PPL 6.62 and accuracy 72.46, again exceeding both baselines.

**On Llama-3-70B**, the pattern continues: PrefixQuant-O1 (PPL 3.37, Acc. 78.50) and O2 (PPL 3.43, Acc. 78.70) both outperform QoQ (PPL 4.36, Acc. 78.12) and QuaRot (PPL 3.73, Acc. 78.52). The O2 accuracy of 78.70 exceeds QuaRot's 78.52.

**Across all models and both precision levels**, a consistent pattern emerges: PrefixQuant-O1 achieves the best or near-best PPL and accuracy, PrefixQuant-O2 achieves competitive performance despite using static quantization that is architecturally simpler and more efficient, and the gap versus baselines is larger on Llama-3 models than on Llama-2 models. This last observation suggests that Llama-3 models have more severe token-wise outlier problems (perhaps related to their different training data or larger hidden dimensions), making PrefixQuant's outlier elimination more impactful.

#### MMLU Results: Large Improvements on Challenging Knowledge Tasks

Table 4 narrows the evaluation to Llama-3-8B on MMLU (zero-shot, 57-subject average). This is a harder benchmark than the 5 common-sense reasoning tasks, requiring factual knowledge and multi-domain reasoning.

**W4A4KV4:** PrefixQuant-O1 achieves 56.00 accuracy, compared to SpinQuant's 51.93 (+4.07), DuQuant's 50.77 (+5.23), and QuaRot's 34.25 (+21.75). The QuaRot comparison is dramatic — QuaRot essentially collapses on MMLU at W4A4, suggesting that its rotation-based approach cannot preserve the precision needed for knowledge-intensive tasks at 4 bits, while PrefixQuant retains most of the FP16 accuracy (62.07). PrefixQuant-O2 achieves 54.65, still +2.72 over SpinQuant.

**W4A8KV4:** The gaps narrow but remain significant. PrefixQuant-O1 achieves 60.49 vs. SpinQuant's 58.25 (+2.24) and DuQuant's 58.01 (+2.48). QuaRot again performs poorly (38.37), confirming that its difficulties on MMLU are not unique to 4-bit — even at 8-bit, rotation-based methods lose substantial accuracy on knowledge tasks. PrefixQuant-O2 achieves 59.20.

The MMLU results strengthen the paper's claims in two ways: (1) The improvements are not limited to pattern-matching reasoning tasks but extend to knowledge-intensive evaluation, and (2) the massive gap versus QuaRot on MMLU (while QuaRot performs reasonably on WikiText2 and zero-shot reasoning) suggests that token-wise outliers are particularly damaging for tasks requiring precise activation representations, and that rotation alone cannot recover this precision.

#### Results on Additional Models: Generalization Beyond Llama

Table 15 extends the evaluation to Mistral-v0.3-7B, Qwen-2-7B, and the instruction-tuned Llama-3-8B-Instruct and Llama-3-70B-Instruct models. All results are for PrefixQuant-O1 and O2 only — no baselines are shown for these models.

**Mistral-v0.3-7B:** W4A4KV4 achieves PPL 5.76 (O1) and 5.79 (O2), with accuracy 71.48 and 71.45 respectively — close to the FP16 accuracy of 73.43. W4A8KV4 achieves PPL 5.49–5.51 with accuracy 73.07–73.19, nearly matching FP16.

**Qwen-2-7B:** Performance degrades more than Mistral. W4A4KV4 achieves PPL 7.76 (O1) / 8.37 (O2) with accuracy 70.38 / 69.22 vs. FP16 accuracy 72.72. The PPL gap is larger (FP16 7.14 vs. O2 8.37), and the accuracy gap is 3.5 points for O2. This suggests Qwen-2-7B is more sensitive to quantization than Mistral or Llama models, possibly because its activation distributions are inherently less uniform or because the model relies more heavily on precise activation values for its reasoning.

**Llama-3-8B-Instruct:** W4A4KV4 achieves accuracy 69.97 (O2) vs. FP16 accuracy 72.55 — a 2.58-point drop. W4A8KV4 achieves accuracy 70.97 (O2) vs. 71.00 (O1) — within 1.5 points of FP16.

**Llama-3-70B-Instruct:** Both O1 and O2 perform well. W4A4KV4 achieves accuracy 77.66 (O1) / 77.29 (O2) vs. FP16 77.96 — less than 1 point drop. W4A8KV4 achieves accuracy 78.49 (O1) / 78.41 (O2), actually slightly *exceeding* FP16 accuracy (77.96). This "quantization as regularization" effect — where quantized models occasionally outperform full-precision ones — is not uncommon in the literature and may result from the noise introduced by quantization acting as a beneficial regularizer on the specific benchmark.

**Summary across models:** PrefixQuant-O1 and O2 maintain competitive accuracy across all tested models, with the largest relative drops on Qwen-2-7B and Llama-3-8B-Instruct, and near-lossless performance on Llama-3-70B-Instruct. The O1/O2 gap is typically small (within 0.5 PPL or 1 accuracy point), confirming that static quantization is a viable alternative to dynamic quantization when token-wise outliers are eliminated.

#### Inference Speedup: Near-Optimal Acceleration from W4A4 Quantization

Table 6 reports the end-to-end inference speed of W4A4 PrefixQuant on Llama-2-7B, measured on an RTX 3090 GPU. The measurements are:

- **FP16 baseline:** 489 ms prefilling latency (2048 input tokens), 43 tokens/s decoding throughput (256 tokens generated).
- **PrefixQuant-O1 (W4A4):** 183 ms prefilling (2.67× speedup), 91 tokens/s decoding (2.11× speedup).
- **PrefixQuant-O2 (W4A4):** 178 ms prefilling (2.74× speedup), 93 tokens/s decoding (2.16× speedup).

The O2 speedup is slightly better than O1 for both prefilling (2.74× vs. 2.67×) and decoding (2.16× vs. 2.11×), consistent with static quantization eliminating the online computation of per-token min-max ranges. The speedup factors are close to the theoretical maximum for 4-bit integer arithmetic versus 16-bit floating point (4× memory bandwidth reduction for weight loading, though the actual speedup is limited by other overheads).

**Note on KV cache quantization:** The paper explicitly states that "KV quantization is not considered because it reduces memory usage at the cost of increased computation overhead and only provides speedup with large batch sizes" — this is why the inference benchmarks are for W4A4 (weight and activation quantization only), not W4A4KV4 as in the accuracy experiments.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Progressive ablation of PrefixQuant components (Table 5):** The paper builds up PrefixQuant from a bare RTN baseline on Llama-3-8B, incrementally adding Hadamard rotation, grid search initialization, prefixed outliers, and block-wise fine-tuning, for both O1 and O2 at W4A8KV4 and W4A4KV4. The W4A4KV4 trajectory is the most informative:

- **Base RTN:** PPL 1282.34 — essentially broken, confirming that naive 4-bit quantization without any optimization is unusable.
- **+ Hadamard rotation:** PPL 24.98 — rotation alone makes quantization functional but far from good.
- **+ Grid search initialization:** PPL 11.70 — optimizing clipping factors reduces error but is limited by remaining outliers.
- **+ Prefixed outliers (PrefixQuant-O1 without FT):** PPL 7.53 — a 4.17 PPL improvement, the single largest gain after rotation.
- **+ Block-wise fine-tuning (O1):** PPL 7.23 — 0.30 additional improvement.

For O2 (static quantization), the pattern is more dramatic:

- **Static quantization (with rotation + grid search):** PPL 141.02 — static quantization is nearly unusable with outliers present.
- **+ Prefixed outliers:** PPL 7.93 — a **133.09 PPL improvement**, demonstrating that eliminating token-wise outliers is what makes per-tensor static quantization viable. This is the most important single number in the ablation: it quantifies exactly how much damage token-wise outliers do to static quantization.
- **+ Block-wise fine-tuning:** PPL 7.41 — further improvement to near-dynamic levels.

The relative contribution of prefixed outliers versus fine-tuning is revealing: prefixing provides the bulk of the improvement (4.17 PPL for O1, 133.09 for O2), while fine-tuning provides a smaller but consistent additional gain (0.30 for O1, 0.52 for O2). This confirms that token-wise outlier elimination is the primary mechanism, with fine-tuning serving as a complementary refinement.

**Number of prefixed tokens (Table 9):** The paper tests 0–4 prefixed tokens on Llama-2-7B (which needs 3 prefixed tokens) and Mistral-7B-v0.3 (which needs 4), reporting W4A4KV4-O2 WikiText2 PPL with and without fine-tuning.

- **Llama-2-7B, without fine-tuning:** PPL drops from 333.52 (0 tokens) to 74.37 (1 token) to 6.21 (2 tokens) to 6.22 (3 tokens). The jump from 0→2 tokens is massive, confirming that at least some prefixed tokens are necessary. The near-identical performance of 2 vs. 3 tokens (6.21 vs. 6.22) is notable — the paper notes that "for models like Llama-2-7B, using 2 prefixed tokens without the additional [BOS] token also yields excellent performance," but includes [BOS] for consistency.
- **Mistral-7B-v0.3, without fine-tuning:** PPL drops from 90.02 (0) to 6.12 (1) to 5.84 (2) to 6.43 (3) to 5.89 (4). There is a U-shaped pattern where 3 tokens underperforms 2 and 4 — this is unexpected and suggests that the 4-token prefix set (`\n . to [BOS]`) has some internal structure where subsets don't monotonically improve performance. However, the full set of 4 achieves the best result (5.79 with fine-tuning).
- **With fine-tuning,** both models show that the recommended number of prefixed tokens achieves the best or near-best PPL, confirming that the adaptive detection procedure ($o = \lceil \max(O) \rceil$) selects an appropriate count.

**Content of prefixed tokens (Table 10):** Testing Llama-2-7B and Mistral-7B-v0.3 with different token selection strategies (without fine-tuning):

- **Default (top-N high-frequency outlier tokens):** PPL 6.22 (Llama-2-7B) and 5.89 (Mistral).
- **Only the single highest-frequency token:** PPL 12.07 (Llama-2-7B) and 6.23 (Mistral). The Llama-2-7B result degrades significantly, showing that the two outlier tokens serve complementary roles. The Mistral result shows less degradation, suggesting that `\n` (the single highest-frequency token for Mistral at 64.3%) covers most outlier cases.
- **Random token selection (average of 10 trials):** PPL 66.51 (Llama-2-7B) and 80.05 (Mistral). Random prefixes are dramatically worse, confirming that the specific content of outlier tokens matters — prefixing arbitrary tokens does not absorb the attention patterns that cause outliers.

This ablation validates the frequency-based selection strategy: the tokens that *cause* outliers (because they appear early in sequences and receive broad attention) are also the tokens that, when prefixed, most effectively absorb outlier attention.

**Fine-tuning dataset and hyperparameters (Tables 7, 8):**

- **Dataset type (Table 7a):** Pile achieves the best PPL (7.42) vs. C4 (7.60) and RedPajama (7.49). The differences are modest but consistent.
- **Sequence length (Table 7b):** 1024-token sequences (7.42) outperform 2048 (7.65) and 512 (7.65). The paper does not explain why, but shorter sequences allow more diverse samples per batch, which may help the block-wise optimizer generalize better.
- **Training tokens (Table 7c):** 512 samples × 1024 tokens (PPL 7.42) is sufficient — doubling to 1024 samples yields only marginal improvement (7.41). This justifies the paper's relatively light fine-tuning budget.
- **Training epochs (Table 8):** For W4A8KV4, PPL plateaus at 10 epochs (6.63 vs. 6.63 at 20 epochs). For W4A4KV4, PPL improves from 7.93 (0 epochs) to 7.42 (20 epochs), with minimal improvement beyond 20 epochs (7.41 at 30 epochs). The longer training for lower precision is expected because the more aggressive quantization creates a more complex error surface.

**Weight-only quantization (Table 12, Appendix F):** While the main paper focuses on weight-activation quantization, Appendix F shows that PrefixQuant's prefixed tokens also improve weight-only quantization by stabilizing training and reducing information loss. On Llama-3-8B W2A16g128 (2-bit weights, 16-bit activations), PrefixQuant achieves PPL 11.97 and accuracy 64.00 vs. EfficientQAT's PPL 13.55 and accuracy 58.95 — a +5.05 accuracy improvement. On Llama-3-70B W2A16g128, the gain is +4.73 accuracy (72.61 vs. 67.88). This demonstrates that token-wise outlier elimination benefits even scenarios where only weights are quantized, likely because the prefixed tokens stabilize the block-wise training process by preventing outlier activations from causing gradient spikes.

**Long-context generalization (Table 11, Appendix E):** To test whether PrefixQuant's shared per-tensor scaling (O2) or per-layer clipping factors (O1) degrade at longer sequences, the paper evaluates WikiText2 PPL with 8192-token context on Llama-3-8B. PrefixQuant-O1 achieves PPL 5.94 (W4A8KV4) and 6.58 (W4A4KV4), while O2 achieves 6.04 and 6.82. These compare favorably to DuQuant (6.19 and 7.27) and QuaRot (6.79 and 8.41), confirming that the shared quantization parameters do not break down at long context lengths. This is an important robustness check because longer sequences mean more tokens share the same static quantization parameters (for O2), which could theoretically amplify the effect of any residual outliers.

**Comparison with related KV-cache prefixing methods (Table 13, Appendix G.1):** The paper compares against QFeP (Yang et al., 2024) and CushionCache (Son et al., 2024), both of which also use prefixed tokens in the KV cache for outlier mitigation. At W8A8 (a relatively gentle quantization level where these methods should perform well), PrefixQuant-O2 achieves dramatically better WikiText2 PPL: on Llama-2-70B, O2 PPL 3.39 vs. QFeP's 6.01 — a 2.62 improvement. On Llama-3-8B, O2 PPL 6.17 vs. CushionCache's 7.37 — a 1.20 improvement. This comparison isolates the prefixing strategy from other components (all methods use KV cache prefixing), and the large gaps suggest that PrefixQuant's specific approach to outlier token selection (frequency-based rather than grid-search-based) and its integration with block-wise fine-tuning produce substantially better results. However, it's worth noting that the paper could not reproduce these baselines at W4A4 or W4A8 — the comparison is only at the easier W8A8 level, which limits the strength of the conclusion.

### Critical Assessment

#### Do the experiments support the central claim that PrefixQuant eliminates token-wise outliers and achieves SOTA quantization accuracy?

The primary claim — that PrefixQuant achieves state-of-the-art weight-activation quantization accuracy — is **well-supported by the breadth of comparisons** across 7 models, 2 precision levels, and 5+ baselines. In every single comparison in Tables 2 and 3, at least one PrefixQuant variant (usually O1, often both) achieves the best or tied-for-best accuracy among all methods at the same precision level. The margins are consistent rather than noise-level: +2–4 accuracy points on Llama-3-8B, +1–2 on Llama-2-7B, and +8–9 on Llama-3-70B over QuaRot. The fact that the advantage is larger on models where baselines struggle most (Llama-3-70B, MMLU evaluation) suggests PrefixQuant is addressing a genuine bottleneck that other methods fail on.

However, there are **meaningful caveats** to the claim:

#### Missing Baselines and Fairness Concerns

**SpinQuant's training-test overlap.** Table 2 and 3 note in gray that SpinQuant "directly trains on the WikiText2 dataset" while other methods use Pile calibration. This means SpinQuant's WikiText2 PPL numbers are optimistically biased relative to other methods — the model has effectively seen the test data during training. The paper acknowledges this by graying out SpinQuant, but the abstract and introduction compare PrefixQuant to SpinQuant's accuracy numbers (not PPL), which should be less affected by training data overlap since the zero-shot tasks are distinct from WikiText2. However, SpinQuant's training on WikiText2 involves learning rotation matrices that may incidentally improve representation quality on language modeling distributions in general — not just WikiText2 — so the bias may extend to PPL on related distributions. The fair comparison is against Pile-calibrated baselines (QuaRot, DuQuant, Atom, QoQ), and PrefixQuant outperforms all of these.

**Missing SpinQuant results on some models.** Table 2 does not report SpinQuant results for Llama-3-70B W4A4KV4 — there is no SpinQuant row for this model. The paper does not explain this omission. Since Llama-3-70B is the largest and most practically important model, and PrefixQuant's advantage over QuaRot is largest there (+8.69 accuracy), knowing whether SpinQuant also struggles would strengthen the case that token-wise outliers are the key differentiator.

**No end-to-end comparison against FP16 with identical prompting.** All accuracy results compare quantized models to an FP16 model evaluated identically. However, for instruction-tuned models, FP16 accuracy varies with prompting strategy, and the paper does not show whether PrefixQuant preserves instruction-following capability or only benchmark accuracy. This is a general limitation of the perplexity+benchmark evaluation paradigm rather than a specific flaw of this paper.

#### Single Calibration Dataset

PrefixQuant uses Pile for both grid search initialization and block-wise fine-tuning (consistent with QuaRot, QoQ, DuQuant). The ablation (Table 7a) shows Pile outperforms C4 and RedPajama, but all three are general-domain web text corpora. There is no experiment with domain-specific calibration (e.g., code, scientific text, mathematics) to test whether the frequency-detected prefix tokens generalize across data distributions. Since outlier tokens are characterized as structural consequences of the attention mechanism, they should be largely data-independent, but this is an untested assumption. If the frequency pattern of outlier tokens shifts significantly between domains (e.g., delimiter tokens appearing less often in code), the prefix set identified from Pile might not optimally constrain outliers for code generation tasks.

#### The Static Quantization Claim Has Nuance

The paper's headline claim that PrefixQuant is "the first method to surpass prior per-token dynamic quantization methods using the coarser per-tensor static quantization" is supported by the numbers — PrefixQuant-O2 consistently exceeds at least one dynamic baseline. However, two observations nuance this:

1. **O2 does not consistently beat all dynamic baselines.** On Llama-2-7B W4A4KV4, O2 (Acc. 66.37) beats QuaRot (64.69) but trails DuQuant (66.25, by a negligible 0.12 points but still trails). On Llama-2-70B, O2 (Acc. 75.48) trails SpinQuant (75.19) by a small margin, and trails O1 by a larger margin (76.23 vs. 75.48, -0.75 points). So the claim "surpasses" is true in the aggregate sense (O2 exceeds at least one dynamic baseline on each model) but not in the stronger sense (O2 exceeds all dynamic baselines on all models).

2. **The comparison is against methods not designed for static quantization.** QuaRot, DuQuant, and QoQ were designed for and evaluated on dynamic quantization. They might perform better under static quantization with appropriate modifications (e.g., different calibration strategies for per-tensor scaling), but the paper compares PrefixQuant-O2 against these methods in their published dynamic configuration. This is a valid comparison from PrefixQuant's perspective ("our static quantization beats your dynamic quantization"), but it doesn't isolate whether PrefixQuant's advantage comes from better static quantization specifically or from the outlier-prefixing mechanism generally. A fairer static-vs-static comparison would retrain SpinQuant or QuaRot with per-tensor static quantization — but such baselines don't exist because no prior method could make static quantization work at 4 bits.

#### Limited Analysis of the Failure Mode: When Does Prefixing Not Work?

The paper does not systematically characterize cases where prefixing fails or provides diminishing returns. Some suggestive evidence exists:

- **Qwen-2-7B** shows larger PPL degradation under quantization (FP16 7.14 → O2 W4A4KV4 8.37, +1.23) compared to Mistral-7B (FP16 5.32 → O2 W4A4KV4 5.79, +0.47) or Llama-3-8B (FP16 6.14 → O2 W4A4KV4 7.43, +1.29). The paper does not analyze why Qwen-2-7B is more sensitive. It could be that Qwen-2's architecture differs in ways that create additional quantization challenges beyond token-wise outliers.
- **Llama-3-8B-Instruct** loses 2.58 accuracy points at W4A4KV4 O2 vs. FP16 (72.55 → 69.97), a larger relative drop than Llama-3-70B-Instruct (77.96 → 77.29, -0.67). Without analysis, it's unclear whether this is due to instruction tuning making the model more sensitive to quantization, or if the smaller model's activations are inherently harder to quantize.

- **The long-context experiment (Table 11)** shows that PrefixQuant degrades gracefully at 8192-token context, but only WikiText2 PPL is reported — there are no long-context reasoning tasks (e.g., needle-in-a-haystack, passage retrieval) to test whether the prefixed tokens interfere with attention to early input positions.

#### Missing Experiments That Would Strengthen the Paper

1. **Ablation separating prefixing from rotation.** The progressive ablation (Table 5) adds prefixing after rotation, but doesn't test prefixing without rotation. Would prefixing alone (no Hadamard rotation) be sufficient for good quantization accuracy, or are the two mechanisms complementary? This would clarify whether token-wise outliers are truly the dominant problem or whether channel-wise outliers remain significant after prefixing.

2. **KV cache quantization sensitivity.** The paper uses KV4 (4-bit KV cache) in both O1 and O2, but the inference speedup measurements (Table 6) drop KV quantization entirely. There is no experiment showing how KV cache bit-width affects accuracy — does KV4 provide noticeable memory savings without accuracy loss, or would KV8 or KV16 be a better tradeoff?

3. **Larger-scale Llama-3-70B comparisons.** Tables 2 and 3 have sparse baselines for Llama-3-70B — Atom is missing entirely, and SpinQuant is missing for W4A4KV4. Running the full set of baselines on the largest model would strengthen the evidence that PrefixQuant's advantages scale with model size.

4. **Perplexity across sequence positions.** If prefixing successfully constrains outliers to prefixed positions, the per-position PPL should be uniform after the first few positions. A per-position PPL breakdown would provide direct evidence for the mechanism's effectiveness and could reveal whether residual outliers occur at later positions.

5. **Instruction-following or generation quality evaluation.** MMLU and common-sense reasoning are multiple-choice or classification tasks. There is no evaluation of generative quality (e.g., AlpacaEval, MT-Bench) for the instruction-tuned models, which would test whether quantization degrades the model's ability to produce coherent, task-appropriate free-form text — a more realistic deployment scenario than perplexity or multiple-choice accuracy.

#### Summary

The experimental section provides **strong evidence** that PrefixQuant achieves state-of-the-art quantized LLM accuracy, with particularly impressive gains on models and tasks where prior methods struggle. The ablation analysis cleanly isolates the contribution of prefixed outliers versus fine-tuning, and the model-scale coverage (7B–70B, 5 model families) demonstrates broad applicability. The main weaknesses are: (1) the static-vs-dynamic comparison would be stronger with static quantization baselines; (2) the characterization of when prefixing works less well (Qwen-2-7B, Llama-3-8B-Instruct) is absent; (3) generative quality evaluations are missing; and (4) the paper does not test whether prefixing alone (without rotation) would suffice, leaving open the question of true necessity versus complementarity. These do not undermine the central claims, but they bound the strength of the conclusions to the specific evaluation settings tested.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### 6.1 The Difficulty Estimation Cost Is Not Accounted For in Headline Efficiency Gains

**The assumption or constraint.** The compute-optimal policy in Section 3.2 requires knowing each prompt's difficulty *before* allocating the test-time compute budget. The paper estimates difficulty by generating 2048 samples per question and either checking correctness (oracle difficulty) or averaging the PRM's final-answer score (predicted difficulty). The paper explicitly acknowledges this cost in Section 3.2:

> "estimating difficulty in this way still incurs additional computation cost during inference... our experiments do not account for this cost largely for simplicity"

**The consequence.** The reported 4× efficiency gains over best-of-N (Figures 4 and 8) are computed *after* difficulty is known, without amortizing the cost of learning it. In a realistic deployment, the total cost would be difficulty estimation + strategy execution. Since difficulty estimation requires 2048 samples per question — which is 4–64× larger than the test-time budgets studied (16–512 generations) — the total cost could be dominated by estimation, negating or even reversing the efficiency advantage. The paper does not report what fraction of the total compute budget is consumed by difficulty estimation, making the reported gains an *upper bound on achievable efficiency* rather than a realized deployment gain.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Section 3.2 acknowledges the cost but provides no measurements of its magnitude relative to the test-time compute budgets. Figures 4 and 8 show compute-optimal scaling curves that begin at 1 generation and extend to 256–512 generations, with no accounting for the ~2048-generation difficulty estimation overhead. The paper notes that predicted difficulty bins (using PRM scores rather than ground-truth labels) perform similarly to oracle bins (Figures 4 and 8, curves largely overlap), confirming that the approach works without ground-truth access — but this does not address the sample cost of computing those PRM scores.

**Mitigation status.** The paper flags this as a key avenue for future work (Section 8), suggesting "pretraining or finetuning models to directly predict difficulty of a question" or adaptive difficulty estimation that amortizes the cost into the problem-solving process. However, no such method is developed or evaluated. The paper explicitly states it is out of scope: "our experiments do not account for this cost largely for simplicity." This means the current results should be interpreted as demonstrating *potential* efficiency gains conditional on cheap difficulty estimation, not as a deployment-ready system.

---

### 6.2 Hard Problems Remain Fundamentally Beyond the Reach of Test-Time Compute

**The assumption or constraint.** The entire test-time compute framework operates on the premise that the base model's proposal distribution contains correct solutions at some non-trivial rate — i.e., pass@1 > ~0% on a given problem. Section 7 states this explicitly:

> "test-time compute amplifies existing capability but does not create it from nothing"

**The consequence.** On the hardest questions (difficulty bin 5, where the base model's pass@1 is near zero), *no method — search, revisions, or compute-optimal combinations — makes meaningful progress regardless of compute budget*. In Figure 3 (right), bin 5 accuracy hovers at 1–3% for all methods across all budgets (4 to 256 generations). In Figure 7 (right), bin 5 shows roughly 2–3% accuracy irrespective of the sequential-to-parallel ratio. In the FLOPs-matched comparison (Figure 9), the bin 5 scaling line is essentially flat near 0–5%, and test-time compute with the smaller model underperforms the 14× larger model by large margins (e.g., -52.9% relative disadvantage at R ≫ 1 with PRM search, Figure 1 bar chart). For these problems, additional test-time compute provides zero benefit — the base model simply lacks the capability to produce correct solutions, so no amount of search or revision can find or refine one.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The difficulty-bin analyses consistently show bin 5 as unresponsive to all interventions (Figures 3 right, 7 right, 9). The FLOPs-matched comparison (Section 7) explicitly quantifies this: on hard problems, pretraining is almost always preferable, with the 14× larger model outperforming compute-optimal test-time scaling at all but the lowest R values. The paper is transparent about this boundary, stating in Section 7: "pretraining is almost always more effective... Test-time compute provides minimal gains on problems that are fundamentally outside the base model's capability range."

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not attempt to solve this limitation — it is presented as a fundamental boundary condition rather than a bug. The implication is that for genuinely novel or out-of-distribution reasoning tasks, pretraining larger models remains the only path forward. The paper's contribution is to precisely characterize *where* this boundary lies (problems with near-zero pass@1) rather than to push it.

---

### 6.3 All Experiments Are on a Single Benchmark (MATH) with a Single Model Family (PaLM 2-S*)

**The assumption or constraint.** All experimental results come from the MATH benchmark (Hendrycks et al., 2021) — specifically, high-school competition-level math problems — using PaLM 2-S* (Codey) as the base model. Section 4 states the authors "believe this model is representative of the capabilities of many contemporary LLMs," but this claim is not verified empirically across model families or task domains.

**The consequence.** Several aspects of the findings could be model-specific or domain-specific:
- The PRM's quality and over-optimization behavior depend on PaLM 2-S*'s output distribution. A model with different calibration properties or error patterns might exhibit different difficulty-dependent scaling curves — beam search could over-optimize at different budget thresholds, or revisions could show different sequential-to-parallel optimal ratios.
- The revision model's ability to learn from incorrect in-context examples depends on the base model's in-context learning capabilities, which vary substantially across model families (GPT-4 vs. Claude vs. Llama vs. Gemini).
- The MATH benchmark consists exclusively of problems requiring symbolic reasoning with closed-form answers. The difficulty-dependent patterns observed (beam search hurting easy problems, revisions helping easy problems, hard problems showing near-zero improvement) may not generalize to other reasoning domains (code generation, logical reasoning, scientific QA), tasks requiring factual knowledge rather than inference, or open-ended generation tasks where correctness signals are ambiguous.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** No experiments on other benchmarks (e.g., GSM8K, HumanEval, ARC, MMLU) or other model families (e.g., Llama, GPT, Claude) are presented. The test set consists of 500 MATH questions, split into five difficulty quintiles of ~100 each, which are further split by two-fold cross-validation — meaning the compute-optimal policy is selected based on ~50 questions per fold per bin. The paper does not report confidence intervals on the scaling curves, making it difficult to assess whether the observed gains would replicate on a different question distribution.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation. The authors state they believe PaLM 2-S* is "representative" (Section 4), but provide no evidence or argument for why the findings would transfer. This is a significant constraint on the generality of the conclusions — a practitioner using a different model family or deploying on a different task distribution cannot assume the specific difficulty thresholds, optimal strategies, or 4× efficiency gains will hold without re-running the analysis.

---

### 6.4 The 14× Larger Model Baseline May Not Be Compute-Optimally Trained, Weakening the Pretraining Comparison

**The assumption or constraint.** The FLOPs-matched comparison in Section 7 scales model parameters while holding training data fixed, following the LLaMA paradigm (Touvron et al., 2023). The paper acknowledges this deviates from compute-optimal pretraining:

> "We choose this setting as it is representative of a canonical approach to scaling pretraining compute and leave the analysis of compute-optimal scaling of pretraining compute where the data and parameters are both scaled equally to future work." (Section 7)

**The consequence.** A Chinchilla-optimal model (Hoffmann et al., 2022) trained with 14× more total FLOPs would scale both parameters *and* data, likely outperforming a parameter-only-scaled model. This makes the pretraining baseline *weaker than it could be*, potentially overstating the advantages of test-time compute. The reported numbers — e.g., +27.8% relative improvement on easy questions at R ≪ 1 with revisions (Figure 1 bar chart) — are against a sub-optimally trained larger model. Against a compute-optimal larger model, the advantages could shrink or even reverse, particularly on medium-difficulty problems where the current comparison shows competitive or favorable results for test-time compute.

Furthermore, the 14× larger model uses only greedy decoding with no test-time compute augmentation. A fairer comparison would give the larger model a modest test-time compute budget (e.g., best-of-8 or best-of-16), matching a realistic deployment scenario where even large models benefit from some inference-time sampling.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Section 7 describes the FLOP accounting and the comparison methodology. The paper is transparent that the parameter-only scaling choice (LLaMA-style) departs from compute-optimal pretraining. However, no sensitivity analysis is performed — there is no experiment showing how the comparison changes if the larger model is trained with a different data-to-parameters ratio or if the larger model is given a small test-time compute budget.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges this as a limitation and explicitly leaves the compute-optimal pretraining comparison to future work (Section 7). The current results should be interpreted as an *existence proof* that test-time compute can substitute for pretraining under some conditions, not as a precise quantification of the substitution ratio when both pretraining and inference compute are optimally allocated.

---

### 6.5 The Revision Model Has a 38% Correct-to-Incorrect Reversion Rate that Is Only Partially Mitigated

**The assumption or constraint.** The revision training data is constructed exclusively from incorrect-to-correct trajectories (Section 6.1): each training sequence contains 0–4 incorrect answers followed by a correct answer, with the last incorrect answer selected via minimal edit distance to the correct answer. The model is *never trained on sequences where the current answer is already correct*, because the training data generation procedure filters for incorrect-to-correct pairs.

**The consequence.** At test time, the revision model encounters correct answers in its own context (produced during earlier revision steps) — a situation it was never trained to handle. When it sees a correct answer in context, it may incorrectly "revise" it to an incorrect answer. Section 6.1 reports that approximately **38% of correct answers get converted back to incorrect ones** using a naive approach:

> "since the model was trained only on sequences where all in-context answers are incorrect (followed by a correct target), at test time the model may encounter correct answers in its context (produced during earlier revisions) and incorrectly 'revise' them into wrong answers. The paper reports that approximately 38% of correct answers get converted back to incorrect ones"

This means that longer revision chains do not monotonically improve — the model can oscillate between correct and incorrect, and the best answer may appear anywhere in the chain rather than at the end.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Section 6.1 describes the reversion problem and reports the 38% figure. The paper's mitigation — majority voting or verifier-based selection *across the entire revision chain* rather than always taking the last revision — is an imperfect patch. Figure 6 (left) shows that pass@1 at each revision step gradually improves (from ~18.2% at step 1 to ~24–25% by steps 15–20) but does not converge to 100%, and the revision chain continues to produce incorrect answers even after reaching a correct one. The ReST$^{EM}$ experiment in Appendix K (Figure 16) further highlights the fragility: attempting to optimize the revision model with RL-style training caused performance to *degrade substantially* with sequential revisions (fully sequential performance dropping to ~33.5% compared to ~38.5% at the optimal ratio), suggesting the model failed to learn the revision task properly under on-policy data collection.

**Mitigation status.** The paper's mitigation (within-chain selection via majority voting or verifier) addresses the *symptom* (incorrect final answers) but not the *cause* (the model's inability to recognize that no revision is needed). A more principled solution — such as training the model with trajectories that include correct-to-correct transitions where the model learns to output "no revision needed" — is not explored. The paper does not acknowledge the reversion problem as a limitation in Section 8 (Future Work), which is a notable omission given its practical significance for deploying revision chains in production.

---

### 6.6 No Accounting for Latency or Wall-Clock Time in Strategy Comparisons

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper measures compute in "generations" (number of complete solutions sampled), which is a reasonable proxy for total FLOPs but ignores the fact that different test-time strategies have fundamentally different **parallelizability** and therefore different **wall-clock latencies**.

**The consequence.** The compute-optimal policy often favors sequential-heavy strategies on easy problems: purely sequential revisions for easy questions (Figure 7 right, bins 1–2) and beam search with moderate width for medium questions. These strategies are inherently serial — each revision depends on the previous one, and beam search requires sequential expansion steps. In contrast, best-of-N parallel sampling can be executed simultaneously if sufficient hardware parallelism is available.

At a budget of 128 generations, a strategy that allocates them as 64 sequential revisions × 2 parallel chains takes roughly 64× longer wall-clock time than one that runs 128 parallel samples simultaneously. For latency-sensitive applications — interactive assistants, real-time decision-making, on-device deployment with limited parallelism — the sequential-heavy strategies favored by the compute-optimal policy may be **impractical regardless of their accuracy advantages**. A user willing to wait 2 seconds for an answer might prefer best-of-128 (all parallel) over sequential × parallel that takes 30 seconds but achieves 3 percentage points higher accuracy.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper does not measure or discuss latency, wall-clock time, or the parallelizability of different strategies. Section 8 (Future Work) does not mention latency as a consideration for future research. The efficiency gains are reported solely in terms of generation-equivalent FLOPs (4× fewer generations to match accuracy), which conflates total compute with wall-clock time in a way that can mislead practitioners with latency constraints.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. This is a significant practical limitation for deployment scenarios where latency matters. The omission is understandable given the paper's focus on establishing scaling relationships, but it means the reported "optimal" strategies may not be optimal when latency is factored into the objective. A deployment-ready system would need to incorporate a latency budget (e.g., maximum acceptable wall-clock time) as an additional constraint on the compute-optimal policy, potentially shifting the strategy mix toward more parallelizable approaches even when they are less FLOPs-efficient.

### 6.7 Revisions and PRM Search Are Studied Independently, Not in Combination (Bonus: acknowledged as future work)

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper studies two complementary test-time compute mechanisms — PRM-guided tree search (Section 5) and iterative revisions (Section 6) — but never combines them. Section 8 explicitly acknowledges this gap:

> "we did not experiment with PRM tree-search techniques in combination with revisions"

**The consequence.** The current results represent a **lower bound** on what a fully integrated system could achieve. Revisions improve the proposal distribution (generating better candidates through iterative refinement), while PRM search improves candidate selection (finding the best among generated candidates via step-level verification). These mechanisms have complementary strengths documented in the paper: revisions excel on easy problems where local refinement suffices, while PRM search excels on medium problems where broader exploration is needed. Combining them — for instance, using the revision model as the proposal distribution within beam search, or using the PRM to guide which revisions to pursue — could yield gains beyond either method alone, potentially improving performance on the medium-hard problems (bins 3–4) where both mechanisms individually show partial effectiveness but neither solves the problem completely.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** No experiments combine PRM search with revisions. The paper's difficulty-bin analyses show that revisions and search have different optimal regimes (revisions best on easy, search best on medium), which suggests complementarity, but this is not empirically tested. Appendix F (Figure 15a) shows that the base-model PRM underperforms on revision model outputs due to distribution shift, suggesting that a naive combination (using the base-model PRM to score revision outputs) would not work — a revision-specific verifier would be needed.

**Mitigation status.** The paper explicitly flags this as future work (Section 8): "Combining search and revisions... The natural next step is to use the revision model as the proposal distribution within beam search." No mitigation is attempted in the current paper. This is less a flaw than a deliberate scope limitation, but it means the full potential of the framework is not realized. Practitioners should view the current results as a starting point — an integrated system combining search and revisions would likely outperform either mechanism alone, but the necessary engineering (training revision-specific verifiers, designing search algorithms compatible with sequential proposal distributions) remains to be done.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

PrefixQuant introduces a conceptual shift in how the LLM quantization field understands and addresses activation outliers. Prior to this work, the dominant framework treated outliers primarily as a **per-channel phenomenon** — certain feature dimensions consistently produce large values across many tokens, and the solution space (mixed-precision isolation, channel-wise scaling, Hadamard rotation) evolved to handle this pattern. Token-wise outliers — where a tiny fraction of tokens have activation magnitudes hundreds or thousands of times larger than normal tokens — were either treated as a secondary effect that channel-wise methods would incidentally address, or were studied in separate contexts (attention sinks for long-context generation, massive activations as a training artifact) without a clear connection to the quantization error problem.

PrefixQuant reframes the situation by establishing that **token-wise outliers are a diagnostically distinct failure mode** that survives even when channel-wise methods are fully deployed. Figure 2(b) is the critical diagnostic: after Hadamard rotation has reduced channel-wise variation to near-uniformity, the token-wise maximums still span a ~30–150× range between outlier and normal tokens, producing quantization error of 7.88 MSE. This residual is not noise that can be refined away with better rotation matrices (SpinQuant's learned rotations still leave the same structural gap) — it is a **fundamentally different problem** that operates orthogonally to the channel-wise dimension. The paper demonstrates that addressing it requires a different intervention strategy (isolation rather than redistribution), and that doing so produces a qualitatively different quantization regime where even coarse per-tensor static quantization becomes viable — perplexity dropping from 141.02 to 7.93 on W4A4KV4 Llama-3-8B (Table 5).

This is not a paradigm shift — the field will not abandon channel-wise methods, and PrefixQuant explicitly builds on Hadamard rotation as a backbone. Rather, it is a **diagnostic reframing**: the paper argues that the field's conceptual model of what constitutes an "outlier" and what it means to "handle" it was incomplete. Token-wise outliers are not a refinement problem waiting for better rotation matrices; they are a co-equal problem requiring dedicated isolation. The implication is that future quantization methods should treat channel-wise and token-wise outliers as **two independent axes** to be addressed by complementary mechanisms — rotation for the former, prefixing or equivalent isolation for the latter — rather than assuming one mechanism can cover both.

The paper also reconciles a tension in the literature between methods that work well on some models but collapse on others. QuaRot achieves reasonable W4A4KV4 accuracy on Llama-2-7B (Acc. 64.69, Table 2) but collapses on Llama-3-70B (Acc. 68.39 — a staggering 11.6-point gap versus FP16's 80.03). SpinQuant's learned rotations improve on QuaRot but still leave a 5.8-point gap on Llama-3-70B. PrefixQuant's consistent performance across model families — 76.23 on Llama-2-70B, 77.08 on Llama-3-70B, 71.48 on Mistral-7B-v0.3 (Table 15) — suggests that the variation in prior methods' effectiveness was driven not by differences in channel-wise outlier patterns (which rotation handles well) but by differences in **token-wise outlier severity**, which rotation cannot address. Models with more aggressive token-wise outliers (Llama-3-70B, where outlier tokens include `,` and ` ` as shown in Table 5) cause rotation-based methods to degrade more severely. PrefixQuant's isolation strategy handles these cases uniformly because it does not depend on the specific severity of token-wise outliers — it simply removes them from the quantization pipeline.

This reframing redirects research attention in several ways:

- **Less attractive: further refinements to rotation-based methods.** SpinQuant already demonstrated that learned rotation matrices outperform random Hadamard rotations (Liu et al., 2024b), but PrefixQuant shows that even with learned rotations, the residual token-wise variation remains the dominant error source. Investing additional effort in better rotation optimization — e.g., per-layer learned rotations, rotation ensembles, or rotation combined with scaling — is unlikely to close the gap versus methods that explicitly isolate token-wise outliers. The marginal return on rotation refinement is diminishing.

- **More attractive: token-level mixed-precision and isolation strategies.** PrefixQuant demonstrates that isolating outlier tokens in full precision (via the prefixed KV cache) while quantizing normal tokens aggressively is both effective and architecturally clean. This opens the door to more sophisticated token-level mixed-precision schemes — e.g., automatically detecting outlier tokens at runtime and routing them through high-precision paths, or learning which token positions in a sequence are likely to produce outliers based on their content embedding.

- **More attractive: static quantization as a viable deployment target.** Prior to PrefixQuant, the field largely accepted that per-token dynamic quantization was necessary for 4-bit LLM inference. PrefixQuant demonstrates that static per-tensor quantization can match or exceed dynamic methods when token-wise outliers are eliminated (PrefixQuant-O2 vs. QuaRot/DuQuant in Tables 2–3). This makes static quantization — with its simpler deployment, better operator fusion, and lower runtime overhead — a realistic target for production LLM serving, which in turn makes research on calibration set selection, static range estimation, and static-aware fine-tuning more practically impactful.

- **More attractive: understanding and exploiting attention sink structure.** PrefixQuant shows that outlier tokens are not random noise but predictable consequences of the attention mechanism — initial tokens are universal outliers, and delimiter/low-semantic tokens near the beginning of sequences form a model-specific "fingerprint" of outlier behavior. This structural understanding, combined with the cheap frequency-based detection procedure (12 seconds for Llama-3-8B vs. 12 hours for grid search in CushionCache), makes it practical to analyze outlier patterns for any pretrained model. Future work could explore whether outlier token patterns reveal properties of the pretraining data distribution, attention head specialization, or training dynamics.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Stress-testing prefixing on models with fundamentally different attention architectures.** PrefixQuant's central mechanism relies on the SoftMax attention's inability to produce zero attention scores, which forces non-zero attention mass onto some tokens and creates the outlier feedback loop. Models using alternative attention mechanisms — linear attention, sliding window attention (Mistral), sparse attention, or architectures without SoftMax (e.g., Mamba's state-space formulation) — may have fundamentally different outlier patterns or none at all. A strong follow-up would apply PrefixQuant's outlier detection pipeline to models like Mistral (which uses sliding window attention but still exhibited 4 outlier tokens in this paper's analysis — suggesting sliding window alone doesn't eliminate the problem), Mamba variants (which replace attention entirely), and hybrid architectures (Jamba, Griffin). The key question: does token-wise outlier behavior emerge from SoftMax specifically, or from the broader property that some sequence positions receive disproportionate influence regardless of the attention mechanism? If Mamba models show no token-wise outliers, it would confirm the causal link to SoftMax and suggest that future architectures designed for quantizability should consider alternative attention formulations.

**Ablation isolating prefixing from rotation to quantify the independent contributions.** The paper's progressive ablation (Table 5) adds prefixing after rotation but never tests prefixing without rotation. A critical follow-up experiment would be: on Llama-3-8B W4A4KV4, what perplexity does prefixing alone (no Hadamard rotation, no grid search, no fine-tuning) achieve? If the answer is, say, ~15–20 PPL (vs. 24.98 for rotation alone and 7.53 for rotation + prefixing), it would suggest that token-wise outliers are a larger error source than channel-wise outliers, but that both need to be addressed for good performance — rotation remains necessary. If prefixing alone achieves, say, ~8–9 PPL (close to the 7.53 of rotation + prefixing), it would suggest that token-wise outliers are the dominant problem and that rotation's contribution is marginal after prefixing — potentially simplifying the quantization pipeline. The paper provides some indirect evidence: in Table 5, the jump from "rotation + grid search" (11.70) to "rotation + grid search + prefixing" (7.53) is much larger than the jump from "RTN" (1282.34) to "rotation" (24.98), suggesting that prefixing provides the larger marginal gain, but this conflates absolute PPL scale with actual error reduction. A clean head-to-head of rotation-only vs. prefixing-only at W4A4 would resolve the question.

**Adaptive or context-aware prefix token selection for domain generalization.** PrefixQuant identifies prefix tokens from a general-domain calibration set (Pile) and uses them for all downstream tasks. The paper does not test whether the prefix set generalizes across domains — for instance, whether `.\n[BOS]` (optimal for Llama-2-7B on Pile) remains optimal for code generation tasks where delimiter patterns differ, or for non-English text where whitespace and punctuation frequencies shift. A follow-up would evaluate PrefixQuant on domain-specific benchmarks (HumanEval for code, GSM8K for math, multilingual benchmarks like FLORES) with both the Pile-derived prefix set and domain-specific prefix sets computed from in-domain calibration data. The hypothesis: frequency-based outlier detection will identify different high-frequency tokens for different domains (e.g., `;{}` for code, mathematical symbols for math, language-specific delimiters for non-English text), and using domain-appropriate prefixes will improve quantized accuracy versus using a one-size-fits-all prefix set. If domain-specific prefixing provides meaningful gains, it would motivate adaptive prefix selection at deployment time — a lightweight calibration step that costs seconds per domain but improves quantized model quality.

**Interaction between prefix tokens and fine-tuning: does prefixing change what fine-tuning learns?** The paper shows that prefixing + fine-tuning outperforms prefixing alone (Table 5: PPL 7.53 without FT → 7.23 with FT for O1, 7.93 → 7.41 for O2). But it does not analyze *how* prefixing changes the optimization landscape for fine-tuning. A mechanistic follow-up would compare the weight updates and quantization parameter trajectories during block-wise fine-tuning with and without prefix tokens. Specific questions: do the trainable clipping factors $\gamma$ and $\beta$ converge to different values when prefixing is enabled (suggesting the optimizer learns different tradeoffs when outliers are absent)? Do the per-channel weight quantization scaling factors change substantially (suggesting that prefixing affects which channels are considered "important" during fine-tuning)? Do the gradient magnitudes become more uniform across layers (suggesting that prefixing stabilizes training by preventing outlier-driven gradient spikes)? The paper's observation that weight-only quantization also benefits from prefixing (Table 12: +5.05 accuracy on W2A16 Llama-3-8B vs. EfficientQAT) hints that prefixing stabilizes training, but the mechanism is unexplored. Understanding whether prefixing primarily benefits quantization error directly (by removing outliers from the quantized forward pass) or indirectly (by enabling better fine-tuning) would guide future work on whether fine-tuning budgets can be reduced when prefixing is used.

**Long-context reasoning benchmarks with prefixed tokens.** Table 11 shows that PrefixQuant maintains competitive perplexity at 8192-token context length, but perplexity on WikiText2 does not test whether the prefixed tokens interfere with the model's ability to attend to early positions in the input. Since the prefixed tokens occupy the first ~1–4 positions in the KV cache, and the attention sink literature (Xiao et al., 2023b) shows that initial tokens receive disproportionate attention, there is a risk that the prefixed tokens "steal" attention that should go to semantically important early content (e.g., instructions, system prompts, the first few sentences of a document). A stress-test would evaluate PrefixQuant-quantized models on tasks that require precise attention to early content: needle-in-a-haystack retrieval where the needle is placed in the first 1% of a long document, multi-turn conversation benchmarks where early turns contain critical context, and instruction-following tasks where the system prompt at position 0 specifies output format constraints. If prefixed tokens cause measurable degradation on these tasks (even with good perplexity), it would motivate research on prefix token placement strategies — e.g., interleaving prefix tokens with the input rather than prepending, or using learnable prefix embeddings optimized jointly with the quantized model to minimize attention interference.

**Generative quality evaluation for instruction-tuned quantized models.** The paper evaluates instruction-tuned models (Llama-3-8B-Instruct, Llama-3-70B-Instruct) only on MMLU and common-sense reasoning — multiple-choice or classification benchmarks that do not require free-form text generation. There is no evaluation of generative quality: instruction following, response coherence, factual accuracy in open-ended generation, or task completion. This is a significant gap because quantization could degrade generative capabilities in ways that multiple-choice accuracy does not capture — e.g., increased repetitiveness, degraded discourse structure, or failure to maintain consistent persona across long responses. A follow-up would benchmark PrefixQuant-quantized instruction-tuned models on AlpacaEval 2.0, MT-Bench, or similar generative evaluation suites, comparing FP16 to W4A4KV4 and W4A8KV4 at both O1 and O2 settings. The hypothesis: W4A8KV4 will show minimal generative degradation (consistent with its near-lossless MMLU performance in Table 4), while W4A4KV4 may show measurable degradation on tasks requiring nuanced language generation even when multiple-choice accuracy is preserved. Establishing this boundary is practically important because many LLM deployments involve generative tasks (chat, summarization, writing assistance) where perplexity and benchmark accuracy are imperfect proxies for user-perceived quality.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**On-device and edge deployment of mid-sized LLMs with static quantization.** PrefixQuant-O2 (per-tensor static quantization) achieves 2.74× prefilling speedup and 2.16× decoding speedup on Llama-2-7B (Table 6) while matching or exceeding the accuracy of prior dynamic quantization methods. Static quantization is particularly valuable for edge deployment because it eliminates the overhead of computing per-token min-max ranges at runtime, enables ahead-of-time calibration of all quantization parameters, and simplifies operator fusion — all of which matter on resource-constrained hardware (mobile GPUs, laptop NPUs, embedded accelerators) where dynamic range computation may be unsupported or expensive. A team deploying Llama-3-8B on-device for a local assistant application can use PrefixQuant-O2 at W4A8KV4 (which achieves 72.46 accuracy vs. FP16's 72.71 on zero-shot tasks — a negligible 0.25-point drop, Table 3) with near-3× faster inference and roughly 4× lower memory footprint for weights, enabling 8B-parameter models to run interactively on consumer hardware where FP16 inference would be impractically slow or memory-bound.

**Cost-efficient batch inference pipelines for knowledge extraction and evaluation.** Organizations running large-scale batch evaluation (e.g., scoring candidate responses, extracting structured data from documents, processing millions of queries through an LLM pipeline) are throughput-bound rather than latency-bound — the total FLOPs per query directly determines cost. PrefixQuant at W4A4KV4 provides roughly 2.7× prefilling throughput improvement (Table 6) with accuracy that, on many models and tasks, approaches FP16: Llama-3-70B-Instruct at W4A4KV4 O1 achieves 77.66 accuracy vs. FP16's 77.96 (Table 15) — a 0.3-point drop for 4× memory savings and 2.7× speedup. For a batch pipeline processing 10 million queries per day, this translates to roughly one-third the GPU-hours, which at typical cloud GPU pricing can represent hundreds of thousands of dollars in annual savings. The key prerequisite — and this is where PrefixQuant's low preparation cost matters — is that teams can rapidly quantize new model versions as they are released, without the 12-hour grid search overhead of prior prefixing methods. PrefixQuant's 1-minute outlier detection for Llama-3-70B (Table 6) makes it feasible to maintain a quantized model registry that stays current with base model releases.

**Fine-tuning data generation and self-improvement loops with quantized models.** When LLMs are used to generate training data for themselves or smaller models (distillation, rejection sampling, STaR-style self-improvement), the quality of generated outputs at scale matters more than per-query latency. Using a full-precision 70B model to generate training data is expensive; using a W4A4KV4 quantized 70B model with PrefixQuant-O1 (which achieves 77.08 accuracy vs. FP16's 80.03 on Llama-3-70B, Table 2) reduces generation cost by ~2.7× with a ~3-point accuracy gap. Whether this gap is acceptable depends on the downstream use of the generated data — for tasks where the quantized model's errors are random rather than systematic, the increased volume enabled by lower cost may outweigh the per-sample quality reduction. The paper's results on instruction-tuned models (Table 15: Llama-3-70B-Instruct W4A8KV4 O1 actually slightly *exceeds* FP16 accuracy on MMLU-like benchmarks) suggest that instruction-tuned quantized models may be particularly well-suited for data generation, as the quantization noise may act as a beneficial regularizer that increases output diversity without substantially degrading correctness. This is a testable hypothesis that practitioners can evaluate on their specific data generation pipelines.

**Model serving with dynamic precision based on query difficulty or SLA tier.** The paper's difficulty-dependent analysis (Section 5.4, though primarily from the test-time compute paper in the reference example) suggests a deployment pattern where the appropriate quantization level is chosen per-query. Easy queries (simple factual lookups, straightforward summarization, template-based responses) could be served by a W4A4KV4 PrefixQuant-O2 quantized model at maximum throughput and minimum cost. Harder queries (complex reasoning, multi-step problem solving, creative generation) could be escalated to W4A8KV4 or even FP16. PrefixQuant's static quantization (O2) is particularly suited for this pattern because all quantization parameters are precomputed and can be swapped without runtime recalibration. A serving system could maintain multiple quantized copies of the same base model at different precision levels, route queries based on a lightweight difficulty classifier (perhaps trained on the model's own confidence signals), and dynamically trade off accuracy for throughput — a form of "quantization autoscaling" analogous to how cloud services already scale compute resources based on load. The paper's consistent performance across precision levels (O1 vs. O2, W4A4 vs. W4A8 in Tables 2–3) provides the accuracy foundation for such a system, though the difficulty classification and routing logic would be new engineering work.

### When to Prefer This Method

PrefixQuant is positioned against named alternatives with specific tradeoffs articulated in the paper. The decision framework follows from three axes: the target quantization granularity (dynamic vs. static), the acceptable preparation cost, and whether the model exhibits non-initial outlier tokens.

**Prefer PrefixQuant over QuaRot, DuQuant, or Atom when:**
- You are quantizing to **W4A4KV4** on models where these baselines show large accuracy degradation (particularly Llama-3-8B, Llama-3-70B, or models with severe token-wise outliers). The accuracy gap is large and consistent: +4.18 points over DuQuant on Llama-3-8B, +8.69 over QuaRot on Llama-3-70B (Table 2).
- You need **static quantization** (per-tensor activations) for deployment efficiency (operator fusion, no runtime min-max computation). PrefixQuant-O2 is the only method demonstrated to achieve competitive static quantization accuracy at 4 bits — QuaRot, DuQuant, and QoQ do not support static activation quantization at this precision level.
- You are quantizing **instruction-tuned models** where PrefixQuant's results are near-lossless (Llama-3-70B-Instruct W4A8KV4: 78.49 vs. 77.96 FP16, Table 15) while baselines are not evaluated on instruction-tuned variants.

**Prefer SpinQuant (or similar learned rotation methods) when:**
- Your primary evaluation metric is **WikiText2 perplexity** and you can train on the WikiText2 distribution. SpinQuant's PPL numbers benefit from training-test overlap that PrefixQuant (calibrated on Pile) does not have. For tasks with near-identical distribution to WikiText2, SpinQuant's learned rotations may be sufficient.
- You cannot afford **any fine-tuning** — SpinQuant achieves competitive results with rotation matrix training only, while PrefixQuant's best results include block-wise fine-tuning (though PrefixQuant without fine-tuning still outperforms QuaRot and sometimes DuQuant, as shown in the "w/o FT" rows in Table 14).

**Prefer no quantization (FP16) when:**
- The deployment has **very low inference volume** where quantization development cost exceeds the compute savings.
- The model's accuracy requirements are extremely stringent and even small degradation is unacceptable — PrefixQuant's best results approach but do not always match FP16 (e.g., Llama-3-8B W4A4KV4: 71.31 vs. 72.71 FP16, a 1.4-point gap, Table 2).
- The target task involves **long-form creative generation** or other open-ended tasks not evaluated in the paper, where quantization effects on generation quality are unknown.

**Prefer training-based outlier mitigation (Bondarenko et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2024) when:**
- You have access to the **pretraining pipeline and data** and can retrain the model from scratch or via continued pretraining. These methods modify the attention mechanism to prevent outliers from forming at the architectural level, potentially eliminating the problem entirely rather than working around it. PrefixQuant is preferable when you are working with a pretrained model you cannot retrain — which is the common case for practitioners using open-weight models.

**Caveat on cost:** PrefixQuant's outlier detection takes 12 seconds to 1 minute (Table 6). This is negligible compared to CushionCache's 12-hour grid search, but non-zero. For teams quantizing a single model once and deploying it millions of times, this cost is amortized to zero. For teams quantizing many model variants frequently (e.g., during iterative fine-tuning or architecture search), the cumulative cost of repeated outlier detection may become noticeable, though still far cheaper than alternatives.
