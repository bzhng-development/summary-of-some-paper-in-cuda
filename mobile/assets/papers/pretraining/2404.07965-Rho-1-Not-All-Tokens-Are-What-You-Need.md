# Rho-1: Not All Tokens Are What You Need

**ArXiv:** [2404.07965](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.07965)

## 🎯 Pitch

This paper introduces **Selective Language Modeling (SLM)**, a new pretraining objective that challenges the standard practice of applying a next-token prediction loss uniformly to all tokens.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **Selective Language Modeling (SLM)**, a new pretraining objective that challenges the standard practice of applying a next-token prediction loss uniformly to all tokens. The authors first analyze token-level training dynamics during standard causal language modeling on the OpenWebMath corpus with Tinyllama-1B and Mistral-7B, revealing that many tokens fall into categories of "easy tokens" already learned, "hard tokens" that resist convergence, or tokens exhibiting persistent loss fluctuations — motivating the core insight that only a minority of tokens show meaningful loss reduction during training. SLM operationalizes token selection by training a reference model on a high-quality corpus, then at each training step scoring every token using the excess loss between the current training model and the reference model (retaining only the top *k%* of tokens for loss computation), thereby focusing gradient updates on tokens that are both learnable and aligned with the desired distribution. When continually pretraining Mistral-7B on 15B OpenWebMath tokens, RHO-1 achieves a 10.4% average few-shot accuracy improvement over standard causal language modeling and matches DeepSeekMath-7B's performance using only 3% of the pretraining tokens (15B vs. 500B), establishing that token-level selection during language model pretraining yields substantial data efficiency gains — though the method's benefits concentrate on domains well-represented in the reference model's training distribution.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Uniform Training on Unequal Tokens

The fundamental assumption this paper challenges is that every token in a pretraining corpus deserves equal attention from the language model during training. Standard causal language modeling (CLM) — the workhorse objective behind virtually all modern large language models — applies the same next-token prediction cross-entropy loss to every token in every sequence, regardless of whether that token represents a mathematical derivation, a garbled string of HTML artifacts, a repetitive navigation menu, or a conjunction word the model has already mastered.

The paper's opening analysis (Section 2.1, Figure 3) demonstrates why this uniformity is problematic. When the authors tracked individual token losses throughout a 15B-token continual pretraining run of Tinyllama-1B on OpenWebMath, they found that only **26% of tokens** showed meaningful loss reduction (H→L: high loss at initialization that decreased substantially). The remaining tokens exhibited three patterns, none of which represent productive learning:

- **51% were already learned (L→L)**: These tokens started with low loss and stayed low, meaning the model was already making accurate predictions. Applying additional gradient updates to these tokens is computationally wasteful — they contribute negligible learning signal.
- **11% were persistently hard (H→H)**: These tokens maintained high loss throughout training, resisting convergence. As the paper discusses in Appendix D.2, many of these tokens originate from chaotic corpora containing custom symbols, unintelligible gibberish, timetables, and bibliographic references. Training on them likely adds noise rather than improving downstream capabilities.
- **12% showed unexpected loss increase (L→H)**: These tokens' losses rose during training — a counterintuitive dynamic where the model becomes worse at predicting certain tokens as training progresses, possibly reflecting interference between different types of knowledge or overfitting to corpus-specific patterns.

Even among the L→L and H→H categories, the authors observe that many tokens exhibit persistent, high-variance loss fluctuations (Figure 3b, 3c) rather than smooth convergence, particularly for tokens originating from noisy or irrelevant text (Appendix D.2, Figures 11-12 contain visualizations of these chaotic token sources).

This means that under standard CLM, roughly **three-quarters of the gradient updates during pretraining are directed at tokens that are either already mastered, inherently unpredictable, or actively detrimental to learning**. This is not merely inefficient — it potentially limits model quality by diluting the signal from genuinely useful tokens with noise.

### Why This Matters: Data Efficiency at Scale

The significance of this finding cannot be overstated in the context of modern LLM development. Pretraining has become astronomically expensive — DeepSeekMath-7B required 500 billion tokens of mathematical data (Table 1), LLaMA-2 used 2 trillion tokens, and frontier models consume tens of thousands of GPU-hours. If 75% of the compute is essentially wasted on tokens that don't contribute to useful learning, then solving the token selection problem could theoretically yield a **3-4× reduction in required pretraining compute** for equivalent downstream performance.

The paper's empirical results largely validate this intuition. RHO-1-7B, trained with selective language modeling on only 15B OpenWebMath tokens (of which ~10.5B were actually selected for loss computation — the 70% selection ratio specified in Section 3.1), achieves few-shot accuracy comparable to DeepSeekMath-7B trained on 500B tokens — a **~50× reduction in effective training tokens** (Figure 1, Table 1). Even accounting for the cost of training the reference model (0.5B tokens), this represents enormous practical savings.

Beyond cost, there is a theoretical significance: the finding that token-level selection meaningfully improves models challenges the implicit assumption in scaling laws research (Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022) that scaling model size and dataset size with a fixed loss formulation is the primary lever for improvement. This paper suggests that **how you train matters at least as much as how much you train**, opening a new axis for optimization that scaling laws have largely ignored.

### Prior Approaches and Their Limitations

The paper positions itself against three categories of prior work, each of which addresses data quality at a coarser granularity or with different objectives.

#### Document-Level Filtering: The Prevailing Approach

The dominant practice in LLM pretraining is document-level filtering — deciding whether an entire document should be included in the training corpus based on heuristics (language detection, length thresholds, perplexity scores), classifiers trained to identify high-quality text, or manual blocklists of problematic content (Brown et al., 2020; Wenzek et al., 2019; Raffel et al., 2020; Computer, 2023). The RedPajama-v2 dataset, for example, uses over 40 quality indicators for document-level filtering.

The paper identifies three specific failures of document-level filtering that motivate token-level selection:

**1. Token-level noise persists even in filtered documents.** Figure 2 (Upper) illustrates this with a concrete example: even in a mathematics corpus, a document might contain the text "The farm has 35 hens <Apr12 1:24> and 12 pigs. ##davidjl123 says totaling 47 animals." The metadata `<Apr12 1:24>`, the username `##davidjl123`, and the word "says" are all tokens that are irrelevant to mathematical reasoning but would all receive equal loss weight under CLM. Figure 12 and 13 show additional real examples from OpenWebMath where navigational elements, forum metadata, timestamps, and bibliographic references are interleaved with genuine mathematical content. Document-level filtering cannot remove these without also losing the useful mathematical text they're embedded in.

**2. Overly strict filtering discards useful data.** The paper cites evidence that aggressive filtering techniques like blocklists (Welbl et al., 2021) and Safety API filters can hurt evaluation loss or induce bias (Dodge et al., 2021; Longpre et al., 2023), and notes that "removing such tokens might alter the text's meaning, while overly strict filtering could exclude useful data" (Section 1). This is particularly acute for technical domains like mathematics where non-standard formatting, LaTeX commands, and specialized notation might trigger heuristic filters.

**3. Document-level curation doesn't guarantee token-level alignment.** Even high-quality documents contain distributional variation at the token level — common function words, punctuation, and formatting tokens appear in nearly all documents regardless of topic. The paper's analysis of token-level training dynamics (Section 2.1) demonstrates that many of these tokens are "easy" (L→L category) and receive wasteful gradient updates even in carefully curated corpora.

#### Sample-Level Data Selection: Right Idea, Wrong Granularity

A substantial body of prior work has explored sample-level selection — choosing which training examples (individual documents or sequences) to train on rather than uniformly training on all available data. The paper's related work section (Appendix B.2) comprehensively surveys this literature, dividing it into several threads:

- **Loss-based and uncertainty-based selection**: Methods like RHO-LOSS (Mindermann et al., 2022) train a small model on a holdout set and use the difference between training and holdout loss (excess loss) to select which samples to train on. Other approaches use perplexity-based filtering (Wenzek et al., 2019), or select samples with high uncertainty or gradient norm (Coleman et al., 2019; Katharopoulos and Fleuret, 2018).
- **Importance resampling**: Xie et al. (2024a) use n-gram models or datamodels to estimate importance weights for pretraining data relative to a target distribution. DoReMi (Xie et al., 2024b) optimizes domain weights to minimize worst-case excess loss relative to a reference model.
- **Curriculum learning** approaches that schedule when different types of data are introduced during training (Chen et al., 2024; Ma et al., 2024).

The paper identifies two critical limitations of sample-level approaches. First, **the granularity is too coarse**: even within a single high-quality document, some tokens carry the essential information while others are noise or filler. Sample-level methods cannot distinguish between the mathematical derivation and the forum signature line in the same document. Second, and more subtly, Kaddour et al. (2023) found that batch selection methods are "not computationally efficient" at scale — the overhead of scoring and selecting examples can outweigh the training speedup.

#### Token-Level Training Strategies for BERT: Different Architecture, Different Objective

The paper acknowledges prior work on token-level strategies for Masked Language Modeling (MLM) in BERT-like encoder models (Appendix B.2), but carefully distinguishes SLM from these approaches:

- **Selective masking** (Gu et al., 2020; Lad et al., 2022): Instead of randomly masking 15% of tokens during MLM pretraining, these methods bias masking toward tokens deemed "important" for downstream tasks. The objective is to focus the model's attention on learning informative token representations. However, this is fundamentally tied to the MLM objective where the model must reconstruct masked tokens from bidirectional context — the paradigm doesn't naturally transfer to autoregressive LM pretraining where tokens are predicted sequentially.

- **Token dropping** (Zhong et al., 2023a; Hou et al., 2022): These methods omit less important tokens from the input entirely to reduce the computational cost of the Transformer's self-attention. This is a computational efficiency technique, not a learning objective modification — dropped tokens aren't seen by the model at all, so the model can't learn their relationships to surrounding context when they do appear at inference time.

- **Error norm truncation** (Li et al., 2023e): This approach assesses token quality based on the skewness of the model's predicted probability distribution and truncates noisy tokens during training. It operates on the output side (masking loss on suspicious tokens) but uses a different scoring criterion (prediction distribution shape rather than reference model alignment).

The paper claims — plausibly — to be "the first to explore token-level data selection for large language model training, aimed at enhancing data quality and information density at the most fundamental granularity" (Appendix B.2). The distinction from BERT-era work is genuine: autoregressive LM pretraining at the billion-token scale presents different challenges and opportunities than MLM pretraining, and the reference-model-based scoring mechanism developed in this paper is conceptually distinct from prior token-level strategies.

### Reconciling Conflicting Observations in Training Dynamics Research

The paper also positions itself relative to prior work on language model training dynamics, particularly Xia et al. (2022), which examined token-level training trajectories across models of different sizes. Xia et al. concluded that tokens with little change in perplexity are "already learned" — an observation the paper partially confirms (the L→L category, which constitutes 51% of tokens) but argues is insufficiently nuanced. 

The paper's key contribution is identifying additional token patterns that Xia et al. did not characterize: tokens whose losses remain persistently high (H→H), tokens whose losses increase during training (L→H), and — crucially — tokens within the L→L and H→H categories that exhibit high-frequency loss fluctuations rather than stability (Figures 3b, 3c). These fluctuating tokens, when visualized (Appendix D.2, Figures 11-12), frequently correspond to noisy or irrelevant content. This more fine-grained categorization provides the empirical foundation for the paper's core claim: rather than all "already learned" tokens being benign, many actively contribute to training instability, and selectively removing their loss can improve overall training quality.

### The Broader Research Landscape: Where SLM Fits

The paper situates SLM within a broader trend toward **data-centric AI** (Appendix B.1-B.2), where optimizing the training data mixture — through synthesis, deduplication, filtering, domain reweighting, or curriculum design — is increasingly recognized as critical to model quality. However, the paper argues that most data-centric work operates at the document or domain level, leaving the token level as an underexplored frontier.

The paper also connects to scaling laws research (Appendix B.4) but makes an important conceptual distinction: "Most scaling laws on model performance study cross-entropy loss on all training tokens, while we focus on the tokens loss of desired distributions." In other words, standard scaling laws assume a fixed, uniform loss over tokens and study how performance changes as model size, data size, and compute scale. SLM introduces a new degree of freedom — which tokens receive loss — that standard scaling laws treat as constant. This opens the possibility of **scaling laws for selective training**, where the effective data quantity depends not just on the total token count but on the quality of token selection.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper frames SLM as a principled solution to a specific, well-characterized problem: the misallocation of gradient updates in standard CLM due to token-level heterogeneity in learnability and relevance. Rather than proposing a fundamentally new architecture or training algorithm, SLM is presented as a **plug-compatible modification** to the standard CLM objective — it simply zeroes out the loss for tokens that fall below a selection threshold based on excess loss relative to a reference model. This minimal design choice (Equation 4 is a straightforward modification of Equation 2) is deliberate: it means SLM can be integrated into existing training pipelines with no changes to model architecture, optimizer, or data loading, and incurs only the additional cost of computing reference model log-probabilities for input tokens (which the paper notes can be done once offline and cached).

The paper's ambitions are clearly stated in the conclusion and limitations (Appendix C): SLM is positioned as a **new axis for pretraining optimization** rather than a final solution. The authors explicitly flag generalizability, scalability to much larger models and datasets, and the necessity of training a reference model as open problems. The self-reference experiments (Section 3.4) — where the reference model is trained on the same pretraining corpus rather than a curated dataset — demonstrate that SLM can provide benefits even without access to high-quality target-distribution data, though the gains are more modest (+3.3% average improvement vs. +10-16% with a curated reference corpus).

This pragmatic framing — acknowledging both the method's strengths and its boundary conditions — is appropriate for a paper introducing a new pretraining paradigm. The core insight (unequal token importance) is well-motivated by empirical analysis, the proposed solution (SLM) directly addresses the identified problem, and the experimental results provide strong evidence for the method's effectiveness within the studied domains while leaving the question of broader applicability open for future work.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

This paper proposes a **pretraining objective modification** — not a new architecture, not a new dataset, but a change to *which tokens receive loss* during autoregressive language model training. The system being built is a language model called RHO-1 that is trained with **Selective Language Modeling (SLM)**, which zeroes out the cross-entropy loss on tokens deemed "undesirable" while training normally on "desirable" tokens, where desirability is determined by a reference model trained on high-quality data. The problem SLM solves is the misallocation of gradient updates during pretraining: standard causal language modeling applies equal loss to every token in every sequence, but the paper's training dynamics analysis shows that only about 26% of tokens exhibit meaningful learning, while the remaining 74% are either already mastered, inherently unpredictable, or actively noisy — SLM identifies the useful 26% (or a configurable fraction thereof) and concentrates learning on those tokens, yielding the same downstream performance with substantially fewer effective training tokens.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The SLM system has four major components, deployed sequentially in a pipeline:

1. **Reference Model (RM)** — a language model trained exclusively on a small, high-quality corpus that represents the *desired distribution* (e.g., curated mathematical text for math pretraining, or a blend of instruction-following datasets for general pretraining). The RM is typically the same base architecture and initialization as the model being pretrained, and it serves as a "taste discriminator": its token-level log-probabilities encode what well-formed, on-topic text looks like.

2. **Scoring Function** — a computation applied to each token in the main pretraining corpus using both the reference model's log-probability and (optionally) the training model's current log-probability. The primary scoring function is **excess loss**: the difference between the training model's loss on a token and the reference model's loss on that same token. Tokens with high excess loss are those the training model still finds difficult but the reference model finds easy — i.e., tokens that are *learnable* and *aligned with the desired distribution*.

3. **Token Selection Mechanism** — a per-batch ranking operation: all tokens in a training batch are scored, ranked by excess loss (or an alternative scoring function), and only the top *k%* are retained for loss computation. The remaining tokens have their loss set to zero (they still flow through the model for computing hidden states and attention, but their cross-entropy contributions are masked out). The selection ratio *k* is a hyperparameter (60% for the 1B model, 70% for the 7B model in the main experiments).

4. **Training Model** — the language model being pretrained, which uses the **Selective Language Modeling (SLM) objective** (Equation 4) instead of standard causal language modeling. It receives full input sequences, computes log-probabilities for all tokens, but backpropagates loss only on the selected subset. The training model and reference model are initialized from the same base checkpoint in all main experiments.

Information flows as follows: a batch of sequences from the pretraining corpus enters the system → the reference model computes log-probabilities for every token (this can be done once offline and cached since the RM is frozen) → the training model computes its own log-probabilities for every token → excess loss is computed per token as `$L_\theta(x_i) - L_{RM}(x_i)$` → tokens in the batch are ranked by excess loss → the top *k%* are selected → the SLM loss (Equation 4) is computed only on selected tokens → gradients flow backward through the training model, updating its parameters → the reference model remains frozen throughout.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the training dynamics analysis that motivated SLM — how tokens were categorized into four types, what proportions and patterns were observed, and why these patterns justify selective training. This is the empirical foundation for the entire method.
- **Second**, the reference model — what data it's trained on, how it's trained, and what property of the reference model makes it useful as a token scorer. This is the linchpin component that determines *what* gets selected.
- **Third**, the excess loss scoring function — the mathematical definition, why excess loss rather than raw loss or other alternatives, and how it operationalizes "learnable and aligned with the desired distribution." This is the core intellectual mechanism.
- **Fourth**, the selective language modeling objective — the formal modification to standard causal language modeling, how token selection is implemented in practice (per-batch ranking), the selection ratio hyperparameter, and how this relates to prior token-level strategies for BERT. This is the actual training procedure.
- **Fifth**, the self-reference variant — how SLM can work without a separate high-quality corpus, using alternative scoring functions (reference model loss directly, information entropy) and the intersection strategy. This establishes the method's applicability in data-constrained settings.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **method paper** whose core idea is that token-level selection during pretraining — guided by a reference model trained on high-quality data — can substantially improve data efficiency and downstream performance by concentrating gradient updates on tokens that are both learnable and aligned with the target distribution.

#### Training Dynamics Analysis: The Empirical Motivation

Before proposing any method, the paper establishes *why* token-level selection is necessary through a detailed analysis of how individual token losses evolve during standard pretraining (Section 2.1).

**Experimental setup for the analysis.** The authors continually pretrain Tinyllama-1B on 15B tokens from OpenWebMath using standard causal language modeling. They save model checkpoints after every 1B tokens of training data processed. At each checkpoint, they evaluate token-level cross-entropy loss on a held-out validation set of approximately 320,000 tokens from OpenWebMath. This yields, for each of the 320,000 validation tokens, a sequence of 16 loss values (one per checkpoint from 1B to 15B tokens trained). The same analysis is replicated for Mistral-7B (Figure 10 in Appendix D.1).

**Categorization procedure.** For each token's loss trajectory, the authors fit a linear regression line to the 16 checkpoint loss values and compute two quantities:

- `$\Delta L$`: the change in loss from the first checkpoint (1B tokens trained) to the last checkpoint (15B tokens trained), computed as the difference between the fitted values at the endpoints: `$L_{\text{end}} - L_{\text{start}}$`. A negative `$\Delta L$` means the loss decreased over training; a positive value means it increased.
- `$L_{\text{mean}}$`: the average loss across all tokens at the final checkpoint.

The authors then apply thresholds to categorize each token into one of four types:

- **H→L (high-loss to low-loss)**: Tokens with `$\Delta L < -0.2$`. These tokens started with high loss and exhibited substantial loss reduction during training. Intuitively, these are the tokens the model *learned* — their prediction became significantly more accurate. The paper reports that **only 26%** of tokens fall into this category for the 1B model (Figure 3a; Appendix D.1 notes 28% for the 7B model in Figure 10a).

- **L→H (low-loss to high-loss)**: Tokens with `$\Delta L > 0.2$`. These tokens started with low loss and *increased* during training — the model became worse at predicting them. The paper reports **12%** for the 1B model (13% for 7B).

- **L→L (low-loss stays low)**: Tokens with `$-0.2 \leq \Delta L \leq 0.2$` and final loss `$\leq L_{\text{mean}}$`. These tokens had low loss initially and remained low, with minimal change over training. These are "already learned" tokens that the model could predict accurately from the start. The paper reports **51%** for both the 1B and 7B models.

- **H→H (high-loss stays high)**: Tokens with `$-0.2 \leq \Delta L \leq 0.2$` and final loss `$> L_{\text{mean}}$`. These tokens started with high loss and stayed high — they resisted convergence throughout training. The paper reports **11%** for the 1B model (8% for 7B).

**What the thresholds mean.** The `$\pm 0.2$` threshold on `$\Delta L$` is a heuristic that separates tokens with meaningful loss change from those that are essentially flat. The paper acknowledges that this is a deliberate simplification — continuous loss trajectories are being discretized into four bins — but the proportions are stark enough that the qualitative pattern is robust: only about a quarter of tokens show meaningful learning, while half are already mastered and another quarter are either stagnant or regressing.

**Fluctuations within categories.** Even within the L→L and H→H categories, the paper makes a critical additional observation: many tokens exhibit *persistent high-variance fluctuations* rather than stability. Figures 3b and 3c (and Figures 10b and 10c for the 7B model) plot the loss trajectories of three example tokens from each category, showing loss values that oscillate substantially across checkpoints rather than smoothly converging. For example, an L→L token might oscillate between 0.05 and 0.25 loss (Figure 3b), and an H→H token might oscillate between 2.0 and 3.5 (Figure 3c). These fluctuations suggest that even for tokens whose average loss is stable, the model's predictions are noisy rather than confidently correct or consistently wrong.

**Visualizing what these tokens look like.** Appendix D.2 (Figures 11-12) provides concrete examples. Figure 11 shows sample text with tokens color-coded by category: blue for H→L (the "good" tokens), green for L→L, yellow for H→H, red for L→H. In a passage about mathematical problem-solving from a GMAT forum, the mathematical reasoning tokens tend to be blue (H→L), while forum metadata ("Followers:", "Kudos", "permalink"), timestamps, and navigational elements tend to be yellow or red (H→H or L→H). Figure 12 provides examples of tokens with "abnormal behavior" — including custom symbols from cellular automaton code, garbled LaTeX fragments, bibliographic references formatted as timetables, and forum signature lines. The paper's interpretation is that many of these tokens originate from "rather chaotic corpora" containing "a mix of custom symbols, unintelligible gibberish, and information such as timetables and bibliographic references."

**The causal interpretation.** The paper does not claim that H→H or L→L tokens are *harmful* in isolation. Rather, the argument is one of **opportunity cost and noise**: gradient updates on tokens that aren't learnable (H→H) or are already learned (L→L) represent computation that could have been directed at the 26% of tokens that actually show learning progress. Moreover, the fluctuating loss patterns suggest that training on these tokens may introduce noise into the optimization process, potentially destabilizing the learning of genuinely useful tokens. This interpretation is supported (though not directly proven) by the downstream results: selectively training only on tokens with high excess loss yields better performance than training on all tokens, even when controlling for total compute.

**The connection to the method.** This analysis directly motivates the design of SLM. The goal is to identify — at training time, without access to ground-truth categorization — which tokens belong to the H→L category (learnable and worth learning) versus the other three categories. The reference model plus excess loss scoring function (Section 2.2) is the proposed mechanism for making this identification.

#### Reference Model Training

The reference model is the cornerstone of SLM — it determines *which* tokens are considered desirable, and therefore which tokens receive loss during training. The paper's design philosophy is that the reference model should encode the **desired data distribution** that the training model should ultimately align with.

**Training data for math reference models.** For the mathematical domain experiments (Sections 3.1-3.2), the authors curate a dataset of **0.5B high-quality, math-related tokens** from multiple sources:

- Synthetic data from GPT models, specifically citing MetaMath (Yu et al., 2024) and Key-Point-Driven Data Synthesis (Huang et al., 2024). These are GPT-generated mathematical problems and solutions designed to cover diverse mathematical reasoning patterns.
- Manually curated data from MAmmoTH (Yue et al., 2024) and Ni et al. (2024). MAmmoTH is a hybrid instruction-tuning dataset combining chain-of-thought and program-of-thought reasoning traces; Ni et al. (2024) study which data is influential for mathematical reasoning.

The key property of this 0.5B-token corpus is that it is **small but clean**: every token is directly relevant to mathematical reasoning, with minimal noise, formatting artifacts, or off-topic content. This stands in contrast to the 14B-token OpenWebMath corpus used for pretraining, which — as the paper's training dynamics analysis shows — contains substantial quantities of non-mathematical tokens.

**Training data for general reference models.** For the general domain experiments (Section 3.3), the authors compile a dataset of **1.9B tokens** from open-source instruction-tuning datasets:

- Tulu-v2 (Ivison et al., 2023): a curated blend of instruction-following datasets including FLAN V2, CoT, Dolly, Open Assistant, and others.
- OpenHermes-2.5 (Teknium, 2023): a large-scale synthetic dataset of conversational and instructional data generated by various models.

The general reference model is thus trained on instruction-following and conversational data, which the paper treats as the "desired distribution" for general-purpose language model pretraining.

**Training hyperparameters.** The reference model is trained using standard causal language modeling (CLM, Equation 2) for **3 epochs** on the curated data, with the following hyperparameters (Section 3.1):

- Maximum learning rate: **5e-5** for 1B models, **1e-5** for 7B models.
- Learning rate schedule: **cosine decay**.
- Maximum sequence length: **2048 tokens** for 1B models, **4096 tokens** for 7B models.
- Multiple samples are **packed** into these sequence lengths (concatenated with appropriate separation tokens) to maximize GPU utilization.
- The reference model is initialized from the **same base model** as the training model in all main experiments. For math pretraining, both the reference model and the continual pretraining model start from Tinyllama-1.1B (for 1B experiments) or Mistral-7B (for 7B experiments). This is an important design choice: it means the reference model and training model share the same tokenizer and vocabulary, and the reference model's log-probabilities are directly comparable to the training model's, since they operate over the same embedding space.

**Why same initialization matters.** If the reference model used a different tokenizer or architecture, the per-token cross-entropy values would not be directly comparable — a loss of 2.5 under one model's probability distribution means something different than a loss of 2.5 under another's. By sharing initialization, tokenizer, and vocabulary, the excess loss computation (Equation 3) is well-defined: it compares how much harder the training model finds a token relative to the reference model, under the same probability space.

**What the reference model encodes.** After training on the curated high-quality data, the reference model assigns low perplexity (low loss) to tokens that are characteristic of the desired distribution, and high perplexity (high loss) to tokens that are uncharacteristic. For example, a math-trained reference model would find `$\frac{d}{dx}\sin(x) = \cos(x)$` to be predictable (low loss per token) because similar patterns appear in the training data, but would find `##davidjl123 says totaling` to be unpredictable (high loss per token) because such forum artifacts are absent from the curated mathematical corpus. This is exactly the property needed for token selection: the excess loss `$L_\theta - L_{RM}$` will be high for tokens that are (a) in-distribution for the desired data and (b) not yet learned by the training model.

**Computational cost of reference model training.** The paper does not explicitly state the GPU-hours for reference model training, but given that the model is trained for only 3 epochs on 0.5B tokens (or 1.9B tokens for general), this is a negligible fraction of the main pretraining cost. The 1B model trains on 15B+ tokens; the reference model training on 0.5B tokens represents roughly an additional 3% overhead. The paper's FLOPs efficiency claims implicitly include this cost, since the comparisons are against models trained on much larger corpora (e.g., DeepSeekMath-7B at 500B tokens).

**Alternative reference model sources.** The paper discusses in Appendix C that an open-source model or even a proprietary model API could serve as the reference model, since only input log-probabilities are needed. This would eliminate the reference model training cost entirely, though potentially at the expense of tokenizer compatibility (requiring token alignment techniques like those in Wan et al., 2024 or Fu et al., 2023, which the paper leaves for future work).

#### The Excess Loss Scoring Function

The scoring function is the mechanism that translates the reference model's knowledge into a per-token decision about whether to include each token in the loss computation. The paper's primary scoring function is **excess loss**, defined in Equation 3.

**Reference loss.** First, the reference model's loss on each token is computed:

$$L_{RM}(x_i) = -\log P_{RM}(x_i | x_{<i})$$

where `$x_i$` is the i-th token in the sequence, `$x_{<i}$` represents all tokens before position i, and `$P_{RM}(x_i | x_{<i})$` is the probability the frozen reference model assigns to the actual token `$x_i$` given the preceding context.

**What it computes:** the standard cross-entropy loss of the reference model on token `$x_i$` — how "surprised" the reference model is by this token in context. A low `$L_{RM}(x_i)$` (e.g., 0.1) means the reference model confidently predicted this token; a high `$L_{RM}(x_i)$` (e.g., 5.0) means the token was very unexpected under the reference model's learned distribution.

**Training model loss.** The training model computes its own loss on the same token:

$$L_\theta(x_i) = -\log P_\theta(x_i | x_{<i})$$

where `$P_\theta$` is the current training model's probability distribution (which evolves during training as parameters `$\theta$` are updated).

**Excess loss.** The excess loss is defined as the difference:

$$L_\Delta(x_i) = L_\theta(x_i) - L_{RM}(x_i)$$

where `$L_\theta(x_i)$` is the training model's current loss on token `$x_i$` and `$L_{RM}(x_i)$` is the frozen reference model's loss on the same token.

**What it computes:** how much *worse* the training model is at predicting token `$x_i$` compared to the reference model, in absolute cross-entropy units. A high `$L_\Delta(x_i)$` (e.g., 3.0) means the training model finds this token much harder than the reference model does — it is a token the training model *has not yet learned but could learn* (since the reference model, with similar capacity and initialization but trained on clean data, finds it predictable). A low `$L_\Delta(x_i)$` (e.g., 0.1) means the training model is already about as good as the reference model at predicting this token — it is either already learned or inherently easy. A negative `$L_\Delta(x_i)$` (which is possible if the training model assigns higher probability than the reference model) would indicate the training model is *better* than the reference model on this token, likely because the reference model is uncertain (high `$L_{RM}$`) and the training model has memorized or overfit this specific token.

**Why this form: the intuition.** The excess loss operationalizes two desirable properties simultaneously:

1. **Learnability:** A token can only have high excess loss if the reference model finds it predictable (low `$L_{RM}$`). Tokens with inherently high aleatoric uncertainty — random noise, garbled text, unpredictable forum artifacts — will have high `$L_{RM}$` (the reference model can't predict them either), so their excess loss will be low even if the training model also struggles. These tokens get filtered out.

2. **Not yet learned:** A token can only have high excess loss if the training model *currently* struggles with it (high `$L_\theta$`). Tokens the training model already predicts well will have low `$L_\theta$`, so their excess loss will be low regardless of the reference loss. These tokens also get filtered out.

Thus, high excess loss identifies tokens at the intersection of "predictable under the desired distribution" and "not yet mastered by the training model" — exactly the tokens that correspond to the H→L category from the training dynamics analysis, which are the tokens where meaningful learning occurs.

**Why excess loss over raw training loss.** If the selection criterion were simply highest training loss `$L_\theta$`, the selected tokens would include all the H→H tokens (inherently unpredictable noise) that the model can never learn to predict well, wasting compute on hopeless cases. If the selection criterion were lowest reference loss `$L_{RM}$`, it would select tokens that are easy under the desired distribution but potentially already mastered by the training model (L→L), again wasting compute. Excess loss elegantly avoids both failure modes by requiring *both* high trainability (low reference loss) *and* current deficiency (high training loss).

**Why excess loss over perplexity ratio.** An alternative formulation would be the ratio `$P_{RM} / P_\theta$` or `$L_\theta / L_{RM}$`. The difference formulation (excess loss) has the advantage of being additive and well-behaved at extremes: when `$L_{RM}$` is very small (near 0), the ratio becomes unstable, but the difference remains well-defined. It also directly connects to the RHO-LOSS formulation from Mindermann et al. (2022), which the paper acknowledges as mathematically identical but applied in a different context (sample-level selection for fine-tuning vs. token-level selection for pretraining).

**The relationship to RHO-LOSS.** Appendix B.2 explicitly addresses the connection. RHO-LOSS (reducible holdout loss) trains a small model on a random holdout set and uses the difference between training loss and holdout loss to select which training *examples* to keep. SLM differs in three ways: (1) the core idea is token-level selection motivated by training dynamics observations, with flexible scoring functions (not limited to excess loss — see Appendix H for alternatives); (2) the reference model is trained on *curated high-quality data* rather than a random holdout set, so it encodes a specific desired distribution rather than just a generalization target; (3) the selection operates at token granularity on large-scale pretraining (billions of tokens) rather than at sample granularity on small-scale fine-tuning tasks like MNIST and SST-2. The paper is transparent about the mathematical similarity: "Although excess loss is mathematically identical to RHO-LOSS, SLM differs in three important ways."

**Alternative scoring functions (Section 3.4, Appendix H).** The paper explores two additional scoring functions for the self-reference setting where no separate high-quality corpus is available:

- **Reference model loss `$L_{RM}(x_i)$`**: directly use the reference model's loss as the score, selecting tokens with *low* reference loss (since these are predictable under the reference model's distribution). The intuition is reversed from excess loss: here, the reference model is trained on the main pretraining corpus itself, so low reference loss indicates tokens that the reference model can predict well — i.e., clean, well-formed text — while high reference loss indicates noisy or garbled tokens. Higher `$L_{RM}$` means *lower* expectation of selection. In the self-reference experiments (Table 3), using `$L_{RM}$` alone with 70% selection ratio yields an average downstream improvement of +2.4% over the baseline.

- **Information entropy `$H_{RM}(x_i)$`**: defined in Equation 8 (Appendix H) as the entropy of the reference model's next-token probability distribution at position i:

$$H_{RM}(x_i) = -\sum_{k=1}^{V} P(t_k | x_{<i}; \theta_{RM}) \log P(t_k | x_{<i}; \theta_{RM})$$

where `$V$` is the vocabulary size, `$t_k$` is the k-th token in the vocabulary, and `$P(t_k | x_{<i}; \theta_{RM})$` is the reference model's predicted probability for token `$t_k$` given the preceding context.

**What it computes:** the Shannon entropy of the reference model's entire probability distribution over the vocabulary at position i. High entropy means the reference model is uncertain about which token comes next (the probability mass is spread across many tokens); low entropy means the reference model is confident (the probability mass is concentrated on one or a few tokens).

**Why entropy as a scoring function:** high entropy indicates "the uncertainty of the next token." The paper's intuition is that if the reference model — trained on the pretraining corpus itself — is still uncertain about a token's identity after training, that token is likely inherently unpredictable or noisy. By filtering out high-entropy tokens, the training model avoids wasting compute on tokens that resist convergence. Table 3 shows that using `$H_{RM}$` alone yields a similar improvement (+2.3% average at 60% selection). When intersecting the selections from both scoring functions (tokens that have both low reference loss AND low entropy), the improvement increases to +3.3% at 60% selection ratio.

**Why intersection works better:** the two scoring functions capture partially complementary signals. `$L_{RM}$` identifies tokens that are predictable (the reference model assigns high probability to the ground-truth token) — these tend to be clean, well-formed text. `$H_{RM}$` identifies tokens where the reference model is confident (sharp probability distribution) — these tend to be tokens in highly predictable contexts. Their intersection selects tokens that are both predictable AND in highly predictable contexts — the cleanest, most learnable tokens. The paper reports (Appendix H, Table 4) that at 60% selection ratio with both `$L_{RM}$` and `$H_{RM}$` filtering, the intersection contains approximately 40% of the original tokens (a 60% reduction in training tokens) while improving downstream accuracy by 3.3% — strong evidence that the filtered tokens were genuinely counterproductive.

#### The Selective Language Modeling (SLM) Objective

This is the actual training procedure — how the scoring function gets integrated into the pretraining loss computation.

**Standard causal language modeling (CLM).** The paper first restates the standard CLM objective for clarity (Equation 2):

$$L_{CLM}(\theta) = -\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \log P(x_i | x_{<i}; \theta)$$

where `$N$` is the sequence length, `$x_i$` is the i-th token, `$x_{<i}$` represents all preceding tokens, and `$\theta$` are the model parameters. **What it computes:** the average negative log-probability across all `$N$` tokens in the sequence — every token contributes equally to the loss regardless of its learnability or relevance.

**Selective language modeling (SLM).** The proposed modification (Equation 4) is:

$$L_{SLM}(\theta) = -\frac{1}{N \cdot k\%} \sum_{i=1}^{N} I_{k\%}(x_i) \cdot \log P(x_i | x_{<i}; \theta)$$

where `$k\%$` is the token selection ratio (e.g., 0.6 for 60%), `$N \cdot k\%$` is the number of selected tokens (the denominator normalizes the loss by the actual number of tokens receiving loss, not the total sequence length), and `$I_{k\%}(x_i)$` is the indicator function defined in Equation 5:

$$
I_{k\%}(x_i) = \begin{cases} 
1 & \text{if } x_i \text{ ranks in the top } k\% \text{ by } S(x_i) \\
0 & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
$$

where `$S(x_i)$` is the scoring function — by default, the excess loss `$L_\Delta(x_i)$`.

**What it computes:** the average cross-entropy loss only on tokens that rank in the top `$k\%$` according to the scoring function, with the sum normalized by the *number of selected tokens* rather than the total sequence length. Tokens with `$I_{k\%}(x_i) = 0$` effectively have their cross-entropy term zeroed out — they still flow through the model's forward pass (the model still computes hidden states and attention for them, since they provide context for subsequent tokens), but they contribute nothing to the loss and therefore produce no gradient updates.

**Implementation detail: per-batch ranking.** The paper specifies (Section 2.2) that "token selection can be implemented by ranking the tokens in a batch according to their excess loss and using only the top `$k\%$` of tokens for training." This means the ranking and selection happen *within each training batch*, not globally across the entire corpus. A batch contains multiple sequences concatenated and chunked to the maximum sequence length; all tokens in the batch are scored, ranked, and the top `$k\%$` are selected. This is computationally efficient because it requires no additional passes over the data — the scores are computed during the forward pass (the training model's `$L_\theta$` is already being computed, and the reference model's `$L_{RM}$` can be pre-computed and cached).

**Why per-batch ranking rather than global ranking:** global ranking across billions of tokens would require either multiple passes over the data or maintaining a running score distribution, both of which add complexity. Per-batch ranking ensures that every batch contributes some gradient signal (since the top `$k\%$` of tokens in each batch are selected), while still enforcing selectivity: within each batch, only the most learnable and distribution-aligned tokens receive loss. The paper does not discuss whether per-batch ranking might introduce biases (e.g., a batch of very clean text might have all tokens selected because they all have high excess loss relative to each other, while a batch of noisy text might have all tokens filtered, resulting in a zero-gradient batch), but this is a relatively minor concern in practice given the large batch sizes (1M tokens per batch; Section 3.1).

**Selection ratio `$k\%$`.** The paper sweeps this hyperparameter and reports the best values:

- **60%** for Tinyllama-1.1B math pretraining.
- **70%** for Mistral-7B math pretraining.

Figure 9 shows an ablation of the selection ratio on the 1B model trained on 5B tokens. Accuracy on GSM8K and MATH is highest at 60% and drops both below and above this value. At lower ratios (40-50%), the paper hypothesizes that too many useful tokens are being discarded; at higher ratios (80-100%), too many noisy or already-learned tokens are being included, diluting the gradient signal.

The optimal selection ratio depends on two factors: the noise level in the pretraining corpus (noisier corpora benefit from lower ratios) and the capacity of the model (larger models can absorb more noisy tokens without degradation). The fact that the 7B model uses a higher ratio (70%) than the 1B model (60%) is consistent with the intuition that larger models are more robust to noisy training signals.

**Why not reweight tokens instead of hard selection?** The paper uses hard selection (indicator function, binary inclusion/exclusion) rather than soft reweighting (multiplying each token's loss by a continuous weight). Hard selection has two advantages: (1) it is simpler to implement and tune (one hyperparameter `$k\%$` vs. a weighting function with multiple parameters); (2) it completely eliminates gradient updates from excluded tokens rather than just downweighting them, which more aggressively reduces noise. The paper acknowledges in Appendix C that "reweighting tokens instead of selecting may improve robustness" as a natural extension.

**Relationship to token dropping in BERT.** The paper explicitly distinguishes SLM from token dropping approaches for BERT (Zhong et al., 2023a; Hou et al., 2022) in Appendix B.2. Token dropping *removes tokens from the input* to reduce self-attention computation — dropped tokens are not seen by the model at all. In contrast, SLM *retains all tokens in the input* (the model still processes them and can attend to them as context) but *masks their loss contribution*. This is a crucial difference: token dropping changes what the model sees, potentially breaking long-range dependencies or losing important contextual information. SLM changes only what the model learns from, preserving full context for prediction while focusing gradient updates on informative tokens. The analogy is not perfect (BERT is bidirectional, SLM is for autoregressive LMs), but the conceptual distinction matters.

**Computational overhead of SLM.** The paper claims (Section 2.2) that "this process eliminates the loss for undesired tokens without incurring additional costs during pretraining, making our approach both efficient and easily integrated." This claim requires qualification. The additional computational cost comes from:

1. **Reference model forward pass:** The reference model must compute log-probabilities for every token in the pretraining corpus. This is a one-time cost that can be done before training and cached. For a corpus of 15B tokens with a 1B-parameter reference model, this is non-trivial but small relative to the total pretraining compute (a single forward pass is roughly 1/3 the cost of a forward-backward pass; the reference model forward pass is equivalent to ~5B tokens of training compute, or about 1/3 of the total 15B training budget).

2. **Token ranking within each batch:** Ranking N tokens by score and selecting the top k% is O(N log N) in the number of tokens, which is negligible compared to the Transformer forward-backward pass.

The paper's claim of "no additional costs" should be understood as "no additional costs *during the training backward pass*" — the gradient computation and optimizer step are identical to standard CLM, just with a masked loss tensor. The reference model forward pass is an additional upfront cost, but it is relatively small and can be amortized across multiple training runs or model sizes. The self-reference variant (Section 3.4) eliminates even this cost by reusing the training model itself (at an earlier checkpoint) as the reference model.

**The full training procedure, step by step:**

1. **Before pretraining begins:** Train the reference model on the high-quality curated corpus using standard CLM. Save the reference model checkpoint. Then, run the reference model in inference mode over the entire pretraining corpus, computing `$L_{RM}(x_i)$` for every token. Store these values (one float per token) on disk — this adds approximately 4 bytes per token of overhead, or 60 GB for a 15B-token corpus, which is manageable for modern storage systems.

2. **During each training step:** Load a batch of sequences from the pretraining corpus, along with the corresponding pre-computed `$L_{RM}$` values for each token. Run the forward pass of the training model to compute `$L_\theta(x_i)$` for every token. On-the-fly, compute `$L_\Delta(x_i) = L_\theta(x_i) - L_{RM}(x_i)$` for each token. Within the batch, rank all tokens by `$L_\Delta(x_i)$`. Set `$I_{k\%}(x_i) = 0$` for all tokens below the `$k\%$` threshold. Compute the SLM loss (Equation 4) using the masked loss tensor. Backpropagate through the training model normally — the mask only affects which positions contribute to the scalar loss, not the architecture or forward computation.

3. **Periodic re-computation (optional):** The paper notes in Appendix G.2 that token selection preferences shift during training (visualized in Figure 14). The theoretical reason is that as the training model improves, `$L_\theta$` decreases for learned tokens, changing which tokens have high excess loss. The paper does not specify a re-computation schedule in the main experiments, but the dynamic analysis (Figure 8, discussed in Section 3.5) suggests that tokens selected by later checkpoints correspond to higher-perplexity tokens that resist convergence, indicating adaptive selection over time. The main experiments appear to use a single pre-computed reference model throughout training.

#### The Self-Reference Variant: SLM Without Curated Data

Section 3.4 addresses a practical limitation: what if the practitioner does not have access to a high-quality curated corpus for training the reference model? The self-reference variant demonstrates that SLM can still provide benefits using only the main pretraining corpus, albeit with more modest gains.

**Procedure.** The reference model is trained on the same corpus that will be used for pretraining (e.g., OpenWebMath). The scoring function is then applied to the same corpus. The intuition shifts: rather than selecting tokens aligned with an external desired distribution, the self-reference approach selects tokens that are *predictable by a model trained on the corpus itself* — essentially, tokens that represent clean, learnable patterns within the corpus, excluding noisy outliers.

**Scoring function alternatives.** Two scoring functions are explored:

- **`$L_{RM}(x_i)$` (reverse selection):** tokens with *low* reference loss are selected, under the rationale that these are predictable and therefore likely to be clean, well-formed text. The paper reports modest improvements over the baseline (Table 3: +2.4% average with 70% selection ratio on OpenWebMath).

- **`$H_{RM}(x_i)$` (entropy-based):** tokens with *low* information entropy are selected, under the rationale that these represent confident predictions (sharp probability distributions) and are therefore well-specified. Similar improvement (+2.3% at 60% selection).

- **Intersection `$L_{RM} \cap H_{RM}$`:** tokens that are selected by *both* scoring functions. This yields the best results (+3.3% average improvement at 60% selection ratio on OpenWebMath, using only 9B out of 15B tokens — a 40% reduction in training tokens while improving performance).

**Extension to larger corpora.** Table 3 also reports an experiment where the reference model is trained on OpenWebMath (14B tokens, a subset of Proof-Pile-2) and used to score tokens in Proof-Pile-2 (55B tokens). The result is a +1.8% average improvement while using only 36B tokens (a 30% reduction). This is notable because the reference model was trained on only a subset of the corpus being scored — it demonstrates that the reference model can generalize its "cleanliness" assessment to related but distinct data.

**Why self-reference works at all.** The self-reference variant essentially performs **noise filtering**: the reference model, trained on the same data, learns to predict the regular patterns in the data well and struggles with the noisy, unpredictable outliers. By selecting low-loss or low-entropy tokens, the training procedure excludes the outliers and focuses on the regular patterns — akin to denoising the corpus at the token level. The gains are smaller than when using an external curated reference corpus (+3.3% vs. +10-16%) because the reference model doesn't encode any distribution shift toward desirable content — it only identifies clean tokens within the existing distribution, not tokens that are specifically useful for downstream tasks.

#### Weak-to-Strong Generalization: Smaller Reference Model for Larger Training Model

Appendix I reports an auxiliary experiment: can a 1B-parameter reference model effectively guide the pretraining of a 7B-parameter training model? This tests whether the reference model needs to be the same size as the training model for effective token selection.

**Setup.** Tinyllama-1.1B is trained as the reference model on the math curated corpus. Llama-2-7B is then continually pretrained on 15B OpenWebMath tokens using SLM with the 1B reference model's scores, at a 70% selection ratio (10.5B tokens actually trained on).

**Results (Table 5).** Despite the 7× parameter gap between reference and training models, SLM with the smaller reference model still improves average few-shot accuracy from 43.5% (standard CLM baseline) to 44.4%. The gains (+0.9 percentage points) are much more modest than when using a same-size reference model (+10.4 percentage points, Table 1), but they are positive — the smaller reference model still provides a useful signal for token selection.

**Why this matters.** This result has practical significance: if a small reference model can be reused across multiple larger training runs (potentially with different architectures, tokenizers with alignment, or data mixtures), the amortized cost of reference model training becomes negligible. The paper acknowledges that cross-vocabulary token alignment (Wan et al., 2024; Fu et al., 2023) would be necessary for models with different tokenizers, and leaves this to future work.

#### Summary of Design Choices and Their Justifications

- **Excess loss over raw training loss or reference loss alone**: excess loss selects for the conjunction of "predictable under desired distribution" (low `$L_{RM}$`) and "not yet learned" (high `$L_\theta$`), filtering out both inherently unpredictable noise and already-mastered tokens. Raw training loss would select for noise; raw reference loss would select for already-learned tokens.

- **Hard selection (indicator function) over soft reweighting**: eliminates gradient updates from excluded tokens entirely rather than downweighting them, providing a stronger noise-reduction effect with a single tunable hyperparameter (`$k\%$`). Soft reweighting is acknowledged as a potential robustness improvement for future work.

- **Per-batch ranking over global ranking**: computationally efficient (no additional data passes), ensures every batch contributes gradient signal, and adapts to local variations in token quality within the corpus.

- **Same initialization for reference and training models**: ensures tokenizer and vocabulary compatibility, and makes the excess loss computation directly meaningful (same probability space). Appendix I shows this is beneficial but not strictly necessary — a smaller reference model with a different architecture still provides useful signal.

- **Reference model trained on curated high-quality data** (not a random holdout set, as in RHO-LOSS): encodes a specific *desired distribution* rather than just a generalization target, enabling SLM to shift the training model's learned distribution toward higher-quality, downstream-relevant patterns.

- **Selection ratio of 60% for 1B, 70% for 7B**: empirically tuned (Figure 9); values reflect a tradeoff between excluding too many useful tokens (low ratio) and including too many noisy/already-learned ones (high ratio). The higher ratio for larger models is consistent with the intuition that larger models are more robust to noisy gradient signals.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Token-Level Training Dynamics as a Diagnostic Framework

The paper's most distinctive intellectual contribution is not the SLM method itself but the **diagnostic decomposition of token-level training trajectories** that precedes and justifies it. Prior work on language model training dynamics (Saphra and Lopez, 2018; Choshen et al., 2021; Xia et al., 2022) studied aggregate phenomena — how internal representations evolve, when linguistic knowledge is acquired, or how overall perplexity decreases. The field's implicit assumption was that loss reduction during pretraining is broadly distributed across tokens, with variation primarily reflecting differences in semantic complexity or frequency. 

This paper overturns that assumption with a simple but revealing analysis (Section 2.1, Figure 3): by fitting linear trends to each token's loss trajectory over 16 checkpoints (1B to 15B tokens trained) and categorizing 320,000 validation tokens into four discrete patterns (H→L, L→L, H→H, L→H), the authors demonstrate that **only 26% of tokens show meaningful learning** — the rest are either already mastered (51% L→L), inherently resistant to convergence (11% H→H), or actively degrading (12% L→H). This is not a small efficiency gap; it suggests that under standard CLM, roughly three-quarters of gradient updates are directed at tokens that contribute minimal or negative learning signal.

What makes this diagnostic move distinctive is its **explanatory power for phenomena the field had observed but not explained**. The finding that many L→L and H→H tokens exhibit high-variance loss fluctuations rather than stability (Figures 3b, 3c) provides a mechanistic account for why pretraining can feel "noisy" even as aggregate validation loss smoothly decreases — the model is oscillating between learning and unlearning on a large fraction of tokens, creating internal interference that the aggregate metric masks. The visualization of these tokens in Appendix D.2 (Figures 11-12), showing that they correspond to forum metadata, garbled LaTeX, bibliographic references, and custom symbols, connects the abstract loss patterns to concrete corpus artifacts.

The significance of this diagnostic framework extends beyond the current paper. It establishes **token-level training dynamics as a first-class object of study** — a lens through which pretraining quality, data curation, and learning efficiency can be analyzed at finer granularity than aggregate perplexity or downstream benchmarks. The field now has a vocabulary (H→L, L→L, H→H, L→H categories) and a methodology (linear fitting to checkpoint loss trajectories with `$\Delta L$` thresholds) for diagnosing *where* in the corpus learning is actually happening. This is analogous to how layer-wise analysis of neural network representations (e.g., probing classifiers) gave researchers tools to study what models know and when they learn it — the paper contributes a similar diagnostic apparatus for the input side.

Compared to the closest prior work (Xia et al., 2022), which classified tokens simply as "already learned" or not based on perplexity change, the four-category decomposition is a qualitative refinement. Xia et al.'s binary classification would have lumped L→L, H→H, and L→H tokens together as "not showing productive learning," missing the crucial distinction between tokens that are *benignly stable* (L→L), *pathologically stagnant* (H→H), and *actively regressing* (L→H). The additional identification of within-category fluctuations (the "non-converging tokens" discussion in Appendix D.2) further refines the picture by showing that even tokens with stable averages can exhibit instability that standard summary statistics hide.

The diagnostic framework also **recontextualizes the data filtering literature**. Prior work on data quality (Brown et al., 2020; Wenzek et al., 2019; Computer, 2023) operated at the document level and evaluated filtering by downstream task performance or aggregate loss — essentially treating data quality as a pre-processing problem. The token-level dynamics analysis suggests that data quality is better understood as a **training-time interaction problem**: the same token might be "easy" for a well-trained model but "hard" for an early checkpoint, and the training signal it provides depends on when it appears relative to the model's current state. This temporal dimension of data quality — that utility changes over the course of training — is absent from static filtering approaches and is a genuinely new perspective.

### Innovation 2: The Reference Model as a Distributional Filter

The second conceptual innovation is the specific role assigned to the reference model in SLM — not as a teacher in distillation, nor as a holdout-based generalization proxy (as in RHO-LOSS), but as a **learned filter that encodes a *desired data distribution* and projects it onto a larger, noisier corpus at the token level**. This framing changes how we think about the relationship between curated and uncurated data in pretraining.

To appreciate this shift, consider the dominant paradigms for using small amounts of high-quality data in pretraining. The first is **data mixing**: blend the curated data directly into the pretraining corpus and train on everything uniformly, hoping the model absorbs the desirable patterns. The limitation is that the curated data is usually too small to dominate the loss — 0.5B curated tokens mixed into 15B OpenWebMath tokens would constitute only ~3% of training examples, and standard CLM has no mechanism to preferentially attend to those tokens. The second paradigm is **curriculum learning**: schedule when different data types appear during training (Chen et al., 2024; Ma et al., 2024). The limitation is that curriculum design is coarse-grained (it operates at the domain or document level) and requires specifying a schedule, which is itself a complex optimization problem. The third paradigm is **importance weighting**: use the curated data to train a classifier or compute importance scores for the larger corpus (Xie et al., 2024a; Engstrom et al., 2024). The limitation is that these approaches typically compute document-level weights, missing the token-level heterogeneity that SLM exploits.

SLM's reference model approach is distinctive because it performs **token-level distributional filtering without mixing**: the curated data never enters the training model's loss computation directly. Instead, the reference model is trained on the curated data to learn what predictable, well-formed text from the desired distribution looks like — specifically, it learns to assign low log-probability (low loss) to tokens that are characteristic of the curated data. This learned "taste" is then applied to every token in the pretraining corpus, and the excess loss mechanism (Equation 3) selects tokens where the training model still finds the curated-distribution-aligned text difficult — i.e., tokens that represent *learnable gaps* between the current model and the desired distribution.

The conceptual elegance of this approach is that it **separates the *what-to-learn* signal from the *where-to-learn* signal**. The reference model answers "what should the model ultimately be good at predicting?" (math derivations, clean reasoning traces). The excess loss answers "where is the current model failing at those things?" (tokens in the pretraining corpus that resemble curated-distribution text but are still poorly predicted). This decomposition means the curated data needs to be only large enough to train a reasonably calibrated reference model (0.5B tokens suffices, per Section 3.1), not large enough to provide the bulk of training signal directly — a separation that dramatically reduces the amount of curated data needed for effective domain adaptation.

This reframes the role of curated data in pretraining from **content** (something the model memorizes or learns patterns from directly) to **taste** (something that defines a target distribution for scoring and filtering). This is a conceptual shift that parallels developments in other areas of machine learning — the reference model acts analogously to a discriminator in GANs or a reward model in RLHF, providing a learned signal that guides training without being the training target itself. The paper does not make this connection explicitly, but it is implicit in the architecture.

The significance of this innovation is partly demonstrated by the **weak-to-strong generalization** result (Appendix I, Table 5): a 1B-parameter reference model can provide useful token-level guidance for a 7B-parameter training model, despite being trained on different starting weights (Tinyllama vs. Llama-2). This suggests the reference model encodes something about the *properties* of the desired distribution (cleanliness, relevance, predictability) that transfers across model scales and architectures, not just specific token-level predictions. The gain is modest (+0.9 percentage points, compared to +10.4 with a same-scale reference model), but the fact that it is positive at all is theoretically interesting — it implies the reference model's scoring function captures distributional properties that are somewhat model-agnostic.

### Innovation 3: Empirical Demonstration That Token-Level Selection Unlocks Order-of-Magnitude Data Efficiency Gains

While the diagnostic framework (Innovation 1) and the reference model as distributional filter (Innovation 2) are conceptual contributions, the paper's third innovation is the **empirical demonstration that these ideas translate into dramatic practical gains** — specifically, that token-level selection during pretraining can reduce required training data by a factor of ~50× while matching downstream performance.

This claim might sound like a benchmark result rather than an innovation, but what makes it intellectually significant is that it **provides the first clear evidence for a specific hypothesized inefficiency in standard pretraining** and quantifies just how large that inefficiency is. The hypothesis — that uniform next-token prediction wastes compute on tokens that don't contribute to useful learning — is intuitive but had not been empirically validated at scale. Prior work on data selection for pretraining (Xie et al., 2024a,b; Albalak et al., 2024) had shown that document-level filtering and domain reweighting can provide modest improvements (typically 5-15% efficiency gains), but nothing approaching the 50× figure that RHO-1 demonstrates (15B selected tokens matching 500B uniformly-trained tokens; Table 1, DeepSeekMath comparison).

The key result that substantiates this innovation is Table 1's comparison between RHO-1-7B (trained on 15B OpenWebMath tokens with SLM, of which ~10.5B received loss) and DeepSeekMath-7B (trained on 500B math-related tokens with standard CLM). RHO-1 achieves an average few-shot accuracy of 66.2% versus DeepSeekMath's 68.4% — statistically comparable performance despite a ~50× reduction in effective training tokens. Even accounting for the reference model training data (0.5B curated tokens) and the cost of computing reference model scores (one forward pass over 15B tokens), the total token budget is ~15.5B versus 500B. While DeepSeekMath uses a different base model, different pretraining data mixture (120B unique tokens from Common Crawl math-related pages, vs. 14B unique for OpenWebMath), and potentially different training configurations, the order-of-magnitude difference is too large to be entirely explained by these confounds.

The significance of this result is not just the magnitude but the **conceptual implication for scaling laws**. Standard scaling laws (Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022) predict model performance as a function of model parameters `$N$` and training tokens `$D$`, assuming a fixed, uniform loss function over tokens. Under this framework, reducing `$D$` by 50× would be expected to substantially reduce performance. The fact that SLM achieves comparable performance with 50× fewer tokens suggests that the relevant variable in scaling laws is not *total tokens seen* but *effective tokens that contribute to useful learning* — a quantity that depends on both the corpus quality and the training objective's ability to focus on useful tokens. This implies that **scaling laws with token selection as an additional degree of freedom** would predict different (more optimistic) efficiency frontiers than standard scaling laws. The paper does not develop this theoretical point explicitly, but it is a direct implication of the results and represents a potential reframing of how the field thinks about compute-optimal training.

The **self-reference results** (Section 3.4, Table 3) provide a complementary demonstration. Even without access to curated data — using only the pretraining corpus itself to train the reference model — SLM improves downstream performance by 3.3% while using 40% fewer training tokens. This is a more modest gain but is significant because it demonstrates that token-level selection provides benefits beyond distribution matching: it also functions as a **noise filter**, removing tokens that are intrinsically unpredictable (garbled text, formatting artifacts) and allowing the model to concentrate on learnable patterns. The fact that intersecting two scoring functions (reference loss and entropy, both derived from a model trained on the same corpus) improves over either alone suggests that these functions capture complementary aspects of token quality — predictability and contextual certainty — that cannot be captured by document-level heuristics.

The **downstream loss correlation analysis** (Section 3.5, Figure 7) adds causal weight to the empirical argument. By showing that the loss on *selected* tokens follows a power-law relationship with downstream accuracy (lower selected-token loss → higher accuracy; Figure 7a) while the loss on *unselected* tokens shows the *opposite* relationship (higher unselected-token loss → higher accuracy; Figure 7b), the paper provides evidence that the token selection mechanism is not merely identifying tokens that are lossy or hard — it is specifically identifying tokens whose learnability correlates with downstream usefulness. This is a stronger claim than "SLM improves benchmarks" because it suggests the selection mechanism captures a property that generalizes beyond the training objective to task performance.

### Innovation 4: The Excess Loss as a Dynamic, Self-Adjusting Selection Criterion

The fourth innovation is a subtle but important property of the excess loss scoring function: it is **inherently dynamic and self-adjusting over the course of training**, even when the reference model is frozen. This property is largely implicit in the paper — the authors do not frame it as a central contribution — but it emerges from the analysis in Section 3.5 and Appendix G.2 and represents a qualitatively different kind of token selection than static filtering approaches.

To see why this matters, consider static token filtering: before training begins, one scores all tokens in the corpus (e.g., by perplexity under some model, or by a classifier, or by heuristics) and removes those below a threshold. The selection is fixed — the same tokens are trained on (or excluded) throughout the entire pretraining run. This approach has a fundamental limitation: a token that is "hard" for an untrained model might become "easy" after a few billion tokens of training, at which point continuing to train on it is wasteful. Conversely, a token that is "easy" early in training might become "hard" later as the model's learned distribution shifts (accounting for the L→H pattern observed in 12% of tokens). Static filtering cannot adapt to these changes.

The excess loss `$L_\Delta(x_i) = L_\theta(x_i) - L_{RM}(x_i)$` is dynamic because `$L_\theta(x_i)$` — the training model's current loss — changes over the course of training. As the training model improves, `$L_\theta$` decreases for tokens it has learned, reducing their excess loss and potentially dropping them below the selection threshold. Simultaneously, tokens that the training model initially predicted well but later finds more difficult (the L→H pattern) will have their excess loss increase, potentially bringing them *into* the selected set.

The paper provides suggestive evidence for this dynamic behavior in Figure 8 and Appendix G.2. Figure 8 shows that tokens selected by later checkpoints tend to have higher perplexity at those later stages — i.e., the selection mechanism naturally shifts toward harder tokens as training progresses. Appendix G.2 (Figure 14) visualizes how token selection preferences change across four checkpoints (0%, 33%, 66%, 100% of training), with color-coded preferences showing that some tokens become more favored (deeper blue) and others less favored (shifting to orange) over time. The paper also observes a "sample-wise double descent" pattern where the perplexity of selected tokens "initially increases before decreasing" — interpreted as the model "first optimizing tokens with a larger learnable space, thereby increasing learning efficiency."

What makes this dynamic property innovative is that it **eliminates the need for a separate curriculum schedule**. Standard curriculum learning requires specifying *when* to introduce different types of training data — a complex hyperparameter optimization problem that depends on model size, data characteristics, and training duration. The excess loss mechanism achieves a form of **emergent curriculum** without explicit scheduling: it naturally selects for tokens that are currently at the frontier of the model's learning capability, filtering out tokens that are either too easy (already learned) or too hard (inherently unpredictable). As the model improves, the frontier shifts, and the selection adapts automatically.

This property distinguishes SLM from RHO-LOSS (Mindermann et al., 2022), despite the mathematical identity of the scoring function. RHO-LOSS was designed for small-scale fine-tuning where the training dynamics are relatively simple and the holdout set is static. In that context, the dynamic property of excess loss is less significant because the model converges quickly and the scoring function is computed infrequently. In the pretraining context, where training spans billions of tokens and the model's capabilities evolve substantially, the dynamic property becomes crucial — it is what allows SLM to maintain efficiency throughout the entire training run rather than only providing benefits early on.

The paper does not fully exploit or characterize this dynamic property in the main experiments. The reference model loss is pre-computed once and cached; the training model loss is computed fresh at each step, so the excess loss is effectively recomputed every batch. However, the paper does not study how frequently re-computing the reference model loss (i.e., updating the reference model from a later checkpoint) would affect performance, or whether the dynamic property alone (without a curated reference model) could drive meaningful curriculum effects. These are natural extensions that the paper's analysis enables but does not pursue — the innovation is in identifying and providing initial evidence for the dynamic selection property, not in fully characterizing it.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The paper uses two primary pretraining corpora. For mathematical reasoning experiments, it uses **OpenWebMath (OWM)** (Paster et al., 2023), comprising roughly 14B tokens from math-related web pages in Common Crawl (Section 3.1). For general domain experiments, it combines **SlimPajama** (Daria et al., 2023) and **StarCoderData** (Li et al., 2023a) — both part of the Tinyllama corpus — with OpenWebMath in a 6:3:1 mix ratio, training on a total of 80B tokens. The reference model for math is trained on a curated 0.5B-token dataset blending synthetic GPT data (Yu et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2024) and manually curated data (Yue et al., 2024; Ni et al., 2024). The general reference model uses a 1.9B-token corpus from Tulu-v2 (Ivison et al., 2023) and OpenHermes-2.5 (Teknium, 2023).

- **Base model(s).** Two model scales are used. For 1B-scale experiments, the base model is **Tinyllama-1.1B** (Zhang et al., 2024), a publicly available pretrained model. For 7B-scale experiments, the base model is **Mistral-7B** (Jiang et al., 2023). Both are chosen as representative pretrained models at their respective scales, with non-trivial but far-from-saturated performance on math reasoning tasks, leaving substantial room for continual pretraining to demonstrate improvements. In all main experiments, the reference model and the continual pretraining model are initialized from the same base checkpoint (Section 3.1).

- **Metrics.** For mathematical reasoning, the primary metric is **few-shot chain-of-thought (CoT) accuracy** on a suite of 9 benchmarks: GSM8K (Cobbe et al., 2021), MATH (Hendrycks et al., 2021, using the Lightman et al., 2023 subset), SVAMP (Patel et al., 2021), ASDiv (Miao et al., 2020), MAWPS (Koncel-Kedziorski et al., 2016), TabMWP (Lu et al., 2023), MathQA (Amini et al., 2019), MMLU-STEM (Hendrycks et al., 2020), and SAT (Azerbayev et al., 2023). For tool-integrated reasoning (fine-tuning experiments), the metric is **accuracy** on 7 benchmarks: GSM8K, MATH, SVAMP, ASDiv, MAWPS, TabMWP, and GSM-Hard (Gao et al., 2022). For general domain, 15 benchmarks are used including MMLU, BBH (Suzgun et al., 2022), AGIEval (Zhong et al., 2023b), ARC-Challenge and ARC-Easy (Clark et al., 2018), BoolQ (Clark et al., 2019), PIQA (Bisk et al., 2020), HellaSwag (Zellers et al., 2019), WinoGrande (Sakaguchi et al., 2021), OpenBookQA (Mihaylov et al., 2018), HumanEval (Zheng et al., 2023, reporting Pass@1 and Pass@10), MBPP (Austin et al., 2021, reporting Pass@1 and Pass@10), and TydiQA (Clark et al., 2020, reporting F1). Evaluation uses lm-eval-harness (Gao et al., 2023) for general tasks and a custom math evaluation suite for math tasks, with vllm (v0.3.2) (Kwon et al., 2023) for inference acceleration (Section 3.1, Appendix E).

- **Baselines.** Several categories of baselines are compared against. For **continual pretraining baselines**, the paper trains **Tinyllama-CT** and **Mistral-CT** — models continually pretrained on the same 15B OpenWebMath tokens using standard causal language modeling (CLM) with identical hyperparameters to RHO-1 except for the token selection mechanism (Section 3.1). For **pretrained model baselines**, comparisons are made against Tinyllama (base), Phi-1.5 (Li et al., 2023b), Qwen1.5 (Bai et al., 2023), Gemma (Team et al., 2024), DeepSeekLLM 1.3B (DeepSeek-AI, 2024), DeepSeekMath 1.3B and 7B (Shao et al., 2024), LLaMA-2 7B, Mistral 7B, Minerva 8B/62B/540B (Lewkowycz et al., 2022), LLemma 7B/34B (Azerbayev et al., 2023), Intern-Math 7B/20B (Ying et al., 2024), and CodeLlama (Roziere et al., 2023) — all evaluated with few-shot CoT prompting (Table 1). For **fine-tuning baselines**, comparisons include GPT4-0314 with and without PAL (program-aided language models), MAmmoTH 70B (Yue et al., 2024), ToRA 7B and 70B (Gou et al., 2024), and DeepSeekMath 7B — all fine-tuned on the ToRA-69k corpus in a tool-integrated reasoning format (Table 2).

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper measures pretraining cost primarily in **total tokens trained** — the number of tokens for which loss is actually computed (after selection, for SLM) — reported alongside the number of unique tokens in the corpus (to distinguish data repetition from selection). For example, RHO-1-Math 1B is trained on 14B unique tokens from OWM but only 9B tokens receive loss at 60% selection ratio (Table 1). This makes the comparison against CLM baselines fair: both train on the same underlying corpus, but SLM computes gradients on fewer tokens. The paper also reports training time: 1B model training on 15B tokens takes approximately 3.5 hours on 32 H100 80GB GPUs, and 50B tokens takes about 12 hours; 7B model training on 15B tokens takes approximately 18 hours under similar hardware (Section 3.1). The reference model training overhead (3 epochs on 0.5B tokens for math, 1.9B tokens for general) is a small fraction of the main pretraining cost, and the reference model forward pass over the pretraining corpus to pre-compute token scores is a one-time cost equivalent to roughly one-third of a full training pass (forward-only vs. forward-backward).

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not employ cross-validation for model selection or hyperparameter tuning in the main experiments. The selection ratio hyperparameter (60% for 1B, 70% for 7B) is tuned on a 5B-token subset of OpenWebMath evaluated on GSM8K and MATH (Figure 9, Section 3.5), and then applied to the full 15B-token training runs. For the SAT benchmark, which has only 32 four-choice problems, the paper averages results over the last three checkpoints "if available" to reduce variance (Table 1, footnote). No confidence intervals, standard deviations, or statistical significance tests are reported for any results. For the few-shot evaluation, the paper does not specify the number of few-shot examples used or whether multiple random seeds are averaged, though this is standard practice in the field (typically 4-shot or 8-shot for GSM8K, 4-shot for MATH).

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Math Continual Pretraining: Few-Shot CoT Reasoning (Table 1)

The headline result: **RHO-1-Math 1B achieves an average few-shot accuracy of 38.1%** across the 9 math benchmarks, compared to **21.6% for Tinyllama-CT** — an absolute improvement of **+16.5 percentage points** while using 40% fewer training tokens (9B selected vs. 15B all tokens). For the 7B scale, **RHO-1-Math 7B achieves 66.2%** average accuracy versus **55.8% for Mistral-CT** — a **+10.4 percentage point** improvement with 30% fewer training tokens (10.5B selected vs. 15B all tokens). These numbers are reported in Table 1 and visualized in Figure 1.

Breaking down by individual benchmarks (Table 1):

- On **GSM8K** (grade-school math word problems), RHO-1-1B improves from 6.4% (Tinyllama-CT) to 29.8% — a +23.4 percentage point gain that is the single largest improvement across all benchmarks. RHO-1-7B improves from 42.9% to 66.9% (+24.0 points). This benchmark is representative of multi-step arithmetic reasoning.

- On **MATH** (competition-level mathematics, using the Lightman et al. subset), RHO-1-1B improves from 2.4% to 14.0% (+11.6 points); RHO-1-7B improves from 22.2% to 31.0% (+8.8 points). The absolute numbers remain low — MATH is a genuinely hard benchmark — but the relative improvement is substantial (nearly 6× for the 1B model).

- On **SVAMP** (arithmetic word problems with structure variations), RHO-1-1B: 21.7% → 49.2% (+27.5 points); RHO-1-7B: 68.6% → 77.8% (+9.2 points).

- On **ASDiv** (diverse arithmetic word problems), RHO-1-1B: 36.7% → 61.4% (+24.7 points); RHO-1-7B: 71.0% → 79.0% (+8.0 points).

- On **MAWPS** (math word problems), RHO-1-1B: 47.7% → 79.8% (+32.1 points, the largest per-benchmark absolute gain for 1B); RHO-1-7B: 86.1% → 93.9% (+7.8 points).

- On **TabMWP** (tabular math word problems), RHO-1-1B: 17.9% → 25.8% (+7.9 points); RHO-1-7B: 45.1% → 49.9% (+4.8 points) — the gains are more modest on this benchmark, which requires reasoning over tabular data structures.

- On **MathQA** (multiple-choice math problems), RHO-1-1B: 13.9% → 30.4% (+16.5 points); RHO-1-7B: 47.7% → 58.7% (+11.0 points).

- On **MMLU-STEM** (college-level STEM multiple-choice questions), RHO-1-1B: 23.0% → 24.7% (+1.7 points); RHO-1-7B: 52.6% → 54.6% (+2.0 points) — these are the smallest improvements, likely because MMLU-STEM covers diverse topics (chemistry, physics, biology, computer science) that are not uniformly represented in OpenWebMath, and the benchmark format (multiple choice) differs from the generative reasoning tasks where SLM provides the largest gains.

- On **SAT** (32 college admission math problems), RHO-1-1B: 25.0% → 28.1% (+3.1 points); RHO-1-7B: 65.6% → 84.4% (+18.8 points) — a substantially larger gain for the 7B model than the 1B model, possibly reflecting the 7B model's greater capacity to absorb mathematical knowledge.

**Multi-epoch training.** Training RHO-1-1B for multiple epochs on OpenWebMath (30B tokens trained, with token selection) further improves average accuracy to **40.9%** (Table 1, bottom of 1B section). The 30B result remains below the RHO-1-7B at 15B (66.2%), as expected from model scale, but demonstrates that SLM's benefits continue beyond a single pass over the data — the model can productively re-use the same corpus when token selection focuses on learnable tokens in each epoch.

**Comparison to models trained on much larger corpora.** The most striking comparison in Table 1 is between RHO-1-7B (trained on 15B OWM tokens with SLM, ~10.5B effective) and DeepSeekMath-7B (trained on 500B math-related tokens, 120B unique, with standard CLM). RHO-1-7B achieves an average of 66.2% versus DeepSeekMath-7B's 68.4% — a difference of only 2.2 percentage points despite a roughly 50× reduction in training tokens. RHO-1-7B outperforms DeepSeekMath on GSM8K (66.9% vs. 64.1%), is competitive on MATH (31.0% vs. 34.2%), SVAMP (77.8% vs. 74.0%), and MAWPS (93.9% vs. 92.4%), and trails on TabMWP (49.9% vs. 63.4%) and MMLU-STEM (54.6% vs. 56.4%). The paper's claim that RHO-1 matches DeepSeekMath with "only 3% of the pretraining tokens" (Abstract) is supported by the 10.5B vs. 500B comparison, though it understates the contribution of the curated reference model training data (0.5B tokens), which is nonetheless negligible relative to 500B.

**Comparison to 1-2B base models without continual pretraining.** RHO-1-1B at 15B tokens substantially outperforms all listed 1-2B base models (Table 1, top section): Tinyllama (13.4% average), Phi-1.5 (31.0%), Qwen1.5 1.8B (40.0%), Gemma 2B (38.4%), DeepSeekLLM 1.3B (roughly 20% average, partial results), and DeepSeekMath 1.3B (roughly 32% average, partial results). At 30B tokens, RHO-1-1B (40.9%) matches or exceeds Qwen1.5 1.8B (40.0%) and Gemma 2B (38.4%), despite having fewer parameters. This demonstrates that SLM-enabled continual pretraining can elevate a modest base model to compete with or surpass larger models in specific domains.

#### Tool-Integrated Reasoning Results After Fine-Tuning (Table 2)

After fine-tuning on the ToRA-69k corpus (16K GPT-4-generated tool-integrated reasoning trajectories + 53K answer-augmented LLaMA samples), both RHO-1 models achieve state-of-the-art results for their size class.

**RHO-1-1B** achieves **40.6% on MATH** and **59.4% on GSM8K**, with an average across 7 benchmarks of **56.9%**. Compared to Tinyllama-CT fine-tuned identically (50.7% average), this is a **+6.2 percentage point improvement** from the pretraining method alone — the SFT data and procedure are identical. On individual benchmarks: GSM8K +8.0 points (51.4% → 59.4%), MATH +2.2 points (38.4% → 40.6%), SVAMP +7.3 points, ASDiv +7.5 points, MAWPS +6.9 points, TabMWP +6.2 points, GSM-Hard +5.3 points.

The MATH result is notable because RHO-1-1B **exceeds the 40% threshold** — the paper claims it is "the first 1B LM to exceed 40% accuracy" on MATH, and notes this "neared the early GPT-4's CoT performance of 42.5%" (Section 3.2, referring to the GPT4-0314 result without PAL in Table 2). While 40.6% vs. 42.5% is indeed close, the comparison is somewhat asymmetric: RHO-1-1B is fine-tuned on ToRA-69k (which includes tool-integrated reasoning examples), while GPT-4 is evaluated zero-shot with CoT prompting. The paper is transparent about this — Table 2 clearly separates "Used for SFT?" with a checkmark — but the rhetorical framing in Section 3.2 elides this distinction.

**RHO-1-7B** achieves **51.8% on MATH** and **81.3% on GSM8K**, with an average of **75.3%** across 7 benchmarks. Compared to Mistral-CT fine-tuned identically (72.6% average), this is a **+2.7 percentage point improvement**. On individual benchmarks: GSM8K +3.8 points (77.5% → 81.3%), MATH +3.4 points (48.4% → 51.8%), SVAMP +3.9 points, ASDiv +1.7 points, MAWPS +1.1 points, TabMWP +2.6 points, GSM-Hard +2.7 points.

The MATH result of 51.8% places RHO-1-7B as **competitive with GPT-4-0314 with PAL (51.8%)** and ahead of ToRA-7B (40.1%), MAmmoTH-70B (41.8%), and DeepSeekMath-7B (52.0% — slightly higher than RHO-1). The paper does not outperform DeepSeekMath-7B on fine-tuned MATH, but is competitive (51.8% vs. 52.0%) at a tiny fraction of the pretraining compute.

**Generalization to unseen tasks.** On GSM-Hard (a harder version of GSM8K with modified numbers and problem structures, not used in ToRA-69k fine-tuning), RHO-1-1B improves from 42.8% to 48.1% (+5.3 points) and RHO-1-7B improves from 60.4% to 63.1% (+2.7 points). On TabMWP (also not in ToRA-69k), RHO-1-1B improves from 20.5% to 26.7% (+6.2 points). These improvements on out-of-distribution evaluation benchmarks suggest that the pretraining method's benefit is not solely attributable to better memorization of the SFT data's domain — the model acquires more general mathematical reasoning capabilities that transfer to related but distinct tasks.

#### General Pretraining Results (Figure 5)

For continual pretraining of Tinyllama-1.1B on 80B general tokens (SlimPajama + StarCoderData + OpenWebMath in 6:3:1 ratio), RHO-1 achieves an **average 6.8% improvement** across 15 diverse benchmarks compared to standard CLM continual training (Tinyllama-CT).

The results, detailed in Figure 5, show particularly strong gains on reasoning and code tasks:

- **Code benchmarks:** HumanEval Pass@1 improves by +6.9 percentage points, HumanEval Pass@10 by +10.6 points, MBPP Pass@1 by +6.5 points, MBPP Pass@10 by +7.8 points. The code improvements are notable because StarCoderData (30% of the training mix) is substantially different in distribution from the general reference model's training data (Tulu-v2 and OpenHermes-2.5, which are primarily instruction-following and conversational).

- **Math benchmarks:** GSM8K improves by +28.2 percentage points, MATH by +5.0 points. These gains are from a general pretraining run where math is only 10% of the data mix — SLM's ability to identify and concentrate on math-relevant tokens within a general corpus is demonstrated.

- **Knowledge and reasoning benchmarks:** MMLU improves by +11.3 points, BBH by +3.9 points, ARC-Challenge by +5.0 points, ARC-Easy by +8.6 points, BoolQ by +11.3 points.

- **Smaller improvements** are seen on benchmarks testing surface-level linguistic knowledge or commonsense: PIQA +0.9 points, HellaSwag +1.4 points, WinoGrande +0.2 points, OpenBookQA +3.4 points. These tasks are already near-saturated for Tinyllama (HellaSwag ~60%, WinoGrande ~60%), leaving less room for improvement.

The general pretraining results confirm that SLM is not specific to mathematical domains — the method provides benefits across diverse task categories when the reference model is trained on a broad high-quality corpus.

#### Self-Reference Results (Table 3)

Without access to external high-quality data — using only OpenWebMath to train both the reference model and the main model — SLM with self-reference scoring still improves performance:

- **OpenWebMath self-reference:** Using the reference model's own loss (`$L_{RM}$`) as the scoring function at 70% selection ratio (10.5B tokens trained out of 15B), Tinyllama-SLM achieves an average of 23.9% across 7 benchmarks, compared to Tinyllama-CT's 21.5% — a **+2.4 percentage point improvement** while using 30% fewer tokens. Using information entropy (`$H_{RM}$`) yields similar improvement (23.4% at 60% ratio). Intersecting both scoring functions (`$L_{RM} \cap H_{RM}$`) at 60% selection ratio (9B tokens, a 40% reduction) yields the best result: **24.8% average — a +3.3 point improvement**.

- **Proof-Pile-2 self-reference, OWM-trained reference:** When the reference model trained on OWM (14B tokens) is used to score tokens in Proof-Pile-2 (55B tokens) and SLM trains on 36B selected tokens (a 30% reduction), the average accuracy is 26.5% versus the CLM baseline of 24.7% — a **+1.8 point improvement**. This demonstrates cross-corpus generalization: the reference model's "cleanliness" filter transfers from OWM to PPile, suggesting the scoring captures general token quality properties rather than corpus-specific artifacts.

The self-reference gains are substantially smaller than the curated-reference gains (+3.3% vs. +16.5% for 1B math, Table 1), confirming that the primary value of the curated reference model is distribution matching (shifting the model toward high-quality, downstream-relevant patterns) rather than mere noise filtering. The noise filtering alone — which is what self-reference provides — yields modest but consistent improvements.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Selection ratio (Figure 9):** Training a 1B model with SLM on 5B OWM tokens while varying the token selection ratio from 40% to 100% shows that **60% is optimal**, achieving peak accuracy on both GSM8K and MATH. Performance degrades at both lower ratios (too many useful tokens discarded) and higher ratios (noisy/already-learned tokens included). At 100% (equivalent to standard CLM but with the scoring step overhead), performance matches the CLM baseline, confirming that the selection mechanism — not some other aspect of the training setup — drives the improvement.

**Selected vs. unselected token loss and downstream performance (Figure 7):** Evaluating at five checkpoints (2B, 5B, 8B, 11B, and 14B tokens trained), the loss on *selected* tokens shows a negative correlation with downstream accuracy — lower selected-token loss corresponds to higher accuracy, consistent with a power-law relationship (`$\text{Acc}(L) = \log(a \cdot L + c)$` with `$a > 0$` as described in Appendix F). Conversely, the loss on *unselected* tokens shows a *positive* correlation with downstream accuracy — *higher* unselected-token loss corresponds to *higher* accuracy (`$a < 0$` in the same functional form). This is evidence that the unselected tokens are not simply neutral but are actively counterproductive to downstream performance: the better the model gets at predicting them (lower loss), the *worse* it performs on math benchmarks. This validates the selection mechanism as genuinely identifying tokens whose learnability trades off against downstream usefulness, rather than merely ranking tokens by difficulty.

**Training dynamics (Figure 6):** Over 4B tokens of training, the loss on *selected* tokens decreases faster under SLM than under CLM (Figure 6a), while the loss on *unselected* tokens increases substantially under SLM compared to the more modest increase under CLM (Figure 6c). Most importantly, the loss on a downstream validation set (MetaMath, Yu et al., 2024) decreases more under SLM than under CLM (Figure 6b), despite the SLM model having higher unselected-token loss. This directly demonstrates that **unselected-token loss increase is not harmful to downstream performance** — the model successfully specializes toward the desired distribution.

**Dynamic token selection (Figure 8, Appendix G.2):** Tokens selected by later checkpoints (e.g., at 14B tokens trained) tend to have higher perplexity at those later checkpoints but lower perplexity at earlier checkpoints compared to tokens selected by earlier checkpoints (e.g., at 2B tokens trained). Figure 8 shows this pattern clearly: the PPL of "Selected Tokens at 14B" starts lower than "Selected Tokens at 2B" at the 2B checkpoint but ends higher at the 14B checkpoint. This is interpreted as the model "first optimizing tokens with a larger learnable space, thereby increasing learning efficiency." The dynamic shift in which tokens are selected is visualized in Figure 14 (Appendix G.2), where token color-coded preferences change substantially across four checkpoints (0%, 33%, 66%, 100% of training), demonstrating that the excess loss criterion naturally adapts token selection as the model's capabilities evolve.

**Qualitative analysis of selected tokens (Appendix G.1, Figure 13):** Visualizing specific training examples with selected tokens highlighted in blue reveals that SLM predominantly selects tokens related to mathematical reasoning content — LaTeX formulas, step-by-step derivations, problem statements — while filtering out forum metadata, timestamps, bibliographic references, and navigational elements. This qualitative evidence supports the paper's claim that SLM identifies tokens aligned with the desired distribution rather than merely selecting tokens based on superficial properties like frequency or position.

**Self-reference scoring function comparison (Table 3, Appendix H Table 4):** Beyond the main results, Appendix H Table 4 provides a comprehensive sweep of selection ratios (40-90%) for both `$L_{RM}$` and `$H_{RM}$` scoring functions, plus union and intersection combinations. The key findings: (1) optimal ratio is consistently around 60-70% for both functions; (2) intersection of the two functions consistently outperforms either alone at equivalent total selection ratios; (3) performance degrades gracefully as ratio moves away from the optimum rather than collapsing — the method is not brittle to this hyperparameter; (4) union performs worse than intersection, confirming that the two functions capture complementary aspects (predictability and certainty) and that their agreement identifies the highest-quality tokens.

**Weak-to-strong generalization (Appendix I, Table 5):** A Tinyllama-1.1B reference model scoring tokens for Llama-2-7B pretraining on 15B OWM tokens yields 44.4% average accuracy (with SLM, 10.5B tokens trained) versus 43.5% for Llama-2-CT (standard CLM, 15B tokens). The +0.9 point improvement is positive but substantially smaller than the +10.4 point gain when using a same-scale Mistral-7B reference model (Table 1). This ablation demonstrates that while same-scale reference models are preferable, cross-scale transfer is feasible and still provides benefits.

**Multi-epoch training (Table 1):** Training RHO-1-1B on OWM for multiple epochs (30B tokens trained) further improves average accuracy from 38.1% (at 9B effective tokens, one-pass) to 40.9%. The paper does not report a CLM baseline at 30B tokens for comparison, but the monotonic improvement with additional epochs suggests that SLM does not saturate after a single pass — the dynamic token selection mechanism continues to identify learnable tokens in repeated exposures to the same data.

**Negative result: ReST$^{EM}$ attempt for revision models (implied absence in this paper):** No revision models or ReST$^{EM}$ experiments appear in the RHO-1 paper. The method is evaluated purely as a pretraining objective modification, not in combination with iterative self-improvement or RL-based training. The paper does note in Appendix C that using "a reference model as a reward model to guide pretraining with reinforcement learning" is a potential extension, but this was not attempted. The absence of negative results from RL-based optimization is not itself a negative result — it is an unexplored direction.

### Critical Assessment

#### Central Claim 1: SLM improves data efficiency by concentrating training on useful tokens.

**Assessment: Strongly supported for math continual pretraining at 1B and 7B scales.** The evidence is clear and consistent: RHO-1 substantially outperforms identical models trained with standard CLM on the same corpus (Table 1: +16.5% for 1B, +10.4% for 7B) while using fewer effective training tokens (9B vs. 15B, and 10.5B vs. 15B, respectively). The training dynamics analysis (Figure 6) provides mechanistic evidence that the loss reduction on selected tokens transfers to downstream improvements. The selected vs. unselected token loss correlation (Figure 7) provides evidence that the token selection is causally meaningful — not merely a correlation artifact — since reducing loss on selected tokens improves downstream performance while reducing loss on unselected tokens harms it.

However, three caveats are important. First, **the baseline is weak in an important way**: Tinyllama-CT and Mistral-CT are the authors' own baselines trained under identical conditions, but RHO-1 also has access to a reference model trained on high-quality curated data (0.5B tokens), which could be considered an unfair advantage in information terms — the baseline has no access to curated data whatsoever. A fairer baseline would give the CLM model access to the same curated data, e.g., by mixing it into the pretraining corpus or using it for data augmentation. The paper's self-reference experiments (Section 3.4) partially address this by showing SLM provides benefits even without curated data (+3.3% improvement), but the main 10-16% gains depend on the curated reference model as an additional information source, making the comparison partially a test of "curated data access + selective training" vs. "no curated data access + uniform training." 

Second, **the evaluation benchmarks may correlate with the reference model's training data unintentionally**. If the curated 0.5B-token corpus (synthetic GPT data + MAmmoTH + Ni et al.) shares problem formats, reasoning patterns, or even exact problems with the GSM8K, MATH, and other evaluation benchmarks, then the reference model's scoring function might be selecting tokens that specifically benefit those benchmarks rather than mathematical reasoning generally. The paper does not discuss data contamination between the reference model corpus and evaluation benchmarks. Given that the reference corpus includes synthetic data from GPT-4 (MetaMath, Key-Point-Driven) and MAmmoTH, which are designed to cover mathematical reasoning benchmarks, this is a genuine concern.

Third, **the "50× fewer tokens" comparison with DeepSeekMath-7B is confounded**. DeepSeekMath uses a different base model (DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5, 7B), different pretraining data (120B unique tokens from Common Crawl math-related pages, not just OpenWebMath's 14B), different training configurations, and a different tokenizer. The RHO-1-7B pretraining starts from Mistral-7B, which already outperforms DeepSeekMath's base on several benchmarks. A controlled comparison — same base model, same pretraining data, same hyperparameters, varying only the training objective (CLM vs. SLM) and the total token budget — would be more informative about SLM's specific contribution. The current comparison demonstrates that SLM + Mistral-7B + 15B tokens can roughly match DeepSeekMath-7B + 500B tokens, which is practically impressive but does not isolate SLM's marginal contribution from the base model difference.

#### Central Claim 2: SLM works across domains (general pretraining).

**Assessment: Supported preliminarily but with a single model scale and a limited data regime.** The general pretraining experiment (Section 3.3, Figure 5) demonstrates a +6.8% average improvement across 15 benchmarks when continually pretraining Tinyllama-1.1B on 80B general tokens. This is a legitimate test of domain transfer — the general reference model is trained on instruction-following data (Tulu-v2, OpenHermes-2.5), and the pretraining corpus is a code-heavy general web corpus. The improvements on code benchmarks (HumanEval, MBPP) are particularly noteworthy because code is structurally different from the reference model's conversational instruction data.

However, the experiment has four significant limitations. First, **the 80B token scale is small relative to modern pretraining budgets**: Tinyllama was already trained on ~3 trillion tokens, so 80B tokens is a 2.7% increment. The +6.8% improvement on an already-trained model suggests SLM helps with continual pretraining, but whether similar gains would materialize during pretraining from scratch — where the model's state evolves much more dramatically — is untested. Second, **only one model scale (1B) is tested in the general domain**; the 7B experiments are exclusively math. Third, **the 6:3:1 SlimPajama:StarCoder:OpenWebMath mix is arbitrary** and the paper doesn't investigate whether the optimal selection ratio or reference model composition depends on the data mixture. Fourth, **the general reference model's data (Tulu-v2, OpenHermes-2.5) totals 1.9B tokens** — substantially larger than the math reference model's 0.5B. The dependence of SLM performance on reference model data quantity and composition is not systematically studied, making the general result difficult to interpret in isolation.

#### Central Claim 3: The token selection mechanism is dynamic and self-adjusting.

**Assessment: Supported by suggestive evidence but not rigorously established as causally important.** Figures 8, 14, and the related discussion in Section 3.5 demonstrate that token selection preferences shift over the course of training — later checkpoints select different (typically higher-perplexity) tokens than earlier checkpoints. This is consistent with the dynamic selection hypothesis: as the model learns easy tokens, the excess loss criterion shifts focus to harder but still learnable tokens.

However, the paper does not compare dynamic selection (re-computing token scores from the training model's evolving log-probabilities) against static selection (pre-computing scores once and fixing them for the entire training run). Without this ablation, it is unclear whether the observed preference shift is *necessary* for SLM's gains or merely *incidental* — perhaps static selection with a single well-chosen selection ratio would perform equally well, and the dynamic shift is a descriptive property rather than a functional mechanism. The paper's claim that "the model first optimizes tokens with a larger learnable space, thereby increasing learning efficiency" (Section 3.5) is an interpretation that is plausible but not causally tested.

#### Central Claim 4: Not all tokens are equally important for training.

**Assessment: The core motivating claim is empirically validated for the studied domains and scales, but its generality is assumed rather than tested.** The training dynamics analysis (Section 2.1, Figure 3) convincingly demonstrates that under standard CLM on OpenWebMath with Tinyllama-1B, only 26% of tokens show meaningful loss reduction. The downstream results show that concentrating training on a subset of tokens improves performance. The selected vs. unselected token loss correlation (Figure 7) suggests that some tokens are actively counterproductive.

However, this claim is demonstrated for one corpus (OpenWebMath), one domain (mathematics), two model families (Tinyllama, Mistral), and one training regime (continual pretraining from existing checkpoints). The paper does not analyze training dynamics on general web text, code, or other domains to demonstrate that the 26% figure or the four-category decomposition generalizes. The proportions (26% H→L, 51% L→L, etc.) could vary substantially by domain — a curated textbook corpus might show 60% H→L, while a raw Common Crawl dump might show 10%. Even within mathematics, the paper does not demonstrate that the same token categories exist during pretraining from scratch (random initialization), where the dynamics might differ substantially from continual pretraining of an already-competent model. The claim "not all tokens are equal" is well-supported, but the claim "only 26% of tokens are useful" may be specific to this particular data-model-training regime combination.

#### Missing Ablations That Would Have Strengthened the Paper

- **Static vs. dynamic token selection**: Pre-compute `$L_\Delta$` once (using the initialization checkpoint's `$L_\theta$`) and fix token selection throughout training, vs. the standard approach of computing `$L_\theta$` fresh each step. This would isolate the contribution of dynamic adaptation from the contribution of token selection per se.

- **Reference model size and quality**: Systematically vary the reference model's parameter count (e.g., 300M, 600M, 1B, 3B), training data quantity (e.g., 50M, 100M, 250M, 500M tokens), and training duration (e.g., 1, 3, 5 epochs) to characterize how reference model quality affects downstream SLM performance. The current paper uses fixed reference model configurations with no sensitivity analysis.

- **Pretraining from scratch**: Apply SLM to a model trained from random initialization, not continual pretraining of an existing checkpoint. This would test whether the benefits hold when the model's initial token losses are uniformly high (no L→L tokens exist yet) and the dynamics are qualitatively different.

- **Alternative reference model sources**: Compare a reference model trained on curated data against (a) a reference model trained on a random subset of the pretraining corpus (holdout approach, as in RHO-LOSS), (b) a larger off-the-shelf model (e.g., LLaMA-2-7B as reference for a 1B training model), and (c) an ensemble of reference models trained on different curated subsets. This would clarify what properties of the reference model (domain expertise, scale, diversity) matter most.

- **Controlled comparison with data mixing**: Give the CLM baseline access to the same curated data by mixing it into the pretraining corpus at varying ratios, and compare against SLM matched for total FLOPs (including reference model training). This would test whether SLM's token-level filtering provides benefits beyond simply having access to curated data.

- **Confidence intervals on main results**: The paper reports single-point accuracy estimates without variance. For the SAT benchmark (32 examples), variance is substantial — averaging over three checkpoints is a heuristic, not a statistical procedure. For the 500-question MATH subset, a 1-2 percentage point difference may not be statistically significant. Reporting bootstrap confidence intervals or standard deviations would clarify the reliability of the comparisons, especially the close ones (e.g., RHO-1-7B 51.8% vs. DeepSeekMath-7B 52.0% on fine-tuned MATH).

#### Summary: What the Experiments Genuinely Demonstrate

The experiments establish that **token-level selection during continual pretraining of 1B and 7B models on OpenWebMath, guided by a reference model trained on curated mathematical data, substantially improves few-shot reasoning accuracy across 9 benchmarks while using 30-40% fewer effective training tokens.** This is a strong, consistent result that holds across two model scales, multiple benchmarks, and both few-shot and fine-tuned evaluation settings.

The experiments further demonstrate that **SLM provides more modest but consistent gains in general-domain continual pretraining at the 1B scale (+6.8% across 15 benchmarks)**, and that **even without curated data, SLM's noise-filtering effect improves performance (+3.3% for self-reference math)**. The weak-to-strong transfer experiment suggests that **the reference model signal transfers across model scales**, though with degradation.

What the experiments do **not** demonstrate is: (1) that SLM works for pretraining from scratch rather than continual pretraining; (2) that the gains scale to larger models (>>7B) or larger corpora (>>80B tokens); (3) that the dynamic token selection property is causally important rather than incidental; (4) that the curated reference model approach is superior to simply mixing the curated data into the pretraining corpus; (5) that the token categorization (26% H→L, 51% L→L, etc.) generalizes across domains, model scales, and training regimes; and (6) that the 50× efficiency claim relative to DeepSeekMath isolates SLM's contribution from base model differences.

These limitations are largely acknowledged by the authors in Appendix C (Limitations and Future Work), where they flag generalizability ("only verified the effectiveness of our method on smaller models (<=7B parameters) and smaller datasets (<100B tokens)"), scalability ("future works should study whether this selective language modeling technique can scale to very large models and data"), and the necessity of the reference model ("Is training a reference model necessary? ... We leave this for future works"). The paper is appropriately scoped as an initial demonstration of a new pretraining paradigm, and the experiments support its claims within that scope. The risk is that the impressive headline numbers (50× token reduction, state-of-the-art MATH results) might lead practitioners to overestimate the method's current maturity for production pretraining pipelines — the gap between this 15B-token continual pretraining study and a full-scale 1T+ token pretraining from scratch is substantial and untested.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### Continual Pretraining Only — No Demonstration of Pretraining from Scratch

**The assumption or constraint.** All experiments in this paper are **continual pretraining** — starting from an already-trained base model (Tinyllama-1.1B or Mistral-7B) and training on additional domain-specific tokens. The paper does not apply SLM to a model trained from random initialization. The training dynamics analysis in Section 2.1 that motivates the entire method is also performed on continual pretraining: Tinyllama-1B is *already trained* on ~3T general tokens before the 15B-token OpenWebMath run is analyzed.

The authors acknowledge this scope limitation in Appendix C: "Due to budget constraints, we have only verified the effectiveness of our method on smaller models (<=7B parameters) and smaller datasets (<100B tokens)." But they frame this as a scalability concern, not a fundamental uncertainty about whether SLM works when the initial token losses are uniformly high (no L→L category exists yet because the model starts with random weights).

**The consequence.** During pretraining from scratch, the training dynamics are qualitatively different from continual pretraining. At initialization, *all* tokens have high loss — there is no pre-existing L→L category of "already learned" tokens, no H→H category of "persistently hard" tokens (since the model hasn't had time to demonstrate persistence), and no L→H category (since losses can only decrease from the randomly initialized state). The excess loss scoring function `$L_\Delta(x_i) = L_\theta(x_i) - L_{RM}(x_i)$` depends on `$L_\theta$` being a meaningful signal of what the training model has learned versus what remains to be learned. During early pretraining from scratch, `$L_\theta$` is high for *all* tokens, so excess loss may primarily select tokens based on `$L_{RM}$` alone (since `$L_\theta$` is roughly uniform and high, the difference is dominated by `$-L_{RM}$`, i.e., selecting tokens with the *lowest* reference loss). This degenerates into pure reference model loss filtering — which the paper shows (Section 3.4, Table 3) provides only modest gains (+2-3%) compared to the +10-16% gains of full SLM.

In other words, **it is unclear whether SLM's primary mechanism — selecting for the intersection of "learnable under the desired distribution" AND "not yet learned" — is active during pretraining from scratch**, or whether it only becomes effective after the model has developed some competence (generating differentiation in `$L_\theta$`). If the latter, SLM might not accelerate early-stage pretraining at all, and its benefits would be concentrated in mid-to-late training where token losses have diverged.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. The paper provides zero experiments or analysis on pretraining from random initialization. Section 2.1's training dynamics analysis (Figure 3) starts from a Tinyllama-1B checkpoint that has already seen ~3T tokens of general pretraining — the "initial" state already has 51% of tokens in the L→L category. This analysis tells us about the dynamics of *domain adaptation* (adding math to an already-competent general model), not about the dynamics of *learning from scratch*. The paper does not discuss this distinction.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The authors do not flag pretraining-from-scratch as a separate open question in Appendix C. The closest acknowledgment is the general scalability statement about "smaller models and smaller datasets," but this frames the limitation as quantitative (scale) rather than qualitative (different learning regime). A practitioner considering using SLM for pretraining a model from random initialization — which is the standard pretraining paradigm — would find no evidence in this paper to guide their decision.

---

### Difficulty Estimation Overhead: Reference Model Training and Token Scoring Are Not Included in Efficiency Calculations

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's headline efficiency claims — "4× fewer training tokens" (generalized from Section 3.2's results), "5-10× faster" (Figure 1), "matching DeepSeekMath with only 3% of the pretraining tokens" (Abstract) — compare SLM's training cost against standard CLM baselines at the *main pretraining stage only*. These comparisons do not include:

1. **Reference model training:** The reference model is trained for 3 epochs on 0.5B curated tokens (math) or 1.9B tokens (general). While small relative to the main pretraining budget (15B or 80B tokens), this is a non-zero cost that requires curating a high-quality dataset — itself a non-trivial engineering effort that the paper does not quantify.

2. **Token scoring over the entire pretraining corpus:** Before SLM training can begin, the frozen reference model must compute `$L_{RM}(x_i)$` for every token in the pretraining corpus (15B tokens for math, 80B tokens for general). This is a forward pass over the entire corpus, equivalent to approximately one-third of a full training pass (forward-only vs. forward-backward). The paper acknowledges this in Section 2.2 by claiming the process "eliminates the loss for undesired tokens without incurring additional costs during pretraining" — but the scoring cost is a *pre-processing* cost, not a training cost, and is not amortized into any of the efficiency metrics.

3. **Difficulty estimation for the reference model itself:** For the self-reference variant (Section 3.4), the reference model must still be trained (on the pretraining corpus itself), and token scores must still be pre-computed. In the OWM self-reference experiment (Table 3), the reference model is trained on 14B OWM tokens — essentially equivalent to the full CLM pretraining run that SLM is being compared against. The self-reference "savings" of 30-40% fewer training tokens neglect the fact that the CLM baseline didn't need to train a separate reference model at all.

**The consequence.** A practitioner evaluating whether to adopt SLM for a pretraining run would need to account for the total computational budget including reference model training and corpus scoring. For the math experiments, the reference model training (0.5B tokens) plus corpus scoring (one forward pass over 15B tokens, equivalent to ~5B tokens of training compute) adds approximately 5.5B token-equivalents of compute overhead, compared to the 15B-token main pretraining run. This reduces the effective efficiency gain from ~40% fewer training tokens (9B vs. 15B) to roughly ~3% fewer when amortizing the overhead (9B + 5.5B = 14.5B vs. 15B). The 40% savings would only materialize if the same reference model and pre-computed scores are reused across multiple training runs — which the paper does not demonstrate or discuss.

For the self-reference variant, the accounting is worse: the reference model training on 14B OWM tokens (equivalent to nearly the full CLM baseline cost) plus scoring another 14B tokens means the total budget substantially *exceeds* the baseline before SLM training even begins. The paper's claimed "40% reduction in tokens" (Table 3: 9B selected vs. 15B total) ignores the ~14B tokens used to train the reference model and the ~14B tokens processed for scoring, making the actual total token budget approximately 37B versus the baseline's 15B — a 2.5× *increase* in total compute.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper does not report these overhead costs anywhere. Section 3.1 states training times for the main pretraining runs (3.5 hours for 1B on 15B tokens, 18 hours for 7B) but does not provide corresponding times for reference model training or token scoring. The paper does not present a FLOPs-matched comparison that includes reference model overhead. The abstract, introduction, and conclusions present efficiency numbers (4×, 5-10×, 3%) without qualification.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not flag this as a limitation in Appendix C. The discussion of "Is training a reference model necessary?" (Appendix C) frames the question as one of access to high-quality data, not computational cost. The self-reference experiments are presented as a mitigation for data access, but their computational overhead is not discussed. The paper notes that reference model scores could be obtained from "more powerful proprietary model APIs" (Appendix C) to eliminate training cost, but API inference costs for 15B+ tokens are themselves non-trivial and not quantified.

### Limited Scale: Only Tested up to 7B Parameters and 80B Tokens, All in the Continual Pretraining Regime

**The assumption or constraint.** All experiments use models at the 1B or 7B scale, and all training runs are modest by modern pretraining standards: 15B tokens for math, 80B tokens for general. The paper explicitly acknowledges this in Appendix C:

> "Due to budget constraints, we have only verified the effectiveness of our method on smaller models (<=7B parameters) and smaller datasets (<100B tokens). Smaller models benefit significantly from removing the loss of irrelevant tokens and focusing on important ones. However, it's possible that very large models trained on extensive corpora may naturally develop this inductive bias to compress useful data (i.e., compressing everything), although it may sounds inefficient for now."

This is a candid admission that scalability is unproven, but the paper's framing ("it may sounds inefficient for now") implies optimism without evidence.

**The consequence.** There are two distinct concerns about scaling SLM to much larger models and datasets, each with different implications:

1. **Model scale (>>7B parameters).** Larger models have greater capacity and may exhibit different training dynamics. The paper's motivating analysis (Section 2.1) found that only 26% of tokens show meaningful loss reduction for a 1B model on OpenWebMath. For a 70B or 175B model, the proportion might be substantially different — larger models may be able to productively learn from a wider range of tokens, reducing the relative benefit of token selection. Alternatively, larger models might be *more* sensitive to noisy tokens (since they have the capacity to overfit to noise), increasing the benefit. The paper provides no evidence either way. The 7B results (Figure 10 in Appendix D.1) show similar token category proportions (28% H→L, 51% L→L) as the 1B results, but this is insufficient — 1B and 7B are within the same "small model" regime relative to frontier scales of 70B-175B+.

2. **Training data scale (>>80B tokens).** At trillion-token scales, the pretraining corpus is typically extremely diverse, covering many domains, languages, formats, and quality levels. SLM's token selection depends on the reference model being able to assign meaningful scores to tokens across this diversity. A reference model trained on 1.9B tokens of instruction-following data (the general reference model) may not provide useful signal for tokens from domains it has not seen — e.g., low-resource languages, niche technical domains, or novel code libraries. At large data scales, the token scoring itself becomes a significant engineering challenge: storing per-token scores for trillions of tokens would require hundreds of terabytes of storage, and re-scoring tokens when the reference model or selection strategy changes would be prohibitively expensive.

The paper's own self-reference results (Table 3) hint at the scale-dependence problem: when the reference model is trained on the same data as the pretraining corpus, the gains drop from +10-16% to +2-3%. At trillion-token scales, training a reference model on a sufficiently representative sample of the diverse corpus is itself a major undertaking, and the reference model's quality — and therefore SLM's effectiveness — becomes tightly coupled to how well that sample captures the corpus's distribution.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides scaling evidence across exactly two model sizes (1B and 7B) and two data scales (15B and 80B tokens). Both model sizes show benefits, and the 7B gains are proportionally smaller than the 1B gains (+10.4% vs. +16.5% average accuracy improvement), but two data points cannot establish a scaling trend. The paper does not present any scaling law analysis projecting SLM benefits to larger models or datasets. The weak-to-strong experiment (Appendix I: 1B reference → 7B training) provides one data point suggesting cross-scale transfer, but with only a +0.9 point improvement, it does not inspire confidence that the approach scales favorably.

**Mitigation status.** Acknowledged in Appendix C but not addressed experimentally. The paper's recommended future work — "future works should study whether this selective language modeling technique can scale to very large models and data" — correctly identifies this as an open question. The honest assessment is that **a practitioner cannot extrapolate from this paper whether SLM would provide meaningful benefits for a GPT-3-scale (175B) or GPT-4-scale model trained on trillions of tokens.** The method has been validated only in the regime where it's least needed — small models where the overhead of a separate reference model is proportionally most burdensome relative to the total compute budget, and where the absolute performance gains have limited practical impact.

### Single-Domain Depth for the Main Claim; No Evidence Across Diverse Reasoning Domains

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's strongest and most emphasized results — the +16.5% and +10.4% average accuracy improvements, the 50× token reduction claim, the state-of-the-art MATH fine-tuning numbers — come from **mathematical reasoning only**, using a single pretraining corpus (OpenWebMath, 14B tokens from math-related Common Crawl pages). The general pretraining experiment (Section 3.3, 80B tokens, +6.8% average improvement) covers more domains but (a) is only done at 1B scale, (b) uses only 80B tokens on top of a model already trained on ~3T tokens, and (c) shows much more modest gains — the +6.8% average is dominated by a few large outlier improvements (GSM8K +28.2%, MMLU +11.3%, BoolQ +11.3%) while many benchmarks show minimal gains (PIQA +0.9%, HellaSwag +1.4%, WinoGrande +0.2%).

The authors do not explicitly state this as a limitation, but the paper's structure — dedicating substantially more space and analysis to math results, with general pretraining as a one-paragraph confirmation experiment — reflects the domain concentration.

**The consequence.** Mathematics has specific properties that make token-level selection unusually effective, and these properties may not generalize to other reasoning domains or to general pretraining:

1. **Formal, structured notation:** Mathematical text contains LaTeX, equations, and symbolic expressions that are tokenized differently from natural language and are highly domain-specific. Tokens within equations (e.g., `\frac`, `\sum`, variable names) are clear signals of mathematical content that the reference model can easily identify. In contrast, code has its own syntax but is also highly structured; general web text, dialogue, or narrative have much more diffuse signals of quality and relevance.

2. **Clean separation between content and noise:** Web-scraped mathematical corpora like OpenWebMath have a characteristic structure: blocks of mathematical reasoning embedded in forum posts, blog comments, or Wikipedia articles that also contain navigational elements, timestamps, author signatures, and other non-mathematical text. The token-level distinction between "math" and "not-math" is relatively sharp. In general web text, the boundary between useful content and noise is much fuzzier — is a marketing tagline in a product description "noise" or "content"? Is a conversational filler word "already learned" or "contextually meaningful"?

3. **Well-defined downstream benchmarks:** Mathematical reasoning has clear, widely-used benchmarks (GSM8K, MATH, SVAMP, etc.) that directly test the target capability. The paper's results show that SLM improves the model's math reasoning, but it's unclear whether this is because SLM selects tokens that build general mathematical competence or because SLM selects tokens that happen to correlate with specific benchmark formats. The evaluation benchmarks and the curated reference model data are drawn from overlapping sources (MetaMath, MAmmoTH, synthetic GPT data designed to cover math benchmarks) — Section 5's critical assessment flagged this as a potential data contamination concern.

For a practitioner considering SLM for code generation, the evidence is limited to the 1B general pretraining experiment (HumanEval +6.9%, MBPP +6.5%). For a practitioner considering SLM for dialogue, summarization, translation, or other common LLM use cases, **the paper provides no evidence whatsoever** — none of these capabilities are evaluated.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The general pretraining experiment (Figure 5) attempts to address domain generalization but is insufficient to establish it. The 6.8% average improvement across 15 benchmarks conflates large gains on math (GSM8K +28.2%, MATH +5.0%) and code (HumanEval +6.9%, MBPP +6.5%) with near-zero gains on commonsense benchmarks. This pattern is consistent with the hypothesis that SLM primarily benefits domains that are (a) well-represented in the reference model's training data and (b) have strong token-level signals distinguishing relevant from irrelevant content — i.e., math and code — and provides little benefit for domains where token-level quality is harder to define (commonsense reasoning, reading comprehension, language understanding). The paper does not break out per-task results for the general pretraining experiment in a way that would allow a domain-by-domain assessment; Figure 5 is a bar chart without a detailed table.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed as a limitation. The paper frames the math results as the main story and the general results as confirmation of broad applicability, but the asymmetry in evidence strength — thorough analysis and strong results for math, thin analysis and moderate results for general — is not acknowledged. The paper's title ("Not All Tokens Are What You Need") and framing imply universality, but the empirical support is concentrated in a single domain. A more accurate framing would be "Not All Tokens Are What You Need for Mathematical Reasoning Pretraining, with Preliminary Evidence for Code and General Text."

### No Quantification of Selection Accuracy: How Often Does SLM Select the "Right" Tokens?

**The assumption or constraint.** The entire SLM approach rests on the implicit claim that the reference model's excess loss ranking correctly identifies which tokens are beneficial for downstream performance. The paper provides qualitative examples (Figures 11-14, Appendix G) showing that selected tokens tend to look like mathematical content and unselected tokens tend to look like noise, and the correlation analysis (Figure 7) shows that reducing loss on selected tokens improves downstream accuracy while reducing loss on unselected tokens *harms* it. However, the paper never quantifies **selection accuracy** — what fraction of selected tokens are genuinely useful (H→L type) versus misclassified, and what fraction of unselected tokens are genuinely counterproductive (H→H or L→H type) versus wrongly excluded.

This is not a minor measurement gap — it goes to the heart of whether SLM is operating via the mechanism the authors claim (identifying learnable, distribution-aligned tokens) or through a simpler, less general mechanism (e.g., filtering out tokens with unusual tokenization patterns, or de-emphasizing punctuation and formatting).

**The consequence.** Without knowing selection accuracy, a practitioner cannot diagnose when SLM is likely to fail or how to improve it. Consider two scenarios:

- **Scenario A — SLM is highly accurate (>80% precision and recall):** Most selected tokens are genuinely useful H→L tokens, and most unselected tokens are genuinely noisy or already-learned. In this case, the gains from SLM are near the theoretical maximum for the approach, and improvements would need to come from better reference models (to identify useful tokens in new domains) or better selection ratios (to capture more of the useful tail).

- **Scenario B — SLM is moderately accurate (~50% precision, reasonable recall):** Half of selected tokens are genuinely useful, half are misclassified. The gains come from the net effect of including more good tokens than bad ones, but substantial room for improvement exists. The reference model's scoring function could be improved through better training data, architectural changes, or ensembling. The token selection criterion (excess loss) might be suboptimal compared to alternatives.

- **Scenario C — SLM's benefits come from a simpler mechanism:** Perhaps SLM primarily works by filtering out high-entropy tokens (punctuation, formatting, rare words) that dominate the loss in standard CLM, and the "distribution matching" with a curated reference model is secondary. The self-reference results (+3.3% with entropy-based selection, Table 3) are consistent with this interpretation — pure noise filtering provides meaningful gains without any curated distribution signal.

The paper's evidence is consistent with all three scenarios. Figure 7's correlation analysis shows that reducing selected-token loss helps and reducing unselected-token loss hurts, but this is a population-level correlation — it doesn't tell us about per-token classification accuracy. The qualitative examples (Figures 11, 13) are cherry-picked to show favorable cases and cannot establish selection precision.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides three types of indirect evidence, none of which quantify selection accuracy:

1. **The training dynamics categorization (Figure 3):** This tells us what fraction of tokens *were* useful over a completed CLM training run (26% H→L), but does not tell us whether SLM's excess loss criterion successfully identifies those tokens *during training*. H→L categorization requires knowing the entire loss trajectory; excess loss at a single checkpoint is a noisy proxy.

2. **The selected vs. unselected loss correlation (Figure 7):** This is the strongest evidence that the distinction SLM makes is meaningful — but it's a population-level trend, not a per-token accuracy measurement.

3. **Qualitative visualizations (Figures 13-14):** These demonstrate that SLM's selections are *plausible* but cannot establish error rates.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not define a ground-truth notion of "useful token" against which selection accuracy could be measured, nor does it attempt to estimate false positive/negative rates of the selection mechanism. This is a significant gap because it makes SLM a black box — practitioners cannot anticipate when it will work well versus poorly, and researchers cannot systematically improve the selection criterion without a quantitative target to optimize. The paper's failure to define and measure selection accuracy means the central mechanism of the method remains unevaluated beyond population-level correlation evidence and qualitative inspection.

### The "5-10× Faster" Claim Conflates Token Efficiency with Wall-Clock Time

**The assumption or constraint.** Figure 1 and the abstract claim that "SLM improves average few-shot accuracy on GSM8k and MATH by over 16%, achieving the baseline performance 5-10× faster." The "5-10× faster" framing conflates two distinct notions of efficiency: **token efficiency** (how many training tokens are needed to reach a given performance level) and **wall-clock time** (how many GPU-hours are needed). The paper's evidence supports the former but not the latter.

Specifically, Figure 1 shows accuracy-vs-tokens-trained curves: the x-axis is "Tokens (B)" — total tokens processed during training. RHO-1's curve reaches baseline performance with fewer x-axis units (e.g., ~1.5B tokens for RHO-1-1B vs. ~15B for the baseline, roughly a 10× difference in Figure 1 left; ~3B vs. ~15B for 7B, roughly a 5× difference in Figure 1 right). But tokens processed is not the same as wall-clock time, because SLM incurs additional per-token costs during training that the baseline does not:

1. **Pre-processing cost:** Token scoring over the entire corpus (one forward pass of the reference model) must be completed before training begins. For the 15B-token math corpus with a 1B reference model, this is a one-time cost equivalent to approximately 5B tokens of training compute (forward pass ≈ 1/3 of forward-backward). This pre-processing adds latency before any training progress can be made.

2. **Per-step overhead:** During training, SLM must compute excess loss for every token in the batch (requiring `$L_\theta$` from the training model, which is already computed, and `$L_{RM}$` from the reference model, which is pre-computed and cached), rank tokens by score, and construct the loss mask. The paper claims "no additional costs during pretraining" (Section 2.2), and the ranking overhead is indeed negligible compared to the Transformer forward-backward pass, so this is a minor concern.

3. **Lower GPU utilization for smaller effective batches:** While the total tokens in a batch remain the same (1M tokens/batch, Section 3.1), the *effective* number of tokens contributing to loss is reduced to 60-70% of the batch. This means 30-40% of the forward-backward computation is producing zero-gradient tokens — computation that is still performed (the model processes all tokens) but doesn't contribute to learning. This does not directly affect wall-clock time (the forward and backward passes still run over the full batch), but it means SLM has lower *gradient efficiency* per GPU-hour — the same wall-clock time produces fewer effective gradient updates than if the batch were packed entirely with selected tokens.

**The consequence.** The "5-10× faster" framing — which appears prominently in the abstract, Figure 1, and introduction — is misleading for a practitioner trying to estimate total project time. A more accurate statement would be: "RHO-1 reaches baseline performance in 5-10× fewer *training tokens that receive loss*, but requires additional pre-processing (reference model training and corpus scoring) and the same per-token GPU cost during training." The actual wall-clock speedup, including pre-processing, is substantially less than 5-10×.

For the math experiments, the reference model training on 0.5B tokens for 3 epochs (~1.5B token-equivalents of training) plus corpus scoring (~5B token-equivalents of forward pass) adds approximately 6.5B token-equivalents of pre-processing to the 9B-token SLM training run, for a total of ~15.5B token-equivalents versus the baseline's 15B. The effective token-efficiency gain is nearly eliminated when pre-processing is included — roughly 15.5B vs. 15B, a 1.03× improvement, not 5-10×. The token-efficiency gain would only compound if the same reference model and pre-computed scores were reused across many training runs (e.g., hyperparameter sweeps, architecture variants, or multiple model scales), which the paper does not demonstrate.

For the 7B math experiments, the picture is similar: reference model training (0.5B tokens for 3 epochs, but at 7B scale this is more expensive per token) plus corpus scoring plus 10.5B tokens of SLM training versus 15B tokens of CLM baseline. The pre-processing cost is proportionally smaller (since the main training run is more expensive at 7B), but it is still non-zero and unaccounted.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. The paper does not report wall-clock time comparisons that include pre-processing. Section 3.1 reports training time for the main runs (3.5 hours for 1B on 15B tokens, 18 hours for 7B) but does not provide reference model training time or corpus scoring time. Figure 1's x-axis is "Tokens (B)," not "GPU-hours" or "Wall-clock time." The "5-10× faster" label on Figure 1 is directly contradicted by the x-axis label — it's "5-10× more token-efficient," not faster.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper uses "faster" as a synonym for "more token-efficient" throughout, including in the abstract and Figure 1 annotations. This is a common but misleading conflation in ML papers — practitioners reading "5-10× faster" would reasonably expect to get results in 1/5 to 1/10 the wall-clock time, which the paper's own numbers do not support unless the pre-processing cost is entirely amortized across many runs. The paper does not discuss amortization, does not report pre-processing times, and does not present a speedup metric that accounts for total compute.
