Here are the takeaways from this Asian Dad Energy interview (a 25-year tech veteran who was laid off from big tech, went viral sharing his story, and is now navigating semi-retirement and a YouTube channel):

**The layoff: a premeditated switcheroo**

He was working at a big tech company that had already gone through multiple rounds of layoffs. Rumors of the next round were circulating, and he knew what was coming—he had a "heads up about the nature of this layoff" and that he himself was on the list to be cut. At the same time, he learned through "the grapevines that a couple of more junior engineers" were also slated for the same wave. So he arranged "a little switcheroo" where he "put myself to the top of the list and got cut," buying the junior engineers "a little bit of time, a mere matter of months. Hopefully they use the time wisely."

The reasons behind the layoff, in his estimation, were not simply cost-cutting. He sees the entire sector locked in a sort of "meta" or arms race: **companies are decreasing headcount while performing AI efficiency**. The logic, as he lays it out, is: keep product lines selling so revenue is unaffected, cut cost by reducing headcount, then use "good marketing, talking about efficiencies gained through AI" to drive up the company’s stock valuation. It’s a show of innovation, whether or not the AI itself actually replaces the lost roles. He suspects we're in a period of "over investment" in AI that is "not yielding clear returns," and so whether companies are laying off to cut costs performatively, because they’re running short on money, or because business lines are collapsing, there’s a "meta trend towards shedding tech employees." He’s careful to note this is just a higher-probability read on "the vibe of this entire sector"—a happier future where AI genuinely multiplies the value of tech labor is "entirely possible," and since his investments and stocks are tied to these companies, he'd "be happy as a clam" if that happened.

The emotional experience of hearing he was on the list was, in his words, "anxiety, panic, excitement, fear, hope, everything." After years of doing the same thing, a sudden major change felt "very, very unstable." The severance package was transparent and generous—structured garden leave, months of severance, a stipend for education or equipment, and a full year of PTO paid out. "If one does the math right, it’s actually a very generous package." Technically it was negotiable, but he wasn't in the right mental state to care about "an extra 20% on the severance package"—he was far more caught up in a broader reflection "on life and what I’ve been doing this whole time."

**25 years in consulting: dropped into the client ecosystem**

He got into programming in high school, majored in computer science, and did side-project consulting in college. His first real job out of college was with a digital consulting agency, where he spent "many years" progressing through the entire ladder: junior software engineer, senior developer, tech lead, application architect, enterprise architect, senior enterprise architect, and eventually technical director. The work meant being inserted into client companies—often big Fortune 500 firms—alongside other vendors and consulting agencies, all "rapidly building out the systems for them." It was a "very dynamic, maybe chaotic system" where "you sort of have to find your place, survive in this ecosystem where you’re one actor in a range of actors, build the thing to the best of your abilities and then you ship out to the next project."

Ryan asks the obvious question: how does software not explode when a consulting team builds something and hands it off? There are two models. The **retainer model**, where the agency works with a client for years, builds the platform from the ground up, and maintains deep institutional knowledge—"those are the kind of better types of projects as a consultant." And the **fly-by-night model**, where an agency needs to keep its pipeline of work full and sells gigs "purely for monetary means." In the latter case, you'd often walk into a client and see "this dizzying patchwork of platforms and sites and systems that are a lot of times redundant and a lot of times incompatible," a dumpster fire left by multiple vendors doing their thing. If you were hired for a digital transformation, "you sort of have to put out the fire and kind of stitch everything together just to have a working platform." Both models, he says, are "kind of fun and anxiety inducing in their own ways."

The experience of being parachuted into a new client environment depended entirely on the client's culture—rigidly hierarchical or open and cooperative, sometimes organized as an internal startup. A real skill of consulting beyond writing code is reading the **topology of the client political environment**: how client teams interact, how they interact with other consulting agencies, how to "navigate and open up a space for your consulting agency" and keep the book of orders flowing. The early rungs of this were "highly, highly enjoyable"—the constant learning of new cultures, new ecosystems, and then adapting and improving them. But as he became more senior, the work shifted from hands-on architecture to "hey, drop me off at the C suite and let me sell this vision of digital transformation to the CMO or the CSO or the CEO of this Fortune 500 corporation." He did that for a couple of years and burned out.

What burned him out was **the gap between the ideal he was selling and the actuality**. The actuality—"the sausage making"—was dirty and chaotic: systems that don’t talk, code bases unmaintained for five years, offshore vendors who can’t communicate with onshore teams. But the vision sold to the C-suite is a "smooth diagram where you capture customer insights, you have this bubble that does a rationale and a decision is made and immediately sales and orders come in and revenue goes up." Being the person who had to "make those two things come together and happen"—eventually as a group technical director—"it just gets too much."

**The integrity that stops consultants from shipping garbage**

Ryan zeroes in on a structural tension: the clients buying digital transformations are often non-technical, so there's an information asymmetry. What stops a consultant from declaring a "steaming pile of turds" to be great? The guest points to **engineering integrity**—"you have to have respect for the truth, for quality and for integrity of what you’re building." He recalls a university course that explicitly taught this, and he "tried my very best to do it right." He's blunt: "If it is a steaming pile of turds, it’s your obligation to tell your client that, hey, man, like the $20 million that you’ve invested is a steaming pile of turds." Are there consultants who don’t? "I’m sure." But reputation is the long-term incentive structure—"you can only do that so many times before the reputation of a firm, of a consulting agency is ruined." He also describes how, in his own career, he went through a phase where the quality of his product was a "personal reflection" on himself: "if you build a steaming pile of turds, you’re going to feel ashamed." At the leadership level, he found "a significant number of people that kind of cares a lot about the quality of their software"—a human element without which "nothing stops us from delivering just crap."

**Why people dislike consultants (and why it’s not really about us)**

The pop-culture aversion to consultants, he thinks, is largely displaced from management consulting onto technical consulting. Management consultants deal with the "people" portion: they sometimes "take a look at all of the teams working on your business and decide that X percent of these teams are redundant," leading to layoffs. "If that happens enough times, there then is the impression that, hey, a consulting team is coming. Oh my goodness, our jobs are at risk." In his personal experience, when you actually drop into a client site and work alongside people, "90% of the times they’re great guys."

**The jump to big tech: compensation goes "bonkers"**

About seven years before the interview, a colleague who had become an executive at a big tech company introduced him to a hands-on chief architect role. After years of being shipped to boardrooms to sell digital transformation, going deep again was "a very refreshing change": he could "take a component that was really, really difficult and code it out and build it," join debug sessions, tweak configurations, and participate in production launches.

But the compensation was the real shock. Consulting pays "comfortably," but **big tech compensation "more than doubles, closer to tripling."** The difference comes from the layered structure: a base salary already "significantly higher" than consulting, plus sign-on and recurring bonuses, plus **RSUs** (restricted stock units that vest over three or four years), plus an **ESPP** (employee stock purchase program) that lets you buy company shares at roughly a 15% discount below market price with post-tax salary. For him, holding onto those shares through the COVID years—when digital platforms saw explosive, forced adoption—meant they "doubled and then tripled in value." He frames his logic simply: "everybody is forced to use digital tools. They don’t have a choice. Oh, and we make a lot of these digital tools. Maybe I should hold on to these stocks. I think they might go up and they did." Asked whether he would have bought his company’s stock with $10,000 in cash, he says no—he’s "risk averse" and would have gone with index funds. But the ESPP’s baked-in discount was essentially free money, and his read on the COVID moment tipped him toward holding. "If someone’s just giving you money for nothing, you should accept that money."

**The most cherished growth period and the mentor’s lesson**

The stretch from senior software engineer up to senior enterprise architect was where he "learned the most about the craft." What drove it was "a series of extremely good, knowledgeable, capable mentors." After becoming a director, the work shifted to more managerial tasks—helping other architects and tech leads, "selling work to C suite executives all the time"—and "just became less fun and there was less growth."

The number one lesson from a mentor came from "this one guy" who taught him something "kind of profound": **"To be flexible, you have to be able to adapt yourself to changing conditions, no matter what the condition is."** No matter the tech stack, the CI/CD pipeline, the client-side teams—"if you can stay flexible, you’ll survive."

**Turning 40: the gymnast hitting the mat**

That flexibility became harder to maintain. After turning 40, "I still tried to keep up in the tech. I still tried to learn all the frameworks and I just noticed that it took me longer and longer and longer to learn." Where in his 20s he could pick up a framework in a few days and be effective, in his 40s "it’s taking me weeks to learn it" and the output is "halting, clumsy syntax." He draws an analogy: "imagine like an Olympic gymnast, like those guys that can do somersaults in the air. And they’re able to do that in their 20s, right? But when they hit their 30s, if they even try one thing, they’ll break a bone. At least to me, mentally, that was what it was like." It’s a little sad, he admits, but "we all get older."

**Why YouTube: laziness, a therapist, and going viral**

The decision to start making videos was born from a crossroads, not a strategy. He was "feeling really confused and lost" post-layoff and had two options: write his thoughts in a journal or shoot a vlog. "Quite frankly, I was just lazy. I’m like, you know what? Why don’t I just do a vlog instead?" A therapist had suggested logging his thoughts as a way to process the anxiety, and "it was simply easier to turn on a camera than it was to spend all this time writing the words down." He had "no clue it would go viral like the way it did." When it did, it "completely blew me away"—he’d never done a vlog before and didn’t understand how the YouTube algorithm worked. The comments were overwhelmingly positive: "it seemed like maybe other people were thinking and feeling similar thoughts and feelings as to me." The pseudonym "Asian Dad Energy" didn’t prevent recognition; quite a few coworkers from his ex-employer and past companies reached out, all positive so far. He’s "prepared for those cringy moments" if a C-suite leader ever takes issue.

As for what’s next, he’s not rushing back. He applied to a few big tech companies post-layoff and "not much came of it"—he doesn’t think it’s a hot jobs market. But more than that, he’s not sure he wants the structure of big tech with "all these schedules and goals and priorities that probably doesn’t mean that much to me as it used to." He’s exploring **semi-early retirement/involuntary early retirement** and finding his physical health and mental state "just going up and up and up this whole time." He’s keeping his mind active with the YouTube channel and a couple of small coding projects, open to "some incredibly motivating project that, you know, I would jump up and work at it for free," but if it doesn’t appear, "we’ll deal with the situation as it comes."

**Speaking advice: detach yourself from your baby**

Asked for speaking advice for engineers, he returns to a story about "one engineer that I worked with, absolutely brilliant, so incredibly brilliant that you have never met such a smart guy." The engineer had done an in-memory optimization of a memory cache in Drupal, but was so personally tied to the subject that he couldn’t explain it to anyone less deep. The core failure: **"he thought so seriously of what he was discussing"** that he couldn’t take a step back. The advice: "just relax and don’t associate yourself with the subject that you’re discussing." An engineer’s project is "your baby, it’s your everything," but to communicate effectively you need to "explain it like the audience is five years old." That’s how you actually get your point across and get what you want to do.
