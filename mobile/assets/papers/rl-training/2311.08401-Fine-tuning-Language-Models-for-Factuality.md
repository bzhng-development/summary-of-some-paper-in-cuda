# Fine-tuning Language Models for Factuality

**ArXiv:** [2311.08401](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.08401)

## 🎯 Pitch

This paper proposes a method for fine-tuning language models to generate more factual long-form text without any human labeling, targeting the problem of “hallucinations”—convincing but incorrect claims.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper proposes a method for fine-tuning language models to generate more factual long-form text without any human labeling, targeting the problem of “hallucinations”—convincing but incorrect claims. The approach constructs factuality preference pairs by evaluating model responses on biographies and medical question-answering using the Llama-2 and Llama-1 7B models, then optimizes those preferences via Direct Preference Optimization—leveraging two scoring mechanisms: a reference-based truthfulness estimator (FactScore, which checks atomic claims against Wikipedia) and a novel **reference-free confidence-based truthfulness estimation** (resampling answers to claim-derived questions and using the model’s own confidence as a proxy for correctness). Fine-tuning with FactScore-generated preferences reduces the factual error rate by 58% on biographies and 40% on medical questions relative to Llama-2-chat, while the reference-free variant achieves a 40% error reduction on biographies, establishing that automated factuality preference learning substantially reduces hallucinations even without external knowledge bases—though the largest gains require reference-based scoring on the biography task.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: LLMs Produce Convincing Falsehoods in Open-Ended Generation

The fundamental problem this paper tackles is deceptively simple to state but deeply challenging to solve: **large language models confidently generate factually incorrect claims in long-form, open-ended text, and we lack practical, scalable methods for training them out of this behavior.** This phenomenon—often called "hallucination" in the literature—manifests as statements that sound plausible but are inconsistent with established knowledge. The paper gives a concrete example in the form of GPT-3.5 producing false citations more often than not when asked about study authorship (citing Agrawal et al., 2023), and the introductory example of the question "Where was Yo-Yo Ma born?" illustrates the broader issue: a model that hedges probability mass over many possible answers (including factually wrong ones) may be rewarded by training objectives that reward matching any response observed in the training data.

This problem is not merely an academic curiosity. The paper positions language models as increasingly being used as "a replacement for traditional search engines" (Section 1), which means factual errors directly lead to the spread of misinformation. When a user asks a language model a question and receives a confident-sounding but incorrect answer, the model has not just failed to help—it has actively misled. The practical stakes include:

- **Misinformation propagation**: Factual errors from widely-deployed models can amplify misconceptions at scale, since LLMs are increasingly used by non-expert users who may lack the domain knowledge to verify claims independently.
- **Trust erosion**: If users cannot rely on model outputs to be factually accurate, the utility of LLMs as information tools is fundamentally limited, regardless of their fluency or creativity.
- **Manual fact-checking costs**: The paper reports that professional fact-checkers took ~9 minutes per model-generated biography and that annotating 505 biographies cost roughly $2,000 (citing Min et al., 2023). This makes human-labeled factuality data prohibitively expensive at scale, creating a chicken-and-egg problem: we need factuality data to train models to be factual, but we can't afford to generate that data through human effort.

The theoretical dimension matters as well. The paper argues (Section 1) that the standard pre-training objective—maximum likelihood—can actually *reward* factually imprecise models. The "Yo-Yo Ma" example makes this concrete: a model that deterministically produces "idk, probably Paris?" when asked about Yo-Yo Ma's birthplace is nearly always correct but receives extremely high loss if any other response exists in the training data. By contrast, a model that hedges probability mass over many possible answers (including incorrect ones like Antarctica) will achieve lower loss because it assigns non-trivial probability to whatever response appears in the data. The pre-training objective thus creates a perverse incentive: **it rewards "smearing" probability mass over many possible completions rather than committing to the single factually correct response.** This means that even a model with the knowledge to answer correctly may not be incentivized to do so by the training signal it receives.

### The Gap: No Practical Fine-Tuning Objective for Factuality in Long-Form Text

While the hallucination problem is well-recognized, the paper identifies a specific gap in the existing toolkit: **there is no established method for directly fine-tuning a language model to produce more factual open-ended text without expensive human annotation.** This gap exists at the intersection of three constraints:

**Constraint 1: Human factuality labels are too expensive to scale.** As noted above, professional fact-checking of a single biography takes ~9 minutes; at scale, this is unsustainable. The paper explicitly frames this as a bottleneck: prior approaches that require human labels (such as standard RLHF, which trains a reward model on human preference rankings) inherit the cost and latency of human annotation for factuality specifically—and factuality judgments are particularly time-consuming compared to other preference dimensions like helpfulness or harmlessness.

**Constraint 2: Retrieval-augmented methods shift complexity to inference time.** Several works have shown that grounding model outputs in retrieved documents can improve factuality (the paper cites works on using external knowledge bases for verification and error correction; Peng et al., 2023; Gao et al., 2023; Dhuliawala et al., 2023). However, these approaches face two practical challenges. First, they require reliable retrieval at inference time, which adds system complexity and latency—the paper notes that "the most common open-source consumer language models thus use purely parametric models" (Section 5), meaning retrieval-based approaches haven't been widely adopted in deployed systems. Second, retrieval systems face the challenge of resolving conflicts between parametric knowledge (what the model already "knows" from pre-training) and retrieved knowledge (Longpre et al., 2022; Chen et al., 2022); if the model's internal knowledge contradicts the retrieved document, naive retrieval augmentation may not correctly resolve the discrepancy. Critically, Mallen et al. (2023) showed that the benefits of retrieval over parametric knowledge diminish as model size increases, suggesting retrieval is not a universal solution.

**Constraint 3: Existing inference-time interventions are heuristic and limited.** Several prior works propose decoding-time modifications to improve factuality: ITI (Inference-Time Intervention; Li et al., 2023) shifts activations along a "truthfulness direction" identified through linear probing, and DoLa (Decoding by Contrasting Layers; Chuang et al., 2023) modifies the decoding process to emphasize factual knowledge encoded in specific model layers. These approaches are appealing because they require no training, but they are fundamentally limited: they apply a fixed, heuristic transformation to model internals rather than directly optimizing the end goal of producing factual text. The paper's results (Figure 3, Table 2) demonstrate this limitation empirically—ITI and DoLa can improve some dimensions of factuality (e.g., increasing correct facts or decreasing incorrect ones) but fail to produce the strict improvement (simultaneously increasing correct facts while decreasing incorrect ones) that direct optimization achieves.

### Prior Approaches and Their Specific Shortcomings

The paper situates itself against several categories of prior work, each with documented limitations:

**Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF).** RLHF (Christiano et al., 2017; Ouyang et al., 2022) has become the de facto standard for aligning language models with human preferences, but it has been primarily applied to dimensions like helpfulness, harmlessness, and instruction-following—not specifically to factuality. The paper's experiments include RLHF-trained models (Llama-2-Chat) as baselines and show that they still produce substantial numbers of factual errors (Table 3: 6.41 incorrect facts per biography on average, 5.50 incorrect facts per medical QA response). This indicates that **RLHF as typically practiced does not adequately address factuality**, likely because human preference annotators prioritize other response qualities (fluency, engagement, perceived usefulness) over rigorous fact-checking. The paper also notes that RLHF optimization via PPO is "quite complex to implement and tune" (Section 2, citing Zheng et al., 2023), creating a practical barrier to applying RLHF to new reward functions like factuality.

**Factuality detection without training.** A rich line of work has developed methods for *detecting* when models are likely hallucinating. These include monitoring model confidence (Kadavath et al., 2022), sensitivity to prompt perturbations (Xu et al., 2023), diversity of outputs under resampling (Kuhn et al., 2023), inconsistency with external knowledge (FactScore, Min et al., 2023; FacTool, Chern et al., 2023), and properties of internal activations (Azaria & Mitchell, 2023). These methods are valuable for identifying errors but stop short of *preventing* them through training. They are diagnostic tools, not therapeutic ones. The paper's key insight is that **these same detection signals can be repurposed as training signals**—if we can reliably determine which of two responses is more factual (using automated methods), we can construct preference pairs and optimize the model to produce the better response.

**Error correction via post-hoc revision.** Some approaches focus on *correcting* factual errors after they have been generated, typically by retrieving relevant evidence and using another LLM to verify and revise claims (Peng et al., 2023; Gao et al., 2023; Dhuliawala et al., 2023). While these methods can improve the factual accuracy of specific outputs, they address symptoms rather than causes. More importantly, they add inference-time complexity: every generation requires a multi-step verification and revision pipeline involving retrieval, claim extraction, natural language inference, and regeneration. This is computationally expensive, adds latency, and relies on the quality of both the retriever and the verifier model. The paper notes that its approach uses retrieval **only during training** (for the FactScore variant), avoiding this inference-time overhead entirely.

**Most prior factuality work focuses on short-form generation.** A crucial gap the paper identifies is that "most past work has focused on short-form NLG tasks like short-form question-answering" (Section 5). Long-form generation—biographies, medical explanations, news articles—presents qualitatively different challenges for factuality. In short-form QA, there is typically a single target answer, and correctness is binary. In long-form text, a single passage may contain dozens of atomic claims, each of which must be individually verified. The paper's focus on long-form factuality fills a gap that had been largely unaddressed by prior training-based approaches.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper's contribution is best understood not as a single method but as **demonstrating a general recipe for converting automated factuality estimates into training signals for preference optimization.** The recipe has three modular components:

1. **A truthfulness estimator**: Either reference-based (FactScore, using Wikipedia) or the novel reference-free confidence-based method (where the model's own uncertainty over claim-derived questions serves as a factuality proxy). These are interchangeable in the pipeline; the choice determines whether external knowledge is needed.
2. **A preference pair construction procedure**: For each prompt, sample multiple candidate responses, score each with the chosen truthfulness estimator, and label the higher-scoring response as preferred. This converts unannotated prompts and raw model outputs into a preference dataset without human involvement.
3. **Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)**: The recently proposed algorithm (Rafailov et al., 2023) that learns from preference pairs without fitting an explicit reward model or running online RL, making the training process stable and straightforward.

The paper positions its approach as complementary to, rather than competing with, inference-time interventions like DoLa (Section 4.3, Table 4), showing that combining factuality tuning with DoLa can yield additional gains. It also positions the approach as composable with RLHF (Section 4.2, Table 3), demonstrating that factuality tuning can further improve the factuality of already-RLHF-ed chat models.

The key conceptual move is reframing factuality improvement from a *detection* problem or an *inference-time augmentation* problem to a **preference learning problem**. If we can rank model responses by factuality (even noisily), we can train the model to prefer more-factual responses using the same algorithmic machinery developed for RLHF—but without the human annotators. This connects two previously separate lines of work: automated factuality evaluation (FactScore, confidence-based methods) and preference-based fine-tuning (DPO, RLHF). The paper's experiments are designed to validate that this connection actually works in practice—that automated factuality scores, despite their imperfections, provide a reliable enough training signal to produce meaningful improvements in how often models generate correct versus incorrect claims.

The paper is explicit about its scope: it targets 7B models (leaving scaling to larger models as future work), focuses on two specific long-form generation tasks (biographies and medical QA), and does not claim to solve factuality for all domains or all types of errors. But within this scope, it aims to demonstrate that **learning to be factual from automated feedback is not only possible but substantially more effective than existing approaches that modify decoding or rely on generic human preference labels.**

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation (Approachable Technical Breakdown)

The system being built is an **automated training pipeline** that takes an existing language model that occasionally hallucinates and fine-tunes it to produce fewer factual errors in long-form text—all without requiring a single human to manually label what is true or false. The pipeline works by (1) automatically generating candidate responses to prompts, (2) scoring each response's factuality using either external knowledge (Wikipedia) or the model's own uncertainty, (3) constructing preference pairs where the more-factual response is labeled as "preferred," and (4) applying the Direct Preference Optimization algorithm to update the model to favor more-factual outputs. The problem being solved is a **data bottleneck**: human factuality labels are prohibitively expensive (~9 minutes per biography, $2,000 for 505 annotations), but preference-based fine-tuning requires preference pairs. The "shape" of the solution is to repurpose existing automated factuality evaluation methods as training signal generators rather than merely as evaluation tools.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The system has four major components organized in a sequential pipeline:

1. **Candidate Response Generator** — The base language model (Llama-1-7B or Llama-2-7B, or their chat variants), prompted with a query (e.g., "Write a biography of Mary Wollstonecraft"), produces multiple candidate responses via temperature sampling (temperature 1.0). This generates raw material from which preference pairs will be constructed.

2. **Truthfulness Scorer** — This component takes a generated response and produces a single scalar factuality score (fraction of claims that are factually correct). It operates in one of two interchangeable modes: **reference-based** (FactScore: extracts atomic claims from the response, then checks each claim against a Wikipedia article using a fine-tuned NLI model) or **reference-free** (the novel confidence-based method: extracts atomic claims, converts each to a question, resamples answers 20 times from the base model, and uses the model's confidence—fraction of answers in the largest equivalence bin—as a proxy for truthfulness). This is the critical innovation that eliminates human labeling.

3. **Preference Pair Constructor** — For a single prompt, given `$n$` candidate responses and their truthfulness scores, all `$\binom{n}{2}$` possible pairs are formed, and the response with the higher truthfulness score is labeled as the "preferred" response (`$y_w$`). Pairs with equal scores are discarded (approximately `$k$` out of `$m \cdot \binom{n}{2}$` total pairs, where `$m$` is the number of prompts).

4. **DPO Fine-Tuning Module** — The Direct Preference Optimization algorithm (Rafailov et al., 2023) takes the constructed preference dataset and fine-tunes the model directly, without fitting an explicit reward model or running online reinforcement learning. The model is first fine-tuned via supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on all generated responses (regardless of factuality score), then further fine-tuned with the DPO objective to shift probability mass toward more-factual completions.

Information flows linearly: Prompt → Candidate Generation → Truthfulness Scoring → Preference Pair Construction → DPO Training → Fine-Tuned Model. At inference time, the fine-tuned model is used normally (no retrieval, no claim extraction, no scoring), so the training pipeline's components are invisible to the end user.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) objective and its relationship to reinforcement learning from preferences, because DPO is the learning algorithm that all factuality signals feed into, and understanding its mechanics is prerequisite to understanding what the pipeline produces.

- **Second**, the reference-based truthfulness estimation procedure (FactScore), because it is the more established of the two scoring methods and its claim extraction step is shared by both approaches.

- **Third**, the novel reference-free confidence-based truthfulness estimation method, because it is the paper's key methodological innovation and its design requires understanding the claim extraction step explained previously.

- **Fourth**, the preference pair construction procedure that bridges the truthfulness estimators to the DPO algorithm, because this is where the automated factuality signal is converted into a format the learning algorithm can consume.

- **Fifth**, the supervised fine-tuning (SFT) stage that precedes DPO training, because DPO requires a reference model and the SFT stage provides that initialization.

- **Sixth**, the key hyperparameters and dataset construction details, because these determine the practical behavior of the pipeline and enable reproducibility.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **methods paper** whose core idea is that automated factuality estimation—whether by reference to Wikipedia or by model confidence—can generate preference pairs of sufficient quality to meaningfully improve model factuality through Direct Preference Optimization, without requiring human annotators.

#### The Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) Objective and Its Mechanics

The paper uses DPO as the learning algorithm because it solves a practical problem: standard RLHF requires fitting a reward model and then running online reinforcement learning (typically PPO), which is "quite complex to implement and tune" (Section 2, citing Zheng et al., 2023). DPO collapses this two-stage pipeline into a single classification-style loss computed directly on preference data, eliminating the need for an explicit reward model and online sampling.

**The RL objective that DPO optimizes.** The standard reinforcement learning objective for language model fine-tuning combines reward maximization with a KL-divergence penalty that prevents the policy from deviating too far from its initialization:

$$\max_{\pi_\theta} \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}_p, y \sim \pi_\theta(y|x)} \left[ r(x, y) - \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)} \right]$$

where:
- `$\pi_\theta$` is the policy being optimized (the language model with parameters `$\theta$`),
- `$\mathcal{D}_p$` is a dataset of prompts `$x$`,
- `$y$` is a response sampled from the current policy `$\pi_\theta(y|x)$`,
- `$r(x, y)$` is a scalar reward function that assigns desirability to prompt-response pairs,
- `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$` is a reference policy (typically the result of supervised fine-tuning on demonstration data),
- `$\beta$` is a hyperparameter controlling the trade-off between reward and divergence—higher `$\beta$` keeps the policy closer to `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$`, reducing overoptimization but also limiting how much the policy can adapt to the reward signal.

**What the objective computes:** For each prompt, the model generates a response `$y$` autoregressively. The reward `$r(x, y)$` scores the response quality. The KL term `$\beta \log(\pi_\theta(y|x) / \pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x))$` penalizes deviations from the reference model: if `$\pi_\theta$` assigns much higher probability to `$y$` than `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$` would, the KL term is large and positive, effectively subtracting from the reward. The expectation is over the distribution of prompts and the model's own generated responses. Optimizing this objective encourages the model to produce high-reward outputs while remaining "recognizable" to the reference model, which prevents reward hacking—the policy exploiting idiosyncrasies of the reward function that don't correspond to genuine quality.

**Why this form:** Without the KL penalty (`$\beta = 0$`), unconstrained reward maximization leads to overoptimization (Gao et al., 2022): the policy discovers outputs that score highly under the reward model but are nonsensical, repetitive, or otherwise undesirable to humans. The KL penalty provides a trust region—the policy is incentivized to find high-reward outputs but only within a neighborhood of behavior that `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$` would produce. This is the same objective that RLHF systems (Christiano et al., 2017; Ouyang et al., 2022; Bai et al., 2022) have used successfully. The choice of `$\beta$` is critical: too low and the model overfits the reward; too high and the model ignores the reward entirely.

**The Bradley-Terry preference model.** DPO assumes that the probability of observing one response being preferred over another follows a Bradley-Terry model:

$$p(y_w \succ y_l) = \sigma(r(x, y_w) - r(x, y_l))$$

where:
- `$y_w$` is the preferred response (the "winner"),
- `$y_l$` is the dispreferred response (the "loser"),
- `$y_w \succ y_l$` denotes that `$y_w$` is preferred over `$y_l$`,
- `$\sigma$` is the sigmoid function `$\sigma(z) = 1 / (1 + e^{-z})$`,
- `$r(x, \cdot)$` is an unobserved scalar reward function.

**What this model states:** The probability that `$y_w$` beats `$y_l$` is a sigmoid-transformed function of the difference in their underlying rewards. If `$r(x, y_w)$` significantly exceeds `$r(x, y_l)$`, the sigmoid output approaches 1 (the preference is near-certain). If the rewards are equal, the sigmoid outputs 0.5 (the preference is a coin flip). This is a standard choice in pairwise preference modeling because it's log-linear, making it computationally tractable and amenable to maximum-likelihood estimation. The key assumption is transitivity—that a single scalar "quality" determines all pairwise preferences—which is a reasonable approximation for factuality if truthfulness can be meaningfully reduced to a one-dimensional score.

**The DPO loss function.** Rafailov et al. (2023) proved that the optimal policy `$\pi^*$` for the RL objective in equation (1) can be recovered without explicitly learning `$r(x, y)$`. Instead, the optimal policy satisfies a relationship that allows the reward difference to be expressed in terms of the policy's own log-ratios relative to the reference model. Substituting this into the Bradley-Terry likelihood yields the DPO loss:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\pi_\theta; \pi_{\text{ref}}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} \left[ \log \sigma \left( \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w | x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w | x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l | x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l | x)} \right) \right]$$

where:
- `$\mathcal{D}$` is a fixed dataset of preference triples `$(x, y_w, y_l)$` (prompt, preferred response, dispreferred response),
- `$\pi_\theta(y|x)$` is the probability the current policy assigns to response `$y$` given prompt `$x$` (computed autoregressively as the product of per-token conditional probabilities),
- `$\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)$` is the same computation under the reference model,
- `$\beta$` is the same KL penalty coefficient from the RL objective,
- `$\sigma$` is the sigmoid function.

**What this loss computes operationally:** For each preference triple in the dataset, DPO computes two log-ratio terms: `$\log(\pi_\theta(y_w|x) / \pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x))$` for the preferred response and `$\log(\pi_\theta(y_l|x) / \pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x))$` for the dispreferred response. The difference of these log-ratios (multiplied by `$\beta$`) is passed through the sigmoid and neg-logged. Minimizing this loss pushes the model to increase the log-ratio for `$y_w$` (make the preferred response more likely relative to the reference model) and decrease the log-ratio for `$y_l$` (make the dispreferred response less likely relative to the reference model). The gradient flows through the model's output logits for every token in both responses, so the model learns to shift probability mass toward phrasings and content choices characteristic of factual responses and away from those characteristic of hallucinated responses.

**Why DPO over PPO for this application:** The authors choose DPO because it enables "efficient, stable learning from objectives other than maximum likelihood" (Section 3) without the engineering complexity of online RL. PPO requires (1) training a separate reward model on the preference data, (2) sampling from the policy during training (which is slow and introduces non-stationarity), (3) estimating advantages, and (4) tuning multiple hyperparameters (clipping range, value function coefficient, GAE `$\lambda$`). DPO avoids all of these: the loss is computed directly on a static dataset with no sampling, no separate reward model, and the only hyperparameter is `$\beta$`. This is particularly important for factuality because (a) the preference data is automatically generated and potentially noisy, making stable optimization critical, and (b) the method should be easy to replicate and extend, which DPO's simplicity facilitates.

**A subtle property of DPO:** The reference model `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$` plays a crucial role as an anchor. If the dataset contains noisy preferences—for example, if the truthfulness estimator incorrectly labels a response with more errors as preferred—the KL penalty through `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$` prevents the model from overfitting that noise too aggressively. The model can only move as far from `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$` as the data convincingly supports, providing implicit regularization. This is particularly valuable when using automated factuality scoring, which will inevitably contain errors.

**The SFT stage that precedes DPO.** Before applying DPO, the paper follows standard practice: the model is first fine-tuned via supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on all generated responses, treating each response as a target for next-token prediction (standard cross-entropy loss). This serves two purposes: (1) it adapts the pre-trained model to the specific format and style of the target task (biography generation or medical QA), and (2) it provides the reference model `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$` for the DPO stage. The SFT model is the model that would result from simply training the base model on all the generated data without any factuality preference signal—it learns to produce plausible responses but has no incentive to prefer factual ones over hallucinated ones. The DPO stage then fine-tunes this SFT model to favor the more-factual responses among the same training data. The paper notes that SFT alone produces models that generate many incorrect facts (Table 2: Llama-1 SFT produces 12.16 incorrect facts per biography), confirming that maximum-likelihood training on model-generated data does not inherently improve factuality—the preference signal is necessary.

#### Reference-Based Truthfulness Estimation: The FactScore Pipeline

FactScore (Min et al., 2023) provides the paper's primary factuality metric and training signal. It is a multi-stage pipeline that decomposes a long-form passage into individual factual claims, retrieves a relevant Wikipedia article, and checks each claim for support, producing a fraction representing the proportion of claims deemed factual.

**Stage 1: Atomic claim extraction.** Given a generated passage (e.g., a biography of Mary Wollstonecraft), the system uses GPT-3.5 to decompose it into "atomic facts"—individual, self-contained declarative statements each expressing exactly one piece of factual information. For example, the sentence "Mukesh Ambani was born in 1957 in Mumbai, India, to billionaire industrialist Dhirubhai Ambani and Kokilaben Ambani" might be decomposed into: (1) "Mukesh Ambani was born in 1957," (2) "Mukesh Ambani was born in Mumbai, India," (3) "Mukesh Ambani's father is Dhirubhai Ambani," (4) "Dhirubhai Ambani is a billionaire industrialist," and (5) "Mukesh Ambani's mother is Kokilaben Ambani." The paper uses GPT-3.5 (specifically `gpt-3.5-turbo`) for this extraction step, with prompts designed to produce a list of atomic claims. The extraction is done once per response and cached; the resulting claims list is then fed to the verification stage.

**Why atomic decomposition:** Long-form text often compresses multiple factual claims into single sentences (e.g., appositives, relative clauses). A binary correct/incorrect judgment on an entire sentence would conflate a partially correct sentence with minor errors with a completely fabricated one. By decomposing to atomic claims, FactScore produces a finer-grained, more interpretable signal: the fraction of claims supported represents the proportion of the passage that is factual, which is more informative than a holistic binary label. Additionally, atomic claims are easier for a verifier model to check because each claim reduces to a single subject-predicate-object relation.

**Stage 2: Wikipedia passage retrieval.** For each entity in the prompt domain (e.g., for biography prompts, the subject of the biography; for medical QA, the medical condition), the relevant Wikipedia article is retrieved using a simple entity-to-Wikipedia-title mapping. The paper generates data only for entities that have Wikipedia pages, ensuring that reference text exists. The Wikipedia article serves as the ground-truth knowledge base against which claims are verified. Retrieval is done once per entity (not per prompt or per response), since multiple prompts about the same entity share the same reference article.

**Stage 3: Natural language inference for claim verification.** For each atomic claim, a smaller, more efficient model—specifically a Llama-1-7B model that has been fine-tuned for fact-checking—performs natural language inference (NLI; MacCartney & Manning, 2008) to determine whether the claim is **supported** by the Wikipedia article. NLI is the task of determining the logical relationship between a premise (the Wikipedia text) and a hypothesis (the atomic claim): whether the premise entails the hypothesis (SUPPORTED), contradicts it (REFUTED), or is neutral/insufficient (NOT ENOUGH INFO). In FactScore's formulation, only claims labeled as SUPPORTED count as correct; both REFUTED and NOT ENOUGH INFO count as errors (or at least not-supported). The fine-tuned NLI model takes the Wikipedia article text and the atomic claim as input and outputs a classification.

**Why a separate NLI model rather than prompting the base model:** The NLI model is specifically fine-tuned for the verification task, making it more reliable at this narrow function than a general-purpose LLM prompted to fact-check. It is also much cheaper to run than calling GPT-3.5 repeatedly per claim. The choice of Llama-1-7B over a larger model represents a practical trade-off: it is fast and efficient for processing thousands of claims, while still having sufficient language understanding to perform NLI on Wikipedia-level text.

**Stage 4: Truthfulness score computation.** The passage's truthfulness score is simply:

$$\text{FactScore}(\text{passage}) = \frac{\text{Number of claims labeled SUPPORTED}}{\text{Total number of extracted claims}}$$

This produces a scalar in `$[0, 1]$` where 1.0 means every extracted claim is supported by Wikipedia and 0.0 means none are. Partial credit is naturally captured: a passage with 8 correct claims and 2 incorrect claims scores 0.8.

**Key limitation the paper acknowledges:** Reference-based truthfulness "requires access to relevant, high-quality reference texts against which to measure consistency" (Section 3.1). This limits applicability to domains where (a) entities have Wikipedia pages, (b) the relevant article is comprehensive enough to verify claims, and (c) the retrieval mapping from prompt to article is reliable. Niche topics, rapidly changing information, and domains without curated reference corpora are not served by this approach. Additionally, the NLI model itself may make errors—labeling a true claim as unsupported or a false claim as supported—which introduces noise into the training signal.

#### Reference-Free Confidence-Based Truthfulness Estimation

To eliminate the dependency on external knowledge bases, the paper introduces a novel method that uses the **model's own confidence** as a proxy for truthfulness. The core intuition, grounded in prior work (Kadavath et al., 2022; Tian et al., 2023), is that "a large language model's confidence in a generated answer is highly correlated with the probability that the answer is correct"—i.e., the model is well-calibrated, so when it is uncertain about a fact, that uncertainty often indicates the fact may be wrong. The challenge is operationalizing this intuition for long-form text containing many heterogeneous facts.

**Stage 1: Atomic claim extraction (shared with FactScore).** The same GPT-3.5-based atomic claim extraction described in Section 3.4 (under reference-based estimation) is applied to decompose the passage into individual factual claims. This step is identical regardless of which truthfulness estimator is used downstream.

**Stage 2: Converting claims to unambiguous questions.** Each atomic claim must be converted to a question whose answer directly tests the factual content of the claim. The paper emphasizes that "careful rephrasing is necessary to ensure that the rephrased question is unambiguous" (Section 3.2). For example:

- Claim: "Yo-Yo Ma plays the cello."
- Good question: "What instrument does Yo-Yo Ma play?" (unambiguous—the answer type is clearly "instrument")
- Bad question: "What does Yo-Yo Ma play?" (ambiguous—could be instrument, sport, video game)

If an ambiguous question is used, the model might assign 50% probability to "cello" and 50% to "basketball," reflecting question ambiguity rather than factual uncertainty about Yo-Yo Ma's instrument. The paper uses GPT-3.5 with few-shot prompting (detailed in Appendix A.1, Table 7) to perform this conversion. The prompts demonstrate the desired transformation with entity-specific examples (e.g., for Hillary Clinton: "Hillary Clinton was born in 1947" → "In what year was Hillary Clinton born?"; for LeBron James: "James was born in Akron" → "In what city was LeBron James born?"; for medical conditions: "Menopause is the time when a woman no longer has menstrual periods" → "Menopause is the time when a woman no longer has what?"). These examples teach GPT-3.5 to identify the key factual content and formulate a question that isolates it.

**Why question conversion is necessary:** Directly resampling completions of the original claim would not produce a meaningful confidence measure, because the model's probability of generating a particular phrasing is confounded by stylistic variation. If the model generated "Yo-Yo Ma plays the cello" with high probability, that might indicate confidence in the fact OR simply that this is the most common phrasing in the training data. By converting to a targeted question, the method isolates factual knowledge: the model's answer distribution to "What instrument does Yo-Yo Ma play?" reflects its uncertainty about Yo-Yo Ma's instrument specifically, not about how to phrase biographical statements.

**Stage 3: Resampling answers and measuring confidence.** For each question, the answer is resampled 20 times from the base model (Llama-1-7B is used throughout the paper for this step, regardless of which model is being fine-tuned) using a few-shot prompt to encourage well-formed answers. The 20 answers are then grouped into semantic equivalence bins. The paper compares two equivalence-checking methods:

- **Heuristic string matching:** Check whether the words in the answer, excluding stop words, are the same. This is a fast, simple approach that treats "cello" and "the cello" as equivalent but "cello" and "violoncello" as different.
- **GPT-3.5 semantic equivalence:** Ask GPT-3.5 whether two answers express the same information, inspired by Kuhn et al. (2023). This can recognize that "cello" and "violoncello" are the same instrument, but adds computational cost.

The confidence score for a claim is the fraction of the 20 resampled answers that fall into the **largest** (most frequent) equivalence bin:

$$\text{Confidence}(\text{claim}) = \frac{\text{Count of answers in largest bin}}{20}$$

For example, if 18 out of 20 answers to "What instrument does Yo-Yo Ma play?" fall into the bin containing "cello" and its string-equivalent variants, the confidence is 18/20 = 0.9. If answers are scattered across "cello" (8), "violin" (6), "piano" (4), and "flute" (2), the confidence is 8/20 = 0.4, indicating the model is uncertain.

**Why resample from the base model rather than the fine-tuned model:** The base model (Llama-1-7B) is used for confidence estimation regardless of whether the model being fine-tuned is Llama-1-7B or Llama-2-7B. This is a deliberate choice: the base model's confidence serves as a stable signal, whereas if the fine-tuned model were used, the confidence estimates would change as training progresses, creating a moving target that could destabilize learning. The base model's confidence provides a fixed, pre-existing measure of factual reliability independent of the fine-tuning process.

**Why maximum confidence over entropy:** The paper experiments with both maximum confidence (probability of the largest bin) and entropy over the semantic bins as confidence metrics. Table 5 shows that maximum confidence provides noticeably better results for biographies under the atomic question setting. The reason is likely that entropy is sensitive to the number of distinct plausible answers—a model that assigns 5% probability to each of 20 different plausible instruments would have very high entropy, but the maximum confidence (5%) would correctly indicate very low certainty. Entropy conflates the depth of uncertainty (how many plausible alternatives exist) with the model's commitment to any specific answer; maximum confidence isolates whether the model has any solid belief. For factuality scoring, knowing that the model has exactly one strong belief (even if that belief is wrong) is more useful than knowing how many weak beliefs it entertains.

**Stage 4: Passage-level truthfulness score.** The passage's overall confidence-based truthfulness score is the average of the per-claim confidence scores:

$$\text{MC-Score}(\text{passage}) = \frac{1}{C} \sum_{c=1}^{C} \text{Confidence}(\text{claim}_c)$$

where `$C$` is the number of extracted claims. This produces a scalar in `$[0, 1]$` where higher values indicate the model is more confident (and therefore, under the calibration assumption, more likely correct) about the facts it generated.

**Why averaging works as a factuality proxy:** The calibration literature (Kadavath et al., 2022) has established that for short-form QA, model confidence and correctness are highly correlated. By decomposing long-form text into short-form QA pairs (claim → question → answer), the method extends this calibration insight to long-form generation. Each claim becomes functionally equivalent to a short-form QA problem, and the average confidence over claims becomes a proxy for the overall factual reliability of the passage. A passage where the model is consistently uncertain about what it just said is likely a passage with many errors; a passage where the model is consistently confident is more likely to be correct.

**Alternative fact extraction: named entity resampling.** The paper also experiments with a simpler, cheaper alternative to GPT-3.5 claim extraction: using a named entity recognizer (spaCy; Honnibal & Montani, 2017) to identify named entities in the response, then resampling those entities in context 20 times, binning them by equivalence, and measuring maximum confidence. For medical QA, the paper uses noun chunks instead of named entities since medical facts may not center on named entities. The results (Table 5) show that atomic question extraction generally outperforms entity extraction, but the difference on medical QA is small, suggesting entity-based extraction may be viable in resource-constrained settings. However, entity extraction is less comprehensive: it only measures confidence in entity mentions, missing factual content in verbs (e.g., "founded," "discovered," "died of"), relations, and numerical claims that don't involve named entities. Atomic claim extraction via GPT-3.5 is more thorough but more expensive.

**The surprising result about GPT-3.5 equivalence matching.** Table 5 shows that using GPT-3.5 for semantic equivalence binning produces **worse** results than the simple heuristic string match. The paper hypothesizes that the heuristic match "consistently underestimates semantic entropy across all examples," providing a biased but **consistent** signal, whereas GPT-3.5 matching could "either over or underestimate samples, resulting in noisier preference pairs" (Section 4.4). In other words, a systematically conservative estimator (always grouping too finely) produces preference pairs that are noisy but not systematically misleading; a more accurate but higher-variance estimator introduces random noise that degrades the training signal. This is a practically important finding: for the purpose of constructing preference pairs for DPO, **consistency of the scoring function matters more than per-sample accuracy**, because DPO only needs the relative ordering of two responses to be correct on average, not the absolute scores.

#### Preference Pair Construction: Converting Scores to Training Data

Given a truthfulness estimator (FactScore or model confidence) and a set of prompted candidate responses, the procedure for constructing a preference dataset is straightforward:

**Step 1: Sampling candidate responses.** For each prompt `$x$`, the model (before fine-tuning) generates `$n$` candidate responses `$y_1, y_2, \ldots, y_n$` using temperature sampling with temperature `$T = 1.0$`. For pre-trained models (not yet SFT-ed), few-shot prompting is used to elicit well-formed responses. The temperature 1.0 is chosen to produce diversity—different responses may contain different factual errors, which means the truthfulness scores will vary within a prompt, creating meaningful preference pairs. If all responses were near-identical (as with low-temperature sampling), most pairs would have equal scores and be discarded.

**Step 2: Scoring each response.** Each response `$y_i$` is passed through the chosen truthfulness estimator to produce a scalar score `$s(y_i) \in [0, 1]$`.

**Step 3: Forming all pairwise preferences.** For each prompt, all `$\binom{n}{2}$` possible pairs `$(y_i, y_j)$` are considered. If `$s(y_i) > s(y_j)$`, then `$y_i$` is labeled as preferred (`$y_w$`) and `$y_j$` as dispreferred (`$y_l$`). If `$s(y_i) = s(y_j)$`, the pair is discarded. For `$m$` prompts with `$n$` responses each, this yields up to `$m \cdot \binom{n}{2}$` preference pairs, minus the `$k$` pairs with tied scores.

**Why all pairs rather than only extreme pairs:** One could imagine only comparing the highest-scoring and lowest-scoring responses per prompt, or constructing a ranking from the scores. The paper takes the exhaustive approach: every pair with a score difference (no matter how small) becomes a training example. This maximizes data utilization—`$\binom{n}{2}$` pairs from `$n$` responses provides `$\mathcal{O}(n^2)$` training signal from `$\mathcal{O}(n)$` generation cost. However, it also means that many pairs have very small score differences, potentially making the preference label noisy. The DPO loss weights all pairs equally, so pairs with small score differences contribute the same gradient magnitude as pairs with large score differences. The implicit assumption is that the truthfulness score is sufficiently well-behaved that even small score differences reliably indicate the more-factual response—an assumption that may not hold in practice but seems to work empirically.

**Dataset sizes (Table 1).** For biographies: 296 training entities (individuals with Wikipedia pages), 1 prompt per entity ("Write a short biography of [NAME]"), 10 responses per prompt. This produces at most `$296 \times \binom{10}{2} = 296 \times 45 = 13,320$` preference pairs before tie removal. For medical QA: 150 training medical conditions (with Wikipedia pages), 6 questions per condition, 6 responses per question. This produces at most `$150 \times 6 \times \binom{6}{2} = 900 \times 15 = 13,500$` preference pairs before tie removal. The number of responses per prompt is adjusted between datasets to keep the total number of pairs roughly similar.

**Test set construction.** For biographies: 59 held-out test entities (separate from the 296 training entities). For medical QA: 50 held-out test medical conditions. This entity-level split ensures that test prompts are about entities never seen during training in any form, testing whether factuality improvements generalize to new topics rather than merely memorizing corrections for training entities.

---

#### Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) Stage

Before DPO, the model is fine-tuned on all generated responses using standard next-token prediction (cross-entropy loss). The paper refers to this as the SFT model, and it serves as both a baseline and as the reference model `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$` for DPO.

**Training data for SFT:** All `$n$` responses for all training prompts are used as SFT targets, regardless of their truthfulness scores. This means the SFT model is trained to imitate the base model's output distribution, including both factual and hallucinated responses. The SFT stage primarily adapts the model to the task format (biography style, medical QA style) and improves fluency—it learns the "how" of generating task-appropriate text without learning the "what" of preferring factual over hallucinated content.

**Why SFT before DPO:** This is standard practice in preference-based fine-tuning (Rafailov et al., 2023; Ouyang et al., 2022). DPO requires a reference model `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$`, and the SFT model provides a natural choice: it represents what the model would produce if trained only to imitate the data distribution, without any preference signal. The DPO stage then refines this model to shift probability mass within the SFT-learned distribution toward more-factual responses. Without the SFT stage, the pre-trained model might not produce well-formed responses in the target format (e.g., biography-style text), making both the truthfulness scoring and the DPO optimization less effective.

#### Key Hyperparameters, Configurations, and Design Choices

**Models:** All experiments use 7B-parameter models—either Llama-1-7B (Touvron et al., 2023a) or Llama-2-7B (Touvron et al., 2023b), including the Llama-2-7B-Chat variant for the RLHF-composed experiments (Section 4.2). The choice of 7B scale is pragmatic: it is large enough to exhibit the factuality problems the paper addresses, but small enough to fine-tune with academic compute resources. The paper explicitly notes that "scaling up our factuality tuning recipe to larger models (and larger preference datasets) may reduce hallucinations even further" (Section 6), framing the 7B experiments as a proof of concept.

**Temperature for candidate generation:** Temperature `$T = 1.0$` is used for sampling candidate responses during preference pair construction. This is higher than typical decoding temperatures (which are often 0.7-0.9) and is chosen deliberately to maximize diversity—the goal is to obtain responses with meaningfully different factuality profiles, not to produce the single best response. At inference time (evaluation), the temperature used for the fine-tuned model is not explicitly stated, but the evaluation protocol (FactScore) operates on sampled generations, suggesting non-zero temperature is used.

**Number of responses per prompt:** For biographies, `$n = 10$` responses per prompt. For medical QA, `$n = 6$` responses per prompt. This choice balances the `$\mathcal{O}(n^2)$` growth in preference pairs with generation cost—10 responses provide 45 pairs, which is a substantial training signal per prompt.

**DPO hyperparameter `$\beta$`:** The paper does not report the specific value of `$\beta$` used in DPO training. This is a notable omission, as `$\beta$` is the critical hyperparameter controlling the KL penalty strength. The absence suggests the value may have been chosen via default or brief sweep without extensive tuning, or that the paper's focus is on the methodology rather than hyperparameter sensitivity.

**Model confidence estimation specifics:**
- Base model for resampling: Llama-1-7B (used consistently even when fine-tuning Llama-2 models).
- Number of resamples per claim-derived question: 20.
- Few-shot prompting is used to encourage well-formed answers to questions.
- Equivalence binning: heuristic string matching (excluding stop words) is the default, with GPT-3.5 semantic equivalence explored as an ablation (found to be worse, Table 5).
- The confidence score is maximum confidence (fraction in largest bin), not entropy.

**Truthfulness scoring models:**
- Claim extraction: GPT-3.5 (`gpt-3.5-turbo`).
- NLI model for FactScore: Llama-1-7B fine-tuned for fact-checking (trained to classify claims as SUPPORTED, REFUTED, or NOT ENOUGH INFO given Wikipedia text as premise).
- Question generation (for confidence method): GPT-3.5 with few-shot prompts (detailed in Appendix A.1, Table 7).

**Training procedure:** The paper does not report specific optimizer, learning rate, batch size, or number of epochs for DPO training. This is a significant gap for reproducibility. The expectation is that standard DPO hyperparameters from Rafailov et al. (2023) are used, but the paper does not confirm this. The SFT stage similarly lacks detailed training configuration.

**Evaluation:** All evaluations use FactScore (Min et al., 2023) as the primary metric, reporting the number of correct facts per response (`# Correct`), the number of incorrect facts per response (`# Incorrect`), and the proportion of correct facts out of total extracted facts (`% Correct`). The paper validates FactScore against human evaluators (Table 6) and GPT-4 judgments (Figure 4), finding good agreement. For medical QA, an additional relevance check is performed using GPT-3.5 (prompt in Appendix A.1) to ensure that extracted claims are relevant to the question—this prevents factually correct but off-topic statements from inflating the score. For biographies, relevance is near 100% so this check is skipped.

#### Why This Design Over Alternatives

**Why DPO over PPO:** The paper explicitly cites implementation complexity as the reason. PPO requires online sampling, advantage estimation, a separate value function, and careful hyperparameter tuning (Zheng et al., 2023). DPO's single classification loss on a static dataset is dramatically simpler and more stable. For a research contribution focused on the *scoring method* rather than the *optimization algorithm*, reducing the algorithmic complexity is the right choice: it isolates the contribution to the truthfulness scoring and preference construction, making it clearer that the gains come from the signal quality, not from RL wizardry.

**Why FactScore over other reference-based methods:** The paper chose FactScore because it uses Wikipedia, which is "relatively consistent and high quality" (Section 3.1) compared to web search results (used by FacTool; Chern et al., 2023). Wikipedia provides a stable, curated knowledge base where articles are specifically written to be comprehensive for their topic. Web search results vary by query, time, and search engine, making reproducibility harder. The trade-off is that Wikipedia limits the domain to entities with Wikipedia articles—but this is acceptable for the proof-of-concept scope.

**Why model confidence as the reference-free method:** Confidence-based methods leverage the calibration property of LLMs that has been independently verified (Kadavath et al., 2022; Tian et al., 2023). Alternative reference-free approaches could include training a factuality classifier on synthetic data or using consistency across multiple model calls without external knowledge. The paper's choice of confidence is motivated by simplicity: it requires no additional training beyond what the base model already provides, and the claim-to-question conversion is a straightforward use of GPT-3.5. The key insight—that long-form factuality can be reduced to short-form QA confidence via atomic claim extraction—is elegant and practically effective.

**Why both reference-based and reference-free methods are developed:** The paper frames these as complementary options for different deployment scenarios. FactScore provides a stronger signal (better empirical results, as shown in Tables 2 and 3) but requires Wikipedia access. The confidence method is fully self-contained—it works anywhere the base model works, with no external dependencies. A practitioner with Wikipedia access would choose FactScore for maximum improvement; a practitioner without it (or in a domain where Wikipedia is insufficient) would choose the confidence method. The paper demonstrates that both are viable, establishing a spectrum of applicability from high-resource to zero-resource settings.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Factuality Improvement as a Preference Learning Problem, Not an Inference-Time Augmentation Problem

The paper's most fundamental conceptual move is reframing how we approach language model factuality. Prior to this work, the dominant paradigm for addressing hallucinations fell into two categories: either (a) modify the model's behavior at inference time through decoding heuristics or activation interventions (ITI, DoLa, truthfulness prompting), or (b) augment the generation process with external retrieval to ground outputs in evidence. Both paradigms share an implicit assumption: **the model's tendency to hallucinate is a fixed property of its weights that must be compensated for at runtime, not something that can be directly optimized away through training without expensive human labels.**

The paper breaks this assumption by showing that automated factuality scoring—whether reference-based (FactScore) or reference-free (model confidence)—can serve as a training signal for preference optimization, using exactly the same algorithmic machinery (DPO) that had previously been applied only to human-labeled preference dimensions like helpfulness and harmlessness. This reframing has three important consequences:

**First, it converts factuality from a diagnosis problem to a treatment problem.** Prior work on detecting hallucinations (Kadavath et al., 2022; Kuhn et al., 2023; Min et al., 2023) developed increasingly sophisticated methods for *identifying* when models were likely wrong, but stopped at detection. The paper shows these same detection signals can be repurposed as *training signals*—the FactScore pipeline used for evaluation in Min et al. (2023) becomes the reward proxy for DPO training. This isn't just reusing existing tools; it's recognizing that detection and improvement share a common signal, and that the gap between them can be bridged by preference learning.

**Second, it decouples factuality improvement from reliance on human annotation.** The paper reports that professional fact-checking costs ~$2,000 for 505 biographies (Min et al., 2023)—a rate that makes large-scale factuality training economically infeasible. RLHF, the standard approach for aligning models with human preferences, inherits this cost. The paper demonstrates that automated factuality scores, despite their imperfections, provide a training signal of sufficient quality to produce meaningful improvements (40-58% error reduction; Tables 2-3). This decoupling matters because it makes factuality training *scalable*: automated scoring costs compute, not human time, and compute scales with model availability.

**Third, it shows that preference learning works for objective quality dimensions, not just subjective ones.** DPO and RLHF were developed for training models to match human preferences on dimensions like helpfulness, harmlessness, and instruction-following—dimensions where "better" is inherently a matter of human judgment. Factuality is different: whether a claim is true or false is (in principle) an objective fact about the world, not a preference. The paper demonstrates that preference-based learning algorithms work for objective quality dimensions *when the preference signal can be automated*, expanding the applicability of DPO beyond its original scope. This is a general insight: any dimension where automated scoring can rank model outputs can potentially be optimized via preference learning, whether that dimension is factual accuracy, mathematical correctness, code functionality, or adherence to formal constraints.

The evidence for this reframing's effectiveness is in the direct comparisons. Table 2 shows that DPO with automated factuality preferences (FactTune-FS, FactTune-MC) consistently outperforms both RLHF (Llama-2-Chat) and inference-time interventions (ITI, DoLa) at 7B scale. Figure 3 makes the point visually: FactTune-FS is the only method in the upper-left quadrant ("strict improvement")—it simultaneously increases correct facts and decreases incorrect facts—while other methods trade one against the other. This pattern is consistent with the interpretation that preference learning directly optimizes for factuality, while inference-time heuristics and RLHF capture correlated but imperfect proxies.

### Innovation 2: Decomposing Long-Form Factuality into Short-Form Confidence via Atomic Claim Extraction

The paper's second key conceptual contribution is the **claim-to-question decomposition** that enables reference-free factuality estimation for long-form text. The core challenge is that model calibration—the correlation between confidence and correctness—had been established only for short-form question answering (Kadavath et al., 2022; Tian et al., 2023). In short-form QA, there's a natural question whose answer the model can be confident or uncertain about. In long-form text, a passage contains dozens of heterogeneous factual claims (biographical dates, institutional affiliations, causal relationships, numerical values), and the model's sequence-level probability confounds factual certainty with stylistic and syntactic choices.

The paper's insight is that **atomic claim extraction followed by question reformulation reduces long-form factuality assessment to many independent short-form QA confidence measurements.** This is a decompositional move: rather than asking "how factual is this passage?" (a holistic, difficult-to-automate judgment), the method asks "for each atomic claim in this passage, how confident is the model in the answer to a targeted question about that claim?" This decomposes an unmanageable problem into manageable subproblems that leverage existing calibration results.

What makes this distinctive isn't the individual components—atomic claim extraction already existed in FactScore (Min et al., 2023), and model confidence for QA was already documented—but the **composition into a reference-free pipeline** and the recognition that the average of per-claim confidence scores provides a usable proxy for overall passage factuality. The paper is explicit about this composition: "the confidence-based truthfulness score is the fraction of extracted atomic claims that are estimated to be supported by the model's own knowledge" (Section 3.2), where the model's knowledge is assessed through the resampling-and-binning procedure.

A subtle but important finding emerges from this decomposition: **consistency of the scoring function matters more than per-sample accuracy for preference pair construction.** Table 5 shows that the simple heuristic string match for answer equivalence produces better factuality outcomes than GPT-3.5 semantic equivalence matching, even though GPT-3.5's equivalence judgments are presumably more accurate on average. The paper hypothesizes this is because the heuristic method systematically underestimates equivalence (always erring in the same direction), providing a *consistent* signal, while GPT-3.5 introduces random noise that varies unpredictably across examples. This is a practically important insight that runs counter to the natural assumption that "more accurate scoring = better training": **for preference learning, systematic bias is less harmful than random variance**, because DPO only needs the relative ordering of two responses to be correct on average. This has implications beyond factuality—any automated scoring pipeline for preference data construction should prioritize consistency over point accuracy.

The reference-free method is not merely an ablation of FactScore; it represents a qualitatively different approach with different scaling properties. FactScore requires Wikipedia articles for relevant entities, limiting its applicability to domains with curated reference corpora. The confidence-based method requires only the base model itself, making it applicable anywhere the model can generate text. This is a fundamental advance in self-supervision: the model provides its own training signal, using its uncertainty as an implicit factuality oracle. The paper shows this signal is noisier than FactScore (FactTune-MC underperforms FactTune-FS in Tables 2-3) but still produces meaningful improvements, establishing a performance/accessibility tradeoff: reference-based scoring for maximum improvement when external knowledge is available; reference-free scoring for broad applicability with moderate gains.

### Innovation 3: Compositionality of Factuality Tuning with RLHF and Inference-Time Methods

The paper demonstrates a third conceptual contribution: **factuality can be improved as an independent dimension that composes with, rather than conflicts with, other alignment objectives and decoding strategies.** This is not obvious a priori. One might expect that fine-tuning for factuality would degrade the conversational quality or helpfulness that RLHF optimizes for, or that it would interfere with decoding-time factuality interventions. The paper provides evidence for the opposite.

**Composition with RLHF (Section 4.2, Table 3):** When FactTune is applied to Llama-2-7B-Chat (a model already fine-tuned with RLHF for helpfulness and safety), factuality improves further—from 74.8% to 83.1% correct facts on biographies (FactTune-FS) and from 63.6% to 68.2% on medical QA. This means RLHF and factuality tuning optimize non-overlapping aspects of model behavior, and the gains stack. RLHF teaches the model to be helpful, engaging, and safe; FactTune teaches it to be correct. The paper doesn't directly measure whether RLHF qualities degrade after factuality tuning (a limitation), but the fact that FactTune-FS generates *fewer* incorrect facts while maintaining similar numbers of correct facts suggests no catastrophic forgetting of helpfulness—the model is still producing substantial amounts of relevant content, just with fewer errors.

**Composition with decoding interventions (Section 4.3, Table 4):** Applying DoLa (decoding by contrasting layers) on top of an already factuality-tuned model yields additional accuracy improvements in most settings: for Llama-1 on biographies, correctness goes from 81.2% (FactTune-FS alone) to 86.4% (FactTune-FS + DoLa); for Llama-2 on medical QA, from 78.3% to 79.4%. The one exception is Llama-2 on biographies, where DoLa slightly reduces correctness when combined with FactTune-FS (from 89.5% to 86.5%). The paper doesn't investigate why this exception occurs, but the broader pattern suggests that **training-based and decoding-based factuality improvements operate through complementary mechanisms**—they're not redundant. Training shifts the model's output distribution toward more-factual completions; DoLa modifies how the model navigates that distribution at decoding time. The fact that they can be productively combined (in most cases) indicates they affect different aspects of the generation process.

This compositionality insight reframes how practitioners should think about factuality: not as a single knob that trades off against other desirable properties, but as an independent axis of model quality that can be optimized alongside helpfulness, harmlessness, and instruction-following. It also suggests a research direction the paper does not fully explore: **multi-objective preference learning**, where a single model is fine-tuned on preference pairs spanning factuality, helpfulness, safety, and other dimensions simultaneously. The fact that sequential optimization (RLHF then FactTune) works implies that joint optimization might work even better by finding Pareto-optimal tradeoffs during training rather than after-the-fact stacking.

### Innovation 4: The Difficulty-Agnostic Nature of Factuality Improvement (A Diagnostic Finding from Figure 3)

While not framed as a "contribution" in the paper's own language, Figure 3 (and the corresponding discussion in Section 4.1) reveals a diagnostic finding with significant implications: **FactTune-FS is the only method tested that produces a strict improvement—simultaneously increasing the number of correct facts and decreasing the number of incorrect facts—while all other methods exhibit a tradeoff.** This is not just a performance comparison; it is evidence about the *mechanism* by which different methods operate.

Consider what the alternatives do:
- **SFT** increases both correct and incorrect facts relative to the base model (more content overall, errors included)—it improves fluency and coverage without selectivity.
- **RLHF** (Llama-2-Chat) increases correct facts but also increases errors relative to SFT—it produces more engaging, detailed responses but the additional detail includes more falsehoods.
- **ITI** (inference-time truthfulness intervention) reduces errors but at the cost of reducing correct facts—it makes the model more conservative, suppressing content broadly rather than selectively suppressing errors.
- **DoLa** shows mixed behavior (helps on some dimensions, hurts on others, not consistently in the "strict improvement" zone).

Only FactTune-FS (and to a near-miss extent, FactTune-MC) occupies the upper-left region of Figure 3 where correct facts increase and incorrect facts decrease simultaneously. This pattern is diagnostic: it suggests that **preference learning from automated factuality scores teaches the model to discriminate between factual and hallucinated content, rather than simply being more or less verbose.** The model isn't just generating more content (which would increase both correct and incorrect facts, like SFT) or suppressing content (which would decrease both, like ITI). It's learning to generate *different* content—replacing specific hallucinated claims with specific factual ones, while maintaining overall informativeness.

This diagnostic finding reframes the challenge of factuality improvement. The hardest case isn't getting a model to produce fewer errors (that can be done by making it terser or more evasive); it's getting it to produce fewer errors *without* reducing the amount of correct information. FactTune accomplishes this because DPO provides a per-response preference signal that rewards responses with fewer errors and more correct statements simultaneously—the preference label captures both dimensions, so the optimization naturally pushes toward the Pareto frontier of the correct/incorrect tradeoff.

The near-miss of FactTune-MC (just outside the strict improvement region in Figure 3) is also informative: it suggests that reference-free confidence scoring is a slightly noisier signal that doesn't perfectly capture the correct/incorrect distinction, but still captures enough of it to substantially outperform non-training baselines. As confidence estimation improves (through better claim extraction, better question generation, or better equivalence checking), FactTune-MC should approach the strict-improvement region—a hypothesis the paper does not test but that follows naturally from this diagnostic framing.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The paper constructs two custom long-form generation datasets: (1) **Biographies**: 355 well-known individuals with Wikipedia pages (296 train, 59 test), with 1 prompt per entity ("Write a short biography of [NAME]") and 10 responses per prompt generated from Llama-1-7B using few-shot prompting and temperature 1.0. (2) **Medical QA**: 200 common medical conditions with Wikipedia pages (150 train, 50 test), with 6 questions about each condition (e.g., "What are the common symptoms of a stroke?") and 6 short-paragraph answers per question, also sampled from Llama-1-7B. The entity-level train/test split (Section 4, Table 1) ensures test prompts are about individuals or conditions never seen during training, testing generalization rather than memorization. The prompts themselves were generated by GPT-3.5, and answers were sampled from Llama-1-7B before any fine-tuning, creating the raw material from which preference pairs are constructed.

- **Base model(s).** All experiments use 7B-parameter models: Llama-1-7B (Touvron et al., 2023a) and Llama-2-7B (Touvron et al., 2023b), including the Llama-2-7B-Chat variant for experiments on composing factuality tuning with RLHF (Section 4.2). The paper states these models were chosen because they represent "open-source consumer language models" that are widely used and representative of contemporary LLM capabilities while being small enough for academic fine-tuning. The 7B scale is explicitly acknowledged as a limitation—the authors frame this as a proof of concept, noting that "scaling up our factuality tuning recipe to larger models (and larger preference datasets) may reduce hallucinations even further" (Section 6).

- **Metrics.** The primary evaluation metric is **FactScore** (Min et al., 2023), which decomposes a generated response into atomic claims using GPT-3.5 and checks each claim against the relevant Wikipedia article using a Llama-1-7B fine-tuned for natural language inference (NLI). Three quantities are reported per response: (1) **# Correct**: the number of atomic claims classified as SUPPORTED by the Wikipedia article; (2) **# Incorrect**: the number of atomic claims classified as either REFUTED or NOT ENOUGH INFO; (3) **% Correct**: the proportion of SUPPORTED claims out of total extracted claims. For Medical QA, an additional **relevance check** is performed using GPT-3.5 (prompt in Appendix A.1) to filter out claims that, while factually correct, are irrelevant to the asked question—this prevents off-topic factual statements from inflating the score. For biographies, the paper notes that "essentially 100% of facts were relevant to the individual" (Section 4), so the relevance check is skipped. To validate FactScore as an evaluation metric, the paper reports (Section 4.5, Table 6) that FactScore measurements correlate with human judgments: on biographies, FactScore rates the FactTune-FS model at 92.1% correct vs. human-rated 84.6%; on medical QA, 80.6% vs. human-rated 83.8%. Additionally, Figure 4 shows that FactScore error counts and GPT-4-counted error counts are highly correlated across all model variants, providing a second automated validation.

- **Baselines.** The paper compares against five baselines: (1) **SFT** (Supervised Fine-Tuning): the model fine-tuned via maximum likelihood on all generated responses for the task, without any preference signal—this serves as both a baseline and the reference model `π_ref` for DPO training. (2) **ITI** (Inference-Time Intervention; Li et al., 2023): at inference time, model activations are shifted along a "truthfulness direction" identified by training linear probes on FactScore labels from the training data. (3) **DoLa** (Decoding by Contrasting Layers; Chuang et al., 2023): a decoding-time method that contrasts logits from later layers against earlier layers to surface factual knowledge. (4) **RLHF** (Llama-2-Chat): Touvron et al. (2023b)'s 7B chat model trained with reinforcement learning from human feedback on helpfulness and safety—this represents the state-of-the-art for aligned dialogue models. (5) **Majority voting** (implicitly referenced in Section 5 but not formally listed): mentioned as a component of prior approaches but not a primary baseline. Note that ITI and DoLa are applied to the SFT model for each task, creating fair comparisons against methods that also start from the same base.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper does not use a unified "generation budget" as a compute metric in the style of scaling law papers (there is no FLOPs counting or budget sweep for inference). Instead, the relevant costs are: (1) **Preference pair construction cost**: For the biographies task, 296 prompts × 10 responses = 2,960 generations from the base model for training data; for medical QA, 150 conditions × 6 questions × 6 responses = 5,400 generations. Each response then undergoes either FactScore evaluation (GPT-3.5 claim extraction + NLI verification) or confidence-based scoring (GPT-3.5 claim extraction + GPT-3.5 question generation + 20 resamples per claim). The paper does not report the total GPT-3.5 API cost or GPU hours for this data generation. (2) **Training cost**: DPO fine-tuning on the constructed preference pairs—the paper does not report training time, number of epochs, or GPU requirements. (3) **Inference cost**: At test time, the fine-tuned model is used normally (no retrieval, no claim extraction, no scoring), so inference cost equals standard autoregressive generation from a 7B model. This asymmetry—expensive training-time scoring, cheap inference—is the core efficiency argument.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not employ cross-validation in the classical sense. Instead, it uses a fixed entity-level train/test split: for biographies, 296 training entities vs. 59 test entities; for medical QA, 150 training conditions vs. 50 test conditions. Because test entities never appear in training, this tests generalization to new topics rather than memorization of specific entity facts. The paper reports average scores across all test examples without confidence intervals or statistical significance tests. The human evaluation (Section 4.5, Table 6) was conducted via Prolific.co with human evaluators compensated at an estimated $16–18/hour, but the number of human evaluators per response and inter-annotator agreement metrics are not reported. The GPT-4 validation (Figure 4) shows correlation between GPT-4 and FactScore error counts, plotted without error bars or regression statistics.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Factuality Tuning Across Models and Domains (Table 2)

The core experimental result appears in Table 2 (Section 4.1), comparing FactTune-FS and FactTune-MC against SFT, ITI, and DoLa baselines on both Llama-1-7B and Llama-2-7B. The headline numbers:

**On biographies with Llama-1-7B:**
- SFT produces 13.78 correct facts and 12.16 incorrect facts per response (56.8% correct).
- FactTune-FS produces 14.81 correct facts and 3.75 incorrect facts (81.2% correct)—a **69% reduction in errors** (12.16 → 3.75) while maintaining slightly more correct facts.
- FactTune-MC produces 10.59 correct facts and 2.94 incorrect facts (78.3% correct)—a **76% reduction in errors** but also a 23% reduction in correct facts.
- ITI (11.67 correct, 6.69 incorrect, 66.9%) and DoLa (11.75 correct, 3.84 incorrect, 75.4%) both underperform FactTune-FS on the % Correct metric, though DoLa achieves comparable error reduction to FactTune-FS at the cost of fewer correct facts.

**On biographies with Llama-2-7B:**
- Llama-2-Chat (RLHF) produces 19.03 correct facts and 6.41 incorrect facts (74.8% correct)—it generates substantially more content than the Llama-1 models but also more errors.
- FactTune-FS produces 17.06 correct facts and 2.00 incorrect facts (89.5% correct)—a **69% reduction in errors** (6.41 → 2.00) relative to Llama-2-Chat, with a modest decrease in correct facts.
- FactTune-MC produces 11.31 correct facts and 2.06 incorrect facts (84.6% correct)—a **68% reduction in errors** but a more substantial drop in correct facts.
- SFT on Llama-2 (12.19 correct, 5.19 incorrect, 70.1%) underperforms all factuality-tuned variants and the RLHF model, confirming that SFT alone does not improve factuality.

**On medical QA with Llama-1-7B:**
- SFT: 10.75 correct, 6.31 incorrect (63.0%).
- FactTune-FS: 10.88 correct, 4.50 incorrect (70.7%)—a **29% reduction in errors** with essentially identical correct facts.
- FactTune-MC: 12.31 correct, 6.88 incorrect (64.2%)—actually *increases* errors relative to SFT, though it increases correct facts even more.
- ITI (8.91 correct, 5.16 incorrect, 63.3%) and DoLa (8.03 correct, 5.91 incorrect, 57.6%) both underperform on % Correct.

**On medical QA with Llama-2-7B:**
- Llama-2-Chat: 9.63 correct, 5.50 incorrect (63.6%).
- FactTune-FS: 12.53 correct, 3.47 incorrect (78.3%)—a **37% reduction in errors** with a substantial increase in correct facts.
- FactTune-MC: 11.41 correct, 4.80 incorrect (70.4%)—a 13% reduction in errors.

The paper highlights two key patterns in these results. First, FactTune-FS is the only method that consistently achieves a "strict improvement"—simultaneously increasing correct facts and decreasing incorrect facts—as visualized in Figure 3. Second, the improvements transfer across base models (Llama-1 → Llama-2) without requiring model-specific tuning of the factuality scoring pipeline. The confidence-based method (FactTune-MC) shows more variable results: it reliably reduces errors (except on Llama-1 medical QA) but often at the cost of fewer correct facts, suggesting its signal is noisier than FactScore's.

#### Composing Factuality Tuning with RLHF (Section 4.2, Table 3)

Table 3 presents the results of applying factuality tuning to Llama-2-7B-Chat, a model already fine-tuned with RLHF for helpfulness and safety. The key finding is that factuality improvements **stack** with RLHF:

- Llama-2-Chat baseline on biographies: 19.03 correct, 6.41 incorrect (74.8%).
- FactTune-FS on Llama-2-Chat: 19.94 correct, 4.06 incorrect (83.1%)—**37% reduction in errors** with a slight increase in correct facts.
- FactTune-MC on Llama-2-Chat: 20.91 correct, 4.84 incorrect (81.2%)—**24% reduction in errors** with increased correct facts.
- DoLa applied to Llama-2-Chat: 21.00 correct, 5.19 incorrect (80.2%)—a smaller error reduction than FactTune variants.

On medical QA, the pattern is similar but the gains are smaller:
- Llama-2-Chat: 9.63 correct, 5.50 incorrect (63.6%).
- FactTune-FS on Llama-2-Chat: 9.38 correct, 5.25 incorrect (68.2%)—a **5% reduction in errors** but slightly fewer correct facts.
- FactTune-MC on Llama-2-Chat: 10.34 correct, 5.69 incorrect (64.5%)—essentially no error reduction.

This experiment demonstrates that RLHF and factuality tuning optimize non-overlapping dimensions: RLHF teaches helpfulness and conversational quality; FactTune teaches factual correctness. The gains are composable because they operate on different aspects of model behavior. However, the medical QA results are notably weaker, suggesting that RLHF training may have already improved factuality on some domains (the gap between SFT and RLHF on medical QA is small) or that factuality tuning has less room to improve on top of RLHF for this task.

#### Complementary Benefits with Decoding-Time Interventions (Section 4.3, Table 4)

Table 4 tests whether DoLa (decoding by contrasting layers) can further improve factuality when applied on top of a factuality-tuned model. The results show generally complementary effects, though with one notable exception:

- **Llama-1 on biographies**: FactTune-FS alone achieves 14.81 correct, 3.75 incorrect (81.2%). Adding DoLa reduces correct facts to 12.44 and incorrect facts to 2.00—net correctness improves to 86.4%, the highest reported on this task. DoLa makes the model more conservative (fewer total facts) but more precise.
- **Llama-1 on medical QA**: FactTune-FS alone achieves 10.88 correct, 4.50 incorrect (70.7%). Adding DoLa improves to 11.47 correct, 3.75 incorrect (76.7%)—a strict improvement.
- **Llama-2 on biographies**: FactTune-FS alone achieves 17.06 correct, 2.00 incorrect (89.5%). Adding DoLa reduces both correct facts (16.22) and increases incorrect facts (2.65), net correctness drops to 86.5%—**performance degrades**. The paper does not investigate why this exception occurs.
- **Llama-2 on medical QA**: FactTune-FS alone: 12.53 correct, 3.47 incorrect (78.3%). With DoLa: 12.56 correct, 3.44 incorrect (79.4%)—a tiny improvement.

The paper interprets these results as evidence that training-based and decoding-based factuality improvements "operate through complementary mechanisms" (Section 4.3) in most cases, but the Llama-2 biography exception suggests the interaction is not universally additive and may depend on the specific model-task combination.

#### Preference Pair Construction Design Decisions (Section 4.4, Table 5)

Table 5 presents an ablation study of the design choices in the reference-free confidence scoring pipeline, with FactTune-MC models evaluated on both tasks. The study varies three factors: fact extraction method (atomic questions via GPT-3.5 vs. entity extraction via spaCy), equivalence checking (heuristic string match vs. GPT-3.5 semantic equivalence), and confidence metric (maximum confidence vs. entropy). The key findings:

**Atomic question extraction outperforms entity extraction.** On biographies, atomic question extraction with heuristic equivalence and maximum confidence achieves 12.2 correct, 2.56 incorrect (84.0%) vs. entity extraction's 12.7 correct, 6.31 incorrect (69.3%)—the entity-based method produces far more errors. On medical QA, the difference is smaller but atomic extraction still wins on % Correct (67.3% vs. 66.0%). The paper attributes entity extraction's weakness to its limited scope—it only measures confidence in named entity mentions, missing factual content in verbs, relations, and non-entity claims.

**Heuristic equivalence matching outperforms GPT-3.5 equivalence checking (surprising).** With atomic extraction and maximum confidence on biographies: heuristic matching achieves 12.2 correct, 2.56 incorrect (84.0%); GPT-3.5 equivalence achieves 13.7 correct, 4.16 incorrect (79.4%). The GPT-3.5 variant increases correct facts but also increases errors substantially, resulting in lower % Correct. The paper hypothesizes this is because the heuristic match "consistently underestimates semantic entropy across all examples" (Section 4.4), providing a *systematically conservative* signal, while GPT-3.5 matching introduces random noise that sometimes overestimates and sometimes underestimates equivalence. For preference pair construction, systematic bias is less harmful than random variance because DPO only requires relative ordering to be correct on average.

**Maximum confidence outperforms entropy.** With atomic extraction and heuristic matching on biographies: maximum confidence achieves 84.0% correct vs. entropy's 81.0%. The gap is larger on medical QA: 67.3% vs. 71.1% in favor of entropy with atomic extraction, but 66.0% vs. 69.3% in favor of maximum confidence with entity extraction. The metric choice interacts with the extraction method, and no single metric dominates across all settings, but maximum confidence with atomic extraction is the best overall configuration for biographies while being competitive for medical QA.

#### Metric Validation: FactScore Agreement with Humans and GPT-4 (Section 4.5, Table 6, Figure 4)

To verify that improvements in FactScore reflect genuine factuality improvements rather than overfitting to the evaluation metric (reward overoptimization; Gao et al., 2022), the paper conducts two validation studies:

**Human evaluation (Table 6).** Human evaluators from Prolific.co were given prompts, model-generated responses, and Wikipedia article titles, and asked to count total facts and incorrect facts. On biographies (Llama-1-7B), the SFT model received a human-rated accuracy of 58.2% vs. FactTune-FS at 84.6%—a 26.4 percentage point gap. On medical QA, SFT received 66.2% vs. FactTune-FS at 83.8%. The FactScore numbers for the same comparisons show larger SFT-to-FactTune-FS gaps (biographies: 66.9% → 92.1%; medical QA: 53.4% → 80.6%). The paper acknowledges that "the improvement in FactScore corresponds to a large increase in human-annotated accuracy" (Section 4.5) but does not address why FactScore's gap is larger than humans'—a potential sign of metric inflation, though the direction of improvement is consistent.

**GPT-4 validation (Figure 4).** GPT-4 was asked to count the number of factual errors in model responses, and these counts were compared against FactScore error counts. Figure 4 shows that the average FactScore error count and average GPT-4 error count are "highly correlated" across all model variants (SFT, FactTune-FS, FactTune-MC) and both datasets. The paper does not report a correlation coefficient but states that "GPT-4 model ratings and FactScore model ratings are highly correlated, and GPT-4 provides another evaluation metric that demonstrates that FactTune-FS significantly reduces average error compared to the SFT models on both datasets" (Section 4.5).

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

- **Reference-based vs. reference-free scoring (Table 2, Table 3):** Across all four model-task combinations in Table 2, FactTune-FS outperforms FactTune-MC on % Correct. The gap is largest on Llama-2 biographies (89.5% vs. 84.6%) and Llama-2 medical QA (78.3% vs. 70.4%), and smallest on Llama-1 medical QA (70.7% vs. 64.2%). On top of RLHF (Table 3), the gap narrows: 83.1% vs. 81.2% on biographies, 68.2% vs. 64.5% on medical QA. Reference-based scoring provides a consistently stronger signal but the reference-free method remains competitive, especially for reducing incorrect facts (FactTune-MC on Llama-1 biographies achieves 2.94 incorrect facts, the lowest of any method in that setting).

- **Fact extraction method (Table 5, rows 1–2 vs. rows 3–6):** Named entity extraction (spaCy) produces substantially more errors than atomic question extraction (GPT-3.5). On biographies, entity extraction with maximum confidence yields 12.7 correct, 6.31 incorrect (69.3%) vs. atomic extraction's 12.2 correct, 2.56 incorrect (84.0%)—same correct facts but 2.5× more errors. On medical QA, entity extraction with maximum confidence yields 9.5 correct, 4.78 incorrect (67.3%) vs. atomic extraction's 10.2 correct and variable incorrect rates. Entity extraction's limitation is that it only captures confidence about named entities, missing factual content in verbs, relations, numbers, and non-entity claims—and these unmeasured claims are where many errors occur.

- **Equivalence checking method (Table 5, rows 3–4 vs. rows 5–6):** Heuristic string matching outperforms GPT-3.5 semantic equivalence when using atomic extraction. With maximum confidence as the metric: heuristic achieves 84.0% on biographies vs. GPT-3.5's 79.4%; on medical QA, the results are mixed (67.3% for heuristic with entropy vs. 66.1% for GPT-3.5 with entropy). The paper's hypothesis that heuristic matching's consistent underestimation is less harmful than GPT-3.5's random noise for preference pair construction is supported by the biographies results but less clearly by the medical QA results.

- **Confidence metric (Table 5):** Maximum confidence (fraction in largest bin) and entropy over bins show task-dependent performance. On biographies with atomic extraction + heuristic matching: maximum confidence (84.0%) outperforms entropy (81.0%). On medical QA with the same configuration: entropy (71.1%) outperforms maximum confidence (67.3%). The paper does not explain this reversal, and no clear principle emerges for when to prefer which metric.

- **Model scale (implicit):** All experiments use 7B models. The paper does not ablate model size (e.g., 1B, 3B, 13B, 70B), so it is unknown whether the factuality improvements scale with model size, plateau, or reverse. The authors acknowledge this as future work (Section 6).

- **Number of resamples for confidence estimation (not ablated):** The paper fixes the number of resamples at 20 for all confidence-based experiments. No ablation of this parameter is reported—it is unknown whether 5, 10, 20, or 50 resamples would change the quality of the preference signal.

- **DPO hyperparameter β (not ablated):** The paper does not report the value of β used for DPO training, nor does it ablate this parameter. Since β controls the KL penalty strength and thus the trade-off between reward optimization and staying close to the reference model, this is a significant omission for reproducibility.

- **Generalization beyond Wikipedia-backed entities (not tested):** All test entities have Wikipedia pages, necessary for FactScore evaluation. The paper does not evaluate on entities without Wikipedia coverage, so it is unknown whether factuality improvements transfer to less-documented topics.

### Critical Assessment

The experiments in this paper are designed to answer a specific question: Can automated factuality scoring generate preference pairs of sufficient quality to meaningfully improve model factuality through DPO? The evidence presented supports an affirmative answer—with important boundary conditions.

**What the experiments actually demonstrate:** The core empirical claim is that FactTune-FS reduces factual errors by ~40–58% relative to Llama-2-Chat (Table 2: from 6.41 to 2.00 incorrect facts on biographies, from 5.50 to 3.47 on medical QA). This is well-supported by the FactScore measurements and validated by human and GPT-4 evaluations (Table 6, Figure 4). However, the experiments demonstrate something narrower than "fine-tuning makes models factual"—they demonstrate that fine-tuning on factuality preferences **for entities with Wikipedia pages** using a specific scoring pipeline reduces errors **as measured by FactScore** on held-out entities of the same type. Whether this transfers to open-domain generation without Wikipedia-backed reference, to entities without comprehensive Wikipedia articles, or to other evaluation metrics is not tested.

The paper's secondary claim—that reference-free confidence estimation provides a viable alternative to reference-based scoring—is supported but with clear qualification: FactTune-MC consistently underperforms FactTune-FS (Tables 2–3), and on Llama-1 medical QA it actually increases errors (12.31 correct, 6.88 incorrect vs. SFT's 10.75 correct, 6.31 incorrect). The reference-free method works, but not as well, and the paper doesn't establish when it will fail. The diagnostic from Figure 3—that FactTune-MC lies "just outside the strict improvement region"—is helpful but qualitative.

**Where the experimental design is strong:** The entity-level train/test split is the right choice for testing generalization—the model must produce factual text about people and conditions it never saw during training, which is the realistic deployment scenario. The dual validation with humans and GPT-4 addresses the legitimate concern that FactScore-optimized models might overfit to the FactScore metric (reward overoptimization). The comparison against RLHF (Llama-2-Chat) and inference-time methods (ITI, DoLa) establishes that factuality tuning provides gains beyond what existing alignment and decoding methods achieve. The composition experiments (Tables 3–4) are valuable for understanding how factuality tuning interacts with other methods.

**Where the experimental design is weak:**

*Small test sets, no confidence intervals.* The test set contains 59 biography entities and 50 medical conditions—small enough that a few outlier entities could meaningfully shift averages. No confidence intervals or statistical tests are reported, so it is impossible to assess whether the differences between methods (especially the close comparisons, like FactTune-FS vs. FactTune-FS+DoLa) are statistically reliable. The human evaluation (Table 6) does not report the number of evaluators, number of responses evaluated per model, or inter-annotator agreement, making it impossible to assess the reliability of the human judgments.

*Metric validation is incomplete.* While FactScore correlates with human judgments (Table 6), the paper does not address the possibility that DPO fine-tuning on FactScore preferences could lead to models that score well on FactScore specifically (by learning to avoid the types of claims FactScore's NLI model tends to reject) without genuinely being more factual in ways that humans would confirm. The correlation in Figure 4 is presented without a correlation coefficient or any quantification of the relationship beyond visual inspection. A more thorough validation would include multiple automated factuality metrics (FacTool, for instance) and more extensive human evaluation.

*Missing hyperparameter details.* The DPO β parameter is never reported, and no SFT or DPO training details (optimizer, learning rate, batch size, number of epochs) are provided. This makes exact replication impossible from the paper alone. Given that DPO's behavior is sensitive to β (it controls the KL penalty, which determines how far the model can move from the reference), the absence of this value is a significant gap.

*Single model scale (7B).* All experiments use 7B models. The paper acknowledges this as a limitation and calls for scaling studies, but the absence of even a single larger-scale experiment (e.g., 13B) means we don't know whether factuality tuning's benefits grow, shrink, or plateau with model size. Prior work (Mallen et al., 2023) showed that larger models rely less on retrieval, suggesting larger models may have more internal factual knowledge to surface through tuning—or, alternatively, that larger models are already close to their factual ceiling and have less room for improvement.

*No evaluation of factuality tuning's effect on other capabilities.* The paper focuses exclusively on factuality metrics. It does not measure whether factuality tuning affects the model's helpfulness, instruction-following, reasoning ability, or other dimensions that matter in deployment. The composition experiments (Table 3) show that factuality tuning on top of RLHF increases % Correct, but they don't measure whether RLHF-trained conversational quality degrades. Similarly, while FactTune reduces incorrect facts, it may also make the model more evasive—refusing to answer questions it would have answered before (with errors)—which would reduce errors at the cost of usefulness.

*The confidence-based method's cost is unanalyzed.* FactTune-MC requires GPT-3.5 for claim extraction and question generation plus 20 resamples per claim from Llama-1-7B. For a passage containing 10–20 claims, this means 200–400 additional forward passes through a 7B model plus multiple GPT-3.5 API calls *per response* during training data construction. The paper presents this as eliminating the need for external knowledge, but it introduces substantial computational cost that is never quantified.

*The Llama-2 biography exception in Table 4 is unexplained.* DoLa degrades FactTune-FS performance on Llama-2 biographies (89.5% → 86.5%) but helps or is neutral in the other three model-task combinations. The paper offers no investigation or hypothesis for this exception, leaving an open question about when decoding interventions and factuality tuning complement versus interfere.

*No experiment combining FactScore and confidence-based scoring.* The paper treats reference-based and reference-free scoring as alternatives, but a natural extension—using both signals jointly (e.g., averaging scores or constructing separate preference pairs from each and combining them in training)—is not explored. This could potentially combine the strength of FactScore with the broader coverage of confidence-based scoring.

**Experiments that would have strengthened the paper:** (1) A scaling study with at least one larger model (13B or 70B). (2) An evaluation on a domain without Wikipedia coverage (e.g., recent events, niche technical topics, fictional entities) to test generalization. (3) Multiple automated factuality metrics beyond FactScore to reduce dependence on a single evaluation pipeline. (4) Measurement of helpfulness/fluency/relevance before and after factuality tuning to assess potential tradeoffs. (5) A held-out test set with human factuality labels collected specifically for evaluation (rather than the small Prolific study), with multiple annotators per response and reported inter-annotator agreement. (6) An ablation of the number of candidate responses per prompt (currently fixed at 10 for biographies, 6 for medical QA) to determine how preference dataset size affects factuality gains. (7) A comparison against a simple baseline: using the FactScore to filter training data (only train on responses above a threshold) rather than preference optimization, which would test whether the DPO machinery is necessary or whether supervised fine-tuning on filtered data achieves similar results.

**Summary of claim support:** The paper's central claim—that DPO on automated factuality preferences reduces factual errors—is well-supported within the tested conditions (7B models, Wikipedia-backed entities, two specific task types, FactScore evaluation). The claim that the reference-free method provides a viable alternative is supported but weaker, with variable results across tasks and consistently lower performance than the reference-based method. The claim that factuality tuning composes with RLHF and decoding interventions is supported but incompletely characterized (missing measurements of other capability dimensions, unexplained exceptions). The paper succeeds as a proof of concept demonstrating that automated factuality scoring can generate training signals of sufficient quality to produce meaningful reductions in hallucination, but the generalizability, scalability, and side effects of this approach remain open questions.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### 6.1 The Method Only Demonstrates Gains on Wikipedia-Backed Entities; Generalization to Other Domains Is Unknown

**The assumption or constraint.** The entire factuality tuning pipeline—both FactScore-based scoring and the confidence-based method—is built and evaluated on entities and medical conditions that have Wikipedia articles. The paper explicitly acknowledges this dependency: "Because FactScore uses retrieval against a given Wikipedia article, we generate data based on individuals and medical conditions that have Wikipedia pages" (Section 4). The confidence-based method eliminates the need for Wikipedia at training time, but the *evaluation* of all methods still relies on FactScore, which requires Wikipedia articles for fact-checking. Even the human evaluation (Section 4.5) provided annotators with Wikipedia article titles for reference.

**The consequence.** The paper provides no evidence that factuality improvements transfer to domains where curated, comprehensive reference corpora do not exist—which describes the vast majority of real-world language model use cases. If a user asks about a niche hobby, a recent news event, a local business, a fictional universe, or a technical subfield without Wikipedia coverage, the training pipeline would need either (a) a different reference corpus of unknown quality, or (b) the reference-free confidence method, which the paper shows is substantially weaker than FactScore (Table 2: FactTune-MC achieves 78.3% vs. FactTune-FS's 81.2% on Llama-1 biographies, and 64.2% vs. 70.7% on medical QA; Table 3 shows smaller but consistent gaps). Worse, because evaluation itself requires FactScore (or human evaluation against a reference), we cannot even *measure* whether factuality tuning helps in domains without reference corpora—the entire evaluation framework collapses. For a practitioner deploying this method in an open-domain setting (chatbots, general-purpose assistants), the most common queries will involve topics without Wikipedia-quality references, and the paper offers no guidance on whether the method works, degrades gracefully, or fails entirely in that regime.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None—this limitation is entirely unmeasured. All 355 biography entities and 200 medical conditions were selected specifically because they have Wikipedia pages. The paper does not include a single evaluation on an entity without Wikipedia coverage, nor does it discuss what factuality evaluation would even look like in such a setting. The confidence-based method is presented as a way to *train* without references, but even that method's evaluation is done via FactScore against Wikipedia.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not attempt to address this limitation. It is not mentioned as a caveat in Section 6 (Conclusion), and no experiments test generalization to non-Wikipedia domains. A practitioner would need to independently validate the method on their target domain before relying on it, and they would need to solve the evaluation problem (how to measure factuality without a reference corpus) themselves.

### 6.2 Factuality Gains Are Validated on Only One Automated Metric with Small-Scale Human Corroboration

**The assumption or constraint.** All quantitative conclusions in the paper—the 40–58% error reduction, the rankings of methods in Tables 2–4, the compositionality results—are measured exclusively by FactScore (Min et al., 2023), which uses a specific pipeline: GPT-3.5 claim extraction, Llama-1-7B NLI verification against Wikipedia. This is a single, opinionated measurement framework. Models fine-tuned to optimize FactScore-based preferences (FactTune-FS) may learn to avoid the specific types of claims that FactScore's NLI model tends to classify as unsupported, without genuinely being more factual in ways that alternative metrics or human evaluators would confirm to the same degree. The paper does validate FactScore against human judgments (Table 6) and GPT-4 (Figure 4), but this validation is thin: Table 6 reports only a single human-rated accuracy per model per dataset, with no sample sizes, inter-annotator agreement, or statistical tests; Figure 4 shows a correlation scatterplot without a correlation coefficient, regression statistics, or error bars.

**The consequence.** The headline improvements (58% error reduction on Llama-2 biographies) may partially reflect **reward overoptimization**—the model learning to exploit the specific evaluation pipeline rather than genuinely improving factuality as a human would judge it. The paper acknowledges this concern explicitly: "Nonetheless, we aim to verify that our results are not specific or overfit to the FactScore criterion" (Section 4.5). However, the validation provided is insufficient to rule it out. The human evaluation (Table 6) shows that FactScore and human judgments agree on the *direction* of improvement (FactTune-FS > SFT), but the magnitude differs: FactScore rates the FactTune-FS model at 92.1% correct on biographies vs. human-rated 84.6%—a 7.5 percentage point gap. This gap could indicate that FactScore systematically overestimates the factuality of FactTune-FS outputs relative to human judgment, which is exactly the signature of metric overoptimization. The GPT-4 correlation (Figure 4) is presented only visually, with the axes scaled per-dataset in a way that compresses the scatter and makes it difficult to assess agreement quality. A practitioner considering this method needs to know how much of the reported improvement would persist under a genuinely independent evaluation (different NLI model, different claim extractor, different reference source, or extensive human annotation), and the paper does not answer this question.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The human evaluation (Section 4.5, Table 6) provides the strongest evidence, showing that human-rated accuracy improves from 58.2% (SFT) to 84.6% (FactTune-FS) on Llama-1 biographies—a 26.4 percentage point gain that is large enough to be convincing despite methodological concerns. On medical QA, the gain is smaller but still substantial: 66.2% → 83.8%. The GPT-4 correlation (Figure 4) provides a second, independent signal. However, both validations share a limitation: they evaluate against Wikipedia as ground truth (human annotators were given Wikipedia article titles), meaning they test whether the model's claims are *Wikipedia-consistent*, not necessarily *true* in a broader sense. If FactScore and FactTune-FS both favor Wikipedia-verifiable claims over true-but-unverifiable claims, the agreement between metrics and humans would overstate genuine factuality.

**Mitigation status.** The paper partially addresses this limitation through the human and GPT-4 validations, but the validation is too small-scale and methodologically underdescribed to fully resolve the concern. The paper does not report: the number of human evaluators, the number of responses evaluated, inter-annotator agreement metrics, or confidence intervals for human-rated accuracy. The GPT-4 validation is qualitative ("highly correlated") without quantitative rigor. A more convincing validation would include multiple automated factuality metrics (FacTool, manually written factuality rubrics), larger-scale human evaluation with multiple annotators per response, and reported measurement reliability statistics.

### 6.3 Training Data Construction Is Computationally Expensive in Ways the Paper Does Not Quantify

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper frames its approach as "cost-effective" and "without human intervention" (Section 1, Section 6), emphasizing that it avoids the ~$2,000/505-biographies cost of human annotation. However, the automated pipeline is not cheap—it simply shifts cost from human labor to API calls and GPU compute, and the paper does not report what those costs actually are. Constructing the preference dataset requires, for each of thousands of model responses: (1) GPT-3.5 API calls for atomic claim extraction (and question generation, for the confidence method), (2) for FactScore, running a Llama-1-7B NLI model on each atomic claim against Wikipedia text, (3) for the confidence method, 20 answer resamples from Llama-1-7B per claim-derived question (with potentially 10–20 claims per passage, this means 200–400 forward passes through a 7B model per response), and (4) GPT-3.5 API calls for equivalence checking if the semantic variant is used.

**The consequence.** A practitioner cannot determine whether the method is economically viable for their use case without understanding these costs. The paper's abstract claims "58% and 40% reduction in factual error rate," but these numbers are achieved with a training pipeline whose total cost is never stated. If the training data construction costs $500 in API credits and $200 in GPU time, that's still cheaper than $2,000 in human annotation—but it may not be cheaper than alternative automated approaches (e.g., simply filtering the SFT training data using FactScore and doing supervised fine-tuning on the filtered subset, a baseline the paper does not compare against). Furthermore, the cost scales linearly with the number of training entities and responses—expanding to cover 10,000 entities with 10 responses each would cost roughly 30× what the paper's 296-entity biography dataset cost. For an organization considering deploying this method at scale, the absence of cost quantification makes it impossible to budget or to compare against alternatives.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. The paper does not report: the total number of GPT-3.5 API calls made during training data construction, the total GPU hours spent on NLI verification or answer resampling, the estimated dollar cost of constructing the preference datasets, or the training time/GPU hours for SFT and DPO fine-tuning. The paper acknowledges that reference-based truthfulness has a "key limitation" of requiring access to reference texts (Section 3.1) and that entity extraction is a "less expensive proxy" compared to atomic question extraction (Section 4.4), but these are relative comparisons within the pipeline, not absolute cost estimates.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper frames the method as "cost-effective" (Section 6) without attempting to quantify costs, and does not flag this as a limitation in the conclusion. Future work on cheap difficulty estimation for related methods (analogous to what other test-time compute papers acknowledge) would be needed here—developing cheaper approximations to FactScore or the confidence pipeline that preserve the training signal while reducing computation.

### 6.4 All Experiments Are at 7B Scale; Generalization to Larger or Smaller Models Is Unmeasured

**The assumption or constraint.** Every experiment in the paper uses a 7B-parameter model: either Llama-1-7B or Llama-2-7B (and their chat variants). The paper is explicit that this choice is deliberate and acknowledges the limitation in its conclusion: "Finally, we explore only 7B models in this work. Scaling up our factuality tuning recipe to larger models (and larger preference datasets) may reduce hallucinations even further" (Section 6).

**The consequence.** Three important unknowns follow from the single-scale evaluation. First, it is unknown whether the *absolute* factuality gains increase, decrease, or plateau with model size. Mallen et al. (2023) showed that larger models rely less on retrieval for factuality, which could mean larger models already have higher baseline factuality (leaving less room for FactTune to improve) or that larger models' internal knowledge is richer (making confidence-based signals more reliable and increasing gains). The paper provides evidence for neither hypothesis. Second, it is unknown whether the *relative* performance of reference-based versus reference-free scoring changes with scale. The confidence-based method uses Llama-1-7B as the confidence estimator regardless of which model is being fine-tuned (Section 3.2). If a 70B model's outputs are being scored, the Llama-1-7B confidence estimates may be poorly calibrated for facts that only the larger model knows—the smaller confidence estimator will be uncertain about facts the larger model correctly produced, flagging them as low-confidence (and thus presumably low-factuality) when they are actually correct. This would make FactTune-MC *less* effective at larger scales, not more. Third, it is unknown whether the DPO hyperparameter β (never reported) needs to be rescaled with model size—larger models have different gradient norms and may require different KL penalty strengths.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The experiments provide a thorough characterization at 7B scale across two base model families (Llama-1, Llama-2) and two task types (biographies, medical QA). The consistency of results across these axes (FactTune-FS consistently outperforms baselines) is encouraging but does not substitute for a scaling study. The paper's Figure 3 shows that RLHF (Llama-2-Chat) already achieves higher absolute factuality than any Llama-1 variant, and FactTune-FS improves on top of it, suggesting that factuality tuning can help even when the baseline is already relatively high. But this is still within 7B scale.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges this limitation explicitly in the conclusion (Section 6) and frames scaling as future work. It does not provide any scaling-related experiments, even a single 13B data point. This is a understandable limitation given academic compute constraints, but it means a practitioner deploying this method at production scale (where 70B+ models are common) is operating outside the paper's evidence base.

### 6.5 Factuality Tuning May Trade Off Against Conversational Quality, Fluency, and Other RLHF-Optimized Attributes

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper focuses exclusively on factuality metrics (% Correct, # Correct, # Incorrect) and does not measure any other dimension of response quality. The qualitative observations in Section 4.1 note that "FactTune-FS and FactTune-MC samples tend to have more objective and direct sentences and less of a conversational or story-telling style compared to the SFT model" and that "GPT-4 rates FactTune-FS as less conversational in tone than the SFT model for 77.5% (n=40) of Llama-1 questions and 65.6% (n=32) of Llama-2 samples." This is a documented but unquantified shift in style.

**The consequence.** The headline improvements in factuality may come at the cost of other attributes that users value—conversational engagement, helpfulness, perceived warmth, willingness to elaborate, or stylistic variety. A model that produces 89.5% correct facts (FactTune-FS, Table 2) but sounds robotic, terse, or evasive may be less useful in practice than a model with 74.8% correct facts (Llama-2-Chat) that users actually enjoy interacting with. The paper's own qualitative samples (Appendix Tables 8–9) illustrate this: the FactTune-FS and FactTune-MC biographies are shorter, more list-like, and lack narrative flow compared to the SFT model's more natural prose. For example, the SFT model's biography of Greta Gerwig includes sentences like "Gerwig became interested in the film industry in 2002, when she attended the San Francisco Art Institute," while the FactTune-FS version states tersely "Born in Sacramento, California in 1983, Gerwig started acting at a young age." The reduction in errors is clear, but so is the reduction in narrative quality.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The GPT-4 tone assessment (Section 4.1) is the only quantitative measure of this tradeoff, and it is reported only for a small subset of questions (n=40 for Llama-1, n=32 for Llama-2). The paper does not report whether helpfulness, relevance, coverage (number of distinct facts about the entity), or instruction-following degrade after factuality tuning. It does not report whether users prefer factuality-tuned outputs to RLHF outputs in a side-by-side comparison. The only quality metric consistently reported is factuality, creating a one-dimensional picture of a multi-dimensional problem.

**Mitigation status.** Partially addressed through the compositionality experiments (Section 4.2, Table 3), which show that applying factuality tuning on top of RLHF (Llama-2-Chat) improves factuality without collapsing correctness. This suggests the RLHF-trained conversational qualities are at least partially preserved. However, the paper does not directly measure those qualities before and after factuality tuning, so the preservation is assumed rather than demonstrated. The paper does not frame this tradeoff as a limitation in the conclusion.

### 6.6 The Reference-Free Confidence Method Has a Fundamental Vulnerability: Model Confidence as a Proxy for Truthfulness Breaks When the Model Is Confidently Wrong

**The assumption or constraint.** The confidence-based truthfulness estimator rests on a single empirical finding from prior work: "a large language model's confidence in a generated answer is highly correlated with the probability that the answer is correct" (Section 3.2, citing Kadavath et al., 2022; Tian et al., 2023). The method then inverts this correlation into a training signal: higher confidence → more likely factual → preferred response. This works when the calibration correlation holds. But it systematically fails when the model is **confidently wrong**—when it assigns high probability to an incorrect answer. In such cases, the confidence method will score the false claim as high-factuality (high confidence) and a correctly uncertain claim as low-factuality (low confidence), producing preference pairs that actively teach the model to prefer false claims.

**The consequence.** FactTune-MC will be least effective—and potentially actively harmful—on exactly the types of errors that are most pernicious: the model's deeply entrenched false beliefs that it expresses with high confidence. If a model is uncertain about whether Marie Curie was born in Poland or France (high entropy in answer resampling), the confidence method will flag claims about her birthplace as low-factuality and train the model to avoid them—which is correct, since the model is uncertain. But if the model is *confidently wrong* that Marie Curie was born in France (90% of resampled answers say "France"), the confidence method will flag the false claim as high-factuality and train the model to prefer it. The method relies on the calibration property that "uncertainty ≈ incorrectness," but the inverse ("confidence ≈ correctness") is not guaranteed. The paper's results bear this out: FactTune-MC consistently underperforms FactTune-FS (Tables 2–3), and on Llama-1 medical QA it *increases* errors relative to SFT (from 6.31 to 6.88 incorrect facts, Table 2). This failure mode—confidently wrong facts receiving high confidence scores and being reinforced—is a plausible explanation.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper does not directly measure how often the model is confidently wrong, nor does it provide any calibration analysis of the confidence estimates used for training. The ablation in Table 5 shows that different confidence estimation design choices produce different downstream factuality, but none are broken out by whether the model was correct or incorrect. The fact that FactTune-MC underperforms FactTune-FS across all settings is consistent with the hypothesis that confidence is an imperfect proxy for truthfulness, but the paper does not analyze *why* it underperforms or *when* the gap is largest. The medical QA result where FactTune-MC increases errors is a concrete warning sign but is not investigated.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper presents the confidence method as a viable reference-free alternative without analyzing its failure modes or characterizing the conditions under which it works versus fails. The conclusion (Section 6) states that "Reference-free approaches like the one we have introduced provide a particularly scalable self-supervision strategy to improve factuality, eliminating the need for a reference corpus of 'gold' texts" without noting the confidently-wrong-facts vulnerability. A practitioner using FactTune-MC should be aware that the method may reinforce exactly the confident falsehoods they most want to eliminate, and the paper provides no diagnostic for detecting when this is happening.
