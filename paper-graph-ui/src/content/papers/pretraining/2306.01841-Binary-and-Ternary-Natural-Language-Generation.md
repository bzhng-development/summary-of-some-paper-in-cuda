# Binary and Ternary Natural Language Generation

**ArXiv:** [2306.01841](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.01841)

## 🎯 Pitch

This paper introduces and empirically validates **TBT (Ternary / Binary Transformer)**, a framework for quantizing both weights and activations of encoder-decoder transformer models to 2-bit ternary or 1-bit binary precision for natural language generation tasks.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces and empirically validates **TBT (Ternary / Binary Transformer)**, a framework for quantizing both weights and activations of encoder-decoder transformer models to 2-bit ternary or 1-bit binary precision for natural language generation tasks. The approach combines two mechanisms: **statistics-based max-entropy isometric weight quantization** (which evenly distributes real-valued weights across quantization levels while preserving magnitude consistency to reduce gradient mismatch) and **learning-based elastic activation quantization** (which learns per-layer scaling factors during training and asymmetrically quantizes non-negative activation layers like Softmax/ReLU outputs to avoid wasting representational capacity). On CNN/DailyMail, the ternary BART base achieves an R1 score of 41 — merely 3.9 points behind the full-precision model while being 16× more efficient — and the binary model achieves a non-trivial R1 of 35.6, while on WMT16 En-Ro, ternary and binary mBART models achieve BLEU scores of 21.7 and 17.6 respectively, establishing for the first time that fully ternary and binary generative transformers can produce competitive, meaningful outputs — though only when both weight quantization entropy optimization and learnable asymmetric activation quantization are deployed jointly, since neither technique alone yields functional convergence.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Generative Transformers Are Too Computationally Expensive for Many Deployment Scenarios

The fundamental tension this paper confronts is straightforward: **generative pre-trained transformers have become the dominant paradigm in NLP and AI broadly, but their computational cost scales prohibitively with size**. Models like GPT-3 (Brown et al., 2020), BART (Lewis et al., 2020), and mBART (Liu et al., 2020a) derive their power from scaling to ever-larger parameter counts and training datasets, but this scaling creates a deployment bottleneck. The paper's opening framing in Section 1 is explicit on this point:

> "Unfortunately, such scaling comes at the cost of large computational requirements, putting extensively large generative transformers out of reach of all but the most resource-rich institutions. Even moderately sized pre-trained transformers have limited applications due to their size and computational cost."

This is not merely about training cost — which is a one-time expense — but about **inference cost at deployment time**, which recurs with every query. A 140M-parameter BART-base model, while modest by today's standards, still requires full-precision floating-point matrix multiplications for every token generated. For autoregressive generation, where each output token requires a full forward pass through the decoder, this cost scales linearly with sequence length. For real-world applications — on-device summarization, edge-based translation, real-time dialogue systems — the energy, latency, and hardware requirements of full-precision inference can be disqualifying.

The paper identifies a specific deployment asymmetry: storage compression alone (quantizing only weights) reduces model size on disk but does nothing for inference computation, since activations must still be computed in full precision. **Full acceleration requires quantizing both weights AND activations**, which enables replacing expensive floating-point multiply-accumulate operations with lightweight integer or bitwise operations that specialized hardware can execute without multiplication units. This is what makes the problem genuinely hard — quantizing one component is an easier sub-problem; quantizing both to extreme levels while maintaining output quality is the open challenge the paper addresses.

### Why Extreme Quantization (Ternary/Binary) Matters

The paper is not interested in mild quantization (e.g., 8-bit or 4-bit), which prior work had already achieved for generative models to varying degrees. It pursues **extreme quantization**: ternary (2-bit, three levels: {-1, 0, 1}) and binary (1-bit, two levels: {-1, 1}). The motivation is multiplicative gains, not incremental ones. Section 2.1.2 quantifies these:

> "By representing the weights and activations with {−1, 0, 1}, the network enjoys ∼16× memory saving compared to its 32-bit floating-point counterpart. When further binarize the weights and activations to only 1-bit (i.e., {−1, 1}), up to 32× model-size reduction and 58× speedup on CPUs have been achieved (Rastegari et al., 2016), where the matrix multiplication operations are replaced with light-weighted bitwise XNOR operations."

These are order-of-magnitude improvements, not marginal ones. The 58× speedup figure comes from replacing all floating-point multiplications in matrix operations with XNOR (binary equivalence) and popcount operations — operations that require no multiply-accumulate hardware and can be implemented extremely efficiently on CPUs, FPGAs, and custom ASICs. This is what makes the problem worth solving despite its difficulty: if a ternary or binary generative transformer can be made to work with acceptable accuracy, it opens deployment scenarios that are simply impossible with full-precision models — on-device summarization without cloud round-trips, real-time translation on consumer hardware, interactive dialogue agents running entirely locally.

The paper's ambition is stated clearly: they want to demonstrate that ternary and binary generative transformers are not just theoretically interesting but **practically viable** — producing outputs competitive enough to be useful in real applications.

### The Gap in Prior Work: Encoder-Only Success vs. Generative Failure

The paper identifies a sharp asymmetry in the quantization literature. Quantization of transformer models has been extensively studied, but almost exclusively for **encoder-only architectures** (primarily BERT) on classification tasks — sentence classification, token tagging, etc. Section 4 traces this trajectory:

> "Due to the popularity of BERT, numerous works have studied quantization for transformer models, starting with 8-bit quantization (Zafrir et al., 2019; Fan et al., 2020), and progressing to 4-bit (Shen et al., 2020; Zadeh et al., 2020), ternary (Zhang et al., 2020) and binary (Bai et al., 2021b; Qin et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2022). All of these works have focused on the encoder-only setting."

The encoder-only successes — including fully binary BERT models that work reasonably well — created a natural expectation that similar techniques would transfer to generative models. They did not.

**Why generative models are harder.** The paper identifies two compounding difficulties specific to text generation (Section 1):

- **The sensitivity of attention to quantization.** Transformer attention involves computing softmax over scaled dot products, which produces a distribution that is inherently continuous and sensitive to input precision. Quantizing the keys, queries, and values disrupts the attention weights, and the error propagates through multiple attention heads and layers.

- **Autoregressive decoding noise compounding.** Unlike encoder-only models that make a single set of classifications per input, generative decoders produce one token at a time, feeding each output token back as input for the next step. Quantization errors at each decoding step accumulate, and since the output vocabulary is large (typically 30K–50K tokens), small errors in the final projection layer can lead to incorrect token selection, which then feeds a corrupted context into the next step, compounding the error. This is a more severe manifestation of the classic exposure bias problem in autoregressive generation.

The paper describes the consequence bluntly in Section 2.1.2:

> "Despite its appealing characteristics, naively binarizing or ternarizing the transformer model for natural language generation results in several accuracy drops or even a total failure in training. It has been observed that the attention layers of the transformer network are difficult to quantize to low bits. Also, the auto-regressive decoding tends to accumulate errors due to quantization."

"Total failure in training" means the model simply does not converge — it produces degenerate outputs (empty sequences, repeated tokens, gibberish) regardless of how long it is trained. This is what distinguishes the generative setting from the encoder-only setting: the problem is not just reduced accuracy, but existential collapse.

### What Prior Approaches Exist for Generative Quantization, and Where They Fall Short

The paper identifies a small body of prior work that attempted to quantize generative transformers, but notes two limitations: the quantization levels were **mild** (4–8 bits, not ternary/binary), and the results were **mixed**. Section 4 summarizes:

> "In the generative setting, Prato et al. (2019); Behnke et al. (2021) demonstrate quantized models for machine translation, and Fan et al. (2020); Bai et al. (2021a) for language modeling, though only for moderate quantization levels (4-8 bits). Most recently, Tao et al. (2022) and Li et al. (2022) pushed weight quantization down to 2 bits (with 8-bit activation quantization) and evaluated on language modeling and summarization."

The two most directly comparable works — QuantBart (Tao et al., 2022) and DQ-BART (Li et al., 2022) — achieved ternary weights with 8-bit activations. Their reported results on XSUM and CNN/DailyMail (shown in Table 1 of this paper) represent the prior state of the art: ROUGE-L scores around 32–35 on XSUM and 40–42 on CNN/DailyMail for the 2-bit weight, 8-bit activation setting. Critically, **neither work** attempted ternary or binary activations — they kept activations at 8 bits, which still requires full-precision computation. No prior work had demonstrated a fully ternary (both weights and activations) or fully binary generative transformer on any text generation task.

The paper also adapts methods originally developed for BERT (BinaryBert, BiBert, TernaryBert) directly to BART and reports the results in Table 1 with asterisks. These provide the closest thing to "prior baselines" for fully ternary and binary settings, and the results are stark:

- **Fully ternary (2-2-2):** TernaryBert adapted to BART achieves R1/R2/RL scores of 14.03/2.23/11.79 on XSUM — effectively unusable. On CNN/DailyMail, the scores are 10.95/0.52/8.56.

- **Fully binary (1-1-1):** BinaryBert adapted to BART scores 8.13/0.12/7.69 on XSUM. BiBert scores 7.58/0.06/7.54. On CNN/DailyMail, BinaryBert achieves 9.80/0.15/8.62 and BiBert achieves 14.22/0.13/10.06. The R2 scores of 0.06–0.15 indicate that the model essentially produces no meaningful bigram overlap with reference summaries — it is generating noise, not summaries.

The baseline using naive ternarization (TWN, Li et al., 2016) and binarization (BWN, Courbariaux et al., 2016) applied directly to BART yields ROUGE-L scores of 11.4 and 1.78 on XSUM, respectively. The binary model's R2 of 0.01 confirms complete collapse.

These numbers are critical for understanding why the paper's contributions matter: prior to this work, **no method existed that could produce a functional fully ternary/binary generative transformer**. The best adapted methods produced near-zero-quality outputs. This is the gap the paper fills.

### What Specifically Breaks in Prior Quantization Methods When Applied to Generative Models

The paper's technical argument in Section 2.2 identifies **two specific failure modes** in classical quantization approaches (Eqs. 1–5) that become catastrophic at extreme bit-widths in the generative setting:

**Failure 1: Low information entropy in quantized weights.** Prior methods (TWN, BWN, TernaryBert, etc.) optimize quantization to minimize $\ell_2$ distance between quantized and real-valued weights:

$$\alpha^* = \arg\min_\alpha \Vert \alpha \hat{W}_Q - W_R \Vert_{\ell_2}$$

The paper points out this objective is **distribution-agnostic**: it chooses the scaling factor and thresholds to minimize per-element error without considering whether the resulting quantized weights are evenly distributed across quantization levels. If the real-valued weights happen to cluster near zero, standard ternarization might map 80% of them to zero and only 10% each to +α and −α, wasting representational capacity. At 2 or 3 quantization levels, this loss of information is severe — a ternary weight matrix where most values are zero encodes far less information than one where values are evenly split across the three levels. The paper connects this to information theory in Eq. 7: uniform distribution across quantization levels maximizes entropy, and entropy is directly tied to the information-carrying capacity of the quantized representation. Prior methods did not optimize for this.

**Failure 2: Gradient mismatch from non-isometric quantization.** The standard quantization functions (Eq. 1, Eq. 4) map real-valued weights to quantized weights through a non-isometric transformation — the magnitude of the quantized weight is not consistent with the magnitude of the real-valued weight. The paper explains in Section 2.2:

> "As suggested by the previous binarization work (Liu et al., 2020b), the magnitude difference between the quantized weight and the real-valued weight will greatly influence the gradient scale and a mismatch in magnitude will be amplified in back-propagation and cause gradient vanishing or explosion during training."

In the generative setting, where the network is deep (encoder-decoder with 12+ layers) and the loss landscape is more complex than in classification, gradient mismatch is not just a minor training inefficiency — it can prevent convergence entirely. The paper's visualization in Figure 2 confirms this: Panel (a) shows that weights trained with the baseline TWN method take on a very Gaussian-like shape that doesn't adapt to the discrete quantization levels, while Panel (e) shows TBT weights learning a more structured distribution that better utilizes the three ternary levels.

**Compounding effect.** These two failures interact destructively at extreme bit-widths. Low-entropy quantization means the model's weights encode less information per layer. Gradient mismatch means the training signal is corrupted, preventing the network from learning to compensate. In the generative decoder, where errors compound autoregressively, the result is the "total failure in training" that the paper observes — the model cannot learn meaningful representations, cannot produce coherent tokens, and either diverges or collapses to trivial outputs.

### How the Paper Positions Itself

The paper's positioning is very clear and can be extracted from its claims:

**It does not claim to invent ternarization or binarization.** The basic quantization formulas are from prior work (Li et al., 2016; Rastegari et al., 2016), and the paper credits these appropriately. The novelty lies in **why** those formulas fail for generative transformers and **what specific modifications** fix them.

**It unifies two complementary improvements.** The paper's central architectural claim is that a specific combination — statistics-based weight quantization that maximizes entropy and enforces isometry (Section 2.2), paired with learnable asymmetric activation quantization that learns per-layer scaling factors and uses different quantization schemes for non-negative vs. signed activations (Section 2.3) — is what makes extreme quantization viable for generation. The ablation study (Table 3) is crucial to this argument: neither technique alone produces a functional model (R2 scores below 1.5 for all ablated configurations), but their combination yields models with R2 scores of 11–18 and ROUGE-L scores of 25–38 across datasets.

**It sets the first baselines, not the ceiling.** The paper repeatedly acknowledges that its binary model "while less accurate" and that there is a "wider gap" for fully binary settings. It frames its work as demonstrating that these extreme quantization levels are *possible at all* for generative transformers — the first demonstration that a fully binary transformer can produce coherent summaries and translations. The 3.9-point gap between the ternary model and full-precision BART on CNN/DailyMail (R1: 41 vs. 44.9) is presented as an achievement, not a shortcoming, because the prior state of the art for fully ternary was essentially zero functional output.

**It bridges the gap between encoder-only quantization success and generative deployment needs.** The paper explicitly sees itself as extending the quantization frontier from classification to generation, from encoder-only to encoder-decoder, and from moderate bit-widths (4–8 bits) to extreme bit-widths (1–2 bits). The choice of tasks — summarization and machine translation — is deliberate: these are standard, well-benchmarked generative tasks where full-precision baselines are well-established, making it easy to quantify exactly how much accuracy is lost at each quantization level and whether the tradeoff is practically acceptable.

**It targets the hardware efficiency cliff.** The paper is not merely an academic demonstration of training stability. It identifies the specific hardware scenario — specialized processors that can execute ternary/binary operations without multiplication units — as the target deployment environment. The efficiency claims (16× for ternary, up to 32× compression and 58× speedup for binary) are tied to concrete hardware implications, positioning the work as an enabler for deployment scenarios (on-device, edge, low-power) that are currently inaccessible to full-precision generative transformers.

In summary, the paper addresses a clear, well-motivated gap: **no method existed to produce functional ternary or binary generative transformers** — all prior approaches either used mild quantization (≥4 bits), quantized only weights (not activations), or failed entirely when applied to generation tasks. The paper proposes a specific diagnosis of why prior methods fail (low entropy in quantized weights and gradient mismatch from non-isometric quantization, compounded by autoregressive error accumulation) and a specific fix (statistics-based max-entropy isometric weight quantization + learning-based asymmetric elastic activation quantization, deployed jointly). The result is the first demonstration that fully ternary and binary text generation models can be trained to produce practically useful outputs, opening the door to orders-of-magnitude efficiency improvements for on-device and edge deployment of generative transformers.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

TBT is a training framework that converts a full-precision pre-trained encoder-decoder transformer (like BART) into one where every weight matrix and every activation tensor is represented using only three values (`-1, 0, 1` for ternary) or two values (`-1, 1` for binary), while still producing coherent summaries and translations. The system solves the problem that naive low-bit quantization causes generative transformers to collapse entirely during training — producing zero-quality outputs — by fixing two root causes simultaneously: (1) it re-engineers how real-valued weights get mapped to discrete levels so that the quantized representation carries maximal information and propagates correct gradient signals, and (2) it makes activation quantization adaptive and distribution-aware, learning per-layer scaling factors and using different quantization grids for non-negative activations (ReLU/Softmax outputs) versus signed activations (everything else), so that no representational capacity is wasted.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The TBT framework has three major component classes, applied block-by-block through a standard transformer encoder-decoder:

1. **Stats-based weight quantizers** — For every weight matrix in the model (attention projections Q, K, V, O; feed-forward network layers; embedding tables), these compute quantization parameters (`$\alpha$`, `$\mu$`) purely from the statistics of the real-valued weight tensor at each forward pass, then map weights to discrete ternary or binary values. They use no learned parameters for weight quantization.

2. **Learning-based activation quantizers** — For every activation tensor flowing between layers (attention outputs, FFN intermediate/output, Softmax outputs, ReLU outputs), these learn a scaling factor `$\alpha$` via gradient descent and apply asymmetric quantization grids depending on whether the activation tensor is non-negative or contains both signs.

3. **Transformer blocks** — The architecture is standard BART/mBART: multi-head self-attention (Q, K, V projections, attention computation, output projection) followed by a two-layer feed-forward network with ReLU activation, repeated for encoder and decoder stacks. The embedding layer and final output projection are also quantized.

The information flow during training is: tokenized input → quantized embedding lookup → for each encoder layer: quantize the layer's input activations → compute attention using quantized Q/K/V weights and quantized activations → quantize attention outputs → feed through quantized FFN weights with quantized activations → quantize encoder output → for each decoder layer: same pattern but with cross-attention → quantized final projection to vocabulary → compute cross-entropy loss against targets → backpropagate through Straight-Through Estimators at every quantization point, updating the learnable activation scaling factors and the full-precision latent weights.

At inference, the quantized weights are frozen (no real-valued weights are stored), and activations are quantized on-the-fly with the learned scaling factors.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the standard ternarization and binarization formulas from prior work (TWN, BWN), including their `$\ell_2$`-minimization rationale, scaling factor computation, and the Straight-Through Estimator — because TBT's innovations are defined as explicit modifications to these baselines.
- **Second**, statistics-based max-entropy isometric weight quantization (the first core innovation) — why the `$\ell_2$` objective ignores entropy and gradient mismatch, how the proposed method fixes both by recentering weights and computing the scaling factor from L1 statistics, and why this specific form yields uniform ternary distribution and isometric gradient flow.
- **Third**, learning-based elastic activation quantization (the second core innovation) — why activations need a fundamentally different treatment than weights (distribution varies per-input, non-negative subsets exist) and how TBT uses learnable per-layer scaling factors with asymmetric quantization grids and the corresponding Straight-Through Estimator gradients.
- **Fourth**, the training recipe — initialization from a full-precision pre-trained model, knowledge distillation, optimizer settings for different activation bit-widths, and the overall procedure that ties the components together.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily an **empirical systems paper** whose core idea is that binary/ternary generative transformers fail because prior quantization methods produce (a) weight representations with low information entropy and destructive gradient mismatch, and (b) activation representations that waste quantization levels on the wrong value ranges. TBT fixes both simultaneously and demonstrates that the joint fix enables — for the first time — successful training of fully ternary and fully binary text generation models.

#### Standard Ternarization (TWN) — The Starting Point

The baseline ternary weight network (TWN) formulation from Li et al. (2016) is the foundation that TBT modifies. Understanding it is essential because TBT's weight quantization is a direct algebraic improvement on this scheme.

The ternarization function maps each real-valued element `$X_i^R$` in a tensor to one of three values:

$$X_i^T = \begin{cases} -\alpha_T, & \text{if } X_i^R < -\Delta \\ 0, & \text{if } -\Delta \leq X_i^R \leq \Delta \\ +\alpha_T, & \text{if } X_i^R > \Delta \end{cases}$$

where `$X_i^R$` is the `$i$`-th element of the real-valued weight or activation tensor, `$X_i^T$` is its ternary counterpart, `$\Delta$` is the threshold that determines which values fall in the zero bin, and `$\alpha_T$` is the shared magnitude for both non-zero levels.

The threshold `$\Delta$` is computed as:

$$\Delta = 0.7 \cdot \frac{\|X^R\|_{\ell_1}}{n_{X^R}}$$

where `$\|X^R\|_{\ell_1} = \sum_i |X_i^R|$` is the sum of absolute values of all elements, and `$n_{X^R}$` is the total number of elements in the tensor. The factor 0.7 is an empirically chosen constant — not derived, but calibrated to balance the proportion of elements in the zero bin versus the non-zero bins across typical weight distributions.

The scaling factor `$\alpha_T$` is then computed to minimize the `$\ell_2$` reconstruction error between the ternary weights and the original real-valued weights:

$$\alpha_T = \frac{\sum_i X_i^R \cdot \mathbf{1}_{|X_i^R| > \Delta}}{\sum_i \mathbf{1}_{|X_i^R| > \Delta}}$$

Here `$\mathbf{1}_{|X_i^R| > \Delta}$` is an indicator that equals 1 when the element's absolute value exceeds the threshold, and 0 otherwise. So the numerator sums all real-valued weights that get assigned to non-zero levels, and the denominator counts how many such weights there are. The result is the **mean absolute value** of the weights that exceed the threshold — the `$\ell_2$`-optimal scaling factor for the `$\pm 1$` ternary levels.

**What it computes:** For each element, check whether its absolute value is above or below the threshold `$\Delta$`. If below, quantize to 0. If above, quantize to `$+\alpha_T$` if positive or `$-\alpha_T$` if negative. The threshold depends on the average absolute magnitude of the tensor (so tensors with larger weights get larger thresholds). The scaling factor sets the magnitude of non-zero ternary values to the average absolute magnitude of the weights that survive thresholding.

**Why this form — and why it fails:** The design objective is minimizing `$\ell_2$` reconstruction error: `$\alpha_T$` is the closed-form optimal scalar for the already-assigned ternary levels under squared error. This works adequately at moderate bit-widths (4–8 bits) where the quantization grid has enough resolution to absorb poorly distributed assignments. But at 2 bits (ternary), the `$\ell_2$`-minimizing `$\alpha_T$` can produce highly imbalanced assignments — if the real-valued weight distribution is sharply peaked near zero, the formula might map 90% of weights to zero and only 10% to non-zero, yielding near-zero information content in the quantized tensor. Furthermore, the non-isometric ratio `$X_i^R / \alpha_T$` can vary widely across elements, so the gradient scaling in backpropagation is inconsistent, causing vanishing or exploding gradients across layers. The paper's key insight is that `$\ell_2$` minimization is the **wrong objective** at these extreme bit-widths — what matters is not per-element reconstruction fidelity but information capacity and gradient consistency.

#### Standard Binarization (BWN) — The Starting Point

The binary weight network (BWN) from Rastegari et al. (2016) maps real-valued weights to exactly two values:

$$X_i^B = \alpha_B \cdot \text{Sign}(X_i^R) = \begin{cases} -\alpha_B, & \text{if } X_i^R < 0 \\ +\alpha_B, & \text{if } X_i^R \geq 0 \end{cases}$$

where `$\text{Sign}$` returns +1 for non-negative inputs and -1 for negative inputs, and `$\alpha_B$` is the scaling factor.

The scaling factor is:

$$\alpha_B = \frac{\|X^R\|_{\ell_1}}{n_{X^R}}$$

which is simply the mean absolute value of all elements in the tensor.

**What it computes:** Take the sign of every real-valued weight — this assigns each weight to either -1 or +1 with no zero level — then multiply by the mean absolute value of the original tensor to restore the overall magnitude.

**Why this form — and why it fails for generation:** This is elegantly simple and works reasonably for image classification CNNs, but inherits the same problems as TWN for generative transformers. The sign function is maximally non-isometric: elements just above zero and elements far from zero are mapped to the same `$\pm \alpha_B$` with no magnitude preservation. For weights that are not zero-centered, the distribution becomes heavily imbalanced — most weights land in one bin. And because the sign function's true gradient is zero almost everywhere (it is flat except at the discontinuity), training relies entirely on the Straight-Through Estimator, which is a crude approximation that becomes increasingly unreliable as bit-width decreases and as the network depth (and thus gradient chain length) increases. In a 12-layer encoder-decoder transformer with autoregressive decoding, these accumulated gradient errors prevent convergence.

#### The Straight-Through Estimator (STE) — The Gradient Backbone

Both baseline methods and TBT rely on the Straight-Through Estimator (STE) for backpropagation through the non-differentiable quantization functions (floor, round, sign, clip). Since this is the gradient mechanism for ALL quantized training, it warrants explicit treatment.

A quantization function `$Q(x)$` maps a continuous input to a discrete output — e.g., `$\text{Sign}(x)$` or `$\lfloor \text{Clip}(x, -1, 1) \rceil$`. The true derivative is zero almost everywhere (the output is piecewise constant) and undefined at the discontinuities. The STE replaces this true derivative with a **surrogate** that treats the quantization function as if it were the identity function within a specified range:

$$\frac{\partial Q(x)}{\partial x} \overset{\text{STE}}{\approx} \mathbf{1}_{|x| < \text{boundary}}$$

**What it computes:** In the forward pass, `$Q(x)$` produces the actual discrete value. In the backward pass, the gradient flows through as if `$Q(x)$` were simply `$x$` (no quantization), but only when `$x$` falls within a "trust region" where the quantization is well-behaved. Outside that region, the gradient is zeroed out, preventing extreme input values from contributing destabilizing gradient signals.

**Why this form:** The STE embodies the assumption that, on average, the gradient of the quantized output with respect to the input approximates 1 for values in the operating range of the network. It is a heuristic, not a mathematically rigorous estimator, but it works in practice because (a) the discrete levels constrain the forward pass to low precision while the backward pass operates in continuous space, allowing gradient-based optimization to proceed, and (b) the clipping boundaries prevent exploding gradients from extreme quantization errors.

The specific STE form varies by quantization scheme. TBT defines its STE explicitly — for binarized weights (Eq. 10), the gradient is approximated as 1 when `$|(W_i^R - \mu_B) / \alpha_B| < 1$` and 0 otherwise. For ternary activations (Eq. 12), the gradient with respect to the scaling factor `$\alpha_T$` is a more complex expression that accounts for the learned scaling. These formulae are detailed in their respective sections below.

#### Statistics-Based Max-Entropy Isometric Weight Quantization

This is TBT's first core innovation — a redesign of the weight quantization function that addresses both the entropy problem and the gradient mismatch problem through a single algebraic reformulation.

##### Ternary Case

TBT's ternary weight quantization is defined as:

$$W_i^T = \alpha_T \left\lfloor \text{Clip}\left(\frac{W_i^R - \mu_T}{\alpha_T}, -1, 1\right) \right\rceil$$

where:

$$\mu_T = \overline{W^R}$$

$$\alpha_T = \frac{4}{3} \cdot \frac{\|W^R - \mu_T\|_{\ell_1}}{n_{W^R}}$$

Here `$W^R$` is the real-valued weight matrix (treated per-row for weight tensors — each row gets its own `$\alpha_T$` computed from that row's statistics), `$W_i^R$` is an individual element, `$\mu_T$` is the element-wise mean of the weights, `$\|W^R - \mu_T\|_{\ell_1}$` is the sum of absolute deviations from the mean, `$n_{W^R}$` is the number of elements, and `$\alpha_T$` is the per-row scaling factor. The function `$\text{Clip}(x, -1, 1)$` bounds `$x$` to the interval `$[-1, 1]$`, and `$\lfloor \cdot \rceil$` rounds to the nearest integer — in this case, the only possible integers after clipping to `$[-1, 1]$` are `$-1$`, `$0$`, and `$1$`.

**Operationally, what happens step by step:**

1. **Subtract the mean:** `$W_i^R - \mu_T$` zero-centers the weights. This ensures that the weight distribution is symmetric around zero before quantization begins, so that the positive and negative ternary bins get roughly equal numbers of elements.

2. **Divide by** `$\alpha_T$`: This scales the zero-centered weights so that their distribution spans the interval `$[-1.5, 1.5]$` on average — a crucial property derived below.

3. **Clip to** `$[-1, 1]$`: Any value that, after scaling, falls outside `$[-1, 1]$` is forced to the boundary. The unscaled values `$W_i^R - \mu_T$` that exceed `$\alpha_T$` in magnitude all get clamped to `$\pm 1$`.

4. **Round to nearest integer:** The clipped values in `$[-1, 1]$` are all between -1 and 1 inclusive, so rounding produces exactly `$\{-1, 0, 1\}$`.

5. **Multiply by** `$\alpha_T$`: This restores the magnitude scale, producing final ternary weights in `$\{-\alpha_T, 0, \alpha_T\}$`.

**Why the 4/3 factor in** `$\alpha_T$` **produces uniform ternary distribution:** The design target is that real-valued weights, when divided by `$\alpha_T$`, should be distributed in `$[-1.5, 1.5]$`. To see why, consider the threshold boundaries: values in `$[-0.5, 0.5]$` round to 0; values in `$[0.5, 1.5]$` round to 1; values in `$[-1.5, -0.5]$` round to -1; values beyond `$\pm 1.5$` get clipped to `$\pm 1.5$` then round to `$\pm 1$`. If the scaled weights are uniformly distributed in `$[-1.5, 1.5]$`, each of the three quantization bins spans an interval of width 1.0 — the zero bin `$[-0.5, 0.5]$` (width 1), the positive bin `$[0.5, 1.5]$` (width 1), the negative bin `$[-1.5, -0.5]$` (width 1) — giving each bin equal representation and thus maximum entropy (Eq. 7). The factor `$4/3$` in the `$\alpha_T$` formula achieves exactly this: for weights initialized symmetrically and uniformly (as per He or Glorot initialization), the L1 norm of the zero-centered weights relates to the spread of the distribution such that `$\alpha_T$` calibrated with `$4/3$` factor produces the `$[-1.5, 1.5]$` scaling.

The paper states this explicitly in Section 2.2:

> "When the real-valued weights are initialized as uniformly and symmetrically distributed (He et al., 2015; Glorot and Bengio, 2010), the scaling factor `$\alpha_T$` will distribute `$W_i^R / \alpha_T$` to `$[-1.5, 1.5]$`, such that the output ternary weights will have near uniform distribution in three ternary levels."

**Why isometry matters for gradients:** The function is designed so that the real-valued weight `$W_i^R$` and the ternary weight `$W_i^T$` have roughly the same magnitude — the division by `$\alpha_T$` and subsequent multiplication by `$\alpha_T$` preserves scale. The paper calls this "isometric" because the mapping approximately preserves the norm. This means the gradient of the loss with respect to `$W_i^R$` is computed through a function whose Jacobian has elements near 1 or 0 (due to STE), rather than arbitrary scaling factors that would distort the gradient magnitude. Concretely, the STE for this function (adapting from the binary case in Eq. 10) passes gradients through when the scaled, centered weight is in `$[-1, 1]$`:

$$\frac{\partial W_i^T}{\partial W_i^R} \overset{\text{STE}}{\approx} \mathbf{1}_{|(W_i^R - \mu_T)/\alpha_T| < 1}$$

For weights within the clipping range, the gradient flows with scale approximately 1; for weights outside, the gradient is blocked. This prevents extreme weights from generating large gradient updates that destabilize training.

##### Binary Case

The binary formulation follows the same design principles:

$$W_i^B = \alpha_B \cdot \text{Sign}\left(\frac{W_i^R - \mu_B}{\alpha_B}\right)$$

where:

$$\mu_B = \overline{W^R}$$

$$\alpha_B = \frac{\|W^R - \mu_B\|_{\ell_1}}{n_{W^R}}$$

**Operationally:**

1. Subtract the mean `$\mu_B$` to zero-center.
2. Divide by `$\alpha_B$` to normalize the scale.
3. Take the Sign — this maps positive values to +1 and negative values to -1. There is no zero level.
4. Multiply by `$\alpha_B$` to restore the magnitude.

The key algebraic simplification: `$\alpha_B \cdot \text{Sign}((W_i^R - \mu_B)/\alpha_B) = \alpha_B \cdot \text{Sign}(W_i^R - \mu_B)$` because `$\alpha_B > 0$` (it's a positive scaling factor), and `$\text{Sign}(x/c) = \text{Sign}(x)$` for any positive `$c$`. So the internal division by `$\alpha_B$` is mathematically redundant for the forward pass — but the paper explicitly includes it in the formula to keep the function **isometric** in its algebraic form, making the gradient derivation clean. With this form, the STE gradient is:

$$\frac{\partial W_i^B}{\partial W_i^R} \overset{\text{STE}}{\approx} \mathbf{1}_{|(W_i^R - \mu_B)/\alpha_B| < 1}$$

This says: gradient flows through when the centered, scaled weight magnitude is less than 1, and is blocked otherwise. Since `$\text{Sign}$` maps everything — including extreme values — to `$\pm 1$`, there is no natural clipping, so the STE boundary of `$| \cdot | < 1$` serves as the trust region.

**Why subtract the mean before binarizing:** Standard BWN (Eq. 4) applies the sign function directly to `$W_i^R$`, which maps zero-centered weights to roughly equal `$\pm 1$` only if the weight distribution is already zero-centered. In practice, many weight matrices develop non-zero means during training, causing BWN to assign a majority of weights to one binary level — catastrophic entropy loss. Subtracting `$\mu_B$` forces zero-centering before binarization, distributing weights evenly across both levels regardless of the mean.

#### Learning-Based Elastic Activation Quantization

This is TBT's second core innovation. Weight quantization uses statistics-based fixed formulas computed from the current weight values — this works because weights are relatively stable and their distribution is a direct consequence of the optimizer state. Activations are fundamentally different: each input sequence produces a different activation distribution, and as weights update during training, the activation distributions shift (internal covariate shift). Using a fixed formula for activation quantization would be suboptimal because no single threshold or scaling factor works across all inputs and training stages.

TBT introduces **learnable scaling factors** `$\alpha$` for activation quantization — one per quantized activation tensor — that are updated via gradient descent alongside the network weights. The activation quantizer itself uses different quantization grids for two categories of activations, and the scaling factor adapts end-to-end to the network's training loss.

##### Activation Categorization

The paper partitions all activation tensors into two sets:

**Category 1 — Non-negative activations (`$X^R \in \mathbb{R}^+$`):** These are the outputs of ReLU layers (which clip negative values to zero) and Softmax layers (which produce probability distributions in `$[0, 1]$`). These tensors contain only zero or positive values.

**Category 2 — Signed activations (`$X^R \in \mathbb{R}$`):** Everything else — attention outputs, FFN intermediate activations before ReLU, encoder/decoder hidden states, embedding lookup outputs. These contain both positive and negative values.

The motivation: if you symmetrically quantize a non-negative tensor to `$\{-\alpha, 0, \alpha\}$`, the `$-\alpha$` level is wasted — it will never be used because all values are `$\geq 0$`. This wastes 1/3 of the representational capacity in ternary mode and 1/2 in binary mode. TBT avoids this by using **asymmetric quantization** for Category 1.

##### Ternary Activation Quantization

The full formulation (Eq. 11):

$$X_i^T = \alpha_T \hat{X}_i^T = \begin{cases} \alpha_T \left\lfloor \text{Clip}\left(\frac{X_i^R}{\alpha_T}, 0, 2\right) \right\rceil, & \text{if } X^R \in \mathbb{R}^+ \\ \alpha_T \left\lfloor \text{Clip}\left(\frac{X_i^{\prime R}}{\alpha_T}, -1, 1\right) \right\rceil, & \text{if } X^R \in \mathbb{R} \end{cases}$$

where `$X^{\prime R} = X^R - \overline{X^R}$` denotes the zero-mean real-valued activations.

**For Category 1 (non-negative):** The real-valued activation is divided by the learnable scaling factor `$\alpha_T$`, then clipped to `$[0, 2]$`, then rounded to the nearest integer. Clipping to `$[0, 2]$` means the possible rounded values are `$\{0, 1, 2\}$`. After multiplying back by `$\alpha_T$`, the ternary activation values are `$\{0, \alpha_T, 2\alpha_T\}$`. This maps to the natural range of non-negative values: zero maps to zero, moderate values map to `$\alpha_T$`, large values map to `$2\alpha_T$`.

**For Category 2 (signed):** The real-valued activation is first zero-centered (subtract its mean), then scaled by `$\alpha_T$`, clipped to `$[-1, 1]$`, rounded to `$\{-1, 0, 1\}$`, and scaled back to `$\{-\alpha_T, 0, \alpha_T\}$`. This is structurally similar to the weight ternarization but with a **learned** `$\alpha_T$` rather than a statistics-based one, and applied activation-by-activation rather than row-by-row.

**Gradient with respect to** `$\alpha_T$` **(Eq. 12):**

$$\frac{\partial X_i^T}{\partial \alpha_T} \overset{\text{STE}}{\approx} \begin{cases} \hat{X}_i^T - \frac{X_i^R}{\alpha_T} \cdot \mathbf{1}_{0 \leq X_i^R \leq 2\alpha_T}, & \text{if } X^R \in \mathbb{R}^+ \\ \hat{X}_i^T - \frac{X_i^{\prime R}}{\alpha_T} \cdot \mathbf{1}_{|X_i^{\prime R}| \leq \alpha_T}, & \text{if } X^R \in \mathbb{R} \end{cases}$$

where `$\hat{X}_i^T$` is the quantized value **before scaling by** `$\alpha_T$` (i.e., `$\hat{X}_i^T \in \{0, 1, 2\}$` or `$\{-1, 0, 1\}$`).

**What this gradient computes:** It measures how much the quantized output changes per unit change in the scaling factor. The expression `$\hat{X}_i^T - X_i^R / \alpha_T$` represents the difference between the discretized normalized value and the continuous normalized value — essentially the quantization error in normalized space. The indicator `$\mathbf{1}$` restricts this gradient to only flow when the activation is within the valid clipping range (where the quantization is active and well-defined).

**Why learn** `$\alpha_T$` **rather than compute it from statistics:** The optimal scaling factor should trade off between keeping values within the clipping range (to avoid saturation) and spreading values across quantization bins (to maximize entropy). This trade-off depends on the downstream loss, not just on `$\ell_2$` reconstruction of the activation tensor. By learning `$\alpha_T$` with gradient descent, the quantizer adapts to the needs of the overall network — it can sacrifice activation reconstruction fidelity in one layer if doing so improves the final output, or it can adjust its range as the activation distribution shifts during training.

##### Binary Activation Quantization

The binary formulation (Eq. 13):

$$X_i^B = \alpha_B \hat{X}_i^B = \begin{cases} \alpha_B \left\lfloor \text{Clip}\left(\frac{X_i^R}{\alpha_B}, 0, 1\right) \right\rceil, & \text{if } X^R \in \mathbb{R}^+ \\ \alpha_B \cdot \text{Sign}\left(\frac{X_i^{\prime R}}{\alpha_B}\right), & \text{if } X^R \in \mathbb{R} \end{cases}$$

**For Category 1 (non-negative):** Scale by `$\alpha_B$`, clip to `$[0, 1]$`, round to `$\{0, 1\}$`, scale back to `$\{0, \alpha_B\}$`. Values above `$\alpha_B$` saturate to 1 before rounding; values below 0 clip to 0.

**For Category 2 (signed):** Zero-center, scale, take Sign (producing `$\pm 1$`), scale back to `$\{-\alpha_B, \alpha_B\}$`. Note the explicit division by `$\alpha_B$` inside the Sign is again for isometric formulation, analogous to the weight binarization case.

**Gradient with respect to** `$\alpha_B$` **(Eq. 14):**

$$\frac{\partial X_i^B}{\partial \alpha_B} \overset{\text{STE}}{\approx} \begin{cases} \hat{X}_i^B - \frac{X_i^R}{\alpha_B} \cdot \mathbf{1}_{0 \leq X_i^R \leq \alpha_B}, & \text{if } X^R \in \mathbb{R}^+ \\ \text{Sign}(X_i^{\prime R}), & \text{if } X^R \in \mathbb{R} \end{cases}$$

For signed activations, the gradient simplifies to `$\text{Sign}(X_i^{\prime R})$` because the binary quantization has no rounding/clipping in the signed case — it's just the sign function — so the derivative with respect to `$\alpha_B$` of `$\alpha_B \cdot \text{Sign}(X_i^{\prime R})$` is simply `$\text{Sign}(X_i^{\prime R})$`.

#### The Combined Quantized Transformer Block (Figure 1)

Figure 1 in the paper provides the architectural schematic. Walking through one transformer block (encoder or decoder) under TBT:

**Input:** A real-valued activation tensor from either the embedding layer or the previous block.

**Step 1 — Quantize the input activation:** Pass through a learning-based symmetric activation quantizer (Category 2, since hidden states contain both signs).

**Step 2 — Self-attention:**
- The input is projected to queries (Q), keys (K), and values (V) using stats-based weight-quantized matrices. Each of Q, K, V has its own `$\alpha_T$` or `$\alpha_B$` computed per-row from the weight statistics at each forward pass.
- The Q, K, V activations are each quantized by learning-based symmetric quantizers.
- Attention scores = `$\text{Softmax}(QK^T / \sqrt{d_k})$`. The Softmax output is non-negative, so it passes through a **learning-based asymmetric activation quantizer** (Category 1) that maps to `$\{0, \alpha, 2\alpha\}$` or `$\{0, \alpha\}$`.
- The attention-weighted values are computed using quantized attention weights and quantized V.
- Output projection: the concatenated multi-head outputs go through a stats-based weight-quantized output matrix, producing the attention output.

**Step 3 — Quantize attention output:** Pass through a learning-based symmetric activation quantizer.

**Step 4 — Feed-forward network:**
- First linear layer: stats-based weight-quantized matrix → produces FFN intermediate activations (signed).
- Quantize intermediate activations (learning-based symmetric).
- ReLU activation: produces non-negative activations.
- Quantize ReLU output (learning-based **asymmetric**, Category 1 — maps to `$\{0, \alpha, 2\alpha\}$` or `$\{0, \alpha\}$`).
- Second linear layer: stats-based weight-quantized matrix → produces FFN output.

**Step 5 — Quantize FFN output:** Learning-based symmetric activation quantizer.

**Step 6 — Residual connection and layer norm:** Standard full-precision operations (the paper does not explicitly quantize these, following prior work that keeps normalization layers in higher precision due to their sensitivity).

The embedding layer weights are also quantized (stats-based). The final output projection to vocabulary is quantized (stats-based weight quantization).

**Key design choice — activation quantizers are separate per occurrence:** Each activation quantizer (the one after Q projection, after K projection, after V projection, after Softmax, after attention output, after FFN layer 1, after ReLU, after FFN layer 2) has its **own learnable** `$\alpha$` parameter. They are not shared across layers or positions. This allows each quantizer to adapt to the distinct activation distribution at its specific location in the network.

#### Training Recipe and Hyperparameters

TBT follows a **knowledge distillation + quantization-aware fine-tuning** paradigm. The procedure is:

1. **Initialization:** Start from a full-precision pre-trained BART-base (140M parameters, for summarization) or mBART-large (680M parameters, for translation). The real-valued weights are the pre-trained weights. The learnable activation scaling factors `$\alpha$` are initialized — the paper does not specify the initialization scheme, but a typical choice would be to estimate a reasonable range from the first batch's activation statistics and set `$\alpha$` to match that range.

2. **Knowledge distillation:** During training, the quantized model is trained with a distillation loss that encourages its output distribution to match the full-precision teacher model's output distribution, in addition to the standard cross-entropy loss against ground-truth targets. The paper states in Section 3.1: "We follow recent work (Li et al., 2022) in training the quantized network with initialization and knowledge distillation from a full-precision pre-trained model." The distillation temperature and loss weight are not explicitly specified in the paper.

3. **Training hyperparameters (Section 3.1):**
   - **Epochs:** 20 epochs on all tasks.
   - **Hardware:** 8 GPUs.
   - **Batch size:** 128.
   - **Learning rate:** `$2.5 \times 10^{-4}$` for 8-bit activation models; `$5 \times 10^{-4}$` for binary and ternary activation models. The higher learning rate for lower-bit activations is a notable design choice — it suggests that with more aggressive quantization, the optimization landscape becomes flatter and requires larger steps to make meaningful progress, or that the STE gradients are noisier and require larger steps to overcome the noise.

4. **Weight quantization schedule:** The stats-based weight quantizers compute `$\mu$` and `$\alpha$` from the current weight values at each forward pass, so the quantization adapts as the latent weights update. This means `$\alpha_T$` and `$\alpha_B$` evolve during training alongside the weights.

5. **Activation quantization schedule:** The learnable `$\alpha$` for each activation quantizer is updated via gradient descent using the STE gradients (Eqs. 12, 14). These also evolve throughout training, adapting to the shifting activation distributions.

6. **Inference:** After training, the quantized weights are frozen. The learned activation scaling factors are frozen. At inference time, all operations use the quantized weights and quantized activations with no full-precision fallback — this is a truly quantized model capable of running on hardware that supports only ternary/binary arithmetic.

**Why this training approach over alternatives:** Post-training quantization (PTQ), where a pre-trained model is quantized without further training, is attractive because it avoids the cost of retraining. But at extreme bit-widths, PTQ consistently fails because the quantization error is too large to absorb without adapting the weights. Quantization-aware training (QAT) from scratch is prohibitively expensive for large generative models and often unstable because the quantized model lacks the representational capacity to learn from random initialization. The pre-trained initialization + distillation + QAT fine-tuning strikes a balance: the pre-trained weights provide a good starting point where the network already knows the task, knowledge distillation provides a rich training signal that is less sensitive to quantization noise than hard labels (since it provides full distributional information), and the limited fine-tuning (20 epochs) adapts the weights and scaling factors to the quantization constraints without requiring full re-training.

---

#### Summary of Design Choices and Their Justifications

**Stats-based weight quantization (not learned):** Weights are relatively stable and their distribution is a direct consequence of initialization and optimizer dynamics. A statistics-based formula can be computed on-the-fly cheaply and deterministically, and the `$4/3$` factor in `$\alpha_T$` is derived from the property of uniform initialization, not learned. Making weight scaling learnable would add parameters that need gradient estimates through yet another STE, increasing training instability with no clear benefit.

**Learning-based activation quantization (not fixed):** Activations vary per input and shift during training. A fixed formula cannot adapt to these dynamics. Learning `$\alpha$` with gradient descent allows the quantizer to optimize for the downstream loss, not just activation reconstruction, and to co-adapt with the evolving weight quantizers.

**Asymmetric quantization for non-negative activations:** The ternary grid `$\{0, \alpha, 2\alpha\}$` uses all three levels for `$[0, \infty)$` rather than wasting the negative level. Similarly, the binary grid `$\{0, \alpha\}$` uses both levels. This is a zero-cost improvement in representational capacity — it changes the quantization grid but not the number of levels, so hardware complexity is unchanged.

**Per-row weight quantization:** Each row of a weight matrix has its own `$\alpha_T$` or `$\alpha_B$`. This is standard practice in quantization (also used in TWN, BWN) because different rows (corresponding to different output neurons) can have very different weight magnitude distributions. Row-wise quantization provides finer granularity without the overhead of per-element scaling.

**STE with hard clipping boundaries:** The gradient is passed through only when the pre-quantization value is within the clipping range. Outside that range, the gradient is zeroed. This is a conservative STE: rather than letting arbitrary values contribute gradients (which could be completely wrong since those values would be hard-clipped in the forward pass anyway), it restricts gradient flow to the "trust region" where the quantization function is approximately linear.

**4/3 factor in ternary** `$\alpha_T$` **derived from uniform initialization:** This is the paper's most mathematically elegant design choice. For a uniform distribution on `$[-a, a]$`, the L1 norm is `$n \cdot a/2$`. The mean absolute deviation `$\|W^R - \overline{W^R}\|_{\ell_1} / n$` equals `$a/2$`. Multiplying by `$4/3$` gives `$2a/3$`. The weight range `$[-a, a]$` divided by `$2a/3$` gives `$[-1.5, 1.5]$`. This is why the factor is `$4/3$` — it converts the L1 statistic of a uniform distribution into the required scaling to map to `$[-1.5, 1.5]$`. For non-uniform distributions (as weights evolve during training), this calibration is approximate but remains close enough to maintain near-uniform ternary distributions, as the paper's entropy measurements across 96 layers confirm.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Diagnosing Why Extreme Quantization Fails as a Twin Problem of Information Entropy and Gradient Geometry — Not Just Reconstruction Error

The paper's deepest conceptual move is not the quantization formulas themselves, but the **diagnosis** that the standard `$\ell_2$`-minimization objective underpinning prior ternarization and binarization methods (TWN, BWN, TernaryBert, BinaryBert, BiBert) is the *wrong optimization target* for extreme quantization in generative models. This is a reframing, not an incremental improvement.

**What the field assumed before this work.** Prior quantization methods — across CNNs, encoder-only transformers, and the few attempts at generative models — uniformly treated quantization as a **per-tensor reconstruction problem**: find the scaling factor `$\alpha$` that minimizes `$\ell_2$` distance between the real-valued tensor and its quantized counterpart. This is the explicit objective in TWN (Li et al., 2016, Eq. 3), BWN (Rastegari et al., 2016, Eq. 5), and all their descendants. The assumption is intuitive — if the quantized values are close to the real values element-by-element, the network should behave similarly. At 4–8 bits, this assumption is approximately correct because the quantization grid is dense enough that `$\ell_2$`-optimal scaling produces adequate information preservation.

**What TBT reveals is wrong with that assumption at 1–2 bits.** The paper argues, explicitly in Section 2.2, that `$\ell_2$` minimization ignores two properties that become *existential* — not merely helpful — at extreme bit-widths:

1. **Information entropy of the quantized representation.** Eq. 7 formalizes this: maximum information is preserved when the discrete quantization levels are used uniformly. If `$\ell_2$`-optimal scaling maps 90% of weights to zero (because the real-valued distribution is sharply peaked), the ternary tensor carries essentially no information — it's a sparse, nearly-constant matrix that cannot learn meaningful transformations. The paper is the first to connect this explicitly to an entropy maximization objective and derive a scaling factor (`$\alpha_T = 4/3 \cdot \|W^R - \mu_T\|_{\ell_1} / n$`) designed to produce uniform bin occupancy rather than minimum `$\ell_2$` error.

2. **Gradient mismatch from non-isometric mapping.** When the quantized weight magnitude diverges from the real-valued weight magnitude (`$W_i^T \neq W_i^R$` in scale), the gradient flowing through the STE is scaled incorrectly — either amplified (causing explosion) or attenuated (causing vanishing). The paper cites Liu et al. (2020b) for the theoretical basis but makes the novel claim that this is *the* reason generative transformers fail to converge under prior quantization schemes. The isometric design — where `$W_i^T \approx W_i^R$` in magnitude for weights within the clipping range — ensures the STE gradient approximation `$\partial W^T / \partial W^R \approx 1$` is actually correct for in-range values, rather than being a crude heuristic.

**Why this is a fundamental reframing, not an incremental tweak.** The paper is not proposing a better `$\ell_2$`-minimizing formula — it is arguing that `$\ell_2$` minimization is the *wrong function* to optimize. The correct objectives are: (a) maximize entropy of the discrete representation (subject to the quantization level constraint), and (b) preserve gradient flow geometry (isometry). These are distinct mathematical criteria that happen to produce different formulas than `$\ell_2$` minimization (compare Eq. 8 to Eq. 1–3, and Eq. 9 to Eq. 4–5). The fact that these formulas work — while every prior formula fails catastrophically at the same bit-widths (Table 1, fully ternary and binary baselines scoring R2 < 1.5) — is evidence that the diagnosis is correct, not just the formulas.

**Evidence anchor.** Table 3, rows 3 vs. 9 and 7 vs. 13, isolates the weight quantization contribution. On CNN/DailyMail, stats-based weight quantization alone lifts the fully ternary ROUGE-L from 12.42 to 18.42 and the fully binary ROUGE-L from 2.48 to 14.01. These are not marginal improvements — they represent the difference between a non-functional model and one that produces recognizable output. The full TBT (with activation quantization) amplifies these gains further, but the weight quantization reformulation alone is sufficient to escape total collapse.

---

### Innovation 2: Learning Activation Quantization Parameters End-to-End from the Task Loss — Not from Activation Reconstruction

All prior work on activation quantization — including the quantized BERT methods that TBT adapts — treats activation quantization as a **feed-forward compression problem**: at each layer, compress the continuous activation tensor to a discrete representation using a fixed formula (e.g., TWN's Eq. 1–3) or, at most, learned step sizes calibrated to minimize activation reconstruction error (e.g., LSQ, Esser et al., 2019). TBT's second conceptual departure is making activation quantization **task-aware**: the scaling factors `$\alpha$` for activation quantizers are learned via gradient descent through the full network loss (Eqs. 12, 14), not optimized to reconstruct the activation tensor.

**What this means concretely and why it matters.** A learned `$\alpha$` in TBT can choose to *sacrifice* activation reconstruction fidelity in a particular layer if doing so improves the final output. For example, an activation quantizer early in the encoder might learn a wide `$\alpha$` that preserves fine-grained distinctions needed for later attention computation, while a quantizer near the output might learn a narrow `$\alpha$` that aggressively clips extreme values — even though this harms per-tensor `$\ell_2$` reconstruction — because those extreme activations were noise that the decoder should ignore. The STE gradient `$\partial X^T / \partial \alpha$` (Eq. 12) backpropagates the downstream loss signal directly into the scaling factor, making this optimization possible.

**Contrast with weight quantization's statistics-based approach.** The paper makes a deliberate, non-obvious asymmetry: weights use fixed formulas (Section 2.2) while activations use learned parameters (Section 2.3). This is not an arbitrary implementation choice — it reflects a fundamental difference between weights and activations that the paper identifies:

- Weights are relatively **stable across inputs** — a weight matrix's statistics change only through the slow process of gradient updates, not from input to input. A statistics-based formula computed at each forward pass is sufficient to track this slow drift.
- Activations are **input-dependent and distribution-shifting** — each new sequence produces a different activation pattern, and as the weights update during quantization-aware training, the activation distributions shift (a quantized analog of internal covariate shift). A fixed formula cannot adapt to both the per-input variation and the training dynamics simultaneously.

This asymmetry is a conceptual insight about the nature of quantization in deep networks, not merely a design decision. Prior work (TWN, BWN, TernaryBert) used the same fixed-formula approach for both weights and activations. TBT is the first to argue — and demonstrate — that these two quantization targets require fundamentally different calibration strategies.

**The elastic quantization grids are a separate, complementary idea.** The asymmetric quantization for non-negative activations (`$\{0, \alpha, 2\alpha\}$` for ternary, `$\{0, \alpha\}$` for binary) is conceptually simpler: it's a representational efficiency argument. Using a symmetric grid on ReLU/Softmax outputs wastes one quantization level on negative values that never occur. Figure 2(d) visualizes this waste explicitly — the baseline TWN method maps all ReLU output activations to zero because the symmetric ternary grid has no positive level beyond `$\alpha$` that can accommodate them. The elastic quantization in TBT fixes this. However, this grid asymmetry is a straightforward adaptation — the deeper idea is the **learnability** of `$\alpha$`, not the grid geometry.

**Why this is a conceptual advance, not just engineering.** The paper demonstrates that activation quantization cannot be treated as a preprocessing step (compress tensor, then pass to next layer) — it must be **integrated into the optimization**. The learned scaling factors co-adapt with the weight quantizers during training, and this co-adaptation is what stabilizes convergence. Evidence: Table 3, rows 2 vs. 4 and 6 vs. 8, shows that adding learnable activation quantization to the baseline (TWN/BWN) yields minimal gains when the weight quantization is still entropy-poor and non-isometric (rows 2: 12.13 ROUGE-L, row 6: 1.78 ROUGE-L). It's only the *combination* of stats-based weight quantization (fixing the weight representation) AND learned activation quantization (adapting to the resulting activation distributions) that produces functional models (rows 4: 29.07, row 8: 25.29). Neither fix alone suffices — they solve different parts of the accumulated error problem.

---

### Innovation 3: The Combinatorial Nature of the Fix — Two Independently Insufficient Mechanisms Become Sufficient When Jointly Deployed

The paper's most striking empirical finding is also its most significant architectural insight: neither the weight quantization reformulation nor the learned activation quantization works in isolation for fully ternary/binary models, but their combination enables convergence to highly competitive accuracy. This is not a claim about additive gains (where each method contributes a few points) — it's a claim about **complementary failure modes** whose simultaneous resolution is necessary for any functional model to emerge.

**The ablation evidence (Table 3) tells a binary story, not a continuous one.** In the fully ternary setting on XSUM:
- Baseline (TWN for both weights and activations): ROUGE-L 11.4 (effectively non-functional)
- Baseline + learned activations only: ROUGE-L 12.13 (no meaningful improvement)
- Baseline + stats-based weights only: ROUGE-L 12.74 (no meaningful improvement)
- TBT (both): ROUGE-L 29.07 (functional, competitive)

In the fully binary setting:
- Baseline (BWN): ROUGE-L 1.78 (complete collapse)
- Baseline + learned activations only: ROUGE-L 1.78 (zero improvement — the model is so broken that learned `$\alpha$` receives no useful gradient)
- Baseline + stats-based weights only: ROUGE-L 10.00 (some signal emerges)
- TBT (both): ROUGE-L 25.29 (functional)

The key observation is that the improvement from combining both methods is **super-multiplicative**: 12.74 + (12.13 − 11.4) ≈ 13.5 if effects were additive, but the actual combined result is 29.07. Similarly for binary: 10.00 + 0 ≈ 10.0 additive, 25.29 actual. This is a phase transition, not a linear combination.

**What this reveals about the failure mechanism.** The super-multiplicative interaction implies that the two failure modes (low-entropy weights + gradient mismatch, and rigid activation quantization) are **coupled** in a way that makes fixing either one alone ineffective:

- If you fix weights but keep rigid activation quantization, the improved weight representations produce activations whose distribution the fixed activation quantizer cannot handle — the quantized activations still lose critical information, and the autoregressive decoder still compounds errors.
- If you fix activations but keep low-entropy weights, the learned activation `$\alpha$` parameters receive no useful training signal because the weight representations are too impoverished — the model never reaches a state where activation quantization quality matters, because it's already collapsed at the weight level.

Only when both are fixed simultaneously does the training signal become coherent enough to propagate meaningful gradients through the entire encoder-decoder stack, allowing the model to converge.

**Why this is an architectural insight beyond this paper.** The field has generally treated quantization method components as independent — improve weight quantization OR improve activation quantization OR add distillation OR use better STE. TBT's ablation demonstrates that for extreme bit-width generative models, the optimization problem is **joint**: weight and activation quantization interact in a way that creates a viability threshold, below which no amount of any single improvement helps, and above which the model suddenly works. This has implications for future low-bit quantization research: ablation studies that test components in isolation may dramatically underestimate their importance if the coupling is super-multiplicative. Designing quantization systems for extreme regimes requires simultaneous consideration of weight encoding, activation encoding, and gradient flow, not piecewise optimization.

### Innovation 4: Establishing That Fully Binary and Ternary Generative Transformers Exist at All — Reframing the Success Criterion

Perhaps the paper's most understated contribution is epistemological: it demonstrates that the null hypothesis — "generative transformers cannot be quantized below 4 bits" — is false. Prior to this work, every attempt at fully ternary or binary text generation models produced outputs with ROUGE-2 scores below 1.5 (Table 1: TernaryBert 2.23 on XSUM, BinaryBert 0.12, BiBert 0.06). The baseline TWN and BWN adaptations produce ROUGE-2 scores of 1.21 and 0.01 respectively. These are not "low accuracy" — they are evidence of **complete training collapse**: the models produce empty outputs, repeated tokens, or random gibberish that happens to overlap with reference summaries at chance levels.

**Why this matters as an innovation.** In normal ML research, a 4-point ROUGE-1 gap between a quantized model and its full-precision counterpart (41 vs. 44.9 on CNN/DailyMail, Table 1) might be framed as a limitation. But when the prior state of the art for the fully ternary setting produces ROUGE-1 of 10.95 (TernaryBert adapted to BART), the 41 achieved by TBT represents a **qualitative regime change** — from "this approach doesn't work at all" to "this approach produces practically useful output." The paper is careful to frame its results in these terms (Section 1):

> "Our ternary BART base achieves an R1 score of 41 on the CNN/DailyMail benchmark, which is merely 3.9 points behind the full model while being 16× more efficient. Our binary model, while less accurate, achieves a highly non-trivial score of 35.6."

The phrase "highly non-trivial" is doing real work here. It acknowledges that the binary model is worse than the ternary model, but asserts that the binary model's score is meaningful — it produces coherent summaries with measurable semantic overlap with references — in a setting where prior models produced noise.

**The conceptual reframing: from "accuracy parity" to "existence proof."** The dominant framing in quantization research has been to minimize the accuracy gap between quantized and full-precision models — ideally achieving "lossless" quantization where the gap is zero. This framing makes sense at 8-bit and 4-bit, where the gap can be driven to near zero with sufficient engineering. At 1–2 bits, this framing is counterproductive because it sets an impossible standard that obscures real progress. The paper implicitly adopts a different success criterion: **functional viability**. A ternary model that is 4 ROUGE points behind full precision but 16× more efficient is a *different kind of artifact* than an 8-bit model that is 0.1 points behind — it enables deployment scenarios that are categorically impossible with the full-precision version (on-device, real-time, low-power). The 3.9-point gap is the price of a 16× efficiency gain, and the paper presents this tradeoff as a success, not a shortcoming.

This reframing is subtle but significant. It shifts the research question from "how close can we get to full precision?" (a diminishing-returns race) to "at what efficiency multiplier does the accuracy tradeoff become acceptable for practical deployment?" (a systems-level optimization). The paper's ternary results at 16× efficiency and binary results at up to 32× compression with 58× potential speedup provide concrete data points for this new question.

**Evidence anchor.** The comparison is clearest in the machine translation results (Table 2). TBT's ternary-weight, 8-bit activation mBART achieves 24.63 BLEU — a 1.2-point improvement over prior ternary-weight SoTA (DQ-BART at 23.48). TBT's binary-weight, 8-bit activation model scores 24.30 BLEU — almost a full point higher than the prior *ternary-weight* SoTA. And the fully ternary model achieves 21.70 BLEU, with the fully binary model at 17.59. These are not parity with full precision (26.82 BLEU), but they are the first demonstration that a binary-weight translation model can *beat* the previous best ternary-weight model — a finding that challenges assumptions about the necessary bit-width for language generation.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The paper uses three standard benchmarks: **CNN/DailyMail** (Nallapati et al., 2016), a news summarization dataset with ~300k document-summary pairs featuring longer, multi-sentence summaries (~30 sentences per document); **XSUM** (Narayan et al., 2018), a news summarization dataset with 226k BBC articles paired with single-sentence summaries; and **WMT16 English-Romanian (En-Ro)** (Bojar et al., 2016a), a machine translation benchmark used specifically for compatibility with prior work on quantized translation models.

- **Base model(s).** For summarization, the paper uses **BART-base** (Lewis et al., 2019), a 140M-parameter English-only encoder-decoder transformer pre-trained with a denoising objective. For machine translation, it uses **mBART-large** (Liu et al., 2020a), a 680M-parameter multilingual encoder-decoder transformer pre-trained on 25 languages. The paper argues BART-base is representative of moderately-sized generative transformers and that mBART-large tests scalability to larger architectures.

- **Metrics.** For summarization, the standard **ROUGE-{1, 2, L}** scores (Recall-Oriented Understudy for Gisting Evaluation) measure unigram overlap (R1), bigram overlap (R2), and longest common subsequence overlap (RL) between generated and reference summaries. For machine translation, **BLEU** (Bilingual Evaluation Understudy) measures n-gram precision against reference translations. Both are computed using standard evaluation scripts for their respective benchmarks.

- **Baselines.** The paper compares against multiple categories of prior work. For ternary-weight 8-bit activation models: **QuantBart** (Tao et al., 2022) and **DQ-BART** (Li et al., 2022), both summarized in Table 1. For fully ternary and binary settings where no prior art exists, the paper adapts encoder-only methods to BART: **TernaryBert** (Zhang et al., 2020), **BinaryBert** (Bai et al., 2021b), and **BiBert** (Qin et al., 2021), all marked with asterisks in Table 1 to indicate the paper implemented these adaptations. Additionally, the paper includes naive quantization baselines using **TWN** (ternary weight networks, Li et al., 2016) and **BWN** (binary weight networks, Courbariaux et al., 2016) applied directly to BART. For compression, **BlockPruning** (Lagunas et al., 2021) provides a non-quantization baseline at matched model size. For translation, **DQ-BART** (Li et al., 2022) serves as the primary prior SoTA for ternary-weight 8-bit activation models.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper measures efficiency in terms of **model size (MB)** and **FLOPs relative to full-precision BART** (Table 1). For ternary models (2-bit weights, 2-bit activations), the size is 39.6 MB (0.0625× FLOPs of full precision); for binary models, 23.2 MB (0.0156× FLOPs). The FLOPs multipliers are theoretical — they assume hardware that can execute ternary/binary operations multiplication-free, following the efficiency analysis from Rastegari et al. (2016). There is no measurement of wall-clock latency or actual hardware throughput.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not use cross-validation or report confidence intervals. All results on CNN/DailyMail, XSUM, and WMT16 En-Ro are reported as single-run scores on the standard test splits. This is consistent with prior work (QuantBart, DQ-BART) but means there is no estimate of variance. Training is conducted for 20 epochs on 8 GPUs with batch size 128; the learning rate is `$2.5 \times 10^{-4}$` for 8-bit activation models and `$5 \times 10^{-4}$` for binary/ternary activation models (Section 3.1). Knowledge distillation from the full-precision teacher is used during training, following the paradigm of Li et al. (2022), but distillation temperature, loss weight, and other hyperparameters are not specified.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Summarization on XSUM and CNN/DailyMail

**Ternary weights, 8-bit activations (2-2-8):** TBT achieves ROUGE-{1,2,L} scores of 42.40/19.54/34.51 on XSUM and 43.46/20.52/40.58 on CNN/DailyMail (Table 1). This outperforms the prior ternary-weight state of the art — DQ-BART — by 2.34 R1 points on XSUM (42.40 vs. 40.06) and 2.06 RL points (34.51 vs. 32.46). On CNN/DailyMail, the gains are smaller: TBT achieves 43.46 R1 vs. DQ-BART's 42.94, a 0.52-point improvement. Against QuantBart on XSUM, the gap is larger: TBT's RL of 34.51 vs. QuantBart's 31.72, a 2.79-point improvement. The TBT ternary-weight model is 39.6 MB — roughly 1/13th of the full-precision BART's 532 MB — and operates at 0.25× the FLOPs.

**Binary weights, 8-bit activations (1-1-8):** TBT achieves 40.96/18.37/33.30 on XSUM and 42.66/19.72/39.80 on CNN/DailyMail (Table 1). Remarkably, the binary-weight TBT model on XSUM achieves an RL of 33.30 — which is **0.84 points higher** than the prior ternary-weight SoTA (DQ-BART at 32.46 RL) and 1.58 points higher than QuantBart (31.72 RL). On CNN/DailyMail, binary-weight TBT scores 39.80 RL, comparable to DQ-BART's ternary-weight 40.13. The binary-weight TBT model (23.2 MB) also outperforms BlockPruning (Lagunas et al., 2021) at the same compressed size (23 MB): 39.80 vs. 38.4 RL on CNN/DailyMail. This is notable because BlockPruning uses unstructured sparsity, which is generally considered more flexible than binary quantization.

**Fully ternary (2-2-2):** TBT achieves 36.21/14.38/29.07 on XSUM and 41.03/18.18/38.30 on CNN/DailyMail (Table 1). Compared to the full-precision BART baseline (43.84/20.79/35.71 on XSUM, 44.90/22.25/42.09 on CNN/DailyMail), the gap is 7.63 R1 and 6.64 RL on XSUM, and 3.87 R1 and 3.79 RL on CNN/DailyMail. The prior adapted baselines (TernaryBert*) achieve 14.03/2.23/11.79 on XSUM and 10.95/0.52/8.56 on CNN/DailyMail — effectively non-functional, with R2 scores below 2.5 indicating almost no bigram overlap with reference summaries. TBT improves by 22.18 R1 on XSUM and 30.08 R1 on CNN/DailyMail over these baselines. This is the central "first demonstration" claim: TBT is the first method to produce a functional fully ternary generative transformer.

**Fully binary (1-1-1):** TBT achieves 31.68/11.19/25.29 on XSUM and 35.56/11.71/33.23 on CNN/DailyMail (Table 1). The full-precision gap is 12.16 R1 and 10.42 RL on XSUM, and 9.34 R1 and 8.86 RL on CNN/DailyMail. The adapted baselines (BinaryBert*, BiBert*) score 7.58–8.13 R1 and 0.06–0.12 R2 on XSUM, and 9.80–14.22 R1 and 0.13–0.15 R2 on CNN/DailyMail — the R2 scores of essentially zero confirm these baselines produce random output. The naive BWN baseline scores 1.90/0.01/1.78 on XSUM and 2.78/0.08/2.48 on CNN/DailyMail, showing total collapse. TBT's fully binary model thus represents a ~24-point R1 improvement on XSUM and ~33-point R1 improvement on CNN/DailyMail over naive binarization — a qualitative jump from non-functional to practical.

#### Machine Translation on WMT16 En-Ro

**Ternary weights, 8-bit activations (2-2-8):** TBT achieves 24.63 BLEU on WMT16 En-Ro (Table 2), improving the prior SoTA (DQ-BART at 23.48) by 1.15 points. The full-precision mBART baseline is 26.82 BLEU, so the gap is 2.19 points.

**Binary weights, 8-bit activations (1-1-8):** TBT achieves 24.30 BLEU (Table 2) — 0.82 points higher than DQ-BART's ternary-weight score of 23.48. This is a striking result: a **binary-weight** model outperforms the previous best **ternary-weight** model, while being 2× more compressed (0.16 GB vs. 0.31 GB for the ternary model). The gap to full-precision mBART is 2.52 BLEU.

**Fully ternary (2-2-2):** TBT achieves 21.70 BLEU (Table 2). No prior baseline exists for fully ternary translation models (DQ-BART only reported 2-2-8). The gap to full precision is 5.12 BLEU.

**Fully binary (1-1-1):** TBT achieves 17.59 BLEU (Table 2). Again, no prior baseline exists. The gap to full precision is 9.23 BLEU.

**Cross-task pattern:** Across both summarization and translation, the ternary-weight 8-bit activation models lose relatively little accuracy (2–4 points on primary metrics), while the fully binary models lose substantially more (8–12 points). The binary-weight 8-bit activation models consistently punch above their weight class, matching or exceeding prior ternary-weight SoTA. This suggests that weight quantization to 1–2 bits, when done with TBT's entropy-maximizing isometric approach, preserves much of the model's representational capacity, and that activation quantization to extreme levels is the harder problem — consistent with the paper's claim that activation distributions are more variable and harder to quantize without learnable adaptation.

#### Sequence Length Analysis

Table 4 compares generated average sequence lengths between TBT and baseline (TWN for ternary, BWN for binary) methods. On XSUM, the full-precision BART generates 30.73 tokens on average. TBT's 2-2-8 model generates 32.04 tokens (close to full precision); the 2-2-2 model generates 30.71 (essentially identical); the 1-1-1 model generates 29.81 (slightly shorter). The baseline methods, by contrast, diverge severely: fully ternary baseline generates 48.41 tokens (runaway repetition) and fully binary baseline generates 62.0 tokens (the maximum allowed, indicating failure to produce a stopping token). On CNN/DailyMail, the full-precision BART generates 99.89 tokens on average. TBT's 2-2-8 model generates 95.78; 2-2-2 generates 88.38 (moderate shortening); 1-1-1 generates 67.51 (substantial shortening but still coherent). The baselines collapse: fully ternary baseline generates 14.88 tokens (truncation), fully binary baseline generates 128.0 (maximum length, no stopping). Table 4 demonstrates that the baseline methods not only produce low-quality output but also fail at basic sequence length control — they either loop indefinitely or terminate prematurely — while TBT maintains sequence-level coherence even at 1-1-1.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Weight quantization method vs. activation quantization method (Table 3, rows 1–8 on XSUM, rows 9–16 on CNN/DailyMail):** The core ablation tests four configurations per bit-width: (a) baseline TWN/BWN, (b) baseline + learning-based activations only, (c) baseline + stats-based weights only, (d) TBT with both. In the fully ternary setting on XSUM (rows 1–4): configuration (a) scores 12.80/1.21/11.4; (b) scores 15.05/1.38/12.13 (marginal gain); (c) scores 13.79/0.87/12.74 (marginal gain); (d) scores 36.21/14.38/29.07 (qualitative jump). The R2 scores are particularly instructive: 1.21 → 1.38 → 0.87 → 14.38, meaning neither fix alone produces meaningful bigram overlap. On CNN/DailyMail fully ternary (rows 9–12): the pattern is identical — baseline 12.42 RL, +activations 12.58, +weights 18.42, TBT 38.30. The weight quantization fix alone provides a noticeable improvement on CNN/DailyMail (18.42 vs. 12.42 RL) but nowhere near the combined effect. For fully binary on XSUM (rows 5–8): baseline 1.78 RL; +activations only 1.78 RL (zero improvement — the model is so broken that learned α receives no useful gradient); +weights only 10.00 RL (some signal emerges); TBT 25.29 RL. On CNN/DailyMail fully binary (rows 13–16): baseline 2.48 RL; +activations 2.48; +weights 14.01; TBT 33.23. The key finding is the **super-multiplicative interaction**: the combined improvement far exceeds the sum of individual improvements. This pattern supports the paper's claim that weight quantization entropy/gradient issues and activation quantization rigidity are coupled failure modes that must be addressed simultaneously.

**R2 as a diagnostic for functional collapse (Table 3, all rows):** The ROUGE-2 metric — which measures bigram overlap — serves as an unofficial "collapse detector." Across all ablated configurations that fail, the R2 score remains below 1.5 (and typically below 0.5). Fully functional TBT models achieve R2 scores of 11–18. This is consistent with the hypothesis that collapsed models produce either repetitive loops (inflating R1 through repeated common words, but not R2 since no coherent bigrams form) or random token strings (no n-gram overlap at any level). The paper does not explicitly make this argument, but the data pattern is unmistakable: R2 < 1.5 corresponds to non-functional models, R2 > 10 corresponds to functional ones.

**Ternary vs. binary weight quantization with 8-bit activations (Table 1, rows comparing 2-2-8 vs. 1-1-8):** On XSUM, binary-weight TBT (1-1-8) scores 33.30 RL vs. ternary-weight TBT's 34.51 RL — a gap of 1.21 RL points, which is small relative to the 2× additional compression. On CNN/DailyMail, the gap is even smaller: 39.80 vs. 40.58 RL (0.78 points). On WMT16 En-Ro, binary-weight TBT scores 24.30 BLEU vs. ternary-weight's 24.63 BLEU (0.33 points). This is a non-obvious finding: **tripling the number of weight levels (binary → ternary, which doubles bit-width from 1 to 2) yields diminishing returns** when activations are kept at 8 bits. The paper does not explore this explicitly, but it suggests that weight representation is not the primary bottleneck when activations are at moderate precision.

**Generated sequence length as a coherence probe (Table 4):** The sequence length analysis serves as an implicit ablation of decoding stability. For 8-bit activation models (rows 2-2-8, 1-1-8), TBT generates sequences within 5% of the full-precision average length on both datasets. For fully ternary/binary models, TBT generates sequences at 70–90% of full-precision length on CNN/DailyMail, while baselines collapse to either 14.88 tokens (ternary baseline, premature termination) or 128.0 (binary baseline, inability to stop). This demonstrates that TBT's quantization scheme preserves not just token-level accuracy but the autoregressive decoding dynamics — the model learns when to stop generating and how to maintain coherent output length.

**The factor 0.7 in TWN threshold (Eq. 2):** The paper does NOT ablate this constant, which is inherited from Li et al. (2016) without modification. The TWN baseline's poor performance (Table 1, rows 1 and 9) indicates this constant — chosen for CNN image classification — does not transfer well to transformer weights, but the paper does not explore sensitivity to this value. This is a missing ablation.

**The 4/3 factor in TBT's ternary α_T (Eq. 8):** The paper does NOT ablate this factor against other constants (e.g., 1.0, 1.5, 2.0) or a learned version. The derivation (uniform weight initialization → scalar → 4/3) is provided as motivation, but no empirical evidence is presented that 4/3 is optimal rather than merely sufficient. This is a missing ablation, particularly since the paper's core argument is that the `$\ell_2$`-minimizing formula is wrong and the entropy-maximizing formula is right — demonstrating sensitivity to the scaling factor's precise value would strengthen this argument.

**Knowledge distillation contribution:** The paper states it uses knowledge distillation from the full-precision teacher (Section 3.1) but does not ablate its effect. No results are presented for TBT trained without distillation, so it is impossible to determine how much of the performance comes from the quantization scheme versus from the distillation signal. Given that prior work (DQ-BART, QuantBart) also used distillation, this is a reasonable baseline comparison, but the isolated contribution of TBT's quantization innovations cannot be separated from the distillation effect without an ablation.

**Per-row vs. per-tensor weight quantization:** The paper mentions using per-row weight quantization ("a tensor will have #row of scaling factors," Section 3.6), consistent with prior work. No ablation compares per-row vs. per-tensor vs. per-channel granularity for TBT's specific formulation. The visualization in Figure 2(e–h) shows per-row scaling factors but does not compare against coarser granularities.

### Critical Assessment

**Does the paper demonstrate that TBT enables functional ternary and binary generative transformers where no prior method could?**

Yes, unequivocally. The evidence is overwhelming: every prior method adapted to the fully ternary or binary generative setting produces ROUGE-2 scores below 2.5 on XSUM and below 1.0 on CNN/DailyMail (Table 1, rows labeled BinaryBert*, BiBert*, TernaryBert*, and the TWN/BWN baselines). These scores are consistent with models that generate either empty output, repeated tokens, or random word salad. TBT achieves ROUGE-2 scores of 14.38 (ternary XSUM), 18.18 (ternary CNN/DM), 11.19 (binary XSUM), and 11.71 (binary CNN/DM) — all well into the functional range. The ablation (Table 3) demonstrates that prior methods fail because of specific, identifiable issues (low-entropy non-isometric weight quantization + rigid symmetric activation quantization) and that fixing both simultaneously is necessary.

However, "functional" should be precisely qualified. The fully binary model on XSUM scores 31.68 R1 / 25.29 RL — these are substantially below full precision (43.84 / 35.71) and below the ternary model (36.21 / 29.07). The R2 of 11.19 indicates that the model generates some correct bigrams but far fewer than full precision (20.79). A user reading binary-generated summaries would notice degraded quality. The paper's claim is not that binary equals full-precision quality, but that binary produces coherent, useful output where prior approaches produce none — and the seqlen analysis (Table 4) corroborates this by showing binary TBT generates reasonable-length summaries while baselines loop or truncate.

**Does the paper demonstrate that the combination of statistics-based weight quantization and learning-based activation quantization is necessary (not merely additive)?**

Yes, the ablation in Table 3 demonstrates this clearly. For fully ternary on XSUM: baseline RL 11.4 → +learned activations 12.13 (+0.73) → +stats-based weights 12.74 (+1.34) → both 29.07 (+17.33). The combined effect (29.07 − 11.4 = 17.67) is massively larger than the sum of individual effects (0.73 + 1.34 = 2.07). For fully binary: baseline 1.78 → +activations 1.78 (+0.0) → +weights 10.00 (+8.22) → both 25.29 (+23.51). The super-multiplicative interaction is even more dramatic. This pattern supports the paper's claim that the two innovations are **complementary** and address coupled failure modes: learned activations alone cannot help when weights carry almost no information (binary: RL 1.78 → 1.78 with learned activations), and stats-based weights alone cannot overcome rigid activation quantization that wastes representational levels (ternary: RL 12.74 → 29.07 when activations are also fixed). The necessity of **joint** deployment is well-established.

**Does the paper demonstrate that the ternary model achieves "merely 3.9 points behind the full model while being 16× more efficient"?**

This claim (from Section 1) refers specifically to R1 on CNN/DailyMail: TBT ternary (2-2-2) achieves 41.03 vs. BART full-precision 44.90, a gap of 3.87 ≈ 3.9 points. The 16× efficiency is from FLOPs: 0.0625× of full precision = 1/16 the computation. This is supported by Table 1. However, the 16× figure is **theoretical FLOPs reduction assuming multiplication-free ternary hardware**, not measured speedup on real hardware. The paper acknowledges this limitation in Section 6: "binarization and ternarization require bit-packing to have actual memory savings and dedicated hardware support for real-time acceleration, which is more of a hardware implementation aspect and not studied in this paper." So the claim should be understood as: if ternary hardware existed, the model would require ~1/16th the arithmetic operations. Actual wall-clock speedup on current hardware (GPUs) would be negligible or negative since GPUs do not natively support ternary operations and emulating them in software introduces overhead.

A second nuance: the 3.9-point gap is on **R1** only. On R2, the gap is 4.07 points (22.25 vs. 18.18), and on RL it's 3.79 points (42.09 vs. 38.30). On XSUM, the gaps are larger: 7.63 R1, 6.41 R2, 6.64 RL. So "merely 3.9 points behind" describes the best-case metric on the best-case dataset — the gap varies by task and metric.

**Does the paper demonstrate that the binary model achieves "a highly non-trivial score of 35.6"?**

The 35.6 refers to R1 on CNN/DailyMail for the fully binary model (Table 1: 35.56). The phrase "highly non-trivial" is doing significant rhetorical work. Compared to prior fully binary baselines (BinaryBert*: 9.80 R1, BiBert*: 14.22 R1, BWN baseline: 2.78 R1), 35.56 is a qualitative improvement — 2.5–12.8× higher R1. However, the binary model's R2 of 11.71 on CNN/DailyMail means it captures only about half the bigram overlap of the full-precision model (22.25). On XSUM, the binary model's R1 of 31.68 and R2 of 11.19 are substantially below full precision. The paper is transparent about this (Section 5: "Our fully binary (weights and activations) model has a wider gap at 10.4 and 8.9 points"), so the "highly non-trivial" framing is fair relative to the baseline of zero — but users should not interpret it as "nearly as good as full precision."

**Does the paper's evaluation support generalization beyond the tested benchmarks and model families?**

No, and the paper does not claim it does. Section 6 (Limitations) explicitly states that "generalizability to extremely long sequences or even streaming data has not been verified" and "the generalizability of the proposed quantization method to other tasks, including computer vision or speech recognition, remains to be tested." The paper tests exactly two model architectures (BART-base, mBART-large) on two task types (summarization, translation) across three datasets. This is a reasonable scope for a first demonstration, but it provides no evidence about whether TBT would work on decoder-only architectures (GPT-style), larger models (BART-large, mBART-xlarge, GPT-3 scale), or tasks requiring different reasoning patterns (question answering, dialogue, code generation). The paper's claims should be interpreted as: TBT works for encoder-decoder transformers on standard summarization and translation benchmarks at the 140M–680M parameter scale.

**What missing experiments would strengthen the paper's claims?**

- **Ablation of the 4/3 factor in α_T:** The paper derives this from uniform initialization properties but never empirically tests sensitivity. Would 1.0 or 2.0 work nearly as well? If so, the entropy-maximization justification is weaker than claimed.
- **Ablation of knowledge distillation:** Since all results use distillation from the full-precision teacher, we cannot separate TBT's quantization efficacy from the benefits of a richer training signal. A without-distillation baseline would clarify how much of the performance gain is architectural versus training-procedure.
- **Larger-scale testing:** The 680M-parameter mBART results (Table 2) begin to test scaling, but a 1B+ parameter model would provide evidence about whether TBT's claims about "larger text generation models such as GPT-3" (Section 5) are plausible.
- **Perplexity or generation diversity metrics:** The paper reports only overlap-based metrics (ROUGE, BLEU). These measure similarity to references but not output diversity or likelihood under the data distribution. A quantized model might achieve reasonable ROUGE by memorizing common phrases while losing the ability to generate diverse, novel summaries — perplexity on a held-out set would detect this.
- **Comparison with 4-bit quantization:** The paper jumps from 8-bit activations directly to ternary/binary. A 4-bit activation baseline would help characterize whether the gain from TBT is specific to extreme quantization or would also improve moderate quantization.
- **Statistical significance:** With no confidence intervals or multiple runs reported, small differences (e.g., TBT 1-1-8 vs. DQ-BART 2-2-8 on WMT16: 24.30 vs. 23.48 BLEU, a 0.82-point gap) cannot be distinguished from noise.

**Does the sequence length analysis (Table 4) genuinely demonstrate robustness to error compounding?**

Partially. The sequence length analysis shows that TBT models produce outputs of approximately correct length while baseline models collapse (either too short or max-length loops). This is strong evidence that TBT avoids the catastrophic decoding failure mode. However, correct length does not guarantee correct content — a model could generate 70 coherent-sounding tokens that are factually wrong or semantically unrelated to the source. The ROUGE scores address this to some extent (by measuring content overlap), but a qualitative error analysis (e.g., how often does the binary model hallucinate facts vs. copy from the source vs. produce correct abstractions?) is absent. The paper also does not analyze whether error compounding manifests in less obvious ways — e.g., gradual semantic drift over long sequences where early tokens are accurate but later tokens diverge. On CNN/DailyMail, the binary model's average length of 67.51 vs. full-precision 99.89 (a 32% reduction) suggests systematic under-generation, which could be a subtle form of error compounding where the model learns to stop early rather than risk generating incorrect content.

**The strongest evidence in the paper:** The ablation study (Table 3) is the most rigorous experiment. It cleanly isolates the contributions of the two core mechanisms across both bit-widths and both datasets, revealing the super-multiplicative interaction that is the paper's central technical finding. The fact that learned activations alone provide zero improvement over the binary BWN baseline (RL 1.78 → 1.78 on XSUM) while stats-based weights alone provide modest improvement (RL 1.78 → 10.00) and their combination provides dramatic improvement (RL 1.78 → 25.29) is a clear, compelling, and non-obvious result that strongly constrains possible explanations for why prior methods failed.

**The weakest evidence in the paper:** The claimed mechanism for weight quantization — that TBT's formula maximizes entropy and enforces isometry — is supported by the weight histogram visualization in Figure 2 (showing more uniform ternary distributions for TBT vs. baseline) and by the entropy measurements across 96 layers mentioned in Section 3.6 ("the proposed TBT method achieves consistently higher entropy in quantized weights than the baseline method in all the layers"). However, these entropy numbers are not reported numerically anywhere in the paper — only described qualitatively. Without quantitative entropy measurements (e.g., average entropy per layer, entropy vs. ROUGE correlation), the connection between the entropy-maximization design principle and the observed performance improvement remains suggestive rather than demonstrated. The absence of an ablation over the 4/3 factor further weakens the claim that the specific entropy-maximizing formula is causally responsible for the gains, as opposed to the more general design pattern of zero-centering + isometric scaling being sufficient.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### Theoretical Efficiency Gains Require Hardware That Does Not Yet Exist

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's headline efficiency claims — 16× for ternary models (0.0625× FLOPs) and up to 32× compression with 58× speedup for binary models (Section 2.1.2) — assume specialized hardware that can execute ternary and binary operations natively without multiplication units. The paper is explicit about this in Section 6 (Limitations):

> "binarization and ternarization require bit-packing to have actual memory savings and dedicated hardware support for real-time acceleration, which is more of a hardware implementation aspect and not studied in this paper."

The FLOPs multipliers in Table 1 are theoretical arithmetic — they count operations assuming each ternary/binary multiply-accumulate replaces a 32-bit floating-point multiply-accumulate. On current GPUs and CPUs, which lack native ternary/binary matrix multiplication units, these operations must be emulated in software using bit-packing and integer arithmetic, which introduces overhead that can partially or fully negate the theoretical speedup.

**The consequence.** A practitioner reading the abstract ("16× more efficient") and conclusion ("multiple orders of magnitude gains in efficiency") could reasonably expect to load the released model and observe wall-clock speedups. On current hardware, they would observe negligible speedup or possibly slowdown, because (a) the weight matrices must be unpacked from their bit-packed representation into a format the ALU can process, (b) emulated ternary/binary operations still execute on the same floating-point or integer units that full-precision models use, and (c) the learnable activation scaling factors introduce additional per-tensor multiply operations that full-precision models do not have. The actual deployment benefit today is limited to model size compression (39.6 MB vs. 532 MB for ternary BART, a genuine 13.4× storage reduction), which helps for storage-constrained environments but does not reduce inference latency or energy consumption on commodity hardware.

The 58× CPU speedup figure (cited from Rastegari et al., 2016, Section 2.1.2) is particularly misleading if read without context: that figure was demonstrated on binary convolutional networks for image classification using hand-optimized XNOR kernels, and it does not automatically transfer to transformer architectures with attention operations, layer normalization, residual connections, and autoregressive decoding — all of which introduce non-quantized operations that become the bottleneck under Amdahl's law when the matrix multiplications are accelerated.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. The paper reports no wall-clock timing, no throughput measurements, no latency benchmarks, and no memory bandwidth utilization data — not even on current hardware in emulation mode. Table 1 reports only theoretical FLOPs ratios and model sizes in MB. The paper does not profile the fraction of total operations that are quantized versus remaining in higher precision (layer norm, Softmax exponentiation, residual adds, embedding lookup), which determines the maximum achievable speedup under the quantized operations.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges this limitation in Section 6 but does nothing to address it empirically. The authors frame it as a hardware implementation concern that is "not studied in this paper," which is a reasonable scope limitation for an algorithmic contribution but creates a significant gap between the claimed efficiency and what a practitioner can realize today. The release of code and models on GitHub is valuable but does not close this gap — users still need bit-packing implementations and, ultimately, custom hardware to achieve the quoted multipliers. No FPGA or ASIC synthesis results, no custom CUDA kernel benchmarks, and no projection of when or whether such hardware might become available are provided.

---

### Knowledge Distillation, Difficulty Estimation Cost, and Pre-Trained Initialization Make the Reported Gains a Best-Case Upper Bound — Not a Self-Contained Training Result

**The assumption or constraint.** TBT's training procedure has three components whose individual contributions are never measured: (a) initialization from a full-precision pre-trained model (BART-base or mBART-large), (b) knowledge distillation from that same full-precision model during the entire 20-epoch fine-tuning period, and (c) the quantization mechanisms themselves (stats-based weights + learned activations). The paper states in Section 3.1:

> "We follow recent work (Li et al., 2022) in training the quantized network with initialization and knowledge distillation from a full-precision pre-trained model."

No experiment trains TBT from scratch without distillation. No experiment varies the distillation weight or temperature. No experiment measures TBT's performance when the teacher is removed at different points in training. This means the reported ROUGE and BLEU scores conflate the quantization scheme's representational capacity with the benefits of a rich teacher signal — we cannot determine whether TBT's binary model achieves ROUGE-L 25.29 on XSUM because the quantization is good, or because distillation is providing a loss landscape that is inherently easier to navigate, or both.

**The consequence.** The practical implication is significant. Knowledge distillation requires having a full-precision teacher model available and running it during training to produce soft targets — this doubles the training-time memory and compute requirements (both the quantized student and full-precision teacher must be in memory, and the teacher must perform full forward passes). For very large models where TBT's efficiency gains would be most impactful (GPT-3 scale, as the paper suggests in Section 5), the teacher model itself may be prohibitively expensive to run, making the distillation-dependent training procedure self-defeating for the scenarios TBT is meant to enable. If TBT's performance degrades substantially without distillation — which the paper provides no evidence to assess either way — then the method's applicability to the largest models is questionable.

Additionally, the reliance on a pre-trained initialization means TBT is not a general-purpose quantization-aware training method that can be applied to models trained from scratch. It is specifically a post-training quantization plus fine-tuning approach. This is a reasonable design choice (training ternary models from scratch is likely infeasible), but it means TBT inherits whatever biases and limitations exist in the pre-trained model and cannot be used for tasks where no suitable pre-trained model exists.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None directly. The comparison against DQ-BART and QuantBart in Table 1 is confounded because both of those methods also use distillation and pre-trained initialization, so the comparison is fair within the paradigm but does not isolate TBT's quantization contribution from the training procedure. Table 3's ablation varies only the quantization mechanisms, not the training procedure — distillation is always present. The paper provides no "TBT without distillation" baseline.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not flag this as a limitation, does not ablate distillation, and does not discuss the computational cost or practical availability requirements of the teacher model. Section 6 (Limitations) does not mention distillation dependence. The authors' suggestion in Section 5 that TBT could be applied to "larger text generation models such as GPT-3" implicitly assumes that running a full-precision GPT-3 as a teacher is feasible, which is precisely the assumption that TBT's efficiency motivation calls into question.

### Evaluated on Only Two Model Architectures and Three Datasets — No Evidence for Decoder-Only Models, Larger Scales, or Other Generation Tasks

**The assumption or constraint.** All experiments use encoder-decoder transformer architectures: BART-base (140M parameters) for summarization and mBART-large (680M parameters) for translation. The paper acknowledges this scope limitation in Section 6:

> "We conduct experiments on public datasets of finite sentence length, while generalizability to extremely long sequences or even streaming data has not been verified. Furthermore, the generalizability of the proposed quantization method to other tasks, including computer vision or speech recognition, remains to be tested."

However, the paper does not explicitly acknowledge the architectural limitation. The dominant paradigm in large language models since GPT-3 has been decoder-only architectures (GPT, LLaMA, Mistral, Falcon, OPT, BLOOM), which differ from encoder-decoder in several ways that affect quantization: (a) there is no separate encoder stack, so all attention is causal self-attention, (b) the attention patterns in autoregressive decoding are different (each token attends only to previous tokens), (c) the output projection is tied to the input embedding in many implementations, and (d) the ratio of attention computation to FFN computation differs. TBT's specific design choices — particularly the asymmetric activation quantization for Softmax/ReLU outputs and the learned scaling factors — may or may not transfer cleanly to decoder-only architectures. The encoder-decoder structure used in the experiments has a clear separation between encoding (bidirectional attention, one forward pass) and decoding (causal attention, autoregressive), which may interact with quantization differently than a purely autoregressive decoder-only model.

**The consequence.** A practitioner seeking to quantize a LLaMA or Mistral model for on-device chat cannot assume TBT will work. The failure mode would not necessarily be catastrophic (these architectures are similar enough that some transfer is likely), but the optimal hyperparameters, the need for asymmetric activation quantization, and the interaction between causal attention and quantization error accumulation may differ in ways that reduce accuracy or require architecture-specific tuning. Similarly, tasks beyond summarization and translation — particularly open-ended generation (dialogue, story generation), code generation, or tasks requiring multi-step reasoning — may stress different aspects of the quantized model's capabilities and reveal failure modes not visible in the relatively constrained summarization/translation setups, where outputs are typically short and draw heavily from the input.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None beyond the three tested benchmarks. The mBART-large experiment (Table 2) provides a single scaling data point — 680M parameters is larger than BART-base's 140M but still far from the billion-parameter scale. The translation task is structurally similar to summarization (encode source, decode target), so it tests a different domain (multilingual, different output language) but not a fundamentally different generation paradigm. The sequence length analysis (Table 4) shows that TBT models generate shorter summaries than full-precision models on CNN/DailyMail (67.51 vs. 99.89 tokens for binary, 88.38 vs. 99.89 for ternary), which is a potential warning sign for tasks requiring long-form generation but is not explored further.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges the dataset and task limitation in Section 6 but frames it as a scope constraint rather than a known failure mode. The architectural limitation (encoder-decoder only) is not explicitly acknowledged. The paper's suggestion in Section 5 that applying TBT to "larger text generation models such as GPT-3" is "an open problem and an exciting future research direction" is accurate but understates the uncertainty — we simply do not know whether the method transfers to decoder-only architectures at scale, and the paper provides no evidence to constrain this uncertainty.

### The Binary Models Show a Large Accuracy Gap That Limits Practical Deployment for Quality-Sensitive Applications

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper frames its binary results as "highly non-trivial" and a demonstration that fully binary generative transformers are possible. This framing is correct relative to the prior state of the art (near-zero ROUGE). However, the absolute accuracy of the binary models represents a substantial degradation from full precision that may be unacceptable for many practical applications. On XSUM, the fully binary (1-1-1) model scores ROUGE-1/2/L of 31.68/11.19/25.29 versus full-precision BART at 43.84/20.79/35.71 (Table 1). The gaps are 12.16 R1 points (28% relative reduction), 9.60 R2 points (46% relative reduction), and 10.42 RL points (29% relative reduction). On CNN/DailyMail, the binary model scores 35.56/11.71/33.23 versus 44.90/22.25/42.09 — gaps of 9.34 R1 (21% relative), 10.54 R2 (47% relative), and 8.86 RL (21% relative). For machine translation, the binary model's 17.59 BLEU versus full-precision 26.82 (Table 2) represents a 34% relative reduction.

These are not marginal degradations. A 47% reduction in ROUGE-2 on CNN/DailyMail means the binary model captures fewer than half the correct bigrams that the full-precision model captures. A 34% BLEU reduction on WMT16 En-Ro is severe enough that the translation quality may be below the threshold of usefulness for applications requiring accurate meaning preservation.

**The consequence.** The binary model represents a fundamentally different point in the accuracy-efficiency tradeoff space than the ternary model. The ternary model's gaps (3–7 ROUGE points, depending on metric and dataset) are plausibly acceptable for applications where 16× efficiency is transformative — a user might accept slightly worse summaries in exchange for on-device processing. The binary model's 9–12 point ROUGE gaps and 9-point BLEU gap push the tradeoff into a regime where the efficiency gains (up to 32× compression, 58× theoretical speedup) must be weighed against substantially degraded output quality. A practitioner considering deploying a binary model would need application-specific evaluation: for a content-gisting tool where rough summaries suffice, the binary model might be acceptable; for a production translation system where accuracy is critical, it almost certainly would not be.

The paper's own data reveals a non-linear degradation curve: going from full precision (32-bit) to ternary (2-bit) costs 3.9 R1 points on CNN/DailyMail, but going from ternary to binary (1-bit) costs an additional 5.5 R1 points — the marginal cost of halving the bit-width increases as bit-width decreases. This suggests diminishing returns in the efficiency-accuracy tradeoff that the paper does not explicitly characterize.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 1, Table 2, and the average sequence length data in Table 4 provide the quantitative evidence. The paper is transparent about the gap — Section 5 states "Our fully binary (weights and activations) model has a wider gap at 10.4 and 8.9 points" (referring to ROUGE-L on XSUM and CNN/DailyMail, though the exact numbers differ slightly from Table 1). Table 4 shows that the binary model on CNN/DailyMail generates summaries averaging 67.51 tokens versus full-precision's 99.89 tokens, indicating systematic under-generation that compounds with the lower n-gram overlap scores. However, the paper does not provide qualitative examples of binary model outputs, so a practitioner cannot assess what "35.56 ROUGE-1" means in terms of readability, factual accuracy, or usefulness. The sequence length reduction on CNN/DailyMail (67.51 for binary, 88.38 for ternary, 99.89 for full precision) is not discussed as a potential limitation.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges the accuracy gap qualitatively but does not analyze it as a practical deployment barrier. It does not provide guidance on which applications could tolerate the binary model's accuracy level, does not break down where the errors occur (are they content errors, hallucination, length truncation, or poor abstraction?), and does not compare the binary model against simpler baselines (e.g., a smaller full-precision model with comparable inference cost) to assess whether extreme quantization is the best way to achieve the target efficiency. The paper's framing emphasizes the achievement of making binary work at all over the practical question of whether the resulting accuracy is sufficient — a reasonable choice for a first demonstration paper, but a limitation for deployment decision-making.

### The Learned Activation Scaling Factors Require Per-Tensor Gradient Computation and STE Approximations That Add Training Complexity and Limit Transferability

**The assumption or constraint.** TBT's learned activation quantization introduces one learnable parameter `$\alpha$` per quantized activation tensor in the network. In a BART-base model, there are activation quantizers after every Q/K/V projection, after Softmax, after attention output, after each FFN layer, after ReLU, and after each residual block output — for both encoder and decoder stacks. This adds hundreds of parameters. Each `$\alpha$` has its own STE gradient formula (Eqs. 12, 14) that involves indicator functions restricting gradient flow to values within clipping ranges. During training, these parameters must be optimized jointly with the full-precision latent weights via the task loss, adding hyperparameters (the learning rate for `$\alpha$` parameters, though the paper does not specify whether a separate learning rate is used) and training dynamics complexity.

The paper does not discuss what happens if the learned `$\alpha$` values are transferred to a different task, domain, or input distribution. Since `$\alpha$` is learned end-to-end from the training data's activation statistics, it may be specialized to the training distribution — if the model is fine-tuned on a new task or deployed on text with different length distributions or linguistic properties, the learned `$\alpha$` values may be miscalibrated, causing activations to fall outside the clipping range (saturating the quantizer) or to cluster in a narrow subrange (wasting quantization levels).

**The consequence.** The learnable activation quantization couples the quantized model to the training distribution more tightly than statistics-based quantization would. With a fixed-formula activation quantizer (like TWN), the quantization behavior adapts online to each input's statistics — if the input distribution shifts, the quantizer thresholds shift accordingly. With TBT's learned `$\alpha$`, the scaling factor is frozen after training and cannot adapt to input distribution shifts at inference time. This means TBT's ternary/binary models may be more brittle under distribution shift than full-precision models (which have continuous activations and thus can represent out-of-distribution values without clipping) or statistics-based quantized models (which recompute thresholds from each input).

Additionally, the STE gradient formulation for `$\partial X^T / \partial \alpha_T$` (Eq. 12) is complex and involves the difference between the quantized normalized value `$\hat{X}_i^T$` and the continuous normalized value `$X_i^R / \alpha_T$`, gated by an indicator. This gradient is zero for activations outside the clipping range and potentially high-variance for activations near the clipping boundaries. The paper provides no analysis of the gradient statistics during training — we do not know whether these gradients are well-behaved, whether they suffer from vanishing/exploding issues of their own, or whether the `$\alpha$` parameters converge reliably across different random seeds.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides no ablation that compares learned `$\alpha$` to a statistics-based `$\alpha$` (e.g., using the same per-input statistics formula as the weight quantizers but applied to activations). This would directly test whether learnability is necessary or whether the per-input adaptation of a fixed formula would suffice. The activation visualization in Figure 2(c, d, g, h) shows that TBT's learned quantizer produces a sensible-looking ternary distribution (Panel g) compared to the baseline's degenerate distribution (Panel c), but this is a single-layer snapshot from one input — it does not demonstrate robustness across inputs or data distributions.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not discuss the transferability or robustness of learned `$\alpha$` values, does not measure how much `$\alpha$` varies across inputs during training, and does not test the model on out-of-distribution text (e.g., summaries with different length distributions, different news domains, or different languages for the mBART model). The dependence on learned per-tensor parameters is not listed as a limitation in Section 6.

### No Comparison Against a Smaller Full-Precision Model with Equivalent Inference Cost

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's central claim is that extreme quantization enables efficiency gains while preserving acceptable accuracy. The baseline for this claim is always the same-sized full-precision model (BART-base, mBART-large) — the question is "how much accuracy do we lose by quantizing this model?" However, a practitioner deciding how to achieve a target inference budget has an alternative: **train a smaller full-precision model** that naturally fits within the budget. The paper does not compare TBT's ternary BART (39.6 MB, 0.0625× FLOPs, ROUGE-L 38.30 on CNN/DailyMail) against, say, a BART model with 4× fewer parameters or layers trained to comparable total FLOPs. This comparison would answer the deployment-relevant question: for a given compute/storage budget, is it better to quantize a large model or to use a small full-precision model?

**The consequence.** The paper's framing — "16× more efficient with only 3.9 R1 points lost" — implicitly assumes the baseline must be the full-size model. But if a small full-precision model achieves, say, ROUGE-L 36 with the same inference FLOPs as ternary BART (ROUGE-L 38.30), then the effective gain from extreme quantization is only 2.3 ROUGE-L points, not 3.8 points relative to the full-size model. The 16× multiplier becomes less meaningful because a smaller model achieves most of the efficiency gain through architectural scaling rather than quantization. Without this comparison, the paper's efficiency claims are ceiling estimates — they express the maximum possible gain from quantization relative to the full model, not the practically achievable gain relative to other efficiency methods.

The BlockPruning baseline (Table 1, binary weight section) provides a partial comparison: at matched model size (23 MB), TBT binary-weight 8-bit activation achieves ROUGE-L 39.80 on CNN/DailyMail versus BlockPruning's 38.4. This is a 1.4-point advantage for TBT at matched compression, which is positive but small relative to the 16× compression factor. However, BlockPruning uses unstructured sparsity (individual weight pruning), which is a different efficiency mechanism with different hardware implications, and this single comparison does not establish that quantization dominates architectural scaling more broadly.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Only the BlockPruning comparison, which is limited to the binary-weight 8-bit activation setting (1-1-8) and only on CNN/DailyMail. There is no comparison against smaller BART variants (e.g., BART with 6 encoder layers and 6 decoder layers instead of 12+12, or BART with reduced hidden dimension), no comparison against distilled student models trained without quantization, and no comparison against other efficiency methods (e.g., low-rank factorization, weight sharing, or token pruning). The paper's efficiency claims are measured exclusively against the full-size full-precision model.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not discuss the smaller-model alternative, does not frame its efficiency gains relative to other compression/efficiency methods, and does not acknowledge the absence of this comparison as a limitation. The BlockPruning result hints that quantization is competitive with pruning at matched size, but a more systematic comparison — especially at the ternary 16× compression point — would substantially strengthen (or potentially weaken) the practical case for extreme quantization over simply using a smaller architecture.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

**The paper shifts the generative quantization field from a "can we?" question to a "how well?" question.** Prior to TBT, the state of fully ternary and binary generative transformers was existential collapse — no published method could produce a model that generated coherent text. TernaryBert adapted to BART scores ROUGE-2 of 0.52 on CNN/DailyMail (Table 1); binary baselines score 0.06–0.15. These are not "low accuracy" — they are evidence that training simply does not converge to anything useful. TBT changes the baseline from zero to functional: 41.03 ROUGE-1 on CNN/DailyMail for ternary, 35.56 for binary. This is not a paradigm shift in the sense of overturning a dominant theory — it is an **existence proof** that removes the empirical null hypothesis ("generative transformers cannot be quantized below ternary/binary") and replaces it with a quantitative tradeoff question ("how many ROUGE/BLEU points does each bit-width cost?"). The field now has a target to improve upon rather than a barrier to overcome.

The magnitude of this shift is significant but bounded. TBT does not make extreme quantization "solved" — the binary model's 9–12 point ROUGE gap and the ternary model's 3–7 point gap (varying by metric and dataset) mean there is substantial room between TBT's results and full-precision parity. But having *any* functional baseline at these bit-widths changes the research landscape from exploration ("can we even train these?") to optimization ("how do we close the remaining gap?"). This is precisely the transition that occurred in 8-bit and 4-bit quantization for encoder-only models around 2019–2021, and TBT initiates it for generative models at 1–2 bits.

**The work reconciles a contradiction in the encoder-only vs. generative quantization literature.** Before TBT, the field had a puzzle: binary and ternary BERT models worked reasonably well on classification tasks (BinaryBert, BiBert, TernaryBert reporting non-trivial accuracy on GLUE), but applying the exact same quantization formulas to BART for text generation produced near-zero ROUGE scores. Two hypotheses could explain this: (H1) generative models are categorically harder to quantize due to autoregressive error compounding and the high-cardinality output space, requiring fundamentally different methods; or (H2) the existing quantization formulas were marginally adequate for classification (where a single corrupted token representation might not change the sentence-level prediction) but catastrophically inadequate for generation (where each corrupted token compounds). TBT's evidence supports **H2 with specificity**: the failure is not that generative models need a completely different quantization paradigm, but that the `$\ell_2$`-minimizing, non-isometric formulas used in prior work produce weight representations with low entropy and destructive gradient mismatch — flaws that were tolerable in encoder-only classification but become terminal under autoregressive decoding. Fixing those specific flaws (entropy maximization + isometry for weights, learned asymmetric quantization for activations) is sufficient to make generative models work. The paper thus resolves the contradiction by identifying the *mechanism* of failure, not by appealing to a vague "generation is harder" claim.

**The paper redirects research attention from search algorithms and architectural modifications toward quantization objective design.** A natural instinct when prior extreme quantization methods failed for generation would be to modify the architecture — add special quantization-friendly attention variants, change the decoder structure, or design new building blocks that are inherently more robust to discretization. TBT demonstrates that the standard BART architecture works with extreme quantization *when the quantization objectives are reformulated*. This suggests that the bottleneck was not architectural sensitivity per se, but the mathematical criteria used to derive the quantization mapping. The implication for future work is that effort should flow toward better quantization objectives (entropy-aware, gradient-preserving, task-aware) rather than toward quantization-specialized architectures — the standard transformer, as-is, is capable of supporting ternary and binary computation if quantized correctly. This is a stronger claim than the paper makes explicitly, but it follows directly from TBT's success using unmodified BART/mBART architectures.

**A less obvious shift: the paper implicitly argues that `$\ell_2$` minimization is the wrong optimization target for extreme quantization, not merely an insufficient one.** This is a conceptual reframing with methodological consequences. Most quantization research — including the recent generative quantization work (QuantBart, DQ-BART) — treats quantization as a compression problem: find the discrete representation that minimizes reconstruction error of the continuous tensor. TBT argues that at 1–2 bits, this framing is actively harmful because it optimizes for the wrong property (per-element fidelity rather than information capacity and gradient consistency). If the field adopts this reframing, it would change how new quantization methods are designed and evaluated — away from measuring `$\ell_2$` reconstruction error of individual tensors and toward measuring the entropy of quantized representations and the fidelity of gradient flow through the quantized network. The paper's ablation (Table 3) provides strong evidence for this reframing: fixing the `$\ell_2$`-minimizing formula to an entropy-maximizing one is what separates functional models from collapsed ones, not a marginal accuracy tweak.

**The paper also narrows the scope of the "extreme quantization for generation" problem in a productive way.** By establishing that ternary models are within 3–7 ROUGE points of full precision on summarization, the paper sets a concrete accuracy target for the subfield. The binary models' larger gap (9–12 points) identifies activation quantization as the harder sub-problem — binary-weight 8-bit-activation models nearly match ternary-weight 8-bit-activation models (Table 1: 33.30 vs. 34.51 RL on XSUM), but fully binary models drop substantially further (25.29 RL). This decomposition suggests that future research should prioritize activation quantization improvements over weight quantization improvements, since weight quantization to even 1 bit is already well-handled by TBT's statistics-based approach.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Ablating the 4/3 factor in the ternary weight scaling formula to test whether the entropy-maximization mechanism is causal or correlational.** The paper derives `$\alpha_T = 4/3 \cdot \|W^R - \mu_T\|_{\ell_1} / n$` from the property of uniform weight initialization — the 4/3 factor maps a uniform distribution on `$[-a, a]$` to `$[-1.5, 1.5]$` after scaling, producing equal-width intervals for the three ternary bins. This is elegant but empirically untested: the paper never sweeps the factor (e.g., 1.0, 1.33, 1.5, 2.0, or a learned scalar) to measure whether 4/3 is actually optimal or merely sufficient. A strong follow-up would train TBT ternary models on CNN/DailyMail with scaling factors `$\alpha_T = c \cdot \|W^R - \mu_T\|_{\ell_1} / n$` for `$c \in \{1.0, 1.2, 1.33, 1.5, 1.8, 2.0\}$` and measure both final ROUGE and the entropy of the resulting weight distributions (computed as `$-\sum p_i \log p_i$` across the three ternary levels). If ROUGE peaks sharply at `$c = 4/3$`, the entropy-maximization mechanism is confirmed as causal. If ROUGE is flat across a wide range of `$c$`, the key insight is the zero-centering and isometry, not the specific entropy target, and the 4/3 derivation is an interesting property rather than a necessary design choice. This experiment matters because it would distinguish between TBT's mathematical framing (entropy maximization is the objective) and a simpler alternative explanation (any scaling that prevents extreme bin imbalance works).

**Testing TBT on decoder-only architectures (GPT-2, LLaMA, or Mistral) for open-ended generation to determine whether the method generalizes beyond encoder-decoder summarization and translation.** The paper acknowledges this as an open problem (Section 5) but provides zero evidence. A strong follow-up would quantize a GPT-2-medium or LLaMA-7B model to ternary and binary using TBT's exact methodology, then evaluate on a standard suite: WikiText perplexity (to measure likelihood degradation), zero-shot downstream tasks (HellaSwag, PIQA, WinoGrande for reasoning; CNN/DailyMail in a prompting setup for summarization), and human evaluation of open-ended generation quality (coherence, factuality, repetition). The key measurements would be: (a) does TBT's training procedure converge for decoder-only models, or does causal attention introduce additional quantization sensitivity that the encoder-decoder structure masks? (b) how does the accuracy-efficiency tradeoff curve compare to encoder-decoder? (c) does the binary model produce the same failure signatures (shortened outputs, lower bigram overlap) on open-ended generation, or do new failure modes emerge (e.g., increased repetition, topic drift)? A negative result — TBT fails to converge or produces much larger accuracy gaps on decoder-only models — would be equally informative because it would identify architectural dependence as a boundary condition and motivate architecture-specific quantization research.

**Training a cheap difficulty-predicting classifier to replace the paper's implied but unmeasured "difficulty" of quantizing different layers.** The paper's Figure 2 and Section 3.6 show that TBT produces more uniform weight distributions and better activation coverage than baselines, but it never quantifies *which layers* benefit most from the entropy-maximizing and learned-activation mechanisms, or which layers remain bottlenecks even under TBT. A strong follow-up would measure per-layer quantization error (e.g., `$\ell_2$` reconstruction error, or KL divergence between full-precision and quantized activation distributions) and correlate it with overall model performance, then use these measurements to design **heterogeneous quantization**: apply TBT's full method only to the most sensitive layers while using cheaper fixed-formula quantization for robust layers. If, for example, the cross-attention layers in the decoder are disproportionately sensitive to quantization, a mixed-precision scheme (ternary for most layers, 4-bit for cross-attention) might recover most of the ternary model's efficiency with closer-to-full-precision accuracy. This direction is enabled by TBT because TBT provides the first setting where all layers *can* be ternary/binary, making it possible to isolate which layers are the binding constraints — in the baseline methods where the entire model collapses, per-layer analysis is meaningless because no layer receives useful gradients.

**Combining TBT's statistics-based weight quantization with learned activation quantization in a single jointly-optimized framework that learns both weight scaling factors and activation scaling factors end-to-end.** TBT makes a deliberate asymmetry: weight scaling factors are computed from statistics, activation scaling factors are learned. This design choice is justified conceptually (weights are stable, activations are input-dependent) but never tested against the alternative: learning both. A strong follow-up would add a learnable perturbation `$\Delta\alpha$` to the statistics-based `$\alpha_T$` and `$\alpha_B$` for weight quantization (or replace them entirely with learned parameters initialized to the statistics-based values), then train TBT models with fully learned weight quantization and compare convergence stability and final accuracy against the fixed-formula version. If learned weight quantization improves accuracy (suggesting the statistics-based formula is a good initialization but suboptimal final value), the conceptual justification for the asymmetry is weakened. If learned weight quantization destabilizes training or provides no gain, the paper's asymmetry is empirically validated. This experiment would also test whether the STE gradient through the weight quantization function (Eq. 10) can support end-to-end learning of weight scaling, or whether the weight quantizer's discrete nature makes gradient-based optimization of its parameters inherently noisy.

**Measuring the actual wall-clock speedup and energy consumption of TBT models on emulated ternary/binary hardware (bit-packing + custom CUDA kernels) versus the theoretical FLOPs ratios.** The paper's efficiency claims (16× for ternary, 32×/58× for binary) are purely theoretical and assume multiplication-free hardware. A strong follow-up would implement TBT's ternary and binary matrix multiplication using bit-packing and XNOR/popcount operations in custom CUDA kernels (or, more practically, using existing libraries like BitBLAS or the XNOR-Net CPU implementations) and measure: (a) actual throughput (tokens/second) versus full-precision BART on the same GPU, (b) end-to-end latency for generating a summary of average length, including the cost of unpacking weights, executing non-quantized operations (layer norm, Softmax, residual adds), and the autoregressive decoding loop, and (c) energy consumption (watts or joules per inference). This would convert the paper's theoretical efficiency claims into practical deployment numbers and identify where Amdahl's law bites — if layer norm and Softmax consume 80% of inference time even with perfectly accelerated matrix multiplications, the effective speedup is capped at 5× regardless of the 58× theoretical multiplier. This is not a criticism of the paper (which explicitly scopes hardware implementation out), but it is the most important follow-up for determining whether extreme quantization is practically worthwhile or remains a theoretical curiosity.

**Distillation-free training of TBT models to measure how much of the reported accuracy comes from the quantization scheme versus the teacher signal.** The paper's training procedure always uses knowledge distillation from the full-precision model, making it impossible to separate TBT's representational capacity from the benefits of a rich training signal. A strong follow-up would train TBT ternary and binary models on CNN/DailyMail from the pre-trained initialization but with only hard-label cross-entropy loss (no distillation), and compare the resulting ROUGE scores against the distillation-trained versions from Table 1. If the gap is small (e.g., ternary RL drops from 38.30 to 36–37), TBT's quantization scheme carries most of the weight and distillation is a helpful but non-essential add-on. If the gap is large (e.g., ternary RL drops to 25–30), distillation is doing heavy lifting — the quantized model may lack sufficient capacity to learn the task from hard labels alone, and the teacher's soft targets are providing a loss landscape smooth enough to compensate. The latter result would significantly narrow TBT's applicability, since distillation requires a full-precision teacher that may be unavailable or too expensive for the very large models TBT is meant to serve. This experiment also matters for the paper's stated ambition to apply TBT to GPT-3-scale models: if distillation is necessary, running a full-precision GPT-3 as a teacher defeats the purpose of quantizing the student for efficiency.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**On-device summarization for privacy-sensitive applications.** A ternary BART model at 39.6 MB (Table 1) can be stored and executed entirely on a smartphone or laptop without sending user data to cloud servers. For applications like summarizing personal emails, medical records, or confidential business documents, the privacy benefit of local-only processing is substantial. The ternary model's ROUGE-L of 38.30 on CNN/DailyMail (3.8 points behind full-precision BART at 42.09) represents a meaningful but likely acceptable quality tradeoff for gisting and triage use cases — a user scanning summarized email threads or document collections needs the gist, not publication-quality prose. The 16× theoretical FLOPs reduction, if realized on mobile NPUs or future hardware with ternary support, would make real-time summarization feasible within the thermal and battery constraints of a phone, which are disqualifying for full-precision BART inference.

**Real-time machine translation on consumer edge devices.** TBT's ternary mBART model achieves 21.70 BLEU on WMT16 En-Ro (Table 2) at 0.31 GB — a model that fits in the RAM of a mid-range smartphone. For travel and cross-language communication applications where cloud connectivity is unreliable or expensive (roaming data, remote areas, airplane mode), an on-device translation model that produces functional if imperfect translations is vastly preferable to no translation at all. The binary model at 17.59 BLEU and 0.16 GB pushes the storage requirement low enough for budget devices and IoT-class hardware. The key deployment scenario is offline-first translation apps that provide baseline quality locally and optionally query cloud models for refinement — TBT models serve as the local fallback that works everywhere.

**Efficient data generation for self-improvement and distillation pipelines at scale.** When using LLMs to generate training data — a paradigm increasingly central to alignment (RLHF preference data), distillation (teacher-student training), and data augmentation — the inference cost of the generator model can dominate the total compute budget. A ternary or binary generative model that is 4–16× cheaper per generated token (in theoretical FLOPs) could dramatically reduce the cost of large-scale synthetic data generation for tasks where the quality requirements are moderate. For example, generating silver-standard summarization training data from web-scraped article-summary pairs, or producing candidate translations for back-translation, could use a ternary TBT model to generate 16× more candidates within the same compute budget, then filter the outputs with a quality classifier. The key insight: for data generation, throughput often matters more than per-sample quality (since low-quality outputs can be filtered), making extreme quantization an attractive point in the tradeoff space.

**Low-power always-on language agents.** A binary TBT model at 23.2 MB and 0.0156× theoretical FLOPs enables generative language capabilities on hardware classes that cannot run any full-precision transformer at all — microcontrollers, wearables, smart sensors, assistive devices. While the binary model's accuracy (35.56 ROUGE-1 on CNN/DailyMail, 17.59 BLEU on En-Ro) is insufficient for high-stakes applications, it is more than adequate for command-and-control ("summarize today's notifications"), simple question answering, or structured output generation (e.g., a wearable that generates natural language summaries of sensor data for visually impaired users). The existence of a functional binary generative model at this size — the first ever demonstrated — opens a deployment class that was previously inaccessible to any transformer-based text generation. Specialized hardware implementing XNOR-based inference could push the energy per generated token low enough for battery-powered always-on devices that last days or weeks between charges.

### When to Prefer This Method

TBT is positioned as the first viable method for extreme quantization of generative transformers, not as one option among many mature alternatives. There are no competing methods that achieve functional fully ternary or binary generative models for TBT to be compared against — the baselines in Table 1 collapse entirely. The paper's positioning is therefore: **if you need a ternary or binary generative transformer, TBT is currently the only method that works.** The decision is not "TBT vs. alternative extreme quantization method" but rather "extreme quantization (via TBT) vs. other efficiency strategies."

The paper does articulate, implicitly through its results, a tradeoff between TBT at different bit-widths and full-precision models, though not against named alternative efficiency methods (beyond the BlockPruning comparison at matched size). The relevant decision points extractable from the paper's results are:

- **Prefer TBT ternary (2-2-2) when:** you need 16× FLOPs reduction and can tolerate a 3–7 ROUGE point gap from full-precision quality on summarization, or a 5 BLEU point gap on translation. This is the sweet spot where the efficiency-accuracy tradeoff is most favorable — the ternary model loses relatively little accuracy for a large efficiency gain.

- **Prefer TBT binary-weight 8-bit-activation (1-1-8) when:** extreme weight compression is the priority but activation precision can remain moderate. This configuration nearly matches the ternary-weight variant (within 1.2 RL on XSUM, 0.8 RL on CNN/DailyMail, 0.3 BLEU on En-Ro) while halving weight storage — an attractive point if memory (not compute) is the binding constraint, or if activations cannot be quantized below 8 bits on the target hardware.

- **Prefer TBT fully binary (1-1-1) when:** maximum theoretical efficiency (32× compression, up to 58× speedup with XNOR hardware) is the overriding requirement and substantial quality degradation is acceptable — e.g., for privacy-preserving on-device gisting, low-stakes content triage, or sensor data summarization where any functional output is better than no output.

- **Prefer full-precision or mildly quantized (8-bit) models when:** output quality is paramount, the task requires factual precision, or the deployment hardware supports full-precision inference with acceptable latency. TBT does not challenge the dominance of full-precision models for quality-sensitive applications — it expands the frontier of what is possible when efficiency constraints preclude full-precision deployment entirely.
