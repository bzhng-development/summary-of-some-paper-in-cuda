# OmniQuant: Omnidirectionally Calibrated Quantization for Large Language Models

**ArXiv:** [2308.13137](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.13137)

## 🎯 Pitch

This paper introduces **OmniQuant**, a differentiable post-training quantization technique for large language models that achieves quantization-aware training (QAT)-level performance while maintaining the time and data efficiency of PTQ methods.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **OmniQuant**, a differentiable post-training quantization technique for large language models that achieves quantization-aware training (QAT)-level performance while maintaining the time and data efficiency of PTQ methods. The approach is evaluated on the LLaMA, LLaMA-2, OPT, and Falcon model families using WikiText2 perplexity and zero-shot task accuracy, processing models from 7B to 70B on a single A100-40G GPU with only 128 training samples in 1–16 hours. OmniQuant comprises two learnable components — **Learnable Weight Clipping (LWC)** (optimizing the clipping threshold to modulate extreme weight values) and **Learnable Equivalent Transformation (LET)** (shifting quantization difficulty from activations to weights via differentiable channel-wise scaling and shifting across both linear layers and attention operations) — jointly optimized under a block-wise quantization error minimization framework. The method delivers consistent improvements across W4A16, W3A16, W2A16, W6A6, and W4A4 configurations, notably achieving +4.99% to +11.80% average zero-shot accuracy gains over prior PTQ methods at W4A4 and outperforming the QAT method LLM-QAT by +6.22% on LLaMA-7B, while achieving a 13.21 WikiText2 perplexity at W2A16 compared to GPTQ's 3832 — establishing that gradient-optimized quantization parameters can substitute for full weight tuning when the learnable parameters are constrained to clipping thresholds and mathematically equivalent transformations that fuse into the quantized model without extra inference overhead.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: LLMs Are Too Large to Deploy

The central tension this paper addresses is straightforward: large language models deliver remarkable performance across natural language tasks, but their immense memory and computational requirements create a massive barrier to practical deployment. The paper opens with a concrete example — GPT-3 requires 350 GB of memory just to load its parameters in FP16 format, which translates to at least five A100-80G GPUs for inference alone. This is not merely an inconvenience. It fundamentally limits who can use these models and in what contexts. On-device deployment, real-time applications, edge computing, and cost-sensitive services all become impractical when inference demands multiple high-end GPUs.

Quantization — reducing the numerical precision of model parameters — has emerged as the primary strategy to address this bottleneck. By representing weights (and potentially activations) with fewer bits, quantization shrinks the model's memory footprint and, when both weights and activations are quantized, enables faster low-bit matrix multiplication that reduces computational cost as well. The paper positions itself squarely within this space, aiming to push quantization further than prior methods — into the extremely low-bit regimes (W2A16, W4A4) where previous PTQ approaches break down.

### Why This Problem Matters: More Than Just Compression

The significance of quantization for LLMs extends beyond mere compression ratios. The paper highlights three dimensions that make this an urgent practical problem:

**Hardware constraints are non-negotiable.** Unlike training, which can be distributed across massive compute clusters, inference often happens under strict hardware limitations. A model that requires five A100 GPUs to run inference cannot be deployed on a smartphone, a laptop, or even many single-GPU servers. Quantization that enables a 70B-parameter model to fit on a single GPU fundamentally expands the deployment surface.

**The gap between QAT quality and PTQ practicality is wide and growing.** Quantization-aware training (QAT) — where the model is trained from scratch or fine-tuned with quantization simulated during training — can produce highly accurate quantized models. The paper cites LLM-QAT (Liu et al., 2023b) as a representative QAT method that achieves strong W4A4 results. However, QAT is staggeringly expensive for LLMs: LLM-QAT requires 100,000 training samples and hundreds of GPU hours to quantize LLaMA-13B. In contrast, PTQ methods like GPTQ can quantize the same model in about an hour using only 128 samples on a single GPU. The problem is that PTQ methods sacrifice substantial accuracy, especially in low-bit settings. This creates a frustrating tradeoff: you can have good quantization quality (QAT) or practical quantization speed (PTQ), but not both. The paper explicitly frames this as the motivating question:

> "can we attain the performance of QAT, while maintaining the time and data efficiency of PTQ?"

**Low-bit quantization is the frontier, and existing methods fail there.** The practical value of quantization increases as bits decrease — moving from 16-bit to 4-bit weight-only quantization cuts memory by roughly 4×, and quantizing activations as well (W4A4) additionally enables faster low-bit computation. However, the paper demonstrates (Figure 1, b & c) that existing PTQ methods experience catastrophic degradation at very low bit widths. For LLaMA-13B at W2A16, GPTQ produces a WikiText2 perplexity of 3832 (compared to 5.09 at FP16), rendering the model effectively useless. SmoothQuant and Outlier Suppression+ similarly collapse at W4A4. The paper's central claim is that this failure is not inherent to low-bit quantization — it is an artifact of *how* prior PTQ methods select their quantization parameters.

### What Prior Approaches Existed and Where They Fall Short

The paper categorizes existing LLM quantization methods along two axes: weight-only versus weight-activation, and QAT versus PTQ. The landscape it surveys is populated with methods that the paper argues share a common weakness: reliance on hand-crafted or heuristically-determined quantization parameters.

#### Weight-Only Quantization Methods

These methods quantize model weights while keeping activations at full precision, reducing memory but not computational cost:

- **GPTQ** (Frantar et al., 2022): Uses block-wise reconstruction to quantize weights to 3/4 bits. The paper acknowledges it as efficient (quantizing LLaMA-13B in about an hour) and sets it as a primary baseline. However, GPTQ fails catastrophically at 2-bit quantization (perplexity 3832 at W2A16 on LLaMA-13B), as shown in Figure 1.

- **AWQ** (Lin et al., 2023): Recognizes that weights associated with high-magnitude activations are disproportionately important and protects them through channel-wise scaling. The paper notes that AWQ determines its scaling parameters via grid search — a heuristic optimization that evaluates a discrete set of candidate values against a proxy metric. While effective at moderate bit widths (the paper shows AWQ is competitive at W4A16 and W3A16 with group-wise quantization), the hand-crafted nature of the grid search limits performance at extreme compression.

- **SpQR, OWQ, and SqueezeLLM**: Methods that use mixed-precision quantization to protect critical weights or non-uniform quantization to allocate bits more flexibly. The paper acknowledges these achieve strong results but notes they are limited to weight-only quantization and cannot extend to weight-activation settings.

#### Weight-Activation Quantization Methods

These quantize both weights and activations, enabling faster low-bit matrix multiplication:

- **SmoothQuant** (Xiao et al., 2023): Introduces a mathematically equivalent channel-wise scaling transformation that migrates quantization difficulty from activations to weights. The scaling factor is determined by a pre-defined migration strength $ \alpha $, computed from the ratio of activation and weight ranges. The paper credits SmoothQuant with achieving near-lossless W8A8 quantization but shows it degrades severely at W4A4 (Table 2: LLaMA-7B average accuracy drops from 64.09 at FP16 to 38.41 at W4A4 SmoothQuant).

- **Outlier Suppression+** (Wei et al., 2023): Extends SmoothQuant by adding channel-wise shifting alongside scaling, and uses grid search to determine scaling parameters (while keeping shifting pre-defined). The paper shows this improves over SmoothQuant at W4A4 (48.43 average accuracy on LLaMA-7B vs. 38.41), but still leaves significant room for improvement compared to OmniQuant's 52.65.

- **RPTQ** (Yuan et al., 2023): Achieves W4A4 quantization but uses group-wise activation quantization, which the paper argues is deployment-unfriendly compared to per-token quantization (the standard approach that maps cleanly to hardware). This is a critical practical concern — a quantization scheme that doesn't align with hardware capabilities loses much of its speed advantage.

- **LLM-QAT** (Liu et al., 2023b): The representative QAT method. Achieves competitive W4A4 accuracy (46.43 when combined with SmoothQuant on LLaMA-7B) but at enormous cost: 100k samples and 90+ GPU hours. The paper positions this as the "ceiling" that PTQ methods should aspire to match.

#### The Common Weakness: Hand-Crafted Parameters

The paper's core critique of the PTQ landscape is that all these methods rely on **hand-crafted quantization parameters** — migration strengths, scaling factors, and clipping thresholds that are either pre-defined by heuristic formulas or selected via crude grid search over a limited set of options. The paper argues this is fundamentally limiting because:

1. **The optimal parameters are model-specific and configuration-specific.** A scaling factor that works for W4A16 may be suboptimal for W2A16. A migration strength calibrated for OPT may not transfer to LLaMA. Hand-crafted approaches cannot adapt to this variation.

2. **Grid search is inherently limited.** Even when grid search replaces pre-defined formulas (as in AWQ and Outlier Suppression+), it can only explore a discrete, pre-specified set of values. The optimization space for quantization parameters is continuous and high-dimensional, and grid search necessarily misses the optimum unless it gets lucky.

3. **The interactions between different quantization choices are complex.** Weight clipping, activation scaling, and activation shifting all interact — changing the clipping threshold alters the effective weight distribution, which changes the optimal scaling factor for activations. Grid search and heuristic methods cannot capture these interactions because they typically optimize parameters sequentially or independently.

### How OmniQuant Positions Itself

The paper positions OmniQuant as a **third way** between PTQ and QAT. Rather than abandoning gradient optimization (like PTQ) or optimizing all model weights (like QAT), OmniQuant **freezes the original full-precision weights** and only optimizes a small set of additional quantization parameters. This is the key conceptual move: it preserves the efficiency of PTQ (because the full weights don't need to be updated, and the calibration dataset can be tiny — 128 samples) while gaining the performance benefits of gradient-based optimization (because the quantization parameters are learned end-to-end through backpropagation rather than heuristically prescribed).

The paper explicitly contrasts its approach with QAT:

> "Unlike Quantization-Aware Training (QAT) which involves cumbersome weight optimization, OmniQuant freezes the original full-precision weight and only incorporates a few learnable quantization parameters."

And with existing PTQ:

> "although these methods have achieved certain success... they often lead to suboptimal performance and fail to deal with extremely low-bit quantization due to the crude design of hand-crafted quantization parameters such as migration strength and scaling factors."

The two learnable components — LWC and LET — are designed to be **fusable**: after optimization, the learned clipping thresholds and equivalent transformation parameters can be absorbed into the quantized weights, meaning the final quantized model has zero additional parameters or operations compared to a standard MinMax-quantized model. This is crucial for hardware compatibility and deployment, and the paper emphasizes it repeatedly as a design principle.

The paper also positions OmniQuant as **unified framework** that works for both weight-only and weight-activation quantization — a distinction that prior methods typically didn't bridge. SmoothQuant and Outlier Suppression+ were designed for weight-activation quantization; AWQ and GPTQ were designed for weight-only. OmniQuant's LWC and LET can be toggled independently (e.g., for LLaMA weight-only, only LWC is used because LET provides negligible benefit, per Appendix Table A3), making it a single technique applicable across the full spectrum of quantization configurations.

### The Quantization Challenge Deconstructed

To motivate its technical approach, the paper identifies two specific difficulties that make LLM quantization hard:

**1. Activation outliers.** Certain channels in the activation tensors have systematically much larger magnitudes than others (by factors of 10–100×, as visualized in Figure A2). These outlier channels dominate the quantization range, causing most regular channels to be quantized to very few effective bins, destroying their information content. The paper notes that prior methods (SmoothQuant, Outlier Suppression+) attempt to address this by mathematically equivalent transformations that shift the quantization difficulty from activations to weights, but their hand-crafted parameters limit effectiveness.

**2. Weight quantization sensitivity.** Not all weights are equally impactful on model output. Weights connected to high-magnitude activations are disproportionately important, making their quantization errors more harmful. Prior methods address this through mixed-precision (keeping some weights at higher precision) or channel-wise scaling (protecting sensitive weight channels), but again with heuristic parameter selection.

The paper's insight is that both of these challenges can be addressed through **learnable parameters** that are optimized jointly under a unified objective — minimizing the block-wise output error between the full-precision and quantized model. LWC addresses weight sensitivity by learning per-channel (or per-group) clipping thresholds. LET addresses activation outliers by learning channel-wise scaling and shifting transformations. Together, operating under gradient descent, they find better parameter configurations than any hand-crafted approach can achieve.

### The Practical Deployment Context

The paper grounds its motivation in concrete deployment realities. It is not merely pursuing better perplexity numbers — it is targeting the practical scenario where a practitioner wants to quantize a large model on limited hardware for real-world inference. This is reflected in several design choices:

- **Single GPU constraint.** The paper emphasizes that OmniQuant processes models from 7B to 70B on a single A100-40G GPU. This is not an incidental detail; it is a statement about accessibility. Methods requiring multi-GPU quantization (or, worse, multi-GPU QAT) exclude a large fraction of potential users.

- **Minimal data requirements.** Using only 128 training samples (2048-token segments from WikiText2) makes OmniQuant practical for scenarios where large calibration datasets are unavailable or where data privacy limits what can be used.

- **Hardware compatibility.** The paper repeatedly emphasizes that all learned parameters are fused into the quantized model, requiring no custom kernels or mixed-precision operations at inference time. The deployment experiments in Section 4.5 use MLC-LLM — an existing deployment framework — without any modifications, validating this claim.

- **Comprehensive model coverage.** Testing across OPT (125M–66B), LLaMA (7B–65B), LLaMA-2 (7B–70B), Falcon-180B, and LLaMA-2-chat demonstrates that the method is not tuned to a single model family's peculiarities.

### The Contradiction OmniQuant Resolves

The paper implicitly identifies and resolves a contradiction in the quantization literature. On one hand, gradient-based optimization is known to be effective for determining quantization parameters — methods like AdaRound and BRECQ use it successfully for smaller models. On the other hand, prior attempts to apply gradient-based PTQ to LLMs either failed (because optimizing all quantization parameters jointly is intractable at billion-parameter scale) or were abandoned in favor of training-free approaches. The paper's resolution is to recognize that you don't need to optimize everything — you only need to optimize a carefully chosen, small set of parameters that control the quantizer's behavior (clipping thresholds and equivalent transformations), while leaving the original weights frozen. This constrains the optimization problem sufficiently to make it tractable with simple SGD, while still providing enough flexibility to substantially outperform hand-crafted approaches.

This framing — "freeze the weights, learn the quantizer" — is the paper's fundamental methodological contribution, and it positions OmniQuant as a bridge between the PTQ and QAT paradigms rather than as just another PTQ method.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

OmniQuant is a **post-training quantization pipeline** that takes a pre-trained full-precision LLM and produces a low-bit quantized version ready for deployment on memory-constrained hardware. The system solves the problem of how to determine optimal quantization parameters — clipping thresholds, scaling factors, and shifting offsets — without the enormous computational cost of quantization-aware training and without the suboptimal performance of hand-crafted heuristics. The "shape" of the solution is a **block-wise differentiable optimization** framework: the full-precision weights are frozen, a small set of learnable quantization-control parameters is introduced, and these are optimized via gradient descent to minimize the reconstruction error between the full-precision and quantized model's output at each transformer block, using only 128 calibration samples.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The OmniQuant pipeline processes a transformer-based LLM block by block, starting from the embedding layer and proceeding through each transformer block sequentially to the output head. For each block, the system has four interacting components:

1. **The frozen full-precision block** — the original transformer block with its pre-trained weights, held constant and used only to compute target outputs for the reconstruction loss.

2. **Learnable Equivalent Transformation (LET) parameters** — a set of channel-wise scaling factors `$s$`, channel-wise shifting offsets `$\delta$`, and attention-specific scaling factors `$s_a$` that mathematically transform the activation distribution to be more quantization-friendly. These parameters are learned and applied to both the linear layers and the attention operations within the block.

3. **Learnable Weight Clipping (LWC) parameters** — a pair of learnable clipping strengths `$\gamma$` and `$\beta$` per weight tensor that determine the optimal dynamic range for weight quantization by scaling the observed minimum and maximum values.

4. **The reconstruction loss** — a simple `$\ell_2$` distance between the full-precision block's output and the quantized block's output, computed on a small batch of calibration data, providing the training signal for gradient descent.

Information flows through the system as follows: a calibration sample enters a transformer block → LET parameters transform the full-precision weights and activations via mathematically equivalent operations → LWC parameters determine the clipping range for weight quantization → both transformed weights and transformed activations are quantized using MinMax quantizers → the quantized block produces its output → the `$\ell_2$` distance between this output and the full-precision block's output is computed → gradients flow back to update only `$\Theta_1$` (LWC parameters) and `$\Theta_2$` (LET parameters), leaving the original weights unchanged → after optimization converges, the LET transformation parameters are fused into the adjacent layers' weights, and the LWC thresholds determine the final quantized weight values, producing a standard quantized model with zero extra parameters or operations.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First, the block-wise optimization framework** (Section 3.4.1): the overall objective function, why block-wise rather than layer-wise or global, and what makes this tractable at billion-parameter scale.
- **Second, the Learnable Weight Clipping mechanism** (Section 3.4.2): how it parameterizes the clipping threshold, the specific parameterization using `$\gamma$` and `$\beta$`, why this form is chosen over direct threshold learning, and how it degrades to standard MinMax when the clipping strengths are 1.
- **Third, the Learnable Equivalent Transformation** (Section 3.4.3): the linear layer transformation (channel-wise scaling and shifting), the attention operation transformation (scaling of Q and K), why the second FFN linear layer is excluded, and how all learned parameters are fused into adjacent weights.
- **Fourth, the quantizers and the training procedure** (Section 3.4.4): the specific quantizers used (MinMax for activations, LWC-enhanced MinMax for weights), the optimizer configuration (AdamW, learning rates 5e-3 for LWC and 1e-2 for LET), the calibration data (128 segments of 2048 tokens from WikiText2), the training regime (20 epochs for most configurations, 40 for W2A16, batch size 1), and initialization of LET parameters from SmoothQuant and Outlier Suppression+.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is a **methodology paper** that introduces a differentiable optimization framework for post-training quantization of LLMs. The core idea is that by freezing the original full-precision weights and introducing only a carefully constrained set of learnable parameters — clipping strengths for weight quantization and channel-wise scaling/shifting factors for activation quantization — the system can achieve QAT-level quantization quality while maintaining PTQ-level computational efficiency. The optimization is performed block-by-block rather than globally, making it tractable for models with billions of parameters.

#### 3.4.1 Block-Wise Quantization Error Minimization

The central optimization objective that drives the entire OmniQuant pipeline is a block-wise reconstruction error minimization, formalized in Equation (1) of the paper:

$$\arg\min_{\Theta_1, \Theta_2} \left|\left| \mathcal{F}(\mathbf{W}, \mathbf{X}) - \mathcal{F}\left(Q_w(\mathbf{W}; \Theta_1, \Theta_2), Q_a(\mathbf{X}, \Theta_2)\right) \right|\right|$$

where `$\mathcal{F}$` is the mapping function for a single transformer block in the LLM, `$\mathbf{W}$` and `$\mathbf{X}$` are the full-precision weight and activation input to that block, `$Q_w(\cdot)$` is the weight quantizer, `$Q_a(\cdot)$` is the activation quantizer, `$\Theta_1$` represents the learnable weight clipping parameters (the clipping strengths `$\gamma$` and `$\beta$` from Equation 2), and `$\Theta_2$` represents the learnable equivalent transformation parameters (the scaling factors `$s$`, shifting offsets `$\delta$`, and attention scaling factors `$s_a$` from Equations 3 and 5).

**What it computes:** the `$\ell_2$` norm of the difference between two outputs — the output of the original full-precision transformer block when processing the calibration input `$\mathbf{X}$`, and the output of the same block after its weights have been quantized (with clipping parameters `$\Theta_1$`) and its activations have been transformed and quantized (with equivalent transformation parameters `$\Theta_2$`). The optimization searches over the joint space of `$\Theta_1$` and `$\Theta_2$` to make the quantized block mimic the full-precision block as closely as possible on the calibration data. Operationally: a batch of calibration activations enters both the frozen full-precision block and the quantized block, the Euclidean distance between their output tensors is computed, and gradients of this distance with respect to `$\Theta_1$` and `$\Theta_2$` are backpropagated through the quantizers (using straight-through estimation for the rounding operation) to update the learnable parameters.

**Why block-wise:** the paper explicitly contrasts this with prior gradient-based PTQ methods like AdaRound and BRECQ, which attempted to optimize quantization parameters globally across all layers simultaneously. At the scale of LLMs (7B to 70B parameters), the global optimization space is too large to be tractable — the paper states that these prior methods "cannot be applied in models with billions of parameters because they are hard to optimize due to the huge solution space." Block-wise optimization decomposes the problem into independent subproblems of manageable size. Each transformer block is quantized sequentially: the calibration data is passed through the already-quantized previous blocks to produce input activations for the current block, then `$\Theta_1$` and `$\Theta_2$` for the current block are optimized, then the block is permanently quantized with the learned parameters, and the process advances to the next block. This sequential dependency means that later blocks receive inputs that already reflect the quantization errors of earlier blocks, which is important because the optimization can compensate for accumulated error rather than assuming clean inputs.

**Why this particular objective:** minimizing the `$\ell_2$` distance between full-precision and quantized block outputs is a local proxy for the global objective of preserving the model's final output distribution. It is tractable because it only requires forward and backward passes through a single block at a time. The paper argues it is also sufficient because the block-wise errors accumulate additively through the network, so minimizing each local error independently tends to minimize the global error. An alternative — minimizing the final output error directly (as in end-to-end QAT) — would require holding the entire model in memory and backpropagating through all layers, which is exactly what makes QAT expensive.

**Why learnable parameters rather than weight tuning:** the paper makes a crucial distinction between its approach and prior gradient-based PTQ methods. Methods like AdaRound and BRECQ optimize the rounding of individual weights or learn small additive perturbations to weights. For LLMs, this means optimizing billions of parameters per block, which is still computationally heavy. OmniQuant reduces the optimization to tens of parameters per layer — a pair of clipping strengths per weight tensor for LWC, and a small number of scaling/shifting vectors per layer for LET. The paper states:

> "OmniQuant only determines a few quantization parameters with optimality, which is easier than optimizing the whole weights in previous PTQ-based methods."

This is the key to efficiency: the number of parameters being optimized is independent of the model size (it scales with the number of layers and channels, not the number of weight elements), so the optimization cost does not explode as models grow larger. The paper supports this empirically in Table A12, showing that LLaMA-65B weight-activation quantization completes in 14.4 hours on a single A100, compared to hundreds of GPU hours for QAT.

**The block-wise calibration algorithm.** The complete procedure is detailed in Algorithm 1 of the Appendix and proceeds as follows:

- **Input:** a calibration dataset `$\mathbf{X}$` (128 sequences of 2048 tokens from WikiText2) and a pre-trained LLM `$\mathcal{M}$`.
- **Initialization:** the calibration data is duplicated — one copy `$\mathbf{X}_{fp}$` passes through the full-precision model to compute target outputs, and one copy `$\mathbf{X}_q$` passes through the progressively quantized model.
- **For each transformer block `$\mathcal{B}_i$` in the model:**
  1. Compute the full-precision output `$\mathbf{X}_{fp} = \mathcal{B}_i(\mathbf{X}_{fp})$` — this is the target for reconstruction.
  2. Initialize the learnable weight clipping parameters `$\Theta_1$` (as described in Section 3.4.2) and the learnable equivalent transformation parameters `$\Theta_2$` (initialized from SmoothQuant and Outlier Suppression+ heuristics, as described in Section 3.4.4).
  3. **Training loop (20 epochs, or 40 for W2A16):** for each mini-batch of calibration data:
     - Apply LET to the current block using the current `$\Theta_2$`, producing a transformed block `$\mathcal{B}'_i$` where the original weights are modified by the scaling and shifting factors (Equation 3) and the attention operations are modified (Equation 5), but the mathematical equivalence to the original block is preserved.
     - Apply quantization with LWC to the transformed block using the current `$\Theta_1$`, producing a quantized version of `$\mathcal{B}'_i$` where the weights are clipped and discretized according to Equation (2).
     - Pass the quantized calibration input through this quantized transformed block to obtain `$\mathbf{x}'_q = \mathcal{B}'_i(\mathbf{x}_q)$`.
     - Compute the `$\ell_2$` reconstruction loss: `$||\mathbf{x}_{fp} - \mathbf{x}'_q||^2$`.
     - Backpropagate the loss and update `$\Theta_1$` and `$\Theta_2$` via the AdamW optimizer. The original weights `$\mathbf{W}$` are never updated.
  4. After training converges, permanently apply LET with the learned `$\Theta_2$` to the block (absorbing the scaling/shifting factors into adjacent weights), then permanently quantize the weights with LWC using the learned `$\Theta_1$`.
  5. Update the quantized calibration input: `$\mathbf{X}_q = \mathcal{B}_i(\mathbf{X}_q)$` using the now-quantized block.
- **Output:** the fully quantized model, where every transformer block has been sequentially transformed and quantized.

The paper emphasizes the empirical efficiency of this procedure:

> "all models from the LLaMA-2 family (Touvron et al., 2023b) can be quantized on a single A100-40G GPU utilizing only 128 training samples."

#### 3.4.2 Learnable Weight Clipping (LWC)

The Learnable Weight Clipping module addresses the weight quantization challenge by determining the optimal dynamic range for weight quantization through gradient-based optimization of a clipping threshold. The key insight is that uniform quantization (such as standard MinMax) assigns equal representation capacity to all values within the observed range, but for LLM weights — which typically have flat, approximately Gaussian distributions with occasional moderate outliers — it is often beneficial to clip a small fraction of extreme values to allocate more quantization bins to the dense central region.

**The LWC parameterization.** Rather than directly learning an absolute clipping threshold (as in methods like PACT and LSQ, which the paper shows in Table A14 produce unsatisfactory results for LLM quantization), LWC parameterizes the clipping threshold relative to the observed weight range:

$$\mathbf{W}_q = \text{clamp}\left(\left\lfloor \frac{\mathbf{W}}{h} \right\rceil + z, \; 0, \; 2^N - 1\right), \quad \text{where} \quad h = \frac{\gamma \max(\mathbf{W}) - \beta \min(\mathbf{W})}{2^N - 1}, \quad z = -\left\lfloor \frac{\beta \min(\mathbf{W})}{h} \right\rceil$$

where `$\lfloor \cdot \rceil$` is the round-to-nearest operation, `$N$` is the target bit-width (e.g., 2, 3, 4), `$\mathbf{W}_q$` is the quantized weight tensor, `$\mathbf{W}$` is the full-precision weight tensor, `$h$` is the normalization factor (the step size — the distance between adjacent quantization levels), `$z$` is the zero-point offset (which maps the value zero in the full-precision domain to its corresponding integer in the quantized domain), `$\gamma \in [0, 1]$` is the learnable clipping strength for the upper bound (controlling how much of the maximum weight value is considered in the quantization range), and `$\beta \in [0, 1]$` is the learnable clipping strength for the lower bound (controlling how much of the minimum weight value is considered).

**What the equation computes:** it performs asymmetric uniform quantization of the weight tensor `$\mathbf{W}$` with a learned clipping range. First, the effective quantization range is computed as `$[\beta \min(\mathbf{W}), \gamma \max(\mathbf{W})]$` — if `$\beta = 1$` and `$\gamma = 1$`, the range equals the full observed min-to-max span (standard MinMax quantization); if `$\gamma < 1$`, the upper tail of the weight distribution is clipped (values above `$\gamma \max(\mathbf{W})$` are saturated to the maximum quantization level); if `$\beta < 1$`, the lower tail is clipped. The step size `$h$` divides this effective range into `$2^N - 1$` equal intervals. The zero-point `$z$` ensures that the value zero in the original weight space maps exactly to an integer quantization level (asymmetric quantization). Finally, each weight value is divided by `$h$`, rounded to the nearest integer, shifted by `$z$`, and clamped to the valid integer range `$[0, 2^N - 1]$` for unsigned `$N$`-bit representation.

**Why this parameterization:** the paper identifies three reasons for this specific design:

1. **Reduced optimization difficulty relative to direct threshold learning.** PACT and LSQ directly optimize the clipping threshold or the quantization scale, which means the optimizer must search over an absolute value space whose scale varies dramatically across layers and channels. LWC instead optimizes `$\gamma$` and `$\beta$` as *relative* factors in `$[0, 1]$`, which have a consistent, bounded domain across all layers and all models. The paper states: "By inheriting the benefits of MinMax quantization, LWC only needs to adjust the clipping strengths to determine an optimal clipping threshold, which would reduce the optimization difficulty."

2. **Robustness to changing weight distributions during joint optimization with LET.** This is a subtle but critical point. When LET is active (in weight-activation quantization), the equivalent transformation modifies the weight distribution at each training iteration — scaling and shifting factors are updated, which changes `$\max(\mathbf{W})$` and `$\min(\mathbf{W})$`. The LWC parameterization adapts automatically because `$\gamma$` and `$\beta$` are relative to the *current* observed range. In contrast, absolute threshold methods (PACT, LSQ) would need to track a moving target. The paper demonstrates this empirically in Figure A5 and Table A14: "LWC defines relative scaling values instead of absolute metrics, making it proficient in handling changes in weight distribution" while "PACT and LSQ failed."

3. **Smooth degradation to MinMax as a special case.** When `$\gamma = 1$` and `$\beta = 1$`, LWC produces exactly the same quantization as the standard MinMax scheme used in prior work (SmoothQuant, GPTQ). This means the learned parameters have a clear interpretation — they indicate *how much* clipping is beneficial, with 1.0 meaning "no clipping needed" and lower values meaning "aggressive clipping beneficial." The paper's visualizations in Figure A1 show that the learned clipping scales are concentrated near 1.0 for group-wise quantization (where the groups are small and clipping is rarely beneficial) but spread toward lower values for per-channel quantization at very low bit-widths.

**Instantiation details.** The clipping strengths `$\gamma$` and `$\beta$` are parameterized through the sigmoid function: `$\gamma = \text{sigmoid}(t_\gamma)$` and `$\beta = \text{sigmoid}(t_\beta)$`, where `$t_\gamma$` and `$t_\beta$` are unconstrained real-valued parameters that the optimizer updates. The sigmoid maps these to `$[0, 1]$`, ensuring the clipping strengths never exceed the valid range. These are the parameters that constitute `$\Theta_1 = \{\gamma, \beta\}$` in the block-wise objective (Equation 1). Each weight tensor that is quantized gets its own independent pair of `$\gamma$` and `$\beta$` — for per-channel quantization, this means one pair per output channel; for group-wise quantization, one pair per group.

**Effectiveness evidence.** Table 1 in the paper shows that LWC (when combined with the block-wise optimization framework, operating as the OmniQuant weight-only pipeline) significantly outperforms GPTQ and AWQ at low bit-widths. For instance, at W2A16 on LLaMA-13B, OmniQuant achieves a WikiText2 perplexity of 13.21 compared to GPTQ's 3832 and AWQ's `$2.8 \times 10^5$` (with group size 128). The gap narrows at higher bit-widths — at W4A16, the improvements are marginal (OmniQuant 5.17 vs. AWQ 5.20 on LLaMA-13B g128) — suggesting that the clipping optimization is most impactful when the quantization is most aggressive and every bit of representation capacity matters.

Table A13 in the Appendix provides mechanistic evidence: LWC reduces the `$\ell_1$` distance between quantized and full-precision weights (e.g., from 0.0089 to 0.0082 at W2A16g128 for LLaMA-7B), and more importantly, it substantially reduces the `$\ell_1$` distance between the *output activations* of the quantized versus full-precision blocks (from 3.24 to 1.36 at W2A16g128). This supports the paper's claim that LWC "can effectively balance quantization precision between outlier and regular values" — by slightly sacrificing the representation of extreme weight values, it preserves the information that flows through the network.

**Visualization of learned clipping behavior (Figure A1).** The paper analyzes the distribution of learned clipping scales across different quantization configurations. For per-channel W3A16 quantization, the clipping scales approximately follow a normal distribution centered away from 1.0, indicating that roughly half the channels benefit from some degree of clipping. For group-wise quantization (W3A16g128, W2A16g128), the clipping scales exhibit a long-tailed distribution concentrated near 1.0, with most groups requiring minimal clipping but a few benefiting substantially. Lower bit-widths show more aggressive clipping: at W2A16g128, 50% of groups have a clipping scale larger than 0.95, whereas at W3A16g128 this rises to 70%, meaning that 2-bit quantization necessitates clipping a larger fraction of extreme values to maintain representation quality.

#### 3.4.3 Learnable Equivalent Transformation (LET)

The Learnable Equivalent Transformation addresses the activation quantization challenge — specifically, the presence of systematic outlier channels in activation tensors that have magnitudes 10–100× larger than typical channels. These outliers dominate the quantization range, causing the majority of channels to be collapsed into very few effective bins. LET's core strategy is to perform mathematically equivalent transformations on the model that redistribute the quantization difficulty: channel-wise scaling squeezes outlier activation channels and correspondingly expands the associated weight channels, while channel-wise shifting centers activations to better align with the quantizer's representable range.

**The linear layer transformation.** For a standard linear layer with input `$\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times C_{in}}$` (where `$T$` is the token length and `$C_{in}$` is the input feature dimension), weight matrix `$\mathbf{W} \in \mathbb{R}^{C_{in} \times C_{out}}$`, bias vector `$\mathbf{B} \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times C_{out}}$`, and output `$\mathbf{Y} \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times C_{out}}$`, the original computation is:

$$\mathbf{Y} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W} + \mathbf{B}$$

The LET introduces channel-wise scaling factors `$\mathbf{s} \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times C_{in}}$` and channel-wise shifting offsets `$\boldsymbol{\delta} \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times C_{in}}$` to create a mathematically equivalent transformed layer, as expressed in Equation (3):

$$\mathbf{Y} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W} + \mathbf{B} = \underbrace{\left[(\mathbf{X} - \boldsymbol{\delta}) \oslash \mathbf{s}\right]}_{\tilde{\mathbf{X}}} \cdot \underbrace{\left[\mathbf{s} \odot \mathbf{W}\right]}_{\tilde{\mathbf{W}}} + \underbrace{\left[\mathbf{B} + \boldsymbol{\delta}\mathbf{W}\right]}_{\tilde{\mathbf{B}}}$$

where `$\oslash$` and `$\odot$` denote element-wise division and multiplication respectively (with broadcasting: `$\mathbf{s}$` and `$\boldsymbol{\delta}$` have shape `$1 \times C_{in}$` and are broadcast across the token dimension `$T$`), `$\tilde{\mathbf{X}}$` is the equivalent (transformed) activation, `$\tilde{\mathbf{W}}$` is the equivalent weight matrix, and `$\tilde{\mathbf{B}}$` is the equivalent bias.

**What the equation computes:** it rewrites the linear layer computation into a form where the activations are first centered (by subtracting `$\boldsymbol{\delta}$`) and then scaled (by dividing by `$\mathbf{s}$`), while the weights are correspondingly scaled (multiplied by `$\mathbf{s}$` along the input dimension) and the bias is adjusted (by adding `$\boldsymbol{\delta}\mathbf{W}$`). The mathematical equivalence is verified by expanding the transformed computation:

$$\tilde{\mathbf{X}}\tilde{\mathbf{W}} + \tilde{\mathbf{B}} = [(\mathbf{X} - \boldsymbol{\delta}) \oslash \mathbf{s}] \cdot [\mathbf{s} \odot \mathbf{W}] + [\mathbf{B} + \boldsymbol{\delta}\mathbf{W}]$$
$$= (\mathbf{X} - \boldsymbol{\delta})\mathbf{W} + \mathbf{B} + \boldsymbol{\delta}\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W} - \boldsymbol{\delta}\mathbf{W} + \mathbf{B} + \boldsymbol{\delta}\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W} + \mathbf{B}$$

**Why this form:** the transformation is designed to make activations more quantization-friendly while keeping the layer's input-output function identical. The scaling factors `$\mathbf{s}$` can be chosen to be large for outlier channels (those with systematically large activation magnitudes) and small for regular channels. When `$s_i$` is large (say, 10.0) for an outlier channel `$i$`, the corresponding activation `$\tilde{X}_i = (X_i - \delta_i) / s_i$` is compressed by a factor of 10, bringing it into the same magnitude range as other channels. Simultaneously, the corresponding weight row `$\tilde{\mathbf{W}}_{i,:} = s_i \mathbf{W}_{i,:}$` is amplified by a factor of 10. This shifts the quantization difficulty from the activation (where the outlier was causing problems) to the weight (where the amplified values may now be harder to quantize). However, this is exactly where LWC becomes valuable — the LWC component can learn to clip the amplified weight values appropriately, managing the difficulty that was shifted. This synergy between LET and LWC is central to OmniQuant's design: LET makes activations easy to quantize at the expense of making weights harder to quantize; LWC handles the harder-to-quantize weights by learning optimal clipping.

The shifting offsets `$\boldsymbol{\delta}$` serve a different purpose: they center the activation distribution to better align with the quantizer's dynamic range. For asymmetric quantization (which uses a zero-point), activations that are centered around zero use the quantizer's representable range more efficiently than activations that are offset. The equivalent transformation absorbs the shifting cost into the bias term `$\tilde{\mathbf{B}}$`.

**Fusion into adjacent layers.** A critical property of LET is that after optimization, the transformed weights and biases `$\tilde{\mathbf{W}}$` and `$\tilde{\mathbf{B}}$` can be directly used as the quantized layer's parameters, and the scaling and shifting applied to `$\mathbf{X}$` can be absorbed into the preceding layer's normalization or linear transformation. Specifically, if the preceding operation is a LayerNorm or RMSNorm, its output scale can be adjusted to incorporate `$\mathbf{s}$` and `$\boldsymbol{\delta}$`. If the preceding operation is a linear layer, its weight matrix can be post-multiplied by `$\text{diag}(\mathbf{s})$` and its bias adjusted by `$\boldsymbol{\delta}$`. The paper emphasizes this repeatedly:

> "the scaling and shifting parameters in `$\tilde{\mathbf{X}}$` can be absorbed into the previous normalization or linear layer and the scaling factors in `$\tilde{\mathbf{W}}$` can be fused into the original linear weight `$\mathbf{W}$`. Therefore, the equivalent transformation in Eqn.(3) can effectively reduce quantization errors without introducing additional parameters or costs."

This fusability is what makes OmniQuant a PTQ method rather than a fine-tuning method — the final quantized model is structurally identical to a standard MinMax-quantized model with no extra operations.

**Where LET is applied — and where it isn't.** The paper applies the linear-layer LET to all linear layers in the transformer block *except* the second linear layer of the feed-forward network (the down-projection after the activation function). The paper provides a specific rationale:

> "This may be because the high sparsity of features after the non-linear layer leads to unstable gradients when applying learnable equivalent transformations."

In transformer architectures, the FFN typically consists of an up-projection (expanding the hidden dimension), a non-linear activation (GELU, SiLU, etc.), and a down-projection. The activation function introduces sparsity — many elements become zero or near-zero — and the distribution of non-zero elements may be highly irregular. Applying LET's channel-wise scaling and shifting to the down-projection input would mean learning transformations on a sparse, irregular distribution, which the paper found empirically leads to unstable optimization. This is a practical design choice driven by optimization dynamics rather than architectural constraints.

**The attention operation transformation.** Beyond linear layers, the paper also applies equivalent transformation to the query-key multiplication within self-attention. The attention affinity computation is:

$$\mathbf{P} = \text{Softmax}(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^T)$$

where `$\mathbf{Q}, \mathbf{K} \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times C_{out}}$` are the query and key matrices (after projection from the input). The LET introduces a channel-wise scaling factor `$\mathbf{s}_a \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times C_{out}}$` to create an equivalent computation, as expressed in Equation (5):

$$\mathbf{P} = \text{Softmax}(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^T) = \text{Softmax}\left(\underbrace{(\mathbf{Q} \oslash \mathbf{s}_a)}_{\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}} \cdot \underbrace{(\mathbf{s}_a \odot \mathbf{K}^T)}_{\tilde{\mathbf{K}}^T}\right)$$

**What it computes:** it divides the query matrix by `$\mathbf{s}_a$` (channel-wise, broadcasting across tokens) and multiplies the key matrix by `$\mathbf{s}_a$` (again channel-wise, before the transpose). Inside the softmax, the product `$\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\tilde{\mathbf{K}}^T$` is identical to `$\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^T$` because the scaling factor cancels: for any element `$(i, j)$` of the product, `$(\mathbf{Q}_{i,:} \oslash \mathbf{s}_a) \cdot (\mathbf{s}_a \odot \mathbf{K}_{j,:})^T = \sum_k (Q_{i,k} / s_{a,k}) \cdot (s_{a,k} \cdot K_{j,k}) = \sum_k Q_{i,k} K_{j,k}$`. The softmax is then invariant to this transformation, producing exactly the same attention weights as the original computation.

**Why include the attention transformation:** the paper's motivation is twofold. First, in weight-activation quantization, both `$\mathbf{Q}$` and `$\mathbf{K}$` are quantized to low bits, and if either has outlier channels, the quantization error in the attention product can be severe. Scaling `$\mathbf{Q}$` and `$\mathbf{K}$` inversely allows the optimizer to balance their dynamic ranges for better quantization. Second, quantizing `$\mathbf{Q}$` and `$\mathbf{K}$` enables low-bit KV cache storage, which is critical for memory reduction in long-sequence generation. The explicit value transformation is omitted because "its distribution has already been channel-wise altered by the inverse transformation associated with the output projection linear layer" — the scaling from the output projection's LET propagates to affect `$\mathbf{V}$` through the residual connections and subsequent layers.

**Fusion of attention transformation.** The scaling factors in `$\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}$` and `$\tilde{\mathbf{K}}$` can be absorbed into the linear projection weights that produce `$\mathbf{Q}$` and `$\mathbf{K}$`. If `$\mathbf{Q} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}_Q$`, then `$\tilde{\mathbf{Q}} = \mathbf{X}(\mathbf{W}_Q \oslash \mathbf{s}_a)$`, meaning the query projection weight matrix is simply divided by `$\mathbf{s}_a$` along the output dimension. Similarly, `$\tilde{\mathbf{K}} = \mathbf{X}(\mathbf{W}_K \odot \mathbf{s}_a)$`. These are one-time transformations applied after LET optimization converges, producing a standard quantized model.

**Effectiveness and interaction with LWC.** Table A2 in the Appendix demonstrates the synergy between LWC and LET on LLaMA-7B W4A4 quantization. The baseline SmoothQuant achieves an average perplexity of 28.78 and average accuracy of 38.41. Adding LET alone (without LWC) improves to 16.97 perplexity and 48.83 accuracy. Adding LWC to SmoothQuant (without LET) achieves 15.80 perplexity and 50.15 accuracy. The full combination (LET + LWC, jointly optimized) achieves 12.87 perplexity and 52.65 accuracy — substantially better than either component alone. This confirms the paper's claim that LET and LWC are complementary and that joint optimization yields gains beyond simple addition.

Table A4 examines the contribution of individual LET components. Removing channel-wise shifting (keeping only scaling, similar to SmoothQuant) degrades W4A4 performance from 10.87 to 11.47 perplexity on LLaMA-13B and more severely on OPT-13B (from 11.65 to 13.64). Removing the attention transformation (keeping only linear-layer LET) degrades to 11.34 and 11.79 respectively. Both components contribute, though channel-wise shifting has a larger impact, consistent with activation centering being important for asymmetric quantization.

Table A5 shows which LET positions matter most. The four LET pairs in a transformer block are: [ln1, (q_proj, k_proj, v_proj)], [v_proj, out_proj], [Q, K] for attention, and [ln2, fc1] for the FFN. Removing the first pair (which handles the post-LayerNorm activations feeding into the attention projections) causes the largest degradation (from 12.87 to 19.87 average perplexity, and from 52.65 to 46.79 average accuracy), confirming that activation outliers are most severe after layer normalization.

**Initialization of LET parameters.** The scaling factors `$\mathbf{s}$` are initialized using the SmoothQuant heuristic: `$s_i = \max(|X_i|)^\alpha / \max(|W_{i,:}|)^{1-\alpha}$` where `$\alpha$` is the migration strength (typically 0.5). The shifting offsets `$\boldsymbol{\delta}$` are initialized using the Outlier Suppression+ heuristic: `$\delta_i = \text{mean}(X_i)$` or a variant that centers the activation distribution. The paper validates that this careful initialization matters — Table A6 shows that initializing scaling factors to 1.0 (no scaling) degrades average perplexity from 12.87 to 13.64 compared to SmoothQuant initialization, and initializing shifting to 0 degrades to 12.95. The scaling initialization is more important, consistent with scaling playing the primary role in outlier mitigation.

#### 3.4.4 Quantizers, Training Configuration, and the Complete Optimization Pipeline

This section consolidates the specific quantizer choices, optimizer settings, calibration data configuration, and initialization procedures that constitute the complete OmniQuant training pipeline.

**Quantizer specifications.** The paper uses two distinct quantizers:

1. **For weights:** the LWC quantizer described in Equation (2) — an asymmetric uniform quantizer with learned clipping strengths. This is used for all weight tensors in both weight-only and weight-activation quantization.

2. **For activations:** the standard MinMax quantizer (without learnable clipping). This is a deliberate choice — the LET already handles activation outlier mitigation through transformation, so additional clipping on activations is unnecessary. The activation quantizer operates per-token (each token in the sequence gets its own quantization range computed from its activations), which the paper notes is "deployment-friendly" in contrast to RPTQ's group-wise activation quantization.

**What gets quantized where:**

- **Weight-only quantization (W4A16, W3A16, W2A16):** Only the weight tensors of linear layers are quantized. Activations remain at FP16. Per-channel quantization (one clipping pair `$\gamma, \beta$` per output channel) is the default, with group-wise variants (e.g., W2A16g128 meaning groups of 128 elements share clipping parameters) explored for improved accuracy-resolution tradeoffs.

- **Weight-activation quantization (W6A6, W4A4):** Both weights and activations of all linear layers and attention operations (`$\mathbf{Q}, \mathbf{K}, \mathbf{V}$` projections and the `$\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^T$` multiplication) are quantized. The SoftMax output is kept at full precision (FP16) because "its long-tail distribution [makes] it unsuitable for uniform quantization." Table A7 explores SoftMax quantization: 8-bit SoftMax causes minimal degradation (average accuracy drops from 52.65 to 51.93), 6-bit is still acceptable (51.70), but 4-bit causes a significant drop to 48.52, confirming the long-tail issue.

**Optimizer configuration.** All learnable parameters (`$\Theta_1$` for LWC and `$\Theta_2$` for LET) are optimized using the AdamW optimizer with zero weight decay. The specific hyperparameters are:

- Learning rate for LWC parameters (`$\gamma$` and `$\beta$`): `$5 \times 10^{-3}$`
- Learning rate for LET parameters (`$\mathbf{s}$`, `$\boldsymbol{\delta}$`, `$\mathbf{s}_a$`): `$1 \times 10^{-2}$`

The paper does not provide explicit `$\beta_1$`, `$\beta_2$`, or `$\epsilon$` values for AdamW, nor does it specify whether these learning rates are decayed during training. The higher learning rate for LET parameters reflects their larger expected range of motion — scaling and shifting factors may need to change substantially from their heuristic initialization to reach optimal values. The LWC parameters, constrained to `$[0, 1]$` by the sigmoid, require smaller steps.

**Calibration data.** All experiments use 128 randomly selected 2048-token segments from WikiText2 as the calibration dataset. The batch size is 1 (one 2048-token sequence per optimization step). The paper validates this data efficiency in Table A11: LLaMA-7B W3A16 converges with as few as 16 samples (WikiText2 perplexity stays at 6.47 for 16, 32, 64, 128, and 256 samples), while W4A4 improves more gradually (from 11.56 at 16 samples to 11.23 at 128 samples, then slightly degrades to 11.41 at 256 samples). Table A10 further demonstrates robustness to the calibration dataset domain: using WikiText2, C4, or Pile as calibration data produces nearly identical results, with variance in WikiText2 perplexity ranging from 0.0006 to 0.17 across datasets.

**Training epochs and convergence.** Table A9 reports WikiText2 perplexity as a function of training epochs for LLaMA-7B across different quantization configurations:

- W4A16: converges at 10 epochs (perplexity 5.87), stable through 40 epochs.
- W3A16: converges at 20 epochs (6.49), stable at 40 (6.47).
- W2A16: requires more training — perplexity drops from 1.1e5 at epoch 0 (initialization only, effectively random) to 27.49 at epoch 10, 17.46 at epoch 20, 15.47 at epoch 40, and 14.77 at epoch 80.
- W6A6: converges at 10 epochs (5.96).
- W4A4: converges at 20 epochs (11.26), stable at 40 (11.23).

Based on these results, the paper standardizes on 20 training epochs for all configurations except W2A16, which uses 40 epochs (a compromise between convergence and training time, as 80 epochs only improves from 15.47 to 14.77).

**What is optimized jointly vs. separately.** For weight-activation quantization, both LWC (`$\Theta_1$`) and LET (`$\Theta_2$`) are active and are optimized simultaneously — each training iteration updates both sets of parameters. For weight-only quantization, the configuration depends on the model family:

- **LLaMA:** Only LWC is activated; LET is disabled. Table A3 shows that for LLaMA-13B W3A16, removing LET (keeping LWC) increases perplexity only from 5.65 to 5.68, a negligible degradation. The paper attributes this to LLaMA having few weight outliers. Removing LWC (keeping LET) increases perplexity to 7.65, showing that LWC is the critical component for LLaMA weight-only quantization.

- **OPT:** Both LWC and LET are activated for weight-only quantization. Table A3: for OPT-13B W3A16, removing LET degrades from 10.87 to 11.29, and removing LWC degrades to 12.98. OPT has more severe weight outliers, making LET beneficial even without activation quantization.

This model-family-dependent configuration is an empirical finding, not a principled distinction. The paper does not provide a theoretical explanation for why LLaMA and OPT differ in this regard.

**Training time.** Table A12 reports the wall-clock training time on a single NVIDIA A100-80G GPU:

- LLaMA-7B: 1.1 hours (weight-only), 1.6 hours (weight-activation)
- LLaMA-13B: 2.2 hours, 3.3 hours
- LLaMA-30B: 4.5 hours, 7.3 hours
- LLaMA-65B: 8.9 hours, 14.4 hours

The paper notes this is "approximately 5× greater than GPTQ" but "markedly faster than QAT methods, which demand hundreds of GPU hours." The training time scales approximately linearly with model size, consistent with the block-wise approach where each block is optimized independently with roughly constant per-block optimization cost.

**Iterative vs. simultaneous optimization of LWC and LET.** Table A8 investigates whether LWC and LET should be trained simultaneously (as in the standard OmniQuant pipeline) or iteratively (alternating between optimizing one while freezing the other). For LLaMA-7B W4A4:

- Simultaneous training (20 epochs): 12.87 average perplexity, 52.65 average accuracy.
- Iterative per-iteration (alternating every optimization step): 13.56, 50.91 — significantly worse.
- Iterative per-epoch (alternating every full epoch): 13.51, 52.06 — also worse.
- Iterative per-epoch with double training epochs (40 total): 12.80, 52.50 — matches simultaneous 20-epoch performance but requires 2× training time.

The paper concludes: "training LWC and LET simultaneously yields the best performance" and argues this is because "the synergy between LET and LWC creates a progressive process, where both techniques reinforce each other rather than interfere." In practice, simultaneous optimization allows the LET to adjust its scaling based on how well LWC is currently handling the weight quantization difficulty at each step, rather than optimizing against a stale snapshot.

**Fusing learned parameters into the final model.** After optimization converges for a block, two permanent transformations are applied before moving to the next block:

1. **LET fusion:** The scaling factors `$\mathbf{s}$` are absorbed into the preceding normalization/linear layer and the current layer's weight matrix (as `$\tilde{\mathbf{W}} = \mathbf{s} \odot \mathbf{W}$`). The shifting offsets `$\boldsymbol{\delta}$` are absorbed into the bias term (as `$\tilde{\mathbf{B}} = \mathbf{B} + \boldsymbol{\delta}\mathbf{W}$`) and the preceding layer's output. The attention scaling factors `$\mathbf{s}_a$` are absorbed into `$\mathbf{W}_Q$` and `$\mathbf{W}_K$`. After fusion, the LET parameters are no longer needed — the model is a standard transformer with modified (but full-precision) weights.

2. **LWC-based quantization:** The full-precision weights (now with LET modifications absorbed) are quantized using Equation (2) with the learned `$\gamma$` and `$\beta$` values. The quantization is permanent — the full-precision weights are discarded and replaced with their quantized integer representations and the quantization parameters (`$h$` and `$z$`).

The final quantized model contains only standard quantized weights with their associated quantization parameters (scales and zero-points). There are no extra operations, no mixed-precision exceptions, and no residual learnable parameters. This is what enables seamless deployment through existing frameworks like MLC-LLM (Section 4.5).

**Straight-through estimator for gradient propagation.** The paper does not explicitly detail how gradients flow through the non-differentiable rounding operation (`$\lfloor \cdot \rceil$`) in the quantizers, but this is implicitly handled via the standard straight-through estimator (STE): during the forward pass, weights and activations are quantized (with rounding); during the backward pass, the gradient of the rounding operation is treated as the identity function, allowing gradients to flow through to the learnable parameters (`$\gamma$`, `$\beta$`, `$\mathbf{s}$`, `$\boldsymbol{\delta}$`, `$\mathbf{s}_a$`) and, importantly, *not* to the frozen full-precision weights `$\mathbf{W}$`. The paper does not specify whether any gradient scaling or clipping is applied.

**Summary of design choices and their justifications:**

- **Block-wise over global:** makes the optimization tractable at scale; local error minimization empirically suffices as a proxy for global output preservation.
- **Learnable relative clipping strengths (`$\gamma, \beta$`) over absolute thresholds:** bounded domain `$[0, 1]$` eases optimization; adapts automatically to changing weight distributions from LET; degrades smoothly to standard MinMax.
- **Channel-wise scaling AND shifting:** scaling handles magnitude outliers; shifting handles distribution centering for asymmetric quantization.
- **Attention transformation:** extends the equivalent transformation paradigm beyond linear layers to the attention product, enabling Q/K quantization for KV cache compression.
- **Excluding second FFN layer:** unstable gradients due to post-activation sparsity.
- **Simultaneous joint optimization of LWC and LET:** captures synergistic interactions; more efficient than iterative alternation.
- **SmoothQuant + Outlier Suppression+ initialization:** provides a good starting point; the learned parameters refine rather than discover from scratch.
- **SoftMax kept at full precision:** long-tail distribution makes uniform quantization inappropriate.
- **20 epochs (40 for W2A16):** sufficient for convergence across all configurations; longer training yields diminishing returns.
- **128 calibration samples:** sufficient for convergence (validated by ablation); matches the convention from prior work (GPTQ, AWQ).
- **Layer-wise learning rates (5e-3 for LWC, 1e-2 for LET):** reflects the different dynamic ranges and expected magnitudes of adjustment for the two parameter types.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Freeze the Weights, Learn the Quantizer — A Third Paradigm Between PTQ and QAT

The paper's most fundamental conceptual move is redefining what "post-training quantization with gradients" means. Prior to OmniQuant, the landscape presented practitioners with a binary choice: either accept the efficiency of PTQ methods (which use hand-crafted or grid-searched quantization parameters and degrade at low bit-widths) or pay the enormous cost of QAT (which tunes all model weights and achieves better accuracy but requires hundreds of GPU-hours and 100k+ training samples). The paper identifies that this tradeoff is not inherent — it is an artifact of *what gets optimized*.

The key reframing is this: you don't need to optimize the model weights to get good quantization. You need to optimize the *quantizer's behavior*. The full-precision weights already encode the knowledge; the problem is that the quantizer — with its clipping thresholds, scaling factors, and shifting offsets — is making poor choices about how to discretize that knowledge. By freezing the original weights and introducing only a small set of learnable parameters that control *how* quantization is performed (not *what* is being quantized), OmniQuant achieves QAT-level performance while preserving PTQ-level data and compute efficiency.

This is fundamentally different from both prior PTQ methods (SmoothQuant, AWQ, GPTQ, Outlier Suppression+) that used heuristics to set quantization parameters, and prior gradient-based PTQ methods (AdaRound, BRECQ) that optimized weight rounding or weight perturbations — which becomes intractable at billion-parameter scale because the number of optimized parameters scales with model size. OmniQuant's optimized parameters scale with layer count and channel dimensions, not weight count, making gradient-based optimization feasible for 70B+ models on a single GPU.

The paper validates this framing empirically in Figure 1(a): on LLaMA-7B W4A4, OmniQuant achieves 52.65% average zero-shot accuracy using 128 samples and 1.6 hours on a single GPU, compared to LLM-QAT's 46.43% using 100k samples and 90+ GPU hours. This is not an incremental improvement — it inverts the QAT-PTQ tradeoff, demonstrating that carefully chosen learnable quantization parameters can *outperform* full weight tuning when the optimization is properly constrained. The paper's Table A2 further supports this: starting from SmoothQuant (average accuracy 38.41%), adding learnable equivalent transformation alone improves to 48.83%, and adding joint LWC+LET optimization reaches 52.65% — each gradient-based component provides substantial gains beyond heuristic initialization.

This is best understood as a **complexity-control insight**: the optimization space for LLM quantization is large but highly structured, and constraining the search to fusable quantization-control parameters (rather than weight modifications) both makes optimization tractable and produces deployment-ready models with zero overhead.

---

### Innovation 2: Joint Differentiable Optimization of Weight Clipping and Activation Transformation Creates Synergy That Sequential/Heuristic Methods Cannot Capture

The paper's second distinctive contribution is the demonstration — and mechanistic explanation — of why jointly optimizing weight clipping (LWC) and activation equivalent transformation (LET) produces gains that exceed the sum of their individual contributions, and why prior methods that treated these problems independently or sequentially left substantial performance on the table.

Prior work addressed weight quantization difficulty and activation outlier difficulty as separate problems with separate solutions. SmoothQuant and Outlier Suppression+ used heuristic scaling/shifting to handle activation outliers, then applied standard MinMax weight quantization — effectively a sequential pipeline where activation handling is fixed before weight quantization begins. AWQ used grid-searched scaling to protect important weight channels during weight-only quantization but never interacted with activation quantization. The paper's insight is that these are **coupled optimization problems**: how aggressively you clip weights depends on how much quantization difficulty LET has shifted from activations to weights; how much scaling LET applies depends on how well LWC can handle the resulting amplified weight values. Optimizing them independently or sequentially misses this coupling.

The empirical evidence for this synergy is striking. Table A2 shows that on LLaMA-7B W4A4: SmoothQuant baseline achieves 28.78 average perplexity; adding LET alone (with MinMax weight quantization, no LWC) achieves 16.97; adding LWC to SmoothQuant (no LET) achieves 15.80; the full joint LET+LWC achieves 12.87. The joint optimization improves perplexity by 2.93 over the better individual component (LWC alone), which is larger than the improvement from SmoothQuant to LWC alone (28.78 → 15.80 = 12.98 improvement) when measured proportionally. In accuracy terms: LET alone 48.83%, LWC alone 50.15%, joint 52.65%.

Table A8 provides additional mechanistic evidence: training LWC and LET simultaneously outperforms iterative alternation per-iteration (13.56 perplexity vs. 12.87) and per-epoch (13.51 vs. 12.87), and iterative training requires double the epochs to match simultaneous performance (12.80 at 40 epochs vs. 12.87 at 20). This confirms that the coupling is dynamic — LET benefits from seeing LWC's current clipping behavior at each gradient step, not a frozen snapshot.

This finding is conceptually significant because it reframes the LLM quantization problem from two independent challenges (weight sensitivity, activation outliers) to a single joint optimization over a combined parameter space. It implies that future quantization methods should not separate these concerns — and that prior methods' decision to handle activation outliers heuristically before weight quantization was not just suboptimal in degree but in kind, because it prevented the adaptive balancing that joint optimization enables.

---

### Innovation 3: Extending Equivalent Transformation to Attention Operations Enables Quantization of the Full Transformer Computation Graph

Prior equivalent transformation methods — SmoothQuant, Outlier Suppression+, and AWQ — restricted their mathematically equivalent scaling and shifting to linear layers only. This left the attention mechanism's matrix multiplications (`Q @ K^T` and the subsequent `attn @ V`) unaddressed by the transformation framework. For weight-only quantization this is acceptable (activations remain full-precision), but for weight-activation quantization — where `Q`, `K`, and `V` must be quantized to low bits to achieve computational speedup and KV cache compression — the omission of attention transformations creates a bottleneck.

The paper's innovation is recognizing that the equivalent transformation principle — applying inverse scaling to two operands of a matrix multiplication so the product is unchanged — extends naturally to the query-key product in self-attention, with the scaling factors fusible into the projection weight matrices `W_Q` and `W_K`. This is conceptually straightforward in retrospect but was not done in prior work (Table A1 explicitly contrasts OmniQuant with SmoothQuant, AWQ, and OP+ on this dimension).

The significance is practical more than theoretical. Table A4 quantifies the benefit: removing the attention transformation from OmniQuant on LLaMA-13B W4A4 degrades perplexity from 10.87 to 11.34, a modest but consistent improvement. The larger implication is that OmniQuant provides a unified equivalent transformation framework covering the entire transformer computation graph — linear layers (both FFN and attention projections) and attention products — making it the first PTQ method to apply learnable, differentiable transformations to every matrix multiplication in the transformer block. This completeness is what enables OmniQuant to function in both weight-only and weight-activation regimes with the same underlying mechanism.

The attention transformation also enables quantization of the KV cache — a critical memory bottleneck in long-sequence generation — without introducing additional mechanisms. By scaling `Q` and `K` inversely, the optimizer can balance their dynamic ranges for more accurate low-bit representation of the cached key tensors. This is not explored deeply in the paper (the deployment experiments in Section 4.5 are weight-only only, due to lack of hardware support for W4A4), but it establishes the architectural foundation for full transformer quantization.

---

### Innovation 4: Verifier Over-Optimization as the Scaling Ceiling — and Why It Implies Robustness Is the Real Bottleneck

The paper's experimental results collectively identify a phenomenon that, while not named as a formal "innovation" in the paper, represents an important diagnostic contribution: **aggressive optimization of quantization parameters can overfit to the calibration objective in ways that harm generalization, and this over-optimization is the primary barrier to further low-bit compression**.

This is visible in several results. Table A9 shows that for LLaMA-7B W4A16, training beyond 10 epochs provides no improvement (perplexity 5.87 at epoch 10, 5.86 at epoch 40, 5.85 at epoch 20) — the optimization saturates. For W2A16, training from 40 to 80 epochs only improves from 15.47 to 14.77, a diminishing return. Table A11 shows that increasing calibration samples from 128 to 256 for W4A4 actually *degrades* perplexity from 11.23 to 11.41 — the optimizer overfits to the larger calibration set. The exclusion of the second FFN linear layer from LET is explicitly motivated by unstable gradients due to post-activation sparsity — an acknowledgment that the differentiable optimization, while powerful, has failure modes.

This is significant because it shifts the narrative from "gradient optimization is always better than heuristics" to "gradient optimization is better up to a point determined by calibration data quality and optimization stability." The paper's design choices — block-wise rather than global optimization, sigmoid-constrained clipping strengths rather than unconstrained thresholds, SmoothQuant initialization rather than random initialization — can be understood as strategies to keep the optimization within a stable regime where it improves generalization rather than exploiting calibration-set artifacts.

The implication for future work is that improving quantization further at low bit-widths will require not just more powerful optimization but better regularized optimization — calibration data augmentation, robust loss functions, or architectural constraints that prevent the learned parameters from over-adapting to the limited calibration distribution. The paper's block-wise `ℓ_2` reconstruction loss, while effective, is a crude proxy for the true objective (preserving the model's output distribution on real data), and the observed saturation and occasional degradation at higher compute budgets suggests this proxy becomes misaligned under aggressive optimization.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The primary benchmark is WikiText2 (Merity et al., 2016) for language generation perplexity evaluation, typically using the test split. Additional perplexity benchmarks include PTB (Marcus et al., 1994) and C4 (Raffel et al., 2020), reported in the Appendix. For zero-shot task accuracy, the paper uses PIQA (Bisk et al., 2020), ARC-easy and ARC-challenge (Clark et al., 2018), BoolQ (Clark et al., 2019), HellaSwag (Clark et al., 2018), and Winogrande, implemented through the lm-eval-harness framework (Gao et al., 2021). For instruction-tuned model evaluation, the Vicuna benchmark (Chiang et al., 2023) is used with GPT-4 as the judge, comprising 80 questions where each comparison pair is evaluated in both orders (160 trials total) to negate position bias (Zheng et al., 2023).

- **Base model(s).** The paper evaluates on five model families spanning a wide size range: OPT (125M–66B; Zhang et al., 2022), LLaMA-1 (7B–65B; Touvron et al., 2023a), LLaMA-2 (7B–70B; Touvron et al., 2023b), Falcon-180B (Penedo et al., 2023), and LLaMA-2-chat (the instruction-tuned variant of LLaMA-2). This comprehensive coverage — from 125M to 180B parameters across different architectures and training paradigms — is intended to demonstrate that OmniQuant's benefits are not model-specific. The paper explicitly states that LLaMA-2-chat is included to validate "generalization capability" and test whether the method works on instruction-tuned models where output quality degradation from quantization may manifest differently than in perplexity scores.

- **Metrics.** For language generation, the primary metric is perplexity (PPL, lower is better) on WikiText2, with C4 and PTB as secondary evaluations. The paper adheres to the GPTQ evaluation settings for language generation experiments. For zero-shot tasks, accuracy (%) on each benchmark and the average across all six tasks are reported. For the Vicuna benchmark on instruction-tuned models, the metric is win rate (%) — the fraction of comparisons where the quantized model's response is preferred by GPT-4 over a baseline quantized model's response, with ties excluded from the win rate calculation. For deployment experiments, metrics include weight memory (GB), running memory (GB), and inference speed (tokens/second generating 512 tokens).

- **Baselines.** For weight-only quantization, the paper compares against three methods: Round-to-Nearest (RTN, vanilla MinMax quantization without optimization), GPTQ (Frantar et al., 2022; block-wise reconstruction-based PTQ), and AWQ (Lin et al., 2023; activation-aware channel-wise scaling via grid search). For weight-activation quantization, baselines include: SmoothQuant (Xiao et al., 2023; pre-defined migration strength for channel-wise scaling), Outlier Suppression+ (OS+; Wei et al., 2023; channel-wise scaling via grid search plus pre-defined shifting), RPTQ (Yuan et al., 2023; reorder-based quantization), and LLM-QAT (Liu et al., 2023b; QAT method). The paper notes that SmoothQuant and OS+ are reproduced with per-channel weight quantization and per-token activation quantization for fair comparison. For the instruction-tuned model evaluation on Vicuna, RTN and AWQ serve as the baselines against OmniQuant.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper does not measure compute in FLOPs or generations (unlike the test-time scaling papers). Instead, the key resource constraints are: (1) hardware — all quantization is performed on a single NVIDIA A100-40G GPU (except Falcon-180B which uses a single A100-80G), (2) calibration data — 128 randomly selected 2048-token segments from WikiText2, and (3) training time — reported in hours for each model size (e.g., 1.6 hours for LLaMA-7B weight-activation, 14.4 hours for LLaMA-65B). The fairness of comparison is maintained by using identical calibration data size and GPU constraints across methods where possible, with the paper explicitly contrasting OmniQuant's requirements against GPTQ's and LLM-QAT's resource demands. For deployment experiments, memory and speed are measured on an NVIDIA A100-80G using the MLC-LLM framework.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not employ cross-validation or report confidence intervals. The evaluation protocol is straightforward: quantize the full model using the specified calibration dataset (128 WikiText2 samples), then evaluate perplexity on the full test splits of WikiText2/PTB/C4 and zero-shot accuracy on the full benchmark sets. No strategy selection or hyperparameter tuning on test data is described beyond the ablation experiments that determine default settings (e.g., 20 epochs for most configurations, 40 for W2A16). The difficulty estimation and bin-based strategy selection framework from the compute-optimal scaling literature is entirely absent here — this is a standard PTQ evaluation. For the Vicuna benchmark evaluation, position bias is controlled by evaluating each pair in both orders (160 total trials for 80 questions), and win rates are reported excluding ties.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Weight-Only Quantization Results (Table 1, Appendix Tables A19–A22)

The central weight-only quantization results are presented in Table 1 for LLaMA-1 and LLaMA-2 models (WikiText2 perplexity), with C4 perplexity continuing in Table A19 in the Appendix. OPT results span Tables A20 (WikiText2), A21 (PTB), and A22 (C4). Falcon-180B results appear in Table A18.

**Headline finding:** OmniQuant consistently outperforms RTN, GPTQ, and AWQ across all weight-only quantization configurations (W2A16, W3A16, W4A16, and their group-wise variants), with the performance gap widening dramatically as bit-width decreases.

For **LLaMA-13B** (Table 1), the progression from W4A16 to W2A16 illustrates this clearly. At W4A16 (channel-wise), OmniQuant achieves WikiText2 PPL of 5.21 versus GPTQ's 5.40 and AWQ's 5.34 — a marginal improvement (0.13–0.19 PPL) that is nonetheless consistent. At W4A16g128 (group-wise, group size 128), all methods perform similarly: OmniQuant 5.17, GPTQ 5.20, AWQ 5.20 — the gap essentially closes. Moving to W3A16, OmniQuant's advantage widens: 5.68 versus GPTQ's 6.76 and AWQ's 7.45. With group-wise W3A16g128, the ordering is OmniQuant 5.44, AWQ 5.51, GPTQ 5.62. The pattern is clear: at moderate compression (4-bit), existing methods are adequate; at 3-bit and below, OmniQuant's learnable clipping provides a substantial edge.

The **W2A16** results are where the differences become stark. For LLaMA-13B W2A16 (channel-wise): OmniQuant achieves 13.21 PPL, while GPTQ collapses to 5.5 × 10³ and RTN reaches 6.8 × 10⁴. With group-wise quantization (W2A16g128), the numbers are: OmniQuant 7.93, GPTQ 15.60, AWQ 2.8 × 10⁵. At W2A16g64 (group size 64, which provides finer granularity and typically better accuracy): OmniQuant achieves 7.34, GPTQ 10.06, AWQ 2.7 × 10⁵. Note that AWQ completely fails at 2-bit — its grid-searched channel-wise scaling cannot compensate for the extreme information loss, while OmniQuant's learned clipping thresholds adapt per-channel to find optimal quantization ranges.

For **LLaMA-65B**, the largest LLaMA-1 model, the pattern holds. At W2A16: OmniQuant 7.58, GPTQ 55.91. At W2A16g128: OmniQuant 5.95, GPTQ 9.51, AWQ 7.4 × 10⁴. Even at W3A16: OmniQuant 4.04 versus GPTQ's 5.06 and AWQ's 5.21. The benefits persist at scale, confirming that OmniQuant's optimization framework does not degrade with model size — consistent with the block-wise approach where per-block optimization cost is independent of total model size.

For **LLaMA-2 models** (7B, 13B, 70B), the same pattern emerges. LLaMA-2-70B at W2A16: OmniQuant 7.81, GPTQ 77.95. At W2A16g128: OmniQuant 6.55, GPTQ produces NaN (the paper reports "NAN"), AWQ is not evaluated at 70B scale (marked as "—" in the table). This is noteworthy: GPTQ fails entirely at 2-bit with group-wise quantization for LLaMA-2-70B, while OmniQuant achieves 6.55 PPL — less than 2× the FP16 baseline of 3.31. At W3A16: OmniQuant achieves 3.92 PPL versus GPTQ's 4.82 and the FP16 baseline of 3.31, a remarkably small degradation for 3-bit quantization of a 70B model.

**OPT family results** (Tables A20–A22 in Appendix) demonstrate similar patterns but with an important difference: the benefits of LET in weight-only quantization are more pronounced for OPT than LLaMA. The paper attributes this to OPT having more severe weight outliers (Table A3 and the discussion in Section 3.4.4). For OPT-13B W3A16: OmniQuant achieves 10.87 WikiText2 PPL, GPTQ achieves 11.73, AWQ achieves 20.09. At W2A16g128: OmniQuant 12.94, GPTQ 21.36, AWQ 14.32 — here AWQ outperforms GPTQ but still lags behind OmniQuant. For OPT-66B, the largest OPT model: at W2A16g64, OmniQuant achieves 10.59 versus GPTQ's 356.01 and AWQ's 12.74. GPTQ's catastrophic failure at 2-bit on OPT-66B (356.01 PPL versus FP16 9.34) highlights the fragility of reconstruction-based methods at extreme compression, while OmniQuant remains stable.

**Falcon-180B** (Table A18) represents the largest model evaluated — 180 billion parameters. OmniQuant at W3A16g512 (group size 512) achieves WikiText2 PPL of 3.71 versus the BF16/FP16 baseline of 3.29, a degradation of only 0.42 PPL. RTN at the same configuration reaches 5.33 — a much larger gap. The model fits on a single A100-80G GPU after quantization (65 GB memory for weights, versus 335 GB for the full-precision model requiring 5 GPUs). Zero-shot task performance is also reported: OmniQuant W3A16g512 achieves 60.92 ARC-c (versus 60.83 for FP16 — essentially lossless on this metric), 84.71 PIQA (versus 84.82), and 79.40 average accuracy (versus 80.58 for FP16). This demonstrates that OmniQuant can compress a 180B model to fit on a single GPU while preserving most of its capabilities.

The **C4 perplexity results** (Table A19) closely mirror the WikiText2 patterns, confirming that the improvements are not dataset-specific. For LLaMA-13B W2A16g128: OmniQuant 10.36 C4 PPL, GPTQ 15.29, AWQ 2.3 × 10⁵. For LLaMA-30B W2A16: OmniQuant 13.89, GPTQ 169.80. The consistency across WikiText2 and C4 (and PTB for OPT, Table A21) is strong evidence against overfitting to the calibration dataset distribution — a concern explicitly addressed in the ablation studies (Tables A10, A11).

**Key takeaway for weight-only quantization:** OmniQuant is competitive with existing methods at 4-bit (where the problem is relatively easy) and increasingly dominant at 3-bit and 2-bit (where hand-crafted heuristics fail). The method enables practical 2-bit quantization of models up to 70B+ parameters, a regime where prior PTQ methods either collapse entirely (GPTQ, AWQ) or produce models that are effectively random (RTN). The learnable clipping mechanism is the primary driver of this benefit, with LET providing additional gains for models with severe weight outliers (OPT).

#### Weight-Activation Quantization Results (Table 2, Appendix Tables A23–A25)

The weight-activation quantization results focus on W6A6 and W4A4 configurations. W8A8 is excluded because the paper states that "SmoothQuant can nearly achieve lossless W8A8 quantized models when compared with full-precision counterparts" — the interesting regime is where existing methods degrade, and OmniQuant's advantages become apparent.

**LLaMA family zero-shot accuracy (Table 2).** This is the paper's primary weight-activation evaluation table, reporting accuracy on six zero-shot tasks plus the average.

For **LLaMA-7B W6A6**, all methods perform similarly, close to the FP16 baseline: SmoothQuant 62.81 average accuracy, OS+ 61.13, OmniQuant 63.17, versus FP16 64.09. The differences are small (within ~3 percentage points), consistent with W6A6 being a moderate compression level where existing heuristics suffice.

At **LLaMA-7B W4A4**, the differences are dramatic:
- SmoothQuant: 38.41 average accuracy (a 25.68 point drop from FP16)
- LLM-QAT (QAT method): 41.27
- LLM-QAT + SmoothQuant: 46.43
- OS+: 48.43
- OmniQuant: **52.65**

OmniQuant outperforms the next-best PTQ method (OS+) by +4.22 percentage points, outperforms the QAT method LLM-QAT by +11.38 points, and even beats LLM-QAT+SmoothQuant by +6.22 points. This is the headline result from Figure 1(a): OmniQuant achieves better W4A4 accuracy than QAT methods while using 128 samples and 1.6 GPU-hours versus QAT's 100k samples and 90+ GPU-hours.

Examining individual tasks for LLaMA-7B W4A4: OmniQuant shows particularly large advantages on HellaSwag (56.44 versus 44.39 for OS+, a +12.05 point gain) and PIQA (66.15 versus 62.73 for OS+, a +3.42 gain). On ARC-challenge, the hardest reasoning task, OmniQuant's 31.14 is close to OS+'s 30.29 and SmoothQuant's 25.80. The patterns suggest that OmniQuant's benefits are most pronounced on tasks requiring nuanced language understanding (HellaSwag, PIQA) rather than factual reasoning (ARC, BoolQ), though improvements exist across the board.

For **LLaMA-13B W4A4**:
- SmoothQuant: 49.36 average accuracy
- OS+: 49.86
- OmniQuant: **54.37** (versus FP16 66.33)

The +4.51 point gain over OS+ is consistent with the 7B results. For **LLaMA-30B W4A4**:
- SmoothQuant: 44.83
- OS+: 52.62
- OmniQuant: **56.63** (versus FP16 67.44)

The gain is +4.01 points. For **LLaMA-65B W4A4**:
- SmoothQuant: 47.71
- OS+: 52.52
- OmniQuant: **59.22** (versus FP16 71.04)

The gain is +6.70 points. Across all model sizes, OmniQuant delivers substantial and consistent improvements at W4A4, with the absolute gain ranging from +4.22% to +11.80% (the paper reports +4.99% ~ +11.80% in the text — the variation depends on how the averages are computed and which individual task gains are emphasized). At W6A6, the improvements are marginal but consistent: OmniQuant matches or slightly exceeds the best prior method across all model sizes (e.g., LLaMA-7B: OmniQuant 63.17 versus SmoothQuant 62.81; LLaMA-65B: OmniQuant 70.28 versus SmoothQuant 69.80).

**LLaMA family perplexity (Tables A23, A24).** The perplexity results corroborate the accuracy findings. For LLaMA-7B W4A4 WikiText2: OmniQuant 11.26, SmoothQuant 25.25. For LLaMA-13B W4A4: OmniQuant 10.87, SmoothQuant 40.05. For LLaMA-30B W4A4: OmniQuant 10.33, SmoothQuant 192.40 — SmoothQuant effectively collapses at larger model sizes in W4A4 (192.40 PPL for a model with FP16 PPL of 4.10), while OmniQuant remains stable. For LLaMA-65B W4A4: OmniQuant 9.17, SmoothQuant 275.53. The C4 perplexity (Table A24) shows the same pattern: LLaMA-65B W4A4 OmniQuant 11.28 versus SmoothQuant 244.35.

For W6A6, the differences are much smaller. LLaMA-7B: OmniQuant 5.96 WikiText2 PPL, SmoothQuant 6.03 (FP16 5.68). LLaMA-65B: OmniQuant 3.75, SmoothQuant 3.88 (FP16 3.53). These are near-lossless results — the degradation from W6A6 quantization with OmniQuant is approximately 0.2–0.3 PPL across model sizes.

**OPT family weight-activation results (Table A25).** The OPT results include comparison with RPTQ. For OPT-13B W6A6: OmniQuant achieves 10.21 WikiText2 PPL, SmoothQuant 10.56, RPTQ (reproduced) 10.25 — all methods are close, with OmniQuant slightly ahead. At W4A4: OmniQuant achieves 11.65 WikiText2 PPL, RPTQ (reproduced) 16.45, SmoothQuant 7.4 × 10³. For OPT-30B W4A4: OmniQuant 10.60, RPTQ (reproduced) 11.50, SmoothQuant 1.2 × 10⁴. For OPT-66B W4A4: OmniQuant 10.29, RPTQ (reproduced) 11.16, SmoothQuant 2.2 × 10⁵. Across the OPT family, OmniQuant consistently outperforms both RPTQ and SmoothQuant at W4A4, with the margin growing from ~0.6 PPL at OPT-13B to ~3 PPL at OPT-66B. This is particularly notable given that RPTQ uses deployment-unfriendly group-wise activation quantization (while OmniQuant uses per-token quantization, which maps cleanly to hardware) — OmniQuant achieves better accuracy with a more deployment-friendly scheme.

**Comparison with QAT.** The paper's comparison with LLM-QAT is limited but striking. For LLaMA-7B W4A4, OmniQuant achieves 52.65 average accuracy versus LLM-QAT's 41.27 and LLM-QAT+SmoothQuant's 46.43 (Table 2). This is a +6.22 point advantage over the *best* QAT variant while using ~1/800 the GPU-hours (1.6 hours versus 90+ hours) and ~1/800 the data (128 samples versus 100k). The paper does not report LLM-QAT results for other model sizes or for W6A6, which limits the comparison but makes the direction clear: OmniQuant dominates the QAT baseline on the one configuration where both are evaluated.

**Weight-activation results beyond uniform bits.** Table A17 explores asymmetric bit configurations: W4A8, W4A6, and W8A4 on LLaMA-7B. W4A8 (4-bit weight, 8-bit activation) achieves 62.40 average accuracy — close to W6A6's 63.17 and retaining most of the FP16 accuracy (64.09). W4A6 achieves 61.64. W8A4 (8-bit weight, 4-bit activation) drops to 53.46 — notably worse than W4A4's 52.65, suggesting that 4-bit activation quantization is more challenging than 4-bit weight quantization for this model size. This asymmetry matters for hardware design: accelerators that support different bit-widths for weights and activations could exploit these findings to optimize for accuracy.

#### Instruction-Tuned Model Quantization (Figure 4, Section 4.4)

The paper evaluates quantized LLaMA-2-chat models (7B and 13B) on the Vicuna benchmark using GPT-4 as a judge. The results, visualized in Figure 4, compare W3A16g128 quantization using RTN, AWQ, and OmniQuant.

For **LLaMA-2-7B-chat**, OmniQuant achieves a 50.0% win rate against AWQ (a statistical tie — the two models perform equally well on average). Both OmniQuant and AWQ substantially outperform RTN: OmniQuant wins 80.3% of comparisons against RTN, while AWQ wins 69.4%. This suggests that both AWQ and OmniQuant preserve instruction-following capability well at W3A16g128 for the 7B chat model.

For **LLaMA-2-13B-chat**, the pattern shifts: OmniQuant outperforms AWQ with a 54.4% win rate, and outperforms RTN with a 56.2% win rate. Meanwhile, AWQ actually *loses* to RTN — AWQ's win rate against RTN is below 50% (the exact number is not provided in the text, but Figure 4(b) shows AWQ losing more comparisons against RTN than it wins, since the "Former Win" and "Former Lost" segments are referenced). This is a significant finding: AWQ's grid-searched scaling, while effective for perplexity, degrades the instruction-tuned model's conversational quality on Vicuna questions for the 13B model, whereas OmniQuant's learned parameters preserve it better.

The practical implication is that quantization method evaluation on base models (via perplexity) may not fully predict quantization quality on downstream tasks, especially for instruction-tuned models where output coherence and instruction-following matter more than next-token prediction accuracy. OmniQuant's gradient-based optimization, which directly minimizes the block-wise output error on calibration data, appears to preserve these qualities better than AWQ's heuristic approach.

#### Deployment Results on Real Hardware (Table 3, Section 4.5)

The deployment evaluation uses MLC-LLM to run quantized LLaMA models on an NVIDIA A100-80G GPU. Table 3 reports three metrics: Weight Memory (WM, the storage required for quantized weights), Running Memory (RM, the total GPU memory used during inference, which is higher than WM due to retained activations and KV cache), and inference speed (tokens/second when generating 512 tokens).

**Memory reduction.** For LLaMA-7B: FP16 requires 12.6 GB for weights and 14.4 GB total running memory. W4A16g128 reduces this to 3.8 GB WM and 5.7 GB RM — roughly 2.5× reduction in running memory. W2A16g128 further reduces to 2.2 GB WM and 4.1 GB RM — roughly 3.5× reduction. For LLaMA-65B, the largest model tested: FP16 is "OOM" (Out Of Memory) on the single A100-80G. W4A16g128 makes it runnable at 33.0 GB WM and 41.0 GB RM. W3A16g128 further reduces to 27.0 GB WM and 35.1 GB RM. W2A16g128 achieves 18.0 GB WM and 25.6 GB RM. This is the critical deployment enabler: a model that cannot run at all on a single GPU at FP16 becomes comfortably deployable at 2-bit weight quantization.

**Speed.** For LLaMA-7B, FP16 achieves 69.2 tokens/second. W4A16g128 achieves 134.2 tokens/second — roughly 1.94× speedup. W2A16g128 achieves 83.9 tokens/second — faster than FP16 but slower than W4A16. This non-monotonic behavior is due to MLC-LLM's suboptimal support for INT2/INT3 operations, as the paper explicitly notes: "MLC-LLM's support for INT3/INT2 is currently suboptimal, particularly for INT3. Enhancements to INT3/INT2 quantization speed are in our future roadmap." For LLaMA-13B: FP16 52.5 tok/s, W4A16g128 91.3 tok/s (+74%), W2A16g128 92.6 tok/s. For LLaMA-30B: FP16 23.9 tok/s, W4A16g128 43.6 tok/s (+82%), W2A16g128 36.7 tok/s. For LLaMA-65B: W4A16g128 24.3 tok/s — a model that couldn't run at FP16 now runs at a usable speed.

The paper only evaluates weight-only quantization in deployment because "W4A4 and W6A6 quantization methods lack out-of-the-box hardware support." This is an important caveat: while the paper demonstrates superior W4A4 accuracy in simulation (Table 2), the deployment speedups for weight-activation quantization remain unrealized due to hardware limitations, not algorithmic ones.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Component efficacy: LWC vs. LET (Table A3).** For LLaMA-13B W4A4: removing LWC (keeping LET) degrades perplexity from 10.87 to 20.75; removing LET (keeping LWC) causes catastrophic failure (5.4 × 10³ PPL); removing both yields 1.8 × 10³. For weight-only W3A16: removing LWC causes the largest degradation (from 5.65 to 7.65), while removing LET has minimal impact on LLaMA (5.65 to 5.68) but substantial impact on OPT (10.87 to 11.29). This confirms that LWC is critical for weight quantization, LET is essential for activation quantization, and the LET benefit for weight-only varies by model family.

**Design choices in LET: shifting and attention transformation (Table A4).** On LLaMA-13B W4A4, removing channel-wise shifting degrades PPL from 10.87 to 11.47; removing the attention transformation degrades to 11.34. Both components contribute, with shifting being more important. On OPT-13B W4A4, the effects are larger: removing shifting degrades from 11.65 to 13.64; removing attention degrades to 11.79. The attention transformation's contribution is modest but consistent.

**LET position importance (Table A5).** On LLaMA-7B W4A4, removing the [ln1, (q_proj, k_proj, v_proj)] LET pair (which follows the first LayerNorm and feeds into the attention projections) causes the largest degradation: average PPL increases from 12.87 to 19.87, average accuracy drops from 52.65 to 46.79. The other pairs show smaller effects ([v_proj, out_proj]: 13.03 PPL; [Q, K]: 13.34; [ln2, fc1]: 14.47). This confirms that activation outliers are most severe after layer normalization, consistent with prior findings from SmoothQuant.

**LET initialization (Table A6).** Initializing scaling factors to 1 (instead of SmoothQuant's heuristic) degrades average PPL from 12.87 to 13.64 and accuracy from 52.65 to 51.37. Initializing shifting to 0 degrades to 12.95 PPL and 52.22 accuracy. The scaling initialization matters more than shifting, consistent with scaling performing the heavy lifting for outlier mitigation.

**Softmax quantization (Table A7).** 8-bit Softmax causes minimal degradation (12.91 PPL, 51.93 accuracy vs. 12.87, 52.65 for FP16 Softmax). 6-bit remains acceptable (13.20 PPL, 51.70 accuracy). 4-bit causes significant degradation (18.80 PPL, 48.52 accuracy), confirming the long-tail distribution problem.

**Iterative vs. simultaneous training (Table A8).** Training LWC and LET simultaneously achieves 12.87 PPL and 52.65 accuracy. Iterative per-iteration degrades to 13.56/50.91. Iterative per-epoch degrades to 13.51/52.06. Iterative per-epoch with double epochs (40 total) matches simultaneous at 12.80/52.50, but requires 2× training time. Simultaneous training is both more effective and more efficient.

**Training epochs (Table A9).** For LLaMA-7B W4A16: converges at 10 epochs (5.87 PPL). W3A16: converges at 20 epochs (6.49). W2A16: improves from 1.1 × 10⁵ at epoch 0 to 27.49 at epoch 10, 17.46 at epoch 20, 15.47 at epoch 40, 14.77 at epoch 80 — slow convergence but continued improvement. W6A6: converges at 10 epochs (5.96). W4A4: converges at 20 epochs (11.26), stable at 40 (11.23).

**Calibration dataset domain (Table A10).** Using WikiText2, C4, or Pile as calibration data for LLaMA-7B W3A16 produces nearly identical WikiText2 PPL (6.47, 6.67, 6.69) and C4 PPL (8.19, 8.13, 8.17), with variance 0.0006–0.17. For W4A4: WikiText2 PPL varies from 11.23 (WikiText2 calibration) to 12.17 (C4 calibration), C4 PPL varies from 14.22 (Pile calibration) to 14.61 (WikiText2 calibration). The method is robust to calibration dataset choice, with W4A4 showing slightly more sensitivity than W3A16.

**Calibration sample count (Table A11).** For LLaMA-7B W3A16, performance is essentially invariant to sample count: 16 samples yield 6.47 WikiText2 PPL, 256 samples yield 6.46. For W4A4, 16 samples yield 11.56 PPL, improving to 11.23 at 128 samples, then slightly degrading to 11.41 at 256 samples — suggesting mild overfitting with larger calibration sets for the more aggressive quantization.

**Comparisons with clipping-based methods (Table A14, Figure A5).** Replacing LWC with PACT (direct threshold learning) or LSQ (direct scale/zero-point learning) in the OmniQuant pipeline degrades performance. For LLaMA-7B W3A16: MinMax baseline 25.73, PACT 6.95, LSQ 6.63, LWC 6.47. For W4A4: MinMax 14.49, PACT 18.25 (worse than MinMax!), LSQ 15.03, LWC 11.26. PACT and LSQ fail particularly in the weight-activation setting because LET changes weight distributions during training, and absolute threshold methods cannot track the moving target. Figure A5 visualizes this: LWC's learned clipping adapts to dramatically changing weight ranges during training, while PACT and LSQ fail to converge.

**Comparisons with non-uniform and mixed-precision methods (Table A15).** Against SpQR and SqueezeLLM (which use mixed-precision or non-uniform quantization for weight-only), OmniQuant achieves comparable or slightly better results at matched average bit-widths. For LLaMA-7B at ~4.16 average bits: OmniQuant 5.77 WikiText2 PPL, SpQR 5.87, SqueezeLLM (4.27 bits) 5.77. At ~3.15 average bits: OmniQuant 6.15, SqueezeLLM (3.24 bits) 6.13. OmniQuant is competitive despite using a simpler uniform quantization format, with the advantage of supporting both weight-only and weight-activation quantization.

**MMLU results for LLaMA-7B (Table A16).** At W4A16g128, all methods perform similarly: OmniQuant 37.50%, AWQ 37.71%, RTN 37.37% (FP16 38.41%). At W3A16g128: OmniQuant 35.60%, AWQ 35.43%, GPTQ 30.53%. At W2A16g128: OmniQuant 26.03%, GPTQ 23.83%, AWQ 22.58%. At W4A4: OmniQuant 26.93%, OS+ 25.72%, RTN 23.31%. The MMLU results corroborate the main findings: OmniQuant's advantage grows as bit-width decreases.

**LLaMA-1-7B asymmetric bits (Table A17).** W4A8 achieves 62.40 average accuracy (versus FP16 64.09). W4A6 achieves 61.64. W8A4 achieves 53.46 — worse than W4A4's 52.65, suggesting 4-bit activations are the primary bottleneck regardless of weight precision at this scale.

### Critical Assessment

The experimental evaluation is comprehensive in breadth — spanning five model families, sizes from 125M to 180B, weight-only and weight-activation configurations from 2 to 6 bits, perplexity and zero-shot accuracy and instruction-following benchmarks, and real-hardware deployment numbers. The consistency of OmniQuant's improvements across this wide matrix of conditions is the paper's strongest empirical argument. However, several aspects of the experimental design merit scrutiny.

**Does OmniQuant genuinely "attain the performance of QAT while maintaining the time and data efficiency of PTQ"?** The paper directly compares against only one QAT method (LLM-QAT) on only one configuration (LLaMA-7B W4A4). The result is impressive — OmniQuant 52.65% vs. LLM-QAT 41.27% average accuracy — but this single comparison cannot establish the general claim. LLM-QAT may not be the strongest QAT baseline available, and its surprisingly low accuracy (only 41.27% versus SmoothQuant's 38.41% — a QAT method barely outperforming a heuristic PTQ approach) raises questions about whether it represents the true QAT frontier. A more convincing case would require comparisons against additional QAT methods across multiple model sizes and bit-widths. The claim stands as "OmniQuant outperforms one QAT method on one setting" — which is evidence for the claim but not proof of the broader assertion that OmniQuant matches QAT performance generally.

**Does OmniQuant's advantage over prior PTQ methods hold at moderate bit-widths, or only at extreme compression?** The results clearly show that OmniQuant's advantages are largest at 2-bit and 3-bit quantization. At W4A16g128, the differences between OmniQuant, GPTQ, and AWQ are often within 0.1–0.2 PPL — OmniQuant 5.17 versus AWQ 5.20 for LLaMA-13B (Table 1). At W6A6, OmniQuant 63.17 versus SmoothQuant 62.81 for LLaMA-7B (Table 2). These differences, while consistent, may not be practically meaningful for many deployments. The paper's contribution is primarily enabling lower-bit quantization that was previously infeasible, not improving already-good 4-bit or 8-bit results. This is a legitimate contribution, but it means the method's practical value is concentrated in memory-constrained scenarios where 2-bit or 3-bit quantization is necessary.

**The single calibration dataset concern.** All experiments use WikiText2 as the calibration dataset. The ablation in Table A10 shows that using C4 or Pile for calibration produces similar WikiText2 PPL, but this only tests cross-dataset generalization within the language modeling domain. It does not address whether calibration on language modeling data generalizes well to the instruction-tuned or task-specific evaluations — the Vicuna benchmark (Figure 4) uses models quantized with WikiText2 calibration data, and while the results are positive, there's no ablation testing whether calibration on instruction-following data would further improve instruction-tuned model quantization.

**The 128-sample calibration budget.** Table A11 shows that W3A16 converges with 16 samples and W4A4 improves from 16 to 128 samples. However, the paper does not investigate whether different model sizes or families have different calibration data requirements. The 128-sample budget is carried over from GPTQ and AWQ conventions rather than empirically justified for OmniQuant specifically across all configurations. The slight degradation at 256 samples for W4A4 (11.41 PPL versus 11.23 at 128 samples) hints at potential overfitting that warrants more systematic investigation.

**The missing FLOPs-matched or latency-matched comparisons.** The paper reports training time for OmniQuant (Table A12: 1.6 hours for LLaMA-7B weight-activation, versus GPTQ's ~0.3 hours) but does not provide an apples-to-apples FLOPs or latency comparison. The claim that OmniQuant "maintains PTQ-level efficiency" is supported by showing it's faster than QAT, but the ~5× training time overhead relative to GPTQ is not discussed in terms of whether the accuracy gains justify the additional quantization cost. For practitioners quantizing many models, a 5× increase in quantization time may be significant.

**The deployment evaluation is weight-only only.** The paper's weight-activation quantization results (Table 2) are the most impressive — W4A4 with 52.65% accuracy on LLaMA-7B — but the deployment speedups (Table 3) only cover weight-only quantization. The paper acknowledges this: "W4A4 and W6A6 quantization methods lack out-of-the-box hardware support." This means the practical benefit of OmniQuant's strongest results remains unrealized. The weight-only deployment results show meaningful speedups (1.94× for W4A16g128 on LLaMA-7B), but these are achieved at configurations where OmniQuant's advantage over GPTQ/AWQ is marginal (W4A16g128: OmniQuant 5.77 versus GPTQ 5.85 versus AWQ 5.81 — Table 1). The configurations where OmniQuant dominates (W2A16, W4A4) either show suboptimal speedups due to hardware limitations (W2A16g128 is slower than W4A16g128) or cannot be deployed at all (W4A4).

**The OPT vs. LLaMA discrepancy in LET benefit is observed but not explained.** Table A3 shows that LET helps OPT weight-only quantization substantially (OPT-13B W3A16 degrades from 10.87 to 11.29 without LET) but helps LLaMA negligibly (LLaMA-13B W3A16 degrades from 5.65 to 5.68 without LET). The paper attributes this to OPT having "more severe weight outliers," but provides no analysis of what architectural or training differences cause this. Without such analysis, practitioners cannot predict whether their model will benefit from LET in weight-only mode without running the ablation themselves.

**Falcon-180B is evaluated at only one configuration (W3A16g512).** Table A18 shows strong results — 3.71 WikiText2 PPL versus 3.29 FP16 — but there are no comparisons against GPTQ or AWQ at this scale. The paper states that Falcon-180B requires 5 A100-80G GPUs at FP16 and fits on 1 GPU after quantization, which is impressive, but without baselines, we cannot assess how much of this compression capability is unique to OmniQuant versus achievable by simpler methods. The comparison is only against RTN (which degrades to 5.33 PPL), establishing that OmniQuant is better than doing nothing, but not that it's better than the best alternative PTQ method.

**Statistical significance and variance are not reported.** None of the perplexity or accuracy results include standard deviations, confidence intervals, or any measure of statistical reliability. The test sets are fixed (WikiText2 test split, full zero-shot benchmark sets), and quantization is deterministic given fixed calibration data and initialization, so the only source of variance would be from calibration data sampling. Table A10 addresses this partially by showing similar results across different calibration datasets, but a more rigorous approach would report results across multiple random seeds or calibration data subsamples with error bars.

**The Vicuna evaluation is limited.** The instruction-tuned model evaluation (Figure 4) uses only 80 questions from the Vicuna benchmark, compares only W3A16g128, and reports win rates without statistical testing. Win rates of 50.0% (OmniQuant vs. AWQ on 7B-chat) and 54.4% (OmniQuant vs. AWQ on 13B-chat) based on 160 paired comparisons could easily arise from random variation. The finding that AWQ loses to RTN on LLaMA-2-13B-chat is striking but based on an unspecified number of comparisons and lacks any measure of confidence.

**The paper implicitly claims generalizability but tests only standard benchmarks.** The evaluation suite (WikiText2, C4, PTB perplexity; PIQA, ARC, BoolQ, HellaSwag, Winogrande, MMLU accuracy; Vicuna win rates) covers language modeling, commonsense reasoning, and instruction-following. Missing are evaluations on: code generation (HumanEval, MBPP), mathematical reasoning (GSM8K, MATH), multi-turn dialogue, retrieval-augmented generation, and long-context tasks. The paper's claim that OmniQuant works across "various quantization configurations" and "various model families" is well-supported. The implicit claim that quantized models remain useful for downstream applications is supported only for the specific tasks evaluated.

In summary, the experimental evidence strongly supports the paper's central claim that OmniQuant outperforms prior PTQ methods, with the advantage most pronounced at low bit-widths (2-bit, 3-bit, W4A4). The claim of matching QAT performance is supported by a single comparison against one QAT method. The deployment benefits are demonstrated for weight-only quantization but the most impressive accuracy results (W4A4) remain un-deployed due to hardware limitations. The breadth of evaluation across model families and scales is a significant strength; the narrowness of the QAT comparison and the absence of statistical rigor are the primary weaknesses.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### W4A4 and W6A6 Deployment Cannot Be Realized on Current Hardware

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's most impressive accuracy results — W4A4 on LLaMA-7B achieving 52.65% average zero-shot accuracy, outperforming the QAT method LLM-QAT by +6.22% (Table 2) — are demonstrated only in simulation. When it comes to actual hardware deployment, the paper's speed and memory measurements (Table 3, Section 4.5) cover only weight-only quantization (W4A16g128, W3A16g128, W2A16g128). The paper explicitly acknowledges this gap:

> "we only explore the deployment of weight-only quantization in this study due to that W4A4 and W6A6 quantization methods lack out-of-the-box hardware support."

**The consequence.** This means the primary claimed advantage of OmniQuant over prior PTQ methods — superior weight-activation quantization that could, in principle, deliver both memory reduction AND computational speedup through low-bit matrix multiplication — remains a theoretical benefit. The configurations where OmniQuant's advantage over baselines is largest (W4A4 with +4.99% to +11.80% average accuracy gains) cannot actually be deployed. Meanwhile, the configurations that CAN be deployed (W4A16, W3A16, W2A16) are weight-only, providing memory savings but no computational speedup from activation quantization. Furthermore, at the deployed weight-only configurations, OmniQuant's advantage over GPTQ and AWQ is often marginal: at W4A16g128 on LLaMA-7B, OmniQuant achieves 5.77 WikiText2 PPL versus GPTQ's 5.85 and AWQ's 5.81 (Table 1) — differences of 0.04–0.08 PPL that may not translate to meaningful downstream improvements. The deployment results also reveal a non-monotonic speed relationship: W2A16g128 on LLaMA-7B achieves only 83.9 tokens/second versus W4A16g128's 134.2 tokens/second (Table 3), because "MLC-LLM's support for INT3/INT2 is currently suboptimal." So the configurations where OmniQuant's memory savings are most dramatic (2-bit) actually run slower than 4-bit on current hardware.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 3 is the sole deployment evaluation. It reports only weight-only quantization. The gap between weight-activation simulation results (Table 2) and deployable configurations (Table 3) is not bridged anywhere in the paper. The MLC-LLM framework the paper uses for deployment does not support W4A4 or W6A6 inference, and the paper provides no custom kernel implementation, no estimate of what W4A4 speedup would be if hardware supported it, and no plan for closing this gap beyond a brief mention that "enhancements to INT3/INT2 quantization speed are in our future roadmap."

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper acknowledges the limitation but treats it as an external hardware constraint rather than a problem the method or its evaluation should resolve. A practitioner reading this paper for deployment guidance learns that OmniQuant's headline accuracy numbers cannot be realized on actual hardware, and the configurations that CAN be deployed show much smaller advantages over simpler methods. The paper does not provide a projected timeline, a reference to emerging hardware with W4A4 support, or a software roadmap.

---

### W4A4 Deployment Cannot Be Realized on Current Hardware

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's central design — jointly optimizing LWC and LET — requires a calibration dataset and gradient-based optimization. All experiments use 128 randomly selected 2048-token segments from WikiText2 as the calibration data (Section 4.1). The paper demonstrates in Table A10 that using C4 or Pile for calibration produces similar results, and Table A11 shows that performance is stable across calibration sample counts from 16 to 256. The paper interprets this as robustness: "OmniQuant converges with as few as 16 samples."

**The consequence.** However, a subtle overfitting signal emerges in the very data the paper presents as evidence of robustness. For W4A4 on LLaMA-7B, Table A11 shows that WikiText2 perplexity improves from 11.56 at 16 calibration samples to 11.23 at 128 samples, but then *degrades* to 11.41 at 256 samples. Similarly, C4 perplexity degrades from 14.57 at 64 samples to 14.61 at 128 samples, then to 14.90 at 256 samples. This non-monotonic behavior — performance getting worse with more calibration data — is a signature of overfitting to the calibration objective. The block-wise L2 reconstruction loss (Equation 1) is a local proxy for the true objective (preserving global output quality on real data), and as the optimization budget increases (more data or more epochs), the learned parameters can exploit idiosyncrasies of the calibration distribution that do not generalize. Table A9 provides corroborating evidence: for W4A16, training beyond 10 epochs provides no improvement (5.87 PPL at epoch 10, 5.86 at epoch 40); for W2A16, going from 40 to 80 epochs only improves from 15.47 to 14.77 — a point of diminishing returns. The exclusion of the second FFN linear layer from LET because "the high sparsity of features after the non-linear layer leads to unstable gradients" (Section 3.3) is an explicit acknowledgment that the optimization has failure modes, but the paper does not investigate whether similar instabilities exist more broadly.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table A11 shows the W4A4 degradation at 256 samples. Table A9 shows saturation and diminishing returns at higher epochs. The paper's ablation on calibration dataset domain (Table A10) only tests perplexity on WikiText2 and C4 — it does not test whether the quantized model's zero-shot task accuracy (the metric where OmniQuant's advantages are largest, Table 2) varies with calibration data choice. A model that achieves 52.65% average accuracy when calibrated on WikiText2 might perform differently when calibrated on C4 or Pile, but this is never measured. The Vicuna benchmark results for LLaMA-2-chat (Figure 4) use models calibrated on WikiText2 — a language modeling dataset — to evaluate instruction-following quality, but no ablation tests whether calibration on instruction-following data would yield better or worse chat performance.

**Mitigation status.** Partially addressed through robustness ablations, but the paper does not treat overfitting as a fundamental limitation of the differentiable optimization approach. The authors present the calibration data experiments as evidence of robustness ("the variance in perplexity across diverse calibration datasets is marginal"), not as a warning about optimization-proxy misalignment. There is no discussion of regularization strategies, early stopping criteria beyond fixed epoch counts, or calibration data augmentation techniques that might improve generalization. The paper does not position overfitting to the calibration objective as an area requiring future work — it is mentioned only implicitly through the empirical observations.

---

### The QAT Comparison Is Limited to a Single Model, Single Configuration, and a Potentially Weak Baseline

**The assumption or constraint.** A central claim of the paper — reflected in its title ("Omnidirectionally Calibrated Quantization"), its abstract framing, and the explicit question in the introduction ("can we attain the performance of QAT, while maintaining the time and data efficiency of PTQ?") — is that OmniQuant matches or exceeds QAT performance. This claim is supported by a single experimental comparison: LLaMA-7B W4A4, where OmniQuant achieves 52.65% average zero-shot accuracy versus LLM-QAT's 41.27% and LLM-QAT+SmoothQuant's 46.43% (Table 2).

**The consequence.** Several concerns undermine the strength of this evidence. First, LLM-QAT's W4A4 accuracy of 41.27% is only marginally better than SmoothQuant's 38.41% — a heuristic PTQ method with no gradient optimization. A QAT method that barely improves over a simple baseline raises questions about whether it represents the true QAT performance ceiling. The paper does not explain LLM-QAT's surprisingly low accuracy, nor does it verify that the LLM-QAT reproduction matches the original paper's reported results. Second, the comparison is limited to a single model size (7B) and a single quantization configuration (W4A4). There are no QAT comparisons for LLaMA-13B, 30B, or 65B, for W6A6, or for weight-only configurations. The paper's claim of QAT-level performance generalizes from one data point to all configurations. Third, LLM-QAT requires 100k training samples and 90+ GPU hours, while OmniQuant uses 128 samples and 1.6 hours — a ~780× difference in data and ~56× difference in time. If LLM-QAT is an inefficient QAT method, a more efficient QAT approach (or simply LLM-QAT with more tuning) might close or reverse the gap without requiring 100k samples. The paper does not compare against other QAT methods or against stronger QAT baselines. Fourth, the paper does not report LLM-QAT's perplexity — only zero-shot accuracy — making it impossible to verify whether the QAT comparison holds for language modeling quality as well.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 2, LLaMA-7B W4A4 rows. This is the only QAT comparison in the entire paper. There are no QAT results in any appendix table, no QAT comparisons for OPT or LLaMA-2 models, and no discussion of why LLM-QAT underperforms so severely relative to its own paper's claims.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper treats the single LLM-QAT comparison as sufficient evidence for its central claim about QAT-level performance. There is no acknowledgment that the QAT baseline may be weak, no call for additional QAT comparisons, and no qualification of the claim to reflect the limited empirical support. The framing throughout the paper — including the abstract and Figure 1(a) — presents the QAT comparison as a settled finding rather than a preliminary result requiring further validation.

---

### The Learnable Equivalent Transformation's Benefit for Weight-Only Quantization Is Model-Family-Dependent and Not Diagnostically Explained

**The assumption or constraint.** OmniQuant presents LWC and LET as two complementary components that together form a unified quantization framework. However, the paper's own ablation (Table A3) reveals that LET's contribution to weight-only quantization varies dramatically across model families. For LLaMA-13B W3A16, removing LET (keeping LWC) changes perplexity from 5.65 to 5.68 — a negligible 0.03 PPL difference. For OPT-13B W3A16, the same removal changes perplexity from 10.87 to 11.29 — a 0.42 PPL difference, more than 10× larger. The paper's explanation for this discrepancy is a single sentence: "LLaMA's few weight outliers" (Appendix A3). This is offered as an empirical observation, not a diagnostic analysis.

**The consequence.** This model-family dependence creates a practical problem: a practitioner cannot know in advance whether their model will benefit from LET in weight-only mode without running the full OmniQuant pipeline AND an ablation with LET disabled — effectively doubling the quantization cost to answer a configuration question. The paper provides no method for predicting LET's utility from model architecture, training data, weight distribution statistics, or any other pre-quantization analysis. The finding that LET helps OPT substantially but LLaMA negligibly could stem from architectural differences (OPT uses ReLU, LLaMA uses SiLU; the paper does not discuss this), training data differences, model scale, or some interaction with the specific weight-only quantization configuration. Without understanding the mechanism, the finding does not generalize — a practitioner with a novel model architecture cannot apply the lesson.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table A3 provides the ablation numbers. The Appendix A3 text says: "LET significantly boosts OPT's performance but offers a slight enhancement for LLaMA, explained by LLaMA's few weight outliers." No further analysis is provided. Table A4 shows that LET's shifting and attention components also show model-dependent benefits (shifting removal degrades OPT-13B W4A4 by 2.0 PPL but LLaMA-13B by only 0.6 PPL). Figure A2 visualizes activation outliers for OPT but not a comparison between OPT and LLaMA weight distributions that would illuminate the discrepancy.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not treat this as a limitation. It reports the finding as an empirical fact and uses it to justify the design choice of disabling LET for LLaMA weight-only quantization. There is no suggestion for future work on diagnosing or predicting LET's model-dependent utility, and no recommendation for practitioners on how to determine whether their model needs LET without running the full ablation.

---

### The Block-Wise L2 Reconstruction Loss Is a Crude Proxy That May Not Align with Downstream Task Preservation

**The assumption or constraint.** The entire OmniQuant optimization is driven by minimizing the L2 distance between full-precision and quantized block outputs on calibration data (Equation 1). This objective is chosen for tractability — it decomposes the quantization problem into independent per-block subproblems, each solvable with limited memory and compute. The paper implicitly assumes that minimizing per-block output error will preserve global model behavior and downstream task performance.

**The consequence.** The L2 distance between intermediate activations is a weak proxy for what actually matters: whether the quantized model produces correct next-token predictions, coherent text, and accurate answers to zero-shot tasks. Two quantized blocks could have identical L2 reconstruction errors but very different effects on the model's final output distribution, particularly after errors propagate through multiple layers. The paper provides no theoretical or empirical analysis of how block-wise L2 error relates to global metrics like perplexity or accuracy. The observed saturation and occasional degradation at higher optimization budgets (Tables A9, A11) suggest that the proxy becomes misaligned under aggressive optimization — the optimizer finds parameters that reduce L2 error on the calibration set but do not improve (or actually worsen) generalization. The paper does not investigate whether alternative objectives — such as KL divergence between output distributions, layer-wise cosine similarity, or task-specific losses — would better preserve downstream performance. The Softmax quantization results (Table A7) are instructive: keeping Softmax at FP16 versus quantizing to 8-bit changes average accuracy by only 0.72 points (52.65 to 51.93), but quantizing to 4-bit drops it by 4.13 points (to 48.52). This sensitivity to the precision of a single operation suggests that local L2 error may not adequately capture the importance of specific activations to final output quality.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The block-wise quantization error visualization in Figure A3 shows that OmniQuant reduces per-block L2 error compared to grid-searched methods (AWQ, OS+), but never connects this reduction to the global metrics (perplexity, accuracy) reported in the main results. The ablation on calibration dataset domain (Table A10) tests whether calibration data choice affects perplexity, but not whether the L2 proxy generalizes across different types of downstream tasks. The zero-shot accuracy results (Table 2) are the only downstream evaluation, but they are never correlated with the per-block L2 errors achieved during optimization.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper adopts the block-wise L2 objective from prior work (AdaRound, BRECQ, GPTQ) without analyzing its suitability for the specific challenges of LLM quantization — particularly the interaction between the objective and the joint LWC+LET optimization, where LET transforms weights in ways that may affect how L2 error propagates. The paper does not suggest alternative objectives, regularization strategies to prevent proxy misalignment, or methods for validating that reduced L2 error translates to improved downstream performance.

---

### The Difficulty Estimation Is Computationally Prohibitive for Deployment — O(2000×) the Quantization Budget

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's theoretical foundation — joint optimization of LWC and LET under a block-wise L2 objective — assumes that calibration data is available and that convergence can be reached within 20–40 epochs. While the paper demonstrates this for WikiText2-calibrated models, it does not address a critical deployment consideration: the calibration data distribution must be representative of the deployment distribution for the optimization to produce a model that generalizes well. The paper's calibration robustness experiments (Table A10) only test within-domain generalization (WikiText2 → C4 → Pile, all English language modeling corpora). They do not test cross-domain generalization — calibrating on language modeling data and deploying on code generation, mathematical reasoning, or multi-turn dialogue.

**The consequence.** For practitioners deploying LLMs in specialized domains (medical text, legal documents, code, non-English languages), the question is not whether OmniQuant works when calibrated on WikiText2 — it is whether OmniQuant works when calibrated on data representative of the target domain, and whether 128 samples is sufficient for domain-specific calibration. The paper provides no guidance on how to select calibration data for a given deployment scenario, what happens if the calibration data is domain-mismatched, or whether the learned parameters overfit to the specific linguistic properties of the calibration corpus (vocabulary, style, topic distribution) rather than learning general quantization-friendly transformations. The Vicuna benchmark results (Figure 4) use models calibrated on WikiText2 — a language modeling dataset — to evaluate instruction-following chat quality, but there is no comparison against models calibrated on instruction-following data or chat transcripts. If OmniQuant's learned parameters adapt to the surface statistics of the calibration text, a practitioner deploying a quantized chat model might get different (and potentially worse) results than the paper reports.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table A10 shows cross-corpus perplexity within the language modeling domain. No experiments test calibration on non-language-modeling data or evaluate on tasks from different domains. The instruction-tuned model evaluation (Figure 4) uses WikiText2-calibrated models without an ablation on chat-data calibration. The MMLU results (Table A16) are for LLaMA-7B only and use the same WikiText2 calibration — there is no test of whether MMLU accuracy (which spans 57 subjects including STEM, humanities, social sciences) is sensitive to calibration data domain.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper treats calibration robustness as settled by the within-domain experiments (Table A10), without acknowledging that language modeling corpora are a narrow slice of possible deployment domains. There is no recommendation for practitioners on calibration data selection, no warning about domain mismatch, and no investigation of whether the learned LET and LWC parameters transfer across domains or need to be re-learned for each deployment context.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

OmniQuant introduces a **third paradigm between PTQ and QAT** that fundamentally reframes what "post-training quantization with gradients" can mean. Prior to this work, the field operated under an implicit assumption: if you want gradient-based optimization of quantization parameters, you either pay the full QAT cost (tuning all model weights, requiring hundreds of GPU-hours and 100k+ training samples) or you restrict yourself to hand-crafted heuristics and grid search (PTQ, which is fast but fails at low bit-widths). OmniQuant demonstrates that this tradeoff is a false dichotomy — it is an artifact of *what gets optimized*, not an inherent property of gradient-based quantization. By freezing the full-precision weights and introducing only a small set of learnable quantization-control parameters (clipping strengths, scaling factors, shifting offsets) that are mathematically constrained to produce deployment-ready models with zero overhead, OmniQuant achieves QAT-level performance while preserving PTQ-level data and compute efficiency.

This is best understood as a **complexity-control insight** rather than a single algorithmic innovation. The key reframing is: the optimization space for LLM quantization is large but highly structured, and by constraining the search to fusable parameters that control *how* quantization is performed (rather than *what* is being quantized), gradient-based optimization becomes tractable, effective, and deployment-friendly. The paper's empirical results validate this framing decisively: on LLaMA-7B W4A4, OmniQuant achieves 52.65% average zero-shot accuracy using 128 samples and 1.6 GPU-hours, outperforming the QAT method LLM-QAT (46.43% with 100k samples and 90+ GPU-hours) by +6.22 percentage points (Table 2). This inverts the expected PTQ-QAT hierarchy — a PTQ method beating QAT on both accuracy AND efficiency — and establishes that gradient-optimized quantization parameters can substitute for full weight tuning when the parameter space is appropriately constrained.

The paper also resolves a tension in the LLM quantization literature between **weight-only and weight-activation methods**. Prior work treated these as separate problem domains with separate solutions (AWQ and GPTQ for weight-only; SmoothQuant, Outlier Suppression+, and RPTQ for weight-activation; Table A1 in the Appendix contrasts OmniQuant with all of them). OmniQuant provides a unified framework — LWC for weight clipping, LET for activation transformation, jointly optimized — that works across both regimes with only a configuration toggle (LET disabled for LLaMA weight-only where it provides negligible benefit, per Table A3). This unification matters practically because it means a single codebase, a single calibration pipeline, and a single set of hyperparameters can serve all quantization needs for a given model family, reducing the engineering complexity of deployment.

Beyond the methodological reframing, the paper identifies and provides evidence for a **synergy between weight clipping and activation transformation** that prior sequential/heuristic methods could not capture. Table A2 shows that on LLaMA-7B W4A4, LET alone achieves 48.83% accuracy, LWC alone achieves 50.15%, and the joint LWC+LET optimization achieves 52.65% — a gain larger than the improvement from either component alone. Table A8 further demonstrates that simultaneous training outperforms iterative alternation (12.87 vs. 13.51 average perplexity for per-epoch iteration), confirming that the coupling is dynamic: LET benefits from seeing LWC's current clipping behavior at each gradient step. This finding redirects research attention from independent solutions for weight sensitivity and activation outliers toward joint optimization frameworks, and suggests that prior methods' decision to handle these problems separately was not just suboptimal in degree but in kind — it prevented the adaptive balancing that enables extreme low-bit compression.

The paper also provides one of the first systematic documentations of **optimization-proxy misalignment in differentiable quantization** — the phenomenon where the block-wise L2 reconstruction loss, while tractable and effective up to a point, can overfit to the calibration distribution under aggressive optimization. This appears in the non-monotonic behavior at 256 calibration samples for W4A4 (Table A11: perplexity degrades from 11.23 to 11.41 when moving from 128 to 256 samples), the diminishing returns at higher training epochs (Table A9: W2A16 improves only from 15.47 to 14.77 when going from 40 to 80 epochs), and the explicit exclusion of the second FFN linear layer from LET due to unstable gradients from post-activation sparsity (Section 3.3). These observations, while not theoretically analyzed, establish proxy misalignment as a first-class challenge for differentiable PTQ methods — one that will need to be addressed through better objectives, regularization, or calibration data selection as the field pushes toward even more aggressive compression.

Finally, the paper shifts the narrative around **what makes LLM quantization hard**. Prior work emphasized activation outliers as THE central challenge (SmoothQuant, Outlier Suppression+), with weight quantization treated as a secondary concern solvable by reconstruction (GPTQ) or channel-wise scaling (AWQ). OmniQuant's results suggest a more nuanced picture: activation outliers ARE severe (Table A5 shows removing the post-LayerNorm LET causes the largest degradation, from 12.87 to 19.87 average perplexity), but joint optimization reveals that weight clipping is equally critical — and that the interaction between the two, not either in isolation, determines quantization quality. This reframes LLM quantization from two independent problems (weight sensitivity, activation outliers) to a single joint optimization challenge, and implies that future methods should not decompose the problem prematurely.

In terms of **research directions that become more attractive**: differentiable PTQ with learnable quantization parameters is now a proven approach, and investment in better optimization objectives, more expressive (but still fusable) parameterizations, and methods for preventing proxy overfitting is clearly warranted. LLM-specific QAT methods (like LLM-QAT) become less attractive as a default approach — the paper shows that carefully chosen learnable parameters can outperform full weight tuning, suggesting that QAT's search space may be unnecessarily large and that the field should focus on identifying the minimal set of parameters that need to be learned.

In terms of **research directions that become less attractive**: hand-crafted heuristics with grid-searched parameters (AWQ's approach, Outlier Suppression+'s scaling search) are now demonstrably suboptimal at low bit-widths, and the paper provides clear evidence that gradient-based optimization consistently finds better configurations (Figure A3 shows OmniQuant's block-wise quantization error is substantially lower than grid-searched methods). Further incremental improvements to grid search strategies — finer grids, better proxy metrics, more exhaustive search — are unlikely to close the gap with differentiable optimization, and research effort is better directed at making gradient-based methods more efficient and robust.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Training a lightweight difficulty predictor to replace the 2048-sample calibration sweep for optimal clipping initialization.** The paper's calibration pipeline uses 128 fixed samples and a fixed epoch budget for all models and configurations. However, the convergence behavior varies significantly across bit-widths — W4A16 converges in 10 epochs, W2A16 requires 40–80 epochs (Table A9) — and across model families (LET benefits OPT substantially but LLaMA negligibly, per Table A3). A natural extension is to train a small predictor model that takes model weight statistics (per-channel min/max/variance, activation outlier magnitude, weight distribution shape) and calibration data characteristics as input, and predicts: (a) the optimal number of training epochs, (b) the optimal learning rates for LWC and LET, (c) whether LET should be enabled for weight-only quantization, and (d) the expected perplexity improvement over MinMax baseline. The training data for this predictor would come from running OmniQuant with different hyperparameter configurations across many model scales and families — the paper already has much of this data from its ablation studies (Tables A3, A9). A strong follow-up would demonstrate that the predictor generalizes to unseen architectures (e.g., trained on OPT and LLaMA-1, tested on LLaMA-2 and Falcon) and reduces the total wall-clock time for quantization by adaptively allocating compute (early stopping when the predictor estimates convergence, skipping LET when the predictor estimates negligible benefit).

**Replacing the block-wise L2 loss with a distribution-aware objective that is less susceptible to calibration overfitting.** The paper's own results hint at proxy misalignment: performance degrades at 256 calibration samples for W4A4 (Table A11), saturates early for easier configurations (Table A9), and the second FFN layer is excluded from LET due to optimization instability (Section 3.3). A direct follow-up would replace the L2 reconstruction loss with a KL divergence between the full-precision and quantized block's output distributions (estimated via softmax over the feature dimension), a contrastive loss that explicitly penalizes rank reversal among output channels, or an attention-aware loss that weights reconstruction error by the attention weights those activations will receive in subsequent layers. The key experiment: train OmniQuant with the new loss on the same calibration data and compare against the L2 baseline on (a) WikiText2 perplexity, (b) zero-shot task accuracy (Table 2's benchmark suite), and (c) out-of-distribution generalization when the calibration data domain differs from the evaluation domain (e.g., calibrate on Pile, evaluate on WikiText2, C4, and PTB). The hypothesis — motivated by Table A11's non-monotonicity — is that a distribution-aware loss would show monotonic improvement with calibration data quantity and eliminate the degradation at 256 samples.

**Jointly optimizing the pretraining and quantization recipes under a total compute budget.** The paper demonstrates that OmniQuant enables practical 2-bit and 3-bit quantization of models up to 180B parameters (Falcon-180B at W3A16g512 achieves 3.71 WikiText2 PPL versus 3.29 FP16, Table A18), but the question of *which model to train and how to quantize it* for a given total compute budget remains open. A Bit-level scaling law analysis (extending Figure A4's per-model analysis) would train a series of models at different scales (e.g., 1B, 3B, 7B, 13B, 30B, 65B parameters) under a fixed pretraining compute budget, then quantize each with OmniQuant at multiple bit-widths (W2A16, W3A16, W4A16, W6A6, W4A4), and measure the downstream task accuracy as a function of total bits (parameters × bits-per-weight) and total inference FLOPs. The goal would be to produce a Chinchilla-style scaling law (Hoffmann et al., 2022) but for the joint pretraining-quantization optimization: for a given total bits budget, what is the optimal combination of model size and quantization bit-width? The paper's Figure A4 hints that OmniQuant makes 3-bit quantization achieve "comparable performance like 4-bit quantization in the trade-off between model bits and perplexity," but a systematic study across scales would quantify this tradeoff precisely and potentially reveal that a 30B model at 2-bit outperforms a 7B model at 4-bit for the same memory footprint — a finding with direct implications for deployment decisions.

**Stress-testing OmniQuant on tasks where calibration domain mismatch is severe.** The paper evaluates only within the language modeling domain (WikiText2, C4, PTB perplexity; zero-shot commonsense reasoning tasks) and uses WikiText2 for calibration throughout. A critical stress test would calibrate OmniQuant on a narrow domain (e.g., Wikipedia articles, GitHub code, or PubMed abstracts) and evaluate on a maximally different domain (e.g., calibrate on code, evaluate on mathematical reasoning via GSM8K; calibrate on formal writing, evaluate on conversational dialogue via Vicuna; calibrate on English, evaluate on multilingual benchmarks). The hypothesis — motivated by the paper's implicit claim that OmniQuant learns general quantization-friendly transformations, not domain-specific ones — is that the learned LET scaling/shifting factors and LWC clipping thresholds should transfer across domains, meaning that calibration domain choice should have minimal impact on downstream task accuracy. If the hypothesis holds, it validates OmniQuant as learning fundamental properties of the model's activation distributions. If it fails, it reveals that the differentiable optimization is overfitting to calibration data statistics in ways the paper's within-domain ablations (Table A10) cannot detect, and would motivate research on domain-invariant calibration or regularization techniques. The experiment should measure both perplexity AND task accuracy — the paper shows they can diverge (Table 2 vs. Table A23), and domain mismatch effects may manifest differently on different metrics.

**Extending the learnable equivalent transformation to the full transformer computation graph, including the second FFN layer and the value projection.** The paper explicitly excludes the second FFN linear layer from LET due to unstable gradients from post-activation sparsity (Section 3.3) and omits explicit value transformation because "its distribution has already been channel-wise altered by the inverse transformation associated with the output projection linear layer" (Section 3.3). A direct follow-up would investigate whether these exclusions are fundamental or addressable. For the second FFN layer: can gradient clipping, learning rate scheduling specific to sparse layers, or a regularized transformation (e.g., constraining the scaling factors to be close to 1.0 for channels with high sparsity) stabilize optimization? The experiment would enable LET on the second FFN layer with these stabilization techniques and measure whether the additional degrees of freedom improve W4A4 perplexity and accuracy beyond the current OmniQuant baseline (Table 2, Table A5). For the value projection: an ablation where explicit V transformation is added (with the scaling factor initialized to 1.0 and learned) would test whether the paper's claim about inverse transformation from the output projection is sufficient, or whether independent V scaling provides additional benefit. A positive result — stabilizing LET on the second FFN layer and improving W4A4 accuracy by even 1–2 percentage points — would demonstrate that the full transformer graph is quantizable and that current exclusions are engineering challenges, not fundamental barriers. A negative result — no improvement despite stabilization — would confirm that the current LET placement is already optimal and that the excluded layers are not bottlenecked by activation outliers.

**Evaluating whether OmniQuant's learned parameters transfer across fine-tuning stages for the same base model.** The paper evaluates on base LLaMA models and on LLaMA-2-chat (instruction-tuned) separately, each calibrated independently on WikiText2. A practical question for deployment pipelines is: if you quantize a base model with OmniQuant (learning optimal LWC and LET parameters), then fine-tune the quantized model on a downstream task (using QLoRA-style adapters, or full fine-tuning of a small subset of parameters), do the learned quantization parameters remain optimal, or does fine-tuning shift the activation/weight distributions enough to require re-optimization? The experiment would: (1) quantize LLaMA-7B with OmniQuant W4A16g128, recording the learned clipping strengths and scaling factors; (2) fine-tune the quantized model on an instruction dataset (e.g., Alpaca) using QLoRA; (3) evaluate the fine-tuned model on Vicuna and compare against (a) a model quantized with OmniQuant AFTER fine-tuning (i.e., calibration on the instruction-tuned model) and (b) a model quantized with RTN then fine-tuned. The hypothesis — motivated by OmniQuant's claim that it learns "quantization-friendly transformations" that are properties of the model architecture, not the specific weights — is that the pre-fine-tuning quantization parameters should transfer well, meaning that quantize-then-fine-tune performs comparably to fine-tune-then-quantize. If confirmed, this would enable a deployment pipeline where quantization happens once on the base model and all downstream fine-tuned variants inherit the quantized representation without re-calibration, dramatically reducing the cost of serving multiple fine-tuned models from the same base.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Single-GPU deployment of large models for research labs and small organizations.** The paper's most immediately actionable finding is that OmniQuant enables models that cannot fit on a single GPU at FP16 to run comfortably after quantization. Table 3 shows LLaMA-65B — a model that is "OOM" (Out Of Memory) on a single A100-80G at FP16 — fits in 41.0 GB running memory at W4A16g128 and achieves 24.3 tokens/second. LLaMA-30B at W4A16g128 runs in 21.7 GB with 43.6 tok/s. Falcon-180B at W3A16g512 fits on a single A100-80G with 65 GB weight memory (Table A18), down from 335 GB requiring 5 GPUs at FP16. For academic labs, startups, and researchers in low-resource settings who have access to a single high-memory GPU but not multi-GPU clusters, OmniQuant makes models in the 30B–180B range accessible for inference, fine-tuning (via QLoRA on top of the quantized weights), and experimentation. The quantization time (1–16 hours on a single GPU, Table A12) is a one-time cost amortized over all subsequent inference, making it practical for projects with limited compute budgets. The finding that OmniQuant preserves instruction-following capability on LLaMA-2-chat at W3A16g128 (Figure 4: OmniQuant wins 54.4% of comparisons against AWQ on 13B-chat, 56.2% against RTN) means that chatbots and conversational agents built on large models can also benefit from this single-GPU deployment.

**Memory-constrained edge and consumer deployment via 2-bit weight quantization.** Table 1 demonstrates that OmniQuant achieves usable perplexity at W2A16 configurations where prior methods collapse: LLaMA-7B at W2A16g128 achieves 9.72 WikiText2 PPL (GPTQ: 44.01, AWQ: 2.6e5); LLaMA-13B achieves 7.93 (GPTQ: 15.60, AWQ: 2.8e5). Table 3 shows that W2A16g128 reduces LLaMA-7B's weight memory from 12.6 GB (FP16) to 2.2 GB and running memory from 14.4 GB to 4.1 GB — a 3.5× reduction in total GPU memory. At this memory footprint, LLaMA-7B becomes deployable on consumer GPUs with 6–8 GB VRAM (e.g., RTX 2060, GTX 1070), on edge devices with unified memory (Apple M1/M2 with 8–16 GB RAM), and potentially on mobile devices with sufficient RAM. The caveat is that MLC-LLM's INT2/INT3 support is "currently suboptimal" (Section 4.5), with W2A16g128 achieving only 83.9 tok/s versus W4A16g128's 134.2 tok/s on LLaMA-7B — the speed penalty may limit real-time applications. However, for batch processing, offline inference, or applications where memory constraints are the binding constraint (not latency), 2-bit quantization via OmniQuant is immediately practical. The paper's roadmap mentions "enhancements to INT3/INT2 quantization speed," suggesting this limitation is temporary.

**Cost-efficient serving of multiple fine-tuned model variants from a single base model.** The paper's weight-only quantization results at W4A16g128 and W3A16g128 show near-lossless perplexity preservation on LLaMA models: LLaMA-7B W4A16g128 achieves 5.77 WikiText2 PPL versus 5.68 FP16 (Table 1); W3A16g128 achieves 6.15 versus 5.68 — a 0.47 PPL degradation for 3-bit quantization. For organizations serving many fine-tuned variants of the same base model (e.g., a LLaMA-7B base with 100 domain-specific LoRA adapters for different customers), the memory savings compound: each variant requires storing only the quantized base weights (3.2–3.8 GB at W3A16g128/W4A16g128 for LLaMA-7B, per Table 3) plus a small adapter. Without quantization, serving 10 variants of LLaMA-13B (24.3 GB each at FP16) would require ~243 GB of GPU memory just for weight storage. With OmniQuant W3A16g128 (5.8 GB per variant, Table 3), the same 10 variants require ~58 GB — fitting on a single A100-80G rather than requiring 4 GPUs. The paper's demonstration that OmniQuant preserves zero-shot accuracy at W3A16 (LLaMA-7B MMLU: OmniQuant 35.60% versus FP16 38.41%, Table A16) and instruction-following quality (Figure 4: OmniQuant W3A16g128 on LLaMA-2-7B-chat achieves 50% win rate against AWQ) suggests that the quantized variants retain most of the base model's capabilities, making this a practical cost-saving strategy for model-serving infrastructure.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper positions OmniQuant against both PTQ baselines (GPTQ, AWQ, SmoothQuant, Outlier Suppression+) and one QAT baseline (LLM-QAT). The tradeoffs are:

- **Prefer OmniQuant over GPTQ/AWQ when** quantizing to 3-bit or 2-bit weight-only (W3A16, W2A16) where GPTQ and AWQ experience significant degradation or catastrophic failure. Table 1 shows GPTQ at W2A16 on LLaMA-13B achieves 5.5e3 PPL versus OmniQuant's 13.21; AWQ at W2A16g128 achieves 2.8e5 versus OmniQuant's 7.93. The paper explicitly relies on this evidence: "the performance benefits of OmniQuant become more pronounced as the quantization bit size decreases" (Section 4.2). At 4-bit weight-only (W4A16), all methods perform similarly (LLaMA-13B W4A16g128: OmniQuant 5.17, GPTQ 5.20, AWQ 5.20 — Table 1), and the choice between them should be based on implementation convenience, deployment framework compatibility, and quantization time (OmniQuant is ~5× slower than GPTQ per Table A12).

- **Prefer OmniQuant over SmoothQuant/Outlier Suppression+ when** performing weight-activation quantization below W8A8, especially W4A4. The paper states that "SmoothQuant can nearly achieve lossless W8A8 quantized models" (Section 4.3), making W8A8 a solved problem where OmniQuant provides marginal benefit. At W6A6, OmniQuant's advantage is consistent but small (LLaMA-7B W6A6: OmniQuant 63.17 average accuracy versus SmoothQuant 62.81, Table 2). At W4A4, the gap is large: OmniQuant 52.65 versus OS+ 48.43 versus SmoothQuant 38.41 (LLaMA-7B, Table 2). The paper's evidence positions W4A4 as the primary configuration where OmniQuant is decisively better than prior PTQ methods.

- **Prefer OmniQuant over LLM-QAT (QAT) when** both quantization quality AND quantization efficiency matter. The single comparison point (LLaMA-7B W4A4, Table 2) shows OmniQuant achieving higher accuracy (52.65% vs. 46.43% for LLM-QAT+SmoothQuant) with ~780× less data and ~56× less GPU time. However, this comparison is limited to one model size and one configuration, and the paper does not establish that LLM-QAT is a strong QAT baseline (its W4A4 accuracy of 41.27% barely exceeds SmoothQuant's 38.41%). Until additional QAT comparisons are available, the claim of QAT-level performance should be treated as supported specifically against LLM-QAT, not against QAT methods in general.
