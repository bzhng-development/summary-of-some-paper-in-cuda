Here are the takeaways from this Ryan Peterman interview (subscriber Q&A on the "big tech vs. startup" decision for new graduates):

**The opening dilemma**

Ryan frames the episode around a question he and his UCLA friends wrestled with at graduation: for your first job, should you join a FAANG-style big tech company or a startup (Series B and earlier)? Seven years into the industry, he's seen enough outcomes to walk through what actually matters. The episode is structured as a five-part comparison followed by a concrete recommendation—with the caveat that he ran his conclusion by friends currently working at startups to make sure it wasn't just big-tech bias talking.

**Prestige as a permanent unlock**

Ryan's most personal data point comes from his own job-search experience. Before having any notable company on his resume, he "applied to hundreds of places only to get a few responses." The next time he applied—now carrying Amazon on his resume—he "was able to get an interview at every place I applied to." The mechanism is blunt: big tech gives you "a well-known stamp of approval" that works across the entire industry. Startups, by contrast, mostly aren't well-known enough to create that wide effect—though top startups with strong funding do carry prestige within smaller entrepreneurial circles. His framing is pragmatic rather than aspirational: "like it or not, the prestige of the companies you have worked at opens many doors."

**Career growth: the ladder vs. the lottery**

In big tech, promotions through the early levels are "fast and exponentially easier than later ones," and once you earn them, they function as another axis of social proof. Ryan cites his own trajectory: getting Staff (IC6) at Meta let him land Staff-level interviews elsewhere "even though I only had a few years of experience." Startups typically don't even have leveling systems; growth is tied entirely to company growth. If you're early at a startup that takes off—Uber is his example—"your career would have grown much faster than if you joined big tech." But startups fail in the average case, so the career-growth dimension follows the same pattern he flags throughout: **higher variance, lower average case outcomes** for startups, with a higher ceiling for the winners.

**Skill development: best practices vs. zero-to-one scrambling**

Big tech offers "excellent structure in place to help you learn industry best practices," and mentorship is easy to get since it's a baseline expectation for senior engineers to grow others. Ryan's thesis is that this accelerates engineering fundamentals. Startups demand wearing many hats, emphasize zero-to-one work, and often pull you into non-engineering tasks—but mentorship is scarcer since small companies are "strapped for resources." His verdict: "developing your engineering fundamentals in this environment is riskier." A learning advantage for startups he does acknowledge is direct access to founders and executives, "which would be unheard of in big tech."

**Talent and network: density vs. depth**

Big tech talent is "solid on average," with deeply technical experts at the high levels—though Ryan notes there's "less entrepreneurial spirit" among them. Startup talent varies dramatically by company. Some (he names OpenAI) have higher talent density than big tech, but he adds the wrinkle that the startups with higher density "probably don't hire new grads." The network advantage of startups is relational proximity: you can build relationships with founders and execs that would be impossible at scale.

**Compensation: liquidity and optionality**

Big tech compensation is "excellent" and liquid, with lower risk. Startups offer lower compensation on average because equity is unlikely to be worth much—but carry the chance of a much higher peak if the company succeeds. Ryan doesn't treat compensation as the main event, but he does make a point about early-career optionality: "many of my friends quit their big tech jobs after a few years to start their own companies. They were able to do this because they built up savings from their high-paying big tech jobs."

**The recommendation: start in big tech**

Ryan's bottom line, even for people whose eventual goal is entrepreneurial: start in big tech for the first one to three years. The compounding reasons are the stamp of approval that "will set you up for the rest of your career," the value of learning industry best practices before going anywhere else, the fast early-career promotions that unlock another tier of social proof, and the savings cushion that creates life optionality. He explicitly calls out that after those first few years, once the fast early-career gains run out, "you can re-evaluate your options." The startup path "could also go well too," but the governing pattern is the same across all five categories: startups offer higher variance and a lower average case. He stresses that he tested this conclusion with friends currently at startups to confirm it wasn't just self-serving bias—and reports that "it's not a hot take to think that big tech is a great choice for your first 1-3 years. After that point, it depends on your career goals and interests."

---

His consistent **higher variance, lower average case outcomes** lens threads every category—prestige, career growth, skill development, talent, and compensation all follow the same risk/reward shape. The **stamp of approval** from a big-tech name functions as a repeatable career asset; the savings from liquid big-tech comp becomes a separate **optionality** engine that fuels later entrepreneurial bets. Together they form a pragmatic first-act playbook: use big tech's structure to build a resume, a network, a skills baseline, and a cash runway, then redirect that accumulated capital toward higher-variance moves once the early-career curve flattens.
