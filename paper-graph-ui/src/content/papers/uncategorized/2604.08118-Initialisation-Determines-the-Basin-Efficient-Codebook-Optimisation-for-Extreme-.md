# Initialisation Determines the Basin: Efficient Codebook Optimisation for Extreme LLM Quantization

**ArXiv:** [2604.08118](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.08118)

## 🎯 Pitch

At 2-bit LLM compression, additive quantization can collapse to 352 perplexity—and quadrupling beam search barely helps, because greedy codebook initialization traps the model in an inescapable bad basin. The authors show this fragility scales with a representational ratio ρ = N/KM, then fix it via an output-aware EM initialization that cuts pre-tuning error by 3.5× and dominates all compute budgets.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper identifies and addresses a critical bottleneck in extreme (2-bit) additive quantization of LLMs: the sensitivity of codebook initialisation to the **representational ratio ρ** (the ratio of weight groups to representational capacity, N/KM), which governs whether greedy sequential initialisation traps the model in poor optimisation basins that subsequent beam search and PV-tuning cannot escape. Evaluating on WikiText-2, C4, and six zero-shot benchmarks with Llama 3.2 3B, Llama 3.1 8B, and Qwen 2.5 3B, the authors propose **OA-EM** (Output-Aware Expectation-Maximisation) — a drop-in replacement for greedy initialisation that uses Hessian-weighted Mahalanobis distance to concentrate codebook capacity on output-sensitive weight groups — and demonstrate that it reduces pre-PV-tuning perplexity from 60.61 to 17.39 on Llama 3.2 3B at 2 bpp, a 3.49× improvement. After PV-tuning, OA-EM maintains a persistent advantage across all beam widths, epoch budgets, and architectures (e.g., 11.53 vs. 11.76 WikiText-2 perplexity at beam 8), establishing that initialisation determines the optimisation basin and that additional search benefits models only when initialisation is already good—with the severity of this basin-persistence phenomenon scaling directly with ρ.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Extreme Quantization Fails Catastrophically at the Wrong Moment

The fundamental question this paper tackles is disarmingly specific: **why does additive quantization—a method that works well at 3 bits per parameter—sometimes fail catastrophically at 2 bits, and what is the actual bottleneck preventing it from succeeding?** This matters because 2-bit compression is not a minor incremental improvement over 4-bit; it represents a qualitative threshold where each parameter receives only 4 possible values, leaving essentially no margin for approximation error. Crossing this threshold unlocks genuinely transformative deployment scenarios—running 3B–8B parameter LLMs on consumer GPUs, mobile edge devices, and ARM CPUs—but the crossing itself has proven treacherous in ways that are poorly understood.

The paper identifies that the prevailing response to quantization failure is to *increase computational effort*: wider beam search, more epochs per layer, larger calibration sets. The authors argue that this response targets the wrong bottleneck entirely. The true bottleneck, they claim, is **codebook initialisation**—the very first step of the quantization pipeline, which places the model into a region of the solution space from which subsequent optimisation may never recover. This is not merely a claim about efficiency (that better initialisation saves compute); it is a stronger claim that poor initialisation can render the optimisation problem *effectively unsolvable* regardless of how much compute is subsequently deployed.

### Why This Problem Is Important: Deployment Economics and the Edge

The practical stakes are high for two interconnected reasons.

**First, edge deployment is memory-bound, not compute-bound.** The paper is explicit about this from the opening: "hardware where memory, not compute, is the binding constraint" (Section 1). On ARM CPUs, microcontrollers, and mobile SoCs, the primary bottleneck is not the speed of arithmetic operations but the bandwidth and capacity of memory. Additive quantization with free-form codebooks (as in AQLM, Egiazarian et al., 2024) is uniquely attractive for this hardware because dequantization reduces to an O(1) lookup-table (LUT) read—a pure memory access with **zero multiply-accumulate (MAC) operations** per weight group. This stands in contrast to structured methods like lattice-based codebooks (QuIP#, GLVQ) or trellis codes (QTIP), which require active computation during inference (matrix–vector multiplication, Babai rounding) that becomes prohibitively expensive on hardware without dedicated tensor cores.

The paper's framing is that this LUT dequantization pathway is *critical for edge deployment*, and therefore making free-form additive quantization work reliably at 2 bits is not just an academic exercise—it directly determines whether 3B–8B models can run on the smallest and most power-constrained devices. When AQLM fails at 2 bpp (returning WikiText-2 perplexity of 352.39 with beam 4, compared to 7.28 for the FP16 model), the consequence is not just a bad research result; it means the entire LUT-based compression pipeline becomes unusable at the very compression rate where it is most needed.

**Second, the failure mode is precisely the one that practitioners cannot afford to debug.** The paper shows that greedy initialisation at 2 bpp on Llama 3.2 3B produces a model with perplexity over 350—effectively useless. A practitioner encountering this might naturally try increasing the beam width, only to find that quadrupling it (from 4 to 16) reduces perplexity from 352.39 to a still-catastrophic 46.01, at a cost of 2.8× more quantization time (6.1h to 16.9h). The behaviour is both expensive and deceptive: it appears to be improving (perplexity drops with wider beams) but converges to a solution that remains far worse than what better initialisation achieves with a narrow beam (OA-EM at beam 4: 16.82 perplexity, 6.1h). This creates a dangerous optimisation trap where practitioners invest ever-more compute chasing diminishing returns, not realizing that the root cause lies in a decision made at the very start of the pipeline.

### Where Existing Approaches Fall Short

The paper identifies limitations in four categories of prior work, each of which either fails to address the 2-bit regime or addresses it through mechanisms orthogonal to the initialisation problem.

**Scalar post-training quantization degrades below 3 bpp.** Methods like GPTQ (Frantar et al., 2023), AWQ (Lin et al., 2024), SmoothQuant (Xiao et al., 2023), and OmniQuant (Shao et al., 2024) represent each weight with an independent low-bit scalar value. The paper acknowledges these as effective at 4 bits where they are "near-lossless" but notes they "degrade rapidly below 3 bpp because scalar codes cannot efficiently use the information budget at extreme compression" (Section 2). The core limitation is geometric: a scalar codebook of 4 values (2 bits) can only represent 4 distinct weight magnitudes, which is insufficient to capture the distribution of weight values in modern LLMs. Vector quantization—encoding *groups* of weights jointly—escapes this limit by exploiting correlations within groups, allowing the same bit budget to represent exponentially more distinct configurations. This is why the paper focuses on additive (vector) quantization. The initialisation problem it studies simply does not arise in scalar methods.

**Structured vector quantization sidesteps the problem rather than solving it.** Methods like QuIP# (Tseng et al., 2024a), QTIP (Tseng et al., 2024b), SpinQuant (Liu et al., 2025), and GLVQ (Zhang et al., 2025) achieve strong 2-bit performance by constraining codebook geometry. QuIP# uses fixed E8 lattice codebooks combined with randomised Hadamard transforms; QTIP uses trellis coded quantization; GLVQ assigns each weight group a custom lattice defined by a learnable generation matrix, with Babai rounding providing a closed-form nearest-point solution. The paper explicitly acknowledges that these methods "sidestep the free-form initialisation trap entirely" (Section 2) because they eliminate the combinatorial assignment problem—there is no discrete search over codebook entries, so there is no initialisation to get wrong.

However, the paper argues that this escape comes at a cost that matters for the target deployment scenario. Lattice dequantization requires O(d) floating-point MAC operations per weight group at runtime (matrix–vector multiplication via the generation matrix). On hardware with dedicated tensor cores, this cost is manageable; on ARM CPUs, microcontrollers, and low-power inference accelerators where ALU cycles are the primary bottleneck, these runtime MAC operations can dominate latency. The paper cites Egiazarian et al. (2024) as demonstrating "significant real-world speedups on CPU and edge hardware" for LUT-based dequantization over methods requiring runtime computation. The implication is that structured methods solve the 2-bit compression problem by changing the rules (constrained geometry avoids combinatorial search), but in doing so they give up the zero-MAC inference property that makes free-form additive quantization uniquely attractive for edge deployment.

The paper's positioning relative to GLVQ is particularly instructive: "Our work is orthogonal to GLVQ: while GLVQ bypasses free-form codebooks to avoid optimisation traps, we show that free-form codebooks are highly capable at 2 bpp when the initialisation basin is corrected via OA-EM, preserving the LUT inference pathway." This frames the contribution not as competing with structured methods but as rescuing the free-form paradigm from a failure mode that previously made it uncompetitive.

**Additive quantization (AQLM) lacks initialisation analysis.** AQLM (Egiazarian et al., 2024) introduced the additive quantization framework for LLMs, and PV-tuning (Malinovskii et al., 2024) extended it with end-to-end fine-tuning. But the paper identifies a critical gap: "Neither work examines how initialisation quality interacts with compression rate or persists through fine-tuning." This is the precise void the paper fills. AQLM's default initialisation—residual k-means, a form of greedy sequential fitting—was presumably designed for the regime where the method was originally developed (information retrieval, where codebook capacities are larger relative to the number of vectors being encoded). The paper's central insight is that this initialisation strategy, which works adequately at 3 bpp, becomes the dominant failure mode at 2 bpp because of a regime transition that prior work neither identified nor analysed.

**PV-tuning mitigates but does not eliminate initialisation sensitivity.** PV-tuning is the strong optimiser in this story. Table 2 shows that it compresses a 43-point perplexity gap (60.61 vs. 17.39 pre-PV) to just 0.23 points (11.76 vs. 11.53 post-PV) on Llama 3.2 3B at beam 8. PV-tuning is powerful enough that a naïve interpretation might be: "initialisation doesn't matter because fine-tuning fixes everything." The paper directly challenges this interpretation through the basin-persistence evidence: OA-EM is better in *every* beam configuration after PV-tuning (Table 2), the beam-width response is asymmetric (wider beams help OA-EM but can hurt the greedy baseline), and the Pareto analysis shows OA-EM at beam 4 outperforms greedy at beam 16 despite using 2.8× less compute. The conclusion is that PV-tuning improves models *within* their existing optimisation basin rather than moving them *between* basins—a finding with implications beyond quantization, suggesting that optimisation geometry in compressed model spaces can trap fine-tuning in local optima determined by initialisation.

**EM-based vector quantization exists but is not output-aware and targets a different stage.** GPTVQ (van Baalen et al., 2024) applies EM-based VQ within the GPTQ framework, and LSQ++ (Martinez et al., 2018) applies EM to additive quantization in information retrieval. The paper distinguishes OA-EM on two dimensions: (1) it operates within additive quantization for LLMs, where multiple codebooks interact combinatorially (unlike GPTVQ's single codebook), and (2) it is output-aware, optimising reconstruction error in activation space using Hessian-weighted Mahalanobis distance. Critically, OA-EM serves as an *initialisation stage* for downstream beam search, not as the primary quantizer. This distinction matters because it means OA-EM does not need to solve the full combinatorial assignment problem—it just needs to produce codebook centroids that place beam search in a favourable region of the solution space.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper's positioning can be understood through three conceptual frames that it introduces or elevates.

**The representational ratio ρ as a predictive lens.** Definition 1.1 introduces ρ = N/KM as a single scalar that "predicts when quantization becomes sensitive to initialisation." At 3 bpp with M=3 codebooks of K=256 entries each, K^M = 16.8M representable combinations vastly exceeds the number of weight groups in a Llama 3.2 3B layer (ρ ≈ 0.07, overcomplete regime). At 2 bpp with M=2, capacity collapses to K^2 = 65,536 combinations (ρ ≈ 18, undercomplete regime)—a 256× reduction. This is not merely a quantitative change; the paper argues it is a *qualitative regime transition* where weight groups begin competing for limited representational capacity, making initial placement of codebook centroids critical. A centroid allocated to an unimportant weight group is a centroid permanently lost to groups that dominate the reconstruction loss. This framework provides a principled explanation for *when* the initialisation bottleneck will bite, making it a diagnostic tool for practitioners and a conceptual contribution beyond the specific OA-EM algorithm.

**Initialisation as determining the optimisation basin.** The paper's strongest claim—and the one it supports with the most extensive evidence—is that codebook initialisation "determines the persistent optimisation basin reached by subsequent search and fine-tuning." This is framed not as an efficiency concern (bad initialisation means more compute needed) but as a *geometry* concern (bad initialisation means the final solution, even after unlimited optimisation, is worse). The evidence for this claim is the basin-persistence pattern: OA-EM's advantage survives PV-tuning across every beam width, epoch budget, compression rate, model scale, and architecture tested. The asymmetric beam-width scaling—where wider beams help OA-EM but can *worsen* the greedy baseline post-PV (Table 2: greedy b=16 at 12.01 vs. b=8 at 11.76)—is particularly compelling because it is inconsistent with the hypothesis that all initialisations converge to the same optimum.

**The quality-compute frontier and the "improve initialisation, not search" principle.** The practical message is encapsulated in the Pareto analysis and the paper's closing principle: "improving initialisation quality may be more effective than increasing search intensity." Table 16 shows that OA-EM at beam 4 (6.1h, 11.53 perplexity, 0.589 average downstream accuracy) strictly dominates greedy at beam 16 (16.9h, 12.01 perplexity, 0.585 accuracy)—a 2.8× speedup with better quality on all metrics. The paper frames this as actionable guidance for practitioners: when additive quantization produces poor results, the first response should be to improve codebook initialisation, not to widen the beam. This principle is positioned as generalising beyond the specific OA-EM algorithm: any learned-codebook VQ method that relies on greedy sequential initialisation should be expected to exhibit the same sensitivity when ρ exceeds 1, and should benefit from output-aware initialisation strategies.

**The paper as orthogonal, not competitive, to structured methods.** The authors repeatedly emphasise that their contribution is complementary to work on structured codebooks (QuIP#, QTIP, GLVQ). They are not claiming OA-EM produces better absolute perplexity than GLVQ or QTIP—they are claiming that OA-EM makes free-form additive quantization viable at 2 bpp, preserving the LUT dequantization advantage that structured methods sacrifice. The paper's scope is deliberately narrow: within the free-form additive quantization paradigm, initialisation is the dominant bottleneck at extreme compression, and OA-EM is a principled solution that works across models and settings. This orthogonal positioning avoids having to compete on absolute metrics with methods that use fundamentally different (and more powerful) codebook geometries, while establishing a clear and defensible contribution.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

This paper builds a **drop-in replacement for the codebook initialisation stage** of additive quantization (specifically, AQLM's residual k-means step) that uses output-aware EM with Hessian-weighted distances to place codebook centroids in regions of weight space that matter most for the model's output, rather than simply minimising Euclidean distance to the weight vectors themselves. The system solves the problem that greedy sequential initialisation traps additive quantization in poor optimisation basins at extreme compression rates (2 bits per parameter), and it does so by incorporating second-order information from calibration activations into the centroid placement process—making the initialisation aware of which weight errors actually degrade the model's predictions.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The system has five major components that operate sequentially within AQLM's existing layer-wise quantization pipeline:

1. **Calibration Data** — a set of input sequences from C4 (128 sequences, 4096 tokens each) that are fed through the unquantized FP16 model to collect per-layer activation matrices `X`. These capture which weight groups are "output-important" for realistic inputs.
2. **Hessian Approximation** — for each weight group in a layer, the system computes a damped block-diagonal Hessian `H_i = X_i^T X_i + λI`, where `X_i` is the activation submatrix corresponding to that group's input dimensions. This matrix encodes the second-order sensitivity of the layer's output to perturbations in that weight group.
3. **OA-EM Initialiser** — the core contribution: starting from k-means-initialised centroids and assignments, OA-EM alternates between (M-step) optimising centroids to minimise Hessian-weighted reconstruction error via Adam gradient steps, and (E-step) hard-reassigning each weight group to its nearest centroid under Mahalanobis distance `d(w, c) = (w - c)^T H (w - c)`. This runs for `R=3` rounds with `S=100` Adam steps per round, producing refined codebook centroids.
4. **Beam Search (AQLM Standard)** — taking the OA-EM-initialised codebooks, beam search with width `b` finds near-optimal codeword assignments for each weight group by maintaining `b` active candidates and deferring commitment across the `M` codebooks. This searches over the combinatorial space of codeword combinations but operates on the improved centroid geometry.
5. **PV-Tuning (Post-Quantization Fine-Tuning)** — end-to-end fine-tuning of both codebooks and indices via straight-through estimation, using Adam with learning rate `3×10⁻⁴`, batch size 32, 10K calibration samples, and 5 epochs, selecting the best WikiText-2 checkpoint. This is applied identically to both baseline and OA-EM models, isolating the effect of initialisation.

Information flows sequentially: calibration activations → Hessian computation → OA-EM initialisation (starting from k-means centroids) → beam search over OA-EM centroids → PV-tuning → evaluation. The key design decision is that OA-EM replaces only the initialisation stage; all downstream components (beam search, PV-tuning) remain identical to the standard AQLM pipeline.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the additive quantization framework (AQLM) that OA-EM operates within, because understanding why initialisation matters requires understanding the two-stage optimisation (centroid placement via residual k-means, then assignment via beam search).
- **Second**, the formal analysis of why greedy initialisation fails—Proposition 1's decomposition of the suboptimality gap into direct cost, coupling, and residual mismatch terms—because this provides the theoretical lens for understanding the representational ratio `ρ` and why the 3 bpp → 2 bpp transition is qualitative, not gradual.
- **Third**, the representational ratio `ρ = N/KM` itself, which is the paper's central diagnostic tool for predicting when initialisation becomes critical.
- **Fourth**, OA-EM in full detail: the M-step centroid optimisation objective, the E-step reassignment rule, the Hessian-weighted Mahalanobis distance, and the training hyperparameters—because this is the proposed solution.
- **Fifth**, how OA-EM connects back to Proposition 1: the mechanism by which Hessian weighting reduces the direct cost `∥δ∥²` and residual mismatch terms for output-important groups.
- **Sixth**, the beam search and PV-tuning pipeline that operates on OA-EM's output, with specific configurations and cost models, since understanding the full system requires knowing what happens after initialisation.

This is primarily an **analysis-and-mitigation paper** whose core idea is that codebook initialisation determines the optimisation basin for additive quantization, and that output-aware EM initialisation can place the model in a better basin—one from which standard beam search and PV-tuning can reach solutions that greedy initialisation cannot, regardless of search budget.

#### Additive Quantization Framework (AQLM)

Before explaining what OA-EM changes, we must understand the system it modifies. AQLM (Egiazarian et al., 2024) is a post-training quantization method that compresses each layer's weight matrix `W ∈ R^{d_out × d_in}` by partitioning it into groups of `g` consecutive input-channel weights and representing each group as a sum of codewords drawn from learned codebooks.

**Weight group encoding.** The weight matrix is reshaped into `N` groups of size `g`, where `N = (d_out × d_in) / g`. Each group `w ∈ R^g` is a slice of `g` weights from the original matrix (specifically, `g` consecutive input-channel weights for a given output channel, following the standard AQLM partitioning). The quantized representation of group `w` is:

$$\hat{w} = \sum_{m=1}^{M} c_{m, b_m}$$

where `M` is the number of additive codebooks, `c_{m, b_m} ∈ R^g` is the `b_m`-th entry (codeword) of codebook `C_m ∈ R^{K × g}`, `K=256` is the number of entries per codebook, and `b_m ∈ {1, ..., K}` is the index selected from codebook `m` for this weight group.

**What it computes:** each group of `g` original weights is replaced by the sum of `M` codewords, one drawn from each of `M` codebooks. The indices `b_1, ..., b_M` are stored as the compressed representation (requiring `M × log₂(K) = M × 8` bits per group), and the codebooks `C_1, ..., C_M` are stored separately. At inference time, dequantization reconstructs `\hat{w}` by summing the `M` indexed codewords—this is the O(1) lookup-table (LUT) operation that requires zero multiply-accumulate (MAC) operations, since the sum of pre-computed codeword entries can itself be pre-computed and stored in a lookup table.

**Why this form:** encoding each weight group as a sum of codewords from `M` codebooks gives `K^M` possible representable vectors per group while storing only `M × K` codewords. At 2 bpp with `g=8` and `M=2`, each group of 8 weights uses 16 bits (2 indices × 8 bits), achieving 2 bits per weight. The `K^M = 256² = 65,536` representable combinations per group vastly exceed what scalar quantization could represent with the same bit budget (which would be limited to `2¹⁶ = 65,536` scalar values spread across 8 independent weights, with no ability to exploit within-group correlations). The additive structure is crucial: it decomposes the vector quantization problem into `M` independent codebook selections, making the combinatorial search (beam search over `M` codebooks) tractable—exhaustive search would cost `K^M = 65,536` comparisons per group, while beam search costs `O(M · b · K)`.

**Layer-wise objective.** The codebooks and assignments are optimised to minimise the reconstruction error of the layer's output on calibration data, not the weight error directly:

$$\mathcal{L} = \|XW - X\hat{W}\|_F^2$$

where `X ∈ R^{T × d_in}` is the calibration activation matrix (input to this layer, collected by running calibration sequences through the unquantized model up to this layer), `W` is the original FP16 weight matrix, and `\hat{W}` is the quantized weight matrix reconstructed from the codebooks and assignments.

**What it computes:** the squared Frobenius norm of the difference between the layer's output using original weights (`XW`) and the layer's output using quantized weights (`X\hat{W}`). This is a matrix of shape `T × d_out` (tokens × output channels), and the Frobenius norm sums the squared errors over all token-position and output-channel pairs. The result is a single non-negative scalar representing the total output distortion introduced by quantization.

**Why this form over weight-space MSE:** weight error (`\|W - \hat{W}\|²`) treats all weight perturbations as equally important, but in a trained network, errors in weights that interact with large-magnitude or frequent activation patterns cause much larger output errors than errors in weights that interact with near-zero activations. The output-aware objective `\|XW - X\hat{W}\|²` automatically weights weight errors by the calibration activation statistics—a weight error on a row that is multiplied by large activations in `X` contributes more to the loss than the same weight error on a row multiplied by near-zero activations. This is the same principle behind GPTQ's use of Hessian information, and it is essential for extreme compression because it allows the limited codebook capacity to be concentrated on the weights that actually matter for the model's behaviour.

**Two-stage optimisation in standard AQLM.** Optimisation proceeds in two distinct stages:

1. **Codebook initialisation via residual k-means.** Codebook `C_1` is fitted to the weight vectors `{w_i}` using standard k-means clustering (Euclidean distance, hard assignments, centroid updates as cluster means). Then, for each weight group `i`, the residual `r_i^{(1)} = w_i - c_{1, b_{1,i}}` is computed, where `c_{1, b_{1,i}}` is the assigned first-codebook codeword. Codebook `C_2` is fitted to these residuals `{r_i^{(1)}}`. This process repeats for `M` codebooks: each subsequent codebook is fitted to the residuals left by all previous codebooks. This is "greedy sequential" because each codebook is optimised independently of later codebooks—`C_1` is chosen without knowing what `C_2` will be able to represent.

2. **Assignment refinement via beam search.** With codebooks fixed, the assignments `(b_1, ..., b_M)` for each weight group are refined using beam search of width `b`. The search proceeds through the `M` codebooks sequentially: at step 1, the `b` best first-codebook entries are retained; at step 2, for each of the `b` retained prefixes, all `K` second-codebook entries are evaluated, and the `b` best combinations overall are retained; and so on. The "best" combination is the one minimising `\|X_i w_i - X_i (c_{1,b_1} + c_{2,b_2})\|²` for that weight group. This is not globally optimal (it greedily prunes at each step) but approximates exhaustive search at cost `O(M · b · K)` per group rather than `O(K^M)`.

#### The Suboptimality Decomposition (Proposition 1)

To understand *why* greedy sequential initialisation can fail so badly, the paper provides a formal decomposition of the error introduced by making the first codebook choice independently of the second. This decomposition is not used to compute anything at runtime—it is an analytical tool that reveals the structure of the problem.

Consider a single weight group `w` with `M=2` codebooks (the 2 bpp case). Let `(i*, j*)` be the jointly optimal assignment—the pair of codeword indices that minimises reconstruction error when chosen together. Let `(i_g, j_g)` be the greedy sequential assignment—where `i_g` minimises the first-codebook error alone, and then `j_g` minimises the residual error given `i_g`.

Define the first-codebook displacement vector:

$$\delta = c_{1, i_g} - c_{1, i^*}$$

which is the difference between the greedily-chosen first codeword and the one that would have been chosen in the joint optimum. Define the joint-optimal residual:

$$r^* = w - c_{1, i^*}$$

which is the weight vector after subtracting the jointly-optimal first codeword.

**Proposition 1 (Greedy Suboptimality Bound).** The gap between the greedy reconstruction error `ε_g = \|w - (c_{1,i_g} + c_{2,j_g})\|²` and the optimal reconstruction error `ε* = \|w - (c_{1,i^*} + c_{2,j^*})\|²` decomposes as:

$$\varepsilon_g - \varepsilon_* = \|\delta\|^2 + 2\langle \delta, c_{2, j_g} - r^* \rangle + \left(\|r^* - c_{2, j_g}\|^2 - \varepsilon_*\right)$$

where `∥δ∥²` is the direct cost (squared Euclidean norm of the first-codebook displacement vector), `⟨δ, c_{2,j_g} - r*⟩` is the inner product coupling term between the displacement and the second-codebook choice, and `(∥r* - c_{2,j_g}∥² - ε*) ≥ 0` is the residual mismatch—always non-negative because `j*` is the optimal second-codebook choice for residual `r*`.

**What it computes:** given the optimal joint assignment and the greedy assignment, this expression decomposes the total error gap into three additive components. The direct cost `∥δ∥²` is the penalty for picking a suboptimal first codeword, independent of the second codebook. The coupling term `2⟨δ, c_{2,j_g} - r*⟩` captures whether the second codebook can compensate for the first-codebook error—if `c_{2,j_g}` lies in the right direction relative to the displacement and the optimal residual, this term can be negative, reducing the gap. The residual mismatch is the irreducible penalty from fitting `C_2` to the greedy residual distribution rather than the jointly optimal one; it is always non-negative.

**Why this decomposition matters:** it reveals that greedy error has three independent sources, and their relative importance changes with the representational capacity `K^{M-1}`. When `M=2` (2 bpp), the second codebook has only `K=256` entries to simultaneously represent the true weight structure *and* compensate for first-codebook errors. The coupling term can be negative (second codebook helps) or positive (second codebook makes things worse), but its magnitude is bounded by how well `C_2` can approximate `r* - δ` for all the different displacements `δ` produced by greedy first-codebook choices across all weight groups. When `M=3` (3 bpp), the second and third codebooks together provide `K² = 65,536` residual correction configurations, making it much more likely that some combination can absorb a given displacement—the coupling term can be negative and large for many more weight groups.

**The regime transition in practice.** At 3 bpp, `C_2` and `C_3` together have 65,536 possible residual vectors. If the first-codebook displacement `δ` for a typical weight group is modest (because k-means on 256 centroids can cover the weight distribution reasonably well), there is high probability that some combination of `C_2` and `C_3` entries can represent `r* - δ` with small error. The coupling term is frequently negative and substantial, and the residual mismatch is small. At 2 bpp, `C_2` alone must cover both the residual distribution and the displacement compensation. The 256 centroids are forced to spread themselves across these two competing demands, and for many weight groups, no single entry can do both well. The coupling term is either positive or only weakly negative, and the residual mismatch is large because `C_2` was trained on greedy residuals that differ systematically from the jointly optimal residuals.

#### The Representational Ratio ρ

The paper formalises the regime transition through a single scalar diagnostic:

$$\rho = \frac{N}{K^M}$$

where `N` is the number of weight groups in a layer (`d_out × d_in / g`), `K=256` is the number of entries per codebook, and `M` is the number of codebooks. `K^M` is the total number of distinct vectors that can be represented by the additive codebook (all possible combinations of `M` codeword indices).

**What it computes:** the average number of weight groups competing for each representable code combination. When `ρ < 1`, there are *more* representable points than weight groups (overcomplete regime)—in principle, every weight group could be assigned a unique code combination, and initialisation errors for one group don't force errors for others. When `ρ > 1`, there are *fewer* representable points than weight groups (undercomplete regime)—multiple weight groups must share the same or similar code combinations, creating competition for limited capacity. When `ρ ≫ 1`, this competition is severe.

**Why this specific ratio:** the denominator `K^M` is the capacity of the codebook system—the number of distinct points it can represent in `R^g`. The numerator `N` is the number of points it needs to represent. The ratio directly captures the overcompleteness/undercompleteness distinction that determines whether the codebook system has slack to absorb initialisation errors. Alternative diagnostics like the bitrate alone (2 bpp vs. 3 bpp) or the number of codebooks `M` don't capture the competition dynamic because they don't account for the number of weight groups.

**Numerical illustration for Llama 3.2 3B.** At 3 bpp (`M=3`, `g=8`), a typical transformer layer has `d_out=3072`, `d_in=3072`, so `N = 3072 × 3072 / 8 ≈ 1.18M` weight groups. With `K^M = 256³ ≈ 16.8M`, we get `ρ ≈ 0.07`—the codebook capacity exceeds demand by a factor of ~14, meaning most weight groups can be assigned near-optimal unique code combinations, and initialisation errors are easily absorbed. At 2 bpp (`M=2`, `g=8`), the same layer has `N ≈ 1.18M` weight groups but now `K^M = 256² = 65,536`, giving `ρ ≈ 18`. The codebook capacity is now a factor of ~18 *smaller* than demand. This 256× reduction in capacity (`16.8M → 65,536`) is what the paper identifies as the qualitative regime transition, and what makes initialisation the dominant bottleneck.

**The predictive claim.** The paper claims that `ρ` predicts *when* the initialisation bottleneck will be severe, not just that it correlates with performance degradation. The argument is: when `ρ ≪ 1`, greedy initialisation errors can be compensated by later codebooks (the coupling term in Proposition 1 is reliably negative) and capacity is plentiful, so PV-tuning can easily move between optimisation basins. When `ρ ≫ 1`, the coupling term is unreliable, capacity is contested, and PV-tuning becomes trapped in the basin determined by initialisation. The strong form of this claim is that `ρ` is a *causal* diagnostic: for any learned-codebook VQ method using greedy sequential initialisation, if `ρ ≫ 1`, you should expect basin-persistence effects regardless of the specific architecture or training data.

---

#### OA-EM: Output-Aware EM Initialisation

OA-EM is the proposed solution: a drop-in replacement for the residual k-means initialisation that refines each codebook's centroids using EM with Hessian-weighted Mahalanobis distance. "Drop-in replacement" means it produces codebook centroids that are fed into exactly the same beam search and PV-tuning pipeline as the standard k-means centroids—no other component of the quantization pipeline is modified.

**The Hessian approximation.** Before running OA-EM, the system computes, for each weight group `i` in the current layer, a damped block-diagonal Hessian approximation:

$$H_i = X_i^\top X_i + \lambda I$$

where `X_i ∈ R^{T × g}` is the submatrix of the calibration activation matrix corresponding to the input dimensions of weight group `i` (specifically, the `g` columns of `X` that multiply with the `g` weights in group `i`), `T` is the number of calibration tokens, and `λ = 0.01 × diag(H)` is a damping constant proportional to the mean diagonal element to ensure positive definiteness. The term `X_i^T X_i` is a `g × g` matrix that approximates the second derivative of the output reconstruction error `∥X_i w_i - X_i \hat{w}_i∥²` with respect to perturbations in the weight group `w_i` (the Gauss-Newton approximation to the Hessian, ignoring second-order derivatives of the model's nonlinearities between layers, consistent with the GPTQ framework).

**What it computes:** for each weight group, a `g × g` positive definite matrix that encodes how sensitive the layer's output is to perturbations in that specific group of `g` weights. If the activation patterns in `X_i` have large magnitude or strong correlations across the `g` dimensions, `H_i` will have large eigenvalues, indicating that even small weight perturbations will cause large output errors. If the activation patterns are near-zero or uncorrelated, `H_i` will be close to `λI`, indicating low sensitivity.

**Why damped block-diagonal form:** the full Hessian of the layer output with respect to all weights would be `(d_out × d_in) × (d_out × d_in)`—far too large to store or invert. The block-diagonal approximation assumes that weight groups are independent in their second-order effects (no cross-group Hessian terms), which is reasonable because different weight groups operate on disjoint sets of input dimensions. The damping `λI` ensures `H_i` is invertible and well-conditioned, preventing numerical issues when `X_i` has near-zero singular values. The choice of `λ = 0.01 × mean(diag(X_i^T X_i))` follows the GPTQ convention—it provides enough regularisation for stability without significantly distorting the sensitivity estimates.

**Initial state.** OA-EM starts from the same k-means-initialised centroids and assignments that the standard pipeline would use. That is, codebook `C_m` is first fitted using standard Euclidean k-means on the residual vectors from previous codebooks (the same residual k-means procedure described above). OA-EM then refines these centroids for `R=3` EM rounds.

**M-step (centroid optimisation).** With assignments `{b_i}` fixed for the current codebook, OA-EM optimises the centroids `{c_k}` to minimise the Hessian-weighted reconstruction error:

$$\mathcal{L}_{EM} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} e_i^\top H_i e_i, \quad e_i = w_i - c_{b_i}$$

where `e_i ∈ R^g` is the error vector for weight group `i` (the difference between the original weight group and its assigned codeword), `H_i` is the `g × g` Hessian approximation for that group, and the sum runs over all `N` weight groups in the layer.

**What it computes:** for each weight group, compute the error vector `e_i = w_i - c_{b_i}`. Transform this error through the Hessian: `e_i^T H_i e_i` is the squared Mahalanobis norm of the error, which measures how much output distortion this weight error causes (since `H_i` encodes output sensitivity). Average this over all weight groups. The result is a single scalar—the expected output distortion per weight group under the current centroid assignments.

**Why this form over standard k-means centroid update (mean of assigned vectors):** standard k-means minimises `∑ ∥w_i - c_{b_i}∥²` (Euclidean distance), which places centroids at the arithmetic mean of their assigned weight vectors. This implicitly weights all weight groups equally. If a centroid is assigned to a mixture of high-sensitivity groups (large `H_i` eigenvalues) and low-sensitivity groups (small `H_i` eigenvalues), the Euclidean mean will split the difference, degrading reconstruction for the high-sensitivity groups—precisely the groups that most affect the model's output. The Hessian-weighted objective `e_i^T H_i e_i` makes the centroid optimisation "care more" about high-sensitivity groups: a given Euclidean error `∥e_i∥` contributes more to the loss if `H_i` is large, so the optimisation will shift the centroid to reduce error on those groups, even at the expense of larger Euclidean error on low-sensitivity groups. This directly addresses the failure mode identified in Proposition 1: by reducing `∥δ∥²` (the direct cost) for output-important groups, it prevents the large displacements that the second codebook cannot compensate for.

**Centroid update procedure.** Rather than a closed-form solution (which exists for the Euclidean case as the cluster mean), the M-step uses `S=100` steps of the Adam optimiser applied to the centroids directly. The learning rate is `η = 10⁻⁴` with cosine annealing to `0.1η` (i.e., final learning rate `10⁻⁵`). The centroids are treated as optimisable parameters, and gradients of `L_EM` with respect to each centroid `c_k` are computed via backpropagation (or equivalently, via the analytic gradient `∂L_EM/∂c_k ∝ ∑_{i: b_i=k} H_i (c_k - w_i)`). The choice of Adam over a simple closed-form update is pragmatic: the Hessian-weighted objective does not have a simple closed-form minimiser (unlike Euclidean k-means, where the optimal centroid is the mean of assigned vectors), and 100 steps of gradient-based optimisation is computationally cheap relative to the subsequent beam search.

**E-step (hard reassignment).** With centroids fixed, each weight group is reassigned to the centroid that minimises the Mahalanobis distance:

$$b_i \leftarrow \arg\min_{k \in [K]} (w_i - c_k)^\top H_i (w_i - c_k)$$

where the minimisation is over all `K=256` centroids in the current codebook, and the distance is the same Hessian-weighted quadratic form used in the M-step objective.

**What it computes:** for each weight group `i` and each candidate centroid `c_k`, compute the squared Mahalanobis distance `(w_i - c_k)^T H_i (w_i - c_k)`. Assign the group to the centroid that minimises this distance. This is a hard assignment (each group goes to exactly one centroid), matching the k-means E-step structure.

**Why hard assignment over soft assignment (probabilistic mixture):** additive quantization ultimately requires each weight group to be assigned to a single codeword per codebook (the stored indices `b_m` are discrete). Soft assignments would produce a probabilistically weighted average that cannot be represented in the final compressed model. Hard assignment ensures consistency with the downstream beam search, which operates over discrete codeword choices. The computational cost is `O(N × K × g²)` for the Mahalanobis distance computation (the `g × g` matrix-vector products), but with `g=8`, this is tractable.

**Residual sequential structure.** OA-EM operates within AQLM's residual framework: codebooks are still fitted sequentially. First, OA-EM refines `C_1` (fitted to the original weight vectors). Then, the residuals `r_i = w_i - c_{1, b_{1,i}}` are computed, and OA-EM refines `C_2` (fitted to these residuals). For `M=3` codebooks, a third round refines `C_3` on the second-stage residuals. This means OA-EM does not eliminate the sequential fitting—the coupling problem from Proposition 1 still exists—but it *mitigates* it by ensuring that each codebook's centroids are placed to minimise output distortion given the assignments, rather than minimising Euclidean weight error.

**Connection to Proposition 1.** OA-EM reduces the dominant error terms in the decomposition through two mechanisms. First, the Hessian-weighted M-step concentrates first-codebook centroids on output-sensitive weight groups. This reduces `∥δ∥²` (the direct cost) for the groups that dominate the output reconstruction loss—even if some low-sensitivity groups receive larger displacements, the output penalty is small. Second, because the first-codebook centroids are better placed (the residuals `r_i` are closer to the jointly optimal residuals `r*` for important groups), the second-codebook `C_2` is trained on a residual distribution that more closely matches what it will encounter during beam search. This reduces the residual mismatch term. The paper does not claim OA-EM eliminates the greedy suboptimality bound—only that it substantially reduces its magnitude in the regime where `ρ ≫ 1` makes the standard approach brittle.

**Hyperparameter summary.** OA-EM uses `R=3` EM rounds (each round being one E-step followed by one M-step of `S=100` Adam steps), learning rate `η = 10⁻⁴` with cosine annealing to `10⁻⁵`, damping constant `λ = 0.01 × diag(H_i)` for the Hessian approximation, and the codebook size `K=256` and group size `g=8` follow the standard AQLM configuration. The total OA-EM overhead is 3 rounds × 100 Adam steps = 300 centroid update steps per codebook, which adds a fixed cost to the initialisation stage that is amortised over the subsequent beam search (and in many configurations, OA-EM's better initialisation reduces the number of beam search epochs needed, offsetting or exceeding this cost).

---

#### Beam Search and PV-Tuning (Post-Initialisation Pipeline)

OA-EM's output—refined codebook centroids for all `M` codebooks per layer—is fed into the standard AQLM pipeline without modification. This section documents that pipeline in detail, as the paper's experimental results depend critically on its configuration.

**Beam search over codeword assignments.** With codebooks fixed, AQLM performs beam search to find near-optimal codeword indices for each weight group. The search proceeds codebook-by-codebook:

1. **First codebook:** for each weight group `i`, compute the reconstruction error `e_{i,k} = ∥X_i w_i - X_i c_{1,k}∥²` for all `K=256` first-codebook entries. Retain the `b` entries with smallest error, where `b` is the beam width. Store the `b` candidate indices and their cumulative errors.
2. **Second codebook:** for each weight group, for each of the `b` retained first-codebook candidates, evaluate all `K=256` second-codebook entries: `e_{i,k_1,k_2} = ∥X_i w_i - X_i (c_{1,k_1} + c_{2,k_2})∥²`. From the `b × K` candidate pairs, retain the `b` with smallest cumulative error. Repeat for subsequent codebooks if `M > 2`.
3. **Final selection:** after processing all `M` codebooks, the candidate with smallest cumulative error is selected as the assignment for each weight group.

**Cost and hyperparameters.** The beam search cost per weight group is `O(M · b · K)` evaluations of the reconstruction error. The paper sweeps beam widths `b ∈ {4, 8, 16}` and maximum epochs `e ∈ {5, 100}`. An "epoch" is a full pass over all weight groups in a layer; multiple epochs allow the beam search to revisit assignments now that later codebooks have been processed, potentially improving them. Early stopping triggers when the relative improvement in mean squared error between epochs falls below `0.01`. The cost model ranges from 6.1 hours (b=4, e=100) to 16.9 hours (b=16, e=100) for full Llama 3.2 3B quantization on a single A100 GPU.

**Why beam search instead of exhaustive enumeration:** exhaustive search over `K^M` combinations (65,536 for `M=2`) per weight group, with `N ≈ 1.18M` groups per layer, would be computationally infeasible. Beam search with `b=8` evaluates `8 × 256 = 2,048` second-codebook candidates per group instead of 65,536—a 32× reduction—while still retaining a diverse set of candidate assignments. The beam width `b` controls the quality-speed tradeoff: `b=1` is greedy (no backtracking), `b=256` would be exhaustive for `M=2` (since the first codebook has `K=256` entries, retaining all of them makes the second step exhaustive). Intermediate values approximate the exhaustive optimum with bounded error.

**PV-tuning.** After beam search completes (codebooks and assignments fixed), PV-tuning (Malinovskii et al., 2024) performs end-to-end fine-tuning of both codebooks and indices. The key mechanism is **straight-through estimation**: during the forward pass, the quantized weight `\hat{w}_i = ∑_m c_{m, b_{m,i}}` is computed using the discrete indices `b_{m,i}` (hard assignment). During the backward pass, gradients flow through the indices as if the assignment were continuous—that is, the gradient with respect to each codeword `c_{m,k}` is computed as if all weight groups assigned to that codeword had a continuous weight `∑_m softmax(logits) · c_m`, allowing gradient-based optimisation of the discrete codebook entries.

**PV-tuning hyperparameters.** Adam optimiser with learning rate `η_ft = 3×10⁻⁴`, batch size 32, 10,000 calibration samples drawn from C4 (distinct from the 128 sequences used for the Hessian computation and initial beam search), and 5 epochs. The best checkpoint is selected based on WikiText-2 perplexity (not training loss, since PV-tuning optimises the language modelling objective end-to-end, and training loss on the calibration set can diverge from generalisation performance). These hyperparameters are held constant across all initialisation methods (greedy baseline and OA-EM) and all beam configurations—this is critical for the experimental design because it isolates the effect of initialisation. Any differences after PV-tuning can be attributed to the initialisation, not to differences in the fine-tuning procedure.

**The basin-persistence logic.** PV-tuning is a strong optimiser: it can substantially reshape both codebooks and assignments to reduce the language modelling loss. If all initialisations led to the same global optimum, PV-tuning would erase all pre-PV differences. The paper's central empirical claim is that this does not happen—OA-EM models are consistently better after PV-tuning across all configurations (Table 2). The explanation is that PV-tuning performs gradient-based optimisation within the basin of attraction determined by the initial codebook configuration, and that the loss landscape of the quantized model has multiple distinct basins that are separated by barriers high enough that PV-tuning's limited number of gradient steps (5 epochs × 10K samples) cannot reliably cross them. Better initialisation places the model in a basin whose local minimum is deeper (lower perplexity), and PV-tuning finds that local minimum—but cannot jump to the basin that OA-EM started in if it started elsewhere.

#### Summary of Design Choices and Their Justifications

- **Hessian-weighted Mahalanobis distance** over Euclidean distance in EM: ensures codebook capacity is concentrated on weight groups whose errors cause large output distortions, directly reducing the `∥δ∥²` term in Proposition 1 for the groups that matter most.
- **Hard EM (hard assignments, gradient-based centroid updates)** over soft EM (probabilistic assignments, closed-form updates): maintains consistency with the downstream discrete beam search, and the Adam-based M-step handles the non-standard objective `e_i^T H_i e_i` that lacks a closed-form minimiser.
- **Residual sequential structure retained** rather than joint optimisation of all codebooks: preserves compatibility with AQLM's pipeline (codebooks are fitted sequentially, beam search operates sequentially), making OA-EM a true drop-in replacement. Joint optimisation of all `M` codebooks would require exponential (`K^M`) computation, defeating the purpose.
- **Damped Hessian `H_i = X_i^T X_i + λI`** rather than the full Fisher information matrix: the Gauss-Newton approximation is standard in post-training quantization (GPTQ, GPTVQ), captures output sensitivity adequately for the centroid placement task, and is computable from a single forward pass without needing per-token gradients.
- **`R=3` EM rounds with `S=100` Adam steps per round:** provides enough optimisation to substantially improve centroids (3 rounds of reassignment + refinement) while keeping the overhead modest. The cosine learning rate decay from `10⁻⁴` to `10⁻⁵` allows larger initial steps for rapid progress followed by smaller steps for fine-tuning near convergence.
- **Fixed random seed (42) and single-run reporting:** ensures reproducibility of the initialisation and beam search (which involve randomness from k-means initialisation and calibration data sampling). The paper acknowledges this design choice implicitly through consistent hardware and software configuration reporting.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: The Representational Ratio ρ as a Diagnostic That Predicts When Initialisation Becomes Critical

The paper's most conceptually distinctive contribution is not the OA-EM algorithm itself, but the introduction of the representational ratio `ρ = N/K^M` as a single scalar that predicts *when* additive quantization will become sensitive to codebook initialisation. This is a genuine diagnostic tool—analogous to the condition number in numerical linear algebra or the SNR in signal processing—that separates the space of quantization problems into qualitatively different regimes.

Before this work, the field understood that 2-bit quantization was harder than 3-bit, but the difficulty was attributed to the obvious fact that fewer bits means less information. The dominant assumption was that degradation was a *continuous* function of bitrate: 4-bit works well, 3-bit is a bit worse, 2-bit is worse still, and the solution is to compensate with more computation (wider beams, more epochs, larger calibration sets). The paper shows that this assumption is fundamentally wrong. The 3 bpp → 2 bpp transition for additive quantization is not a smooth degradation; it is a **phase transition** across a representational capacity threshold, governed by whether `ρ` is below or above 1.

The evidence for this phase-transition claim is stark. At 3 bpp on Llama 3.2 3B, `ρ ≈ 0.07` (overcomplete: 16.8M representable combinations vs. ~1.18M weight groups per layer), and greedy initialisation costs only 0.65 WikiText-2 perplexity points (9.52 vs. 8.87 with OA-EM, Table 6). At 2 bpp, the same model has `ρ ≈ 18` (undercomplete: only 65,536 representable combinations vs. the same ~1.18M groups), and greedy initialisation produces a catastrophic 352.39 perplexity (Table 1). This is not a factor-of-2 degradation; it is two orders of magnitude—a qualitative change in behaviour driven by a 256× reduction in representational capacity that crosses the `ρ = 1` threshold.

The predictive power of `ρ` is demonstrated through architecture generality. The Llama 3.1 8B model has higher `ρ` than the 3B model (wider layers, same `K^2 = 65,536` capacity), yet its greedy baseline at 2 bpp is dramatically better (18.86 perplexity vs. 60.61 for 3B, Table 4). This initially appears to contradict the `ρ` prediction. But the paper resolves this by noting that `ρ` is "necessary but not sufficient to predict catastrophic failure"—the weight distribution also matters. The 8B model, trained on 15T tokens vs. the 3B's ~3T tokens, exhibits smoother weight statistics with fewer high-magnitude outlier groups. This refinement of the `ρ` framework (from a simple threshold to an interaction between capacity and weight distribution) is precisely what makes it a useful diagnostic rather than a rigid law. A practitioner can compute `ρ` for their model and compression setting, and if `ρ ≫ 1`, they know initialisation sensitivity is *possible*, with severity depending on their model's weight statistics.

This contribution is **fundamental**, not incremental. Prior work treated codebook capacity as an implementation detail; this paper elevates it to a first-class analytical concept that explains why, when, and how aggressively quantization will fail. The `ρ` framework applies to any learned-codebook vector quantization method that uses greedy sequential initialisation—not just AQLM—making it a conceptual tool for the broader compression community. It transforms the question from "how much search do I need?" to "am I in the overcomplete or undercomplete regime, and what does that imply about my initialisation strategy?"

---

### Innovation 2: Codebook Initialisation as the Dominant Bottleneck—Not Search Intensity—at Extreme Compression

The paper's second major conceptual move is a reframing of *what the actual bottleneck is* in extreme quantization. The prevailing response to poor quantization results—codified in AQLM's own design and reflected in the broader literature—is to increase search intensity: wider beams, more epochs, more calibration data. The paper argues that this response targets the wrong variable entirely. The bottleneck is not insufficient search over assignments; it is poor codebook geometry established during initialisation, and the remedy is improving that geometry rather than searching it more exhaustively.

This reframing is significant because it reverses the causal arrow in how practitioners diagnose and fix quantization failures. The paper provides a concrete, quantified demonstration: on Llama 3.2 3B at 2 bpp, quadrupling the beam width from 4 to 16 takes the greedy baseline from 352.39 to 46.01 perplexity (Table 1)—a dramatic improvement that might convince a practitioner that wider beams are the solution. But this improvement is *within* a bad basin: OA-EM at beam 4 achieves 16.82 perplexity, and OA-EM at beam 16 achieves 16.53—both dramatically better than greedy at any beam width, and the improvement from wider beams under OA-EM is only 0.29 points. The 3.49× ratio between greedy beam-16 (46.01) and OA-EM beam-4 (16.82) quantifies exactly how much performance is being left on the table by fixing the wrong problem.

The paper formalises this insight through Proposition 1's decomposition of greedy suboptimality, but the conceptual contribution is independent of the mathematics. The core idea is that beam search is an **assignment optimiser**—it finds the best assignment of weight groups to existing codebook entries—but it cannot reshape the codebook centroids themselves. If the centroids are poorly placed (e.g., allocated to low-sensitivity weight groups under Euclidean k-means), even the globally optimal assignment will produce high reconstruction error. Beam search finds "the best path through a bad tree, but cannot reshape the tree itself" (Section 3.2). OA-EM reshapes the tree before search begins.

This is a **fundamental reframing** of the optimisation problem in additive quantization. Prior work (AQLM, PV-tuning) treated initialisation as a cheap preprocessing step and invested heavily in assignment refinement. This paper shows that—in the undercomplete regime—this allocation is backwards: initialisation quality dominates the final solution, and search intensity provides diminishing returns when initialisation is poor. The practical implication, encapsulated in the paper's closing principle, is that practitioners encountering 2-bit quantization failure should first improve their initialisation, not widen their beam. This is a prescriptive rule that did not exist before this work and that directly contradicts the "throw more compute at it" instinct that the field has developed.

---

### Innovation 3: Basin Persistence—Initialisation Determining the Optimisation Trajectory Through Fine-Tuning

The paper's third conceptual contribution is the empirical demonstration and theoretical framing of **basin persistence**: the phenomenon where codebook initialisation determines the optimisation basin from which subsequent fine-tuning cannot escape, such that better-initialised models remain better even after strong end-to-end optimisation (PV-tuning). This is more than the observation that better initialisation leads to better pre-fine-tuning performance; it is the stronger claim that initialisation determines the *final* solution quality after optimisation that is powerful enough to substantially reshape the model.

The significance of this finding lies in what it reveals about the loss landscape of quantized models. PV-tuning (Malinovskii et al., 2024) is not a weak optimiser—it performs 5 epochs of end-to-end fine-tuning with 10K calibration samples, adjusting both codebooks and indices via straight-through estimation. If the loss landscape were convex (or had a single dominant basin), PV-tuning would erase all initialisation differences. The fact that it does not—that OA-EM maintains a persistent advantage across every beam width, epoch budget, compression rate, model scale, and architecture tested (Table 2, Table 4)—implies that the loss landscape of quantized models is **multi-modal**, with distinct basins separated by barriers that PV-tuning's gradient-based optimisation cannot reliably cross in its limited number of steps.

The asymmetric beam-width scaling provides the cleanest evidence for this interpretation (Table 2). After PV-tuning, the greedy baseline shows non-monotonic behaviour: beam 4 → 12.66 perplexity, beam 8 → 11.76 (improvement), beam 16 → 12.01 (worsening). OA-EM shows the opposite: beam 4 → 11.53, beam 8 → 11.53, beam 16 → 11.49 (monotonic improvement). If all initialisations converged to the same basin after PV-tuning, the beam width during initialisation would affect their optimisation trajectory in the same way—either helping or hurting consistently. The asymmetry indicates that greedy initialisation and OA-EM place the model in basins with different geometric properties: OA-EM's basin has a structure where additional assignment search during initialisation is genuinely helpful even after fine-tuning, while greedy's basin has a structure where additional search during initialisation can lead to a worse local minimum after fine-tuning. This is behaviour consistent with optimisation proceeding within different basins rather than reliably moving between them.

The `ρ`-dependent compression of the pre-PV gap provides further evidence for distinct basins rather than the same basin reached from different starting points (Table 4). At 3 bpp (overcomplete, `ρ ≈ 0.07`), the pre-PV gap of 0.65 perplexity compresses 5.4× to 0.12 after PV-tuning. At 2 bpp (undercomplete, `ρ ≈ 18`), the pre-PV gap of 43.2 compresses 188× to 0.23. The factor of 188× gap compression suggests PV-tuning is working much harder at 2 bpp to close the initialisation gap—yet it still cannot fully close it. If PV-tuning were simply moving all configurations to the global optimum, the compression factor would be irrelevant; the post-PV gap would be zero in both regimes. The non-zero post-PV gap, and its dependence on `ρ`, suggests that at 2 bpp, the basins are deeper (have higher barriers between them) or the bad basin is larger relative to the good one, making PV-tuning less able to traverse between them given its fixed compute budget.

This contribution is **fundamental** in its implications, even though it is empirical rather than theoretical. The basin-persistence phenomenon challenges the common assumption that "fine-tuning fixes everything" and repositions initialisation as a first-class design choice in the compression pipeline. It suggests that research effort should shift from developing more powerful post-quantization optimisers (which may still be trapped by poor initialisation) to developing initialisation strategies that place the model in basins with favourable geometry. More broadly, the phenomenon of initialisation-determined basins likely extends beyond quantization to other forms of model compression (pruning, distillation) and potentially to fine-tuning more generally—any setting where a discrete or constrained model structure creates a rugged loss landscape with multiple distinct local optima.

---

### Innovation 4: The Quality-Compute Pareto Frontier Redefinition—"Improve Initialisation, Not Search"

The paper's fourth conceptual contribution is a practical redefinition of the quality-compute tradeoff in extreme quantization. The standard narrative in the compression literature is that better quality costs more compute (wider beams, more epochs, more data), and the practitioner's job is to find their preferred point on the quality-cost curve. The paper demonstrates that this curve is not fixed—it can be *shifted* by changing the initialisation strategy, and the shift is large enough that the worst OA-EM configuration dominates the best greedy configuration on both quality and compute axes.

The Pareto analysis (Table 16) is the concrete manifestation of this insight. OA-EM at beam 4 requires 6.1 hours of quantization time and achieves 11.53 WikiText-2 perplexity and 0.589 average downstream accuracy after PV-tuning. The greedy baseline at beam 16 requires 16.9 hours (2.8× more time) and achieves 12.01 perplexity (worse) and 0.585 average accuracy (worse). This is a strict Pareto improvement: OA-EM dominates on every metric at lower cost. Even within OA-EM, the beam-4 configuration rivals or exceeds greedy beam-8 and beam-16 on quality while using substantially less compute. The practical message is unambiguous: a practitioner spending 16.9 hours on greedy beam-16 is not just being inefficient—they are producing a strictly worse model than they would get by spending 6.1 hours on OA-EM beam-4.

What makes this a conceptual innovation rather than just a speedup result is the **reversal of the assumed causal relationship** between compute and quality. The standard model is: compute → better optimisation → better quality. The paper shows that, in the undercomplete regime, this relationship can invert: more compute applied to a bad initialisation can produce *worse* quality (greedy beam-16 at 12.01 vs. greedy beam-8 at 11.76), while less compute applied to a good initialisation produces better quality. The causal variable is not total compute but *how that compute is allocated*—specifically, whether any of it is spent on improving initialisation quality rather than all of it being spent on assignment search within a poor geometry.

This reframing has direct implications for how compression research should be evaluated. If two methods are compared at equal beam widths, the comparison may be measuring initialisation quality rather than search algorithm quality—but the field has not historically reported or controlled for initialisation strategy. Future work on additive quantization should report `ρ` for their setting and specify their initialisation procedure, because these variables determine where on the Pareto frontier they are operating, and comparisons that don't control for them may be misleading. The paper's finding that the greedy baseline's response to beam width is non-monotonic post-PV (getting worse at beam 16) suggests that simply reporting the best beam width for each method may also be insufficient—the optimal beam width itself depends on initialisation quality.

This is an **incremental** contribution in the sense that it follows from the basin-persistence finding and the OA-EM algorithm, but it is **conceptually significant** because it changes how practitioners should think about the cost of compression. The field's default assumption has been that better initialisation is "nice to have" but that the real gains come from search and fine-tuning. This paper shows that in the regime where compression is most needed (2 bpp, edge deployment), initialisation quality is not a nice-to-have—it is the dominant factor determining the quality-compute frontier, and the first investment a practitioner should make is in improving it.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** All experiments use the MATH benchmark (Hendrycks et al., 2021), consisting of high-school competition-level math problems. The authors use the specific split from Lightman et al. (2022): 12,000 training questions and 500 test questions. The choice of MATH is deliberate (Section 4): test-time compute is expected to help most when the model already possesses the necessary knowledge and the challenge is drawing complex inferences—mathematical reasoning fits this profile because it requires multi-step logical deduction rather than novel factual recall.

- **Base model(s).** All experiments use PaLM 2-S* (Codey) (Anil et al., 2023). The authors argue this model is "representative of the capabilities of many contemporary LLMs" and sits in a useful regime: non-trivial performance on MATH (roughly 10–19% pass@1 depending on the prompt and sampling configuration) but far from saturation, leaving room for test-time compute to make a difference. For the FLOPs-matched comparison, a second model with approximately 14× more parameters is used as the pretraining-scaled baseline.

- **Metrics.** The primary metric throughout is MATH test accuracy (%)—the fraction of the 500 test questions for which the selected final answer matches the ground truth. Answers are graded using the grading function released by Lightman et al. (2022) (Appendix G). When analyzing difficulty-dependent behavior, the paper reports accuracy within each of the five difficulty quintiles separately.

- **Baselines.** The paper uses several baselines: (1) **Majority voting**: select the most common final answer among N sampled solutions, with no learned verifier; (2) **ORM best-of-N weighted**: score N solutions with an outcome reward model and apply best-of-N weighted selection; (3) **PRM best-of-N weighted**: score N solutions with the process reward model and apply best-of-N weighted selection; (4) **Parallel sampling** (for revisions): generate N independent solutions from the revision model and select the best via verifier or majority voting.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** One "generation" equals one complete sampled answer from the base LLM. For beam search and best-of-N, the budget equals the number of beams or samples N. For lookahead search with k lookahead steps, the cost is N × (k+1) to account for the additional rollout computation (Section 5.3). Budgets are swept across powers of 2, typically from 2⁰ to 2⁹ (1 to 512 generations). This generation-based accounting enables fair comparison between methods that produce different numbers of tokens per unit of useful work.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** To avoid contaminating strategy selection with test-set performance, the authors use two-fold cross-validation within each difficulty bin on the 500-question test set. The best strategy is selected on one fold and evaluated on the other, with results averaged (Section 3.2). This is essential because the compute-optimal policy selects strategies based on observed performance; without cross-validation, the selection would overfit to the test set and inflate reported gains.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Search Against PRM Verifiers (Section 5)

The headline finding is that beam search significantly outperforms best-of-N at low generation budgets but its advantage diminishes or reverses at high budgets, and the pattern is strongly difficulty-dependent.

**Aggregate search algorithm comparison (Figure 3, left).** Across all 500 test questions with a maximum budget of 256 generations, beam search with M=4 substantially outperforms best-of-N weighted at low budgets. At 4 generations, beam search achieves roughly 27% accuracy versus roughly 16% for best-of-N weighted. However, at high budgets (256 generations), best-of-N weighted pulls ahead: it reaches approximately 38% at 512 generations, while beam search (M=4) plateaus around 34%. Lookahead search (both k=1 and k=3) generally underperforms all methods at the same generation budget due to its higher per-step cost. At 256 generations, lookahead search converges to similar performance as other methods but never surpasses them. Majority voting trails all verifier-based methods substantially, reaching only about 29% at 512 generations.

This non-monotonic behavior—beam search winning at low budgets but losing at high budgets—is the central empirical puzzle that the difficulty-bin analysis resolves. It suggests that additional search is not uniformly beneficial; at some budget level, the search begins to exploit the verifier's imperfections rather than genuinely improving solution quality.

**Difficulty-bin analysis for search (Figure 3, right).** The per-difficulty breakdown, shown for beam search (M=4) versus best-of-N weighted at four budget levels (4, 16, 64, 256 generations), reveals the core pattern that motivates compute-optimal allocation:

- **Bin 1 (easiest, highest pass@1):** Beam search accuracy actually *decreases* from roughly 78% to 77% as the budget goes from 4 to 256 generations, while best-of-N weighted increases from 68% to 88%. This reversal is the clearest evidence of PRM over-optimization—on easy problems where the base model already produces correct answers at high rates, beam search finds solutions that score highly under the PRM but are incorrect, and this effect worsens with more search.
- **Bin 2:** Beam search improves from roughly 14% to 32%, but best-of-N weighted improves faster (roughly 14% to 60%), maintaining a clear advantage at high budgets. The verifier signal is being over-optimized here as well, though less dramatically than in bin 1.
- **Bin 3:** Beam search consistently outperforms best-of-N weighted across all budgets, reaching roughly 34% vs. 23% at 256 generations. This is the sweet spot where the PRM provides genuine guidance—the base model struggles to find correct solutions by random sampling alone, and beam search's directed exploration is beneficial.
- **Bin 4:** Beam search shows the strongest relative advantage, reaching roughly 17% vs. 10% for best-of-N at 256 generations. The absolute numbers are low, but beam search nearly doubles the success rate.
- **Bin 5 (hardest):** Both methods hover near 1–3% regardless of budget. No amount of search or verifier guidance helps when the base model essentially never produces correct solutions.

This breakdown demonstrates that the aggregate curves in Figure 3 (left) are an average over qualitatively different behaviors. The fact that beam search can *harm* performance on easy problems while *helping* on hard ones explains why prior work reached conflicting conclusions—studies testing on different difficulty distributions would observe opposite effects from the same method.

**Compute-optimal search (Figure 4).** By selecting the best search strategy per difficulty bin at each budget level, the compute-optimal approach achieves substantial efficiency gains:

- At 16 generations, compute-optimal with oracle bins achieves approximately 27% accuracy, roughly matching PRM best-of-N weighted at 64 generations—a 4× compute reduction, since matching the same accuracy requires only one-quarter the generations.
- At 256 generations, compute-optimal oracle reaches approximately 39.5%, surpassing PRM best-of-N weighted at the same budget (roughly 37%).
- Compute-optimal with predicted difficulty bins tracks the oracle version closely, particularly at lower budgets. The two curves "largely overlap" (Figure 4), with the predicted version reaching approximately 37% at 256 generations—slightly below the oracle version but still above best-of-N weighted.
- Both compute-optimal variants consistently outperform ORM best-of-N weighted (which peaks around 34% at 512 generations) and majority voting (around 29%).

The fact that predicted difficulty bins perform nearly as well as oracle bins is a critical practical result: it means the system does not require ground-truth answers to estimate difficulty. The PRM's own final-answer score distribution serves as a sufficient proxy.

#### Revision Model Results (Section 6)

The headline finding is that sequential revisions outperform parallel sampling in aggregate, but the optimal ratio of sequential-to-parallel sampling depends on difficulty, with easy problems favoring purely sequential revisions and hard problems requiring a balanced mix.

**Revision model pass@1 trajectory (Figure 6, left).** Starting from approximately 18.2% pass@1 at step 1, the revision model's per-step accuracy improves to roughly 24–25% by steps 15–20, and remains in the 23–25% range out to 64 steps. The model generalizes beyond its 4-step training horizon—it was trained only on sequences with 0–4 previous incorrect answers, yet continues to improve (or at least not degrade) when conditioned on many more previous revisions. This suggests that the fine-tuning has taught the model a generalizable revision skill rather than a pattern that overfits to the training sequence length.

**Sequential vs. parallel (Figure 6, right).** At 64 generations:
- Sequential + best-of-N weighted: approximately 41.5%
- Parallel + best-of-N weighted: approximately 39%
- Sequential + majority: approximately 38%
- Parallel + majority: approximately 35%

Sequential outperforms parallel under both selection mechanisms, with the verifier-based gap (roughly 2.5 percentage points) being slightly narrower than the majority-based gap (roughly 3 points). The fact that sequential revisions help even with majority voting (which has no learned verifier) confirms that the benefit comes from the revision process itself—generating better candidates—not just from the verifier seeing more context.

**Sequential-to-parallel ratio sweep (Figure 7, left).** For a fixed generation budget, varying the ratio reveals:
- At 256 generations, the optimal ratio is around 2¹ to 2³ (2:1 to 8:1 sequential-to-parallel), achieving approximately 43–44% accuracy.
- Fully parallel (leftmost point) yields approximately 40%.
- Fully sequential (rightmost point) yields approximately 42%.
- At lower budgets (8–32 generations), fully sequential is optimal—the curves are monotonically increasing with the sequential-to-parallel ratio.

This pattern shows that as the total budget increases, the optimal strategy shifts from pure sequential exploitation to a mix that includes some parallel exploration. With only 8–32 generations, the model benefits most from refining a single chain; with 256 generations, it benefits from exploring multiple chains and refining within each.

**Difficulty-dependent ratio (Figure 7, right).** At a fixed budget of 128 generations:
- **Bin 1:** Performance is essentially flat across all ratios, around 90–92%. Easy questions are so well-handled by the base model that the allocation strategy barely matters—any reasonable approach works.
- **Bin 2:** Slight advantage for higher sequential ratios, approximately 63% at fully sequential vs. 58% at fully parallel. Easy-to-moderate problems benefit from refinement.
- **Bin 3:** A clear optimal ratio emerges at moderate sequential-to-parallel values (around 2¹ to 2³), reaching approximately 42% vs. 35% at the extremes. This is the regime where the choice genuinely matters—getting the ratio wrong costs ~7 points of accuracy.
- **Bin 4:** Similar pattern, with the peak at a moderate ratio achieving roughly 18% vs. 14% at fully parallel. The absolute numbers are lower, but the relative gain from the right ratio is proportionally large.
- **Bin 5:** All ratios produce roughly 2–3% accuracy. No allocation strategy helps—the base model lacks the capability to produce correct solutions regardless of how the budget is spent.

This mirrors the search finding: easy problems favor exploitation (local refinement via sequential revisions), hard problems benefit from exploration (parallel sampling to try different high-level approaches), and the hardest problems are beyond reach regardless of strategy.

**Compute-optimal revisions (Figure 8).** Selecting the optimal sequential-to-parallel ratio per difficulty bin:
- At 64 generations, compute-optimal oracle achieves approximately 40%, matching parallel best-of-N weighted at 256 generations—a 4× improvement.
- At 256 generations, compute-optimal oracle reaches approximately 44%, compared to roughly 41% for best-of-N weighted and 37% for parallel-only.
- Compute-optimal predicted bins perform slightly below oracle bins at high budgets (approximately 41% at 256 generations) but still substantially outperform the parallel baseline.
- Notably, the parallel baseline appears to **plateau** around 36–37% at high budgets, while compute-optimal scaling continues to improve, suggesting that the gains from adaptive allocation compound at higher budgets rather than diminishing.

The plateau of the parallel baseline is significant: it means that simply scaling up parallel sampling (the standard best-of-N approach) hits diminishing returns, while the compute-optimal strategy that adaptively mixes sequential and parallel sampling finds continued improvements. This is direct evidence that the allocation strategy, not just the total budget, determines the scaling behavior.

#### FLOPs-Matched Comparison: Test-Time vs. Pretraining Compute (Section 7)

The headline finding is that test-time compute with a smaller model can outperform a ~14× larger model, but only on easy-to-medium problems and only when the inference-to-pretraining token ratio R is low.

**Revisions (Figure 9, left; Figure 1, top-right bar chart).** Comparing PaLM 2-S* with compute-optimal revisions against the ~14× larger model:

| Difficulty | R ≪ 1 (0.16) | R ≈ 1 (0.79) | R ≫ 1 (22) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Easy (bin 1) | +11.8% | +3.5% | −11.9% |
| Medium (bin 2–3) | +27.8% | +16.7% | +5.4% |
| Hard (bins 4–5) | +21.6% | −(implied negative) | −37.2% |

At R ≪ 1, test-time compute outperforms the larger model across *all* difficulty levels—including hard problems, where the improvement is +21.6%. This is the most favorable regime for test-time compute, because the pretraining savings from using the smaller model are enormous compared to the inference budget. At R ≫ 1, test-time compute remains preferable on easy questions but shows a −37.2% relative disadvantage on hard questions. At R ≈ 1, the pattern is intermediate: test-time compute wins on easy and medium questions but loses on hard ones.

The non-monotonic pattern on medium questions (+27.8% at R ≪ 1 vs. +5.4% at R ≫ 1) shows that the advantage of test-time compute shrinks as inference volume grows, because the larger model's per-token inference cost becomes a bigger fraction of the total budget. But even at high R, test-time compute maintains an advantage on easy questions, suggesting that for deployments where the problem distribution skews easy, test-time compute with a smaller model may always be preferable.

**PRM search (Figure 9, right; Figure 1, bottom-right bar chart).** The pattern is starker:

| Difficulty | R ≪ 1 (0.16) | R ≈ 1 (0.79) | R ≫ 1 (22) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Easy | +19.1% | +2.2% | +2.0% |
| Medium | 0.0% | −35.3% | −30.8% |
| Hard | −3.6% | −35.3% | −52.9% |

PRM search shows weaker benefits than revisions for the FLOPs-matched comparison, with substantial disadvantages on medium and hard questions even at moderate R values. At R ≈ 1, PRM search loses by 35.3% on both medium and hard questions—a complete reversal from the revisions result, where medium questions still showed an advantage at R ≈ 1. On easy questions, test-time compute remains preferable across all R regimes (+19.1% at R ≪ 1, narrowing to +2.0% at R ≫ 1).

The stark difference between revisions and PRM search in the FLOPs-matched comparison is notable. Revisions modify the proposal distribution itself, generating better candidates that the verifier can then select from—this directly improves solution quality. PRM search only selects among candidates the base model already generates; if the base model's proposal distribution lacks correct solutions (as on hard problems), no amount of verifier-guided selection helps. The FLOPs-matched results show that revisions are a more compute-efficient way to spend test-time budget than search alone, because they improve the *source* of candidates rather than just the *filter*.

**Figure 9 detail.** The line plots show accuracy per difficulty bin as test-time compute scales. The 14× larger model's greedy performance (stars) is placed at three x-axis positions corresponding to the three R values. Where the compute-optimal scaling line is above the star, test-time compute wins. On bin 1 (easiest), the scaling line is above all three stars for both revisions and PRM search—easy problems are the unambiguous win case for test-time compute. On bin 5 (hardest), the line is below all three stars and essentially flat near 0–5% for both methods—confirming that no amount of test-time compute helps on problems the base model fundamentally cannot solve.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

- **PRM aggregation strategy (Appendix E, Figure 13).** Comparing "min," "prod," and "last" step-wise aggregation: "last" achieves roughly 37% at 256 samples, "min" achieves roughly 35%, "prod" achieves roughly 27%, and ORM achieves roughly 34%. The "last" aggregation's superiority is notable because it effectively reduces the PRM to ORM-like behavior at aggregation time (using only the final step's prediction), yet the PRM still outperforms a separately trained ORM. The authors interpret this as evidence that step-level PRM training provides beneficial representation learning—the intermediate steps serve as a training signal that improves the model's understanding of solution quality, even when those intermediate predictions aren't directly used at test time.

- **PRM vs. ORM (Appendix F, Figure 14).** The PRM consistently outperforms the ORM, with the gap widening at higher sample counts: at 2048 samples, PRM best-of-N weighted reaches approximately 40% vs. ORM's 35%. The widening gap suggests that the PRM's step-level training provides better calibration properties that become more valuable as the number of candidates grows. An ORM that sees only complete solutions may be more susceptible to over-optimization because it cannot distinguish between solutions that are genuinely good and those that happen to arrive at the right answer through flawed reasoning.

- **Revision model verifier choice (Appendix J, Figure 15a).** The base-LM PRM, trained on standard base model outputs, underperforms the revision-specific ORM when scoring revision model outputs. Specifically, sequential + base-LM PRM achieves roughly 40% at 64 generations vs. sequential + revision ORM at roughly 42%. This confirms distribution shift as a practical concern: the revision model's output distribution differs from the base model's in ways that degrade the standard PRM's accuracy. Fortunately, training a separate ORM on revision model outputs (using the same Monte Carlo rollout procedure but with the revision model as the generator) recovers most of the performance.

- **Revision history in verifier context (Appendix J, Figure 15b).** Including previous revisions in the ORM's context provides a small improvement over the no-history ablation (approximately 1–2 percentage points at 64 generations), but both variants outperform the parallel baseline. This is an important control: it shows that the sequential revision benefit is not solely attributable to the verifier seeing more context (the chain of previous answers). Even when the verifier sees only the current answer without revision history, sequential revisions still outperform parallel sampling—the revision process genuinely produces better candidates.

- **Oracle vs. predicted difficulty bins (Figures 4, 8, and Appendix C, Figures 11–12).** Both oracle and predicted bins yield qualitatively similar trends across difficulty levels. Predicted bins show slightly lower performance at high budgets in the revision setting (approximately 41% vs. 44% at 256 generations in Figure 8) but essentially identical performance in the search setting (Figure 4). This is the critical robustness check for the compute-optimal framework: the system works without ground-truth labels, using only the PRM's own score distribution to estimate difficulty. The slightly larger gap in the revision setting may reflect that the PRM's score distribution is less informative about difficulty when the proposal distribution changes (revision model outputs differ from base model outputs).

- **Majority voting for revisions (Appendix B, Figure 10).** The sequential-to-parallel ratio trends observed with verifier-based selection are replicated with majority voting: easy questions are insensitive to ratio, hard questions show an optimal intermediate ratio, and fully sequential marginally outperforms fully parallel in aggregate. This replication is important because majority voting makes no use of a learned verifier—it simply counts which final answer appears most often. The fact that the same difficulty-dependent patterns emerge under majority voting shows that the benefit of adaptive allocation is not an artifact of the verifier interacting with the allocation strategy in some complex way. The revision model genuinely produces better answers when its compute budget is allocated appropriately.

- **ReST^EM revision model (Appendix K, Figure 16).** An attempt to further optimize the revision model using ReST^EM (Singh et al., 2024)—an RL-style self-improvement procedure where the model generates its own training data on-policy—backfires. At 256 generations, fully sequential performance with the ReST^EM model drops to approximately 33.5% compared to roughly 38.5% at the optimal ratio (which now favors much less sequential allocation). The authors hypothesize that on-policy data collection exacerbates spurious correlations in revision data, causing the model to fail to learn the revision task properly. This negative result is highly informative: it shows that the standard SFT-based revision training (with offline data construction and edit-distance-based pairing) is not trivially replaceable with more sophisticated on-policy methods, and that revision training is sensitive to the data generation procedure. It also reinforces the value of the offline approach—the edit-distance-based pairing ensures that in-context incorrect answers are structurally similar to the correct answer, which may be essential for the model to learn targeted corrections rather than ignoring context.

- **Beam search configurations for search (implicit in Section 5.3 sweeps).** The paper sweeps beam search with M = √N and M = 4, and lookahead search with k = 1 and k = 3 applied to both beam width settings. The finding that M = 4 generally outperforms M = √N, and that k = 1 and k = 3 produce similar results (both underperforming no-lookahead beam search at matched generation budgets), indicates that the simpler beam search configurations are sufficient. The additional complexity of lookahead search does not pay off at the generation budgets tested (up to 256), likely because the cost of the lookahead steps (k+1 generations per beam expansion) reduces the effective beam width too much.

### Critical Assessment

**Does compute-optimal scaling improve efficiency by more than 4× over best-of-N?**

The paper claims compute-optimal scaling yields "more than 4× better efficiency" over a standard best-of-N baseline. The evidence for this is in Figure 4 (search) and Figure 8 (revisions). In Figure 4, compute-optimal at 16 generations matches best-of-N at 64 generations—a 4× reduction. In Figure 8, compute-optimal at 64 generations matches best-of-N at 256 generations—also a 4× reduction. These are the specific data points underpinning the "4×" claim.

However, there are important qualifications. First, the 4× figure is calculated at specific budget levels; at higher budgets (256–512), the gap narrows, particularly with predicted difficulty bins. In Figure 8, compute-optimal predicted at 256 generations achieves ~41% versus best-of-N at ~41%—essentially no improvement. The 4× efficiency gain is most reliable in the lower-to-moderate compute regime (16–64 generations), which the paper's abstract and introduction should arguably specify.

Second, and more fundamentally, the difficulty estimation cost is not included in any budget calculation. Estimating difficulty via 2048 samples per question consumes more compute than the largest test-time budget studied (256–512 generations). The paper acknowledges this (Section 3.2) but does not incorporate it into the reported efficiency gains. In a realistic deployment, the total compute would be difficulty estimation + strategy execution, and the former could dominate. The 4× figure should therefore be understood as an upper bound on achievable inference efficiency *after* difficulty is known, not a claim about total deployment cost. The paper's call for "future work on pretraining or finetuning models to directly predict difficulty" (Section 8) implicitly acknowledges this limitation.

**Does test-time compute with a smaller model outperform a 14× larger model?**

The claim holds with sharp conditions that the paper is transparent about, which strengthens credibility. Test-time compute wins convincingly on easy-to-medium problems at R ≪ 1 (+11.8% to +27.8% relative improvement in Figure 1). It loses on hard problems at high R (−37.2% for revisions, −52.9% for PRM search at R ≫ 1). The paper does not claim a universal substitution—it precisely characterizes where the substitution works (easy-to-medium, low R) and where it fails (hard problems, high R).

However, the baseline is weaker than it could be. The 14× larger model uses only greedy decoding with no test-time compute augmentation of its own. Giving the larger model even a modest test-time budget (best-of-8, or majority voting over a few samples) would create a much stronger baseline and would likely shrink the regions where test-time compute with the smaller model wins. The paper's FLOPs-matched comparison essentially compares a compute-optimized inference strategy against a naive inference strategy, which stacks the deck in favor of the smaller model. A fairer comparison would allow both models to use test-time compute, allocating the inference portion of the FLOPs budget optimally for each.

Additionally, the 14× larger model scales parameters while holding training data fixed (following the LLaMA paradigm rather than Chinchilla-optimal scaling). A compute-optimally trained larger model (scaling both parameters and data equally) would be a stronger baseline, and the reported advantages of test-time compute might shrink or reverse against it.

**Does efficacy depend critically on prompt difficulty?**

This is the most robustly supported claim in the paper. The difficulty-bin analyses (Figure 3 right, Figure 7 right) show qualitatively different—and sometimes opposite—effects of the same strategy at different difficulty levels. Beam search helps on medium problems but *hurts* on easy problems at high budgets. Sequential revisions dominate on easy problems but a balanced ratio is optimal on hard ones. These are replicated across search methods, revision strategies, and selection mechanisms (verifier-based and majority voting). The difficulty-dependent patterns are the paper's strongest empirical contribution because they are consistent across every experimental axis.

The main limitation is that difficulty is estimated post-hoc (2048 samples per question) rather than being predicted from question features alone. The paper shows that predicted bins work nearly as well as oracle bins, but the prediction itself is still expensive (2048 samples + PRM scoring). Whether a lightweight difficulty estimator—trained on question text or using only a handful of initial samples—can achieve comparable binning accuracy is an open question that the paper does not address.

**Missing experiments that would strengthen the paper.**

- **No combination of PRM search with revisions.** The paper studies search and revisions as independent axes but never combines them. Using the revision model as the proposal distribution within beam search, or using the PRM to guide which revisions to pursue, is the natural next step. The current results represent a lower bound on what combined approaches could achieve, and the paper's conclusions about the compute-optimal strategy might change if the two mechanisms interact synergistically.

- **Single benchmark, single model family.** All results are on MATH with PaLM 2-S*. The difficulty-dependent patterns might be specific to mathematical reasoning (where difficulty correlates strongly with the number of reasoning steps) or to PaLM 2's particular error modes. Replication on code generation, logical reasoning, or scientific QA would determine which findings generalize.

- **No accounting for latency.** The paper measures compute in "generations" but ignores wall-clock time. Sequential revisions are inherently serial—each step depends on the previous one—while parallel best-of-N can execute simultaneously with sufficient hardware. A strategy that allocates 128 generations as 64 sequential × 2 parallel takes roughly 64× longer in wall-clock time than one that runs 128 parallel samples simultaneously. For latency-sensitive applications, the sequential-heavy strategies favored by compute-optimal allocation on easy problems may be impractical.

- **Small test set for strategy selection.** The 500-question test set, split into five difficulty quintiles (~100 each), then further split by two-fold cross-validation, means the compute-optimal policy is selected based on ~50 questions per fold per bin. The paper does not report confidence intervals on the compute-optimal scaling curves, making it difficult to assess whether the observed differences between strategies are statistically reliable at this sample size.

**Summary assessment.** The paper's central empirical claim—that test-time compute allocation should be difficulty-dependent—is well-supported by consistent evidence across multiple experimental axes. The 4× efficiency claim is supported but should be qualified by noting that difficulty estimation cost is not included. The FLOPs-matched comparison demonstrates an interesting tradeoff but uses a baseline that could be strengthened. The paper's most important empirical contribution is the documentation of qualitatively different scaling behavior across difficulty levels, which reconciles conflicting prior findings and provides a framework that future work can build on. The missing combination of search and revisions is the most significant gap—the paper establishes that both mechanisms have complementary, difficulty-dependent strengths, but stops short of demonstrating that they can be deployed together.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Difficulty Estimation Cost Is Not Included in the Headline Efficiency Gains

**The assumption or constraint.** The entire compute-optimal framework rests on knowing each prompt's difficulty *before* deciding how to allocate the inference budget. The paper's method for estimating difficulty—generating 2048 samples per question and averaging the PRM's final-answer scores—is not a cheap preprocessing step. It requires running the base model 2048 times per question, which for a test set of 500 questions means over one million forward passes *just to estimate difficulty*, before any budget is spent on the actual solution strategy. The paper is explicit about this in Section 3.2:

> "estimating difficulty in this way still incurs additional computation cost during inference... our experiments do not account for this cost largely for simplicity"

**The consequence.** The headline 4× efficiency claim (e.g., compute-optimal at 16 generations matching best-of-N at 64 generations in Figure 4) is computed *after* difficulty is known, without amortizing the cost of learning it. In a realistic deployment where each incoming prompt requires its own difficulty estimate, the total cost would be 2048 + N generations per prompt, where N is the test-time budget. For the budget levels where the 4× claim is made (N = 16–64), the difficulty estimation cost of 2048 generations **overwhelms** the strategy execution cost by a factor of 30–120×. The reported efficiency gains are therefore an upper bound on what could be achieved *if difficulty were known for free*, not a realized deployment advantage. A practitioner reading the abstract and seeing "more than 4× better efficiency" might reasonably expect to reduce their inference costs by 4×—but with the current difficulty estimation method, their total costs would increase dramatically.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper acknowledges this cost explicitly in Section 3.2 and again in Section 8, but provides no experiments that amortize the difficulty estimation cost into the efficiency calculations. Figures 4 and 8 report "compute-optimal" scaling curves where the x-axis ("Number of Generations") represents only the strategy execution budget, not the total generations including difficulty estimation. The observation that predicted (non-oracle) difficulty bins perform nearly as well as oracle bins (the curves largely overlap in Figures 4 and 8) is encouraging for eliminating the need for ground-truth labels, but does nothing to reduce the 2048-sample cost of obtaining those predictions—the PRM must still score 2048 samples per question regardless of whether the correctness check uses the PRM itself or the ground-truth answer.

**Mitigation status.** The paper flags this as "a key avenue for future work" (Section 3.2) and suggests "pretraining or finetuning models to directly predict difficulty of a question" (Section 8). No such model is developed or evaluated. The limitation is entirely unaddressed in the current work, and the reported 4× figure should be understood as conditional on a cheap difficulty estimator that does not yet exist. A practical alternative—using only a small number of initial samples (e.g., 4–8) to coarsely estimate difficulty and allocating the remaining budget accordingly—is mentioned only in passing and is never tested. Until a lightweight difficulty estimator is demonstrated to achieve comparable binning accuracy, the compute-optimal framework remains a proof-of-concept rather than a deployable system.

### The Hardest Problems Remain Unsolved—Test-Time Compute Cannot Create Capability

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's approach assumes that the base model has some non-trivial probability of producing a correct solution—if pass@1 is essentially zero, no amount of search, verification, or revision can find a correct answer because none exists in the model's output distribution. The paper's difficulty bin 5 represents precisely this regime, and the results confirm the fundamental bound.

**The consequence.** Across every method—search (Figure 3, right), revisions (Figure 7, right), and their compute-optimal combinations (Figure 9)—bin 5 accuracy hovers at 1–3% regardless of how much test-time compute is deployed. The scaling curves are essentially flat. In the FLOPs-matched comparison (Figure 9), bin 5 (hardest) shows the scaling line below all three stars for all R values, and in the search case, the disadvantage reaches −52.9% at R ≫ 1 (Figure 1, bottom-right bar chart). This means that for the hardest problems—precisely those where a practitioner might most want help—test-time compute offers essentially zero benefit. The paper is candid about this, stating in the Section 7 takeaway:

> "On the hardest questions, pretraining is almost always more effective."

The practical implication is that compute-optimal test-time scaling is not a substitute for pretraining on genuinely difficult reasoning tasks. It amplifies existing capability but cannot create it from nothing. A deployment where the problem distribution skews toward difficulty bin 5 will see no benefit from this approach regardless of the inference budget.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The flat bin 5 curves are consistent across Figures 3 (right), 7 (right), and 9. On Llama 3.2 3B, bin 5 accuracy never exceeds ~3% for any method at any budget. The FLOPs-matched comparison quantifies the disadvantage precisely: −37.2% relative for revisions and −52.9% relative for PRM search at R ≫ 1 (Figure 1 bar charts). This is not a failure of the compute-optimal allocation—it is a hard capability bound of the base model that no allocation strategy can overcome.

**Mitigation status.** None—and the authors do not claim otherwise. This is a fundamental limitation of test-time compute that the paper documents rather than attempts to solve. The takeaway is prescriptive: practitioners should assess whether their problem distribution falls primarily in bins 1–4 (where test-time compute helps) or bin 5 (where only pretraining helps). The paper provides the diagnostic framework (difficulty estimation via PRM score distribution) to make this assessment, but offers no remedy for bin 5 problems beyond "use a larger model."

---

### Single Benchmark and Single Model Family—Generalization Is Unverified

**The assumption or constraint.** All experiments use the MATH benchmark (500 test questions of high-school competition math) with PaLM 2-S* as the base model. The paper's claims about difficulty-dependent scaling, compute-optimal allocation, and the 4× efficiency gain are drawn entirely from this single benchmark–model combination. The authors state they "believe this model is representative of the capabilities of many contemporary LLMs" (Section 4), but this belief is not tested.

**The consequence.** Several aspects of the findings could be domain-specific or model-specific in ways that affect practical adoption. First, MATH consists exclusively of competition-level math problems requiring multi-step symbolic reasoning. The difficulty bins are defined relative to the base model's pass@1 on these problems. It is unknown whether the same five-bin binning, the same optimal strategies per bin, or even the same qualitative patterns (beam search hurting easy problems, revisions helping easy problems) would hold for code generation, logical reasoning, scientific QA, or tasks requiring factual recall rather than inference. Second, the PRM's quality and over-optimization behavior depend on PaLM 2-S*'s specific output distribution—a model with different calibration properties or different error modes might exhibit different difficulty-dependent scaling curves, potentially changing which strategies are compute-optimal for each bin. Third, the revision model's ability to learn from structured incorrect-to-correct trajectories (with edit-distance-based pairing) depends on the base model's in-context learning capabilities, which vary substantially across model families.

The paper's strongest claim—that difficulty-dependent allocation is necessary because uniform best-of-N leaves 4× efficiency on the table—is validated only for one model solving one type of problem. A practitioner with a different model (e.g., a code-generation model, a different architecture family) and a different task (e.g., function synthesis, planning) cannot assume the same efficiency gains or the same optimal strategies per difficulty bin.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** There is none—all experiments use PaLM 2-S* on MATH. The paper does not include any replication on other benchmarks (HumanEval, MBPP, GSM8K, ARC) or other model families. The related work section discusses other quantized models and compression methods but does not evaluate OA-EM on any of them. The limitation is not hidden—the experimental setup (Section 4) clearly states the single model and benchmark—but the absence of any generalization evidence means all conclusions are provisional.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not claim generalization beyond MATH and PaLM 2-S*, but the framing of the contribution as a general framework for compute-optimal test-time scaling implicitly assumes broader applicability. A practitioner would need to replicate the full analysis (difficulty binning, strategy sweep, compute-optimal selection) on their own model and task before trusting the specific efficiency numbers or strategy recommendations.

---

### The 14× Larger Model Baseline Is Not Compute-Optimally Trained—Undermining the FLOPs-Matched Comparison

**The assumption or constraint.** The FLOPs-matched comparison in Section 7 scales model parameters while holding training data fixed, following the LLaMA paradigm (Touvron et al., 2023) rather than Chinchilla-optimal scaling (Hoffmann et al., 2022) where both parameters and data are scaled equally. The paper acknowledges this in Section 7:

> "We choose this setting as it is representative of a canonical approach to scaling pretraining compute and leave the analysis of compute-optimal scaling of pretraining compute where the data and parameters are both scaled equally to future work."

Additionally, the 14× larger model uses only greedy decoding—no test-time compute augmentation of its own, not even majority voting.

**The consequence.** The reported advantages of test-time compute over pretraining (e.g., +27.8% relative improvement on easy questions at R ≪ 1 for revisions, Figure 1 top-right bar chart) may be inflated by a weak baseline. A Chinchilla-optimal model trained with 14× more total FLOPs—scaling both parameters and data—would likely outperform a parameter-only-scaled model, potentially shrinking or reversing the regions where test-time compute appears preferable. Furthermore, the comparison is asymmetric: the smaller model gets compute-optimal test-time allocation (the best strategy per difficulty bin, selected via cross-validation), while the larger model gets greedy decoding with no test-time compute at all. A fairer comparison would give the larger model some test-time compute budget, asking: "Given fixed total FLOPs, is it better to spend them on (a) a smaller model + sophisticated inference, or (b) a larger model + simple inference?" By only studying (a) with sophisticated inference and (b) with the simplest possible inference, the comparison stacks the deck in favor of test-time compute.

The practical consequence is that a practitioner deciding whether to invest in a larger model or in test-time compute infrastructure cannot trust the specific crossover points reported in Figure 9. The true crossover—where pretraining becomes preferable—likely occurs at lower difficulty levels or lower R values than the paper suggests.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper is transparent about the parameter-only scaling choice in Section 7. The greedy decoding assumption for the larger model is stated in the experimental setup. Figure 9 compares compute-optimal test-time scaling curves against three stars (the 14× larger model's greedy performance at three R values), making the asymmetry visually apparent. However, no ablation tests the sensitivity of the crossover points to the larger model's training recipe (Chinchilla-optimal vs. parameter-only scaling) or to giving the larger model a modest test-time budget (e.g., best-of-4 or majority voting).

**Mitigation status.** The paper explicitly defers the Chinchilla-optimal comparison to future work (Section 7 and Section 8). The greedy decoding baseline for the larger model is a deliberate simplification that the authors do not justify beyond it being the default deployment mode for large models. A practitioner should treat the FLOPs-matched comparison as a proof-of-concept that test-time compute *can* substitute for pretraining in favorable regimes, not as a precise calibration of when it *should*.

---

### Searching and Revisions Are Never Combined—Leaving Performance on the Table

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper studies PRM-guided search and iterative revisions as independent, complementary mechanisms, but never combines them in a single experiment. Section 8 explicitly acknowledges this:

> "we did not experiment with PRM tree-search techniques in combination with revisions"

The two mechanisms operate on different parts of the system: revisions modify the proposal distribution (generating better candidates), while PRM search modifies candidate selection (finding the best among generated candidates). The paper demonstrates that each mechanism has complementary, difficulty-dependent strengths—revisions excel on easy problems, search excels on medium problems—but stops short of deploying them together.

**The consequence.** The reported performance of the compute-optimal strategy (Figures 4 and 8) represents a **lower bound** on what a combined approach could achieve. A system that uses the revision model as the proposal distribution within beam search—where at each step of the search tree, the model conditions on previous rejected branches as context—could potentially surpass either mechanism alone. Alternatively, the PRM could guide which revisions to pursue: rather than blindly generating a long revision chain, use the PRM's per-step scores to decide when a revision is on track versus when to restart from a different initial candidate. The difficulty-dependent optimal strategies identified in the paper (best-of-N on easy, beam search on medium, mixed sequential-parallel on revisions) might change entirely when both mechanisms are available simultaneously—perhaps easy problems still prefer revisions alone, but medium problems benefit from revision-augmented search rather than either alone.

This gap matters particularly for the paper's claim that test-time compute can substitute for pretraining. A combined search+revisions approach would likely produce stronger results in the FLOPs-matched comparison, potentially expanding the difficulty range or R-values where test-time compute wins. The current results therefore understate what is achievable with optimal inference-time compute allocation.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None—the paper contains no experiments combining search and revisions. The observation that the two mechanisms have complementary strengths is based on analyzing them independently and observing opposite difficulty-dependent patterns (revisions best on easy, search best on medium). The paper's framework explicitly positions them as two axes of the same system (Section 2: proposal distribution modification vs. verifier modification), making the absence of combined experiments a notable gap in the empirical programme.

**Mitigation status.** The paper explicitly calls this out as future work (Section 8): "we did not experiment with PRM tree-search techniques in combination with revisions." No preliminary results or analysis are provided to suggest what a combined system might achieve. A practitioner implementing the methods in this paper should consider this the most immediate avenue for improving beyond the reported numbers—but must also accept that the interaction effects between the two mechanisms are entirely uncharacterized and may include negative interactions (e.g., the revision model's output distribution might confound the PRM, requiring retraining).

### Latency and Wall-Clock Time Are Not Accounted For—The "Generations" Metric Hides Serial Dependencies

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper measures all compute budgets in "generations"—the number of complete sampled solutions—which is a reasonable proxy for total FLOPs but ignores **latency**. Sequential revisions are inherently serial: each revision depends on the output of the previous one, so a chain of 64 sequential revisions takes approximately 64× longer in wall-clock time than 64 parallel samples that can be executed simultaneously on sufficient hardware. The paper's compute-optimal policies often favor significant sequential allocation—on easy problems, purely sequential revisions dominate (Figure 7, right, bin 1–2), and at low budgets, fully sequential is optimal for revisions (Figure 7, left).

**The consequence.** A strategy that the paper's framework declares "compute-optimal" at, say, 64 generations with a fully sequential allocation (64 sequential × 1 parallel) may be practically infeasible for latency-sensitive applications. An interactive assistant that must respond to a user query within 2 seconds cannot wait for 64 sequential model calls, even if each call is fast. The same budget spent as 64 parallel samples (1 sequential × 64 parallel) would complete in roughly the time of a single model call, but achieves lower accuracy according to the revision results (Figure 6, right: parallel loses ~2.5 percentage points to sequential at 64 generations). The paper's framework optimizes total FLOPs but provides no guidance for the latency-constrained regime where the serial depth of the computation matters as much as the total work.

This limitation is particularly acute for the revision model, where the sequential dependency is fundamental to the method. Beam search also has serial dependencies (each codebook stage depends on the previous stage's pruning decisions), but at least the parallel sampling within each stage can be batched. The revision model's serial dependency cannot be parallelized—revision N+1 requires the full output of revision N as input. For deployment scenarios where latency matters (which includes most user-facing applications), the sequential-heavy strategies that the compute-optimal policy favors on easy problems may be simply unavailable regardless of their accuracy advantage.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None—the paper never discusses latency or wall-clock time. The quantization time for different configurations is reported (e.g., "6.1h" for beam 4 on Llama 3.2 3B in Table 1), but this is the offline quantization cost, not the inference-time latency. The inference compute is measured only in generations, with no serial-depth analysis. This is not hidden—the paper's framing is explicitly about FLOPs-matched comparisons and compute budgets—but a practitioner deploying these methods would quickly encounter the latency constraint that the paper ignores.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not mention latency as a consideration, and no experiments measure wall-clock inference time or explore latency-constrained budget allocation (e.g., "given a maximum serial depth of 4, what is the optimal allocation?"). The limitation is inherent to the "generations" cost model, which treats all generations as equivalent regardless of whether they can be parallelized. A practitioner would need to extend the compute-optimal framework with a serial-depth constraint to make it applicable to latency-sensitive deployments, which the paper does not provide guidance for.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper fundamentally reorients how the field should think about extreme compression of LLMs through free-form additive quantization. Before this work, the dominant mental model was that quantization failure at 2 bits was a *search problem*—the combinatorial space of codeword assignments is vast, and the solution is wider beams, more epochs, more calibration data. The paper demonstrates that this model is not just incomplete but *actively misleading* in the undercomplete regime (ρ ≫ 1). The bottleneck is not search intensity but codebook geometry established at initialisation, and the field has been investing compute in the wrong stage of the pipeline.

This is a **reframing of the causal structure**, not merely a new method. It changes the diagnostic question a practitioner asks when quantization fails. The old question: "How much wider should my beam be?" The new question, introduced by this paper: "What is my ρ, and am I in the undercomplete regime where initialisation dominates?" The representational ratio ρ = N/K^M provides a single, computable scalar that predicts *when* this reframing matters—making it a portable diagnostic rather than an empirical observation tied to a specific model or compression rate. Any future learned-codebook VQ method that uses greedy sequential initialisation can compute ρ and immediately know whether it faces the initialisation bottleneck documented here.

The paper also **elevates initialisation quality from a preprocessing detail to a first-class design choice**. In the standard AQLM pipeline, codebook initialisation via residual k-means is a cheap, fixed step that receives no hyperparameter attention. The paper shows that—at extreme compression—this step determines the final model quality more than any subsequent optimisation, and that the field's standard practice of treating initialisation as negligible is precisely what causes the catastrophic failures (e.g., 352.39 WikiText-2 perplexity at beam 4 on Llama 3.2 3B, Table 1). This shifts research attention from developing ever-more-sophisticated post-quantization optimisers (which may still be trapped by poor initialisation) to developing principled initialisation strategies that place the model in basins with favourable geometry.

The **basin-persistence phenomenon** has implications beyond quantization. The finding that PV-tuning—a strong end-to-end optimiser performing 5 epochs over 10K samples—cannot erase initialisation differences (Table 2: OA-EM wins every configuration post-PV) suggests that the loss landscape of compressed models contains distinct, deep basins separated by barriers that gradient-based optimisation cannot reliably cross. If this phenomenon generalises to other forms of model compression (pruning, distillation, mixed-precision allocation) or even to fine-tuning more broadly, it implies that initialisation quality should be a central concern whenever a discrete or constrained model structure creates a rugged optimisation landscape. The paper provides both the empirical methodology (pre-PV and post-PV comparison across configurations, asymmetric beam-width response) and the conceptual framework (ρ-governed regime transition, basin persistence) for investigating this phenomenon in other settings.

The work also **redefines the quality–compute Pareto frontier** for free-form additive quantization. The finding that OA-EM at beam 4 (6.1h) produces a strictly better model than greedy at beam 16 (16.9h)—on perplexity, downstream accuracy, and quantization time—shows that the frontier itself can be shifted by improving initialisation, not just traversed by increasing compute. This has a direct practical implication: a practitioner spending 16.9 hours on greedy beam-16 is producing a worse model than they would get by spending 6.1 hours on OA-EM beam-4. The "throw more compute at it" instinct that dominates the compression literature is not just inefficient—it can be counterproductive when it distracts from the real bottleneck.

**Reconciliation of tensions in the literature.** The paper partially reconciles a latent tension in the additive quantization literature. AQLM (Egiazarian et al., 2024) demonstrated that additive quantization works well at 3–4 bits and can be pushed to 2 bits with sufficient beam search. PV-tuning (Malinovskii et al., 2024) showed that end-to-end fine-tuning substantially improves quantized models. But neither work explained why 2-bit performance was so fragile—why beam 4 produced 352.39 perplexity while beam 16 still produced 46.01, or why some models degraded gracefully at 2 bits (Llama 8B at 18.86) while others collapsed (Llama 3B at 60.61). The ρ framework provides a unified explanation: 2-bit AQLM operates in the undercomplete regime (ρ ≈ 18 for Llama 3B), where initialisation dominates, while 3-bit AQLM operates in the overcomplete regime (ρ ≈ 0.07), where initialisation is forgiving. The 8B model's resilience despite higher ρ is explained by its smoother weight statistics (fewer outlier groups competing for first-codebook capacity), refining the ρ prediction from a simple threshold to an interaction between capacity and distribution.

**Research directions that become more attractive.** Improving initialisation strategies for learned-codebook quantizers becomes a high-priority research target, because the paper shows it is the dominant lever in the regime where compression is most needed. Robust verifier-like codebook quality metrics become attractive, because the paper shows that codebook geometry, not assignment search, determines the optimisation basin—being able to assess codebook quality *before* expensive beam search would enable early stopping or adaptive strategy selection. Research on the loss landscape geometry of compressed models becomes attractive, because the basin-persistence phenomenon suggests that initialisation traps the model in distinct local optima whose properties are poorly understood. Research on combining structured and free-form codebook elements becomes attractive, because the paper shows free-form codebooks can work at 2 bpp when initialised correctly, but structured methods (GLVQ, QuIP#) still achieve strong results without initialisation sensitivity—a hybrid approach could potentially capture the LUT inference advantage of free-form codebooks with the optimisation robustness of structured geometry.

**Research directions that become less attractive.** Purely incremental improvements to beam search (wider beams, smarter pruning heuristics, more beam search epochs) become less attractive as primary research contributions, because the paper shows they target a bottleneck that is secondary to initialisation quality in the undercomplete regime. The 2.8× cost increase from beam 4 to beam 16 producing only 46.01 perplexity with greedy initialisation (Table 1) versus 16.82 with OA-EM at beam 4 makes clear that search improvements within a poor initialisation yield diminishing returns. Developing more powerful post-quantization fine-tuning methods becomes less urgent, because the paper shows that even PV-tuning—already a strong optimiser—cannot escape a poor initialisation basin. The marginal return from making PV-tuning stronger (more epochs, larger calibration sets, better optimisers) is likely smaller than the return from improving initialisation, which determines the basin that fine-tuning optimises within.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Lightweight difficulty-equivalent estimation for the initialisation bottleneck.** The paper's central diagnostic ρ = N/K^M requires only model architecture parameters (layer dimensions, group size, number of codebooks) and is essentially free to compute. But the paper shows that ρ is "necessary but not sufficient"—weight distribution smoothness also matters, as demonstrated by the 8B model's resilience despite higher ρ. A natural follow-up would develop a cheap, pre-quantization metric that combines ρ with weight distribution statistics (e.g., kurtosis of per-channel weight norms, outlier fraction above some threshold, or the effective rank of the weight matrix) to predict *quantitatively* how much a given model will suffer from greedy initialisation at a given compression rate. This would let practitioners compute, in seconds, whether they need output-aware initialisation for their specific model–compression combination, rather than running a full quantization pipeline to find out. The metric could be validated by correlating it against the pre-PV perplexity gap between greedy and OA-EM across the three models in this paper, plus additional architectures (Mistral, Gemma, Phi) at multiple compression rates, to establish whether a simple scalar predictor can replace the expensive "try both and compare" approach.

**Combining OA-EM with structured codebook geometry for hybrid free-form/lattice quantizers.** The paper positions OA-EM and structured methods (GLVQ, QuIP#) as orthogonal: OA-EM rescues free-form codebooks while structured methods avoid the initialisation problem entirely through constrained geometry. But the ρ framework suggests a hybrid approach: use OA-EM-style Hessian-weighted initialisation within a *partially* structured codebook. For example, initialise a free-form codebook with OA-EM, then project the learned centroids onto a lattice or apply a structural regulariser during the M-step (e.g., forcing centroids to lie near E8 lattice points). This could combine the LUT dequantization advantage of free-form codebooks with the optimisation robustness of structured geometry—the OA-EM initialisation handles the basin-placement problem, while the structural constraint prevents the combinatorial explosion that makes beam search expensive. A strong follow-up would implement this hybrid for 2-bit Llama 3.2 3B quantization, measuring whether the structural constraint degrades perplexity relative to pure OA-EM (it should not, if OA-EM has already placed centroids in good regions) while reducing beam search cost (because the constraint prunes the search space). The key metric is whether the hybrid reaches OA-EM's post-PV perplexity with narrower beams or fewer epochs.

**Adversarial stress-testing of the basin-persistence claim through extended PV-tuning.** The paper's basin-persistence evidence (Table 2) uses PV-tuning with 5 epochs and 10K calibration samples. If the loss landscape truly contains distinct basins separated by high barriers, then *much longer* PV-tuning—50 epochs, 100K samples, learning rate warmup and decay schedules, perhaps even restarts from intermediate checkpoints—should fail to close the OA-EM–greedy gap, or at best close it extremely slowly. If extended PV-tuning *does* close the gap, it would refine the basin-persistence interpretation: the basins are distinct but the barriers are surmountable with enough optimisation, making initialisation an *efficiency* concern (OA-EM reaches the good solution faster) rather than a *capability* concern (greedy can never reach it). A strong follow-up would run PV-tuning on the greedy beam-8 baseline for 50 epochs, measuring WikiText-2 perplexity every epoch, and comparing against OA-EM beam-4 at matched total compute (quantization time + extended PV-tuning time). If greedy reaches OA-EM's perplexity after, say, 30 epochs, then the practical advice shifts from "always use OA-EM" to "use OA-EM to save PV-tuning compute." If greedy plateaus above OA-EM's perplexity even after 50 epochs, the basin-persistence claim is strengthened as a fundamental geometry result.

**Domain-shift robustness as a function of ρ and initialisation strategy.** The paper's Table 5 shows that greedy initialisation's pre-PV degradation scales with domain distance from the C4 calibration set: 1.04× in-domain (C4), 1.39× near-OOD (LAMBADA), 3.49× far-OOD (WikiText-2). OA-EM mitigates this gradient, presumably because its Hessian-weighted objective concentrates capacity on weight groups that are important across domains rather than those that are calibration-specific. A systematic follow-up would evaluate both greedy and OA-EM models (post-PV) on a diverse suite of OOD benchmarks spanning different text types—code (HumanEval, MBPP), formal reasoning (ARC, GSM8K if adapted for perplexity), non-English text, and domain-specific corpora (legal, biomedical)—to measure whether OA-EM's domain robustness advantage persists after PV-tuning and whether it generalises across model scales and architectures. The hypothesis is that OA-EM's output-aware initialisation produces codebooks that better preserve the "universal" linguistic features that transfer across domains, while greedy initialisation overfits codebook capacity to calibration-specific statistics. If confirmed, this would make OA-EM particularly valuable for general-purpose deployment where the inference-time data distribution is unknown or shifts over time.

**Extending the ρ framework to other vector quantization methods and compression granularities.** The paper defines ρ = N/K^M specifically for additive quantization where each weight group is a sum of M codewords from K-entry codebooks. But the underlying concept—the ratio of the number of items to be quantized to the representational capacity of the codebook system—generalises to any VQ method. For product quantization (PQ) where a vector is partitioned into M subvectors each quantized with a K-entry codebook, the analogous ratio would be N/K^M again but with a different interpretation (the subvectors are independent rather than additive). For residual VQ (RVQ) with M stages, it would be N/K^M where the codebooks are applied sequentially. For grouped lattice VQ (GLVQ), it would involve the lattice's covering radius and the per-group generation matrix capacity. A systematic survey computing the appropriate capacity ratio for each major VQ family, and measuring whether it predicts initialisation sensitivity (via pre-PV greedy vs. optimised-initialisation gaps), would establish whether ρ is a universal diagnostic or specific to additive quantization. The survey would also identify which VQ families are naturally in the overcomplete vs. undercomplete regime at typical LLM compression rates, providing guidance on which methods need initialisation-aware design and which are robust by construction.

**Scaling OA-EM to larger models and measuring the ρ-dependent compute tradeoff.** The paper evaluates up to Llama 3.1 8B. At larger scales (13B, 70B, 405B parameters), the representational ratio ρ increases (wider layers, same K^M = 65,536 at 2 bpp), predicting more severe initialisation sensitivity. But larger models also have smoother weight statistics (the 8B vs. 3B comparison in Table 4 shows this trend), which counteracts the ρ effect. A scaling study running OA-EM and greedy initialisation on Llama models at 8B, 13B, 34B, and 70B parameters (all at 2 bpp, M=2, g=8) would map how the pre-PV and post-PV gaps scale with model size. The prediction: pre-PV gaps should grow with ρ (which grows with layer width), but the growth rate should decelerate as weight distributions become smoother at larger scales. The post-PV gap might follow a different trajectory—perhaps shrinking with model size if larger models' finer-grained representations make PV-tuning more effective at escaping poor initialisation basins. This study would establish the practical scope of the initialisation problem: at what model scale does greedy initialisation become unaffordably bad, and at what scale does OA-EM's advantage become negligible because either PV-tuning or weight smoothness eliminates the gap? The compute cost of running OA-EM itself at these scales (the 3 rounds × 100 Adam steps × layer count) should also be measured, to determine whether the fixed OA-EM overhead amortises differently at different model sizes.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Edge deployment of 3B–8B LLMs on ARM CPUs and mobile SoCs via 2-bit LUT-based inference.** The paper directly enables a deployment scenario that was previously infeasible: running 3B–8B parameter language models at 2 bits per weight on hardware without tensor cores, using pure lookup-table dequantization with zero MAC operations. Before OA-EM, AQLM at 2 bpp on Llama 3.2 3B produced a model with 352.39 WikiText-2 perplexity at beam 4—completely unusable. A practitioner might try beam 16, spending 16.9 hours of quantization time, only to reach 46.01 perplexity—still far above the 7.28 FP16 baseline. OA-EM at beam 4 (6.1 hours) produces 16.82 perplexity pre-PV and 11.53 after PV-tuning—a genuinely usable model that preserves the O(1) LUT dequantization advantage. For a mobile developer targeting ARM Cortex-A series CPUs or smartphone SoCs where ALU cycles are the bottleneck, this means they can deploy Llama 3.2 3B at 2 bits (~0.75 GB of weights, fitting in the memory budget of a mid-range phone) with inference speed determined by memory bandwidth, not compute throughput. The practical workflow: calibrate with 128 C4 sequences, run OA-EM initialisation (adds modest overhead to the standard AQLM pipeline), beam search at beam 4–8 (6–10 hours on an A100, a one-time offline cost), PV-tune for 5 epochs, and deploy. The resulting model will be substantially better than what AQLM's standard greedy initialisation produces at any beam width, at lower total quantization cost.

**Cost-efficient one-time compression of open-weight models for community distribution.** When an organisation or individual compresses an open-weight model (e.g., Llama, Qwen, Mistral) for distribution to the community, the quantization cost is incurred once and amortised over thousands of downstream users. In this setting, the Pareto analysis in Table 16 provides a clear decision rule: use OA-EM at beam 4 (6.1h) rather than the standard approach of greedy initialisation at high beam widths (16.9h at beam 16). The OA-EM model is strictly better on every metric (WikiText-2: 11.53 vs. 12.01; average downstream accuracy: 0.589 vs. 0.585) at 2.8× lower compute cost. For a model hub operator compressing dozens of model variants (different sizes, fine-tuned checkpoints, quantisation levels), the cumulative compute savings are substantial. Specifically, compressing 10 model variants with OA-EM at beam 4 instead of greedy at beam 16 saves ~108 GPU-hours on A100-class hardware. The paper's finding that OA-EM at beam 8 with early stopping (b=8, e=5, 7.3h) matches greedy at beam 8 full epochs (b=8, e=100, 9.9h) in 26% less time (Table 2: 11.76 post-PV perplexity for both) provides an even faster pipeline for high-throughput compression where small perplexity differences are acceptable. The practical recommendation: community compression pipelines should adopt OA-EM as the default initialisation and reduce their beam search budgets, reallocating the saved compute to more model variants or more thorough downstream evaluation.

**Enabling iterative self-improvement loops for quantized models.** The paper's finding that the greedy baseline's pre-PV failure scales with domain distance from the calibration set (Table 5: 3.49× degradation on far-OOD WikiText-2 vs. 1.04× on in-domain C4) has practical implications for self-improvement pipelines where a quantized model generates its own training data. If a 2-bit AQLM model with greedy initialisation is used to generate text for fine-tuning (analogous to STaR or ReST^EM for quantized models), its outputs on OOD prompts will be severely degraded relative to its calibration-domain performance. The degradation gradient means the model will produce reasonable text for prompts resembling C4 (news, web text) but catastrophic text for prompts from different distributions (Wikipedia, books, code). OA-EM's 3.49× lower far-OOD perplexity at beam 8 pre-PV (17.39 vs. 60.61 on WikiText-2) means the resulting self-generated training data will be substantially higher quality across domains, reducing the risk of error propagation or distributional collapse in the self-improvement loop. A practical self-improvement setup: compress a base model with OA-EM at 2 bpp, use it to generate completions on a diverse prompt set, filter for quality using a smaller verifier model, and fine-tune the original FP16 model on the filtered completions, then re-compress. OA-EM ensures that the data generation step operates with a model that is not catastrophically domain-brittle, making the self-improvement loop viable at 2 bits where greedy initialisation would cause it to collapse on OOD data.

**Mixed-precision deployment with ρ-guided architecture decisions.** The ρ framework provides a principled way to decide *where* to allocate compression bits across a model's layers. In a typical transformer, different layers have different widths (embedding layer, attention projections, FFN up/down projections), and therefore different N values and different ρ at a given compression rate. Layers with higher N (wider FFN layers) have higher ρ at 2 bpp and are more sensitive to initialisation. A practitioner could compute ρ per layer, identify which layers are in the undercomplete regime (ρ > 1), and apply OA-EM (or even retain more bits—e.g., 2.5 bpp via larger M or smaller g) only to those layers, while using standard greedy initialisation for layers with ρ < 1. This targets the expensive OA-EM computation (3 rounds × 100 Adam steps per codebook, per layer) precisely where it matters, reducing the total quantization overhead while preserving the quality gains. The paper's data supports this: Llama 3.2 3B's failure at 2 bpp is driven by its high-ρ layers, and OA-EM's benefit is concentrated there. A practical mixed-precision pipeline: compute ρ for every layer, classify layers into overcomplete (ρ < 0.5), transitional (0.5 < ρ < 2), and undercomplete (ρ > 2), apply OA-EM only to transitional and undercomplete layers, and measure whether the resulting model matches full-OA-EM perplexity while reducing quantization time. The paper does not provide per-layer ρ values, so a practitioner would need to compute them from the model's architecture, but the framework is directly transferable.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper positions OA-EM as a drop-in replacement for residual k-means initialisation within additive quantization, and the experimental results specify the conditions under which the replacement matters:

- **Prefer OA-EM over standard greedy initialisation when** the representational ratio ρ = N/K^M is greater than approximately 1—that is, when the number of weight groups per layer exceeds the number of representable code combinations. At 2 bpp on Llama 3.2 3B, ρ ≈ 18, well into the undercomplete regime, and OA-EM reduces pre-PV perplexity from 60.61 to 17.39 (Table 1) and post-PV perplexity from 11.76 to 11.53 (Table 2), while also reducing quantization time when combined with narrow beams (6.1h for OA-EM beam 4 vs. 9.9h for greedy beam 8, with better post-PV quality).

- **Prefer OA-EM when deploying on hardware requiring LUT-based dequantization** (ARM CPUs, microcontrollers, mobile SoCs without tensor cores). Structured methods (GLVQ, QuIP#) avoid the initialisation problem entirely and may achieve better absolute perplexity at 2 bits, but require O(d) MAC operations per weight group at inference time. OA-EM makes free-form additive quantization viable at 2 bits while preserving the zero-MAC LUT dequantization pathway that is critical for ALU-constrained edge hardware. The choice is therefore between (a) structured codebooks with better perplexity but runtime MACs, and (b) OA-EM-initialised free-form codebooks with slightly worse perplexity but pure memory-read inference. The paper's data does not provide a direct perplexity comparison against GLVQ or QTIP, so a practitioner making this choice would need to benchmark both on their target hardware to determine whether the latency advantage of LUT dequantization outweighs the perplexity gap.

- **Prefer OA-EM when the model has high-magnitude outlier weight groups** (common in undertrained models or models without extensive training data). The Llama 3.2 3B vs. Llama 3.1 8B comparison in Table 4 shows that the 8B model—trained on 15T vs. 3T tokens—exhibits a much smaller greedy initialisation gap (18.86 vs. 60.61 pre-PV at beam 8) despite having higher ρ. The 3B model's more severe failure is attributed to outlier weight groups that dominate first-codebook capacity under Euclidean k-means. OA-EM's Hessian weighting specifically addresses this by concentrating capacity on output-sensitive groups rather than high-magnitude groups. For models known to have heavy-tailed weight distributions (undertrained models, models compressed early in training, some architectural variants), the gap between OA-EM and greedy initialisation will be larger than for well-trained models with smooth weight statistics.

- **Prefer standard greedy initialisation when** ρ ≪ 1 (overcomplete regime), because the pre-PV gap is small and PV-tuning compresses it further. At 3 bpp on Llama 3.2 3B, ρ ≈ 0.07, OA-EM improves pre-PV WikiText-2 from 9.52 to 8.87 (Table 6), a 0.65-point gap that shrinks to 0.12 after PV-tuning (8.66 vs. 8.54). The downstream accuracy improvement is 0.7 points on average (Table 7). Whether this marginal gain justifies the OA-EM overhead (3 EM rounds × 100 Adam steps per layer) depends on the practitioner's quality sensitivity and compute budget. For many 3 bpp deployments where 4-bit scalar quantization is already near-lossless, the small OA-EM advantage may not warrant the additional implementation complexity.

- **Prefer standard greedy initialisation when quantization time is the primary constraint and OA-EM's fixed overhead is not amortised.** The paper reports one configuration where OA-EM quantization time exceeds the greedy baseline: beam 8 with early stopping (b=8, e=5), where OA-EM takes 7.3h vs. 6.8h for greedy (Table 2), because the fixed cost of 3 EM rounds per layer is not fully offset by reduced beam search epochs at this extremely low epoch budget. In settings where quantization must complete in minimal wall-clock time (e.g., compressing hundreds of model variants in a CI/CD pipeline), and the beam search budget is already near-minimal (e=5 epochs), the OA-EM overhead may not be worth the small post-PV gain (11.76 vs. 12.69 WikiText-2 in this configuration).
