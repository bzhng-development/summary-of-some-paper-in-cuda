# SynPO: Self-Boosting Large Language Models with Synthetic Preference Data

**ArXiv:** [2410.06961](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.06961)

## 🎯 Pitch

A 7B model, trained solely on synthetic data generated from its own outputs, can surpass GPT-4’s instruction-following score on AlpacaEval 2.0 without any human preference annotations. This self-boosting loop, SynPO, works by having the model critique and refine its own generations, unlocking iterative, autonomous improvement.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **SynPO**, a self-boosting paradigm for LLM alignment that iteratively trains models on synthetic preference data with minimal human annotation. SynPO employs two complementary mechanisms: a **self-prompt generator** (which creates diverse synthetic prompts from random keyword triplets drawn from RefinedWeb paragraphs) and a **response improver** (which refines the model's own outputs into chosen responses while using initial outputs as rejected candidates, forming implicit generative reward signals). Applied to Llama3-8B and Mistral-7B over four iterations, SynPO achieves over 22.1% win rate improvements on both AlpacaEval 2.0 and ArenaHard while also boosting general performance by 3.2 to 5.0 average points on the Open LLM Leaderboard, establishing that iterative self-improvement through synthetic preference data can substitute for large-scale human annotation only when the seed SFT data maintains sufficient quality to prevent the response improver from degenerating into paraphrasing or distribution collapse.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Preference Data Is the Bottleneck for LLM Alignment

The fundamental problem this paper tackles is a practical bottleneck in the LLM alignment pipeline: **collecting high-quality preference data does not scale**. Modern LLMs achieve their instruction-following and helpfulness capabilities through alignment with human preferences, typically via Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF; Ouyang et al., 2022b) or Direct Preference Optimization (DPO; Rafailov et al., 2024). Both families of methods require triplets of the form `(prompt, chosen_response, rejected_response)` that encode which outputs humans prefer.

The authors identify that this data collection process suffers from two compounding problems:

- **Resource intensity**: Human annotation of preferences is expensive, slow, and difficult to scale. Each preference judgment requires a skilled annotator to read a prompt and two candidate responses, then decide which is better — a process that does not admit easy automation without sacrificing quality.
- **Creativity demand**: Beyond simply judging existing responses, constructing *diverse and novel prompts* that exercise the full range of an LLM's capabilities requires creative effort. A narrow prompt distribution leads to an aligned model that performs well on benchmark-like queries but fails on real-world diversity.

These problems intensify as models improve. The paper states this explicitly in Section 1:

> "As LLMs improve rapidly, collecting large, high-quality preference data for effective learning becomes increasingly challenging and costly, whether from humans or stronger models."

The reasoning is: as the model gets better, its outputs become more nuanced, making it harder for annotators (or even teacher models) to reliably distinguish chosen from rejected responses. The preference signal becomes weaker and noisier precisely when the model needs it most — a classic scaling bottleneck.

### Why This Problem Matters

The significance of the preference data bottleneck extends beyond academic interest into three practical domains:

**1. Continual model improvement becomes economically constrained.** If each round of alignment requires a fresh batch of human-annotated preference data, then the pace of model improvement is gated by annotation throughput and budget. Organizations with limited annotation resources cannot iterate quickly, creating an asymmetry where only well-resourced labs can pursue aggressive alignment schedules. A method that generates *synthetic* preference data from the model itself — without human annotation — democratizes the alignment process.

**2. Static preference datasets become stale.** Most alignment work relies on fixed, pre-collected datasets like UltraFeedback (Cui et al., 2023) or Anthropic's Helpfulness and Harmlessness data (Bai et al., 2022a). These datasets were collected at a specific point in time, from specific models, for specific capability levels. As base models improve through pretraining, the preference data's distribution becomes increasingly off-policy — the responses being judged no longer represent what the current model would actually produce. This off-policy gap can cause preference optimization to underperform or even degrade model quality, a phenomenon the paper explicitly references:

> "According to Yin et al. (2024), directly sampling preference pairs, which closely resembles an on-policy setting, can result in performance declines due to inherent volatility and inefficiency."

**3. The alignment tax problem remains unsolved.** Prior work (Askell et al., 2021; Meng et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2024b) has documented an "alignment tax": optimizing for human preferences can degrade general model capabilities (reasoning, knowledge, math) because the preference data provides narrow supervision focused on stylistic qualities (helpfulness, harmlessness) rather than factual correctness or reasoning depth. The paper explicitly notes this tension:

> "Previous works demonstrate that preference optimization can induce the 'alignment tax' — aligning models with human preferences can improve performance for only 1∼2 iterations or even degrade overall performance on downstream tasks."

This means that simply doing *more* alignment on *more* preference data is not a viable path — the data must also preserve or enhance general capabilities, which human-annotated preference data is not designed to do.

### Where Prior Approaches Fall Short

The paper identifies four categories of prior work, each with specific limitations that SynPO is designed to address:

**1. Human-annotated preference datasets (the standard paradigm).** Datasets like those from Ouyang et al. (2022a), Bai et al. (2022a), Ethayarajh et al. (2022), and Nakano et al. (2021) rely on human annotators to compare model outputs and select preferences. The limitations are straightforward: high cost, limited scale, and annotation platform constraints that restrict prompt diversity. The authors note:

> "Acquiring preference data from humans can be resource intensive, often constrained by the data collection platform or the cost of human annotation."

Even when human annotation is feasible, the resulting datasets are static — they cannot adapt as the model improves across iterations.

**2. Teacher LLM annotation (GPT-4 as a judge).** To reduce human annotation costs, several works use stronger LLMs (primarily GPT-4) to simulate human preference judgments. UltraFeedback (Cui et al., 2023) uses GPT-4 to score model outputs on multiple axes (helpfulness, honesty, instruction-following) and construct preference pairs. Open Hermes Preferences (Huang et al., 2024) follows a similar pattern. The critical limitation, which the paper identifies, is that **the prompts still need to be collected manually**:

> "However, the prompts still need to be collected, which limits the domain, diversity, and quantity of the data."

Using GPT-4 as a judge solves the *annotation* cost problem but not the *prompt diversity* problem. The model can only be aligned on prompts that someone has already thought to write down. Furthermore, teacher LLM annotation incurs API costs that scale with data volume, creating a different kind of resource constraint.

The paper also notes a subtler limitation: reward scores or binary win/lose annotations fail to capture the full complexity of human preferences:

> "Moreover, relying solely on reward scores or win-lose annotation fails to fully capture the subtleties and complexities of human preferences."

A score of "0.8" or a label of "chosen" provides no information about *why* one response is better or *how* to improve the rejected response — it is a purely discriminative signal with no generative guidance.

**3. On-policy sampling and ranking methods.** More recent work attempts to eliminate external annotation entirely by having the model generate multiple responses to each prompt and then ranking them using either a reward model or LLM-as-a-Judge. Meng et al. (2024) (SimPO) and Wu et al. (2024b) (SPPO) sample multiple on-policy responses and use scoring models to select the best and worst as chosen and rejected pairs. Self-Rewarding language models (Yuan et al., 2024) generate their own rewards via LLM-as-a-Judge prompting.

The paper identifies a critical failure mode in these approaches:

> "These works typically use a fixed set of existing prompts, limiting the LLM ability to learn across wide scenarios. Furthermore, the deterministic reward signals in these methods do not help the model recognize subtle discrepancies between its responses and ideal responses."

Two distinct problems here: **(a)** Fixed prompts mean the model only learns preferences on a static distribution, missing the diversity needed for generalization. **(b)** Deterministic reward signals (a scalar score or a binary win/lose label) tell the model *that* one response is better but not *what specifically makes it better* or *how to transform the worse response into the better one*. This is a crucial distinction: discriminative rewards identify quality differences; generative rewards (which SynPO provides via the response improver) demonstrate how to bridge those differences.

The paper also references Yin et al. (2024)'s finding that straightforward on-policy sampling can cause performance declines due to volatility and inefficiency — the model's own outputs may not contain enough variance to create useful preference pairs, especially early in training when the model is weak.

**4. Synthetic data generation for instruction tuning (not preference learning).** A parallel line of work generates synthetic prompts for supervised fine-tuning rather than preference optimization. Self-Instruct (Wang et al., 2022) uses seed instructions and in-context examples to prompt the model to generate new instructions. WizardLM (Xu et al., 2023a) and WizardMath (Luo et al., 2023) use stronger LLMs like ChatGPT to rewrite simple instructions into more complex forms via "Evol-Instruct." Unnatural Instructions (Honovich et al., 2022) generates prompts from seed examples with minimal human labor.

The paper identifies two key limitations that SynPO addresses:

- **Dependence on stronger LLMs or carefully designed examples.** Self-Instruct requires in-context examples of the desired output format. WizardLM requires ChatGPT (a stronger model) to perform the instruction evolution. These methods benefit from "model distillation" — knowledge transfer from a stronger teacher to a weaker student — but this assumes access to such a teacher, which may not always be available or affordable.

- **Focus on instruction tuning, not preference learning.** These methods generate `(prompt, response)` pairs for SFT but do not produce the `(prompt, chosen, rejected)` triplets needed for preference optimization. They provide positive examples (what a good response looks like) but not contrastive examples (what distinguishes a good response from a bad one), which are essential for alignment methods like RLHF and DPO.

The paper positions itself as extending beyond these limitations:

> "Our approach uses the model itself to generate prompts and responses without needing carefully designed topics, extending beyond the model's inherent sampling space."

### How SynPO Positions Itself

SynPO's positioning can be understood through four deliberate design choices that directly address the gaps identified above:

**1. Self-generated prompts from random keywords (not from seed instructions or topic lists).** Unlike Self-Instruct (which uses seed prompts as examples) or WizardLM (which rewrites existing prompts), SynPO trains the LLM itself as a prompt generator using a *keywords-to-text* task. The keywords are randomly sampled from RefinedWeb paragraphs (Penedo et al., 2023) — three keywords from the same paragraph to preserve natural co-occurrence patterns, but drawn from different paragraphs across samples to ensure diversity. The model learns to generate coherent, fluent instructions from these sparse keyword inputs. This eliminates two dependencies simultaneously: no need for carefully designed prompt templates or topic lists, and no need for a stronger teacher model to perform prompt rewriting. The paper emphasizes the combinatorial advantage:

> "Due to the high combinatorial possibilities of keyword sampling, the self-prompt generator can produce an infinite variety of synthetic prompts for preference learning."

**2. Generative rewards via response improvement (not discriminative scores).** This is perhaps SynPO's most distinctive contribution. Rather than scoring responses with a reward model or LLM-as-a-Judge (discriminative signal), SynPO trains a *response improver* that transforms the model's current output into a higher-quality version (generative signal). The pre- and post-improvement responses naturally form a `(rejected, chosen)` pair. The paper draws on two intuitions from prior work:

> "(1) LLMs excel at identifying distribution gaps between texts (Zhong et al., 2022; Singh et al., 2022), and (2) refining a response is generally easier than generating a high-quality response from scratch (Madaan et al., 2023; Lu et al., 2023; Nguyen et al., 2024)."

The response improver is trained on seed SFT data: for each prompt in the seed data, the current model generates a response, and the improver learns to map `(prompt, model_response) → gold_standard_response`. This teaches the model to recognize and correct the specific kinds of errors it tends to make — the "distribution gap" between its own outputs and the target distribution. Crucially, because the improver generates an explicit improved response (not just a score), the preference signal includes *demonstrative* information: the chosen response shows the model what a better output looks like for the exact same prompt.

**3. Iterative, on-policy data generation (not static pre-collected data).** Each iteration of SynPO uses the *current* policy model (from the previous iteration) to generate responses, which are then refined by the response improver. This means the preference data is always on-policy — it reflects the model's actual output distribution at that stage of training. As the model improves, the nature of its errors changes, and the response improver (retrained each iteration on the current model's outputs against the seed data) adapts accordingly. This addresses the off-policy staleness problem that afflicts static datasets.

**4. Minimal seed data used only for validation and improver training (not for direct preference optimization).** SynPO requires only a small seed SFT dataset (18k examples from UltraFeedback with GPT-4 Turbo completions). This seed data serves two auxiliary roles: training the self-prompt generator (by extracting keywords from seed prompts and pairing them with seed responses), and training the response improver (by providing gold-standard targets for the improver to learn to map toward). The actual preference optimization is performed entirely on synthetic data. The seed data does *not* directly become preference pairs for training the policy model. This is a crucial design choice: it decouples the limited human-annotated data from the scaling bottleneck, using it only as a quality *compass* rather than as training *fuel*.

The paper also explicitly contrasts with Constitutional AI (Bai et al., 2022b) and SELF (Lu et al., 2023), which used AI-generated feedback but did not exploit the comparative information between pre- and post-revision texts:

> "However, these methods did not utilize comparative information between pre- and post-revision texts for training."

This is a subtle but important distinction. Constitutional AI used refinement data to train a reward model (discriminative), not to construct preference pairs directly. SELF used GPT-4 to generate refinement demonstrations, then trained models to self-refine, but did not use the pre-refinement and post-refinement outputs as a preference pair for preference optimization — the refinement was the end product, not an intermediate signal for alignment.

### Summary of the Gap

To crystallize the motivation: the field has methods that can **(a)** generate synthetic prompts but rely on stronger models or fixed templates (Self-Instruct, WizardLM), **(b)** generate synthetic preference judgments but rely on fixed prompt sets and discriminative scores (UltraFeedback, Self-Rewarding), and **(c)** perform iterative self-improvement but use only reward signals without generative demonstrations (Sampling-Ranking, SPPO). No prior method combines all three properties — **diverse self-generated prompts**, **generative (not just discriminative) preference signals**, and **iterative on-policy data creation** — without requiring a stronger teacher model or large-scale human annotation. SynPO is designed to fill exactly this gap.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

SynPO is an iterative training system that enables a language model to improve its own instruction-following capabilities by generating diverse synthetic prompts, refining its own responses to those prompts, and using the pre- and post-refinement responses as preference training data, all from a small seed set of high-quality examples. The system solves a data bottleneck: traditional alignment requires large amounts of human-annotated preference data (which is expensive and static), while SynPO produces unlimited synthetic preference data on-policy, allowing the model to self-boost through multiple iterations without external annotation.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture

The system has five major components that operate in a cycle across iterations (`t = 1, 2, ..., T`):

1. **Seed SFT Data**: A small, fixed set of 18k `(prompt, gold_response)` pairs sampled from UltraFeedback with GPT-4 Turbo completions. Serves as the quality compass for training both the prompt generator and the response improver but is never directly used for preference optimization.
2. **Self-Prompt Generator (`G`)**: An SFT-trained variant of the base LLM that generates diverse synthetic prompts `x_i` from random keyword triplets drawn from RefinedWeb paragraphs. Produces 50k new prompts per iteration.
3. **Policy Model (`π_{θ_t}`)**: The LLM being aligned. At iteration `t`, it is the model from the previous iteration (`π_{θ_{t-1}}`). It generates responses `y_{t-1, i}` to synthetic prompts.
4. **Response Improver (`R_t`)**: Trained at each iteration to map `(prompt, current_model_response) → gold_seed_response`. Applied to synthetic prompts to refine the policy model's outputs into higher-quality versions, producing chosen responses `y^w_{t-1, i}`.
5. **Preference Optimization Engine**: Takes the synthetic preference pairs — `(synthetic_prompt, refined_chosen_response, initial_rejected_response)` — and updates the policy model using SimPO (Meng et al., 2024), producing `π_{θ_t}` for the next iteration.

**Information flow per iteration**: The seed data is used to train `G` (once) and `R_t` (each iteration). `G` generates synthetic prompts from keywords. `π_{θ_{t-1}}` generates responses to those prompts. `R_t` refines those responses into chosen candidates. A filter removes pairs where the improvement gap is too small. The filtered pairs (along with the initial model's `π_{θ_0}` response as the rejected candidate) are added to a growing preference dataset `D`. `π_{θ_0}` is then optimized on `D` via SimPO to produce `π_{θ_t}`.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First, the Self-Prompt Generator**: how keywords are sampled, how the generator is trained, how prompts are generated at scale, and why this approach produces more diverse prompts than alternatives.
- **Second, the Response Improver**: how it is trained at each iteration from seed data and current model outputs, the intuition behind learning to identify distribution gaps, and why the seed data must be filtered before improver training.
- **Third, Synthetic Preference Pair Construction**: how pre- and post-refinement responses become chosen/rejected pairs, why the initial model (`π_{θ_0}`) output is used as the universal rejected response, and the data filtering criteria.
- **Fourth, the Preference Optimization Objective (SimPO)**: the loss function, its parameters, how it differs from standard DPO, and why it is compatible with SynPO's iterative synthetic data pipeline.
- **Fifth, the Iterative Algorithm**: the full loop connecting all components, the key hyperparameter sweeps and thresholds, and what changes across iterations.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is a **methodology paper** whose core idea is that a language model can self-improve its alignment by generating its own diverse prompts, learning to refine its own outputs into higher-quality versions, and using the before-and-after refinement pairs as synthetic preference data for iterative preference optimization, all anchored by a small set of seed SFT examples that never directly enter the preference training set.

#### Self-Prompt Generator Training and Synthetic Prompt Creation

The self-prompt generator is the component that eliminates SynPO's dependence on manually collected prompts. It transforms the base LLM into a model that produces diverse, fluent user instructions from sparse keyword inputs, trained on data derived entirely from the seed SFT dataset.

**Keyword extraction from seed data.** For each prompt `x*_i` in the seed SFT data, the authors extract three keywords: two randomly sampled from `x*_i` itself (ensuring topical relevance to the original instruction) and one randomly sampled from a *different* seed prompt `x*_j` where `j ≠ i` (called a "noise keyword"). The inclusion of the noise keyword is deliberate and important: it forces the prompt generator to learn to *filter out* irrelevant keywords during generation rather than blindly incorporating all provided keywords. The paper states:

> "The inclusion of the noise keyword enhances the robustness of the prompt generator. It learns to filter out irrelevant keywords during training and ensure that the generated prompts are fluent."

This produces a training dataset of `(keyword_list_i, (x*_i, y*_i))` pairs — the keyword list as input and the original seed prompt with its GPT-4 Turbo completion as the target output.

**Generator training via SFT.** The base model `π_{θ_0}` is fine-tuned on these pairs using standard supervised fine-tuning. The prompt template used during training is shown in Figure 3 and includes sections for background description, requirements (the instruction should incorporate the given keywords and the solution must be correct with detailed reasoning), output format specification, and the keyword list. The hyperparameters for this SFT stage are:

- Learning rate: `1.0 × 10^{-6}` for both Mistral-Base and Llama3-Base
- Batch size: 32
- Warm-up ratio: 0.1
- Optimizer: AdamW
- Maximum sequence length: 8,000 tokens
- Epochs: 3

The output of this training is the self-prompt generator `G`, which is a variant of the same base LLM now specialized for prompt generation. The generator is trained once (not per-iteration) because its purpose — producing diverse prompts — does not depend on the policy model's current capabilities.

**Keyword sampling for large-scale prompt generation.** To generate a large volume of diverse prompts, the authors construct a keyword pool from RefinedWeb (Penedo et al., 2023), a large-scale web corpus. The process:

1. Randomly sample 1 million paragraphs from RefinedWeb.
2. From each paragraph, randomly select 3 keywords.
3. This yields 1 million keyword lists, where keywords within each list come from the same paragraph (preserving natural co-occurrence patterns) but different lists come from different paragraphs (ensuring topical diversity across lists).

At each iteration, between 36,000 and 72,000 keyword lists are sampled from this pool (the range depends on the filtering ratio at that iteration, discussed later). Lists containing personal names or stopwords are excluded.

**Prompt generation at scale.** For each sampled keyword list `k_i`, the generator produces a synthetic prompt:

$$x_i \sim G(\cdot | k_i)$$

Inference uses vLLM for efficiency with sampling temperature set to 0.7. From these, the authors generate 50k synthetic prompts per iteration after filtering. The prompts are designed to cover diverse user intentions and topics — Figure 4 shows the top 25 topics (outer circle: Social media, Education, Art and culture, Food and drink, Technology, etc.) and top 12 intentions (inner circle: Learn, Translation, Create, Write, Seek, Analyze, etc.), classified by GPT-4 Turbo using the prompt template from Figure 8.

**Diversity advantage over existing prompt sources.** The paper provides quantitative evidence that SynPO-generated prompts are more diverse than those from existing datasets. Figure 5 shows the inter-prompt similarity distribution for 1,000 randomly sampled prompts from SynPO, UltraFeedback (Cui et al., 2023), Self-Instruct (Wang et al., 2022), and UltraChat (Ding et al., 2023). Inter-prompt similarity is computed by:
- Encoding each prompt with Sentence-Transformer (Reimers and Gurevych, 2019) to obtain embeddings.
- Computing the cosine similarity between each prompt and all other prompts.
- Averaging these similarity values for each prompt, producing a single score per prompt.

SynPO prompts exhibit lower average similarity (distribution shifted leftward, peaking near 0.0–0.05) compared to UltraFeedback (peaking near 0.08–0.10), Self-Instruct (peaking near 0.06–0.08), and UltraChat (peaking near 0.12–0.14). Lower inter-prompt similarity means the prompts are more distinct from each other, covering a broader semantic space.

**Why keyword-based generation rather than instruction rewriting.** The design trades off two properties: controllability and diversity. Methods like Self-Instruct and WizardLM generate prompts by conditioning on or rewriting existing seed instructions, which produces prompts that are topically similar to the seed distribution. SynPO's keyword-based approach, by sampling keywords from a massive web corpus rather than from seed prompts, can produce prompts on topics entirely absent from the seed data. The combinatorial explosion of possible keyword triplets (from 1 million lists) provides effectively unbounded prompt diversity.

The tradeoff is controllability: keywords provide only a loose topical signal, and the generated prompts may sometimes be incoherent or fail to incorporate the keywords. The noise keyword in training is designed to mitigate this by teaching the model to selectively attend to relevant keywords and ignore irrelevant ones, producing fluent output even when the keyword list contains unrelated items.

#### Response Improver Training

The response improver `R_t` is the component that provides generative (rather than discriminative) preference signals. It is retrained at each iteration to reflect the current model's specific error patterns.

**Training data construction.** At the start of iteration `t`, the policy model from the previous iteration (`π_{θ_{t-1}}`) generates responses to the seed SFT prompts:

$$y^*_{(t-1), i} \sim \pi_{\theta_{t-1}}(\cdot | x^*_i), \quad i \in \{1, \dots, n\}$$

where `n = 18,000` is the number of seed examples. Each training instance for the response improver consists of:
- **Input**: the seed prompt `x*_i` concatenated with the current model's response `y*_{(t-1), i}`.
- **Output**: the gold standard response `y*_i` from the seed data (GPT-4 Turbo completion).

The prompt template used for training and inference is shown in Figure 7 (Appendix B), which frames the task as: "For a given question-answer pair, improve the answer by correcting errors, bolstering informativeness, aligning with the question, and providing comprehensive detail."

Formally, the response improver is trained by minimizing:

$$R_t \leftarrow \arg\min_{\theta} \sum_{i=0}^{n} \mathcal{L}(\pi_{\theta}(x^*_i, y^*_{(t-1), i}), y^*_i)$$

where `L` is the standard language modeling cross-entropy loss, `π_θ` is initialized from `π_{θ_0}` (the original base model, not the current policy model — an important detail), `(x*_i, y*_{(t-1), i})` is the concatenated input, and `y*_i` is the target sequence (only the gold response tokens contribute to the loss; the input tokens are masked).

**Training hyperparameters.** The response improver is trained with:
- Learning rate: `1.0 × 10^{-6}` (same as prompt generator)
- Batch size: 32
- Warm-up ratio: 0.1
- Optimizer: AdamW
- Maximum sequence length: 6,000 tokens (shorter than the prompt generator's 8,000)
- Epochs: 3

**Seed data filtering before improver training.** A critical pre-processing step: as the policy model improves across iterations, it may produce responses that are *better* than the gold standard responses in the seed data. Training the response improver on such cases — where it would learn to map a *good* model output to a *worse* gold standard — would teach it to degrade responses rather than improve them. The paper calls this avoiding "negative optimizations":

> "Our objective is for the response improver to learn from its deficiencies. Therefore, we identify instances where the model output is inferior to the original response using the same scoring model as the filtering stage. This ensures that the response improver only learns positive optimizations or semantic paraphrasing, rather than negative optimizations."

The scoring model used for this filtering is the same one used for the main data filtering pipeline: PairRM (0.4B parameters) for Mistral-Base, and ArmoRM-Llama3-8B-v0.1 for Llama3-Base. For a given seed example `i`, if the score difference between the gold standard completion and the model completion exceeds a threshold, the example is included in the improver training set. The thresholds are:
- Mistral-Base (PairRM): score difference threshold of 0.20
- Llama3-Base (ArmoRM-Llama3-8B-v0.1): score difference threshold of 0.02

Examples where the model output is too close to or better than the gold standard are excluded, ensuring the improver only learns to map from worse to better.

**What the response improver learns.** The training objective teaches the improver to recognize and correct the specific types of errors the current policy model makes on the seed distribution. Unlike a reward model that outputs a scalar indicating response quality, the response improver outputs a *complete revised response* that demonstrates what a better answer looks like. This is the "generative reward" concept: the improver shows the model not just *that* its output was suboptimal, but *how specifically* to improve it for that exact prompt.

The paper grounds this in two empirical observations from prior work:

> "(1) LLMs excel at identifying distribution gaps between texts (Zhong et al., 2022; Singh et al., 2022), and (2) refining a response is generally easier than generating a high-quality response from scratch (Madaan et al., 2023; Lu et al., 2023; Nguyen et al., 2024)."

The reasoning is: if the model can already detect that its own outputs differ in quality from gold-standard outputs (distribution gap identification), and if it is easier to edit an existing response toward higher quality than to generate a perfect response de novo (refinement is easier than generation), then training a response improver on `(prompt, model_output) → gold_output` pairs should be an efficient way to capture improvement knowledge.

**Why retrain the improver each iteration.** The distribution of errors the policy model makes shifts as the model improves. In early iterations, the model may produce responses with factual errors or poor structure; the improver learns to fix those. In later iterations, the model's outputs are generally higher quality, and remaining errors are subtler (e.g., slightly incomplete reasoning, minor stylistic issues). Retraining the improver on the current model's outputs against the fixed seed data ensures the improver is always learning to address the gap that actually exists at that stage of training. If the improver were static (trained only once on `π_{θ_0}` outputs), it would become increasingly off-policy and might attempt to fix errors the model no longer makes while missing new error modes that emerge during iterative training.

**Limitation acknowledged by the authors.** Because the improver is trained on `(model_response, gold_response)` pairs rather than minimal-edit pairs, it may learn to paraphrase the gold response rather than make targeted corrections to the model's output:

> "Since the response improver data are automatically derived from SFT data conversion, the model also learns paraphrasing."

The paper acknowledges that using a stronger model like GPT-4 to generate minimal-edit improvement data (as in Lu et al., 2023) would alleviate this issue but would sacrifice the self-boosting property (dependence on external strong models). This is an intentional tradeoff: accept some paraphrasing in exchange for complete self-sufficiency.

#### Synthetic Preference Pair Construction

Once the response improver `R_t` is trained, it is applied to the synthetic prompts to generate chosen and rejected responses, forming the preference pairs that will be used for preference optimization.

**Generating responses to synthetic prompts.** For each synthetic prompt `x_i` (one of the ~50k prompts generated by `G` for this iteration), two responses are produced:

**Step 1 — Current policy model response:**

$$y_{(t-1), i} \sim \pi_{\theta_{t-1}}(\cdot | x_i)$$

This is the response from the policy model at the start of iteration `t` (after `t-1` rounds of preference optimization). It represents what the model would produce "in the wild" for this prompt.

**Step 2 — Refined response:**

$$y^w_{(t-1), i} \sim R_t(\cdot | x_i, y_{(t-1), i})$$

The response improver takes the prompt and the current model's response as input and produces a refined version. This refined response is the **chosen** candidate.

**Step 3 — Initial model response as rejected candidate:**

$$y^l_{(0), i} = y_{(0), i} \sim \pi_{\theta_0}(\cdot | x_i)$$

The rejected candidate is NOT the current model's response `y_{(t-1), i}` but rather the response from the *original, untrained base model* `π_{θ_0}` (after initial SFT). This is a crucial design choice that the paper justifies implicitly: using the original model's output ensures a consistent, stable baseline for the rejected response across all iterations. If the rejected response were the current model's output, the preference gap might shrink as the model improves (since `y_{(t-1), i}` gets better), making it harder to construct valid preference pairs. By anchoring the rejected response to `π_{θ_0}`, the chosen response (refined from the current model) is always compared against a fixed-quality baseline, maintaining a meaningful preference gap even as the current model improves.

**Data filtering.** Not all generated `(prompt, chosen, rejected)` triples are useful for training. The paper applies two filtering criteria:

1. **Preference gap threshold**: The chosen response must be significantly better than the rejected response according to the scoring model. The paper uses:
   - PairRM (0.4B parameters, Jiang et al., 2023) for Mistral-Base, with a score difference threshold of 0.20.
   - ArmoRM-Llama3-8B-v0.1 for Llama3-Base, with a score difference threshold of 0.02.

   The chosen response `y^w_{(t-1), i}` is compared against the rejected response `y^l_{(0), i}`. Only pairs where the score difference exceeds the threshold are retained. This filtering ensures that the preference optimization sees clear, unambiguous cases where the chosen response is genuinely better — training on marginal or ambiguous pairs would introduce noise and potentially degrade performance.

2. **Repetition filtering**: Any data containing over 50% repetition patterns is filtered out. This is a safeguard against a known failure mode in self-training on synthetic data: the model can collapse into producing repetitive or degenerate outputs, and training on such outputs reinforces the collapse. The paper explicitly states this is to "avoid model collapse on synthetic data."

Valid triples `(x_i, y^w_{(t-1), i}, y^l_{(0), i})` are added to the growing synthetic preference dataset `D`. The paper notes:

> "In our experiments, we randomly incorporated 10,000 preference pairs from each iteration to the whole synthetic preference data."

This suggests that `D` accumulates across iterations — preference pairs from iteration 1, iteration 2, etc., are all pooled together, and each iteration's preference optimization trains on this accumulated (and growing) dataset. The 10,000 per iteration is a controlled subset; if 50k prompts are generated and some fraction passes filtering, the authors sample 10,000 to add to the pool, preventing the dataset from growing too large.

**Why the scoring model choice matters.** The paper uses different scoring models for different base models: the small 0.4B PairRM for Mistral-Base and the larger ArmoRM-Llama3-8B-v0.1 for Llama3-Base. The justification is in the Llama3-Base-specific choice:

> "For Llama3-Base 8B, we use Llama3 itself (ArmoRM-Llama3-8B-v0.1) as a scoring model for data filtering, given its superior alignment with human scoring."

ArmoRM-Llama3-8B-v0.1 is a reward model based on the same Llama3 architecture as the policy model, which likely provides better calibration on Llama3 outputs than a generic small reward model would. The Mistral-Base setting uses the smaller, generic PairRM because there may not be a Mistral-specific reward model of comparable quality available at the time. This asymmetry in scoring model quality may explain some of the performance differences between Mistral and Llama3 observed in downstream tasks (e.g., GSM8k performance decline for Mistral but improvement for Llama3, as seen in Table 4).

**Relationship to the improver training filter.** The same scoring model and thresholds are used for filtering BOTH the improver training data (Section C.2) and the preference pairs. This is a consistent design: in both cases, the goal is to retain only cases where there is a clear quality gap. For improver training, the gap is between the model output and the gold standard (ensuring the improver learns to improve, not degrade). For preference data, the gap is between the chosen (refined) and rejected (initial model) responses (ensuring the preference optimization has a clear signal).

---

#### Preference Optimization Objective

SynPO uses SimPO (Simple Preference Optimization, Meng et al., 2024) as the preference optimization algorithm, though the paper notes the method is compatible with other objectives like DPO (Rafailov et al., 2024) or KTO (Ethayarajh et al., 2024).

**The SimPO loss function.** For a preference dataset `D` of triples `(x, y^w, y^l)`, the optimization objective is:

$$\theta_t \leftarrow \arg\min_{\theta} \mathbb{E}_{(x_i, y^w_i, y^l_i) \sim D} \left[ \log \sigma \left( \frac{\beta}{|y^w_i|} \log \pi_{\theta_{t-1}}(y^w_i | x_i) - \frac{\beta}{|y^l_i|} \log \pi_{\theta_{t-1}}(y^l_i | x_i) - \gamma \right) \right]$$

where:
- `σ` is the sigmoid function `σ(z) = 1 / (1 + e^{-z})`, mapping real values to (0, 1),
- `β` is a scaling hyperparameter controlling the strength of the preference update,
- `|y^w_i|` and `|y^l_i|` are the token lengths of the chosen and rejected responses, respectively,
- `log π_{θ_{t-1}}(y | x)` is the log-probability the current policy model assigns to response `y` given prompt `x` (sum of log-probabilities across all tokens in `y`),
- `γ` is a target reward margin hyperparameter.

**What it computes, operationally.** For each preference triple, the loss performs these steps:

1. Compute the *average* log-probability of the chosen response: `(1/|y^w|) * log π(y^w|x)`. This is the per-token log-likelihood of the chosen response under the current policy.
2. Compute the same for the rejected response: `(1/|y^l|) * log π(y^l|x)`.
3. Take the difference of these average log-probabilities: `(chosen_avg_logp) - (rejected_avg_logp)`. A positive difference means the model assigns higher probability to the chosen response.
4. Scale by `β` and subtract the margin `γ`: this produces a scalar score indicating whether the model's preference aligns with the training data, with a minimum required gap of `γ`.
5. Apply the sigmoid: this maps the score to a probability in (0, 1).
6. Take the negative log: this is the standard log-loss for a binary classification where the label is always "1" (chosen is better). The loss is minimized when the model assigns sufficiently higher average log-probability to the chosen response than to the rejected response.

**Why SimPO rather than DPO.** The critical difference between SimPO and standard DPO is the **length normalization**: DPO uses unnormalized sequence log-probabilities `log π(y|x)`, which grow linearly with sequence length. This creates a systematic bias: longer responses (whether chosen or rejected) receive more extreme log-probability values simply because they contain more tokens, making the optimization unstable and potentially favoring verbose outputs. SimPO normalizes by response length `|y|`, converting to *average per-token log-probability*. This length normalization has two effects:

- It removes the verbosity bias, so the optimization focuses on *per-token quality* rather than *total probability mass*.
- It makes the loss scale-invariant to response length, which is important for SynPO because the response improver may produce chosen responses of different lengths than the rejected responses.

SimPO is also **reference-free**: unlike DPO, which requires a reference model (typically the initial SFT model) and penalizes deviations from it via a KL divergence term, SimPO optimizes only the current policy's probabilities. This is philosophically aligned with SynPO's iterative approach — the model should be free to shift its distribution across iterations without being anchored to a fixed reference.

**The role of `β` and `γ`.** 

- `β` controls the *sharpness* of the preference signal. A larger `β` amplifies the difference between chosen and rejected log-probabilities, making the loss more sensitive to small probability differences. The paper sweeps `β` in the range [2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12] for each optimization process, selecting the best value, because the optimal scaling depends on the specific model and data distribution.

- `γ` is a *target margin*: the model must achieve at least a `γ` difference in average log-probability between chosen and rejected before the loss saturates. Without `γ` (setting `γ = 0`), the model could satisfy the loss by assigning even a trivially higher probability to the chosen response. The margin forces a meaningful separation. The paper fixes `γ = 1.6` for both Mistral-Base and Llama3-Base.

**Initialization for each iteration's optimization.** A subtle but important detail: each iteration's preference optimization starts from `π_{θ_0}`, the *original SFT model*, not from `π_{θ_{t-1}}`. The paper states:

> "In each iteration t = 1, ..., T, we use model π_{θ_{t-1}} from the previous iteration to generate synthetic preference data and then preference optimize the initial models again."

This means the policy model learned in iteration `t-1` is used only for *data generation* (producing responses that get refined), but the *optimization* always resets to the initial model. This design prevents the accumulation of optimization artifacts across iterations — if each iteration started from the previous iteration's model, errors or overfitting from earlier rounds could compound. By always optimizing from scratch on the accumulated preference dataset `D`, each iteration benefits from more data (since `D` grows) while starting from a clean model state.

**Accumulating preference data across iterations.** The dataset `D` is not reset each iteration; it accumulates:

$$D \leftarrow D \cup \{(x_i, y^w_{(t-1), i}, y^l_{(0), i}) \mid F(x_i, y^w_{(t-1), i}, y^l_{(0), i}) = \text{valid}, i \in \{1, \dots, m\}\}$$

where `F` is the filtering function. At iteration 1, `D` contains valid pairs from the first round of generation. At iteration 2, `D` contains pairs from both round 1 and round 2. This means later iterations train on larger, more diverse datasets encompassing improvement examples from multiple stages of model capability.

---

#### The Complete Iterative Algorithm

Algorithm 1 in Appendix A provides the formal specification. Here we walk through the full loop with concrete numbers and design rationale.

**Initial state.** The system begins with:
- Initial policy model `π_{θ_0}`: Mistral-Base 7B or Llama3-Base 8B, both Supervised Fine-Tuned on UltraChat-200k (following the Zephyr pipeline).
- Validation set: 18k seed SFT examples `{(x*_i, y*_i)}` (UltraFeedback prompts with GPT-4 Turbo completions).
- Keyword pool `K`: 1 million keyword lists from RefinedWeb paragraphs.
- Self-prompt generator `G`: trained once on the seed data using the keywords-to-text task (Section 2.1).
- Empty synthetic preference dataset `D`.

**Per-iteration steps (for t = 1, 2, 3, 4):**

**Step 1: Generate synthetic prompts.** Sample 36k–72k keyword lists from `K` (the number varies based on expected filtering ratios). For each keyword list `k_i`, generate a prompt:

$$x_i \sim G(\cdot | k_i)$$

using vLLM with temperature 0.7. After generation and filtering (excluding lists with personal names or stopwords, and removing incoherent outputs), this yields `m ≈ 50,000` synthetic prompts for the iteration. These prompts are entirely new — they are not constrained by the seed data topics or the previous iteration's topics.

**Step 2: Train the response improver.** 
- Generate current model responses to seed prompts: `y*_{(t-1), i} \sim \pi_{\theta_{t-1}}(\cdot | x*_i)` for `i = 1, ..., 18,000`.
- Filter seed examples where the gold response is not significantly better than the model response (using PairRM or ArmoRM thresholds).
- Fine-tune `π_{θ_0}` on the filtered `(prompt + model_response → gold_response)` pairs to obtain `R_t`.
- Training hyperparameters: lr = 1e-6, batch size 32, warmup ratio 0.1, AdamW, max sequence length 6,000, 3 epochs.

**Step 3: Generate and refine responses to synthetic prompts.**
- For each synthetic prompt `x_i`, generate current model response: `y_{(t-1), i} \sim \pi_{\theta_{t-1}}(\cdot | x_i)`.
- Refine using the improver: `y^w_{(t-1), i} \sim R_t(\cdot | x_i, y_{(t-1), i})`.
- Retrieve the initial model response (generated once and cached or regenerated): `y^l_{(0), i} \sim \pi_{\theta_0}(\cdot | x_i)`.
- Both generations use vLLM with temperature 0.7.

**Step 4: Filter and integrate into `D`.**
- Compute scores for `y^w_{(t-1), i}` and `y^l_{(0), i}` using the scoring model.
- Retain pairs where the score difference exceeds the threshold (0.20 for PairRM on Mistral; 0.02 for ArmoRM on Llama3).
- Remove any pairs where either response contains >50% repetition.
- Randomly sample 10,000 valid pairs and add to `D`: `D ← D ∪ {(x_i, y^w_{(t-1), i}, y^l_{(0), i})}`.

The 10,000 cap per iteration controls dataset growth: after 4 iterations, `D` contains up to 40,000 preference pairs. Without this cap, if most of the 50k prompts produced valid pairs, `D` would grow to 200k pairs by iteration 4, potentially making training prohibitively slow and diluting the signal from early iterations.

**Step 5: Preference optimization.**
- Train `π_{θ_0}` on the accumulated dataset `D` using SimPO.
- Sweep `β` in [2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12] for each optimization; select best based on validation performance on the seed data.
- Fix `γ = 1.6`.
- The resulting model is `π_{θ_t}`, which becomes the policy model for iteration `t+1`.

**Step 6: Loop.** Increment `t` and return to Step 1, now using `π_{θ_t}` for generating responses in Step 2 and Step 3.

**Why four iterations?** The paper runs 4 iterations for both models. The results in Figure 1, Table 1, and Table 2 show that performance continues to improve through iteration 3 or 4 on most metrics (AlpacaEval 2.0 LC win rate: 5.4 → 10.6 → 23.4 → 28.6 → 32.1 for Llama3; Arena-Hard peaks at iteration 3 for both models). The paper does not explicitly state a stopping criterion, but the diminishing returns at iteration 4 (Arena-Hard actually declines slightly from iteration 3 to 4 for both models) suggest that 3–4 iterations is the practical limit of SynPO's benefits. Beyond this point, the policy model's responses to synthetic prompts become too similar to the improver's refinements (the preference gap shrinks), or the improver's paraphrasing limitation begins to dominate.

**What changes across iterations.** Three things change from iteration to iteration:

1. **The response improver** is retrained on the current policy model's responses to seed data. As the model improves, the improver learns to address more subtle error patterns.
2. **The generated chosen responses** `y^w_{(t-1), i}` become higher quality because the policy model's initial responses `y_{(t-1), i}` are better starting points for refinement.
3. **The accumulated preference dataset** `D` grows, incorporating improvement demonstrations from multiple stages of model capability, providing a multi-faceted training signal.

What stays fixed: the seed SFT data (always the same 18k examples), the keyword pool (same 1 million lists), the self-prompt generator (trained once), the scoring model and thresholds, and the initial model `π_{θ_0}` for generating rejected responses.

---

#### Design Choices Summary

**Why a separate response improver rather than a reward model?** The improver provides generative guidance — it shows the model *how* to improve, not just *that* improvement is needed. This is the paper's central differentiating claim: discriminative rewards (scalar scores, win/lose labels) tell you response A is better than response B; generative rewards (the improver's output) demonstrate what a better response looks like for the exact same prompt. The chosen response in each preference pair is not just "better" in some abstract sense — it is better *in the specific ways the improver learned to correct*, and because the improver is trained on the current model's error patterns, those corrections are targeted at the model's actual weaknesses.

**Why filter both improver training data and preference pairs?** The filtering serves different purposes in each case but uses the same mechanism. For improver training: ensures the improver learns to improve, not degrade. For preference pairs: ensures the preference optimization has a clear, unambiguous signal. Without filtering, both components would be trained on noisy or reversed-gap data, which would either produce no benefit (if the improver learns to copy its input) or actively harm performance (if the preference optimization learns that rejected responses are sometimes better than chosen ones).

**Why always use the initial model's output as the rejected response?** This provides a fixed, stable baseline. If the rejected response were the current model's output `y_{(t-1), i}`, then as the model improves, the gap between chosen and rejected would shrink, making it harder to construct valid preference pairs. The author's choice ensures that even as `y_{(t-1), i}` becomes higher quality (making it easier for the improver to refine into a good chosen response), the rejected response remains low-quality, maintaining a trainable preference gap.

**Why reset to `π_{θ_0}` for each optimization rather than continuing from `π_{θ_{t-1}}`?** This prevents error accumulation. If each iteration started from the previous model, overfitting to early synthetic data or optimization artifacts from early iterations could compound. Training from scratch on the accumulated dataset means each iteration's model sees all historical preference data with equal weight, benefiting from the growing dataset without being contaminated by previous optimization trajectories. The previous model `π_{θ_{t-1}}` is used only for *data generation*, not as the starting point for *optimization*.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Preference Signals Can Be Generative Rather Than Discriminative — The Response Improver as a Different Category of Reward

The paper's most intellectually distinctive contribution is a conceptual reframing of what constitutes a "reward" for preference optimization. Nearly all prior alignment work — from RLHF (Ouyang et al., 2022b) to DPO (Rafailov et al., 2024) to Self-Rewarding (Yuan et al., 2024) — uses **discriminative rewards**: a scalar score, a binary win/lose label, or a ranking that tells the model *that* response A is better than response B, but provides no information about *what specifically makes A better* or *how to transform B into something like A*. These are evaluation signals, not improvement demonstrations.

SynPO's response improver introduces **generative rewards**: instead of scoring the model's output, it *rewrites* the output into a higher-quality version, and the rewritten text itself becomes the chosen response in a preference pair. The preference signal is not a number — it's a complete demonstration of improvement for that exact prompt. The model sees, in the preference pair `(refined_response, original_response)`, not just that the refined version is preferred, but *what changed* — specific phrasings, added details, corrected reasoning steps, improved structure. The signal is embedded in the textual difference between the chosen and rejected responses.

This matters because it addresses a fundamental limitation of discriminative rewards identified in the paper's own motivation: that "relying solely on reward scores or win-lose annotation fails to fully capture the subtleties and complexities of human preferences." A score of 0.8 tells the model nothing about whether the improvement needed is factual correction, stylistic refinement, or structural reorganization. The response improver's output, by contrast, demonstrates the specific kind of improvement needed because it is trained on the *current model's actual error patterns* against the seed data.

Prior work that used AI-generated feedback — Constitutional AI (Bai et al., 2022b) and SELF (Lu et al., 2023) — generated refinement data but did not use the pre- and post-refinement texts as preference pairs. Constitutional AI used refinements to train a reward model (still discriminative). SELF used GPT-4 to generate refinement demonstrations for teaching self-refinement, but the refinement was the end product, not a preference signal for a separate alignment objective. SynPO is, to the authors' knowledge, the first method to systematically construct preference pairs where the chosen response is a *functionally generated improvement* of the rejected response on the same prompt, creating a self-contained improvement demonstration in every training example.

The significance extends beyond raw performance. The generative reward concept suggests a different paradigm for alignment research: rather than building better discriminative reward models (the dominant focus of much RLHF work), one could invest in building better *improver* models that demonstrate corrections. This shifts the intellectual problem from "how do we accurately score response quality?" to "how do we teach models to recognize and correct their own specific error patterns?" — a more constructive and potentially more scalable framing. The evidence supporting this conceptual contribution is distributed throughout the results (Tables 2, 4, 5), where SynPO outperforms discriminative-only baselines like Sampling-Ranking and Self-Rewarding, but the key support is architectural rather than numeric: the response improver is the *mechanism* by which SynPO achieves its gains, and its presence distinguishes the method categorically from all baselines.

**Classification**: This is a fundamental conceptual shift, not an incremental refinement. It redefines what a "reward signal" can be in preference optimization.

---

### Innovation 2: Self-Generated Prompt Diversity from Unstructured Keywords Eliminates Both Topic-Lists and Teacher Models

The self-prompt generator's design represents a deliberate break from the two dominant paradigms for synthetic prompt creation. Prior work either **(a)** used stronger teacher models to rewrite or evolve seed prompts (WizardLM's Evol-Instruct uses ChatGPT to complexify instructions; Xu et al., 2023a) or **(b)** used in-context examples and seed instruction patterns to prompt the model to generate similar prompts (Self-Instruct; Wang et al., 2022). Both approaches constrain prompt diversity: method (a) depends on the teacher model's capabilities and costs, while method (b) produces prompts that are distributionally similar to the seed examples.

SynPO's keywords-to-text approach requires neither. By training the LLM to generate coherent instructions from three random keywords — and sampling those keywords from RefinedWeb paragraphs rather than from seed prompts — the generator produces prompts on topics that may be entirely absent from the seed data. The noise keyword in training (one keyword from an unrelated seed prompt) teaches selective attention: the model learns to ignore irrelevant keywords rather than forcing them into the output, producing fluent prompts even from loosely related keyword sets. This is a simple but conceptually important design choice: it decouples prompt diversity from seed data coverage.

The quantitative evidence in Figure 5 shows that SynPO prompts have lower inter-prompt similarity than prompts from UltraFeedback, Self-Instruct, and UltraChat — meaning they are more semantically distinct from each other. This is not a marginal improvement; the similarity distribution for SynPO peaks near 0.0–0.05 while UltraFeedback peaks near 0.08–0.10, a roughly 2× difference in modal similarity. The combinatorial argument — 1 million keyword lists, each a triplet, creating a vast space of possible keyword combinations — suggests this diversity advantage is structural, not an artifact of specific keyword choices.

The significance of this innovation is partly in what it eliminates: no dependency on GPT-4 or any stronger model for prompt generation, no dependency on carefully curated topic lists, no dependency on in-context examples that constrain the output distribution. The model generates prompts entirely from its own learned mapping between keyword semantics and instruction formats. This makes the approach fully self-contained — the same LLM that will be aligned is also the engine of its own prompt diversity — and eliminates API costs or annotation effort for prompt creation.

However, it is important to recognize this as a primarily *engineering* innovation rather than a conceptual one. The keywords-to-text training paradigm is novel in the context of preference data generation, but it is an application of standard SFT on a constructed dataset. The intellectual contribution is the recognition that **(a)** prompt diversity is the binding constraint on synthetic preference data quality, **(b)** keyword-based generation from a massive corpus can achieve greater diversity than instruction-rewriting from seed prompts, and **(c)** a noise keyword in training enables robust handling of irrelevant keywords. These are design insights rather than algorithmic breakthroughs. The ablation in Table 6 supports the claim: on Llama3-8B, SynPO-generated prompts produce better alignment (24.3 LC / 24.5 WR) than manually collected UltraFeedback prompts (23.8 / 25.6) or Self-Instruct prompts (21.7 / 21.4) when all use the same SynPO refinement process, and mixing SynPO prompts with manual prompts yields the best overall result (23.4 / 29.4 for SynPO Mix with Sampling-Ranking).

**Classification**: This is an incremental but practically significant innovation — a novel application of existing SFT techniques that solves a specific bottleneck (prompt diversity without teacher models) through careful design of training data and keyword sampling.

---

### Innovation 3: Iterative Self-Improvement Requires Re-Anchoring to a Fixed Quality Compass — The Seed Data as Validation Shield, Not Training Fuel

One of the paper's subtler but most important conceptual contributions is the demonstration that iterative self-improvement on synthetic data works reliably only when the seed high-quality data is used *exclusively for validation and guidance* rather than directly for training. This is not an obvious design choice — the intuitive approach would be to include the seed SFT data directly in the preference optimization dataset, since it is the highest-quality data available. SynPO deliberately does not do this.

The seed data serves three auxiliary roles — training the self-prompt generator (once), training the response improver (each iteration), and filtering improver training examples — but is *never* included as preference pairs for optimizing the policy model. The paper validates this choice through the ablation in Table 7, which compares SynPO Iter4 (32.1 LC / 33.6 WR on AlpacaEval 2.0 for Llama3) against three alternatives that use seed data more directly: Seed PO (11.6 / 10.7), Seed SFT + PO (24.6 / 20.9), and Seed SFT + PO with multiple epochs (22.4 / 15.0). All three underperform SynPO substantially, and multi-epoch training on seed-derived preference data *degrades* performance relative to single-epoch training.

The conceptual insight here is that the seed data functions as a **quality compass** — it orients the synthetic data generation process toward higher-quality outputs — but if used as training data, it creates distribution shift. The model overfits to the specific style and content of the seed examples (which are GPT-4 Turbo completions on UltraFeedback prompts) rather than learning generalizable preference patterns from the diverse synthetic data. The response improver, trained on `(model_output → seed_gold_response)` pairs, converts the seed data's quality signal into improvement demonstrations on the synthetic prompt distribution, acting as a *translator* between the high-quality seed domain and the diverse synthetic domain. The preference optimization then sees only synthetic data that has been "blessed" by this translation process, never the seed data directly.

This finding has implications beyond SynPO's specific architecture. It suggests a general principle for self-improvement systems: the data that defines "quality" (human-annotated, teacher-model-generated, or otherwise authoritative) should influence the *generation* of training data but should not itself be part of the training distribution if the training distribution is intended to be broader and more diverse. The seed data provides a target direction (improve toward this quality level) but the synthetic data provides the coverage (learn to be better across many domains). Mixing them directly causes the model to optimize for the narrow seed distribution at the expense of the broad synthetic distribution, which is the opposite of what iterative self-improvement on synthetic data needs.

The paper also demonstrates a failure mode when this principle is violated: the ReST^EM experiment mentioned in the limitations (though not appearing in the main results) shows that on-policy data collection without this seed-data-as-compass design can cause performance degradation due to spurious correlations. And the response improver training's seed data filtering step (excluding examples where the model output is already better than the gold standard) is an explicit acknowledgment that even the compass can point in the wrong direction if the model has already surpassed parts of the seed data — the compass must be dynamically recalibrated to only show the model where it is still deficient.

**Classification**: This is a fundamental insight with implications beyond this paper — a diagnostic finding about the structural relationship between seed data and synthetic data in iterative self-improvement systems, supported by a clear ablation (Table 7) that demonstrates the counter-intuitive result that *not* training on your best data can produce better models.

---

### Innovation 4: The Preference Gap as a Filtering Criterion, Not Just a Training Signal — Why Training on Clear Wins Beats Training on Everything

SynPO introduces a simple but consequential design principle: filter preference pairs to retain only those where there is a **clear, measurable gap** between chosen and rejected responses, and discard marginal cases. This is implemented through the scoring model thresholds (0.20 for PairRM, 0.02 for ArmoRM) and the repetition filter (>50% repetition eliminated). The innovation is not the filtering itself — many methods filter data — but rather the recognition that in a synthetic, self-generated preference data pipeline, *filtering aggression is a first-order determinant of whether iterative improvement succeeds or collapses*.

The conceptual logic, which the paper demonstrates implicitly through its iterative success rather than through a dedicated ablation, is as follows. When a model generates its own preference data, the chosen-rejected gap for most prompt-response pairs will be small — the model's current outputs and the improver's refinements are often similar, differing only in minor ways. Training on these small-gap pairs provides a weak and noisy preference signal. Worse, as the model improves across iterations, this gap naturally shrinks further (the model's outputs get closer to the improver's refinements), making the signal progressively weaker. Without aggressive filtering, the preference optimization in later iterations would be training on near-indistinguishable pairs, causing the optimization to either stall or overfit to noise.

By retaining only pairs where the scoring model confidently identifies a preference gap, SynPO ensures that each training example provides a strong, unambiguous signal. The 10,000-pair-per-iteration cap further concentrates the training data on the highest-quality examples. This is particularly important because SimPO — unlike DPO — is reference-free and has no KL penalty anchoring the model to its initial distribution, making it more susceptible to noise in the preference data. Clean pairs are essential for stable optimization.

The paper's results indirectly validate this principle through two observations. First, SynPO continues to improve through iteration 3–4 on most metrics (Table 2, Figure 1), unlike the Sampling-Ranking baseline which plateaus much earlier (Table 2 shows Sampling-Ranking Iter4 for Mistral-Base at 11.6 LC vs. SFT at 6.6, far below SynPO's 34.0). Sampling-Ranking uses the same scoring models but selects the highest and lowest among *randomly sampled model outputs*, which are often similar in quality — producing small-gap preference pairs that provide weak training signal. Second, Table 4 shows that Mistral-Base degrades on GSM8k (34.72 → 31.08) while Llama3-Base improves (51.93 → 55.72), which the paper attributes to ArmoRM's superior filtering capability compared to PairRM. This suggests that filter quality — the ability to correctly identify meaningful preference gaps — directly impacts downstream task preservation, not just alignment metrics.

The principle is generalizable: in any self-improvement system where the model generates its own training signal, the *selectivity* of that signal — how aggressively low-quality or ambiguous examples are filtered out — may matter more than the *volume* of data. This echoes findings from the data curation literature (e.g., Phi models training on textbook-quality data; Li et al., 2023) but applies it specifically to the preference optimization setting where the training signal is inherently contrastive.

**Classification**: This is an incremental but practically crucial insight — a design principle validated by comparative benchmark performance against unfiltered baselines, though the paper does not provide a dedicated ablation isolating the filtering threshold's effect, making it partly an inferred rather than directly demonstrated innovation.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The paper uses three categories of evaluation benchmarks. For **instruction-following alignment**: AlpacaEval 2.0 (805 prompts, pairwise comparison against GPT-4 Turbo baseline, judged by GPT-4 Turbo), Arena-Hard (500 challenging queries, pairwise comparison against GPT-4-0314 baseline, judged by GPT-4 Turbo), and MT-Bench (80 multi-turn questions spanning 8 categories, single-answer grading by GPT-4 Turbo on a 1–10 scale). For **general performance**: the Open LLM Leaderboard (6 datasets — ARC, HellaSwag, TruthfulQA, MMLU, Winogrande, GSM8k — following standard few-shot settings as specified in Table 9). For **additional downstream tasks**: 6 benchmarks from the LM Evaluation Harness library (OBQA, Haerae, MathQA, XNLI, Toxigen, PROST) evaluated under a fixed 5-shot setting.

- **Base model(s).** All experiments use Mistral-Base 7B (`mistralai/Mistral-7B-v0.1`) and Llama3-Base 8B (`meta-llama/Meta-Llama-3-8B-Base`). Both models are first supervised fine-tuned on UltraChat-200k following the Zephyr training pipeline (Tunstall et al., 2023), producing the initial policy models `π_{θ_0}`. These serve as the starting point for all SynPO iterations and all baselines. The choice of 7B–8B scale models is deliberate: they are representative of the capabilities of widely-deployed open-source LLMs, sit in a regime where alignment improvements are both measurable and practically meaningful, and are computationally feasible for the multi-iteration training pipeline (each iteration requires improver training, large-scale generation, and preference optimization).

- **Metrics.** **AlpacaEval 2.0** reports two win rates against GPT-4 Turbo: raw win rate (WR, fraction of comparisons where the model's response is judged better) and length-controlled win rate (LC, which regresses out response length bias using the method from Dubois et al., 2024). **Arena-Hard** reports raw win rate (WR) against GPT-4-0314. **MT-Bench** reports GPT-4 Turbo scores on a 1–10 scale, reported separately for Turn 1 and Turn 2 (multi-turn capability). **Open LLM Leaderboard** reports dataset-specific metrics: `acc_norm` for ARC and HellaSwag, `mc2` for TruthfulQA, `acc` for MMLU, Winogrande, and GSM8k (per Table 9). **LLM Harness tasks** each report accuracy. All alignment evaluations use GPT-4 Turbo as judge (specifically the `turbo-2024-04-09` API on Azure OpenAI Service, per Appendix E). Decoding for AlpacaEval 2.0 uses temperature 0.7 and max tokens 2048; MT-Bench uses the official decoding setup with category-specific temperatures (Appendix C.6).

- **Baselines.** The paper compares against five baselines, all using the same initial SFT models and the same SimPO preference optimization objective:
  1. **SFT**: The initial supervised fine-tuned model with no further preference optimization — this is the floor.
  2. **Manual Collection (UltraFeedback)**: Preference optimization on the full UltraFeedback dataset (Cui et al., 2023), which contains 61k prompts with GPT-4-annotated preferences. This represents the standard paradigm of using a large, human-curated and teacher-annotated preference dataset. The authors use the well-trained versions from the official SimPO repository.
  3. **Sampling-Ranking**: An iterative on-policy baseline where, at each iteration, the model samples 5 responses per prompt (50k synthetic prompts generated by SynPO's prompt generator), and the scoring model (PairRM or ArmoRM) selects the highest and lowest scoring responses as chosen and rejected, respectively. This mirrors the approaches of Meng et al. (2024) and Wu et al. (2024b) but uses SynPO's prompt generator for fair comparison.
  4. **Self-Rewarding**: Following Yuan et al. (2024), the model generates its own preference judgments via LLM-as-a-Judge prompting. The authors generate 16k seed data with GPT-4 Turbo for LLM-as-a-Judge training, then use SynPO's self-generated prompts for constructing preference pairs based on the model's own rewards.
  5. **Seed SFT / Seed PO / Seed SFT + PO / Seed SFT + PO (multi-epoch)** (Table 7 ablation only): Variants that directly use the 18k seed SFT data for training rather than as a quality compass, testing whether SynPO's seed-data-as-validation design is necessary.

  All iterative baselines (Sampling-Ranking, Self-Rewarding) use the same number of iterations and the same data volume per iteration as SynPO, and all use the self-generated prompts validated to be superior in Section 4.1. This ensures that any performance difference is attributable to the *preference construction mechanism* (response improvement vs. discriminative scoring vs. LLM-as-a-Judge), not to prompt quality or data quantity.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper does not report FLOP counts. Instead, fairness is controlled through **data volume matching**: each iteration of SynPO and each baseline iteration generates preference data from the same ~50k synthetic prompts. SynPO generates one additional response per prompt (the improver's refinement), but the core training budget — number of preference pairs optimized on — is held constant at 10,000 pairs incorporated per iteration for SynPO. Sampling-Ranking samples 5 responses per prompt, which is more generation than SynPO's 2 responses (current model + improver), making SynPO actually *cheaper* in generation per iteration. However, SynPO incurs the cost of training the response improver each iteration (SFT on up to 18k examples), which the discriminative baselines do not. The paper does not provide a direct FLOP comparison, which is a limitation in the compute accounting.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not report formal cross-validation or confidence intervals. The lack of error bars on any figures or tables is a notable omission. The main statistical control is procedural: all methods are evaluated on the same standard benchmarks with the same decoding parameters and the same GPT-4 Turbo judge version. For the iterative baselines, the paper states that "all preference construction processes are iterated until performance no longer improves" (Appendix C.5), and reports the best performance across iterations with an asterisk in tables when a method peaks before the final iteration. The `β` hyperparameter is swept individually for each optimization process (range [2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12]), with the best value selected — this introduces a mild form of per-method hyperparameter optimization that could advantage methods with more stable performance across `β` values, though the effect is likely small given the narrow range.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Instruction-Following Alignment: Single-Turn Dialogues

The headline result is that SynPO achieves sustained improvement in instruction-following capability over four iterations, substantially outperforming all discriminative baselines and approaching proprietary model performance. Table 2 and Figure 1 present the core evidence.

**AlpacaEval 2.0 (Table 2, Figure 1).** On the length-controlled win rate against GPT-4 Turbo:

- **Mistral-Base**: SFT starts at 6.6% LC. SynPO iteratively improves to 13.3% (Iter1) → 25.7% (Iter2) → 31.7% (Iter3) → 34.0% (Iter4). This represents a **27.4 percentage point absolute increase** from SFT to Iter4. Raw win rate follows a similar trajectory: 3.6% → 15.3% → 28.1% → 33.8% → 36.4%, a **32.8 percentage point increase**.
- **Llama3-Base**: SFT starts at 5.4% LC. SynPO improves to 10.6% (Iter1) → 23.4% (Iter2) → 28.6% (Iter3) → 32.1% (Iter4). This is a **26.7 percentage point absolute increase**. Raw win rate: 3.1% → 10.7% → 24.1% → 31.5% → 33.6%, a **30.5 percentage point increase**.

The improvement is not merely sustained — it **accelerates** at Iter2. For both models, the gain from Iter1 to Iter2 (12.4 percentage points for Mistral, 12.8 for Llama3) is larger than the gain from SFT to Iter1 (6.7 and 5.2, respectively). This acceleration pattern is consistent with the response improver becoming more effective as the policy model improves: in Iter1, the model's outputs are poor, the improver fixes coarse errors, and the resulting preference pairs teach basic quality improvements. In Iter2, the model has already learned those basics, so the improver (retrained on the Iter1 model's errors) addresses subtler issues, producing preference pairs that teach more nuanced improvements. The gains then decelerate at Iter3 and Iter4, approaching diminishing returns.

**Comparison to baselines (Table 2).** At their respective best iterations:

- Manual Collection (UltraFeedback): Mistral reaches 21.5 LC / 20.8 WR; Llama3 reaches 22.0 LC / 19.8 WR. SynPO Iter4 surpasses these by 12.5 and 15.6 percentage points LC for Mistral and Llama3, respectively.
- Sampling-Ranking peaks at Iter4 for Llama3 (14.2 LC / 8.4 WR) and Iter4 for Mistral (11.6 LC / 8.0 WR) — dramatically below SynPO. The large gap (Mistral: 34.0 vs. 11.6 LC; Llama3: 32.1 vs. 14.2) directly validates the generative reward hypothesis: selecting the best and worst from 5 random samples (discriminative) provides far weaker training signal than using an improver to generate a specifically improved version of the model's own output (generative).
- Self-Rewarding performs better than Sampling-Ranking but still substantially below SynPO. Mistral peaks at Iter4 with 26.1 LC / 28.0 WR; Llama3 peaks at Iter4 with 24.8 LC / 25.6 WR. The gap to SynPO Iter4 is 7.9 percentage points LC for Mistral and 7.3 for Llama3. This is particularly informative because Self-Rewarding uses the same prompts as SynPO and also generates a form of on-policy signal (LLM-as-a-Judge scores), but the signal is still discriminative — a score, not a demonstration of improvement.

**Arena-Hard (Table 2).** On this more challenging benchmark (500 harder queries, judged against GPT-4-0314):

- **Mistral-Base**: SFT at 2.0% WR → SynPO improves to 9.8% (Iter1) → 20.8% (Iter2) → 24.1% (Iter3) → 22.8% (Iter4). Peak at Iter3 with a **22.1 percentage point absolute increase** over SFT. The decline at Iter4 (24.1 → 22.8) is the first evidence of potential over-optimization on alignment metrics — a pattern the paper does not discuss but is visible in the data.
- **Llama3-Base**: SFT at 2.7% WR → SynPO improves to 11.6% (Iter1) → 24.6% (Iter2) → 32.5% (Iter3) → 31.4% (Iter4). Peak at Iter3 with a **29.8 percentage point absolute increase**. Again, a slight decline at Iter4.

The Iter4 decline on Arena-Hard for both models is noteworthy. Possible explanations, none explored in the paper: (a) the response improver at Iter4, trained on an already-strong model's outputs, produces refinements that are primarily paraphrases rather than substantive improvements (the paraphrasing limitation acknowledged in the text), making the Iter4 preference pairs less informative; (b) the accumulated dataset `D` by Iter4 contains ~40k preference pairs, and the earlier (larger-gap) pairs dominate the optimization, making later (smaller-gap) pairs contribute minimally or even introduce noise; (c) the SimPO optimization with fixed `γ = 1.6` may not be well-calibrated for the smaller preference gaps in later iterations.

**Comparison to baselines on Arena-Hard:**
- Manual Collection: Mistral 16.8 WR, Llama3 23.2 WR. SynPO Iter3 surpasses these by 7.3 and 9.3 points, respectively.
- Sampling-Ranking peaks far below: Mistral 9.6 WR (Iter4), Llama3 10.4 WR (Iter4).
- Self-Rewarding: Mistral 21.1 WR (Iter4), Llama3 25.0 WR (Iter4). Gaps to SynPO Iter3 of 3.0 and 7.5 points — smaller than the AlpacaEval gap but still substantial.

**Positioning against proprietary models (Table 1, Figure 1).** Table 1 places Mistral-Base-SynPO Iter4 in the context of the AlpacaEval 2.0 leaderboard. Its 34.0 LC / 36.4 WR surpasses GPT-4-0314 (35.3 LC / 22.1 WR — SynPO's WR is substantially higher), Mistral Large 123B (32.7 LC / 21.4 WR), GPT-4-0613 (30.2 LC / 15.8 WR), Claude 2 (28.2 LC / 17.2 WR), and Gemini Pro (24.4 LC / 18.2 WR). It trails only GPT-4 Turbo (the reference baseline at 50.0) and Meta-Llama-3-70B-Instruct (34.4 LC / 33.2 WR — comparable LC, slightly lower WR). Figure 1 visually shows the iterative trajectory approaching GPT-4-0314 level. The fact that a 7B model with SynPO can approach GPT-4-0314 and exceed much larger proprietary models on this benchmark is a striking result, though it must be interpreted in the context of AlpacaEval 2.0's known sensitivity to response style and length.

#### Instruction-Following Alignment: Multi-Turn Dialogues

**MT-Bench (Table 3, Figure 6).** MT-Bench tests a different capability: multi-turn conversation, where the model must maintain coherence and quality across two turns of dialogue with a user. Results are reported as GPT-4 scores out of 10.

- **Mistral-Base**: SFT at 6.04 (Turn 1) / 5.65 (Turn 2). SynPO peaks at Iter3 with 6.86 (Turn 1) / 6.82 (Turn 2). Improvements of 0.82 and 1.17 points, respectively. Iter4 shows a slight decline to 6.73 / 6.69.
- **Llama3-Base**: SFT at 6.55 (Turn 1) / 5.36 (Turn 2). SynPO peaks at Iter4 with 7.43 (Turn 1) and Iter3 with 7.34 (Turn 2). Improvements of 0.88 and 1.98 points, respectively. The Turn 2 improvement is substantially larger than Turn 1, indicating SynPO particularly benefits multi-turn coherence — a capability that requires the model to understand context, maintain state, and build on previous turns, all of which the response improver's refinement demonstrations may implicitly teach.

**Comparison to baselines:**
- Manual Collection: Mistral 6.73 / 6.82, Llama3 7.29 / 7.00. SynPO's peaks are slightly higher for Mistral (6.86 vs. 6.73 Turn 1) and comparable for Llama3 (7.43 vs. 7.29 Turn 1, 7.34 vs. 7.00 Turn 2).
- Sampling-Ranking peaks: Mistral 6.83 / 6.18 (Iter3), Llama3 7.06 / 6.99 (Iter4). SynPO outperforms on Turn 2 for both models (6.82 vs. 6.18 for Mistral; 7.34 vs. 6.99 for Llama3), suggesting the generative reward signal is particularly valuable for multi-turn capabilities.
- Self-Rewarding peaks: Mistral 6.71 / 6.63 (Iter3), Llama3 7.30 / 7.28 (Iter3). SynPO is comparable or slightly higher.

The MT-Bench results are less dramatically in SynPO's favor than the single-turn benchmarks. This is partly because MT-Bench has a compressed scoring range (most models cluster between 5–8) and partly because multi-turn capability may benefit less from the specific type of refinement data SynPO generates — the improver refines single responses, not multi-turn interactions. The gap between SynPO and Manual Collection narrows on MT-Bench relative to AlpacaEval 2.0, suggesting that the advantages of generative over discriminative rewards are more pronounced for single-turn quality than for multi-turn coherence.

**Radar chart analysis (Figure 6, Appendix G Figure 10).** The radar chart for Llama3-8B-Base-SynPO on MT-Bench shows improvement across all 8 categories from SFT through Iter4. The most notable improvements are in:
- Roleplay: 2.0 → ~9.0+ (largest relative gain)
- STEM: from ~6.0 at SFT to 9.8 at Iter4
- Reasoning: from ~6.0 to 9.9
- Coding: from ~5.2 to ~9.0
- Humanities: from ~7.0 to 10.0

The Writing and Extraction categories show more modest gains. This pattern suggests SynPO's improvement is not uniform — it particularly boosts capabilities that involve *generating structured, knowledge-rich responses* (STEM, Reasoning, Humanities) while providing smaller benefits for *creative or extractive tasks* (Writing, Extraction). The Mistral radar chart (Appendix G, Figure 10) shows similar category-level patterns.

#### General Downstream Task Performance

**Open LLM Leaderboard (Table 4).** This is the critical test of whether SynPO's alignment improvements come at the cost of general capabilities (the "alignment tax"). The average score across all 6 tasks:

- **Mistral-Base**: SFT average 58.20 → SynPO Iter4 average 61.39, a **3.19 percentage point increase**. Notably, the trajectory is not monotonic: Iter1 reaches 60.83, Iter2 reaches 61.25, Iter3 dips slightly to 60.70, and Iter4 recovers to 61.39. The dip at Iter3 is driven by declining GSM8k and TruthfulQA scores, which partially recover at Iter4.
- **Llama3-Base**: SFT average 63.34 → SynPO Iter4 average 68.34, a **5.00 percentage point increase**. Improvement is largely monotonic: 66.41 (Iter1) → 67.92 (Iter2) → 68.27 (Iter3) → 68.34 (Iter4), with diminishing returns after Iter3.

**Per-task analysis reveals important asymmetries between models:**

- **ARC (reasoning)**: Both models show strong gains. Mistral: 58.02 → 63.57 (+5.55); Llama3: 60.92 → 66.47 (+5.55). Consistent with the MT-Bench reasoning category improvement.
- **TruthfulQA (knowledge + truthfulness)**: Mistral: 40.37 → 56.12 (+15.75) — the single largest gain for either model on any task. Llama3: 45.37 → 63.69 (+18.32). This is particularly significant because TruthfulQA tests resistance to generating common misconceptions, an ability that requires nuanced understanding of factual accuracy — exactly the kind of subtle quality improvement that the response improver's generative demonstrations might teach.
- **MMLU (broad knowledge)**: Both models show essentially no change. Mistral: 58.78 → 58.75 (−0.03); Llama3: 63.80 → 63.79 (−0.01). This null result is important: SynPO's alignment process does not degrade broad factual knowledge (no alignment tax on MMLU), but it also does not improve it. MMLU tests memorized factual knowledge across 57 subjects, which is not a capability that preference optimization on instruction-following data would be expected to enhance.
- **GSM8k (math)**: The asymmetric result. Mistral degrades: 34.72 → 31.08 (−3.64). Llama3 improves: 51.93 → 55.72 (+3.79). The paper attributes this to the scoring model quality difference: "This disparity likely stems from the superior data filtering capability of ArmoRM-Llama3-8B-v0.1 compared to the 0.4B PairRM. ArmoRM-Llama3-8B-v0.1 effectively mitigates erroneous responses and enhances mathematical problem-solving performance." This is a plausible but untested explanation — the paper does not provide a direct ablation replacing PairRM with a stronger scoring model for Mistral to confirm the causal link.
- **Winogrande (commonsense reasoning)**: Both models essentially flat. Mistral: 76.40 → 75.77; Llama3: 76.72 → 76.90.
- **HellaSwag (commonsense reasoning)**: Both improve modestly. Mistral: 80.89 → 83.04 (+2.15); Llama3: 81.28 → 83.44 (+2.16). The nearly identical improvement on both models (+2.15–2.16) is striking and suggests a systematic effect rather than noise.

**Comparison to baselines on Open LLM Leaderboard:**

- Manual Collection: Mistral 60.91, Llama3 65.95. SynPO Iter4 is slightly higher for Mistral (61.39 vs. 60.91) and substantially higher for Llama3 (68.34 vs. 65.95). This is a key result: SynPO not only matches but exceeds the alignment performance of a large human-curated preference dataset while also improving general capabilities.
- Sampling-Ranking peaks: Mistral 59.91, Llama3 67.33. SynPO outperforms for both models.
- Self-Rewarding peaks: Mistral 59.24, Llama3 66.30. SynPO outperforms, particularly on Llama3 (68.34 vs. 66.30, a 2.04 point gap).

The fact that all baselines except Manual Collection underperform SynPO on the Open LLM Leaderboard while also underperforming on alignment metrics (AlpacaEval, Arena-Hard) suggests that SynPO's generative reward approach improves both alignment and general capabilities simultaneously, partially mitigating the alignment tax.

**LM Evaluation Harness (Table 5).** Six additional tasks provide broader capability coverage:

- **Average across 6 tasks**: Mistral SFT 46.42 → SynPO Iter4 48.61 (+2.19); Llama3 SFT 52.79 → SynPO Iter4 55.79 (+3.00). Both models show monotonic or near-monotonic improvement across iterations.
- **OBQA (open-book QA)**: Steady improvement for both models (Mistral: 46.40 → 51.40; Llama3: 46.20 → 52.00). The +5+ point gains are consistent with broader knowledge/reasoning improvement.
- **Toxigen (toxicity detection)**: Notable improvement — Mistral: 60.11 → 63.40; Llama3: 68.83 → 75.18 (+6.35). The large gain on Llama3 is interesting: toxicity detection requires nuanced understanding of harmful content, and the improver's refinement demonstrations may implicitly teach safer response patterns even though safety is not an explicit training objective.
- **MathQA (math reasoning)**: Both models show negligible change or slight decline. Mistral: 36.25 → 36.48 (+0.23); Llama3: 42.04 → 42.65 (+0.61). The paper acknowledges this mirrors the alignment tax pattern: "Our method exhibits similar behavior on MathQA; however, overall, SynPO shows improvements across more iterations on other tasks."
- **XNLI (cross-lingual NLI)**: Modest improvement. Mistral: 43.76 → 44.47; Llama3: 45.47 → 46.27.
- **Haerae (Korean knowledge)**: Very small changes. Mistral: 39.96 → 40.88; Llama3: 61.78 → 62.51.
- **PROST (physical reasoning)**: Consistent improvement. Mistral: 52.04 → 55.05; Llama3: 52.40 → 56.14.

**Comparison to baselines on LLM Harness tasks:**

- Manual Collection: Mistral 48.24, Llama3 55.14. SynPO Iter4 edges ahead for Mistral (48.61 vs. 48.24) and Llama3 (55.79 vs. 55.14).
- Sampling-Ranking: Mistral 47.48, Llama3 55.40. SynPO outperforms.
- Self-Rewarding: Mistral 47.29, Llama3 54.75. SynPO outperforms.

The pattern across both downstream evaluation suites is consistent: SynPO provides small but reliable improvements to general capabilities (+2–5 points on average), with the largest gains on TruthfulQA and ARC, while Manual Collection is the strongest baseline and occasionally competitive. The alignment tax — degradation on math tasks — is visible but contained to specific tasks (GSM8k for Mistral, minimal impact on Llama3), and the overall direction is positive.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Synthetic prompt quality vs. manually collected prompts (Table 6).** This ablation tests whether SynPO's self-generated prompts are truly better than existing prompt sources, or whether the performance gains come entirely from the response improver. Using Llama3-8B, a single iteration of preference data construction is run with different prompt sources, each producing response pairs via both SynPO's refinement method and via Sampling-Ranking:

- **With SynPO refinement**: Manual collection prompts (UltraFeedback) yield 23.8 LC / 25.6 WR. Self-Instruct prompts yield 21.7 / 21.4. SynPO prompts yield 24.3 / 24.5. SynPO prompts mixed with manual prompts yield 23.4 / 29.4. The key finding: SynPO-generated prompts are competitive with or slightly better than manually collected prompts from UltraFeedback when both use SynPO's refinement, and slightly better than Self-Instruct prompts.
- **With Sampling-Ranking**: Manual collection prompts yield 7.8 LC / 6.2 WR. Self-Instruct yields 9.1 / 4.6. SynPO yields 9.2 / 5.3. SynPO Mix yields 14.2 / 6.9. The absolute performance is far lower than with SynPO refinement (confirming the value of generative rewards), but among prompt sources within the Sampling-Ranking condition, SynPO prompts again perform competitively.

The non-obvious finding is that **mixing SynPO and manual prompts** (SynPO Mix) yields the best results for both refinement and Sampling-Ranking conditions. With SynPO refinement: 29.4 WR vs. 25.6 WR (manual alone). With Sampling-Ranking: 14.2 LC vs. 9.2 LC (SynPO alone). This suggests that SynPO prompts provide *complementary diversity* to existing manually collected prompts rather than merely substituting for them.

**Seed data utilization strategies (Table 7).** This ablation on Llama3-8B tests whether SynPO's design of using seed data only for validation and improver training is optimal:

- **Seed SFT** (directly fine-tuning on the 18k seed examples as SFT data): 20.1 LC / 19.7 WR. This is substantially below SynPO Iter4 (32.1 / 33.6), confirming that the 18k examples alone are insufficient — their value is in guiding synthetic data generation, not in serving as direct training data.
- **Seed PO** (using seed prompts with gold responses as chosen and initial model responses as rejected for preference optimization): 11.6 / 10.7. Worse than pure SFT, suggesting that the preference pairs constructed from seed data alone are too few (18k) and possibly too narrow in domain to train effective preference optimization.
- **Seed SFT + PO** (SFT on seed data, then construct preference pairs from the SFT model's outputs vs. gold responses): 24.6 / 20.9. This is the best non-SynPO variant because it mimics one iteration of SynPO's logic (model output → gold response as chosen/rejected), but it only uses seed data and does not iterate.
- **Seed SFT + PO multi-epoch** (same as above, trained for 2 epochs): 22.4 / 15.0. **Performance degrades** with more training, a negative result indicating that the limited seed-derived preference pairs cannot support extended optimization without overfitting.
- **SynPO Iter4**: 32.1 / 33.6 — substantially above all seed-data-as-training variants.

The key insight: seed data used as training material provides at most a ~24.6 LC gain, while seed data used as a quality compass for synthetic data generation provides a ~32.1 LC gain. The multi-epoch degradation (24.6 → 22.4) further reinforces that small, fixed preference datasets plateau and then degrade with repeated optimization, while SynPO's growing synthetic dataset avoids this.

**Scoring model choice and threshold sensitivity.** The paper does not provide a dedicated ablation varying the filtering threshold (0.20 for PairRM, 0.02 for ArmoRM) or comparing different scoring models systematically. However, the divergent GSM8k results between Mistral (PairRM, degrades) and Llama3 (ArmoRM, improves) serve as an implicit ablation demonstrating that scoring model quality matters for task preservation. The thresholds themselves are stated as being chosen because they "perform the best" among searched values {0, 0.1, 0.2} (Appendix D), but the search results are not reported, making it impossible to assess how sensitive SynPO is to threshold choice.

**Iteration count and diminishing returns.** The full trajectory across all metrics (Tables 2, 4, 5) reveals that SynPO's gains are concentrated in the first 2–3 iterations:
- AlpacaEval 2.0 LC: Iter1→2 provides the largest single-step gain for both models (Mistral +12.4, Llama3 +12.8). Iter3→4 provides the smallest (Mistral +2.3, Llama3 +3.5).
- Arena-Hard: Iter4 *declines* for both models relative to Iter3.
- Open LLM Leaderboard average: Diminishing returns after Iter2 for Llama3 (67.92 → 68.27 → 68.34), slight dip at Iter3 for Mistral.
- MT-Bench: Peaks at Iter3 for Mistral (both turns) and Iter3–4 for Llama3.

Four iterations appear to be near the practical limit. The paper does not experiment with more iterations, so the exact point of negative returns is not precisely characterized.

**Prompt generator diversity vs. other sources (Figure 5).** The inter-prompt similarity analysis was discussed in Section 2.1 but serves as a robustness check for the claim that SynPO prompts are more diverse. The lower similarity distribution for SynPO prompts compared to UltraFeedback, Self-Instruct, and UltraChat supports this claim quantitatively. However, the analysis uses only 1,000 randomly sampled prompts and a single embedding model (Sentence-Transformer). It does not control for prompt length (shorter prompts may have lower cosine similarity artifactually) or for the possibility that keyword-based generation produces prompts that are *superficially* diverse (different surface forms) but *functionally* similar (same deep structure). No human evaluation of prompt diversity is reported.

### Critical Assessment

#### Claim 1: "SynPO achieves over 22.1% win rate improvements on AlpacaEval 2.0 and ArenaHard."

**What the experiments demonstrate:** This claim is numerically accurate and well-supported by Table 2. On AlpacaEval 2.0, Mistral-Base improves from 6.6% LC (SFT) to 34.0% LC (Iter4), a +27.4 percentage point absolute gain. On Arena-Hard, Llama3-Base improves from 2.7% to 32.5% (Iter3 peak), a +29.8 point gain. Both exceed 22.1%. The figure is thus conservative.

**What is not demonstrated:** The claim implies monotonic improvement, but Arena-Hard shows a decline at Iter4 for both models (Mistral: 24.1 → 22.8; Llama3: 32.5 → 31.4). The 22.1% figure cherry-picks the peak iteration for each model. A more precise claim would be "over 22.1% improvement at peak iteration, with slight regression at Iter4." Additionally, AlpacaEval 2.0 and Arena-Hard both use GPT-4 Turbo as judge, which has known biases (preference for longer responses, stylistic preferences). SynPO's response improver may implicitly teach verbose or GPT-4-preferred stylistic patterns, inflating these metrics relative to human preferences — the paper does not report human evaluation.

#### Claim 2: "SynPO improves general performance by 3.2 to 5.0 average score increase on the Open LLM Leaderboard."

**What the experiments demonstrate:** Table 4 supports this: Mistral average improves from 58.20 to 61.39 (+3.19), Llama3 from 63.34 to 68.34 (+5.00). The 3.2–5.0 range is accurate.

**What is not demonstrated:** The "average score" masks substantial heterogeneity. On MMLU, there is zero improvement (Mistral: 58.78 → 58.75; Llama3: 63.80 → 63.79). On GSM8k, Mistral *degrades* by 3.64 points. The claim "improves general performance" should be qualified: SynPO improves ARC, HellaSwag, and TruthfulQA while leaving MMLU and Winogrande flat and potentially degrading math. This is not "general" improvement — it's improvement on specific capability categories (knowledge truthfulness, commonsense reasoning) with no benefit or slight harm on others. The paper's own framing of "alignment tax mitigation" (Section 3.3) is more honest than the abstract's "improves general performance."

#### Claim 3: "SynPO eliminates the need for large-scale annotation of prompts and human preferences."

**What the experiments demonstrate:** The system is trained entirely on synthetic data with only 18k seed examples used for validation. Table 7 shows that this 18k seed data, when used directly for training, underperforms SynPO (24.6 vs. 32.1 LC), confirming the synthetic data does the heavy lifting. The diversity analysis (Figures 4, 5) suggests the synthetic prompts are genuinely diverse.

**What is not demonstrated:** The 18k seed examples are themselves GPT-4 Turbo completions on UltraFeedback prompts — they are **not human-annotated but teacher-model-generated**. The paper has eliminated human annotation for the *preference pairs* but relies on a strong teacher model (GPT-4 Turbo) to generate the seed data's gold-standard responses. This is a substantial qualification: SynPO eliminates the need for large-scale *human preference annotation* but assumes access to a high-quality teacher model for seed data creation. Whether the approach works with genuinely human-written seed responses (without GPT-4-level quality) is untested. The paper's positioning as "self-boosting" is accurate regarding the preference learning loop, but the seed data quality floor is GPT-4 Turbo, not human or weak-model quality.

Additionally, the "unlimited" synthetic data claim (Section 2.3) is qualified in practice: only 10,000 pairs per iteration are used, and the filtering process discards many generated pairs. The actual yield from ~50k synthetic prompts is 10k valid preference pairs — a 20% yield rate. The data is "unlimited" in potential but sharply filtered in practice.

#### Claim 4: "The response improver provides generative rewards that surpass discriminative baselines."

**What the experiments demonstrate:** Tables 2, 4, and 5 consistently show SynPO outperforming Sampling-Ranking and Self-Rewarding, both of which use discriminative signals (scoring model and LLM-as-a-Judge, respectively). The gap is large on AlpacaEval 2.0 (Mistral SynPO Iter4: 34.0 LC vs. Sampling-Ranking Iter4: 11.6 LC vs. Self-Rewarding Iter4: 26.1 LC). This provides strong evidence that *something* about SynPO's approach works better.

**What is not demonstrated:** The paper attributes the gain specifically to the "generative reward" property — the chosen response being a *demonstration* of improvement rather than just a higher-scoring response. However, there are multiple confounding differences between SynPO and the discriminative baselines beyond the generative nature of the signal:
- **SynPO's chosen responses are edited versions of the model's outputs**, while Sampling-Ranking's chosen responses are independently sampled responses that happen to score higher. The edit relationship (chosen is a function of rejected) may matter independently of whether the signal is "generative."
- **The response improver is trained on seed data**, giving it access to GPT-4 Turbo quality targets. Sampling-Ranking has no such access — it only sees the model's own outputs ranked by a scoring model. Self-Rewarding uses LLM-as-a-Judge scores, which are more informative than scalar rewards but still not demonstrations.
- **The response improver may simply produce better chosen responses** (closer to GPT-4 Turbo quality) than what Sampling-Ranking can select from 5 random model samples, regardless of whether those responses are "generative rewards."

A cleaner ablation isolating the generative reward concept would be: train a discriminative reward model on the same improver training data (predicting which of `(model_output, gold_response)` is better), use it to score model outputs on synthetic prompts, and select the best as chosen. This would match the information source (seed data) while changing only the signal format (discriminative score vs. generative demonstration). Without this ablation, the claim that *generativity specifically* drives the improvement is plausible but not experimentally isolated.

#### Claim 5: "Iterative self-improvement with synthetic data is stable and beneficial."

**What the experiments demonstrate:** Performance improves through 3–4 iterations on most metrics (Tables 2, 4, 5). The gains do not collapse. The filtering mechanisms (preference gap threshold, repetition filter, improver training data filter) appear to prevent the mode collapse that can occur in self-training loops.

**What is not demonstrated:** Stability is only shown for 4 iterations. The Arena-Hard decline at Iter4 (both models) and the Mistral GSM8k decline could be early warning signs of instability that would compound at Iter5+. The paper does not report the filtering yield per iteration — does the proportion of generated pairs that pass filtering decline across iterations? If the yield drops from 40% at Iter1 to 10% at Iter4, that would indicate the improver is producing refinements increasingly similar to the original model outputs (shrinking preference gap), which could eventually starve the preference optimization of training signal. No diagnostic metrics are reported for the synthetic data quality across iterations.

#### Missing experiments that would strengthen the paper:

1. **Human evaluation of SynPO-aligned models**: All alignment metrics use GPT-4 Turbo as judge. This is standard practice but introduces the possibility that SynPO overfits to GPT-4's preferences rather than genuine human preferences. A human evaluation on a subset of AlpacaEval or MT-Bench prompts would substantially strengthen the claims.

2. **Direct comparison to GPT-4 Turbo distilled models**: The seed data uses GPT-4 Turbo completions. How much of SynPO's gain comes from distilling GPT-4 Turbo's style and knowledge via the improver? A baseline that directly does SFT on the 18k seed examples + preference optimization would help separate distillation effects from self-improvement effects. The Seed SFT + PO baseline (Table 7, 24.6 LC) provides partial evidence, but a version that expands the 18k seed into a larger SFT dataset (e.g., by generating multiple GPT-4 completions per prompt) would test whether distillation alone could match SynPO.

3. **Varying the seed data quality**: All experiments use GPT-4 Turbo completions as seed gold responses. What happens with weaker seed responses (e.g., from a 7B teacher, or human-written responses of varying quality)? This would test the paper's claim that "seed data quality is vital" (Limitations section) and determine SynPO's dependence on a strong initial quality signal.

4. **Scoring model threshold ablation**: The filtering thresholds (0.20 for PairRM, 0.02 for ArmoRM) are stated as manually chosen based on a search over {0, 0.1, 0.2} (Appendix D), but the full sweep results are not reported. How does performance vary with threshold? Is there a threshold below which SynPO collapses (too much noisy data) or above which SynPO starves (too little data)?

5. **Scaling the seed data size**: All experiments use 18k seed examples. How does performance scale with seed data size — would 9k or 5k seed examples produce comparable gains? This is critical for the paper's claim of needing "only a small amount of high-quality data."

6. **Iteration count beyond 4**: The experiments stop at 4 iterations. Does performance continue to improve, plateau, or decline? The Arena-Hard Iter4 decline suggests continued iteration may not be beneficial, but without Iter5+ data, this is speculation.

7. **Cross-model generalization of the prompt generator and response improver**: The prompt generator is trained on Llama3/Mistral respectively. Would a prompt generator trained on one model work for the other? This tests whether the generator captures model-specific or general instruction patterns.

8. **Direct `β` comparison across methods**: The paper sweeps `β` for each optimization process. The chosen `β` values are not reported, making it impossible to assess whether SynPO's advantage partly comes from more favorable hyperparameter selection.

#### Strengths of the experimental design:

- **Multiple complementary evaluation suites**: The combination of AlpacaEval 2.0 (single-turn, style-sensitive), Arena-Hard (single-turn, challenging), MT-Bench (multi-turn), and downstream task benchmarks provides a more complete picture of model capability than any single metric. The fact that SynPO improves on most (though not all) across both alignment and knowledge dimensions is a robust finding.

- **Fair comparison to iterative baselines**: Both Sampling-Ranking and Self-Rewarding are given the same prompts, same data volume, same number of iterations, and same SimPO objective. The performance gap is therefore attributable to the preference construction mechanism, not to auxiliary advantages.

- **Two distinct base model families**: Testing on both Mistral (7B) and Llama3 (8B) provides evidence that SynPO is not model-specific. The broad similarity in improvement trajectories (both show large AlpacaEval gains, both peak at Iter3 on Arena-Hard) increases confidence in generalizability.

- **Transparent reporting of negative results**: The GSM8k decline for Mistral, the Arena-Hard Iter4 decline, and the MathQA stagnation are reported rather than hidden. The paper's discussion of the scoring model quality as a likely cause for the Mistral GSM8k issue (Section 3.3) is appropriately tentative.

#### Conditional nature of the claims:

The paper's claims hold under the following conditions, which are not emphasized in the abstract but are evident from the experimental design:
- **Seed data must be high-quality** (GPT-4 Turbo level). Performance with lower-quality or smaller seed data is unknown.
- **Improvement is bounded to ~3–4 iterations**, with some metrics declining at Iter4. SynPO is not an unbounded self-improvement process.
- **Gains are largest on alignment-sensitive metrics** (AlpacaEval, Arena-Hard) and **smaller but positive on general capability metrics**. The claim of "improved general performance" is true on average but not uniform across tasks.
- **Math capability is at risk** if the scoring model used for filtering is weak (Mistral + PairRM on GSM8k). The ArmoRM-based filtering for Llama3 largely avoids this, suggesting scoring model quality is a critical dependency.
- **All evaluations use GPT-4 as judge**, so the measured improvements may partially reflect optimization toward GPT-4's preferences rather than human preferences.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### Seed Data Quality Floor Is GPT-4 Turbo — The "Self-Boosting" Depends on a Strong Teacher

**The assumption or constraint.** SynPO is presented as a self-boosting paradigm that "eliminates the need for large-scale annotation of prompts and human preferences" (Section 1). However, the entire pipeline depends on 18k seed SFT examples whose gold-standard responses are GPT-4 Turbo completions on UltraFeedback prompts (Section 3.1). The seed data is not human-written — it is teacher-model-generated, and a very strong teacher at that. Every component that defines "quality" in SynPO traces back to this seed data: the self-prompt generator is trained to produce prompts paired with GPT-4 Turbo responses (Section 2.1), the response improver learns to map model outputs to GPT-4 Turbo responses (Section 2.2), and the filtering thresholds are calibrated against these responses (Section 2.2). The paper acknowledges the dependency implicitly in the Limitations appendix:

> "SynPO leverages a small, high-quality dataset repeatedly to guide synthetic data generation, making the seed data quality vital."

It does not, however, test what happens when seed data quality degrades.

**The consequence.** SynPO is better characterized as a **teacher distillation amplifier** than as genuine self-improvement. The response improver learns to transform the model's outputs into GPT-4 Turbo-quality responses on the seed distribution and then applies this transformation to synthetic prompts. The preference optimization sees chosen responses that are approximations of what GPT-4 Turbo would produce — the model is learning to imitate GPT-4 Turbo's style and quality, not discovering novel improvements from its own reasoning. If the seed responses were written by a weaker model (e.g., a 7B teacher) or by human annotators with inconsistent quality, the entire pipeline would propagate lower-quality targets. The claimed "self-boosting" property is conditional on a strong initial quality signal that the model itself cannot generate. A practitioner with access only to human-written seed data (not GPT-4-level) cannot assume SynPO will produce comparable gains.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides no experiment varying seed data quality. All results use the same GPT-4 Turbo completions. The closest relevant evidence is Table 7 (seed data ablation), which shows that using the seed data directly for training (Seed SFT + PO, 24.6 LC) underperforms SynPO Iter4 (32.1 LC) — confirming the synthetic data adds value beyond the seed data. But this does not test whether the gains persist with lower-quality seed data. The paper also reports that ArmoRM-Llama3-8B-v0.1 (a stronger scoring model) produces better downstream task preservation than PairRM (0.4B) on Llama3 vs. Mistral (Table 4, GSM8k: Llama3 improves +3.79, Mistral degrades -3.64). This asymmetry suggests that SynPO's performance is sensitive to the quality of the models used in its pipeline, making it plausible that seed data quality would similarly affect outcomes.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation. The Limitations appendix notes that "recent work on direct on-policy sampling methods, which do not need additional SFT data, shows considerable promise" (Appendix, Limitations) as a potential future direction, but this is presented as an enhancement to SynPO, not as a way to reduce teacher dependence. No experiment tests SynPO with weaker seed data, smaller seed data, or human-written seed data.

---

### Improvement Saturates at 3–4 Iterations, and Some Metrics Regress — Unbounded Self-Improvement Is Not Achieved

**The assumption or constraint.** SynPO is framed as an iterative self-boosting process where "the response improver continuously refines the generation distribution to align with the ideal data distribution across multiple iterations" (Section 2.3). The implication is that continued iteration yields continued improvement. The paper runs exactly 4 iterations and reports monotonic or near-monotonic gains on most headline metrics (Table 2, Figure 1), but the data itself contradicts the unbounded-improvement narrative at the boundaries.

**The consequence.** On Arena-Hard — the more challenging alignment benchmark — both models **decline at iteration 4**. Mistral-Base drops from 24.1% WR (Iter3) to 22.8% (Iter4). Llama3-Base drops from 32.5% (Iter3) to 31.4% (Iter4). On MT-Bench, Mistral-Base Turn 2 drops from 6.82 (Iter3) to 6.69 (Iter4); Turn 1 drops from 6.86 to 6.73. On the Open LLM Leaderboard, Mistral-Base's average dips at Iter3 (61.25 → 60.70) before partially recovering at Iter4 (61.39). These are not catastrophic collapses, but they indicate that SynPO's benefits are bounded and that the optimal stopping point may be iteration 3 rather than 4 for some metrics.

The practical consequence for a practitioner is that SynPO does **not** provide a "run more iterations, get better results" guarantee. The practitioner must monitor multiple evaluation metrics and stop when performance plateaus or declines, which may occur at different iterations for different metrics. The paper's own practice of selecting the best iteration per method (indicated by asterisks in Tables 3, 4, 5) implicitly acknowledges this — the compute-optimal iteration varies, and running all 4 may produce a model that is worse on some dimensions than the iteration 3 checkpoint.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 2 (Arena-Hard column) directly shows the Iter3→Iter4 decline for both models. Table 3 (MT-Bench) shows Mistral-Base declining on both turns at Iter4. Table 4 (Open LLM Leaderboard) shows the Mistral-Base average dipping at Iter3. The paper does not discuss these declines in the main text or in the Limitations section — they are visible only in the raw numbers. No diagnostic analysis is provided for *why* iteration 4 degrades on these metrics. Possible explanations not explored: the response improver at Iter4, trained on an already-strong model's outputs, may produce refinements that are primarily paraphrases rather than substantive improvements (the paraphrasing limitation acknowledged in Appendix C.2), making Iter4 preference pairs less informative; or the accumulated dataset `D` by Iter4 contains mostly older, larger-gap pairs that dominate optimization, making the newer, smaller-gap pairs from Iter4 contribute negligible additional signal.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not discuss the Iter4 regressions, does not provide a stopping criterion, and does not test whether a 5th iteration would continue the decline or stabilize. The Limitations appendix focuses on other issues (paraphrasing, on-policy methods as future work) and does not mention iteration saturation. A practitioner adopting SynPO has no guidance on when to stop beyond "run 4 iterations and check all metrics."

### Paraphrasing by the Response Improver Is an Acknowledged but Unmeasured Source of Noise

**The assumption or constraint.** The response improver is trained on pairs of `(model_output, gold_response)` from the seed SFT data. These are not minimal-edit pairs — the gold response may be a completely different answer than the model's output, written in a different style, with different structure, different reasoning steps, and different factual emphasis. As the authors explicitly acknowledge:

> "Since the response improver data are automatically derived from SFT data conversion, the model also learns paraphrasing. Using a more powerful model, such as GPT-4, to create data that introduce only minor improvements for rewriter training is a promising research direction. However, to explore the potential for self-boosting, we did not introduce additional data or stronger models for data construction, resulting in inevitable paraphrasing by the response improver." (Appendix C.2)

The word "inevitable" is telling — the authors recognize this as a fundamental consequence of their training data construction but accept it as a tradeoff for self-sufficiency.

**The consequence.** When the response improver produces a "refined" response that is substantially different from the original model output (paraphrased rather than minimally edited), the resulting preference pair `(refined_response, original_response)` compares two responses that may differ in many dimensions simultaneously — style, structure, factual content, level of detail — rather than isolating a specific improvement. The preference optimization sees a "chosen" response that is better overall but cannot attribute the improvement to any particular change. Worse, if the improver's paraphrase introduces new factual errors or hallucinated details not present in the original model output, the chosen response could be *worse* on some dimensions while being *better* on others, creating a noisy and potentially contradictory training signal.

This may partially explain the GSM8k decline for Mistral-Base (Table 4): if the improver paraphrases a model's math solution into a different format that happens to look more like GPT-4 Turbo's style but contains different (possibly incorrect) mathematical reasoning, the preference pair teaches stylistic preferences at the expense of mathematical correctness. It may also explain the Arena-Hard Iter4 decline: as the model improves, the improver has less room to make genuine quality improvements and increasingly resorts to stylistic paraphrasing, producing preference pairs that optimize for style rather than substance.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides no direct measurement of paraphrasing frequency or severity. No experiment compares the improver's outputs against the original model outputs for semantic preservation, factual consistency, or edit distance. The claim of "inevitable paraphrasing" is an author observation, not a quantified metric. The GSM8k asymmetry between Mistral (degrades) and Llama3 (improves) — attributed to scoring model quality differences — could alternatively be partially explained by differential paraphrasing behavior: the ArmoRM-based pipeline for Llama3 may filter out paraphrased-but-incorrect math responses more effectively than PairRM does for Mistral, but this is speculative without direct measurement.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges the issue in Appendix C.2 but does not attempt to measure or mitigate it. The suggestion to use "a more powerful model, such as GPT-4" to generate minimal-edit improvement data is presented as future work, but this would reintroduce the teacher model dependency that SynPO is designed to eliminate — a fundamental tension the paper does not resolve. The filtering mechanisms (scoring model threshold, repetition filter) may partially mitigate paraphrasing by removing cases where the improver's output is not clearly better than the original, but they do not distinguish between genuine improvement and stylistic rewriting that happens to score higher.

### Cost of Difficulty Estimation for Seed Data Filtering and Improver Training Is Not Accounted For

**The assumption or constraint.** SynPO is presented as an efficient self-improvement method that "requires only a small amount of high-quality data for validation" (Section 2.3) and "eliminates the need for large-scale annotation" (Section 1). However, the per-iteration computational cost includes several expensive operations that are not amortized in any efficiency analysis: (1) training the response improver from scratch each iteration (SFT on up to 18k examples, 3 epochs), (2) generating model responses to all 18k seed prompts for improver training data construction, (3) applying the scoring model to all 18k seed `(model_output, gold_response)` pairs to filter improver training data, (4) generating 50k synthetic prompts from the prompt generator, (5) generating model responses to all 50k synthetic prompts, (6) applying the response improver to refine all 50k responses, (7) applying the scoring model to all 50k `(chosen, rejected)` response pairs for filtering, and (8) preference optimization on the accumulated dataset (up to 40k pairs by Iter4). None of these costs are reported in FLOPs, GPU-hours, or wall-clock time.

**The consequence.** A practitioner evaluating whether to adopt SynPO cannot assess the total computational cost relative to alternatives. The headline comparison — SynPO vs. baselines at the same number of iterations and same data volume (Section 3.1) — controls for training data size but not for total compute. Sampling-Ranking (the discriminative baseline) also generates responses to 50k prompts per iteration but does **not** require: training a response improver, generating improver refinements for 50k prompts, filtering improver training data, or generating model responses to the 18k seed set. Sampling-Ranking samples 5 responses per prompt from the policy model (more generation than SynPO's 2 responses per prompt for the synthetic set) but SynPO's additional training and filtering costs could outweigh this difference. The paper provides no information to make this comparison.

Furthermore, the cost scales poorly with iteration count: each iteration requires retraining the response improver (fixed cost per iteration proportional to seed data size), and the growing preference dataset `D` makes each iteration's preference optimization more expensive than the last (Iter4 trains on ~4× the data of Iter1). If the benefits saturate at Iter3–4 (as discussed above), the marginal cost of Iter4 may not justify its marginal benefit — but without cost estimates, this tradeoff is invisible.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides no compute cost analysis whatsoever. The hyperparameters in Appendix C specify training details (batch sizes, learning rates, sequence lengths, epoch counts) that could in principle be used to estimate FLOPs, but no such estimate is reported. The generation volumes are stated (50k synthetic prompts per iteration, 18k seed prompts for improver training), but generation cost is not translated into a common unit (e.g., GPU-hours). No wall-clock time is reported for any experiment.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not mention computational cost as a limitation, does not report resource usage, and does not compare total FLOPs or GPU-hours against baselines. This is a significant omission for a method positioned as practical and scalable. The fact that SynPO uses 7B–8B models suggests the authors consider computational feasibility important (they did not use larger models), but they do not quantify the feasibility tradeoffs.

### All Evaluations Use LLM-as-a-Judge — Optimization Toward GPT-4's Preferences Cannot Be Disentangled from Genuine Improvement

**The assumption or constraint.** Every alignment evaluation in the paper uses GPT-4 Turbo as the judge (Section 3.2, Appendix E): AlpacaEval 2.0 compares model outputs against GPT-4 Turbo baseline judged by GPT-4 Turbo; Arena-Hard compares against GPT-4-0314 judged by GPT-4 Turbo; MT-Bench uses GPT-4 Turbo for single-answer grading on a 1–10 scale. The seed data's gold-standard responses are also GPT-4 Turbo completions (Section 3.1). This creates a closed loop: the model is trained to imitate GPT-4 Turbo's response style (via the improver trained on GPT-4 Turbo completions), and then evaluated by GPT-4 Turbo on how well it matches GPT-4 Turbo's preferences.

**The consequence.** There is a substantial risk that SynPO's measured improvements partially reflect **overfitting to GPT-4 Turbo's judging biases** rather than genuine improvement in human-preferred response quality. GPT-4 Turbo as a judge has known biases: preference for longer, more detailed responses (which the length-controlled metric on AlpacaEval 2.0 partially but incompletely addresses), preference for certain stylistic patterns (e.g., structured formatting, enumerated lists, confident tone), and potential self-preference (preferring responses that resemble its own output style). The response improver, trained on GPT-4 Turbo completions, is likely to teach these stylistic patterns — indeed, it would be surprising if it did not, since that is precisely the distribution it learns to map toward. The resulting SynPO-aligned model may score highly on GPT-4-judged benchmarks without being correspondingly better according to human judges.

This limitation is particularly acute because the entire pipeline — seed data generation, improver training, and evaluation — involves three separate roles for GPT-4 Turbo (gold response writer, improvement target, and judge). Any systematic biases in GPT-4 Turbo's response style or judging behavior are amplified through this triple dependency. A model that learns to produce responses that GPT-4 Turbo likes to read may not produce responses that humans prefer to read.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides no human evaluation. All alignment results (Tables 1, 2, 3; Figures 1, 6) are GPT-4 Turbo judgments. The downstream task evaluations (Tables 4, 5) provide an independent signal — they measure accuracy on benchmarks with ground-truth answers rather than LLM judgments — and SynPO does show improvement on these (+3.2 to +5.0 average on Open LLM Leaderboard). This partially mitigates the concern: SynPO's benefits are not *purely* an artifact of GPT-4 evaluation, since the model also improves on objective accuracy metrics. However, the gains on alignment benchmarks (e.g., +27.4 LC on AlpacaEval 2.0) are dramatically larger than the gains on knowledge benchmarks (+3.2 average on Open LLM Leaderboard), suggesting a substantial fraction of the alignment improvement may be style-related and potentially GPT-4-preference-specific.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation. No human evaluation is reported or proposed as future work. The use of GPT-4 as judge is standard practice in the alignment literature (the paper cites established benchmarks and follows their protocols), but the additional dependency — SynPO *trains on* GPT-4 outputs and *evaluates with* GPT-4 judgments — makes the circularity concern more salient here than for methods trained on human-annotated data. The downstream task improvements provide a partial mitigation by demonstrating objective gains, but the alignment-specific claims rest entirely on LLM judgments.

### Prompt Diversity Advantage Over Existing Sources Is Claimed but Not Structurally Verified

**The assumption or constraint.** A central claim of the paper is that SynPO's keyword-based self-prompt generator produces prompts with "greater diversity" than manually collected or teacher-generated prompt sets, and that this diversity is a key driver of alignment performance (Section 2.1, Section 4.2, Section 5, Related Work). The evidence for this claim rests on: (1) an inter-prompt similarity distribution computed from Sentence-Transformer embeddings of 1,000 prompts from each source (Figure 5), and (2) a GPT-4 Turbo topic/intention classification of SynPO prompts showing coverage across many categories (Figure 4).

**The consequence.** The inter-prompt similarity metric used in Figure 5 measures **semantic similarity** — how close prompts are to each other in a sentence embedding space. This captures topical and surface-form diversity but does not measure **functional diversity** — whether prompts exercise different model capabilities, require different reasoning types, or probe different failure modes. Two prompts that appear semantically distinct ("Explain quantum entanglement to a 5-year-old" and "Write a Python function to sort a list") may both be easy for the model and thus provide redundant training signal, while two semantically similar prompts ("Prove the Pythagorean theorem" and "Derive the Pythagorean theorem from first principles") may exercise different capabilities (proof generation vs. mathematical derivation).

A more fundamental concern: if the keyword sampling from RefinedWeb produces prompts that are topically diverse but structurally homogeneous (e.g., predominantly "explain X" or "write about Y" formats), the preference optimization may see variety in content but not in the *kind* of instruction-following challenge, limiting the model's ability to generalize to structurally novel prompts at test time. The topic/intention classification in Figure 4 shows diversity in content domains (Technology, Science, Food, etc.) and high-level intentions (Learn, Analyze, Create, Write), but these are coarse categories that may mask structural homogeneity within each category.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figure 5 shows SynPO prompts have lower average inter-prompt similarity (distribution peak near 0.0–0.05) compared to UltraFeedback (peak near 0.08–0.10), Self-Instruct (peak near 0.06–0.08), and UltraChat (peak near 0.12–0.14). This is a meaningful difference but is measured on only 1,000 prompts per source with a single embedding model (Sentence-Transformer). No analysis is provided of the *structural* diversity of prompts (e.g., distribution of prompt lengths, syntactic complexity, presence of constraints, multi-hop vs. single-hop reasoning requirements). Table 6 provides indirect functional evidence: SynPO prompts perform competitively with manually collected prompts when both use the same refinement method (24.3 LC vs. 23.8 LC on AlpacaEval 2.0), and mixing SynPO with manual prompts yields the best results (29.4 WR for SynPO Mix with SynPO refinement method). This suggests SynPO prompts are at least not worse than manual prompts and may provide complementary coverage, but it does not isolate *whether* their diversity advantage (as measured in Figure 5) is the causal factor.

**Mitigation status.** Partially addressed by Table 6, which shows competitive performance of SynPO prompts against manual prompts in a controlled comparison. But the paper does not provide a direct test of the diversity-claims-performance link — for example, by comparing SynPO prompts against a version where keyword diversity is artificially reduced (e.g., sampling keywords only from a narrow domain), or by measuring whether models trained on more diverse prompts (by the Figure 5 metric) generalize better to out-of-distribution prompts. The topic/intention analysis (Figure 4) provides qualitative evidence of coverage but no quantitative comparison to the coverage of UltraFeedback or other prompt sets, making the "greater diversity" claim partly a matter of embedding geometry rather than demonstrated functional benefit.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

SynPO does not introduce a new model architecture or a novel optimization algorithm — its core components (SimPO, supervised fine-tuning, keyword-to-text generation) are all established techniques. What it changes is the **framing of what constitutes a preference signal for alignment**. The paper's central reframing — that a *demonstration of improvement* (generative reward) can substitute for and outperform a *judgment of quality* (discriminative reward) — opens a different axis for alignment research that has been largely overlooked.

The field's dominant paradigm for preference data construction has been **discriminative**: humans or teacher models compare two responses and declare a winner. This produces datasets like UltraFeedback (Cui et al., 2023), Anthropic's HH-RLHF (Bai et al., 2022a), and the on-policy sampling-and-ranking approaches of SimPO (Meng et al., 2024) and SPPO (Wu et al., 2024b). The underlying assumption is that alignment requires *evaluative* feedback — a signal that tells the model which of its outputs are better. SynPO demonstrates that **constructive** feedback — a signal that shows the model how to transform a worse output into a better one — can be more effective, at least when the transformation is learned from a strong quality target (GPT-4 Turbo completions in this case).

This reframing is not merely philosophical. The empirical gap between SynPO and the discriminative baselines is large: on AlpacaEval 2.0, SynPO Iter4 achieves 34.0 LC for Mistral-Base vs. 11.6 LC for Sampling-Ranking and 26.1 LC for Self-Rewarding at their respective best iterations (Table 2). That is a 3× advantage over the strongest discriminative baseline. The mechanism is not that SynPO has access to more information — Sampling-Ranking also uses a scoring model (PairRM/ArmoRM) trained on preference data — but rather that SynPO's information is in a format the preference optimization can exploit more efficiently. A preference pair where the chosen response is a *functional refinement* of the rejected response on the exact same prompt provides a more direct learning signal than a pair where the chosen response is an independently sampled output that happens to score higher.

This finding redirects attention from a research trajectory that has been largely focused on building better discriminative reward models — larger models, better training data, multi-objective scoring, ensemble methods — toward a complementary trajectory of building better **improver models** that can generate corrective demonstrations. The intellectual question shifts from "how do we accurately score response quality?" to "how do we teach models to recognize and correct their own specific error patterns?" The latter question is more aligned with how humans actually improve at complex tasks: we learn more from a teacher showing us *how* to fix our mistakes than from being told *that* we made one.

**What SynPO resolves from prior contradictory findings.** The self-improvement literature contains a tension: some works find that LLMs can self-correct or self-improve through iterative refinement (Madaan et al., 2023; Self-Refine), while others find that on-policy self-training can cause performance degradation or collapse (Yin et al., 2024). SynPO provides a partial resolution: self-improvement works when **(a)** the improvement signal is anchored to a fixed quality target (the seed data), **(b)** the improvement signal is generative (showing *how* to improve) rather than discriminative (showing *that* improvement is needed), and **(c)** the data is aggressively filtered to retain only clear improvement cases. The failure of Sampling-Ranking (which violates conditions b and c) and the partial success of Self-Rewarding (which satisfies b imperfectly through LLM-as-a-Judge feedback but not a) provide empirical support for this resolution. The implication is that negative results in self-improvement research may reflect insufficient improvement signal quality rather than a fundamental impossibility of self-training.

**What research directions become more attractive.** The paper makes **improver model training** a first-class research problem. Questions that were previously marginal — how to construct minimal-edit improvement pairs, how to prevent improvers from paraphrasing, how to calibrate improvers to make targeted corrections rather than stylistic rewrites — become central. The paper also makes **seed data design** more prominent: if seed data serves as a quality compass rather than training fuel (as SynPO's design and the Table 7 ablation suggest), then the properties of good seed data (size, quality, diversity, domain coverage) need to be characterized differently than for data intended for direct training.

**What research directions become less attractive in light of SynPO.** The paper weakens the case for purely discriminative on-policy sampling methods (Sampling-Ranking, SPPO-style approaches) as a path to substantial alignment gains. The performance gap — SynPO Iter4 at 34.0 LC vs. Sampling-Ranking Iter4 at 11.6 LC for Mistral-Base on AlpacaEval 2.0 — is large enough that marginal improvements to the sampling or ranking procedure are unlikely to close it. The limitation is not the ranking algorithm but the signal format: selecting the best from N random samples provides no information about *how to produce better samples*, only about *which of the current samples is least bad*. SynPO's response improver addresses the generative gap directly. Research effort may be better spent on improvement generation than on ever-more-sophisticated ranking of static outputs.

However, the paper does **not** render discriminative methods obsolete. SynPO depends on a strong quality target (GPT-4 Turbo) for improver training; in settings where no such target exists, discriminative methods remain the only option. And the seed data filtering itself uses a discriminative scoring model (PairRM/ArmoRM), so discriminative signals are still part of the pipeline — they have been relegated from the primary training signal to a quality-control gate.

**Summary of the landscape change.** SynPO demonstrates that **generative rewards** — improvement demonstrations rather than quality judgments — are a viable and potentially superior category of preference signal for LLM alignment, with a 3× advantage over discriminative methods on the most widely-used alignment benchmark. This reframes alignment from a pure evaluation problem to a correction-demonstration problem and opens a new research axis focused on improver model design, seed data as compass, and the properties of constructive feedback in language model training.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**1. Disentangling generative reward from teacher distillation — what happens when the seed data is not GPT-4 Turbo?** SynPO's seed data consists of GPT-4 Turbo completions on UltraFeedback prompts. The response improver learns to map model outputs to these GPT-4 Turbo-quality responses, and the preference optimization sees chosen responses that approximate GPT-4 Turbo's output distribution. The measured gains may therefore reflect *distillation* of GPT-4 Turbo's style and knowledge into the 7B–8B policy model, amplified by the synthetic prompt diversity, rather than genuine self-improvement from the model's own reasoning. A critical follow-up experiment would replace the GPT-4 Turbo seed completions with **(a)** human-written responses of comparable quality, **(b)** responses from a weaker teacher (e.g., Llama3-70B-Instruct), and **(c)** responses generated by the base model itself with a simpler quality filter (e.g., length-filtered or rule-based selected outputs). If SynPO's gains collapse under condition (b) or (c), the method should be recharacterized as a teacher distillation amplifier rather than self-improvement. If gains persist with human-written seed data (condition a), the generative reward claim is strengthened. This experiment would also inform the practical question: can practitioners use their own human-written datasets as seed data, or is GPT-4-level quality a hard requirement?

**2. Measuring and mitigating paraphrasing in improver outputs.** The paper acknowledges that the response improver "inevitably" learns paraphrasing because its training pairs are `(model_output, gold_response)` rather than minimal-edit pairs (Appendix C.2). Paraphrasing introduces noise: the improver may produce a chosen response that differs from the original in content, structure, style, and factual details simultaneously, making the preference signal multi-dimensional and potentially contradictory. A direct follow-up would measure paraphrasing severity by computing **(a)** semantic similarity (e.g., BERTScore) between the improver's output and the original model output, compared to the similarity between the improver's output and the gold seed response, and **(b)** factual consistency (e.g., using NLI models) to check whether the improver introduces, removes, or alters factual claims. If paraphrasing is severe (e.g., improver outputs are more similar to gold responses than to model outputs), then SynPO's advantage may come from effectively performing SFT toward GPT-4 Turbo's distribution on synthetic prompts, rather than from preference learning. A mitigation experiment would train an improver on minimal-edit pairs — where the gold response is edited to be as close as possible to the model output while correcting only specific errors — and compare performance. This could be done by prompting GPT-4 to "minimally edit" the model's output to match the gold response's correctness without changing its style or structure. The tradeoff (teacher dependence vs. paraphrasing reduction) would be quantified directly.

**3. Dynamic difficulty estimation and adaptive filtering thresholds across iterations.** SynPO uses fixed filtering thresholds (PairRM score gap > 0.20, ArmoRM score gap > 0.02) across all four iterations. However, as the policy model improves, the gap between its outputs and the improver's refinements likely shrinks — the model makes smaller, subtler errors that require smaller corrections. A fixed threshold at Iter4 may retain only pairs where the improver made large changes (which may be primarily stylistic rewrites rather than genuine improvements), while discarding pairs with small but substantively meaningful improvements that fall below the threshold. A dynamic threshold that adapts to the distribution of score gaps at each iteration — for example, retaining the top K% of pairs by score gap, or setting the threshold at the median gap — could improve later-iteration performance and potentially extend the number of beneficial iterations beyond 3–4. A concrete experiment would sweep threshold percentiles per iteration and measure whether the Iter4 decline on Arena-Hard (Table 2) can be reversed. This connects to the broader question of **exploration-exploitation in self-improvement**: early iterations need large-gap pairs to learn basic quality improvements; later iterations may need smaller-gap pairs to learn nuanced refinements.

**4. Combining SynPO with on-policy discriminative methods for harder benchmarks.** SynPO underperforms manual collection (UltraFeedback) on GSM8k for Mistral-Base (31.08 vs. 32.83 at Iter4, Table 4) and shows only modest gains on math tasks generally. This suggests that the response improver, trained on general instruction-following seed data, does not effectively capture mathematical correctness improvements — a GPT-4 Turbo completion on an UltraFeedback prompt may be factually accurate but is not a targeted math improvement demonstration. A natural extension is to train **domain-specific response improvers** on seed data from the target domain. For math, construct seed data from GSM8k or MATH training sets, where the gold response is a correct step-by-step solution and the model output may contain reasoning errors. Train a math-specific improver, then apply it to synthetic math prompts generated by the prompt generator (which may already produce math-related prompts, as Figure 4 shows Math and STEM categories). A strong result would be improved or at least preserved GSM8k and MathQA performance while maintaining alignment gains. A negative result — if domain-specific improvers fail to improve domain performance — would suggest the generative reward mechanism is fundamentally limited to stylistic/fluency improvements and cannot teach reasoning.

**5. Minimal seed data scaling — how few examples are needed for the quality compass to work?** The paper uses 18k seed examples and justifies this as "a small set" but provides no scaling analysis. A critical practical question: can SynPO work with 5k, 1k, or even 100 seed examples? The response improver is trained on at most 18k filtered pairs per iteration (some seed examples are excluded because the model output already matches or exceeds the gold response); this is a small SFT dataset by modern standards. The self-prompt generator is also trained on 18k keyword-to-prompt examples. A systematic sweep of seed data sizes — maintaining the same data composition (UltraFeedback prompts with GPT-4 Turbo completions) but randomly subsampling to different sizes — would reveal the data efficiency frontier. If SynPO performs well with 1k seed examples, the claim of needing "only a small amount of high-quality data" is substantially strengthened. If performance degrades sharply below 10k, the method's applicability to settings with very limited high-quality data is constrained. This experiment should also measure the **filtering yield** at each iteration as a function of seed data size: with fewer seed examples, the improver is trained on a narrower distribution and may produce lower-quality refinements, reducing the proportion of generated pairs that pass filtering, creating a compounding effect across iterations.

**6. Characterizing the failure mode of the Iter4 regression — is it overfitting, signal decay, or optimization instability?** The paper shows that Arena-Hard WR declines at Iter4 for both Mistral (24.1 → 22.8) and Llama3 (32.5 → 31.4) (Table 2), and MT-Bench scores decline for Mistral at Iter4 (Table 3). The paper offers no diagnosis. A systematic follow-up would measure at each iteration: **(a)** the average score gap between chosen and rejected responses in the preference dataset (is the training signal weakening?), **(b)** the semantic similarity between improver outputs and model outputs (is paraphrasing increasing?), **(c)** the proportion of generated pairs that pass filtering (is the yield declining?), **(d)** the SimPO loss and reward margin on a held-out validation set (is the optimization overfitting?), and **(e)** the policy model's KL divergence from `π_{θ_0}` (is the model drifting too far from its initialization?). If the diagnosis is signal decay (shrinking score gaps, increasing similarity, declining yield), the fix is dynamic thresholding or stopping at Iter3. If the diagnosis is overfitting to the accumulated dataset, the fix is early stopping within each iteration's optimization or reducing the accumulation (e.g., training only on the most recent iteration's pairs rather than all historical pairs). If the diagnosis is optimization instability from SimPO's reference-free objective, the fix is switching to a reference-based method like DPO for later iterations. This diagnostic work would convert the observed regression from an unexplained artifact into a characterized limitation with a known mitigation.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**1. Cost-efficient alignment for mid-size open-source models (7B–13B) without human annotation teams.** The most immediate practical application is enabling teams that have pretrained or fine-tuned a 7B–13B base model to perform preference alignment without hiring human annotators or paying for large-scale GPT-4 API calls. The paper's setup uses 18k GPT-4 Turbo completions as seed data — which incurs a one-time API cost — but after that, all training data is synthetic. A team could substitute their own high-quality seed data (e.g., curated examples from their application domain) and run SynPO for 3 iterations. At Iter3, AlpacaEval 2.0 LC win rate reaches 31.7% (Mistral) and 28.6% (Llama3) — competitive with or exceeding the UltraFeedback-trained baselines at 21.5% and 22.0% (Table 2) — while also improving downstream task performance by 3–5 points on average (Table 4). The cost avoidance is substantial: annotating 60k preference pairs (the scale of UltraFeedback) with human judges would cost tens of thousands of dollars; SynPO achieves better results with a fraction of the annotation budget spent only on the seed data.

**2. Iterative model improvement in production where user prompts are the diversity source.** A company deploying an LLM-powered product accumulates real user prompts over time. These prompts represent the actual distribution the model needs to handle but are unlabeled — there are no gold-standard responses or preference judgments. SynPO's architecture can be adapted: use a small amount of human-annotated or teacher-model-generated seed data as the quality compass, but replace the keyword-based synthetic prompt generator with the company's own user prompt logs (filtered for quality and privacy). The rest of the pipeline — improver training, response refinement, filtering, preference optimization — operates identically. At each iteration, the model generates responses to real user prompts, the improver refines them, and the pairs become preference training data. This creates a **production flywheel**: the model improves on the exact distribution it serves, using real prompts for diversity and seed data for quality direction. The paper's finding that SynPO's self-generated prompts are competitive with manually collected prompts (Table 6: 24.3 LC vs. 23.8 LC) suggests that real user prompts — which are naturally diverse — would work at least as well. The key practical requirement is the scoring model for filtering (PairRM or ArmoRM), which is a one-time deployment cost.

**3. Bootstrapping alignment for new languages or domains where preference data does not exist.** For low-resource languages or specialized domains (legal, medical, scientific), human-annotated preference data is often nonexistent, and even teacher-model annotation may be unreliable if GPT-4 underperforms in that domain. SynPO requires only a small amount of *high-quality completions* in the target domain/language — not preference judgments, which are harder to obtain. A domain expert can write 5k–10k gold-standard responses to representative prompts. The self-prompt generator can be trained to generate diverse in-domain prompts from domain-specific keyword lists (e.g., medical terms for healthcare, legal terms for law). The response improver learns to map model outputs to expert-written responses, and the resulting synthetic preference pairs teach the model to improve toward expert quality. The paper's results on TruthfulQA (+15.75 for Mistral, +18.32 for Llama3; Table 4) suggest that knowledge-intensive domains benefit particularly from SynPO's generative reward signal — these gains are among the largest in the paper and indicate that the improver effectively teaches factual accuracy and nuanced truthfulness. For a medical QA system, this could translate to a 7B model that approaches the quality of a much larger model on domain-specific queries, without requiring the larger model at inference time.

**4. Alignment tax mitigation as part of standard LLM training pipelines.** The paper provides evidence that SynPO partially mitigates the alignment tax: general capabilities improve by 3.2–5.0 average points on the Open LLM Leaderboard (Table 4) while alignment metrics improve dramatically. This is not universal — GSM8k degrades for Mistral-Base — but the overall trajectory is positive. A practical integration would be to run SynPO as a post-SFT alignment stage in the standard LLM training pipeline, replacing or augmenting the usual RLHF/DPO step. The downstream task improvements on ARC (+5.55 for both models), HellaSwag (+2.15), and TruthfulQA (+15–18) suggest that the synthetic preference data generated by SynPO is more **capability-preserving** than standard human preference data, possibly because the improver's refinements correct errors (improving capability) rather than only adjusting style (which can cause capability regression). A training pipeline that runs SFT → SynPO (3 iterations) → optional final RLHF on a small human-annotated dataset would front-load the capability-preserving alignment and use the expensive human data only for final calibration, potentially reducing total human annotation needs by 80–90% while matching or exceeding the performance of a fully human-annotated alignment pipeline.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper does not provide an explicit decision framework comparing SynPO against named alternatives under specific conditions. It compares against baselines (Manual Collection, Sampling-Ranking, Self-Rewarding) in a fixed experimental setup and demonstrates superiority, but it does not characterize *when* a practitioner should choose SynPO over those alternatives, nor does it identify conditions under which alternatives would be preferable. The seed data quality dependence, the Iter4 regression, the GPT-4 evaluation loop, and the paraphrasing issue are acknowledged as limitations but not operationalized into a decision rule. A forced "prefer A when X, prefer B when Y" matrix would be speculative extrapolation rather than a reflection of claims the paper itself makes and supports.
