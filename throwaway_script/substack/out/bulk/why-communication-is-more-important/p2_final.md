Here are the takeaways from this Ryan Peterman essay (a standalone argument that engineers should invest more in communication than code):

**Communication as higher-leverage engineering work**

Ryan opens with the observation that junior engineers tend to disproportionately invest in technical skills. The core of his argument is a re-framing of leverage. Programming is already high leverage because code can be written once and deployed everywhere at near-zero replication cost. But communication, he argues, is "even higher leverage since you can influence other engineers to ship what matters." That influence multiplier—steering *what* gets built, not just *how* it's built—is, in his framework, "strictly higher leverage than writing the code yourself." It's a claim about the *return on unit of effort*: an hour spent convincing three engineers to prioritize the right thing compounds more than an hour spent alone in an editor.

**The visibility puzzle and the promotion calculus**

He extends the leverage argument into the realm of career progression. Impactful code is only one piece of the puzzle for promotion. The actual decision happens in a committee room your manager has to walk into and make a case. "If your work is visible, this process will be much smoother since your manager doesn't have to explain as much." Excellent writing and speaking, in this view, aren't soft skills layered on top of technical work—they are the mechanism by which technical work becomes *legible* to the people who control your trajectory. The unspoken corollary: invisible impact is discounted impact.

**AI makes code abundant; communication stays scarce**

The third leg of the argument is forward-looking. Ryan points to tools like GitHub's Copilot producing useful code, and notes the improvement curve is steeper than expected: "the technology is improving much faster than expected." If the trend holds, "programming well will no longer be a scarce skill." His forecast is that communication will resist AI commoditization far longer because it is "harder to replace in-person conversation." In a world where code "flows like water," the differentiator becomes the ability to speak effectively in meetings, to write persuasively in design docs, to align teams around a direction—the things that happen *between* the lines of a diff.

**Two concrete starting points: Toastmasters and writing in public**

He closes with two actionable prescriptions drawn from his own practice. The first is visiting a local Toastmasters club—an international public-speaking organization he has participated in since college, which he credits with helping him "better articulate myself during meetings." The second is writing more and actively soliciting feedback on that writing, which is, he notes, "one of the top reasons I write this Substack." (He adds he can already tell his writing has improved significantly since the first few articles.) Both suggestions share the same underlying mechanic: deliberate practice in a low-stakes, feedback-rich environment, so that the skill is already built when the high-stakes moment—a promo pitch, an architectural debate—arrives.

---

Ryan's core insight is that **communication is strictly higher leverage than writing the code yourself**—a framework that reframes persuasion not as a soft skill but as a force multiplier on technical effort. That stance threads through every section: from the promotion committee's need for legible impact to the AI-era prediction that speaking and writing will outlast raw coding as scarce skills. His two prescriptions—Toastmasters and public writing—are both engineered to deliberately practice that multiplier long before you need it, turning the abstract principle into a concrete career hedge.
