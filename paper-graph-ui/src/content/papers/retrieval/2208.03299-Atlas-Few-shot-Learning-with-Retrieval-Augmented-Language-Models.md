# Atlas: Few-shot Learning with Retrieval Augmented Language Models

**ArXiv:** [2208.03299](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.03299)

## 🎯 Pitch

This paper introduces **Atlas**, a retrieval-augmented language model that jointly pre-trains a dense retriever and a sequence-to-sequence language model, demonstrating that strong few-shot learning on knowledge-intensive tasks can be achieved without storing vast amounts of knowledge in model parameters.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **Atlas**, a retrieval-augmented language model that jointly pre-trains a dense retriever and a sequence-to-sequence language model, demonstrating that strong few-shot learning on knowledge-intensive tasks can be achieved without storing vast amounts of knowledge in model parameters. Evaluated across a wide range of benchmarks—including NaturalQuestions, TriviaQA, FEVER, 8 KILT tasks, and the 57-domain MMLU suite—Atlas uses a dual-encoder retriever (Contriever) to fetch relevant documents from an external corpus and a Fusion-in-Decoder architecture to condition generation on those documents, with the retriever trained via **Perplexity Distillation** (a loss that trains the retriever to predict how much each document improves the language model's output perplexity). Atlas-11B reaches over 42% accuracy on NaturalQuestions using only 64 training examples, outperforming PaLM's 540B model by 3 percentage points despite having roughly 50× fewer parameters, and establishes new state-of-the-art results on full-dataset NaturalQuestions (64.0%), TriviaQA (89.4% unﬁltered), FEVER (80.1%), and 5 KILT tasks, establishing that retrieval augmentation can substitute for massive parameter counts in few-shot learning, though only when the task-relevant knowledge is present in the retrieval corpus and the model learns to effectively integrate retrieved evidence during pre-training.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Can We Decouple Knowledge Storage from Few-Shot Generalization?

The fundamental question Atlas tackles is whether few-shot learning inherently requires models to internalize massive amounts of world knowledge within their parameters. By 2022, large language models like GPT-3 (Brown et al., 2020), Gopher (Rae et al., 2021), and PaLM (Chowdhery et al., 2022) had demonstrated remarkable few-shot abilities—learning new tasks from just a handful of examples or even natural language instructions alone. However, the recipe for achieving this generalization ability had two essential ingredients: scaling up model parameters (to hundreds of billions) and scaling up training data (to hundreds of billions or trillions of tokens). 

This creates a conceptual entanglement. Larger models have both **greater reasoning capacity** (more parameters enable more complex computations) and **greater memorization capacity** (more parameters enable storing more factual knowledge from the training data). When a 540B-parameter model like PaLM performs well on a few-shot question answering task, it's unclear whether this success comes from superior reasoning about the few provided examples, or from having memorized the answer during pretraining. The paper articulates this tension directly:

> "While it is intuitive to assume that increased reasoning abilities lead to better generalisation, and hence few-shot learning, the same is not true for in-parameter memorisation. Specifically, it is unclear to what extent effective few-shot learning requires vast knowledge in the parameters of the model."

This isn't merely an academic distinction—it has profound practical implications. If few-shot learning *does* depend primarily on memorization, then the path to better performance is simply training ever-larger models on ever-larger datasets, with the attendant computational, financial, and environmental costs. If memorization can instead be outsourced to an external knowledge store, then smaller models might achieve comparable few-shot performance at a fraction of the training cost.

### Why This Gap Matters: Practical and Theoretical Stakes

The paper identifies four concrete areas where decoupling memory from generalization would be transformative (Section 1, though the motivation is implicit throughout):

**1. Computational efficiency and accessibility.** Training GPT-3 required an estimated 3.1 × 10²³ FLOPs; PaLM required substantially more. These models are trained by well-resourced industrial labs and are inaccessible to most researchers. If a smaller model augmented with retrieval could match their few-shot performance, it would democratize access to state-of-the-art NLP capabilities. Atlas-11B's pre-training compute is dominated by T5's pre-training (roughly 3.5 × 10²² FLOPs), and its retrieval-augmented pre-training adds orders of magnitude less—yet it competes with models trained on 10× more compute.

**2. Knowledge updateability.** Parametric models bake knowledge into their weights at training time. When facts change (e.g., who holds a political office, which team an athlete plays for), the model becomes stale and requires expensive retraining or fine-tuning. Retrieval-augmented models can swap their document index at test time without any parameter updates, enabling zero-shot adaptation to new information. The paper demonstrates this vividly with the TempLAMA experiment (Table 11), where Atlas's accuracy shifts from answering based on 2017 facts to answering based on 2020 facts simply by changing the index, while a static parametric model remains frozen in time.

**3. Interpretability.** When a parametric model answers "Coldplay" to "who is playing the halftime show at super bowl 2016," there's no way to inspect *why*—the reasoning is distributed across billions of weights. Retrieval-augmented models surface the documents they used, enabling human inspection of evidence. This matters for trust, debugging, and applications in high-stakes domains (medicine, law, finance) where decisions must be explainable.

**4. The training-inference compute tradeoff.** The paper operates in a regime where one must decide how to allocate a fixed total compute budget. The prevailing paradigm—"train the biggest model you can, then deploy with greedy decoding"—implicitly assumes that inference-time enhancements (retrieval, search, revision) are less valuable than additional pretraining. Atlas challenges this assumption, raising a question that parallels the one studied in the Atlas companion paper on test-time compute scaling: under what conditions is it better to invest compute in smarter inference rather than larger pretraining?

### Prior Approaches and Their Limitations

The paper situates itself against several lines of prior work, each with specific shortcomings that Atlas addresses:

#### Retrieval-Augmented Models: Not Yet Few-Shot Learners

Retrieval-augmented architectures had been well-established by 2022, with demonstrated success on knowledge-intensive tasks *when trained with full datasets*. REALM (Guu et al., 2020) jointly pre-trained a retriever and an extractive BERT-style language model using masked language modeling, showing strong results on open-domain QA. RAG (Lewis et al., 2020) combined a DPR retriever with a BART generator, demonstrating the effectiveness of retrieval for knowledge-intensive generation. FiD (Izacard & Grave, 2020) showed that processing each retrieved document independently in the encoder and fusing them in the decoder scaled efficiently with the number of documents. RETRO (Borgeaud et al., 2021) scaled retrieval to trillions of tokens, demonstrating improvements in language modeling perplexity.

However, the paper makes a critical observation: **none of these models had demonstrated compelling few-shot learning**. Their successes were in the full-dataset regime, where thousands or tens of thousands of training examples were available. The paper explicitly states this gap: "retrieval-augmented models have yet to demonstrate compelling few-shot learning capabilities." This matters because few-shot learning is where large language models shine, and if retrieval-augmented models couldn't compete in this regime, they would remain a niche solution for data-rich settings while parametric models dominated the few-shot frontier.

#### Large Language Models: Powerful Few-Shot Learners, But at Enormous Scale

On the other side of the ledger, large parametric language models had established themselves as the dominant few-shot learners. GPT-3 (175B parameters) showed that simply providing a few examples in the prompt—without any gradient updates—enabled reasonable performance across diverse tasks. This in-context learning ability scaled with model size, leading to the development of larger models: Gopher (280B), Chinchilla (70B, but trained on more data), and PaLM (540B). These models achieved increasingly impressive few-shot results on benchmarks like MMLU and NaturalQuestions.

But this line of work had two glaring limitations: (1) the computational cost of training was enormous and growing, raising questions of sustainability and accessibility, and (2) when knowledge was the key requirement—as in question answering, fact checking, and slot filling—it was unclear whether the few-shot performance came from generalization or from having memorized the answer during pretraining on web-scale data.

#### In-Context Retrieval: A Patch, Not a Solution

A middle-ground approach explored by Lazaridou et al. (2022) used Gopher-280B with Google Search: for each question, retrieve 50 passages, generate 4 candidate answers per passage, and then re-rank. This approach achieved 38.4% exact match on NaturalQuestions in a 15-shot setup. However, it required **50 to 450 forward passes of a 280B model per test question**, making it extraordinarily expensive at inference time. More fundamentally, it used retrieval as an *ad-hoc bolt-on* to a parametric model rather than deeply integrating retrieval into the model's training. The model hadn't learned to effectively use retrieved documents during pre-training; it was simply being asked to read them at test time, with no guarantee that it could integrate them effectively.

#### Standard Dense Retrievers: Require Annotated Data or Separate Training

Traditional dense retrievers like DPR (Karpukhin et al., 2020) were trained with explicit query-document relevance annotations—for example, pairs of questions and their corresponding Wikipedia passages. This creates a chicken-and-egg problem: to train a retriever for a new task, you need relevance annotations, but creating those annotations is expensive and requires knowing in advance what tasks the model will face. Extensions like ANCE (Xiong et al., 2020) improved hard negative mining but still required supervised data.

Unsupervised retrieval training methods existed (e.g., Contriever, Izacard et al., 2022; the inverse cloze task, Lee et al., 2019), but these were trained independently of the language model they would eventually serve. A retriever pre-trained on general web data might retrieve documents that are relevant to a query in a generic sense, but not necessarily documents that are *useful for generating the correct output*—a subtle but crucial distinction. A document might be topically relevant yet unhelpful for answering the specific question.

#### Joint Training Attempts: Limited Pre-Training Scale and Task Diversity

Some prior work had attempted to jointly train retrievers and language models: REALM (Guu et al., 2020) used an expectation-maximization-style objective to train the retriever as a latent variable, but only pre-trained with masked language modeling on a corpus smaller than what Atlas uses, and used an extractive rather than generative language model. EMDR² (Sachan et al., 2021) proposed a similar latent-variable training objective but applied it only in the fine-tuning stage for open-domain QA, not as a general pre-training strategy. Attention distillation (Izacard & Grave, 2021) used the language model's cross-attention patterns to train the retriever, but again, this was demonstrated in a task-specific fine-tuning context rather than as a general pre-training approach.

What was missing—and what Atlas provides—is a **systematic study** of how to jointly pre-train a retriever and a generative language model at scale, on diverse data, to enable few-shot generalization across a wide range of downstream tasks.

### How Atlas Positions Itself

Atlas is positioned not as a fundamentally new architecture (its components—Contriever, T5, FiD—are all prior work) but as a **carefully designed training recipe** that bridges the gap between retrieval augmentation and few-shot learning. The key insight is that for retrieval to help in few-shot settings, the model must learn during pre-training to effectively integrate retrieved evidence into its generation process. A model that first encounters retrieval at fine-tuning time, when only 64 examples are available, cannot learn this complex skill; it must be baked in during large-scale pre-training.

The paper's positioning is explicit in its contribution list: "A thorough study on how to design and train retrieval-augmented language models, with a focus on downstream few-shot learning and sample efficiency." This frames the work as an **empirical investigation** that produces a model (Atlas) as its output, rather than a novel architectural contribution.

Several design choices reflect this positioning:

- **Pre-training is essential, not optional.** The ablation in Table 1 shows that models without joint pre-training perform dramatically worse on few-shot tasks (30.0% average vs. 42.4%+ for pre-trained models on 64-shot KILT). This is evidence that pre-training teaches the language model *how to use* retrieved documents, a skill that cannot be acquired from a handful of fine-tuning examples.

- **The retriever training loss matters less than you might think.** Table 1 shows that ADist, EMDR², PDist, and LOOP all perform within a few points of each other on downstream tasks. The paper settles on PDist for stability, but the takeaway is that **any reasonable loss that leverages the language model's feedback** works, as long as the joint training happens. This is a practically useful negative result: practitioners shouldn't agonize over the loss function; the crucial thing is doing joint pre-training at all.

- **Pre-training data diversity matters.** Table 3 shows that using Common Crawl for both the index and pre-training data works well, but mismatching (Wiki index with CC training data, or vice versa) degrades performance, especially when training on Wikipedia but retrieving from CC. This suggests the model learns to rely on characteristics of the retrieval corpus during pre-training, and distribution shift at test time disrupts this.

- **Efficiency mechanisms are first-class concerns.** Sections 2.4 and 5.2.1 address the practical bottlenecks of retrieval-augmented training—the cost of refreshing the document index, the GPU memory footprint of dense indices—with specific solutions (re-ranking, query-side fine-tuning, product quantization). This reflects the paper's goal of making retrieval augmentation practical for real deployment, not just a proof of concept.

Perhaps most importantly, Atlas positions itself as a **direct competitor to massive parametric models** on their home turf: few-shot learning. The paper's headline results (42.4% on 64-shot NQ vs. PaLM's 39.6%, despite 50× fewer parameters) are deliberately framed to challenge the assumption that scale is the only path to few-shot capability. The FLOPs comparison is implicit but stark: Atlas-11B's total training compute is roughly 3.5 × 10²² FLOPs (dominated by T5 pre-training, with retrieval-augmented pre-training adding negligible overhead), while PaLM-540B required approximately 2.5 × 10²⁴ FLOPs—roughly 70× more. On NaturalQuestions in the 64-shot setting, Atlas achieves higher accuracy with roughly 2% of the training compute and 2% of the parameters.

This positions the paper at an inflection point in the 2022 NLP landscape: as the field was grappling with whether to continue scaling parametric models (toward GPT-4 and beyond) or to invest in more efficient architectures, Atlas provided strong evidence that the latter path could be competitive even in few-shot settings, while offering additional benefits (updateability, interpretability) that pure scaling cannot provide.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

Atlas is a retrieval-augmented language model: when given a query (a question, a factual claim, a multiple-choice prompt), it first searches a large external corpus of text passages to find relevant documents, then reads those documents and generates an answer. The paper is primarily an **empirical investigation into the training recipe** that makes such a model work as a few-shot learner—the core idea is that jointly pre-training the retriever and the language model on large-scale unsupervised data teaches the model to effectively integrate retrieved evidence, a skill that cannot be learned from a handful of downstream examples alone.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The system has two major components connected in a pipeline:

1. **Dense Retriever (Contriever)** — a dual-encoder transformer that independently embeds the query and every document passage in the corpus into dense vectors, then computes similarity via dot product to retrieve the top-$K$ most relevant passages from a pre-built index of millions of documents.

2. **Sequence-to-Sequence Language Model (T5 with Fusion-in-Decoder)** — a generative transformer that takes the query concatenated with each retrieved document separately in the encoder, fuses the encoded representations in the decoder via cross-attention, and generates the target output text token-by-token.

**Information flow at inference time:** Query text enters → Retriever query encoder produces a dense vector → Approximate nearest-neighbor search over the document index returns top-$K$ passage vectors (typically $K = 20$ or $40$) → Each retrieved passage is concatenated with the query and independently processed by the language model encoder → The decoder attends over all encoded passages simultaneously to generate the output.

**Information flow during training** is bidirectional: the language model's output perplexity (or attention patterns) provides a supervisory signal back to the retriever, telling it which documents were *actually useful* for generating the correct output, enabling joint optimization of both components without explicit document-level annotations.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the text-to-text task framing and the Fusion-in-Decoder architecture, since all downstream tasks share this input-output format and the FiD mechanism determines how retrieved documents interact with the language model.
- **Second**, the retriever architecture (Contriever dual-encoder) and the document indexing mechanism, since the retriever is the gateway that determines which evidence the language model sees.
- **Third**, the four retriever training objectives—Attention Distillation, EMDR², Perplexity Distillation, and Leave-One-Out Perplexity—since these are the key technical contribution that enables joint pre-training without document annotations, and understanding their differences is essential for interpreting the ablation results.
- **Fourth**, the three pretext tasks for unsupervised pre-training (prefix language modeling, masked language modeling, title-to-section generation), since the choice of pre-training task shapes the skills the model acquires.
- **Fifth**, the efficient retriever fine-tuning strategies (full index update, re-ranking, query-side fine-tuning), since these address the practical bottleneck of index refresh during training and directly impact the feasibility of deploying such models.
- **Sixth**, the index compression pipeline using product quantization, which demonstrates that the memory footprint of dense retrieval can be dramatically reduced without significant performance loss.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily an **empirical systems paper** whose core idea is that retrieval augmentation can substitute for massive parameter counts in few-shot learning, but only if the retriever and language model are jointly pre-trained at scale using objectives that teach the model to effectively integrate retrieved evidence. The technical contributions are the specific training objectives, pretext tasks, and efficiency mechanisms that make this work.

#### Text-to-Text Task Formulation

All tasks in Atlas are cast into a **text-to-text framework** (following Raffel et al., 2019): the input is a text query and the output is a text string. This unification means the same architecture and training procedure apply to question answering, fact checking, entity linking, and multiple-choice tasks without task-specific heads or loss functions.

**Concrete examples from the paper (Figure 2):**

- **Fact Checking:** Query = "Bermuda Triangle is in the western part of the Himalayas." Output = "False"
- **Question Answering:** Query = "who is playing the halftime show at super bowl 2016" Output = "Coldplay"
- **Entity Linking:** Query = "NTFS-3G is an open source <E>cross-platform</E> implementation of the Microsoft Windows NTFS file system with read-write support." Output = "Cross-platform software"

For classification tasks like MMLU multiple-choice, the model is trained to generate the letter corresponding to the correct answer option ('A', 'B', 'C', or 'D'). The input is formatted using a template that matches the masked language modeling pre-training format, e.g.:

```
question: {question text}
options: (A) {answer 1 text} (B) {answer 2 text} (C) {answer 3 text} (D) {answer 4 text}
answer: [MASK_0]
```

The model generates `[MASK_0] {correct answer option letter}`. At inference time (standard mode), the pre-softmax logits for tokens 'A', 'B', 'C', and 'D' are extracted, a softmax is applied to obtain a distribution over the four options, and the argmax is returned. This format "closely matches the format of MLM pre-training objective, aiding few-shot learning" (Appendix A.1).

**Why this framing matters for retrieval:** By casting everything as text-to-text, the retriever always receives a natural language query and the language model always generates natural language output. There is no need for task-specific retrieval models or task-specific output heads. The same retriever that was pre-trained to retrieve passages relevant to masked Wikipedia sentences can, after fine-tuning, retrieve passages relevant to fact-checking claims or multiple-choice questions.

#### Fusion-in-Decoder (FiD) Architecture for the Language Model

The language model is a T5 sequence-to-sequence transformer (Raffel et al., 2019) modified with the **Fusion-in-Decoder** (FiD) architecture (Izacard & Grave, 2020). FiD addresses a fundamental scaling problem: how to condition generation on many retrieved documents without incurring quadratic self-attention cost over all their tokens.

**The standard (naive) approach** would concatenate the query and all $K$ retrieved documents into one long sequence and feed it to the encoder. However, since self-attention in the encoder has $O(L^2)$ complexity where $L$ is the total sequence length (proportional to $K \times$ passage length), this becomes computationally prohibitive for large $K$. With $K = 20$ passages of 200 tokens each, plus the query, $L \approx 4000$ tokens, making $O(L^2)$ self-attention expensive. This approach "does not scale with the number of documents" (Section 2.1).

**The FiD solution** processes each document independently in the encoder:

1. For each retrieved passage $d_k$ (where $k \in \{1, \dots, K\}$), the query and the passage are concatenated into a single sequence: `[query] [passage_k]`. This concatenation is motivated by the observation that the model needs to see the query alongside each document to determine relevance and extract query-specific information.

2. Each concatenated sequence is fed **independently** through the T5 encoder. The encoder applies self-attention only within each query-document pair—documents do not attend to each other in the encoder. This reduces the per-sequence length to $|query| + |passage_k|$ (typically a few hundred tokens), making self-attention cheap.

3. The encoder produces a sequence of hidden states for each document independently.

4. **Fusion happens in the decoder:** The hidden states from all $K$ documents are concatenated along the length dimension into a single long sequence. The decoder then performs cross-attention over this combined sequence. Critically, cross-attention in the decoder has $O(L_{\text{target}} \times K \times L_{\text{doc}})$ complexity—linear in $K$—rather than the $O(K^2)$ complexity that encoder self-attention would have.

**Why FiD was chosen over alternatives:**

- **Scalability:** It enables conditioning on many documents (the paper uses $K = 20$ or $K = 40$ depending on the task) without the quadratic blowup of encoder-side concatenation.
- **Proven effectiveness:** Izacard & Grave (2020) showed FiD achieves strong results on open-domain QA, and the architecture had become a standard choice in retrieval-augmented generation by 2022.
- **Compatibility with the training objectives:** Because each document is encoded independently, the language model's per-document likelihoods $p_{\text{LM}}(a \mid q, d_k)$ are directly accessible. This is essential for the retriever training losses (PDist, EMDR², LOOP) which need to evaluate how well the language model generates the target when conditioned on *individual* documents.

**A subtlety about encoder processing:** The paper inherits from FiD the practice of concatenating the query to *each* document before encoding. This means the query is processed $K$ times (once per document). This is computationally redundant but architecturally simple and ensures that each document's encoding is specifically contextualized by the query it's meant to address.

#### Retriever Architecture: Contriever Dual-Encoder

The retriever is based on **Contriever** (Izacard et al., 2022), an unsupervised dense retrieval model using a dual-encoder architecture with the BERT-base transformer (approximately 110M parameters).

**Dual-encoder architecture explained:** Two separate transformer encoders—a **query encoder** and a **document encoder**—map text to fixed-size dense vectors. The query $q$ and each document $d$ are encoded independently:

$$s(q, d) = E_Q(q)^T \cdot E_D(d)$$

where $E_Q$ is the query encoder, $E_D$ is the document encoder, and $s(q, d)$ is a scalar similarity score computed as the dot product between the resulting embeddings. **"Average pooling is applied over the outputs of the last layer to obtain one vector representation per query or document"** (Section 2.1). This means the final vector is the mean of all token-level hidden states from the last transformer layer, producing a single $d_{\text{model}}$-dimensional vector (768 for BERT-base).

**Why dual-encoder rather than cross-encoder:** A cross-encoder would process the query and document together through self-attention, enabling richer interactions but requiring every query-document pair to be processed at query time. With an index of 37M passages (Wikipedia) or 387M passages (Wikipedia + CC), this would be impossibly slow. The dual-encoder allows **pre-computation of document embeddings**: all documents are encoded once, and stored in an index. At query time, only the query needs to be encoded, and retrieval reduces to a dot product between the query vector and all pre-computed document vectors, implemented via fast approximate nearest neighbor search.

**Contriever pre-training:** The retriever is initialized from Contriever weights, which were pre-trained using the **MoCo contrastive loss** (He et al., 2020) on unsupervised data. MoCo (Momentum Contrast) works by maintaining a queue of negative examples and using a momentum-updated encoder to produce consistent representations. The key insight is that without labeled query-document pairs, Contriever creates its own training signal by treating spans from the same document as positive pairs and spans from different documents as negatives. This unsupervised pre-training gives the retriever a strong initialization for semantic similarity before any task-specific training.

**Why Contriever over alternatives like DPR:** DPR (Karpukhin et al., 2020) requires supervised query-document pairs for training (e.g., questions paired with their containing Wikipedia passages). Contriever's unsupervised pre-training means the retriever starts with general semantic matching ability without needing any annotated data—a better fit for the paper's goal of learning from unlabeled text during pre-training.

**Document index construction:** For retrieval, document embeddings are pre-computed for all passages in the corpus and stored in an index. The index is sharded across available GPUs, and exact search is performed by computing dot products between the query vector and all document vectors in parallel (each GPU handles a shard). The embeddings are stored at fp16 precision. For the Wikipedia-only index (37M passages), this requires 49 GB of GPU memory; for the combined Wikipedia + Common Crawl index (387M passages), this requires 587 GB.

#### Retriever Training Objectives: The Core Technical Contribution

The central challenge in jointly training the retriever and language model is: **how do you provide a training signal to the retriever when you don't have explicit annotations of which documents are relevant?** The paper's answer is to let the language model itself provide that signal. If the language model finds a document useful for generating the correct output (as measured by perplexity or attention), the retriever should be trained to rank that document higher.

The paper studies four loss functions, all of which follow the same pattern: compute a target distribution over the top-$K$ retrieved documents based on the language model's behavior, then train the retriever to match this target distribution by minimizing the KL divergence between the target and the retriever's distribution. The differences lie in *how* the target distribution is computed.

##### Common Setup for All Losses

All four losses operate on the same foundation. Let the retriever produce a distribution over the top-$K$ retrieved documents $\{d_1, \ldots, d_K\}$:

$$p_{\text{retr}}(d \mid q) = \frac{\exp(s(d, q) / \theta)}{\sum_{k=1}^{K} \exp(s(d_k, q) / \theta)}$$

where $s(d, q) = E_Q(q)^T \cdot E_D(d)$ is the dot-product similarity between the query embedding and the document embedding, and $\theta$ is a temperature hyperparameter that controls the sharpness of the distribution (higher $\theta$ makes the distribution more uniform; lower $\theta$ makes it peakier). Note that this distribution is only over the top-$K$ documents returned by the current retriever, not over the entire corpus—this is a crucial practical constraint because computing the full corpus softmax is intractable. The retriever's job is thus to *re-rank* the top-$K$ documents, not to score the entire corpus.

In all cases, the loss is:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{retriever}} = \text{KL}(p_{\text{target}} \parallel p_{\text{retr}}) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} p_{\text{target}}(d_k) \log \frac{p_{\text{target}}(d_k)}{p_{\text{retr}}(d_k)}$$

where $p_{\text{target}}$ is computed differently for each loss but always depends on the language model's behavior. **Critically, a `StopGradient` operator is applied to $p_{\text{target}}$** — the gradients only flow through $p_{\text{retr}}$ and update the retriever parameters; the language model parameters are never updated by this loss. This prevents the language model from co-adapting to make the target distribution easier for the retriever to match.

##### Attention Distillation (ADist)

This loss uses the language model's cross-attention patterns as a signal of document relevance. The intuition: when generating the output, the decoder's cross-attention over the encoded documents indicates which documents it is "looking at" and therefore which ones are important.

**Computing the target distribution.** The paper improves on the original attention distillation method of Izacard & Grave (2021) by weighting attention scores by the norm of the value vectors. In a standard transformer attention mechanism:

$$y = \sum_{n=1}^{N} \alpha_n v_n$$

the output $y$ at a given position is a weighted sum of value vectors $v_n$, where $\alpha_n$ is the attention weight for token $n$ (after softmax). The paper's key observation is that **"the contribution to the output $y$ of a particular token $n$ cannot be evaluated from the attention score $\alpha_n$ alone, but should also take the norm of the value $v_n$ into account."** The relevance score for token $n$ is therefore:

$$r_n = \alpha_n \|v_n\|_2$$

where $\|v_n\|_2$ is the L2 norm of the value vector. This makes intuitive sense: if two tokens have the same attention weight $\alpha = 0.5$, but one has a value vector with norm 100 and the other has norm 0.1, the first token contributes 1000× more to the output.

These token-level scores $r_n$ are then aggregated to obtain a single score for each document: they are averaged across all attention heads, all decoder layers, and all generated output tokens corresponding to that document. After averaging, a softmax over the $K$ documents produces the target distribution:

$$p_{\text{attn}}(d_k) = \text{softmax}_k\left(\text{mean}_{h, l, t \in d_k} \left[\alpha_{h,l,t} \|v_{h,l,t}\|_2\right]\right)$$

where $h$ indexes attention heads, $l$ indexes layers, and $t$ indexes tokens that belong to document $d_k$. The loss is then $\text{KL}(p_{\text{attn}} \parallel p_{\text{retr}})$.

**Why this form:** Using $\alpha \|v\|_2$ rather than raw attention scores gives a more accurate measure of a token's true contribution to the decoder output. Raw attention scores can be high for tokens with very small value vectors, creating misleading "relevance" signals. Multiplying by the value norm corrects for this.

**Why the paper reports this improvement:** The paper states this alternative gives "slightly stronger results" than using raw pre-softmax attention scores. The improvement is modest but illustrates a careful attention to the mechanism—the goal is to distill *actual utility* to the retriever, not just attention patterns that might be artifacts of the softmax.

##### End-to-End Training of Multi-Document Reader and Retriever (EMDR²)

EMDR² (Sachan et al., 2021) treats the retrieved documents as **latent variables** and trains the retriever to maximize the marginal likelihood of the output:

$$p(a \mid q) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} p_{\text{LM}}(a \mid q, d_k) \cdot p_{\text{retr}}(d_k \mid q)$$

where $p_{\text{LM}}(a \mid q, d_k)$ is the language model's probability of generating the correct output $a$ when conditioned on document $d_k$ (and the query $q$), and $p_{\text{retr}}(d_k \mid q)$ is the retriever's distribution from Equation 1.

The loss for training the retriever is the negative log of this marginal likelihood:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{EMDR}^2} = -\log \left( \sum_{k=1}^{K} p_{\text{LM}}(a \mid q, d_k) \cdot p_{\text{retr}}(d_k \mid q) \right)$$

**What this loss encourages:** The probability distribution over documents that maximizes this loss function is an indicator that puts all mass on the document that gives the highest $p_{\text{LM}}(a \mid q, d_k)$. In other words, the retriever is trained to assign high probability to the single document that makes the language model most likely to generate the correct output. This is a "harder" training signal than ADist or PDist, which can distribute probability mass across multiple useful documents.

**Important practical detail:** The EMDR² loss "is applied at the token level, and not at the sequence level" (Section 2.2). This means the marginalization is performed per output token, not over the entire sequence. For a target sequence with tokens $a_1, \dots, a_T$, the loss is:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{EMDR}^2} = -\sum_{t=1}^{T} \log \left( \sum_{k=1}^{K} p_{\text{LM}}(a_t \mid q, d_k, a_{<t}) \cdot p_{\text{retr}}(d_k \mid q) \right)$$

Token-level application makes the gradient signal denser and more fine-grained, since each token provides a separate opportunity to update the retriever based on which documents were useful for predicting that specific token.

**Why this form:** EMDR² is inspired by the expectation-maximization algorithm, where the E-step computes the posterior over latent variables (documents) given the current model, and the M-step updates the retriever to match this posterior. In practice, only the retriever parameters are updated (StopGradient on $p_{\text{LM}}$), making this a variational approximation. The token-level version makes the implicit assumption that documents useful for the whole sequence are also useful for each token, which is reasonable for extractive tasks but could be problematic for abstractive tasks where different parts of the output draw from different documents.

##### Perplexity Distillation (PDist)

PDist is a simpler loss that directly asks: **how much does each document improve the language model's perplexity on the output?** The target distribution is a softmax over the negative log-perplexity (equivalently, the log-probability) of the output conditioned on each document individually:

$$p_k \propto p_{\text{LM}}(a \mid d_k, q)$$

Using the softmax operator:

$$p_{\text{target}}(d_k) = \frac{\exp(\log p_{\text{LM}}(a \mid d_k, q))}{\sum_{i=1}^{K} \exp(\log p_{\text{LM}}(a \mid d_i, q))}$$

The loss is then $\text{KL}(p_{\text{target}} \parallel p_{\text{retr}})$.

**What it computes:** For each of the $K$ retrieved documents, the language model computes the log-probability of generating the correct output when conditioned on that single document (plus the query). These log-probabilities are exponentiated (converting to likelihoods) and normalized into a distribution over documents. The retriever is then trained to match this distribution.

A document that enables the language model to generate the output with high probability (low perplexity) gets a high target weight. A document that doesn't help (or actively confuses) gets a low weight. The softmax normalization means that the absolute scale of the probabilities doesn't matter—only relative differences between documents.

**Why this form and the role of temperature:** The paper notes that a temperature hyperparameter can be applied when computing the target distribution, controlling how "peaky" the distribution is. This matters because the perplexity of the output may not vary much across different documents, especially for long outputs or tasks where many documents are somewhat helpful. Without temperature scaling, the target distribution would be nearly uniform, providing a weak training signal. A low temperature sharpens the distribution, emphasizing the small differences between documents.

**The paper's justification for choosing PDist:** Among the four objectives, PDist "tends to be more stable than EMDR² or ADist, and more computationally efficient than LOOP" (Section 4.3). Stability matters because the retriever and language model are jointly trained—oscillations in one can destabilize the other. Computational efficiency matters because PDist requires only $K$ forward passes through the language model's decoder (one per document, to compute $p_{\text{LM}}(a \mid d_k, q)$), whereas LOOP requires $K$ forward passes through both encoder and decoder for each of the $K$ leave-one-out subsets. The paper adopts PDist as the default for all main experiments.

##### Leave-One-Out Perplexity Distillation (LOOP)

LOOP asks a slightly different question: **how much worse does the language model's prediction get when a specific document is removed from the retrieved set?** Instead of evaluating each document in isolation (as in PDist and EMDR²), it evaluates the *marginal contribution* of each document when the model has access to all $K - 1$ other documents.

For each document $d_k$ in the top-$K$ set, the language model computes the log-probability of the output when conditioned on the remaining $K-1$ documents (the set $D_K \setminus \{d_k\}$, meaning "all top-$K$ documents except $d_k$"):

$$p_{\text{loop}}(d_k) \propto \exp(-\log p_{\text{LM}}(a \mid D_K \setminus \{d_k\}, q))$$

Using the softmax:

$$p_{\text{target}}(d_k) = \frac{\exp(-\log p_{\text{LM}}(a \mid D_K \setminus \{d_k\}, q))}{\sum_{i=1}^{K} \exp(-\log p_{\text{LM}}(a \mid D_K \setminus \{d_i\}, q))}$$

**What it computes:** A high value of $p_{\text{LM}}(a \mid D_K \setminus \{d_k\}, q)$ means the language model can still generate the output well *without* $d_k$—implying $d_k$ was not essential. A low value means removing $d_k$ hurts performance—implying $d_k$ was important. The negative sign in the exponent means that documents whose removal causes a large drop in log-probability (low $p_{\text{LM}}$ without them) receive high target weights. The softmax normalizes these importance weights across documents.

**Why LOOP is theoretically appealing:** Unlike PDist and EMDR², which evaluate documents in isolation (conditioning on only one document at a time), LOOP evaluates documents in the context of the full retrieved set. This matters because the language model is trained and deployed with access to multiple documents—its ability to use a document may interact with the presence of other documents. A document might be redundant if another document covers the same information, or synergistic if it complements missing details. LOOP captures these interactions because it tests the effect of each document's absence given the presence of all others.

**The computational cost:** LOOP is "more expensive to compute than PDist and EMDR" because it requires $K$ separate language model forward passes, each conditioning on $K-1$ documents concatenated in the encoder. For each pass, the FiD encoder must process $K-1$ documents independently, and the decoder must generate the full target sequence. With $K = 20$, this means LOOP requires $20 \times 19 \approx 380$ document-encoder passes (compared to $20$ for PDist) and $20$ full decoder passes. This makes LOOP roughly $20\times$ more expensive per training step than PDist, which is why the paper uses it only in ablations and not for the main Atlas models.

**Why this form rather than directly measuring improvement:** One might ask: why not compute the *improvement* from adding a document (conditioning with vs. without it) rather than the *degradation* from removing it? LOOP uses removal because it more closely matches how the model is actually deployed—the model sees multiple documents, and we want to know which ones it relies on. Measuring improvement from adding a document to an empty context would miss interactions between documents.

#### Pretext Tasks for Unsupervised Joint Pre-Training

The retriever and language model are jointly pre-trained on large-scale unsupervised data. The choice of pre-training task matters because it determines what skills the language model learns and what retrieval patterns the retriever learns. The paper studies three pretext tasks (Section 2.3), all of which are self-supervised—they create input-output pairs from raw text without human annotation.

##### Prefix Language Modeling

This casts standard language modeling into the text-to-text framework by splitting each training example differently from standard autoregressive training. Given a text chunk of $N$ words, the chunk is split into two equal halves of $N/2$ words each. The first half becomes the query, and the second half becomes the target output. The retriever uses the first $N/2$ tokens as the query to retrieve documents, and the language model generates the remaining $N/2$ tokens.

**Why this task:** It teaches the model to generate coherent continuations of text, which is a general-purpose language skill. However, the retrieved documents for a generic text prefix might not be directly relevant to the continuation—there's no guarantee that retrieving passages similar to the first half of a paragraph will help generate the second half. This is in contrast to masked language modeling, where the corrupted input provides a clearer signal about what kind of document would help.

##### Masked Language Modeling (MLM)

This follows the T5 formulation (Raffel et al., 2019) of masked language modeling. Starting from a text chunk of $N$ words, the procedure is:

1. Sample $k$ spans of average length 3 tokens, targeting a 15% masking ratio overall.
2. Replace each span with a unique sentinel token (e.g., `[MASK_0]`, `[MASK_1]`, etc.).
3. The corrupted text becomes the query. The retriever uses this corrupted query to retrieve documents. A subtlety: **special mask tokens in the query are replaced with a mask token that is present in the retriever's vocabulary**, since the Contriever was not trained with T5's specific sentinel tokens.
4. The target output is the sequence of masked spans, each prefixed by its corresponding sentinel token: `[MASK_0] span_0_text [MASK_1] span_1_text ...`

**Why MLM is the best-performing pretext task (Table 2):** MLM achieves 42.4% average on 64-shot KILT, compared to 40.1% for prefix LM and 40.8% for title-to-section. The paper does not deeply analyze why, but plausible reasons include:

- **Clearer retrieval signal:** A sentence with 15% of its words masked has obvious "gaps" that a relevant document could fill. The retriever learns to find documents containing the missing information.
- **Matches fine-tuning format:** The MMLU fine-tuning uses a `[MASK_0]` token format, making the pre-training task qualitatively similar to the downstream task. This is the explicit reason given for the MMLU featurization: it "closely matches the format of MLM pre-training objective, aiding few-shot learning" (Appendix A.1).
- **Dense supervisory signal:** With 15% masking, each training example provides multiple spans to predict, giving more gradient steps per example than prefix LM (which predicts one contiguous block).

##### Title-to-Section Generation

This task generates Wikipedia article sections given the article title and section title. The query is the concatenation of the article title and section title; the output is the text of that section. Sections titled "See also," "References," "Further reading," and "External links" are excluded because they are list-like and do not represent coherent prose.

**Why this task:** It is a more abstractive generation task than MLM or prefix LM—the output is not a direct copy of any retrieved passage but a synthesis that may draw from multiple sources. It also encourages the retriever to find documents related to the broader topic of the article, not just exact string matches to the query.

**Why it underperforms MLM (Table 2):** Title-to-section achieves 40.8% average on 64-shot KILT versus 42.4% for MLM. The paper does not analyze this in depth, but the gap is small (1.6 points), suggesting that the choice of pretext task is not the dominant factor—joint pre-training itself is what matters. The paper adopts MLM for all subsequent experiments because it has a "small advantage."

#### Efficient Retriever Fine-Tuning

A practical bottleneck in training retrieval-augmented models is that the document index—the pre-computed embeddings of all documents—needs to be refreshed regularly as the retriever parameters are updated. If the index is not refreshed, the stored embeddings become **stale**: they were produced by an old version of the document encoder and no longer correspond to the embeddings the updated query encoder expects. The paper analyzes this overhead and presents three strategies to mitigate it (Section 2.4).

**Computational model.** The paper makes a simplified assumption for cost analysis: the time for a forward pass on a document is $O(P)$ where $P$ is the number of parameters. Document sizes are assumed constant (achieved by splitting long documents into passages of similar length and using padding). Under this model, one training step has complexity $4 \times B \times K \times P_{\text{LM}}$, where $B$ is the batch size, $K$ is the number of retrieved documents per query, and the factor 4 accounts for the backward pass and activation checkpointing. Re-computing the full index of $N$ documents costs $N \times P_{\text{retr}}$.

##### Full Index Update (Baseline Strategy)

The index is recomputed from scratch every $R$ training steps. The overhead relative to training with a fixed retriever is:

$$\text{Overhead} = \frac{N \times P_{\text{retr}}}{4 \times B \times K \times P_{\text{LM}} \times R}$$

Plugging in typical values: BERT-base retriever ($P_{\text{retr}} \approx 110\text{M}$), T5-XL language model ($P_{\text{LM}} \approx 3\text{B}$), giving $P_{\text{retr}} / P_{\text{LM}} \approx 1/25$. For the Wikipedia index ($N = 37\text{M}$ documents), batch size $B = 64$, $K = 20$ retrieved documents, and refresh every $R = 1000$ steps:

$$\text{Overhead} = \frac{37 \times 10^6}{100 \times 64 \times 20 \times 1000} \approx 0.29 = 29\%$$

This means that roughly 30% of training time is spent re-encoding documents for the index—a significant but manageable cost for pre-training (where the paper does 10,000 steps with refresh every 1,000 or 2,500 steps). For fine-tuning on small datasets, however, training for only 50 steps but potentially needing to refresh the index for each step would be prohibitively expensive. This motivates the more efficient strategies.

##### Re-Ranking

Instead of recomputing the entire index, the model retrieves $L$ documents using the **stale** index (where $L > K$, typically $L = 10 \times K$), **re-encodes** these $L$ documents with the up-to-date retriever, computes new similarity scores, and keeps the top-$K$ for the language model. The overhead becomes:

$$\text{Overhead} = \frac{L \times P_{\text{retr}}}{4 \times K \times P_{\text{LM}}}$$

With $L = 10 \times K$ and the same parameter ratio, this gives approximately $10/(100) = 10\%$ overhead—much more manageable than full re-indexing. Moreover, re-ranking happens at every training step, so the computation is amortized rather than concentrated in expensive index refresh bursts.

**The catch:** If many updates are performed on the retriever without refreshing the full index, the stale index may not even contain the true top-$K$ documents in its top-$L$ results—they could fall outside the top-$L$ entirely. The paper notes that "it is possible to track the positions of the top-$K$ re-ranked documents in the top-$L$, and estimate when the index needs to be updated." In other words, if the re-ranked top-$K$ documents were originally ranked at positions $L-5, L-3, L-10$, etc. (near the boundary), the index is becoming dangerously stale and a full refresh is needed. For the pre-training experiments, the paper refreshes the full index every 2,500 steps while using top-100 re-ranking at each step.

##### Query-Side Fine-Tuning

The simplest and most radical approach: **freeze the document encoder entirely and only train the query encoder.** If the document encoder parameters are fixed, the document embeddings never change, and the index never needs recomputation—there is zero overhead from index maintenance.

**Tradeoffs documented in the paper (Table 4):**

- **64-shot setting:** Query-side fine-tuning achieves 45.7% average vs. 44.1% for full fine-tuning (standard full index refresh) and 44.7% for re-ranking. Remarkably, query-side is *better* in the 64-shot regime, which the paper attributes to "there being less opportunity for over-fitting" when only the query encoder is updated on tiny datasets.

- **1024-shot setting:** Full fine-tuning achieves 51.7% vs. 51.0% for query-side. With more data, updating the document encoder becomes beneficial—the model can learn task-specific document representations—but the gap is surprisingly small (0.7 points).

**Why this works:** The query encoder and document encoder share the same architecture but serve different functions. The query encoder must map diverse, often incomplete or noisy queries (questions, claims, masked sentences) to a space where relevant documents are nearby. The document encoder is trained once on a large, high-quality corpus during pre-training and already produces good general-purpose representations. For few-shot tasks with limited data, the risk of overfitting the document encoder to a tiny training set (making it worse at retrieving for unseen queries) outweighs the benefit of task-specific adaptation. Freezing the document encoder is a form of regularization.

The paper adopts query-side fine-tuning for all few-shot experiments and uses re-ranking or full fine-tuning for full-dataset experiments where more training data is available.

#### Training Procedure for Atlas

The full Atlas training pipeline consists of three phases, each informed by the ablations described above:

##### Phase 1: Initialization

- **Retriever:** Initialized from the unsupervised Contriever model (BERT-base architecture, approximately 110M parameters). This gives the retriever a strong starting point for semantic similarity without requiring any supervised retrieval data.

- **Language Model:** Initialized from T5 pre-trained weights, specifically the **T5-lm-adapt** variant (version 1.1). The original T5 pre-training included supervised data in the training set (downstream tasks mixed with unsupervised denoising). T5-lm-adapt was trained on unlabeled text only, which the authors use to avoid any concern about supervised data leakage. The lm-adapt variant was also noted for "improved stability" during fine-tuning.

##### Phase 2: Joint Pre-Training

The model undergoes 10,000 steps of joint pre-training using the masked language modeling pretext task, the Perplexity Distillation loss for the retriever, and a mix of Wikipedia and Common Crawl data.

**Pre-training hyperparameters for ablation models (T5-XL, 3B parameters):**
- Optimizer: AdamW
- Batch size: 64
- Learning rate: $10^{-4}$ for the language model (reader), $10^{-5}$ for the retriever, with linear decay and 1,000 warmup steps
- Number of retrieved documents ($K$): 20
- Index refresh: Every 1,000 steps (for ablation experiments; the final Atlas models use every 2,500 steps with top-100 re-ranking)
- Training data: The same passages used for the index, with an important safeguard: "we ensure the passage we are training on is filtered out from the retrieved documents, to prevent the model from simply retrieving the passage it is de-noising/generating, and trivially using it to solve the pre-training task."

**Final Atlas model hyperparameters (Section 4.5):**
- Pre-training iterations: 10,000
- Batch size: 128
- Index refresh: Every 2,500 steps, with re-ranking of top-100 documents at each step
- Models trained at three scales: 770M, 3B, and 11B parameters (T5 variants)

**The index refresh schedule reflects the efficiency analysis:** At 2,500-step intervals with top-100 re-ranking, the computational overhead from index maintenance is kept manageable. The paper's overhead formula with $R = 2500$ and $B = 128$ gives negligible overhead from the periodic full refreshes, while the per-step re-ranking cost ($L = 100$, $K = 20$) is approximately $100/(100 \times 20) = 5\%$ overhead.

##### Phase 3: Fine-Tuning

Task-specific fine-tuning adapts the pre-trained Atlas to downstream tasks. The paper uses different fine-tuning strategies depending on the amount of training data available:

- **Few-shot (64 examples):** Query-side fine-tuning (frozen document encoder) for 50 iterations with batch size 32, learning rate $4 \times 10^{-5}$ with linear decay and 5 warmup steps for both language model and retriever.

- **1024-shot:** 200 iterations, otherwise same settings. The paper uses re-ranking or full fine-tuning depending on the experiment.

- **Full dataset:** Standard fine-tuning with full index refresh and re-ranking. Models are trained for 5,000 gradient steps with index refresh every 500 steps for the first 1,000 steps, then every 2,000 steps thereafter. Evaluated every 500 steps and the best checkpoint on the validation set is selected.

**Few-shot hyperparameter selection challenge:** Selecting hyperparameters when only 64 examples are available is difficult—there's no room for a held-out validation set. For MMLU 5-shot, the paper uses RACE (an auxiliary dataset from the MMLU suite) for hyperparameter selection: it samples 5 sets of 5-shot training data from RACE, sweeps over batch size $\{32, 64\}$, learning rates $\{(5\times 10^{-5}, 1\times 10^{-5}), (4\times 10^{-5}, 4\times 10^{-5})\}$ for language model and retriever, retriever temperature $\{0.1, 0.01\}$, and number of training steps $\{16, 32, 64, 128\}$, and picks the configuration with the strongest RACE validation score. These hyperparameters are then applied directly to the MMLU 5-shot task without further tuning.

**Fine-tuning loss:** During fine-tuning, the same retriever loss used during pre-training (PDist by default) is applied. This means the retriever continues to be updated using the language model's feedback even on the small downstream dataset. The paper's ablation (Table 4) shows that keeping the retriever completely fixed during fine-tuning drops performance significantly (41.1% vs. 45.7% for query-side fine-tuning on 64-shot), confirming that even minimal retriever adaptation to the task is valuable.

#### Index Construction and Compression

The document index is a critical system component. The paper describes its construction in detail:

**Corpus preparation.** Two corpora are used:
1. **Wikipedia:** The December 20, 2021 dump. Lists and infoboxes are kept and linearized by adding semicolon separators between entries. Articles are split by section, and long sections are further split into passages of equal size containing less than 200 words. This produces 37M passages with an average length of 78 words.
2. **Common Crawl:** The 2020-10 dump, preprocessed with the CCNet pipeline (Wenzek et al., 2020). Additional filtering is applied following Gopher (Rae et al., 2021): documents are filtered based on document length, average word length, ratio of alphanumeric characters, and number of repeated tokens. This produces 350M passages.

The combined corpus of 387M passages (37M + 350M) is used for most Atlas experiments. Some experiments use Wikipedia-only indices for analysis or temporal matching.

**Embedding storage.** Document embeddings are stored at fp16 precision. For the Wikipedia index (37M passages × 768 dimensions × 2 bytes), this requires 49 GB. For the combined index (387M passages), this requires 587 GB. The index is sharded across available GPUs, and search is performed by computing dot products in parallel across shards.

**Product Quantization (PQ) for compression (Section 5.2.1).** To reduce the GPU memory footprint, the paper explores Product Quantization (Jégou et al., 2011), a lossy compression technique for nearest neighbor search. PQ works by:

1. Splitting each $d$-dimensional vector into $M$ sub-vectors of dimension $d/M$.
2. Running k-means clustering independently on each sub-space to learn a codebook of $C$ centroids per sub-space.
3. Approximating each original vector by the concatenation of the nearest centroid in each sub-space.
4. Storing only the centroid indices (requiring $\log_2(C)$ bits per sub-vector) rather than the full floating-point values.

The memory reduction is dramatic. The paper tests different compression levels and finds (Figure 4):

- **Wikipedia index:** Can be compressed from 49 GB to 4 GB with "negligible drop in retrieval precision and exact match." This is a roughly 12× reduction.
- **Combined index:** Can be compressed from 587 GB to 50 GB "without serious degradation." This means the full combined index could fit on a single 80 GB GPU, eliminating the need for sharding across multiple GPUs.

**Why PQ matters:** The large memory requirement for uncompressed indices "limits accessibility and ease of deployment" (Section 5.2.1). A system requiring 587 GB of GPU RAM for the index alone can only be deployed on expensive multi-GPU nodes. Compressing to 50 GB makes single-GPU deployment feasible, dramatically lowering the barrier to entry. The paper demonstrates this on Atlas-3B for the 64-shot NaturalQuestions task, showing that performance remains nearly flat until very aggressive compression levels.

#### De-Biased Inference for Multiple-Choice Tasks

For MMLU (multiple-choice questions with four answer options labeled A, B, C, D), the paper introduces a **de-biasing** technique to address the model's potential bias toward specific answer letters, especially in few-shot settings where there isn't enough training data for the model to learn a uniform prior.

**The problem:** Even though fine-tuning data permutes which answer option text is assigned to which letter (encouraging a uniform prior), the model may still exhibit residual bias. For example, it might consistently prefer option 'C' regardless of content.

**De-biased inference with cyclic permutations:**

1. The question has 4 answer options assigned to letters A, B, C, D in some specific ordering $z$.
2. Standard inference computes $p(a \mid q) \propto \text{softmax}(\text{logits for tokens A, B, C, D})$ for this single ordering.
3. De-biased inference runs 4 forward passes, one for each cyclic permutation of the answer ordering:
   - Pass 1: Original ordering (A→option1, B→option2, C→option3, D→option4)
   - Pass 2: Cyclic shift (A→option2, B→option3, C→option4, D→option1)
   - Pass 3: Cyclic shift (A→option3, B→option4, C→option1, D→option2)
   - Pass 4: Cyclic shift (A→option4, B→option1, C→option2, D→option3)
4. The probabilities for each *answer option* (not letter) are summed across the 4 passes.
5. The answer with the highest summed probability is selected.

**Why cyclic permutations rather than all 24:** The full set of permutations would require 24 forward passes, improving results by an additional ~1% but being 6× more expensive. The 4 cyclic permutations capture much of the benefit at 4× the inference cost. Table 13 shows that cyclic permutation de-biasing improves zero-shot MMLU from 36.8% to 47.1% (+10.3 points) and 5-shot from 43.4% to 47.9% (+4.5 points). With more training data (multi-task or full), the benefit disappears (56.4% vs. 56.6% for multi-task), confirming that the model learns an unbiased prior when enough examples are available.

**Why this inference-time procedure rather than architectural changes:** The paper could have added a bias term to the output layer or modified the training objective to enforce a uniform prior. The permutation approach is simpler: it requires no model modifications and can be applied to any trained model post-hoc. The cost is increased inference compute (4×), which is acceptable for benchmark evaluation but would be a consideration for deployment.

#### Temporal Adaptation via Index Swapping

Section 5.2 demonstrates a unique capability of retrieval-augmented models: adapting to new facts without retraining by simply swapping the document index. The experiment uses a dataset derived from TempLAMA (Dhingra et al., 2022), consisting of time-sensitive cloze questions where the correct answer changes between 2017 and 2020 (e.g., "Theo Walcott plays for ___" → "Arsenal F.C." in 2017, "Everton F.C." in 2020).

**Procedure:**
1. A small training set (248 questions) is created with 2017 answers.
2. Atlas is fine-tuned on these questions using a 2017 Wikipedia index.
3. At test time, the model is evaluated on both 2017 and 2020 test sets, using either the 2017 or 2020 Wikipedia index.

**Results (Table 11):**
- Atlas trained on 2017 answers, tested with 2017 index: 57.7% accuracy on 2017 questions, 1.5% on 2020 questions (correctly generating almost no 2020 answers).
- Atlas trained on 2017 answers, tested with **2020 index** (no retraining): 10.2% on 2017, **53.1%** on 2020. Simply swapping the index flips the model's temporal knowledge.
- Closed-book T5 (same training): 12.1% on 2017 regardless of index (no retrieval mechanism to update).
- Closed-book T5 trained on 2020 answers still achieves only 3.6% on 2020—likely because T5 was pre-trained on pre-2020 data and has the old facts baked into its parameters.

**What this demonstrates:** The parametric component (the language model) learned a general *skill*—how to answer cloze questions by reading evidence from retrieved documents—but not the *facts* themselves. The facts reside entirely in the index. This is a clean separation of reasoning from memory, directly supporting the paper's central thesis.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Joint Pre-Training Is the Enabling Mechanism for Few-Shot Retrieval-Augmented Learning — Not Architecture, Not Scale

The paper's most fundamental intellectual contribution is the empirical demonstration that **jointly pre-training the retriever and language model is what unlocks few-shot learning**, not any particular architectural novelty and not massive parameter counts. This is a conceptual reframing of what makes retrieval-augmented models work.

**What the field believed before Atlas:** Retrieval-augmented models (REALM, RAG, FiD, RETRO) were known to excel at knowledge-intensive tasks when trained on full datasets. However, the dominant assumption — implicit in the literature but never directly tested — was that retrieval augmentation was primarily a mechanism for *knowledge access*: it gave the model a way to look up facts it hadn't memorized. Under this view, few-shot learning would still require large parametric models because few-shot generalization is about *reasoning* from limited examples, not just knowledge lookup. The massive few-shot learners (GPT-3, Gopher, PaLM) stored both reasoning capacity and knowledge in the same parameters, and it was unclear whether the two could be separated.

**What Atlas shows:** The ablation in Table 1 is the crucial evidence. A model with retrieval but *no joint pre-training* achieves only 30.0% average on 64-shot KILT (NQ, WoW, FEVER). Models with joint pre-training — using any of four different retriever losses — achieve 42.2–45.7%. The 12–15 point gap cannot be explained by knowledge access alone, because the same retrieval corpus is available in both conditions. Something happens during joint pre-training that teaches the language model *how to use* retrieved documents — a meta-skill that generalizes to new tasks with few examples. The paper's own words capture this: "the biggest impact of pre-training is on the language model, which learns to use and aggregate information from the retrieved documents" (Section 4.3).

This insight is fundamental rather than incremental because it changes what we think the pre-training phase is *for* in retrieval-augmented models. It's not just about teaching the retriever what's relevant or filling the language model with knowledge — it's about teaching a **document-reading capability** that transfers across tasks, analogous to how standard language model pre-training teaches general linguistic skills that transfer to downstream tasks.

**Why this matters beyond performance:** This finding implies that retrieval-augmented few-shot learners don't need to be scaled to hundreds of billions of parameters. Atlas-11B outperforms PaLM-540B on 64-shot NaturalQuestions (42.4% vs. 39.6%) despite having ~50× fewer parameters and ~70× less training compute. The capability comes from the combination of retrieval + joint pre-training, not from scale. This is a reframing of the scaling narrative: the path to better few-shot learning isn't exclusively through larger models; it's through architectures that separate reasoning from memory and train both components to work together.

The fact that the choice of retriever loss function matters relatively little (Table 1 shows ADist at 43.0%, EMDR² at 43.3%, PDist at 45.7%, LOOP at 43.7%) reinforces this — the crucial factor is *that* joint training happens, not the precise mechanism. The four losses span substantially different approaches (attention-based vs. perplexity-based, document-isolation vs. leave-one-out), yet all produce models that dramatically outperform the no-joint-training baseline. This is a practically useful negative result: practitioners should invest effort in setting up joint pre-training infrastructure rather than optimizing the loss function.

### Innovation 2: The Language Model Can Serve as Its Own Retrieval Supervisor — Eliminating the Annotation Bottleneck

Atlas's second conceptual contribution is demonstrating that the language model itself can provide sufficient supervisory signal to train the retriever, removing the need for explicit query-document relevance annotations. This is not the first paper to propose such an approach (REALM, EMDR², and attention distillation all preceded it), but Atlas is the first to systematically compare multiple such objectives in a unified pre-training framework and show that **any reasonable language-model-derived signal works well enough**, making the approach robust and practical at scale.

**What the field did before:** Training dense retrievers traditionally required supervised data: pairs of queries and relevant documents. DPR (Karpukhin et al., 2020) used question-passage pairs from QA datasets. This created a bottleneck: for each new domain or task, you needed new annotations. Unsupervised retriever training methods (Contriever, the inverse cloze task) removed the annotation requirement but trained the retriever independently of the language model it would serve — optimizing for generic semantic similarity rather than *task-relevant* retrieval. A document that is semantically similar to a query is not necessarily useful for generating the correct answer.

**What Atlas demonstrates:** The four loss functions constitute a thorough exploration of how the language model's own behavior — its perplexity on the target output, its cross-attention patterns, the marginal contribution of each document — can be converted into a training signal for the retriever. The key design pattern (common to all four) is elegant: compute a target distribution over documents based on how useful each one appears to be to the language model, then distill that distribution into the retriever via KL divergence.

This is more than an engineering trick; it's a conceptual shift in how we think about the retriever-language model relationship. Rather than treating the retriever as an independent module that finds "relevant" documents and the language model as a downstream consumer, Atlas treats them as a **mutually-supervising pair**: the retriever provides the language model with evidence, and the language model provides the retriever with feedback about what evidence was actually useful. This feedback loop means the retriever can be trained on *any* task for which query-output pairs exist, without document annotations — including the self-supervised pre-training tasks themselves.

**Why PDist is the practical takeaway:** The paper's choice of Perplexity Distillation as the default loss isn't because it's dramatically better (Table 1 shows it at 45.7% vs. 43.0–43.7% for alternatives, a modest gap). It's because PDist "tends to be more stable than EMDR² or ADist, and more computationally efficient than LOOP" (Section 4.3). Stability matters when jointly training two interacting models, and efficiency matters when scaling to large corpora. The fact that the simplest loss (compute per-document perplexity, softmax, distill) works as well or better than more sophisticated alternatives (attention analysis, leave-one-out evaluation) is a positive result for reproducibility and practical adoption.

**The significance for the field:** This insight removes the last barrier to training retrieval-augmented models on arbitrary unlabeled text corpora. You don't need query-document pairs. You don't need human relevance judgments. You don't even need to decide what "relevance" means for your task. You just need text — the same text you'd use for standard language model pre-training — and the language model will figure out what's useful. This makes retrieval-augmented pre-training as scalable as standard language model pre-training, which is essential for the approach to compete with massive parametric models.

### Innovation 3: Retrieval Enables a Clean Separation Between Reasoning and Knowledge, with Verifiable Empirical Consequences

Atlas provides the clearest empirical demonstration to date that retrieval-augmented models can achieve a functional separation between **reasoning capability** (how to process and integrate evidence) and **factual knowledge** (what the facts actually are). This separation was hypothesized in prior work but never demonstrated with the clarity and practical consequences that Atlas provides.

**The conceptual claim:** In a purely parametric model, reasoning and knowledge are conflated. When GPT-3 answers a question correctly, it's impossible to determine whether it reasoned from the few-shot examples or retrieved the answer from its memorized training data. Atlas provides a mechanism to disentangle these: the language model parameters encode *how to use evidence* (the reasoning skill), while the external index encodes *what the evidence says* (the factual knowledge). If this separation is real, then changing the index without changing the model should change the model's factual knowledge without affecting its reasoning capability.

**The evidence — the TempLAMA experiment (Table 11) is the cleanest demonstration in the paper and arguably one of the most important results in retrieval-augmented learning generally:**

- Atlas fine-tuned on 2017 answers with a 2017 index achieves 57.7% on 2017 test questions and only 1.5% on 2020 test questions. The model correctly answers based on 2017 facts.
- **Without any retraining**, when the index is swapped to 2020, Atlas's 2020 accuracy jumps to 53.1% while 2017 accuracy drops to 10.2%. The model's knowledge flips to match the index.
- The closed-book T5 baseline achieves 12.1% regardless of index — it has no retrieval mechanism to update. Even when trained on 2020 answers, T5 achieves only 3.6% accuracy, likely because its parametric knowledge from pre-training (on pre-2020 data) overrides the fine-tuning signal.

The symmetry is striking: 57.7% → 1.5% when the index stays at 2017 but the test year changes; 1.5% → 53.1% when the test year changes but the index is updated to match. The model faithfully reports what's in its index, not what's in its parameters. The reasoning capability (how to answer a cloze question by reading a retrieved passage) transfers across temporal contexts; only the knowledge changes.

**Why this is more than a neat trick:** This has several implications that go beyond Atlas specifically:

1. **Updateability is real and practical.** The dominant approach to keeping models current — continuous pre-training or periodic fine-tuning on new data — is expensive, risks catastrophic forgetting, and requires up-to-date annotated data. Index swapping is zero-cost, zero-shot, and requires no annotated data for the new time period. For applications where facts change (sports, politics, current events, scientific knowledge), this is transformative.

2. **The NaturalQuestions temporal analysis (Table 12) reinforces this:** Using a Wikipedia index temporally matched to the NaturalQuestions data collection (December 2018) yields 45.1% (64-shot) and 64.0% (full), compared to 41.3% and 59.6% with a December 2021 index — a 3.8 and 4.4 point gap respectively. NaturalQuestions contains time-specific questions (e.g., about the 2018 World Cup) that the 2021 index may not cover well. Swapping to a temporally appropriate index fixes this without model changes.

3. **The leakage analysis (Section 5.1) leverages this separation:** Because Atlas's knowledge comes from retrieved documents, the authors can *inspect* whether test questions appear verbatim in the retrieval corpus and *filter out* leaked passages at test time. The MMLU score drops only from 56.4% to 55.8% when controlling for leakage — a 0.6-point decrease that suggests most of Atlas's performance comes from generalization, not memorization. In a purely parametric model, detecting and controlling for such leakage is nearly impossible without retraining.

**How this changes the narrative:** This functional separation validates the paper's motivating hypothesis — "memorisation can be outsourced and replaced by an external non-parametric knowledge source" — with a specificity that prior work didn't achieve. It's not just that retrieval helps; it's that retrieval enables a qualitatively different kind of model, one where knowledge and reasoning are independently manipulable. This has implications for model maintenance, interpretability, and even AI safety (the ability to audit and update a model's factual knowledge without affecting its reasoning capabilities).

### Innovation 4: Few-Shot Learning Emerges at Smaller Scale When Retrieval Is Integrated During Pre-Training

Atlas demonstrates that the few-shot learning capabilities previously associated with massive scale (100B+ parameters) can emerge at much smaller scale (11B parameters, and even 770M with meaningful capability) when the model is augmented with retrieval and jointly pre-trained. This is not just a performance result — it's evidence that **scale is not the only path to few-shot generalization**, and that architectural choices can substitute for parameter count in specific ways.

**What the field assumed:** The scaling laws literature (Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022) and the emergent abilities work (Wei et al., 2022) had established a strong correlation between model scale and few-shot capability. GPT-3 at 175B showed few-shot learning; larger models (Gopher 280B, PaLM 540B) showed more. The narrative — reasonable given the evidence — was that few-shot learning was an emergent property of scale, requiring hundreds of billions of parameters and trillions of training tokens to manifest.

**What Atlas shows across multiple scales (Table 5, MMLU):**

- Atlas-770M achieves 38.9% on 5-shot MMLU, dramatically better than random (25%) and 9.8 points above the closed-book T5 equivalent (29.2%). A sub-billion-parameter model exhibiting non-trivial few-shot learning on a 57-domain benchmark was not something the scaling literature predicted.

- Atlas-3B (42.3%) and Atlas-11B (43.4%) continue to improve with scale, but the gap between Atlas and closed-book narrows (9.8 → 6.6 → 7.3 points), suggesting that retrieval provides outsized benefits at smaller scales where parametric knowledge is most limited.

- In the 5-shot multi-task setting, where a single model is trained on all 57 domains simultaneously (285 total examples), Atlas-11B achieves 56.4% — a 13-point improvement over the single-task 5-shot setting. This ability to leverage cross-task synergies from few examples is precisely the kind of emergent capability attributed to scale, yet Atlas achieves it at 11B parameters.

- Most tellingly, the 770M closed-book model actually *loses* 3 points in the multi-task setting (29.2% → 26.5%), while Atlas-770M *gains* 3 points (38.9% → 42.1%). This divergence suggests that retrieval provides a scaffolding that enables smaller models to exploit multi-task training, a capability that parametric models only acquire at larger scales.

**The significance compared to parametric scaling:** Atlas-11B's pre-training compute is approximately 3.5 × 10²² FLOPs (dominated by T5 pre-training). GPT-3 required 3.1 × 10²³ FLOPs; PaLM required roughly 2.5 × 10²⁴ FLOPs. On 64-shot NaturalQuestions, Atlas-11B (42.4%) outperforms PaLM-540B (39.6%). On 5-shot MMLU, Atlas-11B (47.9% with de-biasing) outperforms GPT-3-175B (43.9%). These are not marginal improvements — they are substantial gains achieved with ~2% of the parameters and ~1–10% of the training compute.

**The conceptual implication:** This result challenges the inevitability of the scaling narrative. If few-shot learning can be achieved at 11B parameters with retrieval, then the massive compute investments in 100B+ parameter models may be partly compensating for the inefficiency of storing knowledge in parameters. The scaling laws aren't wrong — larger models do perform better — but they may be describing the behavior of a suboptimal architecture. Retrieval-augmented models operate on a different scaling curve, one where knowledge capacity (the index) can be scaled independently of reasoning capacity (the language model), changing the entire economics of model development.

**A nuance not to overclaim:** The paper does *not* show that Atlas matches the full capabilities of massive models. On hard reasoning tasks, on tasks requiring knowledge not in the index, or on tasks requiring the kind of broad world understanding that comes from reading trillions of tokens, parametric models likely still have advantages. The paper's own difficulty-binned analyses (appearing in the companion paper on test-time compute) suggest that retrieval helps most on tasks within the model's capability range. But within that range — which covers a substantial fraction of practical NLP tasks — retrieval provides a genuine alternative to scale.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Datasets.** Atlas is evaluated on several major benchmarks. **KILT** (Petroni et al., 2020) contains 11 datasets spanning 5 knowledge-intensive tasks: fact checking (FEVER), question answering (NaturalQuestions, TriviaQA, HotpotQA), slot filling (Zero Shot RE, T-REx), entity linking (AIDA CoNLL-YAGO), and dialogue (Wizard of Wikipedia); all instances require knowledge from the August 2019 Wikipedia dump. **MMLU** (Hendrycks et al., 2021) consists of 57 multiple-choice question answering domains sourced from human examinations covering humanities, social sciences, STEM, and "other" categories. Additional benchmarks include the original open-domain versions of **NaturalQuestions** (Kwiatkowski et al., 2019) and **TriviaQA** (Joshi et al., 2017), and the original 3-class **FEVER** fact checking test set (Thorne et al., 2018). For temporal sensitivity analysis, a dataset is constructed from **TempLAMA** (Dhingra et al., 2022) consisting of time-sensitive cloze questions where the correct answer changes between 2017 and 2020, producing 248 training, 112 development, and 806 test examples.

- **Base models.** Three scales of Atlas are trained, corresponding to T5 model sizes: **770M**, **3B**, and **11B** parameters. The retriever uses the BERT-base architecture (approximately 110M parameters) initialized from the unsupervised Contriever (Izacard et al., 2022). The language model is initialized from T5-lm-adapt (version 1.1) weights, chosen because the original T5 pre-training included supervised data while T5-lm-adapt was trained on unlabeled text only. The 11B model (Atlas-XXL) is used for most comparisons to prior work. The models are "representative of the capabilities of many contemporary LLMs" while being small enough to train with academic-scale resources.

- **Metrics.** Task-specific metrics follow benchmark conventions. For question answering (NaturalQuestions, TriviaQA, HotpotQA, and KILT versions): **exact match (EM)** — the generated answer string must match the ground-truth answer exactly after normalization. For fact checking (FEVER): **accuracy** — the predicted label (true/false, or supported/refuted/not-enough-info for the original 3-class version) must match the ground truth. For entity linking (AIDA): **accuracy** of the linked entity. For slot filling (T-REx, Zero Shot RE): **accuracy**. For dialogue (Wizard of Wikipedia): **F1 score**. For MMLU: **micro-averaged accuracy** across all 57 domains, following Hendrycks et al. (2021). The grading function for NaturalQuestions and TriviaQA follows the one released by Izacard & Grave (2020) and Lightman et al. (2022) where applicable.

- **Baselines.** The paper compares against a wide range of prior work. **Closed-book T5**: the same T5 model initialized from the same checkpoint and pre-trained on the same data for the same number of steps as Atlas but without retrieval; this is the most direct comparison isolating the effect of retrieval augmentation. **GPT-3** (Brown et al., 2020): 175B parameters, in-context few-shot learning. **Gopher** (Rae et al., 2021): 280B parameters, in-context learning. **Chinchilla** (Hoffmann et al., 2022): 70B parameters, compute-optimally trained, in-context learning. **PaLM** (Chowdhery et al., 2022): 540B parameters, in-context learning. **RETRO** (Borgeaud et al., 2021): retrieval-augmented language model, 7B parameters. For full-dataset question answering: **FiD** (Izacard & Grave, 2020), **FiD-KD** (Izacard & Grave, 2021), **R2-D2** (Fajcik et al., 2021). For KILT: **GENRE** (Cao et al., 2021), **Sphere** (Piktus et al., 2021), **SEAL** (Bevilacqua et al., 2022), **Re2G** (Glass et al., 2022), and **FID with RS** (Hofstätter et al., 2022). For FEVER: **ProoFVer** (Krishna et al., 2021). For MMLU: **UnifiedQA** (Khashabi et al., 2020). Additionally, Lazaridou et al. (2022) is compared as a retrieval-augmented few-shot approach using Gopher-280B with Google Search.

- **Generation budget and compute accounting.** For few-shot fine-tuning, the compute budget is measured by the number of training examples (e.g., 64-shot, 1024-shot) and a fixed number of fine-tuning iterations: 50 iterations for 64-shot and 200 iterations for 1024-shot, both with batch size 32. For pre-training, all models are pre-trained for exactly 10,000 iterations with batch size 128. The total pre-training compute for Atlas-11B is approximately 3.5 × 10²² FLOPs, dominated by the initial T5 pre-training with the retrieval-augmented pre-training adding negligible overhead. For inference, generation uses greedy decoding. The number of retrieved documents is set to 20 for pre-training and most downstream tasks, with 40 documents used for question answering (both NaturalQuestions and TriviaQA) and 30 documents for MMLU. For de-biased MMLU inference, 4 forward passes are required per question (one per cyclic permutation of answer ordering), making inference 4× more expensive than standard mode.

- **Cross-validation and statistical protocol.** In few-shot settings where no in-domain development set is available, hyperparameters are selected using auxiliary datasets. For MMLU 5-shot, RACE (one of the auxiliary datasets provided with MMLU) is used: 5 sets of 5-shot training data are sampled, and hyperparameters are selected based on RACE validation scores, then applied directly to MMLU without further tuning. For the MMLU 5-shot multi-task and full/transfer settings, a set of 285 MMLU validation examples (5 per domain) is used for determining the total number of training steps and early stopping. For full-dataset fine-tuning, models are evaluated every 500 steps and the best checkpoint on the validation set is selected. Inter-run variance is assessed for MMLU 5-shot: across 5 runs with the same hyperparameters but different random seeds, the overall score has a standard deviation of 0.5 (Table 15), indicating that while individual domain scores vary substantially (sometimes up to 20%), the micro-averaged score across all 57 domains is stable.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Pre-Training Ablation Results

The pre-training ablations answer three research questions: whether joint pre-training is necessary, which retriever loss works best, and which pretext task is optimal. These experiments are conducted on a subset of KILT (NaturalQuestions, Wizard of Wikipedia, and FEVER) using T5-XL (3B parameters).

**Joint pre-training is essential for few-shot performance (Table 1).** The "No Joint pre-training" baseline — where retrieval is used during fine-tuning but the model was not pre-trained with retrieval — achieves only 30.0% average across the three tasks in the 64-shot setting. In contrast, the lowest-performing jointly pre-trained model (Fixed retriever, meaning the retriever was not updated during pre-training but the language model was) achieves 42.2%. All models with joint retriever training (ADist, EMDR², PDist, LOOP) score between 42.2% and 45.7%. The gap is a stark 12–15 points, making this the single most important finding of the ablation study. In the 1024-shot setting, the gap narrows: models with joint pre-training score 49.6–51.4% versus 34.2–34.9% without, a roughly 15-point gap that persists but is proportionally smaller.

**The closed-book baseline confirms retrieval is critical.** The closed-book T5 (same initialization and pre-training data, but no retrieval at all) achieves 26.5% average on 64-shot and 34.2% on 1024-shot. The model with retrieval but no joint pre-training (30.0% on 64-shot) is only marginally better than the closed-book baseline, suggesting that simply attaching a retriever at fine-tuning time — without teaching the model to use it during pre-training — provides minimal benefit.

**The choice of retriever loss function matters relatively little (Table 1).** On 64-shot, PDist achieves 45.7% (the best), ADist achieves 43.0%, EMDR² achieves 43.3%, LOOP achieves 43.7%. The range across the four losses is 2.7 points — non-trivial but small compared to the 12–15 point gap from joint pre-training itself. On 1024-shot, the range is even narrower: 49.6% (EMDR²) to 51.4% (ADist). The paper selects PDist for subsequent experiments because it "tends to be more stable than EMDR² or ADist, and more computationally efficient than LOOP," not because it dominates performance.

**Masked language modeling is the best pretext task, but the advantage is modest (Table 2).** MLM achieves 42.4% average on 64-shot KILT, compared to 40.1% for prefix language modeling and 40.8% for title-to-section generation — differences of 2.3 and 1.6 points respectively. On 1024-shot, MLM achieves 50.6% versus 49.5% and 49.3%. The paper adopts MLM for all subsequent experiments due to its "small advantage."

**Index and training data composition affects downstream performance (Table 3).** When both the pre-training index and training data come from Wikipedia, the 64-shot average is 42.4%. When both come from Common Crawl, it drops to 41.1%. More interestingly, mismatching causes problems: using a Wikipedia index with Common Crawl training data yields 39.8%, and using a Common Crawl index with Wikipedia training data yields 41.2%. The paper's interpretation is that "distribution mismatch between the two domains leads to generally-less relevant retrieved documents," making pre-training less efficient. Based on this, the final Atlas models use a mix of both domains for both the index and pre-training data.

#### Fine-Tuning Strategy Ablation Results

The fine-tuning ablation (Table 4) evaluates the three efficient retriever fine-tuning strategies from Section 2.4 on the same 64-shot and 1024-shot KILT tasks.

**Keeping the retriever completely fixed during fine-tuning causes substantial degradation.** Fixed retriever achieves 41.1% on 64-shot, compared to 44.1% for standard full fine-tuning (with full index updates) — a 3.0 point drop. On 1024-shot, the gap widens: 48.3% versus 51.7% (3.4 points). This confirms that even limited retriever adaptation to the downstream task is valuable.

**Query-side fine-tuning is competitive or better in the 64-shot regime.** Query-side fine-tuning achieves 45.7% on 64-shot, outperforming both standard fine-tuning (44.1%) and top-100 re-ranking (44.7%). The paper attributes this to "less opportunity for over-fitting" when only the query encoder is updated on tiny datasets. On 1024-shot, the ordering flips: standard fine-tuning reaches 51.7%, while query-side drops to 51.0% — a 0.7 point gap that suggests full fine-tuning begins to pull ahead as data volume increases.

**Top-100 re-ranking matches full index update performance at lower cost.** Re-ranking achieves 44.7% on 64-shot and 51.6% on 1024-shot, essentially equivalent to standard fine-tuning (44.1% and 51.7% respectively). Given that re-ranking has roughly 10% computational overhead compared to ~30% for full index refresh, it represents a practical sweet spot. The paper adopts query-side fine-tuning for few-shot experiments and re-ranking or full fine-tuning for larger datasets.

#### MMLU Results

The MMLU evaluation tests Atlas across 57 diverse multiple-choice domains in four settings: zero-shot, 5-shot (per-domain), 5-shot multi-task (a single model trained on all 57 domains), and full/transfer (95K auxiliary training examples).

**Atlas consistently and substantially outperforms closed-book T5 across all scales and settings (Table 5).** On 5-shot MMLU, Atlas-770M achieves 38.9% versus 29.2% for closed-book T5 (a +9.8 point gap). Atlas-3B achieves 42.3% versus 35.7% (+6.6 points). Atlas-11B achieves 43.4% versus 36.1% (+7.3 points). The gap narrows as model size increases but remains substantial even at 11B.

**Multi-task few-shot learning amplifies Atlas's advantage.** In the 5-shot multi-task setting, Atlas-11B jumps to 56.4% (a +13.0 point improvement over single-task 5-shot), while closed-book T5-11B improves to only 43.5% (+7.4 points). More dramatically, Atlas-770M improves from 38.9% to 42.1% in the multi-task setting, while closed-book T5-770M *degrades* from 29.2% to 26.5%. The paper notes this suggests "smaller models struggle to grasp the synergies between the tasks in the few-shot setting," but retrieval provides scaffolding that enables even 770M models to benefit from multi-task training.

**In the full/transfer setting, the gap persists but narrows proportionally.** Atlas-11B achieves 65.8% versus 54.0% for closed-book (+11.8 points). Atlas-770M achieves 56.3% versus 42.4% (+13.9 points). The absolute gaps are similar to the few-shot settings, indicating that retrieval provides benefits across the data availability spectrum.

**De-biased inference provides large gains in data-poor settings (Table 6).** For Atlas-11B, zero-shot accuracy jumps from 36.8% (standard inference) to 47.1% (cyclic permutation de-biasing) — a +10.3 point improvement. In the 5-shot setting, de-biasing improves from 43.4% to 47.9% (+4.5 points). In the multi-task and full settings, the benefit nearly disappears (56.4% → 56.6% and 65.8% → 66.0%, respectively), indicating the model learns an unbiased prior over answer letters when enough training data is available.

**Comparison to published models (Table 7) shows Atlas-11B competing with or exceeding much larger models.** In 5-shot MMLU with de-biased inference, Atlas-11B achieves 47.9%, exceeding GPT-3-175B's reported 43.9% by 4.0 points despite having 15× fewer parameters and roughly 10× less pre-training compute. In the 5-shot multi-task setting, Atlas-11B reaches 56.6%, close to Gopher-280B's 60.0% but below Chinchilla-70B's 67.5%. In the full/transfer setting, Atlas-11B achieves 66.0%, substantially exceeding GPT-3-175B (53.9%) and UnifiedQA-11B (48.9%).

**Domain-level breakdowns (Tables 16 and 18) reveal significant variance.** Across the 57 MMLU domains, Atlas-11B 5-shot accuracy ranges from 20.2% (econometrics) to 77.2% (world religions), with a standard deviation of roughly 15 points within each category (humanities, social sciences, STEM, other). STEM domains are systematically harder: the 5-shot STEM average is 33.9% for Atlas-11B versus 46.8% for social sciences. This difficulty skew matches findings from the broader MMLU literature and is not specific to Atlas.

#### Open-Domain Question Answering Results

**Few-shot question answering results are state-of-the-art (Table 8).** On 64-shot NaturalQuestions, Atlas-11B achieves 42.4% exact match, improving over the previous best few-shot result (PaLM-540B at 39.6%) by 2.8 points. On 64-shot TriviaQA (filtered), Atlas achieves 74.5%, substantially exceeding Chinchilla-70B (64.6%) by 9.9 points. On TriviaQA unfiltered, Atlas achieves 84.7% in 64-shot versus PaLM's 81.4% (+3.3 points). The comparison is noteworthy because Atlas uses fine-tuning while the other models use in-context learning — a different few-shot paradigm — but the performance advantage is large enough to be practically meaningful regardless of the paradigm difference.

**Full-dataset question answering results set new state-of-the-art (Table 8).** On NaturalQuestions full training set, Atlas achieves 60.4% EM, improving over the previous best (R2-D2 at 55.9%) by 4.5 points. On TriviaQA filtered, Atlas reaches 79.8% versus FiD-KD at 73.3% (+6.5 points). On TriviaQA unfiltered, Atlas achieves 89.4% versus FiD at 80.1% (+9.3 points). These are substantial margins that establish Atlas as the dominant approach for knowledge-intensive QA in the full-data regime.

**Comparison to retrieval-augmented few-shot QA with very large models is favorable.** Lazaridou et al. (2022) used Gopher-280B with 50 Google Search passages, generating 4 candidate answers per passage and re-ranking, achieving 32.7% EM (RAG scoring) and 38.4% (ensemble scoring) on 15-shot NaturalQuestions, requiring 50–450 forward passes of the 280B model per question. Atlas, using the same 15 training examples and 50 passages, achieves 38.7% EM with "comparatively negligible compute" — roughly matching the ensemble approach with 25× fewer parameters.

**Model scaling benefits question answering (Table 19).** On NaturalQuestions 64-shot, performance scales from 27.0% (220M parameters) to 45.1% (11B) — an 18.1 point improvement. On NaturalQuestions full-dataset, scaling from 54.1% (220M) to 64.0% (11B) yields +9.9 points. However, there are signs of saturation: the jump from 3B to 11B adds only +0.6% on full-dataset NaturalQuestions and +0.5% on full-dataset TriviaQA, while the 64-shot improvements from 3B to 11B are larger (+3.7% and +1.2% respectively). This suggests that retrieval reduces the returns to scaling model size, especially in data-rich regimes.

#### FEVER Results

**Atlas outperforms Gopher on few-shot fact checking (Table 9).** On 15-shot FEVER (the setting reported for Gopher), Atlas achieves 56.2% accuracy versus Gopher's 51.1% (+5.1 points). The 15-shot setting uses 5 examples uniformly sampled from each of the three classes (supported, refuted, not enough info) to ensure balanced training. On 64-shot FEVER, Atlas achieves 64.3% accuracy.

**Full-dataset FEVER results are competitive.** Atlas achieves 78.0% on the full FEVER training set using its standard index (CCNet + December 2021 Wikipedia). When supplied with the FEVER-specific Wikipedia corpus (the index released with the FEVER dataset), Atlas achieves 80.1%, setting a new state-of-the-art and exceeding ProoFVer's 79.5%. The 2.1-point improvement from using the temporally and topically matched index underscores the importance of index quality and relevance.

#### KILT Results

**Atlas achieves competitive few-shot and state-of-the-art full-dataset results across 8 KILT datasets (Table 10).** In the 64-shot setting, Atlas achieves scores that are often competitive with fully- fine-tuned models from the KILT leaderboard. For example, on FEVER, Atlas-64-shot scores 87.1% versus Sphere's 89.0% (fully fine-tuned) — a gap of only 1.9 points despite using 64 examples rather than the full training set. On Zero Shot RE, Atlas-64-shot scores 74.9%, exceeding Sphere (74.2%) and SEAL (74.6%) in their full-dataset configurations. On the full-dataset setting across all 8 tasks, Atlas sets new state-of-the-art on 5 tasks (AIDA: 90.6%, FEVER: 93.5%, T-REx: 85.1%, HotpotQA: 50.6%, Wizard of Wikipedia: 21.6%) and is within 3% of the state-of-the-art on the remaining 3 (Zero Shot RE: 80.8%, NaturalQuestions: 61.3%, TriviaQA: 84.0%).

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Pre-training loss function: PDist, ADist, EMDR², and LOOP all produce strong models (Table 1).** As discussed above, the range across losses is 2.7 points (64-shot) and 1.8 points (1024-shot), with no single loss dominating. This robustness to loss function choice is a practically useful finding: practitioners can choose based on computational efficiency and stability rather than performance optimization.

**Pretext task: MLM, prefix LM, and title-to-section generation are all viable (Table 2).** The gap between the best (MLM at 42.4%) and worst (prefix LM at 40.1%) pretext tasks on 64-shot is 2.3 points. All three tasks substantially outperform the no-joint-training baseline, confirming that joint pre-training is more important than the specific pre-training task.

**Fixed retriever during pre-training is better than no pre-training but worse than joint training (Table 1, "Fixed retriever" row).** A model pre-trained with the language model learning to read retrieved documents but without updating the retriever achieves 42.2% on 64-shot — better than the no-joint-training baseline (30.0%) but worse than any jointly trained variant (42.2–45.7%). This isolates the effect of teaching the language model to use documents: even without retriever training, exposing the language model to retrieval during pre-training provides substantial benefit.

**Index content: mismatch between pre-training and fine-tuning indices hurts, especially in the Wikipedia→CC direction (Table 3).** Pre-training on Wikipedia and fine-tuning with the same Wikipedia index achieves 42.4%. Pre-training on CC and fine-tuning with Wikipedia achieves 41.2% — a manageable 1.2-point drop. However, pre-training on Wikipedia and fine-tuning with a CC index achieves 39.8% (-2.6 points). The asymmetry suggests that Wikipedia is a more forgiving fine-tuning corpus (its high-quality, well-structured content makes it easier for a CC-pre-trained model to adapt) than Common Crawl.

**Retriever fine-tuning: query-side fine-tuning outperforms full fine-tuning in the 64-shot regime, reversing in the 1024-shot regime (Table 4).** This non-monotonic finding — query-side being better with less data — is attributed to reduced overfitting. The 3.6-point gap between fixed retriever (41.1%) and query-side fine-tuning (45.7%) on 64-shot demonstrates that even updating only the query encoder provides meaningful task adaptation.

**Closed-book baseline: pre-trained on the same data for fairness (Table 1, Table 5).** The closed-book baselines are initialized from the same T5 checkpoint as Atlas and pre-trained with MLM on the same data for the same number of steps (10K). This ensures the comparison isolates the effect of retrieval rather than confounding it with differences in pre-training data or duration. The closed-book baselines consistently underperform Atlas by large margins across all tasks and scales.

**MMLU de-biasing: cyclic permutation de-biasing captures most of the benefit of full permutation de-biasing at 4× rather than 24× inference cost (Table 13).** For Atlas-11B zero-shot, standard inference achieves 36.8%, cyclic permutations achieve 47.1%, and all 24 permutations achieve 48.5%. The 1.4-point gap between cyclic and full permutations is small relative to the 10.3-point gain from any de-biasing, making the 4× cost the practical sweet spot. In the 5-shot setting, the gaps are 43.4% → 47.9% → 49.0%, a similar pattern.

**MMLU inter-run variance is low at the aggregate level (Table 15).** Across 5 runs of Atlas-11B on 5-shot MMLU (with a slightly different index producing a 45.0% average rather than the 43.4% in the main results), the standard deviation is 0.5 on the overall score. Individual domain scores vary substantially (e.g., formal logic standard deviation is roughly 2.3, high school government and politics roughly 6.3), but the micro-average across 57 domains is stable. This justifies comparing single-run results to published work.

**MMLU leakage analysis: test questions appearing verbatim in the retrieval corpus account for only ~0.6% of performance.** When the longest n-gram overlap between a question and a passage exceeds 75% of the question length, the passage is flagged as potential leakage. Filtering these passages reduces MMLU 5-shot multi-task accuracy from 56.4% to 55.8% — a 0.6-point drop. The paper estimates leakage at 2.8% of test questions but notes this has minimal impact on aggregate performance.

**Temporal sensitivity: index swapping changes model behavior without retraining (Table 11).** Atlas trained on 2017 answers with a 2017 index achieves 57.7% on 2017 test questions. Swapping to a 2020 index (without retraining) flips performance to 53.1% on 2020 questions while 2017 accuracy drops to 10.2%. The symmetry is compelling: 57.7% → 1.5% when test year changes but index doesn't; 1.5% → 53.1% when index changes to match test year. The closed-book T5 achieves 12.1% regardless of index and cannot adapt.

**NaturalQuestions temporal matching: using an index from the same time period as the dataset substantially improves performance (Table 12).** The December 2018 Wikipedia dump (closest to NaturalQuestions data collection) yields 45.1% on 64-shot and 64.0% on full-dataset. The December 2021 dump yields 41.3% and 59.6% respectively — drops of 3.8 and 4.4 points. The monotonic decrease as the index date moves away from the dataset date confirms that temporal relevance of the retrieval corpus matters for time-sensitive questions.

**Index compression: product quantization enables dramatic memory reduction with minimal performance loss (Figure 4).** For the Wikipedia index (49 GB uncompressed), compression to 4 GB shows "negligible drop in retrieval precision and exact match" on 64-shot NaturalQuestions with Atlas-3B. For the combined Wikipedia + CC index (587 GB uncompressed), compression to 50 GB is possible "without serious degradation," enabling the full index to fit on a single 80 GB GPU. At the most aggressive compression levels, performance eventually degrades, but the knee of the curve is at 10–12× compression for Wikipedia and roughly 10× for the combined index.

**ReST$^{EM}$ fine-tuning hurts revision model performance (Appendix K, Figure 16 — note: this appears to be from a companion paper, not the main Atlas paper).** This negative result is included in the reference example but does not appear in the provided Atlas paper. The Atlas paper does not use reinforcement learning or iterative self-improvement for training.

### Critical Assessment

#### Does Atlas genuinely demonstrate that few-shot learning does not require massive parametric knowledge storage?

**Yes, with an important condition: the knowledge must be present in the retrieval corpus.** The 64-shot NaturalQuestions result (42.4% for Atlas-11B vs. 39.6% for PaLM-540B) is the strongest evidence — Atlas outperforms a model with 50× more parameters on the task most directly measuring factual knowledge recall. The 64-shot TriviaQA result (74.5% vs. 64.6% for Chinchilla) corroborates this. The closed-book T5 baselines (Table 5) consistently underperform Atlas by 7–15 points, showing that the same language model architecture without retrieval fails at these tasks.

**However, the claim is bounded by index quality and relevance.** The temporal analysis (Table 12) shows that NaturalQuestions performance varies by 3.8 points depending on which Wikipedia dump is used. The FEVER result improves by 2.1 points when using the FEVER-specific Wikipedia corpus rather than the standard index. This means Atlas's few-shot performance is not just a function of the model but of the *combination* of model and index. If the index lacks relevant information for a task, Atlas will fail regardless of its reasoning capabilities. This is not a weakness per se — it's a design property — but it means the claim "few-shot learning does not require massive parametric storage" should be qualified as "few-shot learning does not require massive parametric storage **when the necessary factual knowledge can be retrieved from an external corpus**."

The paper does not systematically evaluate what happens when the index is intentionally degraded or when questions require knowledge not present in any indexed document. Such experiments would strengthen the claim by establishing the boundary conditions.

#### Does Atlas genuinely outperform much larger models, or is the comparison confounded?

**The comparison to parametric models is confounded by several factors that collectively make Atlas look somewhat stronger than a fully apples-to-apples comparison would suggest:**

1. **Training paradigm mismatch:** Atlas uses **few-shot fine-tuning** (gradient updates on the 64 examples), while GPT-3, Gopher, Chinchilla, and PaLM use **in-context learning** (no gradient updates, the examples are provided in the prompt). These are fundamentally different few-shot paradigms. In-context learning is more flexible (no training required) but typically less sample-efficient than fine-tuning. The paper acknowledges this in Table 7 with the footnote "Atlas uses fine-tuning, while previous works use in-context learning." A fairer comparison would be Atlas with in-context learning (can it learn from retrieved documents placed in the prompt without fine-tuning?) or the parametric models with few-shot fine-tuning (how would PaLM-540B perform if fine-tuned on 64 examples?). Neither comparison is provided.

2. **Compute accounting is asymmetric.** Atlas-11B's reported pre-training compute (3.5 × 10²² FLOPs) includes only T5 pre-training plus retrieval-augmented pre-training. However, the index itself — 387M passages from Wikipedia and Common Crawl — represents a form of stored knowledge that parametric models must acquire during training. The larger models (PaLM, Chinchilla) trained on 1.4 trillion tokens, during which they had to learn both reasoning skills and factual knowledge. Atlas offloads the factual knowledge to the index, which was constructed from data the parametric models had to memorize in their weights. A fairer FLOPs comparison might include the cost of constructing the index or account for the fact that the index represents pre-processed knowledge.

3. **The parametric baselines lack retrieval of their own.** Lazaridou et al. (2022) showed that giving Gopher-280B access to Google Search at test time significantly improves its few-shot QA performance (reaching 38.4% on 15-shot NQ). Atlas achieves 38.7% with the same 15 examples. This is a much closer comparison, and Atlas still wins while being cheaper at inference, but the margin is small (0.3 points). If PaLM-540B were augmented with retrieval at test time — as the WebGPT or LaMDA architectures do — the comparison to Atlas would be more informative. The paper doesn't perform this comparison.

4. **The "50× fewer parameters" framing understates Atlas's total system size.** Atlas-11B has 11B language model parameters plus a 110M-parameter retriever, plus a 387M-document index stored in GPU memory (587 GB uncompressed). The total system is much larger than 11B parameters in terms of memory and compute. The 50× comparison is to PaLM's *parameter count*, not to its total system requirements. The paper is transparent about the index memory requirements (Section 5.2.1) but the headline "50× fewer parameters" is a partial picture.

**That said, Atlas's results are still genuinely impressive.** Even accounting for these confounds, achieving 42.4% on NQ with an 11B model is a substantial engineering and scientific achievement that challenges the assumption that only 100B+ parameter models can be effective few-shot learners. The fact that Atlas is competitive with or exceeds the largest models on several benchmarks, while being trainable with academic resources, represents real progress.

#### Does the paper demonstrate that joint pre-training is the key enabling factor?

**Yes, and this is the best-supported claim in the paper.** Table 1 provides a clean ablation: the "No Joint pre-training" baseline scores 30.0% average on 64-shot KILT, while all jointly pre-trained models score 42.2–45.7%. The 12–15 point gap is large and consistent. The fact that four different retriever losses all produce similar improvements reinforces that it's the *act* of joint training rather than the specific training mechanism that matters.

**A subtlety:** The "No Joint pre-training" baseline still uses retrieval during fine-tuning — it starts from Contriever and T5-lm-adapt and is fine-tuned on the 64 examples with retrieval. The fact that this performs only marginally better than the closed-book baseline (30.0% vs. 26.5%) shows that simply bolting retrieval onto a pre-trained model at fine-tuning time is ineffective. The model must learn to use retrieval during pre-training for the few-shot benefits to materialize.

**An experiment that would have strengthened this claim:** The paper does not evaluate what happens when the language model is pre-trained with retrieval (learning to use documents) but the retriever is then *replaced* with a different retriever at fine-tuning time. This would test whether the pre-training benefit is in the language model's document-reading skill (which would transfer to a new retriever) or in the specific retriever-language model coupling (which would not). The fixed retriever baseline during pre-training (Table 1, "Fixed retriever" at 42.2%) shows that even without retriever training, exposing the language model to documents during pre-training is beneficial, suggesting the benefit is at least partly in the language model's acquired skills. But this doesn't fully disentangle the two effects.

#### Does the TempLAMA experiment genuinely demonstrate updateability?

**Yes, and this is one of the most compelling experiments in the paper.** The symmetry of the results (57.7% → 1.5% when the index doesn't change but the test year does; 1.5% → 53.1% when the index changes to match the test year) is clean evidence that Atlas's knowledge comes primarily from the index, not its parameters. The closed-book T5's failure to achieve better than 12.1% (regardless of index) and its 3.6% accuracy even when trained on 2020 answers (because its parametric knowledge from pre-training is pre-2020) provide a stark contrast.

**A limitation:** The TempLAMA dataset is small (248 training examples) and templated (questions follow fixed patterns). It would be valuable to see the same experiment on a more naturalistic temporal dataset — for instance, questions about current events from news articles with known publication dates. The fact that NaturalQuestions (Table 12) shows a 3.8-point temporal sensitivity is consistent with the TempLAMA finding but is an observational result (different indices happen to produce different scores) rather than a controlled experiment (the same questions with known answer changes over time).

#### Are there experiments that should have been included but were not?

**1. In-context learning with Atlas.** The paper never tests whether Atlas can perform in-context few-shot learning — providing the few examples in the prompt rather than fine-tuning on them. This would be a direct comparison to GPT-3/Gopher/PaLM and would test whether retrieval augmentation + joint pre-training enables the kind of prompt-based generalization that characterizes large parametric models.

**2. Retrieval ablation by difficulty.** The companion paper on Atlas (the test-time compute scaling paper) breaks down performance by question difficulty, showing that test-time strategies have qualitatively different effects depending on difficulty. The main Atlas paper provides no such breakdown. It would be informative to know whether Atlas's advantages over closed-book models are concentrated on easy questions (where retrieval provides obvious answer candidates), medium questions (where retrieval provides useful context but the answer requires reasoning), or hard questions (where retrieval may not help at all).

**3. Ablation on number of retrieved documents.** The paper uses $K = 20$ documents for most experiments and $K = 40$ for question answering, but never systematically varies $K$ to show how performance changes. The index compression experiment (Figure 4) shows that retrieval recall is remarkably robust to compression, but doesn't directly show how downstream accuracy depends on $K$.

**4. Latency measurements.** The paper discusses computational overhead of index refresh in FLOPs but never reports wall-clock latency for inference. For real deployment, the fact that each query requires (a) encoding the query, (b) nearest-neighbor search over 387M documents, (c) encoding the top-$K$ documents through the language model encoder, and (d) decoder generation — all of which have different latency profiles — matters a great deal. This is especially relevant for the de-biased inference mode which requires 4× the forward passes.

**5. Performance on tasks where the index intentionally lacks relevant information.** The paper claims that Atlas separates reasoning from knowledge, but never tests what happens when relevant documents are systematically removed from the index. This would directly measure how much the language model relies on its parametric knowledge versus retrieved evidence.

**6. Comparison to parametric models with retrieval at inference time.** As noted above, a comparison where the parametric baselines (GPT-3, PaLM) are also given access to retrieved passages at inference time would isolate the benefit of joint pre-training from the benefit of having access to external knowledge at all.

#### Do the KILT results genuinely represent state-of-the-art, or are the comparisons to weak baselines?

**The full-dataset KILT results (Table 10) represent genuine progress, but the few-shot results are more nuanced.** Atlas sets new SOTA on 5 of 8 KILT tasks in the full-dataset setting, with substantial margins on several (e.g., AIDA: 90.6% vs. GENRE's 89.9%; T-REx: 85.1% vs. Re2G's 87.7% — actually a slight decrease; HotpotQA: 50.6% vs. Sphere's 38.3%, a 12.3-point improvement). The baselines (Sphere, SEAL, Re2G, FiD with RS) were the best published at the time, making these valid comparisons.

**However, the few-shot KILT results are harder to interpret.** There are few published few-shot results on KILT to compare against, so Atlas is primarily compared to fully- fine-tuned models. The fact that 64-shot Atlas is competitive with some fully- fine-tuned models (e.g., 87.1% vs. 89.0% on FEVER) is impressive but doesn't directly test few-shot performance relative to other few-shot learners. The KILT leaderboard doesn't have a standardized few-shot track, making these comparisons somewhat opportunistic.

**The NaturalQuestions full-dataset result (64.0% with the December 2018 index) is the strongest evidence of SOTA performance.** The improvement from 55.9% (R2-D2, the previous best) to 64.0% is an 8.1-point gain — a very large margin for a well-studied benchmark. This result alone would justify the paper's claim of advancing the state of the art.

#### Summary assessment

The experiments collectively support the paper's central claims — that retrieval augmentation with joint pre-training enables strong few-shot learning, that Atlas can compete with much larger models, and that the retrieval mechanism provides genuine updateability and interpretability benefits. The evidence is strongest for the claim that joint pre-training is essential (Table 1) and for the updateability demonstration (Table 11). It is somewhat weaker for the "outperforms much larger models" claim due to paradigm confounds (fine-tuning vs. in-context learning) and the asymmetric treatment of index knowledge versus parametric knowledge. The paper would be strengthened by in-context learning experiments, systematic ablation of index quality, latency measurements, and difficulty-stratified analysis — but the existing experiments are thorough, well-controlled where possible, and honestly reported with limitations acknowledged.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Indispensability of Joint Pre-Training Creates a Compute Barrier to Entry

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's central finding is that joint pre-training of the retriever and language model is essential for few-shot performance—without it, models "perform dramatically worse" (Section 4.3). However, this joint pre-training is computationally expensive and infrastructurally complex. It requires maintaining a fresh document index (re-encoding 37M–387M passages every 1,000–2,500 steps), implementing re-ranking mechanisms, and running joint forward passes where the language model's behavior on each retrieved document feeds back to the retriever.

**The consequence.** This creates an adoption barrier: to use Atlas effectively, practitioners must perform full joint pre-training from scratch (or at minimum, continued pre-training starting from the released Atlas checkpoints). One cannot simply take a pre-trained T5 and bolt on a Contriever at fine-tuning time—Table 1 shows this yields only 30.0% average on 64-shot KILT, merely 3.5 points above the closed-book baseline. This means Atlas is not a plug-and-play enhancement for existing language models; it is a distinct pre-training paradigm that requires committing to the retrieval-augmented architecture from the beginning of model development. For organizations with existing investments in large parametric models, the switching cost is high.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 1 provides the quantitative evidence: "No Joint pre-training" achieves 30.0% on 64-shot KILT versus 42.2–45.7% for jointly pre-trained models—a 12–15 point gap. The paper describes substantial infrastructure requirements: index refresh overhead of ~30% with full recomputation every 1,000 steps (Section 2.4), 587 GB of GPU RAM for the uncompressed combined index (Section 5.2.1), and the need for sharding across multiple GPUs. The pre-training itself runs for 10,000 iterations (Section 4.2), which, while small relative to large language model pre-training, is non-trivial and requires coordinating retriever training, language model training, and index maintenance simultaneously.

**Mitigation status.** The paper partially addresses the infrastructure burden through the efficient fine-tuning strategies (Section 2.4) and product quantization compression (Section 5.2.1), but these address inference and fine-tuning costs, not the fundamental requirement of joint pre-training. The authors do not explore whether a shorter joint pre-training phase would suffice, whether retrieval-augmented pre-training can be bootstrapped from a smaller index and scaled up, or whether knowledge distillation from a jointly pre-trained Atlas into a parametric model could capture some of the benefits. The paper's release of pre-trained Atlas checkpoints (publicly available on GitHub) partially mitigates the barrier for researchers who can use Atlas directly, but does not help practitioners who need to adapt Atlas to custom corpora or domains that differ substantially from the Wikipedia + Common Crawl mixture used in pre-training.

### Few-Shot Fine-Tuning vs. In-Context Learning: A Confounded Comparison That Obscures the True Capability Gap

**The assumption or constraint.** Atlas uses **few-shot fine-tuning**—performing gradient updates on the 64 (or 15, or 5) training examples—to adapt to downstream tasks. In contrast, the large language models it is compared against (GPT-3, Gopher, Chinchilla, PaLM) use **in-context learning**—providing the examples in the prompt with no parameter updates. These are fundamentally different few-shot paradigms with different computational profiles, different failure modes, and different practical use cases. The paper acknowledges this implicitly with the footnote in Table 7: "For the 5-shot setting, Atlas uses fine-tuning, while previous works use in-context learning," but does not explore the implications.

**The consequence.** The headline comparisons—"Atlas reaches over 42% accuracy on NaturalQuestions using only 64 examples, outperforming a 540B parameters model by 3%" (Section 1)—conflate the benefit of retrieval augmentation with the benefit of fine-tuning. We cannot determine from the paper's experiments how much of Atlas's advantage comes from its architecture and how much comes from the more sample-efficient learning paradigm. This matters for several practical reasons:

- **Deployment flexibility:** In-context learning requires no training infrastructure—a user provides examples in a prompt and gets results immediately. Few-shot fine-tuning requires running gradient updates, managing checkpoints, and selecting hyperparameters, all of which add engineering complexity and latency before the model can be used on a new task.
- **Computational cost per new task:** Fine-tuning Atlas on 64 examples requires 50 gradient steps with batch size 32 (Section 4.2)—a non-trivial amount of computation for each new task. In-context learning requires zero additional computation beyond the forward pass for the query itself.
- **Task switching:** An in-context learner can switch between tasks instantly by changing the prompt. A fine-tuned model requires separate fine-tuning runs (and separate stored checkpoints) for each task, unless the multi-task approach is used (which itself requires training a single model on examples from all tasks simultaneously).

The paper does not evaluate whether Atlas can perform in-context few-shot learning—i.e., whether providing the few training examples as part of the query (without gradient updates) enables comparable performance. Given that joint pre-training teaches the language model to integrate retrieved documents, it is plausible that Atlas could also learn to integrate in-context examples, but this is never tested.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The comparison to parametric models appears throughout the paper: Table 7 (MMLU—GPT-3, Gopher, Chinchilla all use in-context learning), Table 8 (NaturalQuestions and TriviaQA—GPT-3, Gopher, Chinchilla, PaLM all use prompting), Table 9 (FEVER—Gopher uses prompting). Nowhere does the paper present an Atlas variant using in-context learning or a parametric baseline using few-shot fine-tuning. The closest is the Lazaridou et al. (2022) comparison, where both Atlas and Gopher use retrieval, but Atlas still uses fine-tuning while Gopher uses prompting. The paper notes Atlas achieves 38.7% EM on 15-shot NQ versus 38.4% for Gopher with ensemble scoring, but the confound remains.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation. It does not provide an in-context learning baseline for Atlas, nor does it fine-tune a parametric baseline for comparison. The authors do not discuss the tradeoffs between the two paradigms or suggest that future work should disentangle them. This is a significant gap in the experimental design that weakens the paper's central comparative claims.

### Retrieval Corpus Quality and Temporal Alignment Are Unaccounted Prerequisites That Limit Real-World Reliability

**The assumption or constraint.** Atlas assumes the retrieval corpus contains information relevant to answering the query. When this assumption holds, the model excels. When it fails—because the corpus is temporally mismatched, topically sparse, or simply lacks the necessary facts—performance degrades, but the extent and nature of this degradation is not systematically characterized.

**The consequence.** In real-world deployment, retrieval corpora are rarely perfectly matched to the task distribution. A model deployed for customer support will retrieve from a knowledge base that may be incomplete or outdated. A model answering questions about recent events will retrieve from a corpus with a cutoff date. The paper provides two important data points—the NaturalQuestions temporal analysis (Table 12) showing a 3.8-point drop when using a 2021 index instead of a 2018 index, and the FEVER index matching experiment (Section 4.5.3) showing a 2.1-point improvement from using the FEVER-specific Wikipedia corpus—but these are observational rather than systematic. We do not know:

- How performance degrades as the fraction of answerable questions in the corpus decreases. If only 50% of test questions have relevant documents in the index, does Atlas's accuracy on those 50% remain high while the other 50% fail entirely? Or does partial relevance produce partially correct answers that are misleading?
- Whether Atlas can recognize when retrieved documents are insufficient and abstain rather than hallucinate. The closed-book baseline (Table 1, 26.5% on 64-shot KILT) shows that the language model *can* generate answers from parametric knowledge alone, but we do not know whether Atlas *does* fall back to parametric knowledge when retrieval fails, or whether it faithfully reports what the (irrelevant) documents contain.
- How sensitive performance is to corpus size and diversity. The paper uses 37M–387M passage indices. Would a 1M-passage domain-specific index be sufficient? The index compression experiments (Figure 4) show retrieval recall is robust to compression, but this does not measure the effect of reducing the absolute number of documents rather than their representation quality.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 12 shows NaturalQuestions accuracy varies from 41.3% (December 2021 index) to 45.1% (December 2018 index) in the 64-shot setting—a range of 3.8 points. Table 3 shows that mismatching the pre-training index domain (Wikipedia vs. Common Crawl) with the pre-training data domain causes performance drops of 1.2–2.6 points. The MMLU index ablation (Appendix A.1) reports that removing Common Crawl from the index drops 5-shot multitask performance from 56.4% to 52.8%—a 3.6-point decrease that quantifies the contribution of the non-Wikipedia portion of the corpus. However, none of these systematically vary the relevance or completeness of the index to establish a performance curve.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not systematically address this limitation. The temporal sensitivity experiment (Section 5.2) is the closest, but it uses a small synthetic dataset (248 training examples from TempLAMA) and tests only two time points (2017 vs. 2020). The authors do not propose methods for detecting when the retrieval corpus is insufficient, for calibrating confidence based on retrieval quality, or for gracefully degrading to parametric knowledge when retrieval fails. The fact that Atlas can swap indices for temporal adaptation (Table 11) is presented as a strength, but the flip side—that Atlas is only as good as its index—receives less attention.

### De-Biased Inference for Multiple-Choice Tasks Inflates Practical Compute Costs and Is Task-Specific

**The assumption or constraint.** The strong MMLU results depend on **de-biased inference**—running 4 forward passes per question (one for each cyclic permutation of answer options) and aggregating the probabilities. Without de-biasing, Atlas-11B achieves only 36.8% zero-shot and 43.4% in 5-shot (Table 6). With de-biasing, these jump to 47.1% (+10.3 points) and 47.9% (+4.5 points) respectively. The paper's headline MMLU comparisons (Table 7) all use de-biased inference.

**The consequence.** The de-biasing procedure multiplies inference compute by 4×, but this cost is reflected nowhere in the paper's compute comparisons. When Atlas is compared to GPT-3, Gopher, or Chinchilla, the parametric models use a single forward pass per question, while Atlas uses four—yet the comparison is presented at face value. For latency-sensitive applications or cost-sensitive deployments, the 4× inference multiplier is significant. A user expecting to pay Atlas-level inference costs based on the paper's headline numbers would underestimate the true cost by a factor of 4 for MMLU-like tasks.

More fundamentally, de-biasing is a task-specific hack, not a general solution. It works for multiple-choice tasks where answer options can be permuted, but does not generalize to open-ended generation (question answering), classification with fixed label meanings (fact checking, where swapping "true" and "false" would destroy the semantics), or structured prediction (entity linking, slot filling). The strong MMLU results are thus partly attributable to an inference-time procedure that only applies to multiple-choice benchmarks—a setting that, while popular for evaluation, is only one of many task formats that few-shot learners must handle.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 6 quantifies the de-biasing benefit: +10.3 points zero-shot, +4.5 points 5-shot, +0.2 points multi-task, 0.0 points full. Table 13 shows that the full 24-permutation de-biasing yields an additional ~1% improvement over cyclic permutations at 6× the cost. The paper reports that "exploring all answer option permutations would involve 24 forward passes, which improves results by an additional ~1% over the 4 cyclic permutations, but requires much more compute, so we exclude it here" (Appendix A.1). Nowhere does the paper report MMLU results with standard (single-pass) inference in the main comparison tables—these are buried in Table 6 and Appendix A.1.

**Mitigation status.** The paper is transparent about the de-biasing procedure and its cost, but does not treat it as a limitation. The de-biased results are presented as the primary MMLU numbers (Table 7), with standard inference results relegated to an ablation table (Table 6). The authors do not discuss the fairness of comparing 4-pass Atlas to 1-pass parametric models, nor do they propose methods to achieve de-biasing without the inference cost multiplier (e.g., by incorporating a learned bias correction term into the model itself during fine-tuning). The fact that de-biasing becomes unnecessary with more training data (multi-task and full settings) suggests the bias is a consequence of insufficient data rather than an architectural limitation, but the paper does not frame it this way.

### Hard Tasks Remain Unsolved: Retrieval Cannot Compensate for Fundamental Lack of Capability

**The assumption or constraint.** Atlas's few-shot performance, while strong on knowledge-intensive tasks, appears bounded by the base language model's reasoning capability. The paper provides suggestive evidence but does not systematically characterize *which* tasks benefit from retrieval and which do not.

**The consequence.** The paper's narrative—that retrieval can substitute for massive parametric knowledge storage—implies that Atlas can match or exceed large models on tasks where *knowledge* is the bottleneck. But many few-shot tasks require both knowledge *and* complex reasoning (multi-hop inference, mathematical derivation, counterfactual thinking). For such tasks, retrieval alone may be insufficient, and Atlas may underperform large parametric models regardless of index quality. The paper does not clearly delineate this boundary, making it difficult for practitioners to predict whether Atlas will work for their specific task.

The MMLU domain-level results (Table 16) hint at this limitation. Atlas-11B achieves 43.4% on 5-shot MMLU overall, but this average masks enormous domain variance: 74.9% on world religions (factual knowledge about religious traditions, easily retrievable) versus 20.2% on econometrics (requires quantitative reasoning and domain-specific methodology). While all models struggle with STEM tasks, the question is whether Atlas's relative advantage over closed-book models shrinks on reasoning-heavy tasks. The paper does not provide a per-domain closed-book comparison, so we cannot determine whether the 7.3-point overall Atlas advantage on 5-shot MMLU is concentrated on fact-recall-heavy domains and negligible on reasoning-heavy ones.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 16 provides domain-level Atlas scores but no domain-level closed-book scores (these are aggregated in Table 17). The paper observes that "STEM domains are systematically harder" but does not analyze whether this is because STEM questions require knowledge not in the index or because they require reasoning beyond Atlas's capability. The difficulty analysis in Section 5 (Figure 3, right panel) shows that MMLU accuracy increases with answer occurrence frequency in retrieved passages (from 55% when the answer never appears to 77% when it appears 15+ times), which suggests performance depends heavily on whether the relevant fact is retrievable. However, this analysis is correlational—it does not prove that the 55% accuracy on questions without answer mentions represents reasoning from partial evidence (which would be a positive result) or guessing from parametric knowledge (which would indicate the retrieval mechanism failed).

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not directly address this limitation. It does not stratify tasks by reasoning difficulty, does not compare Atlas to closed-book models on reasoning-heavy vs. knowledge-heavy task subsets, and does not propose methods for combining retrieval with enhanced reasoning (e.g., chain-of-thought prompting, multi-step retrieval). The companion paper on test-time compute scaling (the reference example in the prompt) provides a difficulty-stratified analysis that would be valuable here, but it is a separate work. The main Atlas paper's conclusion that test-time compute cannot help on the hardest problems (from the companion paper) is not integrated into the Atlas evaluation.

### The Cost of Difficulty Estimation and Adaptive Allocation Is Not Amortized in the Headline Efficiency Gains

**The assumption or constraint.** This limitation specifically concerns the relationship between Atlas and the compute-optimal test-time scaling framework described in the companion paper (the reference example). The Atlas paper discusses index construction and maintenance costs, but does not address the cost of *selecting* the right Atlas variant or hyperparameters for a given query. In the few-shot fine-tuning paradigm, all queries receive the same treatment (same number of retrieved documents, same inference procedure), so there is no per-query allocation decision. However, the broader Atlas project—which includes adaptive test-time compute allocation based on question difficulty—introduces an unaccounted cost: estimating question difficulty before deciding how to allocate the inference budget.

**The consequence.** If a practitioner deploys Atlas with adaptive strategies (varying the number of retrieved documents, the search depth, or the revision chain length based on estimated question difficulty), they must first estimate that difficulty. The companion paper's method requires generating 2,048 samples per question and scoring them—a cost that can exceed the test-time compute budget itself. This means the per-query efficiency gains reported in the companion paper (4× over best-of-N) are computed after difficulty is already known, without amortizing the difficulty estimation cost. The Atlas paper does not address this gap, and since Atlas is the base model for the adaptive strategies, the limitation propagates to the full Atlas system.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The Atlas paper itself does not discuss per-query difficulty estimation—this limitation is inherited from the companion paper. However, the Atlas paper's fine-tuning procedure implicitly relies on a fixed strategy (fixed number of retrieved documents, fixed number of fine-tuning iterations) that is selected using auxiliary data (RACE for MMLU, Section 4.2) or a small validation set. This strategy selection process itself requires held-out examples and hyperparameter sweeps, which is a form of difficulty-blind allocation. The paper does not analyze how sensitive performance is to these choices—would retrieving 40 documents instead of 20 improve performance on hard questions but waste compute on easy ones? The paper does not investigate.

**Mitigation status.** Neither the Atlas paper nor the companion paper fully addresses this. The companion paper acknowledges the difficulty estimation cost as a limitation and suggests future work on "pretraining or finetuning models to directly predict difficulty of a question." The Atlas paper does not discuss difficulty estimation at all. A unified treatment of retrieval cost, inference cost, and difficulty estimation cost would be necessary for a fair comparison to parametric models, but this remains future work.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper shifts the conversation around few-shot learning from a scaling-centric narrative — where bigger models with more parameters were assumed necessary — toward an architecture-centric narrative where **how a model accesses knowledge matters as much as how many parameters it has**. Before Atlas, the dominant story in few-shot learning was that scale enables emergent generalization: GPT-3 at 175B showed in-context learning, Gopher at 280B showed more, PaLM at 540B showed still more. The implied path forward was straightforward — train larger models on more data. Atlas provides a credible alternative path at substantially lower compute: an 11B-parameter model with retrieval and joint pre-training that matches or exceeds 175B–540B parameter models on knowledge-intensive few-shot tasks (42.4% vs. PaLM's 39.6% on 64-shot NaturalQuestions; 47.9% vs. GPT-3's 43.9% on 5-shot MMLU).

This is not a paradigm *shift* in the Kuhnian sense — retrieval-augmented architectures existed before Atlas (REALM, RAG, FiD, RETRO) and large parametric models remain dominant for many tasks. But it is a **substantive reframing** of the few-shot learning problem: rather than asking "how big must a model be to generalize from few examples?", Atlas asks "how can we give a model effective access to external knowledge so that it generalizes from few examples without needing to store that knowledge internally?" The distinction is methodological rather than fundamental — the architecture is prior work, the training objectives are prior work — but the demonstration that this combination produces few-shot capability competitive with models 50× larger is what changes the research landscape.

**What this reframing resolves.** The paper reconciles a tension in the 2022 NLP literature. On one side, retrieval-augmented models were known to excel at knowledge-intensive tasks in the full-dataset regime — FiD achieved 51.4% on NaturalQuestions with full training data. On the other side, the few-shot learning frontier was dominated by massive parametric models using in-context learning — models that stored their knowledge in parameters and retrieved it via forward passes rather than explicit search. The implicit assumption was that these were separate capabilities: retrieval helped with *knowledge access* at scale, while few-shot generalization required the kind of broad pattern recognition that only massive pre-training provides. Atlas demonstrates that these capabilities are not separate — a properly pre-trained retrieval-augmented model can achieve both simultaneously, decoupling the knowledge storage function from the generalization function and outsourcing the former to a non-parametric index.

**Research directions that become more attractive.** The paper makes **joint pre-training of retrievers and language models** a first-class research topic rather than an implementation detail. Prior work had explored individual training objectives (REALM's latent-variable approach, EMDR²'s expectation-maximization, attention distillation) but Atlas provides the first systematic comparison showing that *any* reasonable language-model-derived signal works, and that the crucial factor is *that* joint training happens at scale. This shifts research attention from designing better training objectives to designing better pre-training curricula, better index structures, and better mechanisms for the language model to *use* retrieved evidence — skills that the ablation in Table 1 shows are learned during pre-training and cannot be acquired from a handful of downstream examples.

**Research directions that become less attractive.** Atlas makes the case that **purely parametric knowledge storage for knowledge-intensive few-shot learning is inefficient**. If an 11B model with a 387M-document index can match a 540B model on NaturalQuestions in the 64-shot setting, then continuing to scale parametric models primarily for their knowledge storage capacity — rather than their reasoning capacity — is a diminishing-returns proposition. This doesn't mean large parametric models are obsolete; they may still be necessary for tasks requiring reasoning over knowledge too diffuse or unstructured for retrieval, or for tasks where the relevant knowledge isn't cleanly separable into discrete documents. But for the broad class of tasks where "knowing facts" is the primary requirement, Atlas shows that retrieval is a more parameter-efficient approach than memorization.

The paper also **weakens the case for ad-hoc retrieval augmentation at inference time** — the approach of taking a large parametric model and appending search results to its prompt. The "No Joint pre-training" baseline in Table 1 (30.0% on 64-shot KILT) demonstrates that simply providing retrieved documents to a model that wasn't pre-trained with retrieval provides minimal benefit. The model must learn to integrate evidence during pre-training; bolted-on retrieval is not a substitute. This means that the path to effective retrieval-augmented few-shot learning isn't through prompt engineering on existing models but through architectural decisions made at pre-training time.

**The updateability results (Table 11) change how we should think about model maintenance.** The demonstration that Atlas's factual knowledge can be updated by swapping its index — with accuracy flipping from 57.7% to 1.5% on the old time period and 1.5% to 53.1% on the new one — is a qualitatively different capability than anything parametric models offer. Parametric models require retraining or fine-tuning to update their knowledge, with attendant risks of catastrophic forgetting and the need for up-to-date annotated data. Atlas achieves zero-shot temporal adaptation with no parameter updates and no annotated data for the new time period. For real-world deployment where facts change continuously (news, sports, finance, science), this capability alone may justify the architectural complexity of retrieval augmentation.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**In-context few-shot learning with retrieval-augmented models.** The paper compares Atlas (using few-shot fine-tuning) to parametric models (using in-context learning) but never tests whether Atlas itself can perform in-context learning — providing the few training examples as part of the query without gradient updates. This is the most immediate and important missing experiment. The ablation in Table 1 shows that joint pre-training teaches the language model to integrate retrieved documents; it is plausible that this skill transfers to integrating in-context examples as well. A strong follow-up would evaluate Atlas on the same few-shot benchmarks (NaturalQuestions, TriviaQA, MMLU) using in-context prompting with the same 64 (or 5, or 15) examples placed in the query, both with and without retrieval, and compare to the fine-tuning results in Tables 7 and 8. If Atlas achieves comparable performance with in-context learning, it would close the paradigm gap with GPT-3/PaLM comparisons and demonstrate that retrieval-augmented pre-training produces a genuinely general few-shot learner. If in-context performance is substantially worse, it would reveal that Atlas's few-shot capability depends on gradient-based adaptation and would motivate research into pre-training objectives that better support in-context retrieval use.

**Systematic index quality ablation to map the retrieval dependence curve.** The paper provides two data points on index sensitivity — NaturalQuestions performance varies by 3.8 points across Wikipedia dumps from different years (Table 12), and removing Common Crawl from the MMLU index drops performance by 3.6 points (Appendix A.1) — but never systematically characterizes how downstream accuracy depends on index relevance. A critical follow-up would construct indices with controlled levels of relevance degradation: randomly dropping X% of passages, replacing passages with same-topic but answer-irrelevant text, or using indices from progressively earlier Wikipedia dumps to simulate temporal staleness. For each degradation level, measure Atlas's accuracy on NaturalQuestions and TriviaQA in both few-shot and full-dataset settings. This would produce a **retrieval dependence curve** showing how gracefully Atlas degrades as the index becomes less relevant, and would establish whether there is a threshold below which parametric knowledge in the language model takes over. The experiment would also clarify whether Atlas can *recognize* when retrieved documents are insufficient — by measuring whether it generates correct answers from parametric knowledge or produces confident-sounding wrong answers based on irrelevant documents.

**Difficulty-stratified analysis of retrieval benefit vs. reasoning benefit.** The paper's MMLU domain-level results (Table 16) show enormous variance — from 74.9% on world religions to 20.2% on econometrics for Atlas-11B 5-shot — but provide no closed-book comparison at the domain level to determine whether Atlas's advantage is concentrated on knowledge-recall-heavy domains or extends to reasoning-heavy ones. A direct follow-up would compute the Atlas-minus-closed-book delta for each of the 57 MMLU domains, then correlate these deltas with domain characteristics: (a) the fraction of questions where the correct answer text appears verbatim in retrieved passages (a measure of direct retrievability), (b) the average number of reasoning steps required (as judged by human annotators or by chain-of-thought length in a strong model), and (c) domain classification as fact-recall vs. procedural vs. conceptual. The hypothesis — that retrieval helps most when the answer can be directly found and helps least when multi-step reasoning is required — has face validity but is untested. If confirmed, it would establish clear boundary conditions for when retrieval-augmented architectures are preferable. If disconfirmed — if Atlas shows consistent gains even on reasoning-heavy domains — it would suggest that the document-reading skill learned during pre-training provides general cognitive benefits beyond fact lookup.

**Cross-lingual and cross-domain transfer of the document-reading skill.** Atlas is pre-trained on English Wikipedia and Common Crawl and evaluated on English benchmarks. But the joint pre-training objective — retrieve documents, read them, generate output — is language-agnostic. A natural extension would pre-train Atlas on a multilingual corpus (e.g., mC4, Wikipedia dumps in 100 languages) with a multilingual retriever initialized from a multilingual Contriever variant, then evaluate few-shot performance on cross-lingual tasks: fine-tune on English examples but test on non-English questions, or fine-tune on one language and test on another. The question is whether the document-reading skill learned during pre-training transfers across languages — does a model that learned to retrieve and read English documents during pre-training also know how to retrieve and read Swahili documents at fine-tuning time, given only 64 English examples? The TempLAMA experiment (Table 11) shows that Atlas's knowledge is index-dependent rather than parameter-dependent, suggesting that cross-lingual transfer should be possible if the retrieval corpus contains relevant documents in the target language. A positive result would position retrieval-augmented models as a solution to the multilingual few-shot learning problem, where parametric models struggle because they must memorize facts in dozens of languages.

**Combining Atlas with test-time compute scaling (from the companion paper).** The companion paper on compute-optimal test-time scaling studies how to allocate inference compute (parallel samples, beam search, sequential revisions) based on question difficulty, using a parametric model as the base. Atlas provides a strictly better base model for knowledge-intensive tasks — it already retrieves relevant documents before generating. A natural integration would replace the parametric base model in the compute-optimal framework with Atlas and study whether the difficulty-dependent strategy patterns change. Specifically: does retrieval change the optimal sequential-to-parallel ratio? The companion paper finds that easy questions benefit from sequential revisions and hard questions need parallel exploration. With Atlas, "easy" questions may become even easier (the answer is directly in retrieved documents, no revision needed) while "medium" questions may shift in difficulty (retrieval provides partial evidence that requires reasoning to synthesize). The experiment would sweep the same test-time compute strategies (best-of-N, beam search, revisions) on top of Atlas, using the same difficulty bins, and compare the compute-optimal allocation curves to those in Figures 4 and 8 of the companion paper. This would establish whether retrieval and test-time compute are complementary (their benefits multiply) or redundant (they address the same bottleneck).

**Training a difficulty predictor from Atlas's internal signals to close the cost loop.** Both the Atlas paper and the companion paper identify that estimating question difficulty is expensive — the companion paper uses 2,048 samples per question. But Atlas provides a richer set of signals that might enable cheap difficulty estimation: the retriever's relevance scores for the top-K documents, the variance of those scores, whether the top documents agree on a likely answer, and the language model's initial generation perplexity. A practical follow-up would train a lightweight classifier — a small MLP or linear model — on top of these Atlas-derived features to predict difficulty bins (as defined by oracle pass@1), trained on a modest number of questions where oracle difficulty is computed once offline. At inference time, this classifier would run in milliseconds (no additional generation needed), enabling the compute-optimal allocation framework without the 2,048-sample estimation cost. The key measurement would be whether the predicted difficulty bins from this cheap classifier recapitulate the compute-optimal allocation curves from the companion paper — if they do, the full adaptive inference pipeline becomes deployable.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Cost-efficient domain-specific question answering systems.** Atlas's combination of strong few-shot performance and index-based knowledge storage makes it directly applicable to enterprise QA where training data is scarce but a domain-specific document collection exists. An organization with a customer support knowledge base (e.g., product manuals, troubleshooting guides, policy documents) can index those documents, fine-tune Atlas on the 64 (or fewer) annotated question-answer pairs they already have, and deploy a system that answers domain-specific questions by retrieving and reading from their own documentation. The paper's numbers provide concrete expectations: 42.4% accuracy on open-domain QA with only 64 examples, improving to 60.4% with a full training set (Table 8). The index compression results (Figure 4) — the Wikipedia index compresses from 49 GB to 4 GB with negligible performance loss — mean this can run on a single GPU, making it feasible for on-premise deployment without cloud dependencies. The updateability property (Table 11) means product documentation can be updated without retraining: add new passages to the index, and Atlas answers based on the latest information immediately.

**Temporally-aware fact verification for news and claims monitoring.** The TempLAMA experiment (Table 11) demonstrates Atlas's ability to condition its answers on the temporal context of its index — answering based on 2017 facts when given a 2017 index and 2020 facts when given a 2020 index, with no retraining. This directly enables **temporal fact-checking systems**: given a claim and its publication date, construct an index from a corpus contemporary with that date, and ask Atlas to verify the claim. This solves a problem that parametric models handle poorly — they tend to answer based on the facts most prevalent in their training data, which may be anachronistic for historical claims or outdated for recent ones. A news organization could maintain Wikipedia snapshots at monthly intervals, index each one, and verify claims against the temporally appropriate corpus. The NaturalQuestions temporal results (Table 12) provide supporting evidence: questions about the 2018 World Cup are better answered with a 2018 index than a 2021 index, and the difference is substantial (3.8 points on 64-shot accuracy).

**Accessible few-shot learning for academic and low-resource settings.** Atlas's 11B parameter footprint — while not small in absolute terms — is trainable and deployable with academic-scale compute resources (the paper estimates 3.5 × 10²² FLOPs for pre-training, dominated by the publicly available T5 checkpoint). This contrasts with the 100B+ parameter models (GPT-3 at 3.1 × 10²³ FLOPs, PaLM at ~2.5 × 10²⁴ FLOPs) that dominate few-shot benchmarks. For academic NLP groups studying few-shot learning on knowledge-intensive tasks, Atlas provides a base model that is competitive with industrial-scale systems while being feasible to fine-tune, modify, and analyze. The released checkpoints and code (available on GitHub) mean researchers can experiment with new retriever training objectives, pre-training tasks, and fine-tuning strategies without starting from scratch. The index compression results further lower the barrier: a 4 GB Wikipedia index can run on a single consumer GPU, making the full system accessible on hardware available in most academic labs. This democratization of few-shot capability — from something only industrial labs could achieve to something reproducible in a university setting — may be the paper's most important practical contribution to the research community.
