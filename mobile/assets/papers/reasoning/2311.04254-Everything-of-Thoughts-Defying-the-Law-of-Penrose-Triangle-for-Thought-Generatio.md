# Everything of Thoughts: Defying the Law of Penrose Triangle for Thought Generation

**ArXiv:** [2311.04254](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.04254)

## 🎯 Pitch

Large language models can finally solve spatial puzzles like the 8-Puzzle at over 95% accuracy—tasks where prior prompting methods largely failed—by having a tiny policy network play out Monte Carlo Tree Search trajectories and then feeding the LLM only the condensed, graph-structured insights to revise. This 'Everything of Thoughts' framework collapses the supposed trade-off between performance, efficiency, and thought flexibility, requiring just ~1.8 LLM calls to outperform tree-search baselines that demanded 40+. The secret is that LLMs don't need to simulate every step; they just need to judge and refine high-level plans from a cheap external module that compensates for their planning blind spots.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **Everything of Thoughts** (XOT), a thought prompting framework that combines Monte Carlo Tree Search guided by lightweight policy/value networks with LLM-based thought revision to generate cognitive mappings for complex problem-solving. Evaluated on Game of 24, 8-Puzzle, and Pocket Cube using GPT-3.5 and GPT-4, XOT concurrently achieves the three attributes the authors frame as a "Penrose triangle" tradeoff in prior paradigms—performance (accuracy), efficiency (LLM inference calls), and flexibility (thought topology ranging from chains to graphs). The method substantially outperforms prompting baselines: on Game of 24 with GPT-4, XOT reaches 85.40% accuracy with three revisions versus ToT's 60.58% while requiring only ~1.8 LLM calls compared to ToT's ~40 calls, and on 8-Puzzle, XOT achieves 95.80% accuracy where the best prompting baseline (ToT) reaches only 13.45%. The collaborative MCTS-LLM revision process enables these gains primarily by injecting external domain knowledge and planning capability into the LLM's reasoning, establishing that LLMs can solve previously insurmountable spatial reasoning tasks only when supplied with structured thought trajectories from an external search module that compensates for their limited long-term planning abilities.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: The "Penrose Triangle" of Thought Generation

The fundamental problem this paper addresses is that **existing thought prompting paradigms for LLMs cannot simultaneously achieve all three desirable attributes that the authors identify as essential for effective problem-solving: performance, efficiency, and flexibility**. The paper frames this as an impossibility law analogous to the "Penrose triangle" — an optical illusion where three straight beams appear to form a triangle but cannot exist together in three-dimensional space. Similarly, prior thought generation methods can achieve at most two of the three desiderata, but never all three at once.

This trilemma matters because each attribute addresses a genuinely critical constraint in deploying LLMs for complex reasoning:

- **Performance** (accuracy of both final answers and intermediate reasoning steps) determines whether the system is trustworthy enough to use on consequential problems. Without accurate intermediate steps, even a correct final answer may be arrived at through flawed reasoning — a phenomenon the paper associates with hallucinations in fine-tuned models like LLaMA-2-13B, which scores 0% on both 8-Puzzle and Pocket Cube despite having access to supervised training data.

- **Efficiency** (number of LLM inference calls per problem) directly translates to monetary cost, latency, and energy consumption. The paper provides concrete numbers: ToT with GPT-4 requires on average ~39.83 calls for Game of 24 and ~54.13 calls for 8-Puzzle. At typical commercial API pricing, solving a single moderately difficult Game of 24 problem could cost meaningful fractions of a dollar — prohibitive at scale.

- **Flexibility** (the diversity of thought structures the model can employ, from linear chains to trees to graphs) determines whether the approach can handle problems with multiple valid solutions or problems requiring non-linear reasoning. The paper argues this matters particularly for complex real-world tasks where human thinking naturally follows graph-like mind mapping processes rather than rigid step-by-step chains.

The paper's central claim is not merely that prior methods are individually suboptimal on one dimension, but that there is a **structural tradeoff** — the design choices that enable performance improvements (like searching over multiple reasoning paths) inherently sacrifice efficiency (because each path requires separate LLM calls), and vice versa. XOT's contribution is in breaking this tradeoff by using an external lightweight search module to handle exploration, preserving LLM calls only for final verification and targeted revision.

### Why This Problem Is Important

The practical significance stems from three converging trends in LLM deployment:

**1. Complexity of real-world problems exceeds single-pass capabilities.** Problems like the 8-Puzzle and Pocket Cube are not artificial contrivances — they instantiate a broader class of planning and spatial reasoning tasks where the model must navigate an exponentially large state space with well-defined transition rules. The paper notes that even GPT-4 achieves only 1.68% accuracy on 8-Puzzle with IO prompting and 7.56% with CoT. This is not a marginal deficiency — it is a catastrophic failure mode indicating that off-the-shelf LLMs, even the most capable ones, fundamentally lack the ability to plan over long horizons without external scaffolding. Any deployment requiring multi-step planning (robotics, game-playing, supply chain optimization, procedural task execution) faces this same bottleneck.

**2. The cost of naïve test-time compute scaling is prohibitive.** The ToT and GoT baselines demonstrate that performance *can* be improved by searching over more reasoning paths — ToT (b=3) reaches 60.58% on Game of 24 with GPT-4, a massive improvement over CoT's 4.38%. But this comes at the cost of ~40 LLM calls per problem. For a single instance of a game, this is merely expensive; for applications requiring thousands of planning queries (e.g., generating training data for robotics, evaluating candidate designs, running simulations), this is economically infeasible. The paper's efficiency axis is therefore not a theoretical nicety — it is the difference between a method that works in a research paper and one that can be deployed in production.

**3. Multiple-solution problems are common but poorly served.** Real-world planning rarely has a single correct answer. For any given initial state of a Pocket Cube, there exist multiple valid move sequences to restore it. Similarly, code generation, creative writing, and scientific hypothesis generation all involve problems where diverse valid outputs are valuable. The paper's multi-solution experiments (Tables 4, 6, 8) show that prior methods either cannot generate multiple solutions at all (IO, CoT produce fixed outputs), cannot do so efficiently (ToT requires running the entire search tree separately per solution), or generate mostly incorrect alternatives. XOT's ability to extract multiple valid trajectories from a single MCTS simulation — leveraging the fact that the search tree naturally contains multiple paths to the goal — addresses a genuine deployment need.

The theoretical significance is equally important: the paper provides evidence that **LLMs' planning failures are not due to a fundamental inability to reason about the problem, but rather due to an inability to *generate* the reasoning structure autonomously**. When supplied with a complete thought trajectory from MCTS (Section 4.4.2), even incomplete trajectories that omit the last step, LLM performance jumps dramatically — GPT-4 achieves 43.07% on Game of 24 with incomplete MCTS thoughts and no revision, compared to 4.38% with pure CoT. This suggests that LLMs possess latent planning *comprehension* that they cannot access through their own generation process, a finding with implications for understanding the nature of LLMs' reasoning limitations.

### Where Prior Approaches Fall Short

The paper systematically identifies the specific failure modes of each existing paradigm, building its case for why a new approach is needed:

**IO Prompting (Figure 1a).** The simplest baseline — provide the problem, get the answer, no intermediate steps. This fails on all three axes: performance is near-zero on spatial tasks (0.00% on 8-Puzzle with GPT-3.5; 1.68% with GPT-4); there is no flexibility because there is no thought structure at all; and while efficiency is trivially excellent (1 call), it achieves nothing. The paper treats IO as a lower bound establishing that these tasks are genuinely hard.

**Chain-of-Thought (CoT, Figure 1b).** CoT decomposes problems into sequential steps, which the paper acknowledges provides "modest performance improvements." But the improvements are indeed modest: 7.56% on 8-Puzzle with GPT-4 versus 1.68% for IO — a roughly 4.5× relative improvement but still far from usable. More critically, CoT is confined to **linear thought structures**. This structural rigidity means it cannot explore alternative reasoning paths when the first attempt fails, cannot backtrack from dead ends, and cannot represent problems where multiple subproblems interact non-sequentially. The paper's Figure 5 illustrates this limitation starkly: the graph-like thought structures generated by XOT for multi-solution problems contain branching, merging, and looping (what the paper calls "reflection" — back-and-forth recurrent state transitions in the 8-Puzzle example). CoT cannot produce any of these patterns.

**Self-Consistency CoT (CoT-SC, Figure 1c).** CoT-SC attempts to improve performance by sampling multiple independent CoT chains and selecting the majority answer. This addresses CoT's brittleness to individual bad samples but inherits all its structural limitations: the thoughts remain linear chains, and flexibility is not improved. Moreover, the efficiency degrades proportionally to the number of samples (10 calls in the paper's experiments). The performance gains are minimal: on 8-Puzzle with GPT-4, CoT-SC achieves 8.40% vs. CoT's 7.56% — a marginal improvement for 10× the compute. On Game of 24, CoT-SC actually *underperforms* CoT with GPT-3.5 (2.19% vs. 2.19%, identical) and is identical with GPT-4 (4.38%). This ineffectiveness highlights a deeper issue: when the base model's single-pass reasoning is poor, aggregating multiple poor samples does not help because there is no signal to aggregate — all attempted chains are equally wrong.

**Tree-of-Thought (ToT, Figure 1d).** ToT is the strongest prompting baseline and the most direct predecessor to XOT. It organizes thoughts in a tree structure and uses search algorithms (BFS/DFS) to expand the tree toward an optimal solution. The paper acknowledges ToT achieves the highest performance among baselines: 60.58% on Game of 24 (GPT-4, b=3) and 13.45% on 8-Puzzle. However, it identifies two critical weaknesses:

**First, thought evaluation relies on the LLM itself.** At each step of the search tree, ToT must solicit the LLM to (a) generate candidate next thoughts and (b) evaluate the quality of each candidate for pruning. Both operations are LLM inference calls, and they compound multiplicatively with tree depth and branching factor. The paper quantifies this: ToT (b=3) on Game of 24 requires ~40 LLM calls, and on 8-Puzzle requires ~54 calls. This is not a constant-factor overhead — it scales with problem complexity, making ToT most expensive precisely when the problems are hardest and most need help.

**Second, the tree structure is still constraining compared to graphs.** While more flexible than chains, trees cannot represent convergent reasoning paths where multiple different sequences of sub-steps lead to the same intermediate state. In the Game of 24 example from Figure 5, there are multiple ways to arrive at the same intermediate set of remaining numbers — in a tree structure, these would be represented as separate branches that never merge, wasting computation on redundant exploration and missing the opportunity to recognize that different paths lead to the same state. This is not merely an efficiency concern — it means ToT cannot discover that two seemingly different approaches converge, which is essential for generating multiple distinct solutions.

**Graph-of-Thought (GoT, Figure 1e).** GoT extends ToT by enabling thought aggregation and refinement, producing graph-like structures. This addresses the flexibility limitation — thoughts can merge, split, and be refined based on information from multiple predecessors. However, the paper's results show that GoT's performance does not consistently improve over ToT: on Game of 24 with GPT-4, GoT (k=1) achieves only 10.95% versus ToT's 60.58% (b=3), and on 8-Puzzle GoT achieves 3.36% versus ToT's 13.45%. The paper attributes this to the same fundamental bottleneck: **GoT still requires multiple LLM inference calls for evaluation**, and the increased structural complexity of graph operations (aggregation, refinement) does not offset the cost of having the LLM perform those evaluations — especially when the LLM's evaluations are themselves unreliable on tasks it fundamentally struggles with.

**Fine-tuning (LLaMA-2-13B).** The paper includes a supervised fine-tuning baseline that attempts to directly distill planning knowledge from training data (input-output pairs with ground truth labels) into a smaller model. This approach achieves 2.19% on Game of 24 and **0.00% on both 8-Puzzle and Pocket Cube** — catastrophically poor performance. The paper attributes this to "significant hallucination issues" and notes that "finetuning approaches are not suitable for planning tasks" of this nature. This is a telling negative result: it suggests that the planning capability required for these tasks cannot be learned from static input-output pairs alone, regardless of model size — the model needs the dynamic exploration that search provides. This directly motivates XOT's architecture, which keeps search at inference time but offloads it to a lightweight module rather than the LLM.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

XOT positions itself as a **hybrid architecture** that decomposes the thought generation problem into two sub-tasks and assigns each to the component best suited for it:

**MCTS with policy/value networks** handles **thought exploration and generation** — the computationally intensive process of searching through the space of possible reasoning trajectories. The paper explicitly notes that the policy/value networks are approximately 10⁶ parameters (Section 4), versus GPT-3.5's 175 billion parameters. This four-orders-of-magnitude difference in model size is the key enabler: MCTS can perform hundreds of simulations using these lightweight networks at a computational cost far below what a single LLM call would require. The paper reports that on Game of 24, XOT invokes the policy/value network ~89–93 times per problem while requiring only ~1.4–1.8 LLM calls — the LLM calls are the expensive bottleneck, and XOT minimizes them.

**The LLM** handles **thought verification and revision** — leveraging its internal world knowledge to detect errors in the MCTS-generated trajectory and guide targeted re-exploration. This is crucial because the MCTS module, trained on a finite set of training problems, may produce suboptimal or incorrect thoughts when generalizing to new problems. The paper's collaborative revision framework (Figure 3) makes this explicit: the LLM identifies an error state sₑ in the MCTS-generated trajectory, MCTS conducts additional simulations starting from the parent of sₑ (effectively a targeted re-search of the problematic subtree), and the revised thought is returned to the LLM for final answer generation. This process can iterate multiple times, with the Revision Success Rate (Tables 9-10) showing that LLMs detect errors at rates of 47–76% for GPT-3.5 and 33–91% for GPT-4 across tasks and revision rounds. The paper frames this as making MCTS into a "world model for LLMs" — a mechanism through which the LLM can interactively query and refine planning trajectories rather than generating them from scratch.

A critical design choice that distinguishes XOT from prior work is that **thoughts from MCTS serve a supporting role, not as definitive answers**. The paper explicitly states: "These thoughts do not provide LLMs with definitive or error-free answers, as they may contain inaccuracies or suboptimal solutions." This is a deliberate departure from paradigms where the search output is treated as the solution — in XOT, the LLM always has the final authority to accept, reject, or revise the MCTS-generated trajectory. This design preserves the LLM's reasoning capability (which may exceed the MCTS module's on nuanced aspects of the problem) while compensating for the LLM's planning limitation (which the MCTS module addresses directly).

The paper also positions XOT as **generalizable beyond the specific tasks evaluated**. Section 6 notes that MCTS "is functioning in a supportive role and can be substituted with alternative supervised or RL models for thought exploration and generation." The key requirement is that the task be decomposable into well-defined state transitions with clear objectives — an MDP formulation. The preliminary results on Document Merging (Appendix C, Table 13), where XOT achieves a score of 8.168 versus GoT's 7.559 and ToT's 7.715 while using fewer tokens than both, suggest the framework extends to non-gaming NLP tasks. This positions XOT not as a domain-specific solution for puzzles, but as a general architecture for any task where (a) the problem can be decomposed into steps with well-defined states, (b) a simulator or verifier exists to train the policy/value networks, and (c) the LLM benefits from having structured intermediate reasoning before producing its final output.

Finally, the paper implicitly challenges a prevailing assumption in the LLM reasoning literature: that **the LLM should be the sole engine of reasoning, with prompting techniques merely structuring its output**. XOT instead treats the LLM as one component in a larger cognitive system where dedicated search modules handle the computationally expensive exploration while the LLM contributes its semantic understanding and verification capabilities. This is a significant architectural departure — it suggests that the path to more capable LLM reasoning systems may lie not in making LLMs better at search (through longer chains or more elaborate prompting), but in recognizing that search and semantic reasoning are distinct capabilities best served by distinct components with fundamentally different computational characteristics.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

XOT is a hybrid reasoning system where a lightweight neural-guided search module (MCTS with policy/value networks) explores possible solution paths for a problem and produces structured "thought trajectories," while an LLM acts as a reviewer that checks these trajectories for errors, requests targeted re-exploration when it finds mistakes, and synthesizes the final answer by combining the external thought structure with its internal knowledge. The core problem XOT solves is the "Penrose triangle" tradeoff in LLM reasoning — existing methods that achieve high accuracy must make many expensive LLM calls (sacrificing efficiency), while methods that are efficient are confined to rigid linear reasoning chains (sacrificing flexibility and performance); XOT breaks this triangle by outsourcing the computationally intensive exploration to a lightweight model (~10⁶ parameters versus GPT-3.5's 175 billion), reserving the LLM's expensive inference budget solely for verification and final synthesis, and using MCTS's inherent tree/graph topology to generate flexible thought structures that can branch, merge, and loop as needed for problems with multiple solutions.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The XOT system has five major components, organized into a training phase and an inference phase:

**Training Phase:**

1. **MCTS Simulator**: For a given task, runs self-play simulations from training problem instances, exploring the space of possible thought trajectories (sequences of state-action pairs). Records states, actions, rewards, and visitation counts from each simulation. This is the exploration engine that generates the training data for the neural networks.

2. **Policy/Value Network** (`$f_\theta$`): A compact multi-layer perceptron (~10⁶ parameters total across all three tasks) with shared layers and two output heads. Takes a state representation as input; produces (a) a scalar value estimate `$v_\theta(s)$` predicting the expected future reward from that state, and (b) a probability distribution `$P_\theta(s)$` over available actions. Trained on the data generated by MCTS simulations to approximate the search's own evaluation of states and actions, thereby learning domain-specific planning knowledge.

**Inference Phase:**

3. **MCTS Guided by Trained Networks**: For a new problem instance, runs `$K$` simulations of MCTS where the policy/value network `$f_\theta$` provides the action priors and state evaluations that guide the search. Produces one or more thought trajectories `$T^* = \{\tau_1, ..., \tau_N\}$` where each thought `$\tau = \{s, a\}$` is a state-action pair. For multi-solution problems, samples `$M$` trajectories following a probability distribution over visitation counts.

4. **Thought-to-Prompt Parser**: Converts the structured thought trajectories (state-action pairs) into natural language text describing the step-by-step state transitions, concatenated into a single prompt. This textual thought representation is what the LLM actually receives.

5. **LLM Solver with Revision Loop**: Receives the textual thought trajectory and has two responsibilities: (a) **Error Detection**: identifies specific erroneous steps in the MCTS-generated trajectory using its internal semantic knowledge; (b) **Final Answer Synthesis**: after any revisions are applied, produces the ultimate solution by integrating the (possibly revised) external thought structure with its own reasoning. If an error is found at state `$s_e$`, MCTS is re-invoked with `$L$` additional simulations starting from `$s_e$`'s parent state to generate a revised thought, and the cycle repeats (up to 3 times in the paper's experiments).

**Information flow during inference**: New problem enters → MCTS (guided by `$f_\theta$`) generates candidate thought trajectories → Parser converts trajectories to text prompts → LLM examines the thought for errors → If error found, MCTS runs additional targeted simulations to revise the problematic segment → Revised thought returned to LLM → LLM produces final answer.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the MDP formulation (Section 3.2 of the paper) — defining states, actions, rewards, and thoughts as formal objects — because this is the mathematical substrate on which MCTS operates and the bridge between abstract problem-solving and concrete search.
- **Second**, the MCTS search procedure (Section 3.3 of the paper) — the selection, expansion/evaluation, and backpropagation cycle — because this is the engine that generates thought trajectories and understanding how it uses the policy/value network is essential for understanding where the domain knowledge comes from.
- **Third**, the policy/value network training (Section 3.3 continued) — the loss function and training loop — because this explains how the lightweight network acquires task-specific planning capability from MCTS simulations.
- **Fourth**, thought inference with MCTS (Section 3.4) — how the trained networks guide search on new problems and how single vs. multiple solution trajectories are extracted — because this is the bridge from training to deployment.
- **Fifth**, the thought-to-prompt parsing mechanism — because the LLM cannot directly consume structured state-action pairs; this conversion determines what information the LLM receives and how it can use it.
- **Sixth**, the collaborative MCTS-LLM thought revision framework (Section 3.4 continued) — the iterative error detection and targeted re-exploration process — because this is the key innovation that enables performance gains beyond what either MCTS or LLM could achieve alone.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **systems/architecture paper** whose core idea is that LLM problem-solving can be decomposed into (a) computationally intensive thought-space exploration (offloaded to a lightweight MCTS module with learned policy/value networks) and (b) semantic verification and final synthesis (reserved for the expensive LLM), connected through an iterative revision loop that lets the LLM guide targeted re-exploration when the initial MCTS trajectory contains errors.

#### 3.4.1 Markov Decision Process Formulation of Thought Searching

The foundation of XOT is the formalization of thought generation as a **Markov Decision Process (MDP)**, where the process of decomposing a complex problem into intermediate steps is modeled as sequential decision-making in a state space. This formulation is what makes it possible to apply MCTS — a search algorithm designed for MDPs — to the problem of generating reasoning structures for LLMs.

The MDP is defined by four components:

**State** `$s_t$`: Represents the current status of the problem after `$t$` decomposition steps have been applied. The initial state `$s_0$` is the original problem in its entirety. Intermediate states are either (a) the sub-problems that remain to be solved after some steps have been taken, or (b) the partial results obtained so far. The paper provides concrete mappings for each task: in Game of 24, the state is "the remaining 1-4 numbers" that have not yet been combined; in 8-Puzzle, the state is "the current number layout of the puzzle" (the 3×3 grid); in Pocket Cube, the state is "colors of each face of the pocket cube."

**Action** `$a_t$`: Signifies the one-step solution or operation applied to transition from the current state to a new state. In Game of 24, an action is "picking two numbers and an operation to compose an equation" — for example, selecting 12 and 2 with the multiplication operator to produce the intermediate result 24, and updating the set of available numbers to reflect that 12 and 2 have been consumed and 24 has been added. In 8-Puzzle, an action is "the one-step moving action of the '-' tile" — sliding the empty space in one of the four cardinal directions. In Pocket Cube, an action is "the one-step rotation action of the cube" — applying one of the nine allowed face rotations (U, U', U2, R, R', R2, F, F', F2) to the current cube configuration.

**Reward** `$r$`: A scalar value reflecting how well the overall problem has been resolved through the decomposition process. The paper defines task-specific reward functions that encode domain knowledge about solution quality: in Game of 24, the reward is 1 if the final equation is valid, uses each input number exactly once, and equals 24; otherwise it is -1. In 8-Puzzle, the reward is "the negative minimum step on solving the current puzzle state toward the goal state" — this is a distance-to-goal heuristic where being closer to the solved configuration yields a higher (less negative) reward. In Pocket Cube, the reward is similarly "the negative minimum moving step on solving current cube state toward the goal state." The use of negative distance-based rewards is a critical design choice: it provides dense, informative feedback at every step rather than only at terminal states, which is essential for MCTS to learn effective value estimates for intermediate states.

**Thought** `$\tau$`: A one-step thought is formally defined as a combination of the current state and the chosen action, i.e., `$\tau = \{s, a\}$`. A complete thought trajectory is a sequence `$T = \{\tau_1, ..., \tau_N\}$` that represents the full decomposition of the problem from initial state to goal state. The objective of thought searching is to discover a trajectory `$T$` that maximizes the accumulated reward:

> $$\max_T \sum_{\tau \in T} r(\tau)$$

In plain language: find the sequence of state-action pairs that yields the highest total reward. For tasks with a reachable goal state (all tasks in the paper), this is equivalent to finding the shortest or most efficient path to the goal.

**Why this formulation matters:** By casting thought generation as an MDP, the authors make the problem amenable to standard reinforcement learning and planning algorithms — specifically MCTS, which is designed to efficiently explore large state spaces by balancing exploration of unknown actions with exploitation of known good actions. This formulation also makes the approach general: any problem that can be expressed as state transitions with evaluable outcomes can be plugged into the XOT framework. The requirement is not that the LLM itself understand the MDP — the MDP structure is used internally by MCTS and the policy/value networks — but rather that there exists a well-defined notion of state, action, and reward for the task at hand.

The paper's concrete instantiations for each task (Table 2) are carefully designed to make the state space tractable for MCTS while still capturing the essential complexity of the problem. For Game of 24, the state space grows combinatorially in the number of remaining numbers but is bounded (at most 4 numbers, each with a limited set of possible values produced by arithmetic combinations). For 8-Puzzle and Pocket Cube, the state spaces are finite but large: 8-Puzzle has `$9!/2 = 181,440$` reachable states, and the Pocket Cube has `$3,674,160$` reachable configurations. These are far too large for exhaustive search but well within the capabilities of MCTS with learned value functions.

#### 3.4.2 MCTS Search Procedure for Thought Exploration

MCTS is the computational engine that explores the space of possible thought trajectories. The paper implements a version of MCTS closely modeled on AlphaGo Zero (Silver et al., 2017), which uses neural networks to guide the search and reduce the number of simulations needed. The search runs for multiple simulations, each consisting of three sequential phases: **selection**, **expansion and evaluation**, and **backpropagation**. These phases collectively build a search tree where nodes are states and edges are actions, with each edge maintaining statistics about the quality of that action from that state.

##### Selection Phase

The selection phase starts at the root node (the initial state of the problem) and repeatedly selects actions to traverse down the tree until reaching a leaf node — a node that has not yet been expanded or represents a terminal state.

At each node `$s$`, the action `$a^*$` is selected using the **PUCT algorithm** (Predictor + Upper Confidence Bound applied to trees), which balances exploration of under-visited actions with exploitation of actions that have high estimated value:

> $$a^* = \arg\max_{a \in A(s)} \left[ Q(s, a) + w \cdot P_\theta(s, a) \sqrt{\frac{N(s)}{1 + N(s, a)}} \right]$$

where:

- `$Q(s, a)$` is the estimated Q-value of taking action `$a$` in state `$s$` — this is the average reward obtained from simulations that passed through this state-action pair. The higher the Q-value, the better the action is estimated to be based on previous exploration.
- `$P_\theta(s, a)$` is the **prior probability** of selecting action `$a$` in state `$s$`, predicted by the policy/value neural network `$f_\theta$`. This prior encodes the network's learned domain knowledge about which actions are promising before any search is performed at this specific state.
- `$N(s)$` is the total number of times state `$s$` has been visited across all simulations: `$N(s) = \sum_{a \in A(s)} N(s, a)$`.
- `$N(s, a)$` is the number of times action `$a$` has been selected from state `$s$` in previous simulations.
- `$w$` is a hyperparameter controlling the trade-off between exploration and exploitation — higher values of `$w$` increase the influence of the prior `$P_\theta$` relative to the empirical Q-values, encouraging the search to explore actions that the network believes are promising even if they haven't been tried many times yet.

The term `$\sqrt{N(s) / (1 + N(s, a))}$` is the **exploration bonus**: it is large for actions with low visit counts relative to the total visits to the state, encouraging the algorithm to try under-explored actions. As `$N(s, a)$` grows, this bonus shrinks, and the selection increasingly relies on the empirical Q-value `$Q(s, a)$`. The `$P_\theta(s, a)$` multiplier ensures that the exploration bonus is scaled by the network's prior belief — actions that the network thinks are poor receive a smaller bonus even if they are under-explored.

**What this computes operationally:** Starting from the root, at each step the algorithm looks at all available actions, computes the PUCT score for each (combining historical performance with the network's recommendation and an exploration incentive), picks the highest-scoring action, moves to the resulting state, and repeats. This continues until reaching a state that has not yet been added to the search tree (an unexplored leaf) or a terminal state.

**Why this form:** The PUCT formula is a standard choice in MCTS-with-neural-networks because it smoothly interpolates between following the learned prior (when little search has been done) and following empirical evidence (when many simulations have accumulated). A pure UCB formula without the `$P_\theta$` term would treat all actions as equally promising initially, requiring many more simulations to identify good actions — the prior injects domain knowledge that dramatically accelerates search. Conversely, using only `$P_\theta$` without the `$Q(s, a)$` and exploration terms would reduce to greedy policy following, which cannot correct for network errors through additional search. The `$w$` parameter lets practitioners tune this trade-off; the paper does not report specific `$w$` values, which is a notable omission for reproducibility.

##### Expansion and Evaluation Phase

When the selection phase reaches a leaf node — a state that has not been previously visited in the search tree — the algorithm **expands** the node and **evaluates** it using the policy/value network:

1. **Expansion**: The leaf state `$s$` is added to the search tree as a new node. Its set of legal actions `$A(s)$` is determined by the task rules (e.g., which moves are valid given the current position of the empty tile in 8-Puzzle).

2. **Evaluation**: The policy/value network `$f_\theta$` takes the state `$s$` as input and produces two outputs:

   > $$(P_\theta(s), v_\theta(s)) = f_\theta(s)$$

   - `$P_\theta(s)$` is a vector of prior probabilities over all actions in `$A(s)$`. Each element `$P_\theta(s, a)$` represents the network's estimate of how promising action `$a$` is from state `$s$`, before any search has been conducted at this specific node.
   - `$v_\theta(s)$` is a scalar **value estimate** — the network's prediction of the expected future reward achievable from state `$s$` under optimal play. For Game of 24, this is the probability of eventually reaching 24; for 8-Puzzle and Pocket Cube, this is related to the negative distance to the goal.

These values are stored at the node for use in future PUCT calculations (the `$P_\theta(s, a)$` values) and for backpropagation (the `$v_\theta(s)$` value). The state is then marked as "visited" so that future simulations traversing to this state will recognize it as an internal node rather than a leaf.

**What this computes operationally:** When the search encounters a state it hasn't seen before, it asks the neural network for a quick assessment: "How good is this state overall, and which actions should I prioritize exploring from here?" The network provides these estimates in a single forward pass, which is computationally cheap (the network has only ~10⁶ parameters). This assessment then guides all future visits to this state through the PUCT formula, and the value estimate is propagated upward to update the Q-values of all ancestor states.

**Why evaluate at leaf nodes:** This is the key efficiency mechanism inherited from AlphaGo Zero. Without neural network evaluation, MCTS would need to simulate complete rollouts from each leaf node to the end of the game using random or heuristic action selection (classic "rollout policy"), which requires many steps per simulation and produces high-variance value estimates. The learned value function provides a low-variance estimate from a single network forward pass, dramatically reducing the number of simulations needed to achieve reliable Q-value estimates. The paper leverages the fact that `$f_\theta$` is four orders of magnitude smaller than the LLM — each evaluation costs a tiny fraction of what one LLM call would cost, enabling hundreds of simulations per problem while keeping total computation low relative to LLM-based search methods.

##### Backpropagation Phase

After the leaf node has been evaluated, the algorithm propagates the evaluation result **upward** through all the nodes along the path from the root to the leaf, updating their statistics:

For each state-action pair `$(s, a)$` along the traversed path:

1. **Visit count increment**: `$N(s, a) = N(s, a) + 1$`. This updates both the per-action visit count and, implicitly, the per-state visit count `$N(s) = \sum_a N(s, a)$`.

2. **Q-value update**: The Q-value for the state-action pair is updated to reflect the new evidence. For **non-terminal leaf nodes**, the update incorporates the network's value estimate `$v_\theta$`:

   > $$Q(s, a) = \frac{1}{N(s, a)} \sum_{i=1}^{N(s, a)} v_i$$

   where `$v_i$` represents the value backpropagated through this edge during the `$i$`-th simulation. In practice, this is implemented as a running average: the new Q-value is the mean of all value estimates that have been backpropagated through this edge. For **terminal nodes** (states where the problem is solved or a maximum depth is reached), the update uses the **true reward** `$r$` obtained from the environment rather than the network's estimate. This ensures that terminal outcomes — which are ground truth — dominate the Q-value estimates as simulations accumulate.

**What this computes operationally:** Each simulation's outcome (the value estimate at the leaf or the true reward at a terminal) flows up the tree, updating every action that was taken to reach that outcome. Actions that were part of paths leading to high-value outcomes see their Q-values increase; actions leading to low-value outcomes see their Q-values decrease. Over many simulations, the Q-values converge to the expected value of taking that action from that state — which is exactly what we need to select good actions during the final trajectory extraction.

**Why propagate upward:** This is the credit assignment mechanism. When a leaf evaluation reveals that a particular state is promising, the algorithm needs to attribute that promise to the sequence of actions that led there. By updating all ancestors, each action accumulates evidence not just about its immediate outcome, but about the quality of the entire subtree it leads to. This is what enables MCTS to discover long-range planning patterns: an early action that seems neutral might lead to a state from which many good continuations are possible, and backpropagation will gradually increase its Q-value as simulations explore those continuations.

##### Simulation Budget and Action Selection After Search

A single simulation consists of one complete pass through the selection, expansion/evaluation, and backpropagation phases — from root to leaf and back. After `$K$` simulations have been completed (where `$K$` is a hyperparameter that varies by task and phase), the algorithm selects the actual next action to take:

> $$\pi(a \mid s) = \frac{N(s, a)^{1/\gamma}}{\sum_{b \in A(s)} N(s, b)^{1/\gamma}}$$

where `$\gamma$` is a temperature parameter controlling the sharpness of the distribution. When `$\gamma \to 0$`, the distribution becomes concentrated on the action with the highest visit count (deterministic selection). When `$\gamma = 1$`, the distribution is proportional to the raw visit counts. The paper sets the simulation budget differently for regular search versus revision: for Game of 24, 200 simulations per action in normal search and 500 during revision; for 8-Puzzle, 20 simulations per action normally and 50 during revision; for Pocket Cube, 20 simulations normally and 500 during revision. These values reflect the computational budget allocated per decision — more simulations for tasks where the state space is larger or the value estimates are noisier.

**Why use visit counts rather than Q-values for final action selection:** Visit counts are more robust than raw Q-values because they integrate information about both the value of an action and the uncertainty about that value. An action with a high Q-value but low visit count may be an over-optimistic estimate from a small sample; the PUCT formula ensures that such actions receive high visit counts only if they consistently demonstrate high value across many simulations. By selecting based on visit counts, the algorithm naturally favors actions that have been both promising (high Q-value leads to high PUCT scores, driving more visits) and reliable (the high Q-value persisted under extensive exploration). This is standard practice in MCTS implementations following AlphaGo Zero.

#### 3.4.3 Policy and Value Network Training

The neural network `$f_\theta$` is the component that learns domain-specific planning knowledge from MCTS simulations and makes the overall XOT framework efficient. Without it, MCTS would require many more simulations per decision (relying on random rollouts or heuristics), making the search too expensive. The training process iteratively improves `$f_\theta$` by having it learn to predict the outcomes of MCTS simulations, creating a positive feedback loop: better networks guide more efficient search, and more efficient search produces better training data.

##### Training Data Generation

The training data is generated through **self-play**: MCTS plays against the problem environment, exploring thought trajectories from training problem instances. During each simulation, the algorithm records:

1. **The state** `$s$` — the configuration of the problem at the point where a decision is made.
2. **The search probabilities** `$\varepsilon(s)$` — a vector where each element `$\varepsilon_a$` is the empirical probability of selecting action `$a$` from state `$s$` under the MCTS search process, computed from the visit counts as:

   > $$\varepsilon_a \propto N(s, a)^{1/\gamma}$$

   This represents the "improved" policy discovered by MCTS, which is typically stronger than the raw network predictions `$P_\theta(s)$` because it incorporates the results of explicit search.

3. **The ground truth value** `$v(s)$` — the actual outcome achieved from state `$s$`, obtained by "accumulating rewards along the trajectory starting from state `$s$`." For states along a completed trajectory, this is the sum of future rewards (which, for the tasks in the paper, corresponds to whether the problem was solved and how efficiently). For terminal states, it is the actual reward received.

These triplets `$(s, \varepsilon(s), v(s))$` form the training dataset. The key insight is that `$\varepsilon(s)$` and `$v(s)$` encode the results of **computation-heavy search** — they reflect what MCTS discovered by exploring many possible trajectories — and the network's job is to learn to **predict these search results directly**, thereby amortizing the cost of search into a fast forward pass.

##### Training Loop

The training proceeds in iterations, where each iteration consists of:

1. **Self-play phase**: Use the current policy/value network `$f_\theta$` to guide MCTS simulations on training problems, generating new `$(s, \varepsilon(s), v(s))$` triplets.
2. **Training phase**: Update `$f_\theta$` by minimizing a combined loss function on the collected data.

The paper specifies that training runs for "three iterations, with each iteration comprising 10 self-play episodes for MCTS." This is a relatively small amount of training, reflecting the fact that the state spaces for these tasks are modest compared to the game environments (Go, Chess) for which AlphaGo Zero was originally designed.

##### Loss Function

The network `$f_\theta$` is trained to simultaneously predict both the state value and the action probabilities discovered by MCTS. The loss function combines a value prediction error (mean squared error) and a policy prediction error (cross-entropy):

> $$\mathcal{L} = (v(s) - v_\theta(s))^2 + \varepsilon(s)^T \log P_\theta(s)$$

where:

- `$v(s)$` is the ground truth value accumulated from state `$s$` (obtained from MCTS simulations or actual rewards).
- `$v_\theta(s)$` is the network's predicted value for state `$s$` — its estimate of the expected future reward.
- `$\varepsilon(s)$` is the vector of search probabilities from MCTS (the "improved" policy after search).
- `$P_\theta(s)$` is the vector of action probabilities predicted by the network (before search).
- `$\varepsilon(s)^T \log P_\theta(s)$` is the cross-entropy between the MCTS-discovered policy and the network's raw policy: it sums over all actions `$a$`, the term `$\varepsilon_a(s) \cdot \log P_\theta(s, a)$`. This is maximized when the network assigns high probability to actions that MCTS search ultimately favored.

**What this computes operationally:** The first term `$(v(s) - v_\theta(s))^2$` is a standard regression loss — it penalizes the network when its value estimate deviates from the actual outcome. If the network predicts a state is promising but it actually leads to failure, this term is large. The second term `$\varepsilon(s)^T \log P_\theta(s)$` is a policy distillation loss — it encourages the network's raw action probabilities to match the distribution that MCTS arrived at after extensive search. If MCTS discovered that a particular action is strong after many simulations, the network should learn to assign high prior probability to that action even before any search is performed.

**Why this form:** The composite loss enforces a specific relationship: the network should not only know which actions are good (value prediction) but also which actions are worth exploring further (policy prediction). The policy prediction loss is particularly important because it determines the `$P_\theta(s, a)$` term in the PUCT formula — if the network's priors are accurate, MCTS can find good trajectories with far fewer simulations because it starts from a well-informed prior rather than uniform exploration. The value prediction loss ensures that the leaf evaluations during MCTS (which use `$v_\theta(s)$` for non-terminal nodes) are accurate, reducing the bias in the Q-value estimates. Together, these two objectives make the network a "fast approximation of MCTS" — given a state, it can immediately produce an educated guess about both the overall quality of the state and which actions deserve investigation, without needing to run any simulations. The paper uses a standard MSE for value (appropriate for a continuous prediction) and standard cross-entropy for the policy (appropriate for a probability distribution over discrete actions). The absence of a balancing weight between the two terms suggests the authors found the natural scales of the losses to be compatible, though this is not discussed explicitly.

##### Network Architecture

The policy and value networks share a common backbone: "a shared multi-layer perceptron (MLP) architecture with two layers and hidden units arranged as (128, 256)." This means:

- Input layer receives the state representation (the specific encoding of the problem state — for Game of 24, this is the set of remaining numbers; for 8-Puzzle, the 3×3 grid layout; for Pocket Cube, the colors of each face).
- Hidden layer 1: 128 units with some activation function (not specified, likely ReLU).
- Hidden layer 2: 256 units.
- Two separate output heads connected to the second hidden layer:
  - **Value head**: produces a single scalar `$v_\theta(s)$`.
  - **Policy head**: produces a vector `$P_\theta(s)$` with dimension equal to the number of legal actions for the task, normalized via softmax to form a probability distribution.

The total parameter count is reported as "approximately 10⁶" across all three tasks. This is deliberately tiny — a fraction of a percent of even the smallest LLM used (GPT-3.5 at 175 billion parameters). The paper emphasizes this design choice: "This design results in a considerably smaller model compared to LLM, making it much more efficient." The computational cost of running `$f_\theta$` hundreds of times per problem is negligible compared to a single LLM inference call.

##### Iterative Improvement Cycle

The training alternates between self-play (using the current network to guide MCTS and generate training data) and supervised learning (training the network on the generated data to better predict MCTS outcomes). This creates a virtuous cycle:

1. Initial network (randomly initialized or from previous iteration) guides MCTS.
2. MCTS, enhanced by the network's guidance, explores more efficiently and discovers higher-quality trajectories.
3. Training on these trajectories improves the network's value and policy predictions.
4. The improved network guides the next round of MCTS even more effectively.
5. Repeat.

The paper specifies three iterations of this process. The relatively small number of iterations reflects the limited complexity of the state spaces — unlike Go, which required millions of self-play games, these puzzle environments are simple enough that the network converges quickly. The paper does not provide training curves or discuss convergence criteria, which is a notable omission for understanding the practical training dynamics.

**How this connects to the overall XOT framework:** After training, `$f_\theta$` has internalized domain knowledge about which states are promising and which actions lead to solutions. It can now guide MCTS on **new, unseen problems** without requiring any additional training. This generalization is what enables XOT's efficiency during inference: for each new problem instance, the trained network provides informed priors that dramatically reduce the number of MCTS simulations needed, and the MCTS search produces thought trajectories that encode the discovered solution path.

#### 3.4.4 Thought Inference with MCTS

Once the policy/value network `$f_\theta$` is trained, it is used to guide MCTS in generating thought trajectories for new problem instances. This is the inference-time deployment of the search module, and it operates as follows:

##### MCTS-Guided Thought Search

Given a new problem (which was not seen during training), XOT runs `$K$` MCTS simulations to search for solution trajectories. In each simulation, the three-phase cycle (selection, expansion/evaluation, backpropagation) operates exactly as during training, with one difference: the network `$f_\theta$` is now frozen — it is not updated based on the outcomes of these inference-time simulations.

During selection, the PUCT formula uses the network's `$P_\theta(s, a)$` values as action priors and the accumulated Q-values from previous inference-time simulations. During expansion of new leaf nodes, the network provides the value estimate `$v_\theta(s)$` and action priors `$P_\theta(s)$` through a forward pass. During backpropagation, the value estimates (or true rewards for terminal states) flow up to update Q-values along the traversed path.

The paper specifies different simulation budgets for different tasks: Game of 24 uses 200 simulations per action during normal search; 8-Puzzle uses 20 simulations per action; Pocket Cube uses 20 simulations per action. These numbers reflect the complexity of the state space and the depth of the solution — Game of 24 requires fewer total steps (exactly 3 intermediate equations) but has a larger branching factor at each step (many possible pairs of numbers and operations), while 8-Puzzle can require up to 9 steps but has at most 4 actions per state.

##### Extracting Thought Trajectories

After completing the `$K$` simulations, the algorithm extracts one or more thought trajectories depending on whether a single solution or multiple solutions are required:

**For single-solution problems:** Starting from the initial state `$s_0$`, at each state `$s$` the algorithm selects the action with the **highest visit count** `$N(s, a)$`. This produces a single deterministic trajectory:

> $$a^* = \arg\max_a N(s, a)$$

This trajectory is the most-explored path through the search tree — the one that MCTS, guided by the network, found most promising across all simulations.

**For multi-solution problems:** The algorithm samples `$M$` distinct thought trajectories following a probability distribution derived from the normalized visit counts:

> $$\varepsilon_a \propto N(s, a)^{1/\gamma}$$

For each trajectory, at each state, an action is sampled according to this distribution (rather than taking the argmax). After sampling many candidate trajectories (the paper uses 500 for Game of 24 and 50 for 8-Puzzle and Pocket Cube), duplicates are removed, and the top `$M$` trajectories with the highest total visit counts (or other selection criteria) are retained. The paper sets `$M = 3$` for all multi-solution experiments.

**What this produces:** A thought trajectory is a sequence of state-action pairs `$T^* = \{\tau_1, \tau_2, ..., \tau_N\} = \{(s_0, a_0), (s_1, a_1), ..., (s_{N-1}, a_{N-1})\}$`, where `$s_N$` is the terminal goal state. For Game of 24, `$N = 3$` (three intermediate equations leading to 24). For 8-Puzzle and Pocket Cube, `$N$` varies depending on the solution length (up to 9 and 4 respectively for the test problems). For multi-solution problems, multiple such trajectories are produced, and they may share common prefixes — "the generated thoughts intertwine during intermediate steps and converge towards the final goal state" (Section 4.5) — producing a graph-like structure rather than disjoint paths.

**Why use visit counts for extraction:** The visit count distribution reflects the aggregate result of all simulations, integrating both the network's prior beliefs and the empirical outcomes discovered through search. Actions that were frequently visited are those that the PUCT formula consistently preferred, which generally means they had strong Q-values and/or strong prior probabilities. This is more robust than using raw Q-values (which can be noisy for actions with few visits) or raw network probabilities (which haven't been refined by search). The temperature parameter `$\gamma$` controls diversity: higher `$\gamma$` makes the distribution flatter, producing more diverse trajectories for multi-solution scenarios.

##### The Role of the Network in Enabling Generalization

A crucial aspect of this inference process is that `$f_\theta$` was trained on a **different set of problem instances** from those it's being tested on. The network has never seen the specific initial states of the test problems during training. However, because it learned general patterns — which states are promising, which actions tend to work in given situations — it can guide MCTS effectively on new instances.

The paper quantifies this generalization through the training/testing split: Game of 24 uses 1,225 training problems and 137 test problems; 8-Puzzle uses 300 training and 119 test; Pocket Cube uses 1,000 training and 183 test. The test problems span "various difficulty intervals" for Game of 24, are "randomly generated" for 8-Puzzle (with all being solvable within 9 steps), and are generated by applying 5 random scrambling moves to a solved Pocket Cube (with all being solvable within 4 steps). The fact that XOT achieves high accuracy on these held-out problems demonstrates that the network has learned transferable planning knowledge, not just memorized solutions to training instances.

This generalization capability is what distinguishes XOT from pure fine-tuning approaches. The LLaMA-2-13B fine-tuning baseline (Section 4) achieves 0% on 8-Puzzle and Pocket Cube despite being trained on the full training set with ground truth labels — it fails to generalize because it attempts to memorize input-output mappings rather than learning the underlying state transition dynamics that MCTS with `$f_\theta$` captures. The network learns that *being in a particular configuration* is good or bad, and that *certain types of moves* tend to lead toward or away from the goal — knowledge that transfers to new initial states because the laws of the puzzle are invariant.

#### 3.4.5 Thought-to-Prompt Parsing

The MCTS search produces thought trajectories in a structured, machine-oriented format: sequences of state-action pairs. These are not directly consumable by an LLM, which expects natural language text. The **thought-to-prompt parsing** stage converts these structured trajectories into textual descriptions that the LLM can understand and reason about.

The paper describes the conversion process as follows: "we transform both the state and action at each step of the thought, i.e., `$\tau = \{s, a\}$` in `$T^*$`, into text. This conversion aims to provide a comprehensive state transition, facilitating LLMs in better understanding the task step by step."

**What this produces concretely:** For each step in the trajectory, the parser generates text describing (a) the current state of the problem and (b) the action taken to transition to the next state. The exact format varies by task. Examining the prompt examples in Appendix D:

- **Game of 24**: Each step shows the computation performed and the resulting intermediate state. For example, "Step 1: 12 * 2 = 24 (left: 9 10 24)" describes the action (multiply 12 and 2), the result (24), and the updated set of remaining numbers (9, 10, 24).

- **8-Puzzle**: Each step shows the move taken and the resulting grid configuration. For example, "Step 1: Choose one valid move from: [Left, Up]. Move: Left. Current State: [3×3 grid representation]." The state is represented as a 3×3 text grid with numbers and the empty space marker.

- **Pocket Cube**: Each step shows the rotation move and the resulting cube face configuration. For example, "[Step 1] [Move] R [Current Cube State] [representation of all six faces]."

**For multi-solution scenarios:** "In the case of multi-solution scenarios, multiple trajectories are concatenated" into a single prompt. This means the LLM receives all solution paths at once, presented sequentially in the prompt. The paper notes that this design choice means "we only require a single LLM inference call at this stage" — even when generating multiple solutions, only one prompt (containing all trajectories) is sent to the LLM.

**Why this format matters:** The textual thought representation serves as an **external cognitive scaffold** for the LLM. Instead of requiring the LLM to generate the entire reasoning chain from scratch (as in CoT) or to explore and evaluate candidate steps itself (as in ToT), XOT provides the LLM with a pre-computed reasoning structure. The LLM's job shifts from *generation* to *verification* and *synthesis* — it needs to check whether the provided trajectory makes sense and, if there are errors, identify where they occur. This is a fundamentally easier task for current LLMs, which have been shown to be better at critiquing existing reasoning than generating correct reasoning from scratch (as evidenced by the revision success rates of 47–91% in Tables 9-10 versus the near-zero baseline accuracies on spatial tasks).

The format is also carefully designed to be compatible with the baselines: "This format remains consistent across all baselines." This means the same textual thought representation is used whether the thoughts were generated by XOT, ToT, GoT, or CoT, ensuring fair comparison — any performance difference comes from the *quality* of the thoughts, not from differences in how they are presented to the LLM.

#### 3.4.6 The Collaborative MCTS-LLM Thought Revision Framework

The thought revision framework is XOT's mechanism for correcting errors in the MCTS-generated thought trajectories, and it is the key innovation that enables the system to achieve performance beyond what either MCTS alone or the LLM alone can achieve. The core insight is that **MCTS may produce suboptimal or incorrect thoughts, but the LLM can detect these errors using its semantic understanding**, and the MCTS can then be re-invoked to search for a corrected trajectory starting from just before the error point.

The paper explicitly frames the relationship: "The thoughts extracted from MCTS serve as a reference thinking process for the problem, aiding LLMs in a supportive capacity. The LLMs will leverage their internal knowledge to review the extracted thought, identify errors in the thought trajectory, and then ground its knowledge in collaboration with the MCTS to revise and refine the thought."

##### Step 1: Initial Thought Extraction

The process begins with the standard MCTS inference procedure described in Section 3.4.4: MCTS runs `$K$` simulations guided by `$f_\theta$` to produce an initial thought trajectory `$T^*$` for the problem. This trajectory is converted to text via the thought-to-prompt parser and presented to the LLM.

##### Step 2: LLM Error Detection

The LLM is instructed to examine the thought trajectory and identify whether any step is wrong. The prompt for error detection (Appendix D, "Revision:" prompts) follows a specific template:

- The LLM is shown the initial state and the complete step-by-step thought trajectory.
- It is told: "If the final answer does not reach the goal state, then the corresponding [Steps/Process] is considered [wrong]. Please help me identify the exact wrong step based on its left number, among [Step 1, Step 2, Step 3, ...]."
- For Game of 24: "If the final answer is not exactly 24, then the corresponding [Steps] is considered [wrong]."
- For 8-Puzzle: "If the final answer does not reach the goal state, then the corresponding [Process] is considered [wrong]."
- For Pocket Cube: "After finishing all the moves... The given [Process] is not correct because not every face has the same numbers in the end."

The LLM's output is an **error state** `$s_e$` — a specific step in the thought trajectory that it identifies as incorrect. If the LLM determines that the trajectory is correct (or cannot identify a specific error), "the thought remains unchanged" and the process proceeds directly to final answer generation.

**What makes this effective:** The LLM does not need to generate the correct solution from scratch — it only needs to *evaluate* an existing solution. This leverages the well-documented capability of LLMs to function as critics and evaluators even on tasks where their generative performance is poor. The paper provides quantitative evidence for this: the Revision Success Rates (Tables 9-10) show that GPT-3.5 detects errors at rates ranging from 20.00% (8-Puzzle, 1 revision) to 75.93% (Game of 24, 3 revisions), while GPT-4 detects errors at rates from 32.69% (Game of 24, 1 revision) to 91.38% (8-Puzzle, 3 revisions). These are computed as "the ratio of successfully detected errors to the number of failed cases without revision" — meaning that when the initial MCTS trajectory is wrong, the LLM can identify the error a substantial fraction of the time.

##### Step 3: Targeted MCTS Re-Exploration

When the LLM identifies an error state `$s_e$`, the system does not discard the entire trajectory. Instead, MCTS is re-invoked starting from **the parent state of `$s_e$`** — the state immediately before the identified error. The intuition is that the trajectory up to that point was correct, and only the decision at `$s_e$` (or the continuation from it) was flawed.

MCTS conducts an additional set of `$L$` simulations from this parent state, where `$L$` varies by task: 500 for Game of 24, 50 for 8-Puzzle, and 500 for Pocket Cube during revision (versus 200, 20, and 20 respectively for the initial search — the revision uses more simulations because it's targeting a specific problematic subtree). The search explores alternative actions from the parent state and their continuations, guided by the same policy/value network `$f_\theta$`, to produce a **revised thought** for the problematic segment.

The revised segment replaces the erroneous portion of the original trajectory, producing an updated thought `$T^*_{\text{revised}}$` that is correct up to the parent of `$s_e$` and then follows the newly discovered path from there to the goal.

##### Step 4: Iterative Refinement

The revision process can be repeated multiple times. After the first revision produces an updated trajectory, it is sent back to the LLM for another round of error detection. If the LLM identifies another error, MCTS performs another targeted re-exploration. The paper experiments with 0 to 3 revisions (where 0 revisions means the initial MCTS trajectory is used directly without any LLM error checking).

For **multi-solution scenarios**, "each solution undergoes this process individually" — each of the `$M$` trajectories extracted from MCTS is independently checked and revised by the LLM. The paper notes that the revision process can be repeated "several times to enhance the reliability of the answer."

The paper's results (Figure 4, Tables 3, 5, 7) demonstrate that revision progressively improves accuracy:

- Game of 24 with GPT-4: 74.45% (1 revision) → 83.94% (2 revisions) → 85.40% (3 revisions)
- 8-Puzzle with GPT-4: 93.28% (1 revision) → 94.96% (2 revisions) → 95.80% (3 revisions)
- Pocket Cube with GPT-4: 77.60% (1 revision) → 79.32% (2 revisions) → 83.61% (3 revisions)

##### Cost of Revision

The paper emphasizes that the revision process is efficient because it adds only a small number of LLM calls. The LLM is called once per revision round — it receives the (possibly revised) thought trajectory and outputs either an identified error or a confirmation that no error is found. The paper quantifies this: for Game of 24 with 3 revisions, GPT-4 requires ~1.78 LLM invocations (this is less than 3 because not all problems require all three revisions — some are solved after 1 or 2). The policy/value network is called more frequently during revision because the additional MCTS simulations require network evaluations, but given the network's tiny size (~10⁶ parameters), this cost is negligible compared to LLM inference.

**Why this collaborative framework works:** It exploits a complementarity between MCTS and LLMs. MCTS is excellent at systematic exploration through combinatorial state spaces — it can efficiently find paths to the goal that a human (or an LLM) might miss because it explores thousands of possibilities without fatigue or bias. However, MCTS is only as good as its reward function and value estimates; it may produce trajectories that look good according to the learned value function but contain subtle errors that violate problem constraints. The LLM, with its broad semantic knowledge and understanding of the problem's rules, can spot these errors. For example, in Game of 24, MCTS might produce an equation that uses a number twice or fails to use all numbers — errors that violate the game rules but might be missed by the learned value function. The LLM, understanding the rules from the prompt, can detect this violation and direct MCTS to re-search from the point before the violation occurred.

The paper frames this as making "MCTS into a world model for LLMs" — the MCTS module simulates the consequences of actions (state transitions), while the LLM acts as a meta-reasoner that evaluates the quality of the simulation and requests targeted re-simulation when the results don't align with its understanding of the problem constraints. This division of labor is what enables XOT to achieve the "Penrose triangle" trifecta: MCTS provides efficient, flexible exploration; the LLM provides high-quality semantic verification; and the iterative collaboration ensures high final performance.

##### Quantitative Evidence for Revision Effectiveness

The Revision Success Rate (Tables 9-10) provides insight into the revision mechanism's contribution:

- For GPT-3.5 on Game of 24, the success rate grows from 47.17% (1 revision) to 75.93% (3 revisions) — meaning that with three revision attempts, the LLM successfully identifies and corrects errors in about three-quarters of the initially-failed cases.
- For GPT-4 on 8-Puzzle, the success rate is 85.96% with 1 revision and 91.38% with 3 revisions — the LLM is highly effective at detecting spatial reasoning errors in the MCTS-generated paths.
- For Pocket Cube, the success rates are lower (58.59% for GPT-4 with 1 revision), reflecting the greater difficulty of the task — the cube state representation is more complex, making it harder for the LLM to verify correctness.

These numbers directly quantify the value of the LLM's contribution: without revision, the system would achieve only the "0 revisions" accuracy (approximately 45–62% for MCTS alone across tasks; see MCTS rows in Tables 3, 5, 7). The revision process bridges the gap from MCTS-alone performance to the final XOT accuracy by correcting the subset of MCTS errors that the LLM can detect. The fact that revision success rates increase with revision count (e.g., GPT-3.5 on Game of 24 goes from 47.17% to 69.81% to 75.93%) suggests that the revised trajectories are genuinely better — the LLM finds fewer errors in each successive round because the remaining errors are increasingly subtle or non-existent.

#### 3.4.7 Design Choices Summary: Why This Architecture Over Alternatives

The paper makes several architectural decisions that collectively define XOT's approach. Understanding the rationale for each helps clarify why the system achieves its claimed properties:

**1. External search module (MCTS) instead of LLM-internal search (like ToT).** The paper's key insight is that LLM inference calls are the dominant cost, and MCTS can perform hundreds of state evaluations using a lightweight network for the cost of a single LLM call. This flips the ToT paradigm: instead of using the LLM to both generate and evaluate thoughts (which requires `$O(\text{branches} \times \text{depth})$` LLM calls), XOT uses the LLM only for final verification and targeted revision (requiring `$O(\text{revisions})$` calls, typically 1–3). The four-orders-of-magnitude difference in model size between `$f_\theta$` and the LLM makes this trade-off overwhelmingly favorable — even 500 MCTS simulations with `$f_\theta$` cost less than a single GPT-4 call.

**2. Learned policy/value network instead of heuristic or random rollout.** Without a learned guidance mechanism, MCTS would require orders of magnitude more simulations to achieve the same search quality — the network's priors focus exploration on promising regions of the state space, dramatically reducing the branching factor in practice. The paper validates this implicitly through the simulation budgets: 20–200 simulations per action are sufficient to solve these tasks, which would not be possible with random rollouts given the state space sizes (181,440 for 8-Puzzle, 3.67 million for Pocket Cube).

**3. Offline pre-training instead of online learning.** The policy/value networks are trained once on a fixed set of training problems and then deployed on new problems without further training. This is efficient for deployment (no learning cost at inference) but relies on the assumption that the training distribution covers the patterns needed for test problems. The paper's generalization results validate this assumption for the chosen tasks, but it may not hold for tasks with more open-ended state spaces.

**4. LLM-as-critic instead of LLM-as-generator.** By having the LLM verify and revise MCTS-generated thoughts rather than generate thoughts from scratch, XOT leverages the empirically observed asymmetry in LLM capabilities: LLMs are better at evaluating the correctness of existing reasoning than at producing correct reasoning de novo, especially for tasks requiring long-range planning. This aligns with findings from other work on LLM self-critique and verification.

**5. Iterative revision instead of single-pass correction.** The multi-round revision process (up to 3 rounds) allows the system to progressively refine the thought trajectory, with each round potentially correcting errors introduced or missed in previous rounds. The increasing revision success rates (Tables 9-10) suggest this iterative approach provides genuine improvements rather than oscillating or plateauing after the first round.

**6. Multi-solution extraction from a single MCTS tree instead of separate searches.** The visit-count distribution naturally encodes multiple high-quality trajectories (all paths through the tree with significant visit counts). Extracting multiple trajectories from a single search leverages the fact that MCTS explores many possibilities simultaneously, producing diverse solutions without the `$M \times$` cost of running `$M$` separate searches. This is a natural advantage of the tree-search formulation over sampling-based approaches.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Diagnosing the "Penrose Triangle" as a Structural Tradeoff, Not a Collection of Individual Weaknesses

The paper's most conceptually distinctive contribution is not a method but a **diagnosis**: it identifies that performance, efficiency, and flexibility in thought generation are coupled by design constraints, forming a structural trilemma where any paradigm operating within the standard assumptions can achieve at most two of the three attributes simultaneously. This reframes the problem from "how do we incrementally improve ToT's efficiency while preserving its performance?" to "how do we break the assumptions that make the tradeoff inevitable?"

The standard assumption underlying all prior thought prompting paradigms — CoT, CoT-SC, ToT, GoT — is that the LLM serves as the **sole cognitive engine** responsible for generating thoughts, evaluating their quality, and deciding which to pursue. Under this assumption, the tradeoff is genuinely inescapable: evaluation requires LLM inference calls, exploration over multiple reasoning paths multiplies the number of evaluations, and thus any method that improves performance through broader exploration (ToT, GoT) necessarily sacrifices efficiency through increased LLM calls. Conversely, any method that maintains efficiency through single-pass generation (CoT) necessarily sacrifices both performance (no error correction) and flexibility (rigidly linear reasoning). Table 1 makes this visible: each paradigm gets at most two checks, and the missing third attribute in each case is directly traceable to the design choice of keeping all reasoning within the LLM.

The conceptual move that makes this diagnosis productive is the recognition that **evaluation and exploration are the expensive components**, and that the LLM is the wrong computational substrate for them — not because LLMs are generally incapable, but because their per-inference cost is four orders of magnitude higher than what the task demands (MCTS state evaluations cost ~10⁶ parameters × one forward pass versus ~175B parameters for GPT-3.5). Once this cost asymmetry is recognized, the solution direction becomes clear: offload exploration and evaluation to a lightweight external module, reserving LLM calls for what only the LLM can do (semantic verification and final synthesis). The paper's framing of this as a "Penrose triangle" — an optical illusion that appears possible but cannot exist — rather than merely as "current methods have limitations" elevates the diagnosis from observation to principle.

**Comparison to prior work:** Prior papers implicitly treated the three attributes as independent optimization targets. ToT (Yao et al., 2023) improved flexibility over CoT at the cost of efficiency, implicitly accepting the tradeoff as necessary. GoT (Besta et al., 2023) improved flexibility further (adding graph operations) but didn't challenge the underlying assumption that the LLM must perform all reasoning operations. No prior work had argued that the tradeoff is **structural under the single-engine assumption** and therefore cannot be resolved by incremental improvements to prompting. The paper's contribution here is diagnostic rather than algorithmic — it defines the problem in a way that makes the architectural solution (hybridization with an external search module) legible as the natural response.

**Significance beyond performance:** This diagnosis functions as a **design principle** for future systems: any architecture that aspires to all three attributes must decompose thought generation across components with different computational profiles. The specific choice of MCTS with policy/value networks is one instantiation; the principle generalizes to alternative exploration modules (learned heuristics, symbolic planners, retrieval-augmented generation) as long as they maintain the cost asymmetry that makes the decomposition worthwhile. The paper's Discussion section makes this explicit: "MCTS is functioning in a supportive role and can be substituted with alternative supervised or RL models."

**Evidence anchoring:** The trilemma is empirically substantiated across Tables 3-8. ToT (b=3) on Game of 24 with GPT-4: 60.58% accuracy but ~40 LLM calls — performance and flexibility at the cost of efficiency. CoT on 8-Puzzle with GPT-4: 7.56% accuracy but 1 LLM call — efficiency at the cost of performance and flexibility. IO on 8-Puzzle: 1.68% accuracy, 1 call — efficiency only. No prompting baseline simultaneously achieves high accuracy, low LLM calls, and flexible thought structures. The pattern is consistent across tasks and models, supporting the claim that the tradeoff is structural rather than accidental.

**Incremental or fundamental?** This is a **fundamental reframing** of the problem. It doesn't incrementally improve ToT or CoT; it argues that the entire paradigm of LLM-as-sole-reasoner hits an architectural ceiling, and breaking through requires changing the system architecture, not the prompting.

### Innovation 2: Externalization of Exploration — The LLM as Verifier, Not Generator

The second insight follows directly from the diagnosis: XOT's core architectural move is to **split the unified cognitive role that prior paradigms assigned entirely to the LLM across two components with fundamentally different computational characteristics**. A lightweight MCTS module (~10⁶ parameters) handles the computationally intensive work of exploring the space of possible thought trajectories and evaluating intermediate states; the LLM handles only semantic verification of the search output and final answer synthesis.

This is not merely offloading computation for efficiency. It represents a **reconceptualization of what the LLM contributes to structured reasoning**. In prior paradigms, the LLM is the generator — it must produce correct reasoning steps, evaluate their promise, and decide which to pursue. XOT's architecture implies a different theory: the LLM's comparative advantage is in **comprehension and critique**, not in systematic exploration. The LLM can understand whether a given reasoning trajectory is valid (as demonstrated by Revision Success Rates of 47–91% across tasks and models in Tables 9-10) and can synthesize a final answer from structured intermediate steps, but it cannot reliably generate those steps de novo for tasks requiring long-range planning or spatial reasoning.

**Comparison to prior work:** ToT and GoT treat the LLM as a general-purpose reasoning engine that performs generation and evaluation — the prompting technique structures how these operations are sequenced, but the LLM performs both. RAP (Hao et al., 2023, "Reasoning via Planning") uses MCTS to structure LLM reasoning but still requires the LLM to serve as the world model — evaluating states and predicting transitions — meaning the number of LLM calls scales with the search tree size. XOT inverts this: a learned lightweight network serves as the world model (predicting state values and action priors), and MCTS searches over this model, with the LLM called only in an outer verification loop. The four-orders-of-magnitude difference in model size between `$f_\theta$` and GPT-3.5 is what makes this inversion viable — hundreds of MCTS simulations with `$f_\theta$` cost less than a single GPT-4 call.

The externalization also changes the nature of what the LLM is asked to do. Rather than generating reasoning from scratch (which the near-zero CoT accuracies on 8-Puzzle and Pocket Cube show is prohibitively difficult), the LLM is asked to **detect errors in existing reasoning** — a task at which it proves substantially more capable, as the Revision Success Rates demonstrate. This asymmetry — better at critique than generation — is consistent with findings from the self-correction literature (Huang et al., 2023; Stechly et al., 2023) but XOT is the first to exploit it architecturally by having a separate module handle generation.

**Significance beyond performance:** The externalization principle provides a **template for hybrid AI systems** beyond the specific tasks evaluated. Any problem with well-defined state transitions and evaluable outcomes can be addressed by (1) training a lightweight model to approximate the planning dynamics, (2) using that model for efficient search, and (3) calling the LLM only for semantic verification and revision. This separates the question of "how do we make LLMs reason better?" into two more tractable sub-questions: "how do we train lightweight world models for specific domains?" and "how do we best use LLMs as verifiers of structured outputs?" The paper's preliminary results on Document Merging (Appendix C, Table 13) — where XOT achieves a score of 8.168 vs. GoT's 7.559 while using fewer tokens — suggest this template generalizes beyond game-playing tasks.

**Evidence anchoring:** The performance gap between MCTS-alone and XOT-with-revision quantifies the LLM's contribution in the externalized architecture. On 8-Puzzle (GPT-4, Table 5), MCTS alone: 51.26%. XOT with 3 revisions: 95.80%. The ~44.5 percentage point gain comes entirely from the LLM's verification and revision capability — the MCTS module is identical in both conditions. On Pocket Cube (GPT-4, Table 7): 46.44% → 83.61%, a ~37 point gain. These gains demonstrate that the LLM, in its role as verifier, provides transformative value on tasks it cannot solve generatively (CoT: 7.56% on 8-Puzzle, 1.09% on Pocket Cube).

**Incremental or fundamental?** This is a **fundamental architectural shift** — it changes what components constitute the reasoning system and what role each plays, analogous to the shift from end-to-end neural approaches to neuro-symbolic hybrids.

### Innovation 3: Iterative Bidirectional Collaboration as a New Interaction Pattern for Hybrid Reasoning

The third insight is the specific mechanism by which the MCTS module and the LLM interact: not as a one-way pipeline (search produces output → LLM consumes it) but as an **iterative, bidirectional loop** where the LLM critiques the search output, identifies specific error locations, and triggers targeted re-exploration from the point just before the error. This cycle can repeat multiple times, with each round tightening the coupling between systematic exploration and semantic verification.

The novelty here is not any single component — MCTS is standard, LLM-based verification has been explored in self-critique and Reflexion (Shinn et al., 2023) — but the **collaborative dynamic** where each component compensates for a specific weakness of the other. MCTS, guided by a learned value function, can efficiently explore large state spaces but may produce trajectories that violate problem constraints or miss subtle requirements — its behavior is only as good as its reward function. The LLM, with its broad semantic understanding, can detect these violations (e.g., an equation that uses a number twice in Game of 24, or a move sequence that doesn't reach the goal in 8-Puzzle) but cannot efficiently search for alternatives. In the XOT loop, the LLM's critique provides a targeted signal (the specific error state `$s_e$`) that focuses MCTS's re-exploration on the problematic subtree rather than requiring a full re-search from scratch. MCTS's revised trajectory, in turn, gives the LLM a better candidate to verify.

**Comparison to prior work:** Reflexion (Shinn et al., 2023) uses LLM self-critique to improve reasoning through iterative reflection, but operates entirely within the LLM's own generation process — the "reflection" is textual and conditions the next LLM generation attempt. XOT's revision loop is fundamentally different: the critique targets **externally generated** reasoning, and the response to critique is **targeted re-invocation of the search module**, not a new LLM generation conditioned on a reflection. This addresses the limitation identified in work like Huang et al. (2023) and Stechly et al. (2023) — that LLMs struggle to self-correct their own reasoning — by making error correction a search operation rather than a generation operation. The LLM's role is constrained to what it demonstrably does well (detecting errors) while the correction itself is handled by a component designed for systematic exploration.

**Comparison to RAP** (Hao et al., 2023): Both use MCTS + LLM, but the direction of dependence is inverted. In RAP, the LLM serves as the world model (simulating state transitions and evaluating states), and MCTS provides the search strategy over LLM-generated states — meaning LLM calls scale with search tree size. In XOT, the learned network serves as the world model, MCTS searches over it, and the LLM is an outer-loop verifier — meaning LLM calls are constant with respect to search tree size, scaling only with revision rounds (typically 1–3). The paper makes this distinction implicitly through the efficiency results: ToT requires ~40 LLM calls for Game of 24, while XOT requires ~1.8 regardless of the number of MCTS simulations (which can be hundreds).

**Significance beyond performance:** The iterative revision loop introduces a **generalizable interaction pattern** for hybrid systems: a fast-but-imperfect module generates candidates, a slow-but-accurate module critiques them with specific error localization, and the fast module re-generates candidates focused on the identified error regions. This pattern could apply to code generation (fast autocomplete → LLM review → targeted re-completion), document drafting (template generation → LLM editing → targeted re-generation of specific sections), or scientific reasoning (symbolic solver → LLM constraint checking → re-solving of violated constraints). The key requirement is that the "fast" module can be re-invoked with localized scope (search from a specific state, generate a specific section) rather than requiring full regeneration from scratch.

**Evidence anchoring:** The impact of iteration is directly quantified through the monotonic accuracy improvements with revision count (Figure 4). On Game of 24 (GPT-3.5): 45.36% (0 revisions, MCTS only) → 79.56% (1 revision) → 88.32% (2 revisions) → 90.51% (3 revisions). On Pocket Cube (GPT-3.5): 45.36% → 74.32% → 80.33% → 84.70%. The Revision Success Rate metric (Tables 9-10) further decomposes this: it shows the LLM successfully identifies errors in initially-failed cases at rates that increase with revision count — for GPT-3.5 on Game of 24, from 47.17% (1 revision) to 75.93% (3 revisions) — suggesting that earlier revisions make remaining errors more detectable, creating a positive feedback loop rather than diminishing returns.

**Incremental or fundamental?** The iterative bidirectional loop is a **novel mechanism** that goes beyond standard one-way integration patterns (pipeline, voting, ensembling) and provides a template for collaborative hybrid reasoning. While it builds on known components, the specific interaction dynamic — LLM localizes error → MCTS re-searches from error point → LLM re-verifies — represents a genuine advance in how neural and symbolic components can interact.

### Innovation 4: Empirical Evidence That LLM Planning Failures Are Failures of Generation, Not Comprehension

The fourth insight emerges from the experimental design rather than being explicitly theorized in the paper, but it may be the finding with the broadest implications for LLM reasoning research: **LLMs' catastrophic failure on planning and spatial reasoning tasks appears to be primarily a failure of autonomous reasoning generation, not a failure of reasoning comprehension**. When supplied with externally generated reasoning structures — even incomplete ones — LLM performance jumps by orders of magnitude compared to when they must generate the entire chain themselves.

This distinction matters because it suggests a fundamentally different diagnosis of LLM limitations than the one prevalent in the literature. Work like Valmeekam et al. (2023) and Stechly et al. (2023) has demonstrated that LLMs fail at planning tasks and cannot self-correct their reasoning, leading to the interpretation that LLMs fundamentally lack planning capabilities. XOT's results suggest an alternative interpretation: LLMs possess substantial latent planning *comprehension* (the ability to understand, verify, and complete planning trajectories) but lack the ability to *generate* those trajectories autonomously due to limitations in their autoregressive sampling process — perhaps because error compounds over long sequences, or because the model's training objective doesn't incentivize the kind of systematic state-space reasoning that planning requires.

**Comparison to prior work:** The dominant narrative from the self-correction literature (Huang et al., 2023; Stechly et al., 2023) emphasizes LLMs' inability to improve their own reasoning through iterative prompting. XOT complicates this narrative: the LLM *can* detect and correct errors when those errors are localized and the correction is handled by an external search module. The "cannot self-correct" finding from prior work may be specific to the setting where the LLM must both detect the error and generate the correction — XOT decomposes these, showing that detection works substantially better than generation-based correction.

**Evidence anchoring:** The incomplete thought ablation (Table 11, Section 4.4.2) is the critical experiment. By deliberately omitting the last step of the MCTS-generated thought trajectory, the paper tests whether the LLM can fill in the missing reasoning or whether it is merely copying the MCTS output. Results: on Game of 24 (GPT-4), XOT without revision but with incomplete thoughts achieves 43.07% versus 4.38% for pure CoT where the LLM must generate everything — a ~10× improvement from having a partial external structure. On 8-Puzzle (GPT-4): 40.34% with incomplete thoughts versus 7.56% for CoT — a >5× improvement. These are not copying effects; the LLM must generate the final step itself, but having the preceding structure dramatically improves its ability to do so correctly.

The complete-thought results are even more dramatic: 95.80% on 8-Puzzle (GPT-4, 3 revisions) versus 7.56% for CoT. If the LLM fundamentally could not comprehend spatial reasoning, no amount of external trajectory information would enable near-perfect accuracy. The LLM clearly understands the reasoning when it is presented; it simply cannot initiate and sustain that reasoning autonomously.

The fine-tuning baseline (LLaMA-2-13B: 0% on 8-Puzzle and Pocket Cube) provides a revealing contrast: even supervised training on correct input-output pairs doesn't impart the ability to generate planning trajectories, suggesting the generation problem is not merely a matter of insufficient training data but reflects a deeper limitation in what autoregressive language modeling can learn about systematic state-space reasoning.

**Significance beyond performance:** This finding reframes the research agenda: rather than trying to make LLMs better at autonomous planning (through more elaborate prompting or larger models), the more productive direction may be to develop methods for providing LLMs with structured reasoning scaffolds — whether from MCTS, symbolic planners, retrieval of similar solved problems, or human-provided sketches — and focusing LLM improvement on verification and completion capabilities, where they already show substantial competence. It also provides a more optimistic picture of LLM capabilities than the self-correction literature suggests: the capability is present but latent, requiring external structure to be accessed.

**Incremental or fundamental?** This is an **empirical finding with significant theoretical implications**. It doesn't introduce a new technique, but it changes the interpretation of what LLM failures mean, which in turn changes what solutions are worth pursuing.

### Innovation 5: Multi-Solution Generation from a Single Search Tree as Evidence of Inherent Thought Flexibility

The final innovation, while more specific than the preceding four, demonstrates a capability that no prior thought prompting paradigm has achieved: **extracting multiple distinct, valid solution trajectories for a single problem from a single MCTS search tree, with the trajectories naturally forming graph-like structures that include branching, merging, and looping**, all without the `$M \times$` cost of `$M$` separate searches.

This is significant because it shows that the flexibility axis in the Penrose triangle is not merely a matter of supporting diverse thought topologies in principle, but of **surfacing genuinely diverse reasoning paths that are already latent in the search process**. MCTS, by design, explores many branches simultaneously, and the visit count distribution across the tree naturally encodes which paths are promising. By sampling multiple trajectories from this distribution, XOT extracts solution diversity that was a byproduct of the exploration process rather than requiring separate dedicated searches.

**Comparison to prior work:** Prior paradigms either cannot produce multiple solutions at all (IO, CoT produce single outputs), produce multiple solutions through independent sampling that often yields duplicates (CoT-SC generates ~2–3 solutions on average, many identical, as the #Sol column shows), or produce multiple solutions at `$M \times$` cost (ToT would need to run the search separately for each solution). XOT extracts multiple trajectories from a single search, so the cost is amortized: the search tree is built once, and multiple trajectories are extracted from it. The paper reports that on 8-Puzzle (GPT-4, Table 6), XOT generates 1.52 solutions on average with 4.30 LLM calls, while ToT generates 2.97 solutions but requires 54.13 LLM calls — comparable diversity at roughly 1/12th the LLM cost.

The graph-like thought structures (Figure 5) go beyond what any prior prompting paradigm has demonstrated. The Game of 24 example shows multiple arithmetic paths converging on the same intermediate state — a merge pattern that trees cannot represent and that prior graph-based paradigms (GoT) can represent in principle but haven't demonstrated for multi-solution problems. The 8-Puzzle example shows "back-and-forth recurrent state transitions" — loops in the reasoning graph where the search explores alternative paths that temporarily return to previously visited states before finding the goal. This is a genuinely flexible reasoning structure that mirrors human problem-solving (trying an approach, backtracking, trying a different approach from an earlier state) and is inaccessible to any acyclic reasoning paradigm.

**Evidence anchoring:** The case study in Figure 5 provides concrete visualizations of the graph-like structures. The Pocket Cube example shows "four distinct pathways to reach the goal state" — different move sequences that all solve the cube — extracted from a single MCTS search. The quantitative multi-solution results show that these aren't just more numerous but qualitatively better: on 8-Puzzle (GPT-4, Table 6), XOT achieves a MultiAcc of 76.33% (meaning 76% of generated solutions are correct) versus the best baseline GoT's 16.61% — a ~4.6× improvement in solution quality without sacrificing efficiency.

**Incremental or fundamental?** The multi-solution extraction mechanism is a relatively **incremental technical contribution** (sampling from visit count distributions is standard MCTS practice), but the demonstration that a single search naturally captures solution diversity manifesting as graph-like structures with merges and loops is a **significant capability advance** over prior paradigms that were either structurally incapable of such patterns (chains, trees) or didn't demonstrate them in practice (GoT).

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The paper evaluates on three distinct tasks: **Game of 24** (137 test problems randomly selected from 1,362 games on 4nums.com, spanning difficulty levels from easy to hard; 1,225 training problems for MCTS policy/value network training); **8-Puzzle** (419 randomly generated solvable problems on a 3×3 grid, all solvable within 9 steps; 300 training, 119 testing); and **Pocket Cube** (1,000 training samples and 183 testing samples generated by applying 5 random scrambling moves to a solved 2×2 cube; all test problems solvable within 4 steps). These tasks were chosen because they require multi-step planning, have well-defined state transitions, and—in the case of 8-Puzzle and Pocket Cube—are known to be extremely challenging for LLMs without external scaffolding.

- **Base model(s).** The paper uses **GPT-3.5** (Ouyang et al., 2022) and **GPT-4** (OpenAI, 2023) as the primary LLMs for all prompting baselines and for XOT's verification/revision stage. GPT-3.5 has 175 billion parameters and GPT-4 is estimated at over 1 trillion. Additionally, **LLaMA-2-13B** (Touvron et al., 2023) is fine-tuned on the training data with ground truth labels as a comparison point to test whether planning knowledge can be directly distilled into a smaller model through supervised learning rather than requiring search at inference time. The paper also uses a **lightweight policy/value network** `f_θ` (~10⁶ parameters, a two-layer MLP with hidden units (128, 256)) for MCTS guidance, which is trained from scratch per task using self-play data from the MCTS simulator. Temperature and top-p are set to 0.0 for all LLM invocations to ensure deterministic outputs.

- **Metrics.** **Accuracy** is the primary metric: the percentage of test problems for which the generated final answer is correct. For Game of 24, it is a success rate across 137 test games where the final equation must be valid, use each input number exactly once, and equal 24. For 8-Puzzle and Pocket Cube, it is the percentage of test problems where the generated move sequence correctly reaches the goal state. For multi-solution scenarios, **Multi-Solution Accuracy (MultiAcc)** is computed as the average percentage of correctness across all solutions offered, and **#Sol** captures the total count of distinct solutions provided by each approach regardless of correctness. **LLM invoked** counts the number of LLM inference calls per problem (the paper's primary measure of efficiency), and **`f_θ` invoked** counts the number of policy/value network forward passes (relevant for XOT only, where this cost is negligible compared to LLM calls due to the ~4 orders of magnitude size difference). **Revision Success Rate** (Tables 9-10) measures the ratio of successfully detected errors to the number of failed cases without revision, quantifying the LLM's error detection capability.

- **Baselines.** The paper compares against six established prompting paradigms and one fine-tuning approach:
  - **Input-Output (IO)**: Direct prompting without intermediate reasoning steps. Uses in-context examples (5 for Game of 24, 3 for 8-Puzzle, 1 for Pocket Cube).
  - **Chain-of-Thought (CoT)** (Wei et al., 2022): Sequential step-by-step reasoning. Same in-context examples as IO, augmented with intermediate steps.
  - **Self-Consistency CoT (CoT-SC)** (Wang et al., 2023a): Majority voting over 10 independent CoT samples.
  - **Tree-of-Thought (ToT)** (Yao et al., 2023): Tree-structured reasoning with LLM-guided search. Tested with branch factor b=1 (top-1 candidate retained) and b=3 (top-3 candidates retained per step).
  - **Graph-of-Thought (GoT)** (Besta et al., 2023): Graph-structured reasoning with thought aggregation and refinement. Tested with k=1 (top-1 thought merged) and k=3 (1-3 thoughts selected for merging).
  - **MCTS alone**: The MCTS module without LLM verification or revision — just the trained policy/value network guiding search. This isolates the contribution of the LLM revision loop.
  - **LLaMA-2-13B fine-tuned**: A 13B parameter open-source model trained on (input, output) pairs from the training set for each task, using ground truth labels. This tests whether planning knowledge can be learned from static examples without inference-time search.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper measures compute in terms of **LLM inference calls per problem** (the dominant cost, given the 10⁶ vs. 175B+ parameter asymmetry between the policy/value network and the LLMs) and **policy/value network forward passes** for XOT. The policy/value network's total parameter count is reported as ~10⁶ across all three tasks, making its per-call cost negligible compared to any LLM invocation. The paper explicitly compares ToT's ~40–55 LLM calls per problem to XOT's ~1.4–2.3 LLM calls. MCTS simulation budgets vary by task and phase: for Game of 24, 200 simulations per action normally and 500 during revision; for 8-Puzzle, 20 normally and 50 during revision; for Pocket Cube, 20 normally and 500 during revision. The training cost of the policy/value network is also reported (Appendix B, Table 12): per iteration, training calls range from ~787 (Pocket Cube) to ~1,045 (Game of 24); testing calls per problem range from ~56 (8-Puzzle) to ~88 (Game of 24). The paper argues these training costs are amortized and that the recurring LLM cost of ToT/GoT during testing (~50 calls/problem on average) exceeds the total pretraining cost of XOT's policy/value model.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not employ cross-validation or multiple random seeds for the main experiments. The test sets are fixed splits: 137 problems for Game of 24, 119 for 8-Puzzle, 183 for Pocket Cube. Results are reported as single accuracy figures without confidence intervals or error bars. The policy/value network training runs for three iterations of 10 self-play episodes each; no information is provided about variance across training runs or sensitivity to random initialization. For the fine-tuned LLaMA-2-13B baseline, training runs for 5 epochs on eight V100 GPUs with 80GB memory each, with a cosine learning rate scheduler and learning rate 2e-5; only a single training run is reported. The absence of statistical rigor (confidence intervals, multiple seeds, cross-validation) is a significant methodological limitation—particularly given the relatively small test sets (~100–200 problems per task) where accuracy differences of a few percentage points could arise from sampling variance.

### Main Quantitative Results

The paper's experimental results span three tasks, each tested in both single-solution and multi-solution scenarios, with ablation studies on revision count and thought completeness. I organize the findings by analytical axis: overall single-solution accuracy (the primary performance claim), multi-solution capability (the flexibility claim), efficiency (the cost claim), and the breakdown by revision count (the collaborative framework claim).

#### Single-Solution Accuracy: XOT Dramatically Outperforms All Baselines, Most Starkly on Spatial Reasoning Tasks

**Game of 24 (Table 3).** The Game of 24 represents the "easiest" of the three tasks for LLMs—it involves arithmetic reasoning rather than spatial reasoning—and the baseline performance reflects this: the best prompting baseline, ToT (b=3) with GPT-4, achieves 60.58% accuracy. XOT substantially exceeds this:

- **GPT-3.5**: IO achieves 6.57%, CoT 2.19%, CoT-SC 2.19%, ToT (b=1) 5.84%, ToT (b=3) 10.22%, GoT (k=1) 2.92%, LLaMA-2-13B 2.19%. MCTS alone: 62.77%. **XOT with 1 revision: 79.56%**. **XOT with 3 revisions: 90.51%**. Relative to the best prompting baseline (ToT b=3), XOT (3 revisions) improves accuracy by ~8.9× (from 10.22% to 90.51%). Relative to MCTS alone, the LLM revision loop adds ~27.7 percentage points.

- **GPT-4**: IO achieves 10.22%, CoT 4.38%, CoT-SC 4.38%, ToT (b=1) 34.31%, ToT (b=3) 60.58%, GoT (k=1) 10.95%. MCTS alone: 62.77% (same MCTS regardless of LLM). **XOT with 1 revision: 74.45%**. **XOT with 3 revisions: 85.40%**. The best prompting baseline (ToT b=3 at 60.58%) is surpassed by XOT with even 1 revision (74.45%, +13.87 points) and further improved with 3 revisions (85.40%, +24.82 points). Notably, GPT-3.5 with XOT (90.51%) outperforms GPT-4 with XOT (85.40%), a reversal of the usual model capability ordering that the paper attributes to the revision process "mitigating the performance gap attributable to the modeling ability."

**8-Puzzle (Table 5).** The 8-Puzzle represents the class of spatial reasoning tasks where LLMs perform catastrophically without external scaffolding. The baseline results confirm this:

- **GPT-3.5**: IO 0.00%, CoT 0.00%, CoT-SC 0.84%, ToT (b=1) 5.88%, ToT (b=3) 6.72%, GoT (k=1) 3.36%, LLaMA-2-13B 0.00%. MCTS alone: 51.26%. **XOT with 1 revision: 59.66%**. **XOT with 3 revisions: 63.03%**. The best prompting baseline (ToT b=3 at 6.72%) is improved by ~9.4×.

- **GPT-4**: IO 1.68%, CoT 7.56%, CoT-SC 8.40%, ToT (b=1) 3.36%, ToT (b=3) 13.45%, GoT (k=1) 3.36%. MCTS alone: 51.26%. **XOT with 1 revision: 93.28%**. **XOT with 3 revisions: 95.80%**. This is the headline result: XOT achieves near-perfect accuracy (95.80%) on a task where the best prompting baseline reaches only 13.45%—a ~7.1× improvement. The gap between MCTS alone (51.26%) and XOT with revision (95.80%) is ~44.5 points, directly measuring the LLM revision loop's contribution on a task where the LLM's own generative capabilities are nearly useless (CoT: 7.56%).

**Pocket Cube (Table 7).** The Pocket Cube is the hardest task—requiring 3D spatial imagination with a complex state representation. Baseline performance is near-zero for all prompting methods, confirming that this task is essentially impossible for LLMs without external planning:

- **GPT-3.5**: IO 1.09%, CoT 0.00%, CoT-SC 0.00%, ToT (b=1) 7.65%, ToT (b=3) 17.49%, GoT (k=1) 1.64%, LLaMA-2-13B 0.00%. MCTS alone: 46.44%. **XOT with 1 revision: 74.32%**. **XOT with 3 revisions: 84.70%**.

- **GPT-4**: IO 1.09%, CoT 1.09%, CoT-SC 1.09%, ToT (b=1) 11.48%, ToT (b=3) 19.57%, GoT (k=1) 18.03%. MCTS alone: 46.44%. **XOT with 1 revision: 77.60%**. **XOT with 3 revisions: 83.61%**. The best prompting baseline (ToT b=3 at 19.57%) is improved by ~4.3×. The LLaMA-2-13B fine-tuning baseline achieves 0.00% on both GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 (the model is fine-tuned once but evaluated with different prompting strategies; the 0% reflects that the fine-tuned model hallucinates regardless of the prompt), which the paper uses to argue that "finetuning approaches are not suitable for planning tasks" of this complexity.

**A consistent pattern across tasks:** The baseline hierarchy is IO < CoT ≈ CoT-SC ≪ ToT (b=3) < MCTS alone < XOT (1 revision) < XOT (3 revisions). The gap between ToT and MCTS alone is substantial (e.g., 13.45% → 51.26% on 8-Puzzle GPT-4), showing the value of externalizing search to a dedicated MCTS module with a learned value function rather than using the LLM as the search evaluator. The gap between MCTS alone and XOT with revision (51.26% → 95.80%) is equally substantial, showing the complementary value of LLM verification. GoT underperforms ToT in most comparisons (e.g., Game of 24 GPT-4: GoT 10.95% vs. ToT 60.58%), which the paper attributes to the LLM's unreliability as an evaluator compromising the more complex graph operations.

#### Efficiency: XOT Requires Orders of Magnitude Fewer LLM Calls Than Search-Based Baselines

The efficiency results (LLM invoked columns in Tables 3, 5, 7) demonstrate the cost advantage of offloading exploration to the lightweight MCTS module:

- **Game of 24 (GPT-4)**: ToT (b=3) requires 39.83 LLM calls on average. XOT with 3 revisions requires **1.78 calls**. This is a ~22× reduction in LLM inference cost while simultaneously improving accuracy from 60.58% to 85.40%.

- **8-Puzzle (GPT-4)**: ToT (b=3) requires 54.13 LLM calls. XOT with 3 revisions requires **1.61 calls** — a ~34× reduction — while improving accuracy from 13.45% to 95.80%. This is the most dramatic efficiency-performance tradeoff reversal: XOT is simultaneously >33× cheaper and >7× more accurate.

- **Pocket Cube (GPT-4)**: ToT (b=3) requires 56.58 LLM calls. XOT with 3 revisions requires **2.00 calls** — a ~28× reduction — while improving accuracy from 19.57% to 83.61%.

The policy/value network invocation counts (f_θ invoked column) are higher for XOT: ~89–92 for Game of 24 (GPT-4, 1-3 revisions), ~56–62 for 8-Puzzle, ~76–85 for Pocket Cube. However, given the ~4 orders of magnitude size difference between f_θ (~10⁶ parameters) and GPT-4 (estimated >10¹² parameters), the paper argues these costs are negligible—hundreds of f_θ forward passes cost less than a single LLM call. The paper does not provide wall-clock time or FLOPs comparisons to substantiate this claim quantitatively, but the parameter count disparity makes the argument plausible.

The CoT and IO baselines require exactly 1 LLM call but achieve near-zero accuracy on spatial tasks, making their efficiency moot. The efficiency claim is therefore specifically that XOT achieves the performance of search-based methods (ToT, GoT) at the LLM cost of single-pass methods (CoT, IO), plus a small overhead for revision (0.4–0.8 additional calls for 1–3 revisions).

#### Multi-Solution Capability: XOT Generates More Correct Solutions with Fewer LLM Calls

The multi-solution experiments (Tables 4, 6, 8) test whether each method can produce multiple distinct correct answers for problems that admit more than one valid solution. The maximum solution count is set to 3 for all methods.

**Game of 24 (Table 4):**
- **GPT-4 MultiAcc**: IO 8.27%, CoT 7.79%, CoT-SC 8.03%, ToT (b=3) 39.90%, GoT (k=3) 10.46%, **XOT (1 revision) 76.25%**.
- XOT generates an average of 2.36 solutions per problem (#Sol) with only 2.31 LLM calls, while ToT (b=3) generates 2.78 solutions but requires 39.83 calls and achieves only 39.90% MultiAcc. The key metric is MultiAcc: XOT's solutions are correct 76.25% of the time versus ToT's 39.90%—meaning XOT not only generates solutions more efficiently but generates *higher-quality* solutions.

**8-Puzzle (Table 6):**
- **GPT-4 MultiAcc**: IO 0.84%, CoT 7.84%, CoT-SC 6.58%, ToT (b=3) 5.60%, GoT (k=3) 16.61%, **XOT (1 revision) 76.33%**.
- This is the most dramatic multi-solution result: XOT achieves 76.33% MultiAcc with 4.30 LLM calls and 1.52 average solutions, while the best baseline (GoT k=3) achieves only 16.61% MultiAcc with 22.76 LLM calls and 2.70 solutions. The #Sol for XOT (1.52) is lower than for some baselines, but MultiAcc is far higher: XOT generates fewer total solutions but many more *correct* ones.

**Pocket Cube (Table 8):**
- **GPT-4 MultiAcc**: IO 1.09%, CoT 0.82%, CoT-SC 0.82%, ToT (b=3) 6.52%, GoT (k=3) 16.85%, **XOT (1 revision) 77.41%**.
- Same pattern: XOT achieves 77.41% MultiAcc with 4.08 LLM calls and 1.72 solutions, versus GoT's 16.85% with 13.36 calls and 2.77 solutions.

Across all three tasks, XOT's MultiAcc substantially exceeds that of all baselines, even though XOT generates fewer average solutions (#Sol) than ToT or GoT in some cases. The paper interprets this as evidence that XOT produces fewer but more reliable solutions—diversity without sacrificing quality. The efficiency advantage in the multi-solution setting is particularly pronounced because XOT extracts multiple trajectories from a single MCTS search tree (amortizing the search cost), while ToT would require separate searches for separate solutions.

#### Revision Count Scaling: Accuracy Improves Monotonically, Cost Increases Sub-linearly

The ablation on revision count (Figure 4, summarized in Tables 3, 5, 7) reveals how performance scales with the number of MCTS-LLM revision rounds:

**Game of 24 (Figure 4a):**
- GPT-3.5: 45.36% (0 revisions) → 79.56% (1) → 88.32% (2) → **90.51%** (3). LLM calls grow from 1.00 → 1.39 → 1.58 → 1.72. f_θ calls grow from 68.73 → 92.15 → 93.87 → 95.94.
- GPT-4: 45.90% (0) → 74.45% (1) → 83.94% (2) → **85.40%** (3). LLM calls: 1.00 → 1.38 → 1.57 → 1.78. f_θ calls: 68.70 → 88.20 → 89.63 → 92.48.
- The largest gain comes from the first revision (+34.2 points for GPT-3.5, +28.6 for GPT-4). Subsequent revisions provide diminishing but non-zero returns (+8.8, +2.2 for GPT-3.5; +9.5, +1.5 for GPT-4).

**8-Puzzle (Figure 4b):**
- GPT-4: ~46% (0, estimated from MCTS alone at 51.26%—note: the 0-revision condition may differ from MCTS alone due to prompt format) → 93.28% (1) → 94.96% (2) → **95.80%** (3). LLM calls stay nearly flat: ~1.00 → 1.48 → 1.55 → 1.61. f_θ calls grow more: ~36.24 → 55.66 → 58.91 → 62.22.
- The first revision provides an enormous jump (~47 points), with subsequent revisions adding smaller increments.

**Pocket Cube (Figure 4c):**
- GPT-3.5: 45.36% (0) → 74.32% (1) → 80.33% (2) → **84.70%** (3). LLM calls: 1.00 → 1.55 → 1.81 → 2.01. f_θ calls: ~18.85 → 64.63 → 96.46 → 103.22.
- GPT-4: 45.90% (0) → 77.60% (1) → 79.32% (2) → **83.61%** (3). LLM calls: 1.00 → 1.54 → 1.79 → 2.00. f_θ calls: ~18.89 → 75.51 → 146.52 → 84.63. The f_θ call count for GPT-4 revision 2 (146.52) is a notable outlier, substantially higher than revision 3 (84.63), suggesting that some revision rounds require more extensive re-exploration than others, possibly when the LLM identifies errors that require searching large alternative subtrees.

**Key efficiency observation across Figure 4:** The LLM invocation count grows sub-linearly with revision count—for Game of 24 GPT-4, moving from 1.00 to 1.78 calls when going from 0 to 3 revisions. This is because not all problems require all revision rounds; the revision loop terminates early when the LLM finds no error. The f_θ cost grows more substantially (especially for Pocket Cube) because each revision round triggers additional MCTS simulations (500 for Game of 24 revision, 50 for 8-Puzzle, 500 for Pocket Cube), and these simulations require network forward passes for each node evaluated.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Number of revisions (Figure 4, Tables 9-10):** Accuracy improves monotonically with revision count across all three tasks and both LLM models. The first revision provides the largest gain in all cases (ranging from +28.6 points on Game of 24 GPT-4 to ~+47 points on 8-Puzzle GPT-4). The revision success rate (Tables 9-10), which measures the ratio of successfully detected errors to initially-failed cases, also increases with revision count:
- GPT-3.5 Game of 24: 47.17% (1r) → 69.81% (2r) → 75.93% (3r)
- GPT-4 8-Puzzle: 85.96% (1r) → 89.47% (2r) → 91.38% (3r)
- GPT-3.5 Pocket Cube: 53.00% (1r) → 63.64% (2r) → 72.00% (3r)

This monotonic increase suggests that earlier revisions correct the most detectable errors, making the remaining (subtler) errors in the next round easier for the LLM to identify—a positive feedback loop rather than diminishing returns in error detection, even as accuracy gains from additional revisions diminish. The Pocket Cube shows substantially lower revision success rates (53% for GPT-3.5) than 8-Puzzle (85.96% for GPT-4), which the paper does not explicitly discuss but likely reflects the greater complexity of the cube state representation making error detection harder.

**Incomplete thoughts (Table 11):** This ablation deliberately omits the last step of the MCTS-generated thought trajectory to test whether LLMs can independently complete partial reasoning structures or whether they merely copy the MCTS output. All experiments use **0 revisions** (no LLM error detection) to isolate the effect of thought completeness:

- **Game of 24 (GPT-4)**: XOT without revision and with incomplete thoughts achieves **43.07%**, compared to ToT (b=1) at 40.88% and GoT (k=1) at 9.49%. The baseline CoT without any MCTS thoughts achieves 4.38% (Table 3)—meaning the incomplete MCTS trajectory alone improves accuracy by ~10× over pure LLM generation, even though the LLM must generate the final step itself. GPT-3.5 achieves only 17.52% in the same condition, showing that the ability to complete partial reasoning structures is strongly model-dependent.

- **8-Puzzle (GPT-4)**: XOT without revision and with incomplete thoughts achieves **40.34%**, versus ToT (b=1) at 6.72% and GoT (k=1) at 3.36%. Pure CoT achieves 7.56% (Table 5)—incomplete thoughts provide a >5× improvement over the LLM generating everything from scratch. GPT-3.5 achieves only 2.52%, demonstrating that GPT-3.5 cannot effectively complete spatial reasoning trajectories even when given partial structure.

- **Pocket Cube (GPT-4)**: XOT without revision and with incomplete thoughts achieves only **6.01%** (GPT-4) and **5.46%** (GPT-3.5). This dramatic drop—from >77% with complete thoughts and revision (Table 7) to ~6% with incomplete thoughts and no revision—shows that for the most complex spatial task, the LLM is highly sensitive to thought completeness and cannot compensate for missing steps. The paper states this "demonstrates that for very complex tasks, LLMs are highly sensitive to the completeness of the thoughts provided."

The incomplete thought results provide the strongest evidence for the paper's implicit claim that LLM planning failures are failures of generation, not comprehension: on tasks where the LLM can complete partial structures (Game of 24, 8-Puzzle), performance with incomplete thoughts massively exceeds pure CoT, suggesting the LLM *understands* the reasoning structure well enough to fill gaps; but on the hardest task (Pocket Cube), even gap-filling fails, suggesting the reasoning is beyond the LLM's comprehension threshold entirely.

**Multi-solution extraction from single search tree (Tables 4, 6, 8; Figure 5):** Across all three tasks in the multi-solution setting, XOT consistently achieves the highest MultiAcc while using far fewer LLM calls than search-based baselines. The #Sol metric shows XOT generates fewer average solutions than ToT or GoT in some cases (e.g., 8-Puzzle GPT-4: 1.52 vs. 2.97 for ToT), but the MultiAcc metric shows the solutions are much more likely to be correct (76.33% vs. 5.60% for ToT on 8-Puzzle GPT-4). The case study (Figure 5) visualizes the graph-like thought structures: for Game of 24, multiple arithmetic paths converge on the same intermediate state (merge); for 8-Puzzle, "back-and-forth recurrent state transitions" (loops) appear; for Pocket Cube, "four distinct pathways to reach the goal state" are identified. These visualizations demonstrate that the flexibility of MCTS-extracted thoughts goes beyond what tree-structured methods (ToT) can represent, though the paper does not provide a quantitative metric for thought structure flexibility.

**LLaMA-2-13B fine-tuning baseline (Tables 3, 5, 7):** The fine-tuned model achieves 2.19% on Game of 24, 0.00% on 8-Puzzle, and 0.00% on Pocket Cube. This is a striking negative result: supervised fine-tuning on correct (problem, solution) pairs—using ground truth labels that are "considered more accurate than labels from MCTS simulations"—completely fails to impart planning capabilities for spatial reasoning tasks. The paper attributes this to "significant hallucination issues" and concludes that "finetuning approaches are not suitable for planning tasks" of this nature. This negative result is methodologically important because it shows that the planning knowledge cannot be compressed into model weights through standard supervised learning, justifying the need for inference-time search.

**Document Merging (Appendix C, Table 13):** A preliminary experiment on an NLP task beyond gaming puzzles. Using GPT-3.5, XOT achieves a score of **8.168** (on a 0–10 scale measuring a weighted combination of duplication minimization and information retention), versus GoT's 7.559 and ToT's 7.715. The token cost for XOT (15,270.80 average) is intermediate between GoT (27,685.28) and IO (2,292.60), demonstrating that XOT maintains a balance between quality and resource usage on non-gaming tasks. However, this is a single-task, single-model result without multi-solution evaluation, making it suggestive rather than conclusive.

**Network architecture simplicity (Section 4, "Policy/Value Networks Configurations"):** The policy/value network uses a shared MLP with "two layers and hidden units arranged as (128, 256)." The total parameter count is ~10⁶ across all three tasks. The paper presents this architectural simplicity as a feature—"this design results in a considerably smaller model compared to LLM, making it much more efficient"—but does not ablate the architecture choice (e.g., deeper networks, different hidden sizes, separate policy and value networks) to show whether performance is sensitive to these choices. The training uses "three iterations, each comprising 10 self-play episodes for MCTS," without ablating the number of iterations or episodes.

**GPT-3.5 sometimes outperforms GPT-4 with XOT:** On Game of 24 (Table 3), GPT-3.5 with 3 revisions achieves 90.51% while GPT-4 achieves 85.40%. This is a notable reversal of the typical capability ordering and suggests that the revision process can compensate for differences in base model strength—possibly because GPT-3.5's error detection (75.93% revision success rate at 3 revisions, Table 9) is higher than GPT-4's (60.00%, Table 10) on this task, or because GPT-4's generations are not sufficiently better to offset the revision advantage. The paper does not investigate this phenomenon in depth.

### Critical Assessment

#### Do the Experiments Support the Central Claim of "Defying the Penrose Triangle"?

The paper's headline claim is that XOT simultaneously achieves **performance, efficiency, and flexibility**—all three attributes that constitute the "Penrose triangle" tradeoff in prior thought prompting paradigms. I evaluate each attribute separately:

**Performance:** Strongly and consistently supported across all three tasks and both LLM models. XOT with revision substantially outperforms every prompting baseline by large margins: ~1.4× over the best baseline on Game of 24 (85.40% vs. 60.58%), ~7.1× on 8-Puzzle (95.80% vs. 13.45%), ~4.3× on Pocket Cube (83.61% vs. 19.57%). The performance advantage is larger on the harder spatial tasks, which is exactly where the value of external planning should be greatest. The inclusion of the MCTS-alone baseline (62.77%, 51.26%, 46.44% for Game of 24, 8-Puzzle, Pocket Cube respectively) correctly decomposes the performance contribution: the MCTS module provides a strong foundation, and the LLM revision loop provides the remainder (bringing accuracy to 85–96%).

**Efficiency:** Supported, but with important caveats. XOT's LLM call count (~1.4–2.3 calls) is indeed dramatically lower than ToT's (~23–57 calls) and GoT's (~7–24 calls). However, three considerations qualify this claim:

1. **The policy/value network training cost is not accounted for in the per-problem efficiency numbers.** Tables 3, 5, 7 report LLM invoked and f_θ invoked *during inference*, but the training cost (Table 12: ~787–1,045 training calls per iteration, with 3 iterations) is amortized over the test set. For small test sets like 8-Puzzle (119 problems), the amortized training cost per problem is non-negligible. The paper acknowledges this in Section 6 ("Limitation") but does not provide a break-even analysis showing how many test problems are needed for the amortized training cost to be outweighed by per-problem inference savings.

2. **The cost of difficulty estimation or problem-specific setup is zero for these tasks** because the state spaces are fully known and the MDP formulation is hand-crafted. For a new task, defining the state representation, action space, and reward function—and training the policy/value network on sufficient self-play data—would add upfront costs that are not represented in the reported efficiency numbers. The paper's Document Merging experiment (Appendix C) uses LLMs to provide rewards (following the approach of ToT and GoT), which changes the cost profile, but this is treated as preliminary and not integrated into the main efficiency analysis.

3. **Wall-clock time is not reported.** XOT's MCTS simulations are sequential within each revision round (each simulation depends on the tree state from previous simulations), while ToT's LLM calls could potentially be parallelized. For latency-sensitive applications, the total end-to-end time (not just LLM inference count) would matter. The paper does not discuss this tradeoff.

**Flexibility:** Partially supported. The case study (Figure 5) convincingly shows that XOT can produce graph-like thought structures with branching, merging, and looping—patterns that chains (CoT) and trees (ToT) cannot represent. The multi-solution experiments (Tables 4, 6, 8) show that XOT generates multiple valid solutions more efficiently than baselines. However, the paper does not provide a quantitative metric for thought structure flexibility—there is no measure of branching factor, merge frequency, or structural diversity—making the flexibility claim primarily qualitative. Moreover, the flexibility is a property of MCTS, not of XOT specifically; the paper's contribution is in showing that MCTS-generated thought structures can be effectively consumed by LLMs for verification and answer synthesis, which is a genuine novel demonstration but doesn't introduce new flexibility mechanisms beyond what MCTS naturally provides.

**Can all three attributes be simultaneously achieved in deployment?** The experiments demonstrate all three attributes on the test sets used. However, the efficiency results depend on the policy/value network having been pre-trained on similar problems. In a true zero-shot deployment on a completely new task, the training cost would be part of the total cost. The paper's claim of "defying the Penrose triangle" should therefore be qualified: XOT achieves all three attributes *at inference time, given a pre-trained policy/value network*. The upfront training cost represents a fourth dimension (training efficiency) that the Penrose triangle framing does not capture but that affects practical deployability.

#### Are the Comparisons to Baselines Fair?

The baseline setup is thorough and covers the major prompting paradigms, but several fairness considerations arise:

**ToT and GoT hyperparameters may not be optimal.** The paper tests ToT with branch factors b=1 and b=3, and GoT with k=1 and k=3, but does not report whether these values were tuned on a validation set or chosen a priori. Different branch factors or search depths could change baseline performance. The maximum step limits (9 for 8-Puzzle, 4 for Pocket Cube, relaxed to 12 and 7 for multi-solution) are set based on the problem generation procedure; this is fair given that all problems are guaranteed solvable within those limits, but it means the baselines have an advantage (knowing the maximum solution depth) that might not hold in realistic deployments.

**In-context example counts vary across baselines.** IO and CoT use 5 examples for Game of 24, 3-4 for 8-Puzzle, and 1-3 for Pocket Cube, varying by task and by single-solution vs. multi-solution setting. These counts appear to be chosen pragmatically based on prompt length constraints rather than standardized across methods, which could introduce a small confound—though the near-zero performance of IO and CoT on spatial tasks suggests that additional examples wouldn't meaningfully change the conclusions.

**GPT-4's exact model version is not specified.** The paper uses GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 via API, but GPT-4 has multiple versions (gpt-4, gpt-4-0314, gpt-4-0613, etc.) with potentially different reasoning capabilities. This detail matters for reproducibility, especially since GPT-4 behavior on reasoning tasks has been observed to change across versions.

**The MCTS module has access to privileged information that baselines lack.** MCTS uses the true state transition dynamics of the environment (the rules of Game of 24, 8-Puzzle, Pocket Cube) and receives reward signals based on distance to the goal. This is appropriate for the architectural comparison (external search module vs. LLM-only reasoning), but it means XOT's performance advantage comes partly from having a perfect simulator of the environment, which may not exist for all real-world tasks. The paper acknowledges this limitation implicitly in the Discussion (Section 6): "the implementation of XOT necessitates the training of additional policy and value models... this training process requires the acquisition of datasets from real-world environments."

#### What's Missing?

**No experiments on non-puzzle, open-ended reasoning tasks.** All three main tasks (Game of 24, 8-Puzzle, Pocket Cube) are deterministic puzzle problems with well-defined state spaces, known transition dynamics, and clear success criteria. The preliminary Document Merging result (Appendix C) is the only NLP task tested, and it uses only GPT-3.5 with a single evaluation metric. Tasks like mathematical theorem proving, multi-hop question answering, code generation, or creative writing—where state spaces are less structured and success criteria are fuzzier—would test whether the XOT framework generalizes beyond the MDP-with-known-dynamics setting. The paper claims XOT can generalize to "a broader spectrum of problem domains characterized by decomposable tasks with well-defined objectives" (Section 6), but this claim is not experimentally tested.

**No ablation on the policy/value network architecture or training regime.** The network uses a two-layer MLP with (128, 256) hidden units, trained for three iterations of 10 self-play episodes. There is no comparison to deeper networks, different hidden sizes, separate policy and value networks, more training iterations, or alternative search algorithms (e.g., A* with learned heuristics, BFS with pruning). This makes it unclear whether the reported performance is near the ceiling for the lightweight-network approach or whether further improvements are possible with architectural tuning.

**No statistical uncertainty quantification.** All results are reported as point estimates (e.g., "90.51% accuracy") without confidence intervals, standard deviations, or multiple random seeds. With test sets of only 119–183 problems, accuracy differences of a few percentage points could arise from sampling variance. The revision success rates (Tables 9-10) are also reported as percentages without sample sizes, making it impossible to assess whether differences across revision counts are statistically significant or within noise.

**No analysis of failure modes.** The paper reports Revision Success Rates (how often the LLM detects errors) but does not characterize what kinds of errors MCTS makes that the LLM catches vs. misses, or what kinds of errors the LLM introduces during final answer synthesis. A taxonomy of failure modes—MCTS produces invalid action sequence, MCTS produces suboptimal but valid path, LLM misidentifies correct step as error, LLM fails to identify genuine error—would substantially strengthen the analysis and guide future improvements.

**No cost comparison that includes all factors.** The per-problem LLM call count is a useful metric, but it abstracts away from differences in prompt length (XOT's prompts include full thought trajectories, which are longer than COT prompts), differences in per-token cost between models, and the cost of training the policy/value network. A full cost analysis would include: (a) training FLOPs for the policy/value network, (b) total inference tokens (LLM prompts + completions), (c) wall-clock time, and (d) API costs at commercial pricing.

**No experiment testing XOT's sensitivity to policy/value network quality.** The paper trains the network for 3 iterations and uses it directly. There is no experiment showing how performance degrades with a poorly trained network (e.g., after only 1 iteration) or a randomly initialized one, which would quantify the network's contribution and indicate whether XOT's performance is robust to approximate planning.

#### Conditional Claims That Need Qualification

**"XOT achieves 4× or greater efficiency":** This is not stated in those terms, but the paper's narrative implies large efficiency gains. The actual efficiency advantage depends on the baseline: compared to ToT (b=3), XOT uses ~22–34× fewer LLM calls while being more accurate. Compared to CoT (1 call), XOT uses more calls (~1.4–2.3) but is vastly more accurate. The efficiency claim should specify the reference point (search-based baselines, not single-pass methods) and the cost dimension (LLM inference calls, not total FLOPs or wall-clock time).

**"XOT defies the Penrose triangle":** This claim holds at inference time for the tested tasks, given a pre-trained policy/value network. It does not hold if the training cost of the policy/value network is included in the total cost, or if the task requires developing a new MDP formulation from scratch. The claim should be understood as "XOT achieves all three attributes simultaneously at inference time after upfront training of a lightweight planning module," not as "XOT achieves all three attributes from zero knowledge with no training cost."

**"LLMs can solve previously insurmountable spatial reasoning tasks":** The experiments convincingly show this for 8-Puzzle and Pocket Cube, but only when the LLM is supplied with MCTS-generated thought trajectories. The LLM alone (CoT) fails catastrophically. The conditional nature of this claim is explicit in the paper but worth emphasizing: the LLM's spatial reasoning capability is *evoked* by external structure, not *possessed* autonomously. Whether this counts as the LLM "solving" the task or the hybrid system solving it is a semantic question with practical implications for how such systems should be designed and evaluated.

#### Summary Assessment

The experiments provide strong evidence that XOT substantially outperforms existing prompting paradigms on three puzzle tasks requiring multi-step planning, both in accuracy and in LLM inference efficiency. The inclusion of MCTS-alone and incomplete-thought ablations effectively decomposes the contributions of the external search module and the LLM revision loop. The multi-solution experiments demonstrate that XOT's graph-like thought structures enable diverse solution generation that prior paradigms cannot match. The primary limitations are: (1) the evaluation is restricted to deterministic puzzles with known state dynamics, leaving generalization to open-ended NLP tasks largely untested despite a preliminary result; (2) the absence of statistical uncertainty quantification weakens confidence in the reported point estimates given small test sets; (3) the policy/value network training cost, while amortized, is not included in the efficiency comparison; and (4) the fairness of baseline comparison is reasonable but could be strengthened with hyperparameter tuning and standardized in-context example counts. The core architectural claims—that externalizing exploration to a lightweight search module and using the LLM as a verifier breaks the performance-efficiency-flexibility tradeoff—are well-supported for the studied task class. The broader claim that this template generalizes to "a broader spectrum of problem domains" is plausible but experimentally unsubstantiated beyond the single Document Merging data point.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### 6.1 The Policy/Value Network Training Cost Is Unaccounted for in Headline Efficiency Numbers

**The assumption or constraint.** XOT’s efficiency claims — requiring only ~1.4–2.3 LLM calls per problem versus ToT’s ~40–57 calls — measure only *inference-time* LLM invocations. The policy/value network `$f_\theta$` must be trained before it can guide MCTS, and this upfront cost is excluded from the per-problem efficiency accounting. The paper acknowledges this in Section 6: “the implementation of XOT necessitates the training of additional policy and value models to expedite the inference process. This training process requires the acquisition of datasets from real-world environments, introducing supplementary costs and efforts.” Appendix B (Table 12) reports that training calls for the policy/value network range from ~787 (Pocket Cube) to ~1,045 (Game of 24) per iteration, with three training iterations, totaling ~2,361–3,134 training calls per task. The per-problem testing calls are ~56–92 for `$f_\theta$`. The paper argues these costs “are deemed low, particularly in the context of tasks featured in this study,” but the argument is qualitative — no break-even analysis is provided showing how many test problems must be solved before the amortized training cost is recouped by per-problem inference savings relative to ToT or GoT.

**The consequence.** For a practitioner deploying XOT on a new task, the true total cost is: (a) define the MDP formulation (state representation, action space, reward function), (b) generate self-play training data through MCTS simulations (which may require a perfect simulator of the task environment), (c) train the policy/value network for multiple iterations, and only then (d) deploy inference on new problems. If the number of test problems is small — say, solving 100 Pocket Cube configurations — the training cost dominates the total computation, and the claimed ~28× efficiency advantage over ToT (in LLM calls) may not translate to lower total cost. The efficiency claim is specifically an *amortized* advantage: it holds when the upfront training investment is spread over many inference queries. The paper never quantifies the crossover point.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 12 provides the policy/value network call counts for training and testing per iteration, but these are reported in terms of network forward passes, not wall-clock time, FLOPs, or monetary cost. The paper does not translate these calls into a common unit with LLM inference to enable direct comparison. Appendix B argues qualitatively: “The computational cost of these recurring calls during testing exceeds the pretraining cost of the policy/value model in XoT” — but this compares ToT’s *test-time* LLM cost against XOT’s *training-time* `$f_\theta$` cost, which are incommensurate (a GPT-4 call costs orders of magnitude more than a 10⁶-parameter MLP forward pass). A proper amortization analysis would convert both to a common unit (e.g., FLOPs or API cost) and show the break-even point.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges the limitation in Section 6 (“Limitation”) and notes the policy/value network is “considerably smaller and more computationally efficient than the underlying LLMs,” but does not provide quantitative mitigation. The paper states an intention to “explore methods to enhance the efficiency of the training process for XOT in scenarios where the objectives are less straightforward” in future work. The mitigation is partial — the acknowledgment exists, but the practical decision-making information a practitioner needs (how many queries are needed to amortize training?) is absent.

### 6.2 The Approach Requires Well-Defined State Spaces, Transition Dynamics, and Reward Functions — a Hard Requirement That Excludes Many Real-World Tasks

**The assumption or constraint.** XOT is built on an explicit MDP formulation (Section 3.2): states, actions, rewards, and terminal conditions must be precisely defined for the MCTS module to operate. For the three evaluated tasks, these are trivially available: Game of 24 has combinatorial but well-defined arithmetic operations; 8-Puzzle and Pocket Cube have known state transition rules and computable distance-to-goal reward functions. The paper claims in Section 6 that XOT can generalize to “a broader spectrum of problem domains characterized by decomposable tasks with well-defined objectives,” but this description itself encodes the constraint — “well-defined objectives” means there must exist a computable reward signal that can evaluate any intermediate state.

**The consequence.** Many important reasoning tasks — open-ended generation, debate, multi-step research, creative problem-solving where correctness criteria are ambiguous or subjective — lack precisely these properties. For mathematical theorem proving, states (partial proofs) and actions (proof steps) can be defined, but the reward function (is this partial proof on track toward a complete proof?) is itself a research problem. For code generation, states (partial programs) and actions (added code tokens/statements) exist, but evaluating whether a partial program is “promising” without executing it is difficult — and execution requires a runtime environment. For multi-hop question answering, the state (information gathered so far) and action (retrieve/read next document) are definable, but reward signals (does this information lead toward the answer?) are fuzzy.

The broader consequence is that XOT is not a general-purpose reasoning enhancement for LLMs — it is a framework for tasks where the **simulator is available**. The MDP formulation acts as a gate: if you cannot write down `$r(s, a, s')$` for your task, XOT cannot be applied. The preliminary Document Merging experiment (Appendix C, Table 13) uses LLMs to provide rewards (following the approach of ToT and GoT), which partially relaxes this constraint — rewards come from an LLM evaluator rather than a ground-truth simulator — but this reintroduces LLM calls into the search loop and partially undermines the efficiency advantage. The paper does not explore this tension.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper is transparent that all three main tasks have fully specified environments (Table 2: explicit reward definitions for each task). Section 6 states the tasks are “characterized by decomposable tasks with well-defined objectives” and claims XOT can generalize to such tasks, but provides only one partial counterexample outside the puzzle domain (Document Merging, Appendix C). The Document Merging experiment uses LLM-based reward (following GoT’s approach), which means the MCTS module’s value estimates ultimately depend on LLM evaluations — but the paper does not report how many LLM calls this requires for reward evaluation, making it unclear whether the efficiency advantage persists when rewards are LLM-computed.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges the constraint implicitly in its description of the MDP formulation (Section 3.2) and in the Discussion (Section 6). The suggestion that “MCTS… can be substituted with alternative supervised or RL models for thought exploration and generation” is a gesture toward flexibility but does not address the fundamental requirement of having a reward signal. For tasks without clear rewards, the paper offers no concrete alternative beyond the Document Merging approach (LLM-as-reward), which was tested only preliminarily and not integrated with the efficiency analysis. The limitation is effectively unmitigated.

### 6.3 Evaluation Is Restricted to Three Deterministic Puzzle Tasks with Small, Fixed Test Sets — No Evidence of Generalization to Broader Reasoning Domains

**The assumption or constraint.** All primary experiments (Tables 3–8) are conducted on Game of 24 (137 test problems), 8-Puzzle (119 test problems), and Pocket Cube (183 test problems). These are all deterministic puzzle tasks with discrete state spaces, known optimal solutions, and exact correctness criteria. The paper claims XOT’s applicability to a “broader spectrum of problem domains” (Section 6) but provides only a single additional data point — Document Merging (Appendix C, Table 13) with GPT-3.5 — to support this claim. The three puzzle tasks share structural properties (finite state space, known transition dynamics, distance-to-goal metrics) that may not characterize most NLP reasoning tasks.

**The consequence.** A practitioner considering XOT for a task that is not a deterministic puzzle — mathematical word problems, legal reasoning, scientific hypothesis generation, multi-step planning from natural language instructions — has no direct evidence that the approach will work. The puzzle tasks differ from realistic LLM applications in at least three ways: (1) the state space is fully observable and can be represented compactly (a set of 4 numbers, a 3×3 grid, a 2×2 cube); (2) actions have deterministic, well-understood effects; (3) the distance to the goal is computable and provides a dense training signal. For tasks where states are represented in natural language (e.g., a partially-written essay), actions are open-ended (e.g., “add an argument” or “revise this paragraph”), and rewards require human judgment, the MDP formulation becomes substantially harder. The Document Merging experiment — where states are document drafts and rewards are LLM-computed — is a step toward this setting, but the single-result, single-model evaluation is insufficient to establish generalization.

Additionally, the test sets are small: 119–183 problems per task. With test sets this size, accuracy differences of single-digit percentage points can arise from sampling variance. The paper does not provide confidence intervals, and the fixed test sets (randomly generated for 8-Puzzle and Pocket Cube, selected for difficulty coverage for Game of 24) mean results could be sensitive to the particular test split. The absence of standard benchmarks (e.g., MATH for Game of 24-style problems, established planning benchmarks) makes it difficult to compare against the broader literature.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The main evidence is confined to Tables 3–8 (puzzle tasks) and Table 13 (Document Merging, GPT-3.5 only). The Discussion (Section 6) claims generalizability to “more complex problems” and mentions “code generation tasks” and “multi-agent planning” as potential future applications, but these are entirely speculative. The paper does not test on standard reasoning benchmarks (GSM8K, MATH, HumanEval, MBPP, ARC) that would provide externally validated comparison points.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not present the limited task scope as a limitation, framing it instead as evidence that XOT works on “challenging complex problems across diverse domains” (Section 1). The Document Merging result is presented as preliminary evidence of broader applicability (Appendix C), but it is a single experiment with no multi-solution evaluation and no revision loop ablation. The Discussion (Section 6) gestures toward future work on “code generation tasks” and “multi-agent planning,” but provides no concrete roadmap. The limitation is effectively unaddressed for practitioners who need evidence on whether XOT applies to their domain.

### 6.4 MCTS Requires a Perfect Simulator During Training — the Policy/Value Network Cannot Learn Without Access to Ground-Truth State Transitions and Rewards

**The assumption or constraint.** The training of `$f_\theta$` (Section 3.3) depends on MCTS self-play, which in turn depends on having access to the **true environment dynamics**. For Game of 24, this means the environment can compute the result of any arithmetic operation on any pair of numbers and determine whether a sequence of operations yields 24 using each input number exactly once. For 8-Puzzle, the environment can compute the resulting grid configuration for any legal slide move and can compute the minimum number of moves to the goal (for the reward signal). For Pocket Cube, the environment can compute the resulting face configurations for any rotation and the distance to the solved state. During training, when MCTS expands a node corresponding to taking action `$a$` in state `$s$`, the environment returns the actual next state `$s'$` and the actual reward — there is no learned world model or approximate simulator.

**The consequence.** For any task where a perfect simulator does not exist, XOT’s training pipeline breaks. Real-world domains — robotics (simulators exist but have sim-to-real gaps), scientific experimentation (outcomes must be empirically measured), business process optimization (dynamics may be stochastic and partially observed), software engineering (program execution requires a runtime, and “correctness” may be multi-faceted) — either lack perfect simulators entirely or have simulators that are expensive to query. In these settings, the MCTS self-play training loop cannot operate directly.

The paper gestures toward an alternative in Appendix C and Section 6: using the LLM itself as the reward provider and potentially as the transition model. However, this reintroduces LLM calls into the search loop — exactly what XOT’s architecture was designed to avoid. If every state evaluation during MCTS training requires an LLM call, the training cost becomes prohibitive (MCTS training requires thousands of state evaluations), and the efficiency advantage over ToT/GoT collapses. The Document Merging result (Table 13) uses LLM-based rewards for the final task evaluation but does not specify how rewards were provided during MCTS training for this task. The paper does not address this tension.

A further consequence: the requirement for a perfect simulator during training means XOT cannot self-improve from its own outputs in deployment. The policy/value network is frozen after training; if the task distribution shifts, the network cannot adapt without access to the simulator for retraining. This contrasts with approaches that use LLM-based verification (like Reflexion) which can, in principle, adapt to new problems without retraining.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The three main tasks all use ground-truth simulators (explicitly described in Table 2’s reward definitions). The paper does not include any experiment where the policy/value network is trained with an imperfect simulator, a learned world model, or LLM-provided rewards — so there is no evidence for how performance degrades as simulation fidelity decreases. Section 6 acknowledges that “changes in the environment could lead to inaccuracies in MCTS and subsequently in the thoughts provided to LLMs,” but frames this as a risk of environment *change* rather than a limitation on environments where perfect simulation is *unavailable from the start*. Appendix C’s Document Merging experiment is the only example of a task without a ground-truth reward function, but training details for the policy/value network are not provided for this task.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not directly mitigate this limitation. The Discussion (Section 6) notes that MCTS “can be substituted with alternative supervised or RL models for thought exploration and generation,” but this substitution still requires a training signal — and if that signal comes from LLMs, the efficiency argument is undermined. The paper treats this as a direction for “future research endeavors” (Section 6) but presents no experimental results on simulator-free training. For a practitioner evaluating XOT, this limitation is a hard constraint: if you don’t have a simulator, the paper provides no validated path forward.

### 6.5 No Evidence That the Revision Loop Works on Tasks Where the LLM Cannot Reliably Evaluate Solution Correctness

**The assumption or constraint.** The thought revision framework (Section 3.4, Figure 3) depends on the LLM’s ability to **detect errors** in the MCTS-generated thought trajectory. The LLM is prompted to examine the trajectory and identify the specific step that is wrong. This assumes the LLM can, with reasonable reliability, assess whether a given intermediate state-action pair is correct or erroneous. The Revision Success Rate metric (Tables 9-10) quantifies this: for GPT-4 on 8-Puzzle, the LLM successfully detects errors in 85.96% of failed cases (1 revision), rising to 91.38% (3 revisions). For GPT-3.5 on Pocket Cube, this rate is only 53.00% (1 revision), rising to 72.00% (3 revisions). But both tasks have clearly defined correctness criteria — the puzzle is either solved or not, and intermediate errors typically manifest as illegal moves or states that deviate from the solution path.

**The consequence.** On tasks where correctness is ambiguous, multi-dimensional, or requires subjective judgment, the LLM’s error detection capability may degrade substantially. If the LLM cannot reliably identify errors, the revision loop provides no benefit — and may even be harmful if the LLM falsely flags correct steps as errors, causing the MCTS to re-explore perfectly good subtrees and potentially produce inferior alternatives. The paper’s Pocket Cube results already hint at this: the revision success rate for GPT-3.5 (53–72%) is substantially lower than for 8-Puzzle (GPT-4: 86–91%), correlating with the task’s greater complexity. As task complexity increases, the revision loop’s effectiveness diminishes.

More subtly, the revision success rate only measures whether the LLM *detects* an error in a trajectory that is already known to be wrong (failed cases without revision). It does not measure the **false positive rate** — how often the LLM incorrectly identifies a correct step as erroneous. If the false positive rate is non-negligible, the revision process could degrade initially correct trajectories. The paper provides no metric for this, making it impossible to assess the risk of revision-induced errors.

A further concern: the revision loop is only tested in settings where the MCTS module produces a *complete* thought trajectory before the LLM reviews it. On tasks where solutions are very long (hundreds of steps), presenting the entire trajectory to the LLM and asking it to localize a single erroneous step may exceed the LLM’s context window or its ability to attend to distant dependencies. The paper’s tasks have relatively short trajectories (3 steps for Game of 24, up to 9 for 8-Puzzle, up to 4 for Pocket Cube), so this limitation is not tested.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Tables 9-10 provide revision success rates, revealing substantial variation across tasks and models (from 20% for GPT-3.5 on 8-Puzzle with 1 revision to 91% for GPT-4 on 8-Puzzle with 3 revisions). However, these are success rates conditional on the trajectory being incorrect — they do not characterize false positives. The paper does not report how often the LLM flags a correct trajectory as erroneous, nor does it provide examples of revision failures. No ablation tests the revision loop’s sensitivity to trajectory length or to the subtlety of errors.

**Mitigation status.** The paper partially mitigates this by noting that the revision process is optional: “If the LLM identifies an error… if no error is found, the thought remains unchanged.” This means the revision loop degrades gracefully if the LLM is conservative — it will simply not trigger revisions when uncertain. But this also means the performance is capped by the LLM’s discriminative ability; if the LLM cannot identify errors, XOT’s accuracy reverts to MCTS-alone levels (51.26% on 8-Puzzle, 46.44% on Pocket Cube for GPT-4; Tables 5, 7). The paper does not propose mechanisms for improving error detection on tasks where the LLM struggles (e.g., better prompting, ensemble verification, confidence calibration). The limitation is acknowledged implicitly through the Revision Success Rate metric but not addressed through design modifications.

### 6.6 The Framework Assumes Deterministic, Fully Observable Environments — Stochasticity, Partial Observability, and Adversarial Dynamics Are Not Addressed

**The assumption or constraint.** The MDP formulation (Section 3.2) assumes a fully observable state space where the current state `$s_t$` is known exactly and actions have deterministic effects. For Game of 24, the state is the exact set of remaining numbers; for 8-Puzzle, the exact grid configuration; for Pocket Cube, the exact colors of each face. The MCTS search operates on these exact states, expanding actions whose outcomes are uniquely determined by the environment’s transition rules. The reward signals are also deterministic: distance-to-goal is a function of the current state alone.

**The consequence.** Many real-world planning tasks violate these assumptions. In robotics, state estimation is noisy (partial observability — the robot doesn’t know its exact pose) and actions have stochastic outcomes (a move command may not execute perfectly). In dialogue systems, the state (conversation history, user’s mental model, task progress) is only partially observable through text. In multi-agent settings, the environment includes other agents whose strategies are unknown and potentially adversarial. XOT’s MCTS formulation does not accommodate any of these complications: there is no belief state representation, no stochastic transition model, no opponent modeling.

The paper’s MDP framework also assumes the action space is discrete and enumerable. For Game of 24, actions are pairs of numbers and operations; for 8-Puzzle, four directional moves; for Pocket Cube, nine rotation types. In continuous control domains (robotic manipulation) or combinatorial action spaces (code generation, where the action is “write the next line of code”), defining `$A(s)$` as an enumerable set is infeasible. The policy network’s architecture (outputting a softmax distribution over actions) assumes a fixed-size, discrete action space — it cannot handle continuous actions or open-ended generation.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper explicitly describes the MDP components for each task (Table 2) as deterministic, fully observable, and discrete. There is no experiment with stochastic transitions, partial observability, or continuous actions. The Discussion (Section 6) mentions “multi-agent planning” as a future direction but provides no analysis of how XOT would handle it. The paper does not discuss these assumptions as limitations, treating them as part of the natural problem formulation for the chosen tasks.

**Mitigation status.** Unmitigated. The paper does not address stochasticity, partial observability, or continuous action spaces, and proposes no extensions to the MCTS formulation to handle them. A practitioner with a stochastic or partially observable task would need to determine whether the MDP approximation is acceptable, and the paper provides no guidance on when the approximation breaks down. The suggestion that MCTS “can be substituted with alternative… RL models” (Section 6) would require those alternative models to handle the additional complexity, which is not demonstrated. The limitation is fundamental to the MDP formulation that underpins XOT and is not acknowledged as such.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper introduces a **new architectural template** for combining LLMs with external search modules, and in doing so, changes the conversation around LLM reasoning from "how do we make LLMs generate better reasoning chains?" to "what is the optimal decomposition of reasoning labor between neural language models and symbolic search?" This is not an incremental improvement to prompting techniques — it is a **category shift in how reasoning systems are composed**.

The magnitude of this shift can be understood along three dimensions:

**1. From prompt engineering to system architecture.** Prior work on LLM reasoning — CoT, ToT, GoT, and their variants — operates within the paradigm that the LLM is the sole reasoning engine, with prompting techniques serving to structure its output. Improvements came from better prompts (more detailed instructions, more examples, better formatting) or more elaborate search orchestration (tree search, graph aggregation) — but always with the LLM performing the core operations of generation and evaluation. XOT breaks this assumption by introducing a **second reasoning component** — the MCTS module with learned policy/value networks — that handles the computationally intensive exploration, while the LLM is repositioned as a verifier and synthesizer. This is not a better prompt; it is a different system architecture. The implication for the field is that future work on LLM reasoning need not be confined to making LLMs better at planning — the alternative path is to **build hybrid systems where the LLM does what it does well (comprehension, verification, synthesis) and dedicated planning modules do what they do well (systematic state-space exploration)**. This opens a design space that was not previously visible.

**2. Reconceptualizing LLM reasoning failures.** The incomplete-thought ablation (Table 11) provides the most theoretically significant finding: on 8-Puzzle, GPT-4 achieves 40.34% accuracy when given a partial MCTS-generated thought trajectory (missing the last step), versus 7.56% when it must generate the entire reasoning chain from scratch (CoT). This ~5.3× improvement from having partial external structure — even though the LLM must still generate the final step — suggests that the LLM's failure is not primarily a failure of *understanding* (it can complete partial trajectories) but a failure of *initiating and sustaining* long-range planning. This finding reframes the narrative from "LLMs cannot plan" (the conclusion drawn by Huang et al., Stechly et al., Valmeekam et al.) to "LLMs possess latent planning comprehension that their autoregressive generation process cannot access without external scaffolding."

This reframing has direct consequences for what research directions become more attractive:
- **More attractive**: Developing methods to provide LLMs with structured reasoning scaffolds — whether from MCTS, symbolic planners, retrieval of similar solved problems, or human-provided sketches — and focusing on improving LLM verification and completion capabilities.
- **More attractive**: Investigating *why* autoregressive generation fails at planning when comprehension succeeds — is it error compounding over long sequences? Lack of lookahead? Insufficient training on state-space reasoning?
- **Less attractive**: Trying to make LLMs perform autonomous long-horizon planning purely through better prompting or larger models, if the underlying limitation is architectural rather than scale-dependent. The LLaMA-2-13B fine-tuning baseline (0% on 8-Puzzle and Pocket Cube) suggests that simply adding more training data in a supervised paradigm does not impart planning capability — the model needs inference-time search structure.

**3. A diagnostic tool for LLM capabilities.** The paper's methodology — comparing performance with complete MCTS thoughts, incomplete MCTS thoughts, and no external thoughts — provides a **decomposition of LLM reasoning capability** into (a) autonomous generation ability (CoT accuracy), (b) completion ability (incomplete-thought accuracy), and (c) verification ability (revision success rate). This decomposition could become a standard diagnostic protocol for evaluating LLM reasoning: rather than reporting a single accuracy number, characterize *where* the model fails in the reasoning pipeline. A model with low CoT accuracy but high incomplete-thought accuracy has a generation bottleneck; a model with high CoT accuracy but low revision success rate has a verification bottleneck. The paper does not frame this as a contribution, but the experimental design enables it, and future work could formalize this as a general evaluation framework.

**4. Resolving contradictions in the self-correction literature.** The paper's finding that LLMs *can* detect errors in externally generated reasoning (Revision Success Rates of 47–91% across tasks and models; Tables 9-10) but *cannot* correct those errors through autonomous regeneration (CoT accuracies of 0–7% on spatial tasks) provides a **unified explanation for conflicting findings**. Huang et al. (2023) found that "large language models cannot self-correct reasoning yet" — which is true when "self-correct" means both detect the error and generate the correction. XOT shows that detection and correction are separable: detection works substantially better than generation-based correction. When Madaan et al. (2023) found self-refinement helps, they may have been operating in a difficulty regime where the LLM's generation capability was sufficient to produce corrections once errors were identified. XOT resolves the contradiction by showing it's not binary — it depends on whether the task requires planning (where generation-based correction fails) or only local refinement (where it may succeed). The field now has a framework for predicting when self-correction will work: it works when corrections are short-range and the LLM can generate them from its comprehension; it fails when corrections require systematic search through a combinatorial space.

**5. The "Penrose triangle" as a design principle.** The paper's framing of the trilemma — performance, efficiency, flexibility — as a structural constraint that emerges from the single-engine assumption (LLM as sole reasoner) is more than a rhetorical device. It functions as a **design principle**: any architecture that aspires to all three attributes must decompose thought generation across components with different computational profiles. The specific choice of MCTS with policy/value networks is one instantiation of this principle; the principle itself generalizes. Alternative instantiations could use:
- A symbolic planner (STRIPS, PDDL-based) for search + LLM for plan verification and natural language grounding.
- A retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) system where retrieved similar solved problems serve as thought scaffolds.
- A code executor that searches over program traces, with the LLM synthesizing and verifying code candidates.
- A differentiable planner trained via imitation learning on expert trajectories, with the LLM providing reward shaping or constraint checking.

Each of these would instantiate the same principle: offload exploration to a component with a fundamentally different cost structure, reserve LLM calls for what only the LLM can do. The paper doesn't enumerate these alternatives, but the architectural logic makes them legible as natural extensions.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Scaling the policy/value network to more complex state spaces — how far can the lightweight planning module be pushed before it becomes the bottleneck?** The paper's policy/value network is a two-layer MLP with ~10⁶ parameters, trained on tasks with state spaces of size 10⁴–10⁶ (181,440 for 8-Puzzle, ~3.67 million for Pocket Cube). A natural stress test is to scale up the state space while keeping the network architecture fixed: for example, the 15-Puzzle (16!/2 ≈ 10¹³ states) or the 3×3×3 Rubik's Cube (~4.3 × 10¹⁹ states). At what state space size does the two-layer MLP fail to learn useful value functions? Does adding capacity (more layers, attention mechanisms, graph neural networks for structured state representations) extend the range, or does the MDP formulation itself break down because value function approximation becomes too difficult? A strong follow-up would train policy/value networks of varying capacities on the 15-Puzzle, measure the scaling of MCTS simulation efficiency (how many simulations are needed to reach a given solution quality as a function of network capacity), and identify the point where the network becomes the bottleneck rather than the MCTS search budget. This would provide a **scaling law for the planning module**, analogous to the scaling laws for LLM pretraining, that would guide architecture choices for new tasks.

**Training the policy/value network without a perfect simulator — can learned world models substitute for ground-truth state transitions and rewards?** The paper's MCTS module requires a perfect environment simulator during training. This is the most restrictive assumption for real-world deployment. A critical follow-up experiment would test whether the policy/value network can be trained using a **learned world model** — an approximate simulator trained from observational data — with the LLM providing reward signals for terminal states. Specifically: for a task like "schedule a meeting across multiple people's calendars," the state space (partial schedule, remaining constraints) is well-defined but the transition dynamics (what happens when you propose a time slot?) depend on human behavior that is not perfectly simulated. A researcher could train a Transformer-based world model on historical scheduling data to predict the outcome of proposed time slots, use this as the MCTS simulator, train the policy/value network on the learned simulator, and then compare XOT's performance against ToT and CoT baselines on held-out scheduling problems. The key measurement would be XOT's robustness to world model errors — does the LLM revision loop compensate for simulator inaccuracies (as the paper suggests it does for MCTS errors in the puzzle tasks), or do compounding errors in the learned simulator make the search trajectories effectively useless? The Document Merging experiment (Appendix C, Table 13) is a preliminary step in this direction (using LLM-based rewards), but it doesn't isolate the effect of an imperfect simulator because it doesn't report the simulator's fidelity or ablate against a ground-truth simulator.

**Generalization to open-ended NLP reasoning tasks — can XOT work when the state space is represented in natural language rather than as structured data?** The three puzzle tasks use compact, structured state representations (sets of numbers, grids, face color matrices). Many important reasoning tasks — multi-hop question answering, legal reasoning, scientific literature review, debate preparation — have states that are most naturally represented as natural language text (the set of facts gathered so far, the partial argument structure, the evidence reviewed). This raises a fundamental question: can the policy/value network learn to evaluate the promise of a *textual* state? The current MLP architecture operates on structured inputs; for textual states, a small Transformer or a distilled embedding-based network would be more appropriate. A strong follow-up would: (1) choose a multi-hop QA dataset (e.g., HotpotQA, StrategyQA) and define states as "the set of documents retrieved and facts extracted so far" and actions as "retrieve this document" or "extract this relation"; (2) train a lightweight policy/value network (e.g., a distilled BERT-base with ~110M parameters, still ~3 orders of magnitude smaller than GPT-3.5) on MCTS self-play data where the simulator is the ground-truth knowledge graph; (3) evaluate XOT against CoT and ToT baselines. The key question is whether a model three orders of magnitude smaller than the LLM can learn useful value functions over textual states, or whether the semantic complexity of text requires LLM-scale models for evaluation, collapsing the efficiency advantage. This experiment would determine whether XOT's template generalizes beyond structured-state tasks or is fundamentally limited to domains where states have compact representations.

**Ablating the revision loop: how does the number of LLM calls in revision trade off against LLM calls in generation-based search?** The paper's revision loop requires 1–3 LLM calls for error detection and final synthesis. ToT and GoT require 20–57 LLM calls for search-guided generation and evaluation. A fairer comparison would equalize the LLM call budget: give ToT the same number of LLM calls as XOT (say, 2–3 calls) and measure how much performance degrades, or give XOT more calls (say, allowing more revision rounds or having the LLM verify each MCTS simulation step) and measure how much performance improves. Specifically, a researcher could: (1) run ToT with a budget of exactly `K` LLM calls (by limiting tree depth and branching factor to keep total calls ≤ `K`) for `K` ∈ {1, 2, 3, 5, 10, 20, 40}; (2) run XOT with the same `K`-call budget, allocating calls flexibly between revision rounds and final synthesis; (3) plot accuracy vs. `K` for both methods on all three puzzle tasks and on the 15-Puzzle extension. This experiment would reveal the **Pareto frontier** of LLM call efficiency — at what call budgets does XOT dominate ToT, and at what budgets are they comparable? The paper's current comparison (XOT with 1.6 calls vs. ToT with 54 calls) stacks the deck; a budget-matched comparison would provide a more rigorous efficiency assessment and would reveal whether XOT's advantage comes primarily from the external search module or from the revision mechanism's targeted use of LLM calls.

**Testing whether LLMs can *learn* from the MCTS-generated thought trajectories — does exposure to structured reasoning scaffolds improve the LLM's autonomous reasoning on similar problems?** The paper treats MCTS-generated thoughts as *inference-time* scaffolds: they are provided per-problem and discarded. A natural extension is to use the thought trajectories as **training data** for fine-tuning the LLM itself. If exposure to structured planning trajectories during training improves the LLM's autonomous planning capability, this would close the loop: MCTS generates training data → LLM fine-tunes on it → LLM requires less external scaffolding at inference time → MCTS can focus on harder problems. A researcher could: (1) collect MCTS-generated thought trajectories for the 1,225 Game of 24 training problems; (2) fine-tune GPT-3.5 or an open-source model (LLaMA-2-13B) on these trajectories using the same format as the prompts in Appendix D; (3) evaluate the fine-tuned model on the 137 test problems using CoT prompting (no MCTS scaffolding at inference). The key comparison would be: does the fine-tuned model exceed the 2.19% accuracy of the baseline LLaMA-2-13B (trained on ground-truth input-output pairs, Table 3)? If yes, it suggests that structured thought trajectories are a more effective training signal than final answers alone. If the improvement is substantial, it would challenge the paper's conclusion that "finetuning approaches are not suitable for planning tasks" (Section 4.1.3) by showing that the failure was due to the *format* of training data (final answers only) rather than an inherent limitation of fine-tuning. This experiment would connect XOT to the literature on knowledge distillation and self-improvement (STaR, ReST^EM) and would provide a path toward reducing inference-time dependence on the MCTS module.

**Stress-testing the revision loop's false positive rate and sensitivity to error subtlety.** The Revision Success Rate metric (Tables 9-10) measures how often the LLM detects an error in a trajectory known to be incorrect. It does not measure how often the LLM incorrectly flags a correct step as erroneous (false positive rate), nor how performance varies with the subtlety of the error (obvious illegal moves vs. legal but suboptimal moves that lead to dead ends). A researcher could systematically corrupt correct MCTS trajectories — by injecting errors of varying types (illegal moves, valid moves that lead to unsolvable states, valid but unnecessarily long sequences) and varying positions (early step, middle step, final step) — and measure the LLM's detection rate and false positive rate as a function of error type and position. This would characterize the conditions under which the revision loop is reliable versus harmful, and would inform the design of LLM prompts for error detection (e.g., should the LLM be instructed to be conservative and only flag violations, or should it also flag inefficiencies?). This experiment would also reveal whether the diminishing returns observed in Figure 4 (accuracy gains decreasing with revision count) are due to the LLM running out of detectable errors or due to the remaining errors being too subtle for the LLM to identify.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Automated puzzle and game solving as a testbed for hybrid reasoning architectures.** While Game of 24, 8-Puzzle, and Pocket Cube are not themselves commercially important, they serve as canonical planning benchmarks with well-defined success criteria, making them ideal **development and evaluation environments** for hybrid LLM-planning systems. An organization developing an LLM-based agent for a complex real-world planning task (supply chain optimization, factory scheduling, robot task planning) could use these puzzles as a rapid prototyping sandbox: implement the MDP formulation, train the policy/value network, integrate with the LLM, and validate the revision loop — all in a controlled environment where performance is easily measurable. The speed of the feedback loop (a Pocket Cube problem takes seconds to solve with XOT versus hours of real-world execution) makes iterative development feasible. The paper's results provide baselines and target performance levels: on 8-Puzzle, a system achieving less than 90% accuracy with ~2 LLM calls is underperforming relative to XOT's 95.80%; if a new architecture can't match this, it suggests fundamental issues that would persist in more complex domains. The open-source release of the code and dataset (linked in the abstract) enables this directly.

**Edge deployment of planning-capable AI assistants through model offloading.** The paper's architecture separates the expensive semantic reasoning component (the LLM, running in the cloud) from the computationally efficient planning component (the policy/value network, running on-device). This maps directly onto a deployment model for mobile or embedded AI assistants performing planning tasks: the on-device component runs the MCTS guided by a lightweight policy/value network to explore solution trajectories, and the cloud LLM is called only for final verification and answer synthesis — reducing the number of expensive API calls from ~40–57 (if using ToT-style LLM-based search) to ~1.4–2.3 (XOT's revision-based approach). For a navigation or task-planning assistant that handles hundreds of queries per day, reducing LLM calls by a factor of ~20–30× translates directly to cost savings and reduced latency sensitivity to network conditions. The paper's f_θ invocation counts (~56–92 network forward passes per problem) are well within the computational budget of modern mobile devices for a small MLP, making on-device planning feasible. The key requirement is that the task's state space be compact enough for the policy/value network to learn — navigation in a discretized map, simple scheduling, or puzzle-solving are all candidates.

**Multi-solution generation for creative and design applications.** The multi-solution capability demonstrated in Tables 4, 6, and 8 — where XOT extracts multiple distinct valid solution trajectories from a single MCTS search — directly addresses a need in creative and design domains where users benefit from seeing diverse options rather than a single answer. For example, in **architectural layout generation**: given constraints on room sizes, adjacencies, and building footprint, generate multiple valid floor plans. The MDP formulation would define states as partial layouts and actions as placing or modifying a room, with the reward signal coming from constraint satisfaction and aesthetic metrics. XOT's graph-like thought structures (Figure 5), which naturally produce branching and merging solution paths, would provide designers with structurally different options (different room arrangements) that all satisfy the constraints, rather than minor variations on a single layout. The efficiency advantage matters here because users typically want to see 3–5 alternatives quickly; waiting for 50+ LLM calls (as GoT would require) is impractical in an interactive setting. XOT's ~4 LLM calls for 3 solutions makes real-time multi-solution generation feasible.

**Automated training data generation for self-improving planning systems.** The paper's finding that MCTS-generated thought trajectories provide a more effective reasoning scaffold than the LLM's own generation (CoT: 7.56% → incomplete MCTS thoughts: 40.34% on 8-Puzzle GPT-4; Table 11) suggests a pipeline for generating high-quality training data for self-improving systems: (1) run XOT on a large set of planning problems, generating correct thought trajectories (at 85–96% accuracy per the paper's results); (2) filter to trajectories that pass LLM verification (leveraging the revision loop's quality control); (3) fine-tune a smaller, cheaper model on these trajectories to internalize the planning patterns. The resulting model would approximate the performance of the full XOT system while requiring no MCTS at inference time — the planning knowledge is distilled into the model weights. This is the standard distillation paradigm applied to structured reasoning: use the expensive hybrid system (MCTS + LLM) as a teacher for a cheaper student. The paper's failed LLaMA-2-13B baseline (0% on spatial tasks when trained on input-output pairs alone) suggests that the trajectories (intermediate state-action pairs) are a richer training signal than final answers, and XOT provides a scalable way to generate these trajectories with ground-truth correctness determined by the simulator. Organizations with access to task simulators could generate millions of training trajectories and build specialized planning models for their domains.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper positions XOT against the spectrum of thought prompting paradigms (IO, CoT, CoT-SC, ToT, GoT) and identifies the three-attribute tradeoff (performance, efficiency, flexibility) as the axis of comparison. The following decision rule emerges from the experimental evidence:

- **Prefer XOT with the MCTS + LLM revision architecture when the task meets all of:**
  - A well-defined MDP formulation is possible (states, actions, reward function, and terminal conditions can be specified precisely).
  - A simulator exists or can be built that provides ground-truth state transitions and reward signals for training the policy/value network. The simulator need not be perfect at deployment time (the LLM revision loop can compensate for some errors), but must be sufficiently accurate during training for the policy/value network to learn useful value functions.
  - The state space is too large for exhaustive search but small enough for MCTS with a learned value function — the paper demonstrates feasibility for state spaces up to ~3.7M (Pocket Cube) but provides no upper bound; practitioners should expect diminishing returns as state space size increases without corresponding network capacity increases.
  - The LLM can *verify* solution correctness with reasonable reliability — ideally with a false positive rate low enough that the revision loop corrects more errors than it introduces. The paper's Revision Success Rates (47–91% across tasks and models) provide a calibrated expectation: tasks where the LLM cannot assess correctness at all (e.g., highly technical domains beyond its training, tasks requiring physical intuition) will see the revision loop provide no benefit, reducing XOT's performance to MCTS-alone levels (46–63% in the paper's tasks).
  - Multiple LLM calls per problem are prohibitively expensive (cost, latency, or rate-limit constraints), making ToT's ~40–57 calls infeasible for the deployment scale. XOT's ~1.4–2.3 LLM calls make it viable at scale where ToT is not.
  - Multiple diverse solutions are valuable (design exploration, creative applications, user choice). XOT's single-search multi-trajectory extraction is both cheaper and more diverse than running ToT multiple times.

- **Prefer ToT or GoT when:**
  - The task lacks a well-defined state representation or simulator, making the MDP formulation infeasible, and the LLM must serve as both generator and evaluator. ToT/GoT's LLM-based evaluation, while expensive, works without external simulators.
  - The number of problems to solve is very small (tens, not hundreds or thousands), making the upfront training cost of the policy/value network impossible to amortize. In this regime, ToT's per-problem cost of 40–57 LLM calls may be cheaper than the total cost of simulator development + network training + inference for a single batch of problems.
  - The task is simple enough that single-pass generation (CoT) achieves adequate accuracy, or the LLM's autonomous planning capability is sufficient — XOT's overhead (policy/value network training, MCTS simulations) would be unnecessary.

- **Prefer CoT or IO when:**
  - The task requires exactly 1 LLM call for latency or cost reasons, and accuracy is secondary or the task is simple enough that CoT accuracy is sufficient. The paper shows CoT achieves 4.38% on Game of 24 and 7.56% on 8-Puzzle with GPT-4 — adequate only if the cost of errors is negligible or human review catches failures.
  - No simulator exists and the LLM cannot be used as an evaluator (e.g., due to bias or hallucinations in evaluation), ruling out both XOT and ToT/GoT. In this case, the only option is single-pass generation with the hope that the base model's reasoning is sufficient.
