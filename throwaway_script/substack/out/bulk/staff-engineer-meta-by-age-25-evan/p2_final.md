Here are the takeaways from this Evan King interview (went from Junior to Staff Engineer at Meta in 3 years, later founded his own company):

**Cornell to Junior Engineer at Meta**

Evan began programming at the end of high school and didn't feel naturally gifted—"it didn't come naturally to him at all." He invested heavily in Leetcoding, building projects, and doing Capture the Flag (CTF) challenges, which helped him land a role at Meta when they recruited him at Cornell. During his third year, his CTF interest led him to found the Cornell Hacking Club. Leading that club forced him to recruit people, organize events, and rally a group around a shared mission—soft skills that later proved essential for Senior+ promotions at Meta. On side projects for a resume, he warns against building random projects: solving "real problems you care about" helps you learn more and stand out from the crowd, because the depth of engagement shows. Getting involved in leading a campus club isn't just resume padding—it develops genuine leadership behaviors.

**Junior to Mid-Level in a Single Half**

With his CTF background, Evan initially wanted to join Meta's cybersecurity team. During onboarding he found the team culture didn't interest him, so he pivoted to Content Integrity—a backend systems team that filtered violating content from Meta's platforms. Promotion wasn't on his mind; he just worked, and got promoted within his first half. That early signal reassured him he was doing well. A core insight from that period: **the opportunities you seek out are far more impactful than the opportunities that seek you**. Desirable roles have many people competing for them; having the agency to chase them makes a big difference across a career. He also stresses looking for projects outside your defined scope—things connected to your work but beyond your formal responsibilities help you stand out. His intern manager at Zillow called him the "best intern" he'd ever had specifically because of that instinct to build beyond his lane. On shipping fast: a clean workflow, extra hours particularly as a junior, knowing who and what to ask, and strong code-search skills are the levers that compound.

**Mid-Level to Senior in Two Halves**

At mid-level—only six months into industry—Evan's manager trusted him to lead a small portion of the team with a few engineers. Ryan notes that's striking: he'd barely started his career and already had enough trust to lead people, several of whom were more senior than him. Evan admits he initially got territorial about his team's scope versus a more senior engineer he was working alongside. He had to learn to be more humble and collaborate rather than guard turf—once he did, he learned faster and the team produced better results. His manager was placing him in a leadership role at the edge of his capabilities. The pattern here is deliberate: developing tech-lead skills early created leverage that scaled him later, which was necessary for the Staff promotion. Learning to collaborate with someone he originally saw as taking his scope turned into an accelerant for both his growth and team outcomes.

**Senior to Staff in Three Halves**

The catalyst was the Christchurch mosque shootings, when videos of the event slipped through Meta's content filtering systems. Evan's manager was on leave, so he stepped up and took the lead in addressing the situation. In the aftermath, he and a PM were tasked with creating and leading a new team to handle real-time integrity—building it from scratch. The impact and visibility of that workstream made an obvious case for Staff. What got him promoted wasn't a technically complex project; it was a simple project that had extraordinary results. **Impact is far more important than complexity** for the IC6 promo—Ryan notes this matched his own promotion case exactly. Because levels at Meta aren't public, Evan couldn't rely on seniority to gain authority; he had to influence without it. His strong reputation earned him the trust to lead and build the new team while still IC5, and the soft skills to influence others were critical for making Staff.

**Leaving Meta to Start a Company**

Evan had never planned to enter startups. While still a Staff Engineer at Meta, he started working with his manager on an after-hours project driven by a shared interest in crypto. The project got 10,000+ users relatively quickly, and the pull of building something from scratch became more compelling than his day job. By then he had more than enough financial cushion to sacrifice a few years of stable salary. His advice is pragmatic: do what makes sense financially, which varies for everyone. On technical growth, he feels strongly that **startups promote faster technical growth than big tech**. The distinction Ryan and Evan aligned on: big tech gets you depth, startups get you breadth. Short-term, he's earned less despite two acquisitions compared to what he would have made staying in big tech, but he believes the faster learning from startups compounded over a career will lead to higher lifetime earnings.

**Career Reflections and Regrets**

Evan's biggest regret is making his whole life work early in his career. Even though he had time to invest in outside friendships and relationships, most of his free time and self-reflection revolved around being a software engineer. He acknowledges he took shortcuts to reach Staff quickly, partly influenced by Meta's "move fast and break things" culture—sacrificing deeper technical understanding for faster promotions, which caught up to him later when building his own company and needing that depth. On the other hand, his work-life balance was actually pretty good: he mostly worked 9-to-5, though when genuinely excited about something he'd often do more after getting home. The arc shows a deliberate sprint early on, followed by a reckoning: speed buys promotions and impact, but it can defer the deeper learning that matters when you're the one making all the technical decisions.

---

Evan’s trajectory embodies his **“the opportunities you seek out >> the opportunities that are seeking you”** axiom at every stage—from switching teams during onboarding, to stepping up during the Christchurch crisis when his manager was absent, to leaving big tech for a side project that hit 10k users. His early habit of finding projects outside his defined scope (what his Zillow manager called “best intern” behavior) directly fed the **Impact >> Complexity** logic that made his Staff case: a simple, high-visibility project born from taking initiative, not technical wizardry. Yet the speed came with a tradeoff he now names plainly—**sacrificing technical depth for faster promotions**—and his conviction that startups give you breadth faster than big tech gives you depth is both a post-hoc justification and a blueprint for how he thinks about lifetime compounding.
