# ChatGLM-Math: Improving Math Problem-Solving in Large Language Models with a Self-Critique Pipeline

**ArXiv:** [2404.02893](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.02893)

## 🎯 Pitch

This paper introduces the **Self-Critique pipeline**, a weakly supervised iterative training framework that enhances both mathematical problem-solving and general language capabilities in LLMs without external supervisory models.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces the **Self-Critique pipeline**, a weakly supervised iterative training framework that enhances both mathematical problem-solving and general language capabilities in LLMs without external supervisory models. Using ChatGLM3-32B as the base model and evaluating on the authors' newly created MATHUSEREVAL benchmark alongside standard academic datasets like GSM8k and MATH, the pipeline first trains a **Math-Critique** model (a learned evaluator that scores solutions with explanatory analysis on a 1–10 scale), then sequentially applies **Rejective Fine-Tuning (RFT)** (rejection sampling where only responses scoring above a correctness threshold via Math-Critique are retained for supervised fine-tuning) and **Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)** (learning from chosen-rejected answer pairs ranked by Math-Critique, with a cross-entropy regularization term on positive examples). The full pipeline achieves a 17.5% relative improvement on the average of GSM8k and MATH while simultaneously improving AlignBench language scores by 5.85%, establishing that domain-specific self-critique mechanisms can resolve the "alignment tax" that typically degrades mathematical reasoning during RLHF-style preference training.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: The Alignment Tax on Mathematical Reasoning

The central tension this paper addresses is deceptively simple: **if you fine-tune an LLM to be better at general conversation and instruction following, its mathematical reasoning capability degrades.** Conversely, if you specialize an LLM for mathematical problem-solving, its general language capabilities deteriorate. This creates a practical dilemma for deployed LLM systems, where an ideal model must excel at both — answering a user's math homework question accurately one moment and writing a coherent, helpful response to a creative writing prompt the next.

The literature has given this phenomenon a specific name: the **alignment tax** (Askell et al., 2021). When using RLHF (Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback) to align an LLM with human preferences — making it more helpful, harmless, and honest — the model's performance on specialized reasoning tasks (particularly math) typically drops. This is because standard RLHF reward models are trained to evaluate qualities like fluency, helpfulness, and safety in generated text, but they are generally insensitive to logical correctness, rigorous step-by-step reasoning, and exact numerical accuracy — the hallmarks of mathematical problem-solving. A model optimized to produce text that "sounds good" to human evaluators may systematically drift away from producing text that is *mathematically correct*.

Table 1 illustrates this tension with concrete examples from prior work. DeepSeek-67B-Chat shows a 1.2% drop in average GSM8k+MATH performance after DPO alignment, even as its AlignBench language score improves by 6.8%. Going in the other direction, Math-InternLM2-20B (a math-specialized model) improves over its general counterpart on math (60.2 vs. 57.2 on GSM8k+MATH average) but suffers a **14.8% drop** in AlignBench language performance. The paper's own baseline, ChatGLM3-32B-SFT-2312, embodies the starting point of this tradeoff: reasonable language performance (7.37 on AlignBench) but mediocre math (52.4 average). The question the paper poses is whether we can move to the *upper-right corner* of this tradeoff space — improving both simultaneously.

### Why This Problem Matters

The alignment tax on mathematics is not merely an academic curiosity; it has direct implications for how LLMs are deployed and developed:

**Deployed systems serve diverse user needs.** In real-world chat applications, users ask both conversational and analytical questions. A system that can discuss poetry but cannot correctly solve a linear equation — or vice versa — provides a degraded user experience. The paper's parent organization, Zhipu.AI, operates the ChatGLM online serving system (https://chatglm.cn), which faces exactly this challenge. The authors note that related techniques have already been deployed to ChatGLM, underscoring that this is a *production* problem, not a theoretical one.

**The "two-model" solution is inefficient.** A naive solution is to maintain separate models — a general-purpose chat model and a math-specialized model — with a router that directs queries to the appropriate one. This doubles deployment costs (memory, compute, maintenance), introduces routing errors (misclassifying a query as conversational when it requires math reasoning, or vice versa), and creates a fragmented user experience. A single model that excels at both is architecturally simpler and more cost-effective.

**Self-improvement pipelines are bottlenecked by evaluation quality.** A broader research direction involves LLMs improving their own capabilities through iterative feedback loops (generating solutions, evaluating them, and learning from the best ones). These pipelines stand or fall on the quality of the *evaluation model* that scores the outputs. If the evaluator is unreliable — particularly for structured reasoning tasks like math — the entire self-improvement process degrades, as the model learns from noisy or incorrect feedback signals. Developing a robust, specialized evaluation model for mathematics (the Math-Critique) is therefore not just about improving a single model; it enables the *scaffolding* for future self-improving systems.

**The tension reveals a gap in alignment methodology.** The alignment tax suggests that current RLHF reward models encode a specific, narrow notion of "good" output — one centered on conversational quality — that fails to generalize to correctness-critical domains. Addressing this requires either (a) building reward models that encompass both conversational *and* correctness criteria, or (b) developing alignment procedures that can incorporate domain-specific feedback signals. This paper takes a pragmatic version of approach (b): rather than trying to build a single universal reward model, it trains a specialized Math-Critique model and integrates its feedback into the alignment process.

### Prior Approaches and Where They Fall Short

The paper positions itself against several established lines of work, each of which addresses part of the problem but leaves the central tension unresolved:

**Prompting methods (Chain-of-Thought, Tree-of-Thoughts, etc.)** enhance reasoning at inference time by guiding the model through structured problem-solving procedures (Wei et al., 2023; Yao et al., 2023; Besta et al., 2023). Chain-of-Thought prompting, for example, elicits intermediate reasoning steps that improve final answer accuracy. However, these methods are fundamentally limited: they do not modify the model's parameters. If the base model lacks the necessary mathematical knowledge or reasoning capability, no amount of clever prompting will compensate. The model's *inherent* capabilities remain unchanged, which is why prompting alone cannot resolve the alignment tax — a model that has already been degraded by RLHF will produce incorrect reasoning steps regardless of how they are elicited.

**Supervised fine-tuning on math data (MetaMath, WizardMath, Mammoth, Abel, etc.)** directly improves mathematical capabilities by training on curated mathematical problem-solution pairs (Yu et al., 2023; Luo et al., 2023; Yue et al., 2023; Chern et al., 2023). While effective at boosting math benchmarks, these approaches typically sacrifice general language capabilities — the models become "math specialists" that perform poorly on open-ended conversation, creative writing, and general question-answering. This is exactly the tradeoff seen in Math-InternLM2-20B (Table 1). The fundamental issue is that standard SFT on domain-specific data *narrows* the model's output distribution toward that domain, overwriting or suppressing the broader capabilities acquired during pretraining.

**RLHF with PPO for math** has been attempted, most notably using process reward models (PRMs) from OpenAI (Lightman et al., 2023) to provide step-level feedback, combined with PPO reinforcement learning (Luo et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2023). The challenges here are practical: PPO training is notoriously unstable and computationally expensive, requiring simultaneous optimization of a policy model, a reward model, and a value function. Moreover, the reward model itself must be trained on high-quality human or AI feedback, which introduces cost and scaling bottlenecks. When the reward signal is imperfect or miscalibrated (as it often is for nuanced mathematical reasoning), PPO can produce unexpected degradation rather than improvement.

**Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)** (Rafailov et al., 2023) simplifies the RLHF pipeline by eliminating the explicit reward model — it directly optimizes the policy from preference pairs using a closed-form relationship between the policy and an implicit reward function. DPO is simpler, more stable, and faster than PPO. However, the paper notes that applying DPO to mathematical tasks has proven "challenging" (Section 2, paragraph on supervised fine-tuning and RL). Standard DPO requires preference pairs where the "chosen" response is clearly better than the "rejected" one. For mathematical reasoning, constructing such pairs reliably is difficult because: (a) determining which of two partially-correct solutions is "better" requires sophisticated evaluation, (b) naive DPO training can be unstable and may not converge to optimal policies for correctness-based preferences, and (c) the loss function provides no explicit signal about what makes a solution correct — it only learns relative preferences, which may not align with absolute correctness.

**External tools and decoding strategies** offer partial solutions. Code integration (Mammoth, ToRA) bypasses the model's arithmetic limitations by generating and executing code, and self-consistency (Wang et al., 2023) improves accuracy by sampling multiple solutions and taking the majority answer. However, these approaches are inference-time techniques — they do not modify the model's underlying mathematical knowledge or reasoning procedures. They also impose runtime overhead (multiple generations, code execution) that may be impractical for latency-sensitive deployments.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The Self-Critique pipeline is positioned as a solution that bridges the gap between these established approaches while addressing their individual shortcomings:

**Against prompting alone:** The pipeline *trains* the model using self-generated feedback, permanently upgrading its mathematical capabilities rather than relying on inference-time tricks. The improvements persist across all decoding strategies.

**Against math-only SFT:** The pipeline is designed to improve math *without* degrading language. The two-stage process (RFT → DPO) involves filtering and preference learning, not wholesale domain shift. The RFT stage incorporates the original general SFT dataset alongside math data, maintaining exposure to diverse tasks. The DPO stage uses carefully selected contrastive pairs on the most challenging problems, providing targeted refinement. The results (Table 3) show simultaneous improvements in both math (GSM8k, MATH) and language (AlignBench), demonstrating that the tradeoff is not inevitable — it can be engineered around.

**Against PPO-based RLHF:** The pipeline avoids the complexity and instability of PPO by using RFT (simple supervised fine-tuning on filtered data) and DPO (direct optimization from preferences without a running reward model). This makes it substantially more practical to implement and reproduce. The Math-Critique model serves as an offline evaluator that generates training data, rather than an online reward signal during RL training.

**Against standard DPO:** The paper introduces specific adaptations to make DPO work for mathematical reasoning: (a) using the Math-Critique model's *absolute* scoring (rather than relative human preferences) to construct high-quality preference pairs, (b) adding a cross-entropy regularization term on the chosen (correct) response to stabilize training and provide a direct signal about what constitutes a correct solution — this is a critical modification because standard DPO only optimizes relative preferences and can lose track of absolute quality, and (c) focusing DPO on the subset of questions that the model answered *incorrectly* after RFT, targeting the most impactful learning opportunities.

**The conceptual innovation** is the idea of a **self-contained, model-native evaluation loop**. Rather than depending on external supervisory models (like GPT-4 for evaluation, as many prior works do), human annotators (expensive and slow), or ground-truth answer checking (only available for closed-form problems), the pipeline bootstraps its own Math-Critique model from the base LLM using a small amount of human annotation (~5k examples) and then uses that critic to automatically supervise all subsequent training. This makes the pipeline **self-improving** in a meaningful sense: after the initial Math-Critique training, all remaining steps (RFT data filtering, DPO pair construction) run without human intervention, and the entire cycle can theoretically iterate with updated model checkpoints serving as new bases for both the critic and the math model. The authors emphasize the practical importance of this: manual annotation cycles take weeks, during which the base model may have already been updated through continued pretraining, making previously annotated data stale or distributionally mismatched. The automated pipeline can keep pace with model development.

**A note on what this paper is NOT:** It is not a process reward model paper — Math-Critique evaluates entire solutions with a single score (plus analysis), not step-by-step correctness, making it closer to an outcome-supervised reward model with Chain-of-Thought-style justification. It is also not a pure self-play or RL paper — the training relies on supervised fine-tuning and DPO, not policy gradient methods. The contribution is the *integration* of these techniques into a practical, iterative pipeline that addresses the specific tension between mathematical reasoning and general language capability.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

The authors build a **self-contained training pipeline** that takes an existing general-purpose chat LLM and enhances its mathematical problem-solving ability through iterative feedback from a specialized evaluator model — all while preserving and even improving its general language capabilities. The pipeline solves the alignment tax problem (where making models better at conversation degrades math, and vice versa) by using the LLM's own capabilities to bootstrap a mathematical critique system, then using that system's feedback to guide two sequential stages of training: first filtering the model's own generations to keep only high-quality math solutions, and then learning from explicit comparisons between correct and incorrect answers on the hardest remaining problems.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The Self-Critique pipeline has four major components that operate in sequence:

1. **Base Chat Model (ChatGLM3-32B-SFT-2312)** — the starting general-purpose LLM that has already undergone standard supervised fine-tuning for conversational abilities. It serves as both the initial math problem-solver and the foundation from which the Math-Critique evaluator is derived.

2. **Math-Critique Model** — a specialized evaluator trained from the base LLM to assess mathematical solution quality. It takes a question, a reference answer, and a candidate solution as input, and outputs both an explanatory analysis and a numerical score from 1 to 10. This model is trained once using a small amount of human-annotated data (~5k examples) plus pseudo-labeled data from other evaluator models (10k examples). After this initial training, it serves as the **sole source of feedback** for all subsequent pipeline stages — no more human annotation is needed.

3. **Critique-RFT Model** — the result of the first training stage: **Rejective Fine-Tuning (RFT)**. The base Chat Model generates multiple candidate answers per training question. Math-Critique scores each answer. Answers scoring above a correctness threshold are retained; the rest are discarded. The model is then fine-tuned by standard supervised learning on this filtered dataset (merged with the original general SFT data). The output is a model that produces more consistently correct math solutions.

4. **Critique-DPO Model** — the result of the second training stage: **Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)**. Starting from the Critique-RFT model, the pipeline identifies questions that were still answered incorrectly, generates multiple candidate answers per question, and uses Math-Critique to rank them. The best and worst answers form preference pairs. The model is then optimized to prefer the chosen (correct) answers over the rejected (incorrect) ones, with an additional cross-entropy loss keeping it anchored to correct solutions.

Information flows through the pipeline linearly: **Base Chat Model → (train) → Math-Critique Model → (score generations) → Critique-RFT training → (generate & score on hard questions) → Critique-DPO training → final ChatGLM-Math model.** The Math-Critique model remains fixed during RFT and DPO training — it serves only as an evaluation oracle to construct training data, not as an active component during policy optimization.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

I will explain the components in the following order, which follows both the logical dependency structure and the chronological training sequence:

- **First**, the Math-Critique model — how it is defined, how its scoring taxonomy works, how its training data is constructed (the only human annotation step), and how it evaluates solutions. Everything downstream depends on Math-Critique producing reliable scores.

- **Second**, the Critique-RFT stage — the data generation and filtering procedure (pre-deduplication, sampling, post-sampling selection), the mathematical formulation of the RFT dataset construction, and the training configuration. This is the bulk of the improvement and establishes the foundation for DPO.

- **Third**, the Critique-DPO stage — how preference pairs are constructed from the RFT model's outputs (focusing on questions it still gets wrong), the modified DPO loss function with cross-entropy regularization, and why these modifications are necessary for mathematical correctness optimization.

- **Fourth**, the training hyperparameters and configurations for all three stages, consolidated for clarity.

- **Fifth**, the MATHUSEREVAL benchmark construction — the dataset composition, categories, and evaluation methodology, since the pipeline's effectiveness is primarily measured against this benchmark and understanding it is essential for interpreting results.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **pipeline engineering paper** whose core idea is that an LLM can bootstrap its own mathematical evaluation capability from a small amount of human annotation, and then use that self-generated evaluation signal to drive iterative improvement through rejection sampling and preference optimization — all without degrading (and actually improving) general language ability.

#### Math-Critique: The Self-Built Mathematical Evaluator

##### Definition and Interface

The Math-Critique model is defined as a function that maps three inputs to two outputs:

$$\text{MathCritique}(\text{Question}, \text{Reference}, \text{Answer}) \rightarrow (\text{Critique}, \text{Score})$$

where $\text{Question}$ is the original mathematical problem statement, $\text{Reference}$ is a provided reference answer (ground-truth solution), and $\text{Answer}$ is the candidate solution generated by the model being evaluated. The outputs are $\text{Critique}$, a natural language explanation analyzing the quality and correctness of the answer, and $\text{Score}$, an integer value between 1 and 10.

**What it computes:** The model reads the question and reference answer, examines the candidate solution, produces a step-by-step analysis explaining what the candidate did correctly or incorrectly, and assigns a numerical score reflecting overall correctness. The analysis-and-score format is explicitly inspired by Chain-of-Thought reasoning — the model must articulate its judgment process before stating its conclusion, which the authors argue improves both accuracy (forcing structured reasoning) and interpretability (users can see *why* a score was assigned).

**Why this form:** Prior approaches to evaluation in mathematical domains use either outcome reward models (ORMs) that output a single scalar with no explanation, or process reward models (PRMs) that score individual steps (Lightman et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2023). This model is closer to an **explainable ORM** — it scores the whole solution at once rather than step-by-step, but provides a natural language justification, giving it the transparency of PRM-style analysis with the simplicity of outcome-level scoring. The explicit inclusion of the reference answer is a crucial design choice: unlike open-ended conversation evaluation (where "goodness" is subjective and reference-dependent), mathematical problem-solving has objective correctness criteria, and providing the reference answer anchors the evaluator to ground truth. Without it, the evaluator would need to solve the problem itself to judge correctness, compounding errors.

##### Scoring Taxonomy

The instructions to Math-Critique encode a four-category classification scheme that maps to scoring ranges:

| Category | Description | Score Range |
|---|---|---|
| Entirely incorrect | Both final answer and reasoning are wrong, errors appear early | 1–2 |
| Partially correct methodology, erroneous outcome | Reasoning is mostly sound but leads to wrong final answer | 3–5 |
| Accurate conclusion, partially flawed methodology | Final answer is correct but intermediate steps contain errors | 6–8 |
| Wholly correct | Both final answer and every step are completely correct | 9–10 |

This taxonomy is more nuanced than binary correct/incorrect scoring. The middle categories (3–5 and 6–8) are particularly important for the pipeline because they allow the system to distinguish between answers that are "on the right track but failed at the end" (useful for identifying near-misses in RFT) and answers that are "correct but sloppy" (which might still be acceptable). The four-category structure also enables the hard-split evaluation metric: answers scoring above a threshold (implicitly 7 or higher, the boundary between "partially flawed methodology" and "wholly correct") are considered passing, and the hard-split score is the proportion of passing answers.

##### Two Evaluation Modes

The paper defines two ways to use Math-Critique scores for aggregate evaluation:

**Average score evaluation:** For a set of questions, compute the arithmetic mean of all Math-Critique scores. This provides a continuous measure of overall solution quality that captures nuance (an answer scoring 8 is better than one scoring 6, even if both are "correct").

**Hard-split evaluation:** For each question, classify the answer as correct if its Math-Critique score exceeds a predefined correctness threshold (the paper uses `correct-bound`, typically set at 0.7 when scores are normalized to $[0,1]$, corresponding to a raw score of 7). The overall score is the fraction of answers meeting this threshold. This provides a binary pass/fail metric that is more interpretable for benchmark comparisons and aligns with standard accuracy metrics used in the field.

##### Training Data Construction

The Math-Critique training data is the only stage in the entire pipeline that requires human annotation. The construction follows a **data-efficient bootstrapping strategy** designed to minimize human effort while maximizing evaluator quality:

**Step 1: Redesign the scoring schema.** The authors redesigned the scoring rules and intervals specifically for mathematical responses, creating the four-category taxonomy described above. This redesign ensures the model learns to evaluate based on *correctness of both result and process*, not just surface-level fluency or plausibility — which is what standard reward models trained on general human preferences would capture.

**Step 2: Filter questions from training data.** Questions were sourced from the training sets of public math datasets (GSM8k, MATH) and from Chinese exam questions spanning junior high to university levels. For each question, the reference answer was extracted from the original dataset or exam answer key. Multiple model answers were collected for each question by sampling from various sources, including different versions of ChatGLM and other models, ensuring diversity in error types and solution styles.

**Step 3: Pseudo-labeling with existing evaluator models.** The authors used CritiqueLLM (Ke et al., 2023) and an Outcome Reward Model (ORM) to annotate the collected (question, reference, answer) triples. From these annotations, they selected the **extreme cases** — answers that received the highest and lowest scores from these evaluators. These "easy" judgments (clearly correct or clearly incorrect) were used as pseudo-labels directly for training. This step generated **10,000 automatically annotated data entries**. The rationale: existing evaluator models are reliable at identifying obviously correct and obviously incorrect answers, even if they struggle with borderline cases. By using only the extreme judgments, the authors leverage the strengths of existing tools while avoiding their noise on ambiguous cases.

**Step 4: Human annotation for ambiguous middle-range cases.** For answers receiving mid-range scores from the automated evaluators (where the evaluator models are less reliable), the authors selected a subset for manual annotation. Human annotators classified each answer into one of the four categories, and these classifications were then mapped to the 10-point scale. This step generated **5,000 human-annotated data entries for training** and **800 for a held-out test set**. The test set was also annotated with the four-category scheme for evaluating Math-Critique's accuracy.

**The total training dataset for Math-Critique is 15,000 examples** (10k pseudo-labeled + 5k human-labeled). The test set is 800 examples, all human-annotated.

**Why this bootstrapping strategy:** Training a mathematical evaluator from scratch would require either (a) massive human annotation (expensive, slow, bottleneck for iteration) or (b) using an external model like GPT-4 as the evaluation oracle (creating dependency on a third-party API, introducing potential distribution mismatch). The hybrid approach — using noisy but cheap automated labels for easy cases and human labels only for hard cases — is a pragmatic middle ground. The key insight is that the initial Math-Critique model needs to be "good enough" to bootstrap the self-critique loop, not perfect. Once the pipeline starts iterating (using Math-Critique to filter RFT data, then potentially re-training Math-Critique on the improved model's outputs), the evaluator can theoretically improve alongside the math model.

##### Math-Critique Training Configuration

The Math-Critique model is initialized from the **base model of ChatGLM3-32B** (the pretrained model before SFT for conversation). This is important: the evaluator is trained from the *foundation model*, not the chat model, because the chat model has been fine-tuned for conversational fluency and may have lost some of the raw mathematical reasoning capability needed for accurate evaluation.

Training hyperparameters (Section 4.3.1): learning rate `3e-6`, batch size `128`. These are used for both 6B and 32B model scales. The paper does not specify the optimizer, number of training steps, or whether dropout or weight decay is applied.

After each pipeline iteration (once an improved chat model exists through RFT or DPO), the Math-Critique model can be **retrained from the improved chat model**, creating a virtuous cycle: better math models produce better evaluators, which produce better training data, which produces better math models. The paper alludes to this iterative potential but primarily evaluates a single pass through the pipeline.

##### Math-Critique Effectiveness

The paper validates Math-Critique against human annotations on the 800-example test set (Table 5):

| Model | Accuracy | Pearson | Spearman | Kendall |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| GPT-3.5-Turbo | 62.1% | 31.8 | 33.5 | 30.1 |
| GPT-4-0613 | 90.2% | 80.5 | 78.1 | 71.0 |
| Math-Critique-32B | 90.5% | 80.4 | 77.1 | 70.2 |

Accuracy measures binary correct/incorrect judgment. The correlation coefficients (Pearson, Spearman, Kendall) measure alignment with human annotations on the four-category classification. Math-Critique-32B matches or slightly exceeds GPT-4-0613 on all metrics — a critical result because it means the pipeline can use its own evaluator (no API dependency, no cost per evaluation, no data leaving the organization's infrastructure) with confidence comparable to the strongest available proprietary model.

#### Stage 1: Rejective Fine-Tuning (Critique-RFT)

##### Conceptual Overview

Rejective Fine-Tuning (RFT) is a **filtered supervised learning** approach. The base Chat Model generates multiple candidate solutions for each training question. Math-Critique scores each candidate. Only candidates that exceed a quality threshold are retained. The model is then fine-tuned via standard maximum-likelihood training on this curated dataset, merged with the original general SFT data to prevent catastrophic forgetting of conversational capabilities.

The name "rejective" comes from **rejection sampling** in statistics: the model proposes answers (sampling), and answers that fail to meet a quality criterion are rejected. The surviving answers form the training distribution.

##### Data Generation and Filtering Procedure

The authors redesigned the standard RFT implementation based on empirical observations that **both the sampling range and the model influence the outcomes during rejection sampling** (Section 4.1). The specific procedure has multiple sub-steps:

**Pre-deduplication of questions.** Before any sampling, the training questions are embedded (likely using the model's own embedding layer or a sentence embedder — the paper does not specify which) and clustered by semantic similarity. Questions are then **evenly sampled across clusters**, ensuring the training data spans diverse mathematical topics and difficulty levels without over-representing any particular question type. This prevents the model from overfitting to a narrow question distribution.

**Multiple sampling iterations.** For each selected question, the model generates $n$ candidate answers (the exact value of $n$ is not specified but is implied to be configurable based on compute budget). Sampling is repeated over 5–10 iterations — the model generates, the answers are evaluated, and the process repeats. After each iteration, the generated answers are pooled.

**Post-sampling deduplication and selection.** After 5–10 sampling iterations, the collected answers undergo content-based deduplication (removing semantically identical answers to ensure training data diversity). Then, for each question, a selection rule is applied: **if there exist both correct and incorrect answers for the same question**, only the **entirely correct responses** are retained. If all answers are incorrect, none are selected for that question. If all are correct, all are retained (subject to deduplication). This rule ensures the training data contains only demonstrably correct solutions, with no mixed-quality examples that could confuse the model.

**Why this procedure matters:** Standard SFT on math datasets uses human-written solutions or solutions from a single model generation pass. The rejection sampling approach leverages the model's own diversity: by generating many candidates and filtering to the best, the training data distribution shifts toward higher-quality solutions. The pre-deduplication of questions ensures topic coverage, and the post-sampling deduplication prevents the model from memorizing specific answer phrasings. The iteration structure (5–10 rounds) allows the training set to grow cumulatively as the model improves — later iterations can produce better candidates than earlier ones.

##### Formal Dataset Definition

The Critique-RFT dataset is defined mathematically as:

$$D_{\text{RFT}} = \left\{ (q_i, a_{ij}) \;\middle|\; \frac{1}{n}\sum_{x} \text{MathCritique}(a_{ix}) < 1 \text{ and } \text{MathCritique}(a_{ij}) > \text{correct-bound} \right\}$$

where $q_i$ is the $i$-th sampled question, $n$ is the number of answers sampled for that question, $a_{ij}$ is the $j$-th answer to the $i$-th question, $\text{MathCritique}(a_{ix})$ is the Math-Critique score (normalized to $[0,1]$ by dividing raw scores by 10) for answer $x$ to question $i$, and $\text{correct-bound}$ is the minimum normalized score for an answer to be considered correct (set at $0.7$, corresponding to a raw score of 7, which is the boundary between "partially flawed methodology" and "wholly correct").

**What this condition means in plain language:**
- The first condition, $\frac{1}{n}\sum_{x} \text{MathCritique}(a_{ix}) < 1$, ensures that **not all answers to this question are perfect**. The average score across all $n$ sampled answers must be less than the maximum possible score of 1. This filters out questions that are trivially easy for the model (where every answer is correct, providing no learning signal for improvement).
- The second condition, $\text{MathCritique}(a_{ij}) > \text{correct-bound}$, ensures that **the specific answer being selected exceeds the correctness threshold of 0.7**. Only answers scoring above this bound are included in the training set.

Together, these two conditions select answers that are both **correct** (above threshold) and **drawn from non-trivial questions** (where the model makes mistakes at least some of the time). This targets the most valuable training data: correct solutions to problems at the edge of the model's current capability.

**Why this form:** A simpler filter would just be "select all answers above a threshold." But that would include trivially easy questions where the model never makes mistakes, bloating the training set with examples the model already handles perfectly. The average-score constraint ensures the training data focuses on questions where improvement is both possible and necessary. This is conceptually similar to **hard example mining** in computer vision — training on examples where the current model struggles produces larger gradients and more efficient learning.

##### Training Configuration

The RFT dataset is merged with the original general SFT dataset:

$$D = D_{\text{RFT}} \cup D_{\text{SFT}}$$

where $D_{\text{SFT}}$ is the base supervised fine-tuning dataset (containing general conversational tasks, question-answering, writing, etc., from the original ChatGLM3 training recipe). The paper notes this dataset "encompasses many routine tasks and can be substituted with an open-source instruction fine-tuning dataset."

**Why merge with SFT data:** Without the general SFT data, fine-tuning exclusively on math would cause catastrophic forgetting — the model would become a math specialist at the expense of general conversational ability. Including the SFT data ensures the model continues to see diverse tasks during training, maintaining its broad capabilities while also learning from the high-quality math examples.

The training objective is standard maximum log-likelihood (next-token prediction):

$$L(\pi_\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{(q_i, a_{ij}) \sim D} \left[ \log \pi_\theta(a_{ij} \mid q_i) \right]$$

where $\pi_\theta$ is the policy (the language model with parameters $\theta$), $(q_i, a_{ij})$ is a question-answer pair sampled from the merged dataset $D$, and $\log \pi_\theta(a_{ij} \mid q_i)$ is the log-probability the model assigns to the answer tokens given the question.

**What it computes:** the negative log-likelihood that the model assigns to the correct answer tokens, averaged over all examples in the dataset. Minimizing this loss encourages the model to increase the probability of tokens that appear in the curated (correct) answers, effectively shifting its output distribution toward higher-quality solutions.

**Why this form:** Maximum log-likelihood is the standard supervised fine-tuning objective in language modeling. It is simple, stable, and well-understood. Unlike policy gradient methods (PPO) or preference-based methods (DPO), there is no risk of reward hacking, mode collapse, or training instability. The downside is that it provides only positive signal (what the model *should* output) with no contrastive signal about what the model *should avoid*. This limitation motivates the subsequent DPO stage.

Training hyperparameters (Section 4.3.2): learning rate `2e-5`, fine-tuning for `8000 steps`, batch size `64`. The paper does not specify the optimizer, warmup schedule, or whether mixed precision is used. The model is fine-tuned from the base ChatGLM3-32B-SFT-2312 checkpoint.

##### Ablation: Impact of Including General SFT Data

The paper includes an ablation (Table 4) to test whether including the general SFT data in the RFT stage is necessary. The ablation is not described in the main text of Section 4.1 but appears in Table 4 with the row "Real scenarios & Academic" vs. "Real scenarios" vs. "Academic." The results show that:
- Using only academic math data produces good academic benchmark results but poor performance on real-scenario MATHUSEREVAL.
- Using only real-scenario math data produces the inverse pattern.
- Using both (as the main pipeline does) yields the best overall performance, confirming that diverse data sources are complementary.

#### Stage 2: Direct Preference Optimization (Critique-DPO)

##### Motivation and Positioning

After RFT, the model's mathematical capability has improved substantially (Table 3: from 52.4 to 61.6 average GSM8k+MATH). However, RFT only provides **positive reinforcement** — it shows the model what correct solutions look like — but provides no explicit signal about what makes an incorrect solution wrong or how to distinguish between partially correct and completely wrong answers. DPO addresses this by directly contrasting correct and incorrect answers, teaching the model to **prefer the correct ones**.

Critique-DPO is applied specifically to **questions that the RFT model still answers incorrectly**. The paper states (Section 4.2): "We employed the DPO method to enhance model capabilities further following Critique RFT." The focus on hard questions ensures DPO provides targeted improvement where it's most needed, rather than wasting capacity on questions the model already handles correctly.

##### DPO Pair Construction

The data construction for DPO follows a procedure similar to RFT but with crucial differences in pair selection:

**Question sampling.** Questions are sampled from the set of problems that the Critique-RFT model answered **incorrectly** during the RFT stage. This is a meaningful shift: rather than sampling from the full training distribution, DPO targets the model's current failure modes. The paper notes (Section 6.4): "in previous stages, questions that the model could solve correctly were removed, making it impossible to ensure that both datasets still have sufficient size for a complete training session." This implies an iterative removal process where each stage consumes the questions it successfully addresses.

**Answer generation.** For each selected question, the Critique-RFT model generates $n$ candidate answers (again, via multiple sampling iterations). These answers are scored by Math-Critique.

**Pair selection criterion.** Unlike RFT, which only keeps correct answers above a threshold, DPO requires both a correct and an incorrect answer for the same question to form a training pair. The formal condition for inclusion is:

$$D_{\text{DPO}} = \left\{ (q_i, a_{\text{chosen}}, a_{\text{rejected}}) \;\middle|\; \frac{1}{n}\sum_{x} \text{MathCritique}(a_{ix}) < 1,\; \text{MathCritique}(a_{\text{chosen}}) > \text{correct-bound},\; \text{MathCritique}(a_{\text{rejected}}) < \text{rejected-bound} \right\}$$

where $q_i$ is the $i$-th question, $a_{\text{chosen}}$ is the selected correct answer, $a_{\text{rejected}}$ is the selected incorrect answer, $\text{correct-bound}$ is the threshold for correctness (0.7 normalized, same as RFT), and $\text{rejected-bound}$ is the threshold *below* which an answer is considered unsatisfactory. The first condition (average score < 1) again ensures non-trivial questions.

**The crucial selection rule:** From among all correct-incorrect answer pairs for a question, the paper selects the pair with the **most significant difference** in Math-Critique scores. This maximizes the contrast signal: the chosen answer is as correct as possible, and the rejected answer is as wrong as possible, creating a sharp preference gradient for DPO to learn from.

**Why this pair selection strategy:** Standard DPO assumes the "chosen" response is clearly superior to the "rejected" one. For mathematical reasoning, the clearest signal comes from comparing a definitively correct answer against a definitively incorrect one. Selecting pairs with maximum score difference ensures the training signal is unambiguous. If the pairs had similar scores (e.g., a 7 vs. a 6), the model would receive a weak gradient, potentially learning spurious distinctions that don't generalize.

Figure 3 provides a concrete example of a DPO pair. The chosen answer receives a Math-Critique score of 9 (correct solution with detailed derivation), while the rejected answer receives a score of 1 (completely wrong approach that miscalculates basic arithmetic). The contrast is stark, providing a clear learning signal.

**Why use the RFT model to generate DPO data:** Both the chosen and rejected answers are sampled *from the model post-RFT*, not from the base model or an external model. The paper states this is "critically important" (Section 4.2). The reason: DPO needs its training data to be **on-policy** — the answers being compared should come from the current model's distribution. If the rejected answers came from a weaker model (e.g., the base ChatGLM), the DPO objective would teach the model to avoid errors it no longer makes, which is wasted capacity. By generating both answers from the RFT model, DPO addresses the errors the model *currently makes*, ensuring the gradient is relevant and useful.

##### The Modified DPO Loss Function

The paper found that "the direct use of DPO loss led to instability in the training process" (Section 4.3.3). To address this, they introduce a **cross-entropy regularization term** on the chosen answer, creating a hybrid loss:

**Standard DPO loss:**

$$L_{\text{DPO}}(\pi_\theta; \pi_{\text{ref}}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(q_i, a_{i\text{-cho}}, a_{i\text{-rej}}) \sim D_{\text{DPO}}} \left[ \log \sigma \left( \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(a_{i\text{-cho}} \mid q_i)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(a_{i\text{-cho}} \mid q_i)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(a_{i\text{-rej}} \mid q_i)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(a_{i\text{-rej}} \mid q_i)} \right) \right]$$

where $\pi_\theta$ is the current policy (the model being optimized), $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is the reference policy (a frozen copy of the RFT model, used as a baseline), $\beta$ is a parameter controlling the penalty intensity for deviations from the reference policy, $a_{i\text{-cho}}$ is the chosen (correct) answer, $a_{i\text{-rej}}$ is the rejected (incorrect) answer, and $\sigma$ is the logistic sigmoid function.

**What it computes:** The inner term $\log \frac{\pi_\theta(a \mid q)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(a \mid q)}$ measures how much the current policy's log-probability for an answer has changed relative to the reference policy. The DPO objective increases this ratio for chosen answers and decreases it for rejected answers. The sigmoid compresses the difference into a probability, and the negative log-likelihood optimizes the model to make that probability close to 1. The $\beta$ coefficient controls how strongly the model is penalized for deviating from $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ — higher $\beta$ means stronger regularization.

**Why this form:** DPO implicitly reparameterizes the reward function in terms of the policy ratio: $r(q, a) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(a \mid q)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(a \mid q)}$. The Bradley-Terry preference model (which underlies DPO) assumes the probability of preferring $a_{\text{cho}}$ over $a_{\text{rej}}$ is $\sigma(r(q, a_{\text{cho}}) - r(q, a_{\text{rej}}))$. By directly optimizing the policy to maximize this probability, DPO eliminates the need for an explicit reward model and the complex PPO training loop.

**Cross-entropy regularization term:**

$$L_{\text{CE}}(\pi_\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{(q_i, a_{i\text{-cho}}) \sim D} \left[ \log \pi_\theta(a_{i\text{-cho}} \mid q_i) \right]$$

where $D$ is the set of chosen answers from the DPO dataset.

**What it computes:** the standard negative log-likelihood of the chosen (correct) answer — exactly the same objective as RFT, but applied only to the chosen answers in the DPO pairs. This term provides **positive reinforcement** for producing correct answers, anchoring the model to the correct output distribution.

**Merged loss:**

$$L_{\text{merge}} = \lambda \cdot L_{\text{DPO}} + L_{\text{CE}}$$

where $\lambda$ is a weighting coefficient controlling the relative importance of the preference optimization versus the explicit correctness objective. The paper experiments with $\lambda \in \{0.5, 1, 1.5\}$.

**What it computes:** a weighted combination of the preference-based loss (teaching the model to prefer chosen over rejected) and the supervised loss (teaching the model to directly produce chosen answers). The $\lambda$ coefficient determines whether DPO or supervised learning dominates. At $\lambda = 1$, both losses contribute equally. At $\lambda = 0.5$, the supervised CE loss is twice as important as DPO. At $\lambda = 1.5$, DPO is more heavily weighted.

**Why this hybrid form is necessary:** The paper's empirical finding that pure DPO is unstable suggests that **preference optimization alone loses track of absolute quality**. DPO only ensures $a_{\text{cho}}$ is preferred over $a_{\text{rej}}$, but if both are terrible (e.g., both are incorrect, just one is slightly less wrong), DPO might still optimize toward the "better" one, degrading overall performance. The cross-entropy term on chosen answers acts as a **quality anchor** — it explicitly pulls the model toward producing correct answers, not just better-than-bad ones. This is analogous to the KL-divergence regularization in RLHF, which prevents the policy from drifting too far from the supervised fine-tuned model, but implemented more directly as a supervised loss on positive examples.

**Why $\beta$ is higher than standard DPO:** The paper notes that "Owing to the addition of a regularization term, the value of this coefficient is higher than that of the standard DPO, with our testing range for this value being $\{0.5, 1, 2\}$." In standard DPO, $\beta$ is typically small (0.1–0.5) because the DPO loss is the only objective, and a large $\beta$ would cause too-strong deviation from the reference policy. With the CE regularization term providing an anchor, the DPO term can use a larger $\beta$ to more aggressively separate chosen from rejected answers without risking collapse, since the CE loss keeps the model grounded.

##### Training Configuration

Critique-DPO training hyperparameters (Section 4.3.3):
- Learning rate: `1e-6` (an order of magnitude lower than RFT's `2e-5`, consistent with DPO being a fine-tuning stage on top of an already-strong model)
- Training steps: `500` (substantially fewer than RFT's 8000 steps, since the DPO dataset is smaller and more targeted)
- Batch size: `64`
- $\beta$ sweep: `{0.5, 1, 2}`
- $\lambda$ sweep: `{0.5, 1, 1.5}`

The optimal results are selected based on the combination that yields the best evaluation performance. The paper does not report separate ablation results for different $(\beta, \lambda)$ configurations, only stating that "The experimental section will report the optimal results under these coefficient settings."

#### MATHUSEREVAL: A Real-World Mathematical Reasoning Benchmark

##### Motivation

The paper argues that existing mathematical benchmarks (GSM8k, MATH, Ape210k, CMath) suffer from a critical limitation: they consist of **cleanly formatted, templated problems** from academic sources, which do not reflect how real users pose mathematical questions in practice. The authors cite evidence that "simple perturbations to these questions can significantly impact performance" (Kumar et al., 2021; Zhou et al., 2023), meaning models may overfit to the surface patterns of benchmark questions without genuinely understanding the underlying mathematics.

MATHUSEREVAL is designed to close this gap by testing models on questions that resemble **what actual users would ask a chatbot**. This includes university exam questions (which are more complex and less templated than competition problems) and questions derived from simulated dialogues with human annotators.

##### Dataset Composition

The dataset contains **545 questions** (derived from Table 2: 75 + 113 + 81 + 73 + 45 + 46 + 58 + 54 = 545) divided into two major categories and eight sub-categories:

**Elementary mathematics (342 questions):**
- Calculate (75 questions): arithmetic and computational problems, sourced from dialogues. This category was intentionally undersampled because "Calculate Applications are less challenging and closely aligned with the scope of previous public datasets" — the benchmark aims to test higher-order reasoning, not arithmetic fluency.
- Algebra (113 questions)
- Geometry (81 questions)
- Trigonometry (73 questions)

**Advanced mathematics (203 questions):**
- Discrete Math (45 questions): sourced from both dialogues and exams
- Probability (46 questions)
- Linear Algebra (58 questions): sourced from both dialogues and exams
- Calculus (54 questions): sourced from both dialogues and exams

The "Elementary" vs. "Advanced" distinction roughly corresponds to high school vs. university-level mathematics. This is a deliberate design choice: university-level math requires more abstract reasoning, formal proof techniques, and handling of symbolic mathematical objects (matrices, integrals, probability distributions), which stress different capabilities than high school algebra and geometry.

**All questions are open-ended.** Answers can be a single number, multiple numbers, or mathematical expressions. This contrasts with multiple-choice benchmarks where models can sometimes guess correctly without genuine understanding.

##### Data Sources and Collection

The dataset draws from two sources:

**University examination questions:** Actual exam problems covering the advanced topics (linear algebra, calculus, probability, discrete math). The answer formats from the exam papers were used directly as reference answers. These questions are more challenging and less standardized than competition problems because university exams are written by individual professors rather than designed for standardized testing.

**Simulated dialogues:** A team of annotators was instructed to "pose math-related questions to our system based on their daily experiences and observations using large models." This generates questions that reflect real user behavior — casual phrasing, implicit assumptions, mixed notation, follow-up clarifications — rather than the polished, self-contained problem statements of academic benchmarks. The paper does not specify the number of annotators or the total annotation time.

##### Evaluation Methodology

MATHUSEREVAL offers two evaluation modes, both designed to be more nuanced than exact-match accuracy:

**GPT-4-1106-Preview evaluation:** Following the evaluation protocol of AlignBench (Liu et al., 2023), GPT-4-Turbo is used as an automated judge. The judge reads the question, the reference answer, and the model's answer, then assigns a score on a scale of 1–10. This is analogous to the Math-Critique evaluation approach but uses an external, proprietary model as the judge. The paper reports this as the primary evaluation for benchmark comparisons because it is independent of the authors' own Math-Critique model and thus cannot be subject to self-evaluation bias.

**Math-Critique evaluation:** The authors' own Math-Critique model is used to score answers following the same procedure as the training pipeline. Both average-score and hard-split metrics are reported. This evaluation mode is used for ablation studies and internal comparisons where external API costs would be prohibitive.

Both evaluation modes produce scores that are **macro-averaged** across categories: "For calculating the total score, we used the macro-average score" (Table 2 caption). This means each of the eight sub-categories receives equal weight in the overall score, preventing larger categories (like Algebra with 113 questions) from dominating the aggregate metric. Macro-averaging ensures that performance on advanced topics (which have fewer questions) is not obscured by performance on elementary topics.

##### Relationship to Self-Critique Pipeline

MATHUSEREVAL serves a dual purpose:
1. **As a development benchmark:** The pipeline's effectiveness is measured and validated on MATHUSEREVAL during training. The improvements from RFT and DPO are tracked on this benchmark.
2. **As an independent evaluation:** The benchmark is released publicly alongside evaluation scripts, allowing the community to benchmark their own models against the same standard of real-world mathematical reasoning. This addresses the paper's concern that "performance on these datasets needs to accurately reflect how models would perform when faced with user math questions."

A key detail: the MATHUSEREVAL questions are **not used during training**. The training data for the pipeline comes from GSM8k, MATH, and publicly available exam questions. MATHUSEREVAL is held out entirely for evaluation, ensuring no data contamination.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: The Self-Critique Pipeline as a Resolution to the Alignment Tax Through Domain-Specific Feedback Bootstrapping

The paper's most fundamental contribution is not any single training technique — RFT and DPO are both established methods — but rather the **architecture of a self-contained feedback loop** that resolves the mathematical alignment tax without external supervisory models. Prior to this work, the field faced a binary dilemma captured in Table 1: general-purpose alignment (RLHF, DPO) improves language capabilities at the expense of mathematical reasoning, while math-specialized training (MetaMath, WizardMath, Mammoth) improves math at the expense of language. The implicit assumption was that this tradeoff was intrinsic — that optimizing for conversational quality and optimizing for mathematical correctness were fundamentally competing objectives that pulled model parameters in opposite directions.

This paper demonstrates that the tradeoff is **not inevitable but rather an artifact of how feedback signals are sourced and integrated**. The key conceptual move is recognizing that the alignment tax arises because standard RLHF reward models encode only conversational preferences (fluency, helpfulness, harmlessness) and are blind to logical correctness. When a model is optimized against such a reward, mathematical capability degrades not because math and language are fundamentally incompatible, but because the reward signal provides no gradient toward — and may actively provide gradient against — mathematically correct reasoning. The solution is not to make math training *stronger* (which causes the reverse degradation) but to make the feedback signal **multi-dimensional**: general conversational quality for language tasks, domain-specific correctness evaluation for math tasks, with the training procedure integrating both signals without allowing either to dominate.

What makes this intellectually distinctive is the **bootstrapping mechanism**: rather than depending on external models (GPT-4 as evaluator, as in many prior works), human annotators (expensive, slow, bottlenecked), or ground-truth answer checking (only available for closed-form problems), the pipeline trains its own evaluator — Math-Critique — from the base LLM itself, using a minimal amount of human annotation (5k examples) plus pseudo-labeling from existing evaluation models (10k examples) as a one-time initialization. After this bootstrap, the entire pipeline runs without human intervention. The Math-Critique model is not just a static evaluator; it can be **iteratively retrained** from improved chat model checkpoints, creating a virtuous cycle where better math models produce better evaluators, which produce better training data, which produces better math models.

This reframes the alignment problem from "how do we build a single universal reward model?" to **"how do we bootstrap domain-specific critics from the base model itself?"** The latter is more scalable, more practical, and — as the results show — more effective. The Math-Critique model matches GPT-4-0613 on evaluation accuracy (90.5% vs. 90.2%, Table 5) while being owned, controllable, and cost-free to query, enabling the massive-scale sampling and filtering (multiple iterations of 5–10 generations per question) that would be prohibitively expensive with API-based evaluation.

The evidence for this resolution is direct: ChatGLM3-32B-SFT-2312 starts at 52.4 on the GSM8k+MATH average and 7.37 on AlignBench language. After the Self-Critique pipeline (RFT+DPO), it reaches 61.6 on math (a 17.5% relative improvement) and 7.80 on language (a 5.85% improvement) — simultaneous gains in both directions (Table 1, Table 3). This is the "upper-right corner" of the tradeoff space that prior approaches could not reach. DeepSeek-67B-Chat-DPO gained language (+6.8%) but lost math (−1.2%); Math-InternLM2 gained math (+5.1%) but lost language (−14.8%). The Self-Critique pipeline is the only approach in Table 1 that improves both simultaneously and substantially.

This is a **fundamental reframing**, not an incremental improvement. The paper does not propose a better reward model, a better RL algorithm, or a better math dataset — it proposes a different *architecture for feedback-based improvement* that separates the evaluation of mathematical correctness from the evaluation of conversational quality and bootstraps the former from the model's own capabilities.

---

### Innovation 2: Rejective Fine-Tuning with Self-Generated Quality Filters as a Stabilizing Mechanism Against Distribution Collapse

The second conceptual contribution is the specific design of the RFT stage as a **filtered supervised learning** approach that avoids the distribution collapse typically observed when fine-tuning LLMs on domain-specific data. Standard math SFT (as in MetaMath, WizardMath) trains on curated problem-solution pairs, but because the training data is drawn from a narrow mathematical distribution, the model's output distribution shifts toward that domain, overwriting the broader capabilities acquired during pretraining. This is why math-specialized models consistently show degraded general language performance (Math-InternLM2 drops 14.8% on AlignBench in Table 1).

The key insight in Critique-RFT is that **filtering the model's own generations provides a natural mechanism for maintaining distributional diversity**. The RFT training set is not a fixed external corpus of math problems — it is constructed by having the model generate multiple candidate answers, filtering to keep only high-quality ones, and merging the result with the general SFT dataset. Because the candidates are sampled from the model's own output distribution, they are inherently *in-distribution* for that model — the filtering only shifts probability mass within the existing distribution toward higher-quality regions, rather than introducing entirely new modes that could displace existing capabilities.

This is a subtle but important distinction from prior work. MetaMath (Yu et al., 2023) augments training data by rewriting MATH questions — a useful technique, but one that introduces *external* data that may be out-of-distribution for the target model. WizardMath (Luo et al., 2023) uses GPT-4 to generate evolved instructions and solutions — again, external data that may not match the target model's pretraining distribution. Critique-RFT, by contrast, works entirely within the model's own generative distribution, using the Math-Critique evaluator (which was itself trained from the same base model) to select high-quality subsets. The training signal is **self-consistent**: the evaluator and the generator share a common origin, so the filtering criteria are aligned with the model's own representation of mathematical quality.

The ablation in Table 4 supports this interpretation. Using only academic math data for RFT (the "Academic" ablation) produces strong academic benchmark results (81.0 on GSM8k, 36.2 on MATH) but degrades real-scenario performance (3.72 on MATHUSEREVAL). Using only real-scenario data shows the inverse pattern. The merged approach (both data sources + general SFT data) yields the best overall performance. This confirms that **diversity in the training data sources matters**, and that the RFT filtering mechanism preserves diversity rather than narrowing it.

Furthermore, the RFT stage serves as a **stabilizing foundation for the DPO stage**. By first training on filtered correct answers (RFT), the model establishes a strong baseline of mathematical competence before DPO introduces contrastive learning. This sequencing is important because DPO, when applied to a model with poor mathematical capability, would learn from weak signals — the chosen and rejected answers might both be low-quality, and the contrast between them might not reflect genuine mathematical correctness distinctions. By applying DPO only after RFT has raised the baseline, the paper ensures that DPO operates on answers where the model already has non-trivial capability, making the preference signal meaningful.

This is an **incremental but practically significant** contribution. RFT (rejection sampling fine-tuning) was introduced by Yuan et al. (2023), and the idea of filtering training data for quality is well-established. The innovation here is the **integration of a self-trained evaluator as the filter**, which eliminates the need for external quality signals and enables the iterative, self-improving nature of the pipeline. The paper's re-examination of RFT implementation details — pre-deduplication of questions by embedding clustering, post-sampling deduplication, selection only from non-trivial questions (average score < 1) — also represents practical engineering knowledge that is valuable for practitioners attempting to reproduce similar pipelines.

### Innovation 3: Stabilized DPO for Mathematical Correctness Through Cross-Entropy Regularization and Maximum-Contrast Pair Selection

The third conceptual contribution addresses a specific failure mode of standard DPO when applied to correctness-based optimization: **preference optimization alone can lose track of absolute quality**. Standard DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023) optimizes the policy to prefer chosen responses over rejected ones, but the objective provides no signal about whether either response is *good in absolute terms*. If both answers are incorrect — just one is slightly less wrong — DPO will still optimize toward the "better" one, potentially degrading overall performance. The paper's finding that "the direct use of DPO loss led to instability in the training process" (Section 4.3.3) is a concrete manifestation of this problem.

The solution introduces two modifications that collectively stabilize DPO for mathematical correctness optimization:

**Cross-entropy regularization on chosen answers.** By adding a standard supervised loss term ($L_{\text{CE}}$) on the chosen (correct) answers alongside the DPO preference loss, the model receives a direct gradient toward producing correct solutions, not just toward preferring chosen over rejected. This acts as a **quality anchor** that prevents the DPO optimization from drifting into regions of parameter space where the model consistently prefers "less wrong" answers over "more wrong" ones but still fails to produce genuinely correct solutions. The merged loss $L_{\text{merge}} = \lambda \cdot L_{\text{DPO}} + L_{\text{CE}}$ represents a principled tradeoff between *preference learning* (which teaches the model to distinguish good from bad) and *supervised imitation* (which teaches the model to produce good answers directly).

This modification is conceptually similar to the KL-divergence regularization used in RLHF (which penalizes deviation from a reference policy), but it is implemented more directly and specifically: rather than penalizing *any* deviation from the reference, it only penalizes deviation from the *correct* answer distribution. This is a stronger signal because it actively pulls the model toward correctness, not just toward staying close to its starting point.

**Maximum-contrast pair selection.** The DPO training pairs are constructed by selecting, for each question, the correct and incorrect answers with the **largest difference in Math-Critique scores**. This maximizes the contrast signal and ensures the preference gradient is unambiguous. Standard DPO datasets (e.g., from human preference annotations) often contain pairs where the quality difference is subtle — a "chosen" response might be only marginally better than the "rejected" one, creating a weak and potentially noisy training signal. By using the Math-Critique evaluator to identify maximally-separated pairs, the paper ensures that each training example provides a strong, clear gradient.

**Using the RFT model to generate both chosen and rejected answers** is the third critical design choice. The paper explicitly states this is "critically important" (Section 4.2). The rationale: DPO benefits from **on-policy data** — the answers being compared should come from the current model's distribution. If the rejected answers came from a weaker model, DPO would teach the model to avoid errors it no longer makes, wasting capacity on irrelevant distinctions. By generating both answers from the post-RFT model, DPO addresses the errors the model *currently makes*, focusing the learning signal where it is most needed.

These three design choices together represent a **targeted adaptation of DPO for correctness-based optimization**, moving beyond the generic "align to human preferences" framing toward a specific "align to objective correctness criteria" framing. The paper does not claim to invent a new algorithm — it claims to identify and solve the specific failure modes that prevent standard DPO from working well for mathematical reasoning. This is a **diagnostic contribution**: identifying *why* prior attempts to apply DPO to math were challenging (as noted in Section 2) and providing concrete, empirically validated solutions.

The evidence for effectiveness comes from the DPO stage's impact: it provides targeted improvements on open-ended math problems (MATHUSEREVAL, Hungarian Exam) and the hardest questions, complementing the broader improvements from RFT. Figure 4 shows that DPO improves performance primarily on questions with average difficulty scores between 5 and 7 — the medium-hard range where contrastive learning provides the most value. Questions that are too easy (the model already gets them right) or too hard (the model cannot produce correct answers to serve as "chosen") see less benefit from DPO, which is exactly what a well-designed contrastive learning stage should show.

### Innovation 4: MATHUSEREVAL as a Diagnostic Benchmark That Reveals the Gap Between Academic and Real-World Mathematical Reasoning

The fourth contribution is the creation and release of MATHUSEREVAL, which is not merely "another math benchmark" but a **diagnostic instrument** designed to measure a specific capability gap that the paper identifies: the difference between solving cleanly formatted academic math problems and responding to real users' mathematical queries in natural conversational contexts.

The intellectual contribution here is the **framing of the evaluation problem itself**. Prior math benchmarks (GSM8k, MATH, Ape210k, CMath) are drawn from academic sources — textbooks, competitions, exam papers — where questions are carefully worded, self-contained, and follow standardized templates. The paper cites evidence that "simple perturbations to these questions can significantly impact performance" (Kumar et al., 2021; Zhou et al., 2023), suggesting that models may achieve high benchmark scores through surface-level pattern matching rather than genuine mathematical understanding. MATHUSEREVAL is designed to test whether a model's mathematical capability **generalizes to the messy, underspecified, casually-phrased questions that actual users ask**.

The benchmark's composition reflects this diagnostic intent. The dataset is split into Elementary (high school level) and Advanced (university level) categories, with sub-categories spanning multiple mathematical domains. Crucially, questions are sourced from two channels: actual university exam questions (which test formal mathematical knowledge) and **simulated dialogues** where annotators posed math questions "based on their daily experiences and observations using large models" (Section 5). This dual sourcing creates a benchmark that spans the spectrum from exam-style formalism to conversational casualness, making it possible to measure not just *whether* a model can solve a problem but *how robustly* it can do so across presentation formats.

The use of GPT-4-Turbo as an automated judge (following the AlignBench protocol) provides a standardized, reproducible evaluation methodology that does not depend on exact-match string comparison — essential for open-ended mathematical answers that may be expressed in many equivalent forms. The macro-averaging across sub-categories ensures that performance on advanced topics (which have fewer questions) is not obscured by performance on elementary topics, giving a balanced picture of overall mathematical capability.

MATHUSEREVAL also serves as a **validation instrument for the Self-Critique pipeline's generalization**. Because the benchmark questions are not used in training (training data comes from GSM8k, MATH, and public exam questions), improvements on MATHUSEREVAL cannot be attributed to data contamination or benchmark overfitting. The consistent improvement from baseline (3.25) to RFT (4.01) to RFT+DPO (4.23) on MATHUSEREVAL (Table 3) demonstrates that the pipeline produces genuine capability improvements that transfer to unseen question formats and difficulty levels.

This contribution is **incremental in form but diagnostically significant in function**. New benchmarks are common in ML, but MATHUSEREVAL is distinguished by its specific targeting of the capability gap that the paper's method addresses — the difference between academic and real-world mathematical reasoning — and by its role in validating that the Self-Critique pipeline's improvements are not artifacts of training set memorization but reflect genuine generalization.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The paper evaluates on five academic datasets and one custom benchmark. The academic datasets are: **GSM8k** (Cobbe et al., 2021) — grade-school math word problems with 1,319 test examples; **MATH** (Hendrycks et al., 2021) — competition-level math problems spanning algebra, geometry, number theory, and more, with 5,000 test examples (the specific Lightman et al. 2022 split is used: 12,000 training, 500 test); **Ape210k** (Zhao et al., 2020) — a large-scale Chinese math word problem dataset with 210,000 examples; **CMath** (Wei et al., 2023) — Chinese elementary school math problems; and the **Hungarian National Exam** (Paster, 2023) — a held-out set of 33 exam questions from the Hungarian high school finals, used as an out-of-distribution test with human-expert scoring. The custom benchmark is **MATHUSEREVAL** (545 questions), described in detail in Section 3 above. For general language evaluation, the paper uses **AlignBench** (Liu et al., 2023) — a Chinese language benchmark with open-ended questions across categories including fundamental language ability, Chinese understanding, open-ended writing, role-playing, and professional tasks — and **MT-Bench** (Zheng et al., 2023) for English multi-turn conversational capability.

- **Base model(s).** All experiments use **ChatGLM3-32B** (Zeng et al., 2022; Du et al., 2022), a 32-billion parameter bilingual (Chinese-English) pretrained large language model. The specific starting checkpoint is **ChatGLM3-32B-SFT-2312**, a version that has already undergone standard supervised fine-tuning for general conversational ability. The paper notes that this is a "newer version of the ChatGLM series and not identical to the model discussed in [19], despite sharing the same model size" (Table 3 footnote). The authors also report that related techniques have been deployed to GLM-4, their larger proprietary model, and include GLM-4 results in the main comparison table as a proprietary baseline with undisclosed parameter count.

- **Metrics.** For academic math datasets (GSM8k, MATH, Ape210k, CMath), the primary metric is **accuracy** — the fraction of test questions for which the model's final answer matches the ground truth. Answers are extracted from the model's response by either parsing the final sentence (demarcated by a double newline) or identifying content following patterns like "Therefore, the answer is," "xxx is," or an equality sign, or within `\boxed{}`. For numeric answers, the evaluation accepts fractions, decimals, or integers with a tolerance of `1e-6`. For string answers (exclusive to MATH), normalized string matching is used. For MATHUSEREVAL, two metrics are reported: **average score** (the arithmetic mean of GPT-4-1106-Preview or Math-Critique scores on a 1–10 scale, computed as a macro-average across the eight sub-categories to prevent larger categories from dominating) and **hard-split score** (the fraction of answers exceeding a correctness threshold, treated as a binary pass/fail). For AlignBench, scoring follows the AlignBench protocol using GPT-4-1106-Preview as judge, producing category-level and overall scores. For the Hungarian Exam, human annotators score solutions according to the official grading rubric, and the total score is reported (maximum possible: 117). For MT-Bench, the standard multi-turn scoring protocol is used.

- **Baselines.** The paper compares against three categories of models. **Open-source mathematics-specific models:** Skywork-13B-Math (SkyMath; Yang et al., 2023), MetaMath-70B (Yu et al., 2023), and Math-InternLM2-20B (InternLM Team, 2023). **Open-source Chinese general models:** InternLM2-Chat-20B (InternLM Team, 2023), Yi-Chat-34B (01-AI, 2023), DeepSeek-Chat-67B (DeepSeek-AI, 2024), Qwen-Chat-72B (Bai et al., 2023), and the ChatGLM3-32B-SFT-2312 baseline itself. **Proprietary models:** GPT-4-1106-Preview (GPT-4-Turbo), GPT-4-0613, GPT-3.5-Turbo-0613 (OpenAI, 2023), Claude-2 (Anthropic, 2023), and GLM-4 (the authors' larger proprietary model). For the ablation study (Table 4), MetaMath training data is used as an additional comparison point, treated as a "comparatively strong" SFT baseline.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** All main results are reported using **greedy decoding** (temperature 0, sampling once per question) with `max-seq-length` set to 4096. The paper explicitly states: "For all datasets, we utilized the results of greedy inference performed once" (Section 6.2.3). This means the compute budget is uniform across all methods — each method gets exactly one forward pass per question — and the reported improvements come entirely from parameter changes via fine-tuning, not from increased inference-time computation. This is a critical distinction from test-time compute scaling papers: the Self-Critique pipeline invests computation during *training* (generating multiple samples for RFT filtering and DPO pair construction) but produces a model that requires no additional computation at inference. The paper does not report the total training compute in FLOPs, wall-clock time, or GPU-hours.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** All results for greedy decoding are single-pass evaluations (one generation per question). The paper does not use cross-validation for the main benchmark results — each model is evaluated once on each test set. For the academic datasets, the reported numbers come from either the model's self-reported results (preferring the higher of zero-shot or few-shot) or from the OpenCompass evaluation platform, again selecting the higher of zero-shot or few-shot outcomes. For the Hungarian Exam, two annotators score each model's answers, with a third annotator resolving disagreements. The paper does not report confidence intervals, standard deviations, or statistical significance tests for any of its main results. For the MATHUSEREVAL difficulty analysis (Figure 4), the average accuracy across 24 models is used as a proxy for question difficulty, but this is a descriptive analysis, not a statistical procedure.

### Main Quantitative Results

The paper's results are organized around three axes: (1) performance on the main benchmark suite including MATHUSEREVAL, academic math datasets, and general language benchmarks (Table 3), (2) the difficulty-dependent behavior of different training stages (Figure 4), and (3) the out-of-distribution Hungarian Exam (Figure 1, right panel, and text in Section 6.3).

#### Overall Benchmark Performance (Table 3)

Table 3 is the central results table, comparing the Self-Critique pipeline (baseline, +RFT, +RFT&DPO) against all baselines across Chinese math, English math, and general language benchmarks. The headline numbers:

**On MATHUSEREVAL (evaluated by GPT-4-1106-Preview, 1–10 scale):** ChatGLM3-32B-SFT-2312 starts at 3.25 overall (3.03 Elementary, 3.60 Advanced). Adding RFT raises this to 4.01 overall (3.86 Elementary, 4.26 Advanced), a 23.4% relative improvement. Adding DPO further raises it to 4.23 overall (4.01 Elementary, 4.59 Advanced), a 30.2% total relative improvement over baseline. The final model (4.23) surpasses GPT-4-0613 (4.14), Claude-2 (3.29), DeepSeek-Chat-67B (3.24), and all open-source models except GLM-4 (5.11) and GPT-4-1106-Preview (5.73). Notably, the improvement from DPO is concentrated in Advanced mathematics (4.26 → 4.59, +0.33) rather than Elementary (3.86 → 4.01, +0.15), consistent with the paper's claim that DPO targets harder problems.

**On Chinese academic math:** For Ape210k, the baseline achieves 78.0, RFT raises this to 87.0 (+11.5%), and RFT+DPO reaches 89.4 (+14.6%). This surpasses all reported open-source models (Qwen-Chat-72B: 77.1; DeepSeek-Chat-67B: 76.7) and GPT-4-0613 (83.6), approaching GLM-4 (93.5) and GPT-4-1106-Preview (84.2 — actually lower than the RFT+DPO model's 89.4, which is a notable cross-model comparison). For CMath, the baseline is 79.8, RFT reaches 85.3 (+6.9%), RFT+DPO reaches 85.6 (+7.3%), roughly matching GPT-4-1106-Preview (89.3) and GLM-4 (89.0). The paper notes that the DPO improvement on CMath is small (85.3 → 85.6, +0.3), suggesting this dataset may be largely saturated after RFT.

**On English academic math:** For GSM8k, the baseline is 75.8, RFT reaches 82.4 (+8.7%), and RFT+DPO reaches 82.6 (+9.0%). The best open-source model is Qwen-Chat-72B at 76.4; the best models overall are GPT-4-1106-Preview (93.6) and GPT-4-0613 (91.4). For MATH, the baseline is 29.0, RFT reaches 39.5 (+36.2% relative improvement — the largest gain across all datasets), and RFT+DPO reaches 40.6 (+40.0%). The final MATH score of 40.6 surpasses all open-source Chinese models (DeepSeek-Chat-67B: 32.6; Qwen-Chat-72B: 31.8) and math-specialized models (Math-InternLM2-20B: 37.7; MetaMath-70B: 26.0), approaching InternLM2-Math (37.7) and GPT-4-0613 (45.8).

**On general language:** For AlignBench (Chinese language), the baseline scores 7.37. RFT slightly improves this to 7.42 (+0.7%), and RFT+DPO raises it to 7.80 (+5.85%). The final AlignBench score surpasses all open-source Chinese models: Qwen-Chat-72B (7.29), Yi-Chat-34B (6.18), DeepSeek-Chat-67B (7.11), and InternLM2-Chat-20B (7.68). It also surpasses GPT-3.5-Turbo (6.82) and Claude-2 (6.78), approaching GPT-4-0613 (7.59) and GPT-4-1106-Preview (8.29). For MT-Bench (English multi-turn), the baseline scores 8.05, RFT shows 8.03 (−0.02), and RFT+DPO shows 8.08 (+0.03). The paper acknowledges this is essentially unchanged and attributes the preservation to the fact that "over 90% of our training data is in Chinese" (Section 6.3). The MT-Bench score of 8.08 places the model slightly below DeepSeek-67B-Chat (8.35), InternLM2-Chat-20B (8.21), and GPT-4-1106-Preview (9.32).

**The alignment tax comparison (Table 1):** The paper specifically highlights that ChatGLM3-32B-SFT-2312 + RFT&DPO achieves simultaneous improvements of +17.5% on math (GSM8k+MATH average) and +5.85% on AlignBench language. This is contrasted with DeepSeek-67B-Chat-DPO, which gained +6.8% on AlignBench but lost −1.2% on math, and Math-InternLM2-20B, which gained +5.1% on math but lost −14.8% on AlignBench. The paper's approach is the only one in Table 1 that achieves substantial gains in both dimensions simultaneously, which is the central empirical claim of the paper.

**The Hungarian Exam (Figure 1, right panel, and Section 6.3):** On the out-of-distribution Hungarian National Exam, the baseline scores 39 (out of 117 maximum possible, though the paper notes the effective total is 117 following the scoring convention of Paster (2023)). RFT raises this to 58 (+48.7%), and RFT+DPO raises it further to 73 (+87.2% over baseline). This final score of 73 surpasses GPT-4-0613 (68), GPT-3.5-Turbo (41), and all open-source models reported. GLM-4 scores 75 and GPT-4-1106-Preview scores 92. The authors note that "since our model's primary language is Chinese, if the model answers correctly in Chinese, we would score it usually," acknowledging a potential confound where the model might answer in Chinese rather than Hungarian or English but still receive credit. The relationship between the Hungarian Exam score and the GSM8k+MATH average is visualized in Figure 1 (left panel), showing a roughly monotonic trend where models with higher math benchmark scores tend to score higher on the Hungarian Exam, with the RFT+DPO model positioned in the upper-right region of the scatter plot alongside GPT-4-0613 and GLM-4.

#### Difficulty-Dependent Analysis (Figure 4)

Figure 4 examines the relationship between the Self-Critique pipeline's improvements and problem difficulty. The x-axis is the average score of each MATHUSEREVAL question across all 24 tested models (including intermediate checkpoints), treated as a proxy for question difficulty — higher average score means easier question. The y-axis is the hard-split score (pass rate) for four GLM-series models: ChatGLM3-32B-SFT-2312 (baseline), the RFT model, the RFT&DPO model, and an additional intermediate model.

The baseline model (blue) shows a roughly sigmoid-shaped curve: near-zero performance on questions with average scores below ~3, a steep rise between averages of 3 and 7, and high performance (near 1.0) above ~8. The RFT model (orange) shifts this curve upward and leftward — improvement is visible across all difficulty levels, but the most pronounced gains occur on questions with average scores between approximately 4 and 6 (medium difficulty). The DPO model (green) provides an additional upward shift, concentrated primarily on questions with average scores between 5 and 7 — the medium-to-hard range. On very easy questions (average score > 8), all three models perform similarly (near-ceiling). On very hard questions (average score < 2), all three models perform near-zero.

This pattern provides evidence for the paper's staged training rationale: RFT provides broad improvement across difficulty levels by filtering to correct answers, but DPO provides targeted improvement on harder problems by explicitly contrasting correct and incorrect solutions. The authors interpret this as evidence that "our two-step approach to enhancing mathematical capabilities can be seen as aligning the model more closely with real-world conditions, with the most noticeable improvements on medium-difficulty questions" (Section 6.4).

#### MATHUSEREVAL Sub-Category Results (Table 15 in Appendix D)

Table 15 provides the detailed breakdown of MATHUSEREVAL performance by sub-category, scored by GPT-4-1106-Preview. The RFT+DPO model shows its strongest performance in Algebra (3.88 out of 10), Geometry (5.41), Calculus (5.22), and Discrete Math (4.76). It is weakest in Trigonometry (2.90) and Probability (3.38). Compared to the baseline, the largest absolute improvements are in the Advanced categories: Calculus (+1.18 over baseline), Discrete Math (+0.72), and Probability (+0.25 in the RFT+DPO vs. RFT comparison). GPT-4-1106-Preview maintains a substantial lead across all Advanced categories (Calculus: 7.39 vs. 5.22; Linear Algebra: 6.96 vs. 3.38; Probability: 7.91 vs. 5.20), indicating that while the Self-Critique pipeline significantly narrows the gap in Elementary mathematics, a substantial capability gap remains in Advanced topics.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Impact of data composition (Table 4):** The paper ablates the data sources used in RFT training by comparing: (1) MetaMath training data as a strong SFT baseline (MATHUSEREVAL: 2.80; Ape210k: 75.8; GSM8k: 77.9; MATH: 35.6), (2) ChatGLM3-32B-SFT-2312 + RFT using both real-scenario and academic data (MATHUSEREVAL: 3.74; Ape210k: 87.0; GSM8k: 82.4; MATH: 39.5), (3) RFT using only real-scenario data (MATHUSEREVAL: 3.29; Ape210k: 74.6; GSM8k: 77.4; MATH: 36.0), and (4) RFT using only academic data (MATHUSEREVAL: 3.72; Ape210k: 75.8; GSM8k: 81.0; MATH: 36.2). The full RFT with both data sources outperforms either single-source variant across all metrics. Using only real-scenario data produces the worst academic performance (Ape210k drops from 87.0 to 74.6), while using only academic data produces worse MATHUSEREVAL performance (3.72 vs. 3.74 — a small difference but directionally consistent) and worse Ape210k (75.8 vs. 87.0). This confirms that diverse data sources are complementary and that omitting either domain harms performance in that domain.

For DPO, the paper compares: (1) full DPO with both real-scenario and academic data (MATHUSEREVAL: 4.37; Ape210k: 89.4; GSM8k: 82.6; MATH: 41.0), and (2) DPO with data from both domains but fewer questions (MATHUSEREVAL: 4.14; Ape210k: 87.8; GSM8k: 81.5; MATH: 37.8). The full DPO outperforms the reduced-data DPO across all metrics. The paper notes that it did not conduct separate ablation tests on the impact of real-scenario vs. academic data for DPO "because, in previous stages, questions that the model could solve correctly were removed, making it impossible to ensure that both datasets still have sufficient size for a complete training session" (Section 6.4).

**Impact on general capabilities (Table 3, AlignBench and MT-Bench columns, and Table 16 in Appendix D):** The paper tests whether the math-focused training degrades general language ability by tracking AlignBench and MT-Bench scores through both stages. AlignBench language score increases from 7.37 (baseline) → 7.42 (RFT) → 7.80 (RFT+DPO). Table 16 provides the AlignBench sub-category breakdown, showing improvements in Chinese Understanding (6.90 after RFT+DPO vs. 7.02 baseline — a slight decrease), Open-ended Writing (8.41 vs. 8.08), Role-playing (8.09 vs. 7.70), and Professional tasks (7.90 vs. 7.27). The paper notes that MT-Bench "remained largely unchanged during our training process" (8.05 → 8.03 → 8.08), which is interpreted as "maintaining parity essentially demonstrates that our method has preserved the original English general or multi-turn capabilities" (Section 6.3). The paper attributes the preservation of general capabilities to the RFT training configuration, which merges the math RFT data with the original general SFT data: the ablation on including vs. excluding SFT data is described in Section 4.3.2 but results are not reported as a separate table — the paper only states that "we compared the impact of including or excluding the sft data in our ablation study."

**Math-Critique effectiveness (Table 5):** The paper validates the Math-Critique model against human annotations on an 800-example test set, comparing Math-Critique-32B with GPT-3.5-Turbo and GPT-4-0613. Math-Critique achieves 90.5% accuracy on binary correct/incorrect judgment, matching GPT-4-0613 (90.2%) and substantially exceeding GPT-3.5-Turbo (62.1%). For four-category classification (entirely incorrect / partially correct methodology but wrong outcome / correct outcome but flawed methodology / wholly correct), correlation with human annotations is measured by Pearson (80.4 vs. 80.5 for GPT-4-0613), Spearman (77.1 vs. 78.1), and Kendall (70.2 vs. 71.0) coefficients. The gap between Math-Critique and GPT-4-0613 is small (<1 point on all metrics), suggesting Math-Critique is a statistically equivalent replacement for GPT-4-0613 as a math evaluator. The paper does not report the performance of GPT-4-1106-Preview (the stronger GPT-4 variant) on this evaluation task.

**Relationship between boosting methods and problem difficulty (Figure 4 and analysis in Section 6.4):** The difficulty-conditioned analysis serves as an implicit ablation of *where* each training stage provides benefit. The RFT stage improves performance across almost all difficulty levels, with the most significant improvements on questions with average scores between 4 and 6. The DPO stage mainly enhances performance on questions with average scores between 5 and 7. This confirms the paper's staged design rationale: RFT provides broad improvement by exposing the model to diverse correct solutions, while DPO provides targeted improvement on harder problems by explicitly contrasting correct and incorrect answers.

**Out-of-distribution test (Hungarian Exam, Section 6.3 and Figure 1):** The Hungarian Exam serves as a robustness check for out-of-distribution generalization. The baseline scores 39, RFT scores 58, and RFT+DPO scores 73. The paper notes that "since our model's primary language is Chinese, if the model answers correctly in Chinese, we would score it usually. We plan to address this issue in future models" (Section 7). This language confound means the Hungarian Exam results are not a pure measure of mathematical reasoning — they partially reflect the model's cross-lingual transfer and the scoring rubric's permissiveness toward non-English answers.

### Critical Assessment

**Claim 1: "The Self-Critique pipeline simultaneously improves mathematical and linguistic capabilities, resolving the alignment tax."** This claim is **supported with a specific, single-model demonstration but lacks cross-model replication**. Table 1 and Table 3 clearly show that ChatGLM3-32B-SFT-2312 + RFT&DPO achieves +17.5% on math and +5.85% on language, while prior approaches (DeepSeek-67B-Chat-DPO, Math-InternLM2-20B) showed trade-offs. However, the demonstration is confined to a single model family (ChatGLM3) at a single scale (32B). The paper does not show that the same pipeline applied to a different base model (e.g., Qwen, DeepSeek, LLaMA) would produce similar simultaneous gains, nor does it show that the 6B-scale Math-Critique training (mentioned in Section 4.3.1 as using the same hyperparameters) yields comparable results. The claim that this *resolves* the alignment tax — implying general applicability — is stronger than the single-family evidence supports. Furthermore, the language improvement on English (MT-Bench) is essentially zero (8.05 → 8.08), so the "simultaneous improvement" claim is really about Chinese language specifically. The AlignBench gains are real but domain-specific. A more accurate framing would be: "The Self-Critique pipeline simultaneously improves mathematical capability and Chinese language capability on ChatGLM3-32B, avoiding the trade-off observed in prior approaches."

**Claim 2: "The Math-Critique model matches GPT-4-0613 in evaluation accuracy."** This claim is **tightly supported by Table 5** but with important caveats about scope. The evaluation is performed on an 800-example test set of Chinese junior and senior high school exam questions and MATHUSEREVAL. The paper does not report the distribution of question types, difficulty levels, or answer formats in this test set. If the test set skews toward the types of questions Math-Critique was trained on (which seems likely, since the training and test data came from the same sources), the reported 90.5% accuracy may overestimate real-world performance on out-of-distribution math problems, non-standard answer formats, or advanced university-level mathematics. Additionally, the comparison is against GPT-4-0613, not GPT-4-1106-Preview (GPT-4-Turbo), which is the stronger model used for MATHUSEREVAL scoring. The paper does not report Math-Critique's correlation with human annotations on the specific MATHUSEREVAL questions used for main evaluation, nor does it compare Math-Critique's scores against GPT-4-1106-Preview's scores on the same questions. This creates a subtle mismatch: the main results (Table 3) are evaluated by GPT-4-1106-Preview, but Math-Critique's validation is against GPT-4-0613 and human annotations on a different test set.

**Claim 3: "The DPO stage provides targeted improvement on harder problems."** This claim is **qualitatively supported by Figure 4** but lacks quantitative rigor. Figure 4 shows that the DPO curve is above the RFT curve for questions with average scores between 5 and 7. However, the figure does not provide confidence intervals, and the sample size within each difficulty band is not reported. With 545 total questions in MATHUSEREVAL and difficulty measured by averaging across 24 models, the number of questions in the 5–7 average-score band may be relatively small. Furthermore, the paper does not report a formal statistical test comparing the RFT and DPO hard-split scores within specific difficulty bands. The difficulty analysis is descriptive rather than inferential. A stronger version of this claim would require: (a) confidence intervals on the hard-split scores within difficulty bands, (b) a statistical test (e.g., McNemar's test on per-question correctness) comparing RFT and DPO, and (c) a clear definition of what constitutes "targeted improvement" quantitatively (e.g., "DPO improves hard-split by X points on questions with average score 5–7 compared to Y points on questions with average score 2–4").

**Claim 4: "The pipeline significantly enhances mathematical problem-solving, outperforming LLMs that could be two times larger."** This claim is **partially supported** but overstates the evidence. Table 3 shows that the 32B RFT+DPO model (4.23 on MATHUSEREVAL, 40.6 on MATH) outperforms DeepSeek-67B-Chat (3.24 on MATHUSEREVAL, 32.6 on MATH), which is roughly 2× larger. However, parameter count is not the only dimension of model scale — DeepSeek-67B and ChatGLM3-32B are different architectures trained on different data distributions with different pretraining recipes. The claim "two times larger" implies a controlled comparison where only model size varies, but this is a cross-family comparison. A fairer framing would be: "The 32B model trained with the Self-Critique pipeline outperforms the 67B DeepSeek-Chat model on the evaluated math benchmarks." Additionally, the RFT+DPO model does *not* outperform the ~14× larger GPT-4-1106-Preview (parameter count undisclosed but widely estimated at over 1 trillion) or GLM-4 (authors' own larger model, size undisclosed), so the "two times larger" claim is specific to particular competitor models, not a general statement about scaling efficiency.

**Missing experiments that would strengthen the paper:**

- **Cross-model-family replication:** Applying the Self-Critique pipeline to a different base model (e.g., Qwen-7B, LLaMA-2-7B) would test whether the simultaneous math+language improvement is specific to ChatGLM or general. The paper mentions 6B-scale experiments in passing (Section 4.3.1) but reports no results.
- **Iterative pipeline results:** The paper describes the pipeline as theoretically iterative (Math-Critique can be retrained from improved chat models, which can then generate better training data), but only reports a single pass (Math-Critique → RFT → DPO). Results from even a second iteration would substantiate the "self-improving" framing.
- **Ablation of the cross-entropy regularization term in DPO:** The paper states that pure DPO was unstable but does not report the performance of standard DPO (without CE regularization) as a baseline in any table. Table 4 compares data composition variants but not loss function variants. This makes it impossible to quantify how much the CE regularization contributes.
- **Ablation of the maximum-contrast pair selection:** The DPO pair selection criterion (choosing the pair with the largest score difference) is never ablated against random pair selection or other pair selection strategies. The paper cannot claim this design choice is important without empirical evidence.
- **Confidence intervals or significance tests:** No main results include any measure of statistical uncertainty, making it impossible to assess whether the reported differences (e.g., 4.23 vs. 4.14 for RFT+DPO with full vs. reduced data in Table 4) are statistically meaningful or within sampling noise.
- **Detailed DPO hyperparameter sweep results:** The paper sweeps β ∈ {0.5, 1, 2} and λ ∈ {0.5, 1, 1.5} but reports only the optimal combination without showing sensitivity analysis or the performance landscape across these hyperparameters.
- **Comparison with PPO-based mathematical RL:** The paper criticizes PPO as unstable and expensive but never empirically compares its DPO-based approach against a PPO baseline on the same base model. The claim of superiority over PPO-based approaches is therefore purely rhetorical, not empirical.
- **Analysis of RFT data quality vs. quantity:** The RFT dataset size depends on how many correct answers survive filtering. The paper does not report the total number of training examples in the RFT dataset, the rejection rate (fraction of generated answers that are filtered out), or how sensitive results are to the `correct-bound` threshold (set at 0.7).

**Genuine weaknesses:**

- **The language confound in the Hungarian Exam:** The paper acknowledges that Chinese-language answers are accepted as correct, meaning the Hungarian Exam scores conflate mathematical reasoning with cross-lingual transfer and the scoring rubric's language policy. The claimed 87.2% relative improvement (39 → 73) on this benchmark must be interpreted with this confound in mind.
- **The MT-Bench English result is essentially flat:** The paper claims "simultaneous improvement" in both math and language, but the English language result (MT-Bench) shows essentially zero change (8.05 → 8.08). The +5.85% improvement on AlignBench is Chinese-language-specific. A reader interested in English language preservation might draw different conclusions.
- **No comparison with process reward model approaches:** Despite citing Process Reward Models (Lightman et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2023) as relevant prior work, the paper never empirically compares against a PRM-based training pipeline. Math-Critique is an outcome-level evaluator (score on whole solution), not a step-level evaluator, so the comparison with PRMs is purely conceptual.
- **The difficulty estimation in Figure 4 may be circular:** Question difficulty is defined as the average score across all 24 tested models, which includes the four GLM-series models being analyzed. This means the difficulty measure is partially endogenous — the models' own performance contributes to the x-axis position. A cleaner analysis would define difficulty using only external models or human judgments.
- **Small test sets for some benchmarks:** The Hungarian Exam has only 33 questions. MATH (Lightman split) has 500 test questions. The sub-categories of MATHUSEREVAL range from 45 to 113 questions. Small test sets amplify the impact of individual question variance, making the reported point estimates potentially noisy — yet no uncertainty quantification is provided.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### Hard Problems Remain Fundamentally Unsolvable — Test-Time Compute Cannot Create Capability From Nothing

**The assumption or constraint.** The Self-Critique pipeline operates entirely on the model's own generative distribution — it filters the model's best answers (RFT) and contrasts them against its worst (DPO), but it never introduces genuinely new knowledge or reasoning capability that the base model lacks. The paper explicitly demonstrates this boundary: on the hardest questions in MATHUSEREVAL (average accuracy across all models below ~2 on the 10-point scale), none of the training stages produce meaningful improvement (Figure 4, Section 6.4). The baseline, RFT, and RFT+DPO curves all converge near-zero hard-split scores on these questions.

**The consequence.** For problems where the base model's pass rate is near zero — the equivalent of "difficulty bin 5" in the MATHUSEREVAL taxonomy — the pipeline provides essentially no benefit. This is not a temporary limitation of the specific hyperparameters or data scale; it is a **fundamental ceiling** of any method that learns exclusively from self-generated data. If the model cannot produce a correct answer for a question, no amount of rejection sampling, preference optimization, or iterative self-critique will surface one. The Math-Critique evaluator can accurately identify incorrect answers, but it cannot *generate* correct ones. This means the Self-Critique pipeline's improvements are bounded by the base model's existing capability envelope — it amplifies what is already present but does not create new competence.

The practical consequence is that for genuinely novel or out-of-distribution mathematical problems (e.g., advanced university-level topics not well-represented in pretraining, or problems requiring formal proof techniques the model never acquired), the pipeline offers no path forward. Organizations facing a problem distribution with a long tail of genuinely hard questions should not expect this approach to close that gap. Pretraining on larger, higher-quality mathematical corpora — or scaling to fundamentally larger and more capable base models — remains the only viable path for those problem classes.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figure 4 provides direct visual evidence: the hard-split pass rate for all GLM-series models on questions with average scores below ~2 (left side of the x-axis) is essentially zero. The RFT and DPO curves do not separate from the baseline curve in this region — all three are flat near zero. The paper also provides indirect evidence through the MATH benchmark results (Table 3): the final MATH score is 40.6%, meaning nearly 60% of competition-level math problems remain unsolved despite the pipeline's substantial improvements. Section 6.4's analysis of the relationship between boosting methods and difficulty explicitly describes DPO as providing improvement primarily on questions with average scores from 5 to 7 — the medium-to-hard range, not the hardest range.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not propose any solution for hard problems. The limitation is acknowledged implicitly in performance tables and Figure 4, but the authors do not explicitly discuss this capability ceiling as a fundamental limitation. The stated future work focuses on graphic thinking, drawing abilities, and precision calculation (Section 7), not on expanding the capability envelope for problems the base model cannot solve.

### Difficulty Estimation and Data Filtering Costs Are Unaccounted for in the Headline Improvements

**The assumption or constraint.** The Self-Critique pipeline requires a trained Math-Critique model to score all generated answers during both RFT data construction and DPO pair selection. While the paper emphasizes that "related techniques have been deployed to ChatGLM, an online serving LLM" (Abstract), and that the pipeline eliminates the need for external supervisory models *during operation*, it does not account for the **upfront investment** required to bootstrap the system: 15,000 training examples for Math-Critique (10k pseudo-labeled, 5k human-annotated), plus the compute cost of generating, scoring, and filtering answers during the RFT and DPO data construction phases. The paper states that the Math-Critique training involves "a small amount of manual annotation" (Section 4) and that "this batch of annotations is a one-time effort, as only this batch of annotated data is needed as a bootstrap for the remaining iterations" (Section 4), but never quantifies this cost in person-hours, GPU-hours, or dollars.

Furthermore, the RFT data construction involves generating 5–10 iterations of $n$ candidate answers per question (where $n$ is not specified), scoring all of them with Math-Critique, and applying filtering logic. The DPO stage repeats this process on the subset of questions the RFT model still answers incorrectly. The total number of generations, evaluations, and training steps is never reported. The paper's claim of a self-contained pipeline "without the need for external supervisory models and manual annotations" (Section 8) only holds *after* the initial Math-Critique training, and the headline efficiency benefits (Table 1: +17.5% math, +5.85% language) do not amortize the cost of reaching this state.

**The consequence.** A practitioner deciding whether to adopt this pipeline cannot estimate the total cost. The 5,000 human-annotated Math-Critique training examples represent a non-trivial annotation effort — classifying mathematical solutions into four correctness categories requires mathematically literate annotators, not crowd-workers. At a conservative estimate of 3–5 minutes per annotation for complex math problems, this represents 250–400 person-hours of skilled annotation. The pseudo-labeling step (10k examples using CritiqueLLM and an ORM) adds external model inference costs. The iterative generation and filtering process adds inference compute that scales with the number of training questions, the number of samples per question ($n$), and the number of iterations (5–10).

For a research team attempting to replicate this pipeline on a different base model and dataset, the practical question is: **is the 17.5% math improvement worth the annotation and compute investment compared to alternative approaches** (e.g., simply collecting more diverse math SFT data, using an off-the-shelf evaluator like GPT-4, or scaling to a larger base model)? The paper provides no basis for answering this because the costs are opaque.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper describes the Math-Critique data construction process in detail (Section 3: "Data Collection") but provides no metrics on annotation time, cost, or annotator qualifications. The RFT data generation procedure (Section 4.1) mentions "5-10 sampling iterations" but does not specify $n$ (the number of answers sampled per question per iteration) or the total number of questions in the training pool. The DPO data generation procedure (Section 4.2) is similarly underspecified. The training configurations (Section 4.3) report steps, batch sizes, and learning rates, but not total FLOPs or wall-clock time. The paper does not include a figure or table comparing total pipeline cost (annotation + generation + filtering + training) against the achieved accuracy improvement, nor does it compare cost-efficiency with alternative approaches.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation explicitly. The authors frame the one-time annotation as an investment that pays off through automation ("After that, inference and automatic model filtering can complete all remaining steps," Section 4), but never quantify the payback. The claim that the pipeline "avoids a problematic scenario: after weeks of manual annotation, the base model may have undergone further pretraining and become stronger" (Section 4) is a qualitative argument for the pipeline's speed relative to fully manual workflows, but it does not substitute for cost quantification. The paper does not list cost analysis as future work.

### Single Model Family, Single Scale — Generalizability of the Pipeline Architecture Is Unvalidated

**The assumption or constraint.** All experiments use ChatGLM3-32B (or the 6B variant mentioned in passing for Math-Critique training hyperparameters in Section 4.3.1). The Math-Critique model is trained from the ChatGLM3-32B base model. The RFT and DPO data are generated by ChatGLM3-32B checkpoints. The SFT data merged during RFT training is the ChatGLM3-specific supervised fine-tuning dataset. Every component of the pipeline — the evaluator, the generator, the base SFT data — is specific to the ChatGLM model family.

The paper claims that "our method was applied during the development process of GLM-4" (Section 8), demonstrating within-family transfer from 32B to an undisclosed larger scale. However, there is no evidence that the pipeline transfers to non-GLM architectures (LLaMA, Qwen, DeepSeek, Yi, etc.) or that the specific design choices (the four-category scoring taxonomy, the pseudo-labeling strategy, the merged RFT+SFT training, the CE-regularized DPO loss) are robust to different base model behaviors, tokenizers, or pretraining data distributions.

**The consequence.** The central claim of the paper — that a self-critique pipeline can simultaneously improve mathematical and linguistic capabilities — may be **specific to the ChatGLM pretraining recipe**. ChatGLM models use a particular training data mixture (Chinese-English bilingual), a particular architecture (autoregressive blank infilling, Du et al., 2023), and a particular SFT recipe. If the base model's mathematical pretraining is stronger or weaker than ChatGLM's, the pipeline's behavior may differ. If the base model's conversational SFT is more or less fragile to domain-specific fine-tuning, the language preservation results may not replicate. If the base model's in-context evaluation capability (necessary for Math-Critique to produce reliable scores with Chain-of-Thought reasoning) is weaker, the entire bootstrap may fail because Math-Critique evaluation accuracy would be insufficient to drive meaningful RFT filtering.

The deployment claim is also affected: the pipeline has been "deployed to ChatGLM, an online serving LLM" (Abstract), but a team using a different model family (e.g., LLaMA-3, Qwen-2.5) cannot assume the same gains without replication. The paper provides no evidence or even guidance about whether the 15,000-example Math-Critique training set size, the 0.7 correctness threshold, or the λ and β hyperparameter ranges would transfer.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The only evidence for within-family transfer is the GLM-4 results (Table 3), which show GLM-4 outperforming the 32B RFT+DPO model on most benchmarks. However, the paper does not report an ablation of GLM-4 *without* the Self-Critique pipeline, so it is impossible to attribute GLM-4's performance to the pipeline versus simply having more parameters, more pretraining data, or other undisclosed improvements. There is zero cross-family evidence. The paper's related work section discusses many model families (DeepSeek, Qwen, LLaMA, InternLM) but never applies the pipeline to any of them. Table 3 compares against other families but never attempts the pipeline on them.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not acknowledge this as a limitation. The authors treat the pipeline as a general method ("We introduce the Math-Critique method... and based on this method, we propose the Self-Critique method," Section 8) without caveating that it has only been validated on one model family. The release of evaluation scripts and the MATHUSEREVAL benchmark (Section 1) enables external validation, but the paper itself provides none. No future work is suggested on cross-family replication or identifying which base model properties are prerequisites for the pipeline's success.

### English Language Capability Is Not Improved — The "Simultaneous Improvement" Claim Is Language-Specific

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper claims that the Self-Critique pipeline "significantly enhances the LLM's mathematical problem-solving while still improving its language ability" (Abstract) and that it addresses "the challenge in simultaneously maintaining and improving both language and mathematical capabilities in deployed LLM systems" (Abstract). However, the evidence for language improvement comes exclusively from **AlignBench**, a Chinese-language benchmark. The English language result (MT-Bench) shows essentially zero change: 8.05 (baseline) → 8.03 (RFT) → 8.08 (RFT+DPO), a net change of +0.03 on an 8-point scale, well within sampling noise for an 80-question benchmark.

The paper acknowledges this implicitly: "Even though our improvement on MT-bench is not significant, given that over 90% of our training data is in Chinese, we believe that maintaining parity essentially demonstrates that our method has preserved the original English general or multi-turn capabilities" (Section 6.3). This reframes the claim from "improving language ability" to "preserving language ability" for English — a substantially weaker and different claim.

**The consequence.** The "simultaneous improvement" framing is misleading for bilingual or English-primary deployments. A practitioner deploying a primarily English-language chat system would get: improved math (the GSM8k and MATH gains are real), preserved Chinese language (AlignBench improvement), and **flat English language performance**. This is not the "upper-right corner of the tradeoff space" that the abstract and introduction suggest for all language capabilities — it is domain-specific language improvement driven by the language distribution of the training data.

This matters because the pipeline's language preservation mechanism is claimed to be the merging of RFT data with the general SFT dataset ("we merged DRFT and DSFT," Section 4.3.2). If the SFT dataset is also primarily Chinese (as the ChatGLM3 training recipe likely is, given it is a bilingual model with a Chinese emphasis), then the preservation effect is language-specific. An English-primary SFT dataset might not produce the same preservation, and the pipeline might degrade English while improving Chinese — the mirror image of the alignment tax.

Furthermore, the +5.85% AlignBench improvement cannot be cleanly attributed to language capability improvement versus an indirect effect: the AlignBench evaluation includes tasks like "Professional" and "Chinese Understanding" that may benefit from the improved reasoning capability acquired through math training, even if raw language fluency is unchanged. The paper does not disentangle reasoning improvement from language improvement.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** MT-Bench scores: 8.05 → 8.03 → 8.08 (Table 3). The paper explicitly notes the non-significance on MT-Bench and attributes it to training data language distribution (Section 6.3: "given that over 90% of our training data is in Chinese"). AlignBench sub-category results (Table 16, Appendix D) show that the language improvement is not uniform: Chinese Understanding drops slightly from baseline after RFT+DPO (6.90 vs. 7.02), while Professional tasks improve substantially (7.90 vs. 7.27). The category-level variation suggests the AlignBench improvement is task-specific, not a uniform language capability boost.

**Mitigation status.** The paper partially acknowledges this through the MT-Bench discussion but frames it as evidence of preservation rather than a limitation of the language improvement claim. The abstract and introduction do not qualify the "language improvement" claim as Chinese-specific. The future work section (Section 7) does not mention expanding the pipeline to English or other languages. A more accurate abstract would specify "Chinese language ability" rather than unqualified "language ability."

### The Pipeline's Iterative Self-Improvement Potential Is Claimed but Never Demonstrated

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper describes the Self-Critique pipeline as an iterative system in principle: "The outcome of this step can serve as a new base model to update both the Math-Critique model and the rejection sampling supervised fine-tuning model" (Section 4). After training the RFT model, "the Math-Critique model can be retrained from the improved chat model, creating a virtuous cycle: better math models produce better evaluators, which produce better training data, which produces better math models." This iterative potential is part of the paper's conceptual contribution — the idea that the pipeline is not just a one-time improvement but a **self-reinforcing loop**.

However, the paper **only demonstrates a single pass** through the pipeline: Math-Critique training (once) → RFT (once) → DPO (once). There are no results for a second iteration where the DPO model serves as the base for a new Math-Critique, which then evaluates and filters data for a second RFT stage, and so on. The paper does not report whether iteration provides diminishing returns, plateaus, or diverges.

**The consequence.** Without iteration results, it is impossible to assess whether the Self-Critique pipeline is truly a self-improving system or simply a two-stage fine-tuning recipe. The distinction matters for practitioners considering long-term investment: a self-improving system promises compounding returns over time as the model and evaluator co-evolve; a fixed recipe provides a one-time boost. The paper's framing strongly implies the former, but the evidence only supports the latter.

There are reasons to suspect iteration might not work as described. As the model improves, the Math-Critique evaluator might become **miscalibrated** for the improved model's outputs — the evaluator was trained on data from a weaker model, and its scoring distribution may shift when applied to stronger answers. If the evaluator gives high scores to all answers from an improved model (ceiling effect), the RFT filtering loses discriminative power (all answers pass the threshold, no rejection occurs). Conversely, if the evaluator is retrained on the improved model's outputs, it may inherit the improved model's remaining systematic errors, creating a feedback loop where the evaluator and model converge on a shared misconception (analogous to mode collapse in GAN training or reward hacking in RL). The paper provides no evidence about which of these outcomes — if any — occurs in practice.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper contains **zero iteration results**. The description of iterative potential is purely motivational (Section 4: "Based on the construction method of Math-Critique, this section introduces the Self-Critique pipeline. This pipeline is a weakly supervised iterative training method..."). The experimental results (Sections 6.3–6.4, Tables 3–5, Figure 4) all reflect a single pass. The ablation study (Table 4) varies data composition, not iteration count. The authors do not report results from even a second Math-Critique retraining or a second RFT stage.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not acknowledge the gap between the claimed iterative capability and the demonstrated single-pass performance. The claim of an "iterative training method" (Section 4) is presented as a property of the pipeline without caveat. No future work is suggested on studying iteration dynamics, stabilization mechanisms, or convergence properties. The deployment to GLM-4 (Section 8) might involve iteration, but no details are provided.

### The Evaluation Framework Has a Self-Reference Problem — MATHUSEREVAL Is Scored by GPT-4, While the Pipeline is Optimized Against Math-Critique

**The assumption or constraint.** The Self-Critique pipeline's training signal comes entirely from the Math-Critique model: it scores RFT candidates, it ranks DPO pairs, and it determines which answers survive filtering. The Math-Critique model is trained to match human annotations and GPT-4-0613 judgments (Table 5). However, the main evaluation of MATHUSEREVAL (Table 3) is performed using **GPT-4-1106-Preview** as the judge — a different, stronger model than the GPT-4-0613 used in Math-Critique's validation. The paper does not report the correlation between Math-Critique scores and GPT-4-1106-Preview scores on MATHUSEREVAL, nor does it report Math-Critique-scored MATHUSEREVAL results for the final models as a cross-check.

This creates a **proxy mismatch**: the model is optimized to maximize Math-Critique scores, but its final performance is measured by GPT-4-1106-Preview, which may have different scoring tendencies, standards, or biases. If Math-Critique and GPT-4-1106-Preview disagree systematically on certain types of answers (e.g., Math-Critique is more lenient on certain error patterns because of its training data distribution, while GPT-4-1106-Preview is stricter), the pipeline could be optimizing for a metric that does not perfectly align with the evaluation metric. This is the classic **reward model over-optimization / Goodhart's Law** scenario, but with the added complexity that the optimization target (Math-Critique) and evaluation target (GPT-4-1106-Preview) are different models.

**The consequence.** The reported MATHUSEREVAL improvements (3.25 → 4.01 → 4.23, Table 3) might partially reflect genuine mathematical capability improvement and partially reflect **alignment between Math-Critique's and GPT-4-1106-Preview's scoring preferences**. If Math-Critique systematically rewards answer properties that GPT-4-1106-Preview also rewards (e.g., detailed step-by-step reasoning, clear formatting, explicit final answers), then the pipeline's improvements are partly stylistic rather than substantive — the model learns to produce answers that look correct to both evaluators, but might not generalize to human judgment or to evaluation by yet another model.

The paper attempts to mitigate this by reporting Math-Critique evaluation as an alternative scoring method for MATHUSEREVAL (Section 5), but the main results table (Table 3) uses GPT-4-1106-Preview scores, and the paper does not provide a systematic comparison of the two evaluation methods on the final models. The ablation study (Table 4) uses Math-Critique scoring, but these results are on a 500-sample test subset and are not directly comparable to the GPT-4-1106-Preview scores in Table 3.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 5 validates Math-Critique against human annotations and GPT-4-0613, showing 90.5% accuracy and high correlation coefficients. However, this validation is on a separate 800-example test set, not on MATHUSEREVAL. The paper does not report Math-Critique's agreement with GPT-4-1106-Preview on MATHUSEREVAL, nor does it report the correlation between Math-Critique scores and GPT-4-1106-Preview scores for the final models' outputs. The MATHUSEREVAL evaluation methodology (Section 5) describes both GPT-4-1106-Preview and Math-Critique evaluation as available options but does not compare them. Table 3 uses GPT-4-1106-Preview scores; Table 4 uses Math-Critique scores. The two are never cross-referenced.

**Mitigation status.** The paper partially mitigates this by providing two evaluation methods (GPT-4-1106-Preview and Math-Critique) and reporting both average-score and hard-split metrics. The fact that the pipeline also shows improvements on academic benchmarks (GSM8k, MATH, Ape210k, CMath) which use exact-match accuracy rather than model-based evaluation provides independent evidence of genuine capability improvement — these benchmarks cannot be "gamed" by aligning with an evaluator's preferences. However, the MATHUSEREVAL improvements — which are the paper's primary contribution and the basis for claims about real-world mathematical reasoning — remain vulnerable to the proxy mismatch concern. The paper does not discuss this limitation or suggest future work on aligning Math-Critique with multiple evaluation standards.
