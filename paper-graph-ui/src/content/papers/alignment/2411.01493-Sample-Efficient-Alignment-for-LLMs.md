# Sample-Efficient Alignment for LLMs

**ArXiv:** [2411.01493](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.01493)

## 🎯 Pitch

LLM alignment methods can be made 2–5× more sample-efficient by formalizing the problem as a contextual dueling bandit and using Thompson sampling to actively select which response pairs to query for human feedback. This unified algorithm, SEA, outperforms all prior active exploration strategies, requiring tens of thousands fewer preference labels to reach the same model quality.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **SEA (Sample-Efficient Alignment)**, a principled algorithm for aligning LLMs with human preferences under a budgeted online feedback constraint, formalizing the problem as a contextual dueling bandit and deriving a Thompson sampling-based solution. The method is empirically validated across three model scales (Pythia 1B, 2.8B, 6.9B) and three preference learning algorithms (DPO, IPO, SLiC) on the TL;DR summarization benchmark. SEA unifies three techniques — an epistemic reward model for posterior sampling (a deep ensemble of MLP heads on a frozen transformer), policy-guided search to approximate intractable argmax over token sequences (greedy selection from on-policy proposal sets), and mixed preference learning to align the policy with both the oracle and the epistemic reward model (synthetic labels from ensemble members interpolated with real feedback) — to achieve up to **2–5× better sample efficiency** over passively online methods (matching the win rate of the passive baseline with 10,000–30,000 fewer queries, as shown in Figure 1 right). The paper further establishes that the optimal exploration strategy depends on the alignment scenario — **E&E-TS** prioritizing reward maximization for online serving settings, while **BAI-TS** maximizes both reward and information gain for crowdsourcing annotation — demonstrating that Thompson sampling variants tailored to the learning objective outperform pure uncertainty-based exploration.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: LLM Alignment Is Starved for Human Feedback

The central problem this paper tackles is deceptively simple: **how do we align LLMs with human preferences using as little human feedback as possible?** This matters because the standard alignment pipeline — whether through reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF; Stiennon et al., 2020; Ouyang et al., 2022) or direct alignment from preferences (DAP; Rafailov et al., 2023; Azar et al., 2024) — is a **data-hungry process**. Training a helpful, harmless assistant typically requires collecting hundreds of thousands or millions of pairwise preference labels from human annotators, each of which involves reading a prompt, comparing two model-generated responses, and selecting the better one.

The cost and logistical burden of this annotation pipeline creates a fundamental bottleneck. As the paper states in Section 1:

> "the volume of human feedback becomes a major bottleneck in practical alignment scenarios"

This bottleneck manifests in several concrete ways. First, **cost**: human annotation at scale is expensive, requiring paid annotators (Christiano et al., 2017; Ouyang et al., 2022) or sophisticated user-facing systems that elicit feedback naturally (ChatGPT, 2024). Second, **latency**: the time required to collect, clean, and integrate human feedback slows the iteration cycle for model improvement, creating a gap between when a model is deployed and when it is adequately aligned. Third, and perhaps most ambitiously, **scalability of superhuman alignment**: if verifying is truly easier than generating — a foundational assumption behind scalable oversight — then sample-efficient alignment becomes the key to enabling iterative self-improvement loops where models generate novel behaviors and humans provide manageable amounts of feedback to steer them. Each query saved in the alignment process is a unit of human effort that can be redirected to other problems or eliminated entirely.

This is not merely a practical engineering concern; it is a **theoretical question about the informational efficiency of preference learning**. If human preferences can be modeled as an implicit reward function $r^\star(x, y)$ over prompts and responses, how many pairwise comparisons $\{y \succ y' \mid x\}$ does an algorithm need to identify responses that approximately maximize this reward? The answer depends fundamentally on *how* the algorithm selects which pairs to query — a question of exploration strategy that the paper argues has been largely neglected in the LLM alignment literature.

### The Missing Properties in Current Alignment Methods

The paper formalizes the criteria for sample-efficient alignment through a **contextual dueling bandit (CDB)** framework (Section 2). In this formulation, the agent (the LLM system) iteratively receives prompts (contexts), selects two responses (actions) for comparison, receives stochastic preference feedback from humans (the environment), and updates its behavior. From this lens, two properties emerge as necessary for efficient learning:

**Property 1: online interaction.** The agent should interact and learn simultaneously — acting with its latest learned policy, collecting feedback on those actions, and immediately using that experience to improve. This is in contrast to offline learning, where a fixed dataset is collected once (typically from a static behavior policy) and the model is trained on it without ever observing the consequences of its own improved behavior. The problem with offline-only approaches is that they suffer from **distributional shift**: the policy being optimized may generate responses that look very different from those in the training data, and the learned reward model — which was only trained on the behavior policy's outputs — cannot provide reliable feedback in those regions. This is why RLHF pipelines include a KL penalty (Equation 7) to keep the learned policy close to the reference policy that generated the training data:

$$J(\pi_\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_X} \left[ \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot|x)} [r_\phi(x, y)] - \beta D_{\text{KL}}(\pi_\theta(\cdot|x) \| \pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot|x)) \right]$$

While this regularizer is necessary for stability, it fundamentally limits how much the policy can improve — if $\pi^\star$ is far from $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ in KL divergence, the regularized objective prevents reaching it.

**Property 2: active exploration.** Rather than passively sampling pairs of responses from the current policy (as in online DPO; Guo et al., 2024), the agent should *strategically select* which pairs to query for maximal information gain about human preferences. Passive exploration ignores the fact that some comparisons are far more informative than others: comparing two nearly identical responses teaches the agent little, while comparing responses that represent genuinely different tradeoffs reveals more about the underlying preference structure. Active exploration addresses this by choosing dueling pairs that reduce epistemic uncertainty about the reward function, accelerating the rate at which the agent learns to identify high-quality responses.

The paper argues that **most existing alignment methods fail to satisfy one or both properties**, and this is the root cause of their sample inefficiency. Let us examine the landscape of prior work through this lens.

### The Landscape of Prior Approaches and Their Shortcomings

The paper categorizes existing alignment paradigms into four archetypes (visualized in Figure 3), each representing a different solution strategy within the CDB framework. Understanding these archetypes is essential because SEA is positioned as a synthesis that addresses the shortcomings of each.

#### Offline Learning (Figure 3a and 3b, without interaction)

The dominant paradigm in practice uses a **fixed offline preference dataset** collected once from a behavior policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ (typically the supervised fine-tuned model). Two major subfamilies exist:

- **RLHF** (Figure 3a): Train a proxy reward model $r_\phi$ via negative log-likelihood on the preference data (Equation 5):
  $$\mathcal{L}_r(\phi | \mathcal{D}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y^+, y^-) \sim p_\mathcal{D}} \left[ \log \sigma \left( r_\phi(x, y^+) - r_\phi(x, y^-) \right) \right]$$
  Then optimize a policy $\pi_\theta$ to maximize the KL-regularized proxy reward (Equation 7) using PPO (Schulman et al., 2017). The fundamental limitation is that $r_\phi$ is only reliable near the distribution of $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ — it may wildly overestimate the quality of out-of-distribution responses, causing the policy to exploit reward model errors rather than genuinely improve.

- **DAP** (Figure 3b): Bypass the explicit reward model entirely and directly optimize the policy using contrastive losses on preference pairs. For example, DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023) minimizes:
  $$\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\theta) = -\log \sigma \left( \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y^+|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y^+|x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y^-|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y^-|x)} \right)$$
  This reparameterization elegantly eliminates the need for a separate reward model and RL optimizer, but **does not address the fundamental data coverage problem** — the policy is still constrained by what the offline dataset contains.

Both offline approaches fail Property 1 (no online interaction) and Property 2 (no active exploration, since the queries were already made). Their performance is fundamentally bounded by the quality and coverage of the initial behavior policy's outputs.

#### Passively Online Learning (Figure 3b, with interaction)

An improvement over purely offline methods is to collect data **iteratively**: train a policy on the current dataset, use the updated policy to generate new responses, collect human feedback on those responses, and repeat. This satisfies Property 1 because the agent acts with its latest policy and learns from the consequences. Two notable instantiations are:

- **Iterative DPO** (Xu et al., 2023): Alternate between training DPO on the accumulated preference dataset and using the updated policy to generate new candidate pairs for annotation. This shows improved results over single-pass offline DPO.
- **Online AI Feedback (OAIF)** (Guo et al., 2024): Extend iterative DPO to a **fully online** setting where the policy is updated after every batch of on-policy preference data, demonstrating that online learning prevents overfitting and yields continual improvement.

However, OAIF employs **passive exploration**: it simply samples two responses from the current policy $\pi_\theta$ and asks for their preference label. This ignores Property 2 — the agent does not strategically select *which* pairs to query. Passive sampling can waste queries on comparisons that are uninformative (e.g., both responses are similarly good or similarly bad) or that fail to explore uncertain regions of the preference landscape.

#### Active Exploration with Fixed Policies (Figure 3c)

A line of recent work recognizes the need for active exploration but applies it only to **learning a reward model**, without updating the policy that generates responses. These methods (Mehta et al., 2023; Das et al., 2024; Melo et al., 2024; Dwaracherla et al., 2024) maintain an uncertainty-aware reward model (typically an ensemble) and use active learning criteria (UCB, information gain, Thompson sampling) to select which dueling pairs to query. However, because they sample all responses from a **fixed proposal policy $\pi_\beta$** (or even a fixed dataset), the data coverage problem remains — the reward model can only be learned accurately on the support of $\pi_\beta$, and if $\pi^\star$ lies outside that support, the agent can never discover it.

As a concrete example, Dwaracherla et al. (2024) apply **Double Thompson Sampling (DTS)** (Wu & Liu, 2016) to select dueling pairs for querying, building an ensemble of reward models. But their policy is frozen throughout training — only the reward model improves. This means that even with perfect active exploration, the agent converges to the best response *within the fixed policy's output distribution*, which may be far from optimal. The paper makes this critique explicit in Section 3:

> "all these works primarily focus on learning uncertainty-aware RMs online without updating LLM policies. Therefore, all responses are sampled from a fixed proposal policy $\pi_\beta$ (or even a fixed dataset), making the data coverage a critical concern."

#### Active Exploration with Policy Updates (Figure 3b with optimistic DPO)

The most recent line of work (Zhang et al., 2024a; Xie et al., 2024; Muldrew et al., 2024) attempts to simultaneously update the policy and incorporate active exploration. These methods are all tightly coupled with DPO and work by modifying the DPO loss to encode an **optimistic exploration bonus**:

- **XPO** (Xie et al., 2024) and **SELM** (Zhang et al., 2024a): Reparameterize the reward model in terms of the policy (using the DPO implicit reward $r_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}$) and add an optimistic term to the DPO loss that encourages the policy to explore responses where the model's confidence is low. This effectively creates an "optimistic direct optimizer."

- **APL** (Muldrew et al., 2024): Uses the standard DPO loss but actively selects dueling responses based on the **implicit reward margin** — the difference in implicit rewards between candidate responses — to identify pairs that are most informative.

While these methods represent progress, the paper identifies three significant limitations:

1. **Tight coupling with DPO:** They are incompatible with other direct optimizers like IPO (Azar et al., 2024) or SLiC (Zhao et al., 2023). If a practitioner prefers a different loss function (e.g., IPO for its theoretical guarantees against overfitting to the reference policy), these methods cannot be applied.

2. **Limited online interaction:** The paper notes that these works' experiments are "also limited to a few online iterations, possibly due to the implementation difficulty of a faithfully online learning system" (Section 3). This means their theoretical benefits from online learning may not be fully realized in the empirical evaluations, and their sample efficiency claims may be overstated relative to what a genuinely online system could achieve.

3. **Implicit, policy-embedded exploration:** By encoding exploration directly into the policy loss, these methods conflate the exploration and exploitation objectives. The policy is simultaneously trying to generate high-quality responses (exploit) and reduce uncertainty (explore) through a single loss function, which may lead to suboptimal tradeoffs. In contrast, Thompson sampling — the foundation of SEA — maintains an explicit separation between the reward model (which captures uncertainty) and the policy (which acts greedily with respect to sampled rewards), allowing each component to specialize.

### How SEA Positions Itself: A Unifying, Principled Solution

SEA is positioned as the first method to simultaneously satisfy **Properties 1 and 2** while being **optimizer-agnostic** (compatible with any DAP loss) and **scenario-aware** (adapting its exploration strategy to either the E&E or BAI setting). The key intellectual move is to center the algorithm not on the policy but on a **posterior distribution over reward functions** $p(r | \mathcal{D})$, from which Thompson sampling provides a provably efficient exploration mechanism. This is inspired directly by bandit theory: in contextual dueling bandits with the Bradley-Terry preference model, maintaining a posterior over the implicit reward $r^\star$ and acting greedily with respect to sampled rewards from this posterior yields regret bounds that are near-optimal in many settings (Russo et al., 2018).

The paper's positioning relative to prior work can be summarized through three design choices:

**First, against offline methods:** SEA operates fully online, updating both the reward posterior and the policy after every batch of preference feedback. This eliminates the distributional shift problem — the policy is always trained on data from its own current output distribution — and allows the policy to progressively move toward $\pi^\star$ without the KL penalty serving as a hard constraint. The paper's empirical results (Figure 5) show that even passive online methods significantly outperform their offline counterparts, validating Property 1.

**Second, against passively online methods:** SEA augments online interaction with active exploration via Thompson sampling. Rather than randomly sampling dueling pairs from the policy, SEA uses the posterior over rewards to select pairs that are either (a) both high-quality under a sampled reward function (E&E-TS, for serving scenarios where both responses must be acceptable) or (b) maximally informative about the reward function's uncertainty (BAI-TS, for annotation scenarios where response quality during exploration is less important). This is the realization of Property 2.

**Third, against existing active exploration methods:** SEA is the only method that updates *both* the reward model and the policy online with active exploration, and does so in a way that is compatible with any direct optimizer (not just DPO). Unlike Dwaracherla et al. (2024), SEA's policy evolves with the reward model, avoiding the data coverage bottleneck. Unlike XPO/APL, SEA maintains an explicit separation between exploration (handled by Thompson sampling over the reward posterior) and exploitation (handled by the policy's contrastive learning), enabling each component to be optimized independently. Furthermore, SEA introduces **mixed preference learning** (Section 4.2.3), where the policy is trained on both real human feedback and synthetic labels from the epistemic reward model — a Dyna-style (Sutton, 1990) model-based RL technique that amplifies the information content of each human query by generating additional training signal from the learned reward model.

### The Two-Alignment-Scenario Distinction

A subtle but important contribution of the CDB formulation is the distinction between two scenarios that require different exploration strategies, which prior work largely conflates (Section 2.2):

- **Explore & Exploit (E&E):** Corresponds to aligning an LLM serving an online application (e.g., ChatGPT asking users to compare two responses, as shown in Figure 10). Here, the **cumulative regret** matters — every response shown to a user must be of reasonable quality, because poor responses degrade user experience. Exploration must be "safe," biased toward high-reward regions even at the cost of slower information gain.

- **Best Arm Identification (BAI):** Corresponds to aligning via crowdsourced annotation, where paid annotators provide feedback and the quality of individual responses during data collection is irrelevant — only the final policy's performance matters. Here, the **anytime regret** (offline evaluation performance) matters, and exploration can be "aggressive," deliberately querying uncertain or low-quality responses to maximize information gain per label.

This distinction explains why some prior active exploration methods underperform in practice: Dwaracherla et al. (2024) uses DTS (designed for E&E) but evaluates on BAI metrics, creating an **objective mismatch** that the paper empirically demonstrates leads to suboptimal performance (Section 6.3, Figure 7). SEA explicitly tailors the dueling pair selection to the scenario — Line 5 of Algorithm 1 for E&E (both responses maximize sampled reward, with resampling until they differ) versus Line 6 for BAI (second response maximizes uncertainty about the preference relative to the first response).

### The Practical Barrier: No Infrastructure for Truly Online Experimentation

Beyond algorithmic contributions, the paper identifies a critical **infrastructure gap** that has hindered progress in online LLM alignment research. The paper observes in Section 5.1:

> "The absence of a performant open-source online alignment system has restricted many existing works to only a few iterations of batch learning, which creates a mismatch with their theories that typically require a large number of online interaction rounds."

This is a significant observation. The theoretical benefits of online learning and active exploration require **many rounds of interaction** — policies must be updated, new responses generated on-policy, feedback collected, and the cycle repeated hundreds or thousands of times. Without a system that can execute this loop efficiently (minimizing the latency of autoregressive generation, preference labeling, and distributed training), experiments default to a handful of large-batch iterations that approximate online learning poorly and make fair comparisons between exploration strategies impossible.

The paper addresses this by building and open-sourcing `oat` (online alignment), a distributed Actor-Learner-Oracle architecture (Figure 4) that achieves up to 2.5× latency reduction over existing implementations (Appendix C, Figure 9). This is not merely an engineering contribution — it enables the *first fully online experimental verification* of active exploration for LLM alignment, as the paper claims:

> "To our knowledge, this is the first work to study active exploration for online LLM alignment with fully online experimental verification." (Section 7)

The significance of this claim is that prior methods reporting sample efficiency gains from active exploration may have done so under experimental conditions (few iterations, large batches, offline evaluation) that did not faithfully reflect the online setting their theories assumed. SEA's results are validated in the setting where the theoretical benefits should actually manifest, strengthening the empirical evidence.

### Summary: Why This Paper Exists

This paper exists because **LLM alignment is bottlenecked by human feedback**, and existing methods leave substantial sample efficiency on the table by either (a) not interacting online, (b) not actively exploring, (c) not updating the policy alongside the reward model, or (d) not adapting exploration to the deployment scenario. The CDB formulation provides a rigorous language for diagnosing these failures, and Thompson sampling — adapted through the three practical techniques of epistemic reward modeling, policy-guided search, and mixed preference learning — provides a principled solution that addresses all four shortcomings simultaneously. The open-source infrastructure ensures that future work can build on these results without fighting the same engineering battles.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

SEA is a complete online learning agent that actively chooses which pairs of responses to show human annotators, learns both a reward model and a language model policy from the resulting preferences, and adapts its selection strategy depending on whether it is serving users live or collecting labeled data offline. The system solves the problem of **sample-efficient LLM alignment** — getting the best possible policy with the fewest possible human preference queries — by maintaining an explicit uncertainty model over human preferences (an ensemble of reward models) and using Thompson sampling to balance the tradeoff between exploring uncertain regions of the preference space and exploiting what it already knows.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The SEA system has six major components that operate in a continuous online loop:

1. **Prompt Distribution (`$p_X$`)** — a stream of text prompts (in the experiments, Reddit posts from the TL;DR dataset) that arrive sequentially, simulating the prompts an LLM would encounter in deployment.

2. **Online Policy (`$\pi_{\theta_t}$`)** — the current generative language model that produces responses. It is initialized from a supervised fine-tuned model (`$\pi_{\text{sft}}$`) and updated after every batch of preference data using any direct alignment from preferences (DAP) optimizer (DPO, IPO, or SLiC). This component is the "proposal distribution" — it generates candidate responses that will be evaluated.

3. **Epistemic Reward Model (ERM, `$\mathcal{R}_{\Phi_t}$`)** — an ensemble of `$K = 20$` independent MLP heads on a frozen 0.4B-parameter transformer backbone (Jiang et al., 2023). Each member `$r_{\phi_t^k}$` outputs a scalar reward for a prompt-response pair. The ensemble collectively approximates the **posterior distribution over human implicit rewards** `$p(r \mid \mathcal{D}_t)$`, where `$\mathcal{D}_t$` is all preference data collected up to time `$t$`. The ERM is updated online using the regularized negative log-likelihood loss (Equation 8) on real human feedback only.

4. **Proposal Set Generator** — for each incoming prompt `$x_t$`, the agent samples `$M = 20$` candidate responses from the current policy `$\pi_{\theta_{t-1}}$` (with temperature `$\eta = 0.7$`), constructing a finite approximation of the action space `$\mathcal{S}_t = \{y_t^i\}_{i=1}^M$`. This "policy-guided search" replaces the intractable `$\arg\max_{b \in \mathcal{Y}}$` over the infinite token sequence space with a tractable greedy selection over the `$M$` on-policy proposals.

5. **Thompson Sampling Duel Selector** — the algorithmic core that uses the ERM ensemble to select which two responses from `$\mathcal{S}_t$` should be presented for preference labeling. The selection logic depends on the deployment scenario:
   - **E&E-TS (Lines 4–5 of Algorithm 1):** Sample one ERM member uniformly, pick the response that maximizes its reward (first response). Re-sample ERM members until a different maximizing response is found (second response). Both responses are greedily optimal under *some* plausible reward function, ensuring high online quality while exploring.
   - **BAI-TS (Lines 4 and 6–7 of Algorithm 1):** Pick the first response via standard Thompson sampling, then pick the second response to maximize the **variance of the preference probability** over the first response across ensemble members:
     $$y'_t = \arg\max_{b \in \mathcal{S}_t} \mathbb{V}_\phi \left[ \sigma \left( r_\phi(x_t, y_t) - r_\phi(x_t, b) \right) \right]$$
     This targets responses where the ensemble most disagrees about whether they beat `$y_t$`, maximizing epistemic uncertainty reduction per query.

6. **Mixed Preference Learner** — the component that updates the policy `$\pi_\theta$` using a loss function `$\mathcal{L}_\pi(\theta_t \mid \mathcal{B}_t, \pi_{\text{ref}}, \mathcal{F})$` (Equation 9), where `$\mathcal{F}$` can be DPO, IPO, or SLiC. Critically, the training batch `$\mathcal{B}_t$` is drawn from a **mixture distribution**:
   $$p_{\mathcal{B}^{\text{mix}}_t} = \gamma \, p_{\mathcal{B}_t} + (1 - \gamma) \, p_{\mathcal{B}^{\text{ERM}}_t}$$
   where `$\gamma$` controls the ratio, `$\mathcal{B}_t$` contains preference pairs labeled by the real oracle, and `$\mathcal{B}^{\text{ERM}}_t$` contains pairs labeled by randomly sampled individual ERM members. `$\gamma$` starts at 1.0 and drops to 0.7 after 1,000 samples. This Dyna-style (Sutton, 1990) model-based RL technique amplifies the information content of each human label by generating additional training signal from the learned reward model, while ensuring the policy also learns to explore under the uncertainty captured by the ERM.

**Information flow per round:** A prompt arrives → the policy generates `$M=20$` candidates → the Thompson sampling selector picks two candidates using the ERM ensemble → the pair is labeled either by the real oracle (with probability `$\gamma$`) or by a sampled ERM member (with probability `$1-\gamma$`) → the labeled pair is added to the experience buffer `$\mathcal{D}_t$` (if labeled by the oracle) → the ERM is updated on real data from `$\mathcal{D}_t$` → the policy is updated on the mixed batch → updated weights are broadcast to the inference engine → the loop repeats.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the formal Thompson sampling algorithm for LLM alignment (Algorithm 1), which defines the abstract objective and the two dueling pair selection strategies (E&E-TS and BAI-TS). Understanding this ideal algorithm establishes *why* exact implementation is intractable and motivates the approximations that follow.
- **Second**, the epistemic reward model (ERM) — how the deep ensemble approximates the reward posterior, the training objective, the architecture choices, and the regularization that preserves diversity. ### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **methodology paper** that (1) formalizes LLM alignment as a contextual dueling bandit problem, (2) proposes a Thompson sampling algorithm tailored to this setting, and (3) develops practical approximations — epistemic reward modeling, policy-guided search, and mixed preference learning — to make the algorithm implementable at LLM scale.

#### The Contextual Dueling Bandit Formalization

The paper's entire technical approach rests on the mapping between LLM alignment and **contextual dueling bandits (CDB)** established in Section 2. To understand *why* Thompson sampling is the proposed solution, we must first understand the formal objective it optimizes.

A CDB problem is defined by a tuple `$(\mathcal{C}, \mathcal{A}, \mathcal{P})$` where `$\mathcal{C}$` is the context space, `$\mathcal{A}$` is the action space, and `$\mathcal{P} : \mathcal{A} \times \mathcal{A} \times \mathcal{C} \mapsto [0, 1]$` is an unknown preference oracle. In each round `$t$`, the agent receives a context `$c_t \sim p_\mathcal{C}$`, selects two actions `$a_t, a'_t \in \mathcal{A}$`, and receives binary feedback `$z_t \sim \text{Bernoulli}(\mathcal{P}(a_t \succ a'_t \mid c_t))$` indicating which action is preferred.

In the LLM alignment instantiation: contexts are text prompts `$x_t \in \mathcal{X}$`, actions are text responses `$y_t, y'_t \in \mathcal{Y}$`, and the preference oracle `$\mathcal{P}(y \succ y' \mid x)$` represents human annotators. The standard assumption — which enables everything that follows — is that human preferences follow the **Bradley-Terry (BT) model**:

> $$\mathcal{P}(y_t \succ y'_t \mid x_t) = \frac{\exp(r^\star(x_t, y_t))}{\exp(r^\star(x_t, y_t)) + \exp(r^\star(x_t, y'_t))} = \sigma(r^\star(x_t, y_t) - r^\star(x_t, y'_t))$$

where `$\sigma$` is the sigmoid function and `$r^\star$` is the **unknown, implicit human reward function** that the agent must learn.

**What this equation states:** The probability that a human prefers response `$y_t$` over `$y'_t$` for prompt `$x_t$` depends only on the difference in their true (latent) rewards `$r^\star(x_t, y_t) - r^\star(x_t, y'_t)$`, transformed through the sigmoid to map to `$[0, 1]$`. If `$r^\star(x_t, y_t)$` is much larger than `$r^\star(x_t, y'_t)$`, the preference probability approaches 1; if they are equal, it is exactly 0.5.

**Why this form:** The BT model is the standard model for pairwise comparison data in statistics (Bradley & Terry, 1952) and underlies nearly all modern RLHF pipelines (Christiano et al., 2017; Ouyang et al., 2022). It converts a complex preference relationship into a scalar reward function, enabling the use of standard supervised learning techniques to estimate `$r^\star$`. Without this assumption, we could not define a coherent regret or optimal policy, because preferences would not be transitively consistent. The alternative — learning a direct preference classifier that does not assume an underlying reward — would prevent us from using Thompson sampling over reward functions, which is the core algorithmic engine.

With the BT model, the **immediate regret** of selecting responses `$y_t, y'_t$` is:

> $$R_t = \mathcal{P}(y^\star_t \succ y_t \mid x_t) + \mathcal{P}(y^\star_t \succ y'_t \mid x_t) - 1$$

where `$y^\star_t = \arg\max_{y \in \mathcal{Y}} r^\star(x_t, y)$` is the best possible response. Intuitively, if both selected responses are exactly as good as the optimal response (`$\mathcal{P}(y^\star_t \succ y_t \mid x_t) = 0.5$`), then `$R_t = 0$`. If both are maximally suboptimal (`$\mathcal{P}(y^\star_t \succ y_t \mid x_t) = 1$`), then `$R_t = 1$`. Under the BT model, this simplifies to `$R_t = r^\star(x_t, y^\star_t) - (r^\star(x_t, y_t) + r^\star(x_t, y'_t))/2$`.

The **von Neumann winner policy** `$\pi^\star$` is the policy that beats or ties every other policy on average:

> $$\forall \pi' \in \Delta_{\mathcal{X}}^{\mathcal{Y}}, \; \mathcal{P}_{p_\mathcal{X}, \mathcal{P}}(\pi^\star \succ \pi') \geq \frac{1}{2}$$

where `$\mathcal{P}_{p_\mathcal{X}, \mathcal{P}}(\pi \succ \mu) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_\mathcal{X}} \left[ \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi(\cdot|x)} \mathbb{E}_{y' \sim \mu(\cdot|x)} [\mathcal{P}(y \succ y' \mid x)] \right]$`.

Under the BT assumption, this von Neumann winner is equivalent to the policy that maximizes expected true reward:

> $$\pi^\star \in \arg\max_{\pi \in \Delta_{\mathcal{X}}^{\mathcal{Y}}} J(\pi), \quad \text{where } J(\pi) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_\mathcal{X}} \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi(\cdot|x)} [r^\star(x, y)]$$

**What this objective states:** The goal of alignment — finding the policy that humans prefer most on average — reduces to finding the policy that generates responses with the highest expected *true* human reward. This is the standard expected reward maximization objective in reinforcement learning, but with the crucial difference that `$r^\star$` is never directly observed — only pairwise comparisons reveal information about it.

**Why this form matters for the technical approach:** Because the optimal policy is defined in terms of `$r^\star$`, any algorithm that learns `$\pi^\star$` must learn `$r^\star$` (either explicitly, as in RLHF and SEA, or implicitly, as in DPO). Thompson sampling over the reward function directly targets this objective: by maintaining a posterior over `$r^\star$`, sampling a plausible reward function, and acting greedily with respect to it, the agent explores in proportion to the probability that each response is optimal. This is the key insight that motivates the entire technical architecture.

The paper distinguishes two learning objectives that determine *how* the agent should explore:

**E&E (Explore & Exploit):** Minimize cumulative regret `$\sum_{t=1}^T R_t$` — every round matters because every response pair is shown to real users. The objective calls for exploration that is "safe," staying near high-reward regions.

**BAI (Best Arm Identification):** Minimize the number of interactions needed to identify `$\pi^\star$`, with performance evaluated offline (anytime regret). The objective calls for aggressive exploration that maximizes information gain per query, regardless of the quality of the dueling responses themselves.

These two settings correspond to the two practical scenarios described in the paper: serving an LLM-based application online (E&E, where ChatGPT asks users to compare responses) versus hiring annotators for preference labeling (BAI, where individual response quality during data collection is irrelevant to the final evaluation).

#### The Ideal Thompson Sampling Algorithm (Algorithm 1)

Algorithm 1 presents the **idealized Thompson sampling procedure** that would be used if exact computation were possible. We walk through it step by step because every approximation in the practical SEA implementation (Section 4.2) is a direct response to a computational intractability in this ideal algorithm.

**Step 0: Initialization.** The agent starts with an empty experience buffer `$\mathcal{D}_0 = \emptyset$`.

**Step 1: Receive prompt.** At each round `$t$`, a prompt `$x_t \sim p_\mathcal{X}$` is presented.

**Step 2: Select first response via standard Thompson sampling (Line 4).**

> $$\text{Sample } r \sim p(r \mid \mathcal{D}_{t-1}), \quad \text{set } y_t \leftarrow \arg\max_{b \in \mathcal{Y}} r(x_t, b)$$

**What this does:** The agent draws one plausible reward function `$r$` from its current posterior belief `$p(r \mid \mathcal{D}_{t-1})$` (the distribution over possible true reward functions given all data seen so far). It then selects the response `$y_t$` that maximizes this sampled reward for prompt `$x_t$`.

**Why this form (Thompson sampling):** The probability that a response `$b$` is selected as `$y_t$` equals the probability that `$b$` has the highest reward under the posterior — that is, the probability that `$b$` is the *true* optimal response. This is the defining property of Thompson sampling: exploration is driven by the posterior probability of optimality. When the agent is uncertain (posterior is wide), many different responses have non-trivial probability of being selected, driving exploration. When the agent is confident (posterior is concentrated), it consistently selects the same high-reward response, driving exploitation. This probability-matching property gives Thompson sampling strong theoretical regret bounds in bandit settings (Russo et al., 2018) without requiring manually tuned exploration parameters like `$\epsilon$` in `$\epsilon$`-greedy or bonus coefficients in UCB.

**Step 3: Select second response (scenario-dependent, Lines 5–6).**

For the **E&E setting** (Line 5):

> $$\text{Repeat: sample } r \sim p(r \mid \mathcal{D}_{t-1}), \text{ set } y'_t \leftarrow \arg\max_{b \in \mathcal{Y}} r(x_t, b), \text{ until } y'_t \neq y_t$$

**What this does:** The agent repeatedly samples new reward functions from the posterior and finds their greedily optimal responses until it finds one different from `$y_t$`. Both responses in the duel are greedily optimal under *some* plausible reward function from the posterior.

**Why this form for E&E:** Both responses are guaranteed to be near-optimal under the agent's current belief, which is critical for online user-facing applications — the quality of both responses affects user experience. The resampling ensures diversity (two different responses are selected), which is necessary for the preference comparison to be non-trivial. However, the paper notes (Section 4.1, citing Russo, 2016) that this strategy can have "poor asymptotic performance for BAI problems, because sub-optimal responses with confidently high rewards might be tried for a long time at the expense of not exploring other potentially better choices." In other words, E&E-TS explores efficiently but may not identify the absolute best response as quickly as strategies that deliberately query uncertain regions.

For the **BAI setting** (Line 6):

> $$y'_t \leftarrow \arg\max_{b \in \mathcal{Y}} \mathbb{V} \left[ \sigma \left( r(x_t, y_t) - r(x_t, b) \right) \right]$$

where `$\mathbb{V}[\cdot]$` computes variance over the posterior `$p(r \mid \mathcal{D}_{t-1})$`.

**What this does:** Given the already-selected first response `$y_t$`, the agent selects the second response `$y'_t$` to maximize the *variance of the preference probability* (under the BT model) over the two responses. The preference probability `$\sigma(r(x_t, y_t) - r(x_t, b))$` is the probability that the first response is preferred over candidate `$b$`; its variance across different plausible reward functions `$r \sim p(r \mid \mathcal{D}_{t-1})$` measures how much the ensemble disagrees about which response is better.

**Why this form for BAI:** Maximizing this variance is an **information-maximizing** strategy. When the posterior variance of the preference probability is high, the ensemble members disagree strongly about the relative quality of `$y_t$` and `$b$`, meaning that querying this pair will provide substantial information to reduce epistemic uncertainty. Unlike E&E-TS, this criterion does *not* require `$y'_t$` to have high reward — it may deliberately select responses that are likely poor if they are informative. This is acceptable in the BAI setting because annotators are paid; the quality of individual responses during data collection does not affect user experience. The paper empirically validates (Section 6.3, Figure 7 Middle) that BAI-TS achieves more efficient offline performance improvement than E&E-TS, confirming that uncertainty-directed exploration accelerates policy learning even if it temporarily selects suboptimal responses.

**Step 4: Query oracle and update experience (Line 7).**

> $$\text{Query } \mathcal{P} \text{ to label } \{y_t, y'_t\}, \text{ update } \mathcal{D}_t \leftarrow \mathcal{D}_{t-1} \cup \{x_t, y^+_t, y^-_t\}$$

The selected pair is presented to the preference oracle (human annotator or a simulated reward model in experiments), which returns the winning and losing responses `$(y^+_t, y^-_t)$` based on binary feedback `$z_t$`. The new preference triplet is appended to the experience buffer.

**Why the algorithm is intractable for LLMs.** The paper identifies three fundamental obstacles (Section 4.1) that prevent direct implementation of Algorithm 1:

1. **The reward posterior `$p(r \mid \mathcal{D})$` is intractable.** For large transformer-based neural networks, computing and sampling from a true Bayesian posterior is computationally impossible. The posterior distribution over the model's millions or billions of parameters cannot be exactly represented, and even approximate methods like Hamiltonian Monte Carlo are far too slow for online learning.

2. **`$\arg\max_{b \in \mathcal{Y}}$` over the token sequence space is intractable.** Unlike finite action spaces in standard bandit problems, `$\mathcal{Y}$` is the space of all possible token sequences of arbitrary length — a combinatorially vast discrete space. Finding the globally optimal response under a given reward function is a search problem equivalent to decoding from a language model, which is NP-hard in general.

3. **The agent needs a generative model `$\pi_\theta$`, not just a reward model.** An LLM agent must be able to *produce* text responses, which requires a generative model. The reward posterior `$p(r \mid \mathcal{D})$` alone does not define a distribution over responses — it only scores them. Converting a reward function into a generative policy requires additional optimization (e.g., RL fine-tuning), which Algorithm 1 does not address.

The practical SEA agent (Algorithm 2) addresses each of these intractabilities through a specific approximation technique, which we now examine in detail.

#### Epistemic Reward Model for Posterior Sampling (Section 4.2.1)

The first intractability — representing and sampling from the reward posterior — is addressed by using a **deep ensemble** as an approximate posterior. The core idea is that an ensemble of `$K$` independently-initialized neural networks, trained on the same data with stochastic gradient descent, provides a practical approximation to Bayesian inference: each ensemble member represents a sample from an approximate posterior, and the disagreement across members captures epistemic (model) uncertainty (Lakshminarayanan et al., 2017).

**Architecture.** The ERM is built on top of a **pretrained and frozen 0.4B-parameter transformer** (Jiang et al., 2023). The transformer's language modeling head is removed, and an ensemble of `$K = 20$` independent MLP heads is added. Each MLP head has **2 hidden layers of 128 nodes** and outputs a scalar reward `$r_{\phi_t^k}(x, y)$` for a given prompt-response pair. The transformer backbone is shared across all ensemble members and is *not* updated during online learning — only the 20 MLP heads are trained. This design choice dramatically reduces the computational cost of maintaining an ensemble because gradients only flow through the small MLPs, and the heavy transformer backbone is frozen and only used in inference mode.

**Why an ensemble of MLP heads on a frozen backbone?** The alternative of training a full ensemble of 20 separate transformers would be prohibitively expensive — 20 forward and backward passes through a 0.4B model per training step is infeasible. By sharing the frozen transformer, the ERM achieves ensemble diversity through the independently-trained MLP heads while keeping training cost manageable. The frozen backbone also ensures that the feature representations remain stable throughout online learning, which is important because if the backbone were fine-tuned, the ensemble members might collapse to similar solutions. This design is directly inspired by Dwaracherla et al. (2024), who also use a frozen backbone with trainable heads for their uncertainty-aware reward model.

**Training objective.** Each ensemble member is trained independently using the standard Bradley-Terry negative log-likelihood loss, augmented with an L2 regularization term toward its initial random weights:

> $$\mathcal{L}_R(\Phi_t \mid \mathcal{D}_t) = \sum_{k=1}^K \left[ \mathcal{L}_r(\phi_t^k \mid \mathcal{D}_t) - \lambda \lVert \phi_t^k - \phi_0^k \rVert \right]$$

where `$\Phi_t = \{\phi_t^k\}_{k=1}^K$` is the set of all ensemble member weights at time `$t$`, `$\mathcal{L}_r$` is defined as:

> $$\mathcal{L}_r(\phi \mid \mathcal{D}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y^+, y^-) \sim p_\mathcal{D}} \left[ \log \sigma \left( r_\phi(x, y^+) - r_\phi(x, y^-) \right) \right]$$

and `$\lambda = 0.5$` controls the strength of regularization toward the initial weights `$\phi_0^k$`.

**What this loss computes:** The first term `$\mathcal{L}_r$` is the maximum-likelihood objective for the Bradley-Terry model: it encourages the reward model to assign higher scores to preferred responses `$y^+$` than to dispreferred responses `$y^-$`, with the sigmoid `$\sigma$` converting the score difference into a preference probability. Minimizing the negative log-likelihood makes the model's predicted preference probabilities match the observed human preferences. The second term `$-\lambda \lVert \phi_t^k - \phi_0^k \rVert$` is an L2 penalty that pulls each ensemble member's weights back toward its random initialization.

**Why this regularization?** The key insight from Dwaracherla et al. (2024) is that without the regularization toward diverse initializations, ensemble members trained on the same data with stochastic gradient descent tend to converge to similar solutions, reducing the ensemble's ability to capture epistemic uncertainty. By penalizing deviation from each member's unique initialization, the regularization preserves the diversity that was randomly introduced at initialization time, ensuring that the ensemble continues to represent a meaningful posterior approximation even as it converges on the training data. The coefficient `$\lambda = 0.5$` was selected through a "coarse hyperparameter search" (Appendix D).

**Posterior sampling via uniform selection.** With the ensemble approximating the posterior, "sampling `$r \sim p(r \mid \mathcal{D}_{t-1})$`" in Algorithm 1 reduces to the trivial operation of randomly picking one member `$\phi \sim \text{Uniform}(\Phi_{t-1})$` and using its reward function as the sampled `$r$`. This is computationally cheap — it is a single integer random draw.

**Why this approximates Thompson sampling:** Each ensemble member `$\phi_t^k$` was trained on the same data but started from a different random initialization (and, due to stochastic mini-batch ordering and the L2 penalty, converged to a slightly different solution). Qin et al. (2022) provide theoretical results showing that ensemble sampling approximates Thompson sampling in linear bandits, and Chapelle & Li (2011) demonstrate its empirical effectiveness at scale. While there is no formal guarantee for deep nonlinear models, the paper relies on the empirical success of deep ensemble methods for uncertainty quantification (Lakshminarayanan et al., 2017) as justification.

**ERM update protocol (Algorithm 2, Line 9).** At each round `$t$`, the ERM is updated using `$m = 5$` batches randomly sampled from the cumulative experience buffer `$\mathcal{D}_t$`. The paper notes: "We find setting `$m = 5$` suffices to achieve reasonable accuracy" (Appendix A). This replay-based approach is necessary because the preference data arrives online, and the ERM needs to revisit earlier data to avoid catastrophic forgetting — if it only trained on the most recent batch, it would quickly lose knowledge about the reward function in regions of the prompt/response space that it hasn't explored recently.

**Why replay from a buffer rather than online SGD on each new batch?** In non-stationary online learning, replay buffers are standard practice for stabilizing training and preventing catastrophic forgetting (Espeholt et al., 2018). The ERM's training distribution is the cumulative experience `$\mathcal{D}_t$` — all data collected so far — because the goal is to learn a reward model that is accurate across the entire support of the policy's trajectory, not just the most recent data. Sampling `$m = 5$` random batches per round amortizes the cost of maintaining accuracy without requiring a full pass over the buffer every round.

**Critical detail: Synthetic labels are NOT added to the experience buffer.** The paper explicitly notes (Appendix A, after Algorithm 2): "the synthetic experience is not added into `$\mathcal{D}_t$` to ensure reward learning always uses ground truth environment data." This is important because if the ERM were trained on its own synthetic labels, errors would compound — a classical problem in model-based RL where training a model on its own predictions leads to divergence. The ERM is trained exclusively on real human feedback, ensuring it remains grounded in the true preference oracle.

#### Policy-Guided Search to Approximate argmax (Section 4.2.2)

The second intractability — `$\arg\max_{b \in \mathcal{Y}}$` over the infinite token sequence space — is addressed by replacing global optimization with **local search over a finite set of on-policy proposals**. The key design choice is that the proposal set is generated from the current online policy `$\pi_{\theta_{t-1}}$`, not from a fixed reference model.

**The problem.** In Algorithm 1, response selection requires computing `$\arg\max_{b \in \mathcal{Y}} U(b)$`, where `$U$` is a utility function that encapsulates the prompt, the sampled reward function (from Thompson sampling), and optionally the already-selected first response (for BAI-TS). `$\mathcal{Y}$` is the space of all possible text sequences — infinitely large and defined implicitly through the language model's token-by-token generative process. Standard approaches from contextual bandits with large action spaces typically assume a linear structure `$U(b) = \langle \theta, \psi(b) \rangle$` where `$\psi(b)$` is a known feature map (Katz-Samuels et al., 2020; Zhu et al., 2022), enabling efficient search via maximum inner product search. This assumption does not hold for LLMs, where the utility function is a deep neural network applied to token sequences.

**The solution: policy-guided search.** The paper replaces `$\arg\max_{b \in \mathcal{Y}}$` with **greedy selection from a finite proposal set `$\mathcal{S}_t$`** constructed by sampling `$M$` candidate responses from the current policy:

> $$\mathcal{S}_t = \{y_t^i\}_{i=1}^M, \quad \text{where } y_t^i \sim \pi_{\theta_{t-1}}(\cdot \mid x_t)$$

with sampling temperature `$\eta = 0.7$` (Appendix D). Then, instead of `$\arg\max_{b \in \mathcal{Y}}$`:

> $$y_t = \arg\max_{b \in \mathcal{S}_t} r_{\phi}(x_t, b) \quad \text{for some sampled } r_\phi$$

That is, the agent simply evaluates the utility function on each candidate in `$\mathcal{S}_t$` and picks the one with the highest score — a `$\text{Best-of-}M$` selection with `$M = 20$`.

**What this approximates and why it works.** The proposal distribution `$\pi_{\theta_{t-1}}$` is optimized to place high probability on responses with high oracle reward (since it is trained via DAP on preference data). Therefore, the proposal set `$\mathcal{S}_t$` is concentrated in the region of `$\mathcal{Y}$` where high-reward responses are likely to be found. Selecting the best response from this finite set approximates selecting the globally best response because the mass of `$\pi_{\theta_{t-1}}$` is already near the optimum. This is a form of **importance sampling**: rather than searching the full space, we restrict attention to a region that is likely to contain good responses, and we sample densely within that region.

**Why `$M=20$`?** This is a practical tradeoff between approximation quality and computational cost. Larger `$M$` provides a better approximation to `$\arg\max$` (more candidates means a higher chance that one is near-optimal) but costs more inference compute. The paper likely selected 20 as a reasonable balance, though no ablation over `$M$` is reported.

**Why sample with temperature `$\eta = 0.7$` rather than greedily?** Greedy decoding (`$\eta = 0$`) would produce only the single most likely response under the policy, providing no diversity in `$\mathcal{S}_t$` and making the `$\arg\max$` over `$\mathcal{S}_t$` trivial. Temperature `$\eta = 0.7$` introduces stochasticity that produces a set of diverse, on-policy candidates, enabling meaningful comparison under the utility function. During offline evaluation (when measuring BAI performance), the paper uses greedy decoding — this makes sense because at evaluation time, the goal is to extract the single best response, not to explore.

**The critical dependence on `$\pi_{\text{prior}}$`.** The proposal distribution must place high probability on good responses; otherwise, the proposal set `$\mathcal{S}_t$` will not contain any near-optimal candidates, and the `$\arg\max$` approximation fails regardless of `$M$`. The paper argues (Section 4.2.3) that `$\pi_{\theta_{t-1}}$` — the current online policy — is the right choice for `$\pi_{\text{prior}}$` because it is being continuously optimized toward `$\pi^\star$`. This is in contrast to Dwaracherla et al. (2024), which uses a fixed behavior policy for proposal generation and therefore cannot find responses outside that policy's support.

**What about BAI-TS's uncertainty-maximizing objective?** For BAI-TS (Algorithm 1, Line 6), the utility function is not a single reward but the variance of the preference probability across ensemble members:

> $$U(b) = \mathbb{V}_\phi \left[ \sigma \left( r_\phi(x_t, y_t) - r_\phi(x_t, b) \right) \right]$$

where `$y_t$` is the already-selected first response. The same proposal set `$\mathcal{S}_t$` is reused — the agent computes this variance for each candidate `$b \in \mathcal{S}_t$` and selects the maximizer. The paper notes (Section 4.2.2): "We also reuse the same `$\mathcal{S}_t$` for different U functions at time `$t$` to save computation." This is an important practical detail: for each prompt, the policy generates `$M = 20$` candidates once, and those candidates are used for both steps of the dueling pair selection (Thompson sampling for the first response, then either E&E-TS or BAI-TS for the second), avoiding redundant autoregressive generation.

#### Online Policy Learning from Mixed Preferences (Section 4.2.3)

The third intractability — converting a reward model into a generative policy — is addressed by using any **direct alignment from preferences (DAP) optimizer** to update the policy online. This is the component that actually produces the LLM `$\pi_\theta$`, and it introduces the novel **mixed preference learning** mechanism.

**Policy learning loss.** The policy is updated at each round `$t$` using a batch `$\mathcal{B}_t$` of preference data:

> $$\mathcal{L}_\pi(\theta_t \mid \mathcal{B}_t, \pi_{\text{ref}}, \mathcal{F}) = \mathbb{E}_{(x, y^+, y^-) \sim p_{\mathcal{B}_t}} \left[ \mathcal{F}_{\theta_t}(x, y^+, y^-, \pi_{\text{ref}}) \right]$$

where `$\mathcal{F}$` is any DAP loss function, `$\pi_{\text{ref}} = \pi_{\text{sft}}$` is the supervised fine-tuned reference policy, and `$\mathcal{B}_t$` is a batch of preference data.

**What this does:** Given a batch of preference triplets `$(x, y^+, y^-)$` — prompts paired with winning and losing responses — the policy parameters `$\theta$` are updated to increase the probability of generating winning responses and decrease the probability of generating losing responses, while staying close to the reference policy `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$` (through a KL penalty or similar mechanism embedded in `$\mathcal{F}$`). The exact form depends on `$\mathcal{F}$`:

- **DPO** (Rafailov et al., 2023):
  $$\mathcal{F}_\theta(x, y^+, y^-, \pi_{\text{ref}}) = -\log \sigma \left( \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y^+ \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y^+ \mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y^- \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y^- \mid x)} \right)$$
  where `$\beta = 0.1$` controls the deviation from `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$`. DPO implicitly represents the reward as `$\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\text{ref}}}$` and directly optimizes the logistic preference loss on the policy's implicit reward differences.

- **IPO** (Azar et al., 2024):
  $$\mathcal{F}_\theta(x, y^+, y^-, \pi_{\text{ref}}) = \left( \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y^+ \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y^+ \mid x)} - \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y^- \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y^- \mid x)} - \frac{1}{2\beta} \right)^2$$
  where `$\beta$` is tuned from `$\{0.2, 0.3, 0.5, 1.0\}$` per scale. IPO is a squared loss on the log-ratio difference, which avoids the sigmoid saturation issues of DPO and provides theoretical guarantees against overfitting.

- **SLiC** (Zhao et al., 2023):
  $$\mathcal{F}_\theta(x, y^+, y^-, \pi_{\text{ref}}) = \max \left( 0, 1 - \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y^+ \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y^+ \mid x)} + \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y^- \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y^- \mid x)} \right)$$
  with `$\beta = 0.2$`. SLiC uses a hinge loss that only penalizes the model when the log-ratio margin between winning and losing responses is below a threshold, making it robust to easy preference pairs.

**Why use direct optimizers instead of RL (PPO)?** DAP methods are "largely simplifies and stabilizes the alignment process" compared to RLHF with PPO (Section 3). They avoid the instability of RL training (reward model exploitation, policy collapse, sensitivity to hyperparameters) and the computational overhead of maintaining a separate reward model during policy optimization. By reparameterizing the reward in terms of the policy itself (in the case of DPO and IPO) or using a simple ranking loss (SLiC), DAP methods enable end-to-end supervised learning on preference data. The paper's choice to show results with three different DAP optimizers demonstrates that SEA's sample efficiency gains are not tied to any particular loss function — the active exploration mechanism is **optimizer-agnostic**.

**The mixed preference distribution.** This is the novel contribution of Section 4.2.3. Rather than training the policy solely on real human feedback `$\mathcal{B}_t$` (which is expensive and limited), SEA trains on a mixture:

> $$p_{\mathcal{B}^{\text{mix}}_t} = \gamma \, p_{\mathcal{B}_t} + (1 - \gamma) \, p_{\mathcal{B}^{\text{ERM}}_t}$$

where:
- `$\mathcal{B}_t = \{x_i, y^+_i, y^-_i\}_{i=1}^b$` contains preference data labeled by the **real preference oracle** (in experiments, a strong scalar reward model Skywork-Reward-Llama-3.1-8B).
- `$\mathcal{B}^{\text{ERM}}_t = \{x_i, \tilde{y}^+_i, \tilde{y}^-_i\}_{i=1}^b$` contains preference data labeled by **randomly sampled individual ERM members**. Specifically, a random ensemble member `$\phi_t^k \sim \text{Uniform}(\Phi_t)$` is selected and used to score the candidate responses and determine the winner.
- `$\gamma \in [0, 1]$` controls the mixture ratio, initialized to `$\gamma = 1.0$` (start with only real data) and adjusted to `$\gamma = 0.7$` after a burn-in period of 1,000 samples.

**What the mixture achieves.** The policy is simultaneously trained to:
1. Maximize true human preferences (through the `$\gamma$`-weighted real data), driving exploitation toward high `$r^\star$`.
2. Generate responses that are high-quality under individual ERM members (through the `$(1-\gamma)$`-weighted synthetic data), driving the policy to cover the regions where the ERM ensemble members predict high reward.

**Why this addresses both Q1 and Q2 from Section 4.2.3.** The paper poses two questions: (Q1) "how to choose a sensible `$\pi_{\text{prior}}$` at each time `$t$`" and (Q2) "how to get a good generative policy online." The mixed preference learning answers both:

- **Q2 (getting a good policy):** Training on real oracle data (the `$\mathcal{B}_t$` component) optimizes the policy toward `$\pi^\star$`, which is the standard online DAP objective. This provides exploitation.

- **Q1 (making `$\pi_{\text{prior}}$` suitable for Thompson sampling):** The paper argues (Section 4.2.3): "optimizing `$\pi_{\theta_t}$` only with oracle data can average out the epistemic uncertainty of `$\mathcal{R}$`, hindering the exploration efficiency." In other words, if the policy only optimizes for the *average* reward across ensemble members, it will converge to a single mode that does not reflect the diversity of plausible reward functions, and the proposal set `$\mathcal{S}_t$` will not contain good responses under individual ensemble members — undermining the `$\arg\max$` approximation for Thompson sampling. Training on synthetic labels from individual ensemble members (the `$\mathcal{B}^{\text{ERM}}_t$` component) ensures that the policy's output distribution covers the regions where *different* ensemble members predict high reward, maintaining the diversity necessary for effective Thompson sampling.

**Why the `$\gamma$` schedule starts at 1.0 and drops to 0.7.** At the beginning of training (the first 1,000 samples), the ERM ensemble is poorly calibrated because it has seen little data. Using its synthetic labels would misdirect the policy. Starting with `$\gamma = 1.0$` ensures that early policy updates are guided by real human feedback. After the ERM has converged somewhat (the "burn-in period"), `$\gamma = 0.7$` introduces synthetic data to accelerate policy learning and maintain exploration diversity. The specific value `$0.7$` (30% synthetic, 70% real) represents a balance between leveraging the model's learned knowledge and staying grounded in real preferences.

**Connection to Dyna and model-based RL.** The paper explicitly draws this connection in Appendix B: "SEA depicted in Figure 3d resembles a class of online model-based RL algorithms, known as Dyna (Sutton, 1990; Janner et al., 2019), that learns a world model from environment experience and trains a base agent... from both environment experience and model experience." In standard Dyna, the agent learns a transition model and reward model of the environment, then uses those models to generate synthetic rollouts that augment real experience for policy training. In SEA, the "world model" is the ERM (there is no transition model since the prompt-response interaction is single-step), and the "model experience" is the synthetically labeled preference data `$\mathcal{B}^{\text{ERM}}_t$`. This model-based perspective explains why mixed preference learning improves sample efficiency: each real human query generates both a real training example (for the policy and ERM) and supports the ERM's ability to generate many synthetic training examples (for the policy only), amplifying the information content of each label.

**Why only the policy, not the ERM, trains on synthetic data.** The paper states that "synthetic experience is not added into `$\mathcal{D}_t$` to ensure reward learning always uses ground truth environment data." This prevents a feedback loop where ERM errors compound — if the ERM were trained on its own synthetic labels, initial estimation errors would be amplified through successive training rounds, leading to reward model degradation. The ERM only learns from human feedback; the policy learns from both human feedback and ERM predictions. This asymmetric arrangement ensures that the ERM remains a faithful (if imperfect) model of the true preference oracle, while the policy benefits from the ERM's ability to provide abundant training signal.

#### Synthesis: The Complete SEA Algorithm (Algorithm 2)

Algorithm 2 integrates the three approximation techniques into a practical online learning loop. Here is the complete procedure with all hyperparameters and design choices:

**Initialization (Line 1):**
- Experience buffer `$\mathcal{D}_0 = \emptyset$`
- Policy `$\pi_{\theta_0} = \pi_{\text{ref}} = \pi_{\text{sft}}$` (the supervised fine-tuned starting model)
- ERM weights `$\Phi_0 = \{\phi_0^k\}_{k=1}^K$` with `$K=20$`, each randomly initialized

**For each round `$t = 1, \ldots, T$`:**

**Line 3-4: Generate proposal set and select first response**
- Receive prompt `$x_t \sim p_\mathcal{X}$`
- Sample `$M = 20$` responses `$y_t^i \sim \pi_{\theta_{t-1}}(\cdot \mid x_t)$` with temperature `$\eta = 0.7$`
- Construct proposal set `$\mathcal{S}_t = \{y_t^i\}_{i=1}^M$`
- Sample an ERM member `$\phi \sim \text{Uniform}(\Phi_{t-1})$`
- Set `$y_t = \arg\max_{b \in \mathcal{S}_t} r_\phi(x_t, b)$` (first response via TS)

**Lines 5-7: Select second response (scenario-dependent)**
- **If E&E setting (Line 5-6):** Repeat the TS procedure (sample `$\phi$`, compute `$\arg\max$`) until `$y'_t \neq y_t$`
- **If BAI setting (Line 7):** Set `$y'_t = \arg\max_{b \in \mathcal{S}_t} \mathbb{V}_\phi [\sigma(r_\phi(x_t, y_t) - r_\phi(x_t, b))]$`

**Line 8: Label the duel**
- With probability `$\gamma$`: Query the real preference oracle `$\mathcal{P}$` (human or simulated reward model) to obtain `$(y^+_t, y^-_t)$`, create batch `$\mathcal{B}_t$`, and update `$\mathcal{D}_t = \mathcal{D}_{t-1} \cup \mathcal{B}_t$`
- With probability `$1-\gamma$`: Use a randomly sampled ERM member `$\phi \sim \text{Uniform}(\Phi_{t-1})$` to generate synthetic labels `$(\tilde{y}^+_t, \tilde{y}^-_t)$` and create batch `$\mathcal{B}_t$` (NOT added to `$\mathcal{D}_t$`)

**Line 9: Update ERM**
- Sample `$m = 5$` random batches from `$\mathcal{D}_t$`
- Update each ensemble member independently using the regularized NLL loss: `$\Phi_t = \Phi_{t-1} - \alpha_R \nabla_\Phi \mathcal{L}_R(\Phi_{t-1} \mid \mathcal{D}_t)$` (Equation 8 with `$\lambda = 0.5$`)

**Line 10: Update policy**
- Update the policy using the direct optimizer: `$\theta_t = \theta_{t-1} - \alpha_\pi \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_\pi(\theta_{t-1} \mid \mathcal{B}_t, \pi_{\text{ref}}, \mathcal{F})$` (Equation 9)
- The Adam optimizer is used with learning rate `$\alpha_\pi = 5 \times 10^{-7}$` and cosine scheduling

**Line 11: Repeat**

**Batch size.** The paper notes that "in implementation we set `$b$` to be the training batch size (e.g., 128)" — the online loop processes a batch of 128 prompts per round rather than a single prompt, which is more efficient for GPU utilization.

**Weight synchronization.** After each optimizer step, the updated policy weights are broadcast from the learner to all actor processes via NCCL for on-policy response generation in the next round. This is a critical systems detail: the next batch of proposals `$\mathcal{S}_{t+1}$` must be sampled from the latest policy `$\pi_{\theta_t}$`, not from a stale version.

**Why Algorithm 2 satisfies Properties 1 and 2 simultaneously.** The online nature of the loop (continuous interaction and immediate policy updates) satisfies Property 1 (online interaction). The Thompson sampling dueling pair selection, driven by the ERM's uncertainty estimates, satisfies Property 2 (active exploration). The mixed preference learning amplifies the benefits of both properties by generating additional training signal from the learned uncertainty model, making each human query more informative than it would be in a purely passive online system.

**Why the ERM head architecture is so small (2 layers of 128 nodes on a 0.4B backbone).** The authors explicitly note: "the ERM is chosen to be much smaller than the reward oracle following Dwaracherla et al. (2024), which reflects the fact that human preferences can be more complex than what the agent can model." This is a deliberate design choice: the ERM is an internal model that the agent uses for exploration, not a final product. It should be cheap to train and update (hence the small heads) while still capturing meaningful uncertainty (hence the ensemble). Making it smaller than the true oracle also prevents the agent from overfitting to its own reward model — if the ERM were as powerful as the oracle, the agent might learn to exploit ERM errors rather than learning genuine human preferences.

#### Summary of Design Choices and Their Justifications

- **Deep ensemble of MLP heads on a frozen transformer backbone** rather than a full Bayesian neural network: provides a computationally tractable approximation to posterior sampling while preserving ensemble diversity through random initialization and L2 regularization toward initial weights (`$\lambda = 0.5$`).

- **Thompson sampling over the ERM for dueling pair selection** rather than `$\epsilon$`-greedy or UCB: probability-matching provides principled exploration without manual tuning of exploration parameters, and the scenario-dependent second-response selection (E&E-TS vs. BAI-TS) adapts the exploration strategy to the deployment context.

- **Policy-guided search with `$M=20$` on-policy candidates** rather than continuous optimization or linear action embeddings: avoids the intractability of `$\arg\max$` over token sequences while leveraging the policy's learned bias toward high-reward regions. The temperature `$\eta = 0.7$` ensures proposal diversity.

- **Mixed preference learning with `$\gamma = 1.0 \to 0.7$` schedule** rather than pure oracle training or pure synthetic training: combines the benefits of exploitation (real data) and exploration diversity (synthetic data from individual ensemble members), with the burn-in period preventing policy misdirection from an initially poorly-calibrated ERM.

- **Any DAP optimizer** rather than a specific loss: makes SEA optimizer-agnostic, compatible with DPO (`$\beta = 0.1$`), IPO (`$\beta$` tuned per scale), and SLiC (`$\beta = 0.2$`), allowing practitioners to choose the loss function that best suits their needs.

- **ERM updated on `$m=5$` random replay batches from the full experience buffer** rather than online SGD on single new batches: prevents catastrophic forgetting and stabilizes reward model training across the non-stationary data distribution.

- **Synthetic labels not added to the experience buffer for ERM training:** prevents compounding errors in the reward model, ensuring the ERM remains grounded in real human preferences.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Reframing LLM Alignment as a Dueling Bandit Problem to Diagnose Sample Inefficiency

The paper's most fundamental intellectual contribution is not a new algorithm but a **diagnostic framework**. By formalizing LLM alignment as a contextual dueling bandit (CDB), the authors provide the field with a precise language for understanding *why* existing methods are sample-inefficient and *what properties* a solution must have.

Prior to this work, the LLM alignment literature operated with an implicit, undertheorized taxonomy. Methods were categorized by surface-level design choices — "RLHF vs. DPO," "offline vs. online," "reward-model-based vs. direct" — without a unified framework that could explain the performance differences between them. This lack of formalism made it difficult to identify what was actually being gained or lost when switching between paradigms. Was online DPO better than offline DPO because of distributional shift, because of exploration, or because of overfitting? The field lacked the vocabulary to answer this question precisely.

The CDB formulation resolves this ambiguity by decomposing the alignment problem into two orthogonal axes: **online interaction** (Property 1 — does the agent learn from its own actions?) and **active exploration** (Property 2 — does the agent strategically choose which pairs to query?). Through this lens, the paper's survey of prior work in Section 3 becomes a systematic diagnostic exercise rather than a conventional literature review. Offline methods (Stiennon et al., 2020; Rafailov et al., 2023) fail Property 1. Iteratively online methods (Xu et al., 2023; Guo et al., 2024) satisfy Property 1 but fail Property 2 — they collect data from the current policy but do not strategically select which comparisons to make. Active reward learning methods (Dwaracherla et al., 2024; Mehta et al., 2023) satisfy Property 2 for learning the reward model but fail Property 1 for the policy, since the generative model is frozen. The methods that come closest to satisfying both (XPO, APL, SELM) do so only through tight coupling with a specific loss function (DPO) and with limited online verification.

This framework resolves a puzzle that had been accumulating in the literature: if iterative DPO improves over offline DPO, and active exploration improves reward model learning, why weren't methods combining both dominating the field? The CDB diagnosis provides the answer — prior attempts at combination either froze the policy (Dwaracherla et al., 2024), constrained themselves to a single optimizer (XPO, APL), or never ran at the online scale where the theoretical benefits should manifest (limited to "a few online iterations"). The CDB framework makes explicit that **all three ingredients — online policy updates, active exploration, and optimizer agnosticism — must coexist** for fully sample-efficient alignment.

This is a **fundamental contribution** rather than an incremental refinement because it changes what the field optimizes for. Before this framework, a new alignment method was evaluated on final win rate at a fixed data budget — a scalar metric that conflates policy quality, exploration efficiency, and optimizer choice. After this framework, the field can ask more precise questions: Does this method improve sample efficiency through better exploration (Property 2) or through better policy optimization given the same exploration (Property 1)? Is a performance gain attributable to the optimizer, the exploration strategy, or the interaction mode? The paper's Figure 3 — which visually maps existing paradigms onto the CDB axes — is likely to become a standard reference for contextualizing new alignment methods.

A subtle but critical aspect of this reframing is the **scenario distinction** between E&E and BAI embedded within the CDB formulation (Section 2.2). Prior work implicitly assumed a single objective — learn the best policy — without distinguishing between the setting where the learning process itself affects users (ChatGPT asking for side-by-side comparisons) and the setting where it doesn't (paid annotators). The CDB framework forces this distinction because the two settings have different regret definitions: cumulative regret for E&E, anytime regret for BAI. The paper validates that this distinction matters empirically (Section 6.3, Figure 7): E&E-TS outperforms BAI-TS on online performance metrics, while BAI-TS achieves faster offline improvement. A method optimized for the wrong setting — such as Dwaracherla et al. (2024)'s DTS (designed for E&E) evaluated on BAI metrics — will appear suboptimal not because the algorithm is flawed, but because the objective is mismatched. This insight alone could redirect how future alignment algorithms are designed and evaluated relative to their deployment context.

---

### Innovation 2: Thompson Sampling as a Unifying Exploration Principle for LLM Alignment

The choice of Thompson sampling (TS) as the exploration backbone is intellectually distinctive not because TS is novel in bandit theory — it dates to 1933 — but because the paper demonstrates that it **unifies and resolves the tensions between existing active exploration approaches** for LLMs when properly instantiated.

Prior active exploration methods for LLM alignment fell into two camps that made incompatible design choices. The first camp (Mehta et al., 2023; Das et al., 2024; Dwaracherla et al., 2024) treated exploration as a reward-model-learning problem, maintaining uncertainty estimates over preferences and selecting dueling pairs to reduce that uncertainty. But because they used a fixed policy for response generation, they decoupled exploration from policy improvement — the agent could learn a perfect reward model without ever being able to generate responses in the high-reward region it discovered. This is a **data coverage trap**: the policy is constrained to the support of the behavior model, and no amount of intelligent querying can escape it.

The second camp (Zhang et al., 2024a; Xie et al., 2024; Muldrew et al., 2024) embedded exploration directly into the policy loss via optimistic bonuses in the DPO objective. This approach does update the policy, but it **conflates exploration and exploitation into a single loss function** — the policy must simultaneously try to generate high-quality responses (the standard DPO objective) and reduce uncertainty (the optimistic bonus). These objectives can conflict: a response that is highly uncertain under the current model may also be low-quality, and the loss function must resolve this tension through a single tradeoff coefficient. Moreover, these methods are **tightly coupled to DPO**, inheriting its implicit reward parameterization and making them incompatible with other direct optimizers.

Thompson sampling cuts through this dilemma by **maintaining an explicit separation between the reward model (which captures uncertainty) and the policy (which exploits)**. The reward posterior `$p(r \mid \mathcal{D})$` — approximated by the ERM ensemble — is the sole locus of exploration. The policy, by contrast, is always trained to maximize reward; it never receives an explicit exploration signal. Exploration emerges *automatically* from the fact that the policy is being optimized toward a moving target — as the ERM posterior updates, the synthetic labels `$\mathcal{B}^{\text{ERM}}_t$` shift the policy's training distribution toward regions where different ensemble members predict high reward, naturally maintaining output diversity without an explicit exploration bonus. This is a clean conceptual separation: the ERM explores, the policy exploits. Neither component needs to compromise its objective.

The scenario-adaptive TS variant (E&E-TS vs. BAI-TS in Algorithm 1) further demonstrates the flexibility of this framework. Rather than designing entirely separate algorithms for online serving and crowdsourcing, the paper shows that **a single mechanism — posterior sampling over reward functions — can be reconfigured to serve either objective** by changing only the second-response selection criterion. E&E-TS selects both responses to be greedily optimal under sampled reward functions (maximizing online quality), while BAI-TS selects the second response to maximize preference variance (maximizing information gain). This is a conceptual unification: exploration for safety (E&E) and exploration for efficiency (BAI) are two applications of the same underlying principle, differentiated only by whether the second action optimizes reward or information.

This is a **fundamental contribution** because it establishes Thompson sampling — and more broadly, posterior sampling — as the *natural* exploration framework for preference-based LLM alignment, analogous to how UCB and TS became standard in multi-armed bandits after decades of empirical validation. The paper's empirical demonstration that TS-based methods (E&E-TS, BAI-TS) outperform both pure uncertainty-based exploration and passive online baselines (Figure 7) provides the first systematic evidence that posterior sampling is effective at LLM scale, even with approximate posteriors from deep ensembles, validating a theoretical principle that had not been tested in this domain.

### Innovation 3: Mixed Preference Learning as Dyna-Style Model-Based RL for LLMs

The introduction of **mixed preference learning** — training the policy on a blend `$\gamma = 0.7$` real feedback and `$1-\gamma = 0.3$` synthetic labels from the ERM ensemble — is a conceptual bridge between the active exploration literature for LLMs and the model-based RL literature (specifically Dyna; Sutton, 1990). This connection had not been made explicit in prior LLM alignment work, despite its potential to explain and improve sample efficiency.

In standard online alignment, each human query produces exactly one training example for the policy — the preference triplet `$(x, y^+, y^-)$`. The policy's rate of improvement is therefore limited by the rate of human feedback. Prior methods attempted to accelerate this in two ways: (1) make each query more informative by choosing dueling pairs strategically (active exploration — Property 2), or (2) improve the policy optimization algorithm to extract more signal from the same data (better optimizers — the online vs. offline DAP distinction). Mixed preference learning introduces a third mechanism: **use the learned reward model to generate additional training signal without additional human queries**.

From the Dyna perspective, the ERM serves as a learned world model of human preferences. After each interaction with the real environment (a human query), the agent can generate multiple "dreamed" interactions — synthetic preference labels — by querying the world model. These synthetic interactions augment the real data for policy training, effectively multiplying the information gained per human label. This is precisely the model-based RL argument for sample efficiency: if the learned model is accurate, planning with it can compensate for limited real-world interaction.

What makes this innovative in the LLM alignment context is the **asymmetric training design**: the ERM is never trained on synthetic labels, only on real human feedback, while the policy trains on both. This prevents the well-known failure mode of model-based RL — model exploitation, where the policy learns to exploit inaccuracies in the world model rather than optimizing the true objective (Janner et al., 2019). By keeping the ERM grounded in human data, the synthetic labels `$\mathcal{B}^{\text{ERM}}_t$` remain (approximately) faithful to the true preference oracle, and the policy's exploitation of the ERM is bounded by the ERM's fidelity.

The mixture also serves a second, subtler purpose that the paper identifies explicitly: **maintaining policy diversity for Thompson sampling**. If the policy were trained only on real oracle data, it would converge toward a single mode that maximizes expected reward under the (unknown) true `$r^\star$`. This would cause the proposal set `$\mathcal{S}_t$` — which is sampled from the policy — to collapse to a narrow region of response space, reducing the diversity of candidates available for the Thompson sampling dueling pair selector. By training on synthetic labels from individual ERM members (each of which has a slightly different reward function due to different random initializations and the L2 regularization), the policy is encouraged to spread probability mass across the regions where different ensemble members predict high reward. This maintains the proposal diversity that Thompson sampling needs to function effectively. Without this component, Thompson sampling would degenerate into passive sampling — the policy would produce similar responses regardless of which ERM member is sampled.

The ablation in Figure 6 (comparing Variant-3 to Variant-2, and Variant-6 to Variant-5) provides direct evidence for the contribution of mixed preference learning independent of other components. Variant-2 (active exploration without ERM synthetic labels for the policy) already shows substantial improvement over Variant-1 (passive online), confirming that Thompson sampling alone improves sample efficiency. But Variant-3 adds the ERM sync (mixed preference learning) and yields a further improvement, demonstrating that aligning the policy with the ERM's uncertainty distribution amplifies the benefits of active exploration. Similarly, in the Best-of-N inference setting (right panel), Variant-6 (with ERM sync) outperforms Variant-5 (without), showing that the effect persists even when the policy is used differently at evaluation time.

This is a **fundamental contribution** because it reframes LLM alignment as a **model-based RL problem** and provides a concrete, working instantiation of Dyna for this domain. The Dyna connection has implications beyond the current paper: it suggests that other model-based RL techniques — uncertainty-aware planning, conservative model exploitation (Kidambi et al., 2020), or model-based policy gradients — could be productively adapted to LLM alignment, opening a research direction that the prior focus on model-free DAP methods had obscured.

### Innovation 4: The First Fully Online Experimental Validation of Active Exploration for LLMs

While this innovation is partly infrastructural, its intellectual significance lies in **revealing a systematic gap between theory and practice** in the LLM alignment literature and demonstrating that closing this gap changes empirical conclusions.

The paper's observation in Section 5.1 is pointed: prior active exploration methods for LLMs (Zhang et al., 2024a; Xie et al., 2024; Muldrew et al., 2024) were evaluated under conditions that did not match their theoretical assumptions. Specifically, these methods propose exploration strategies whose benefits — regret bounds, information gain rates, posterior convergence — are derived assuming **many rounds of online interaction** with small batches of data per round. Yet their experiments were conducted in an **iterative batch** regime: a handful of large-batch iterations (often 2–4 rounds) where a model is trained, used to generate new data, and retrained. This is not the setting the theory was built for, and it is not obvious that the theoretical benefits of, say, an optimistic exploration bonus in the DPO loss should manifest under such limited interaction.

The paper attributes this gap to infrastructure: "The absence of a performant open-source online alignment system has restricted many existing works to only a few iterations of batch learning." The `oat` system (Figure 4, Appendix C) is designed to eliminate this barrier, achieving up to 2.5× latency reduction over existing implementations by co-locating vLLM-based inference, Mosec-based reward model serving, and DeepSpeed-based training within a unified distributed architecture.

Why does this matter beyond engineering convenience? Because the paper's empirical comparisons reveal that **the relative ranking of methods changes under fully online evaluation**. In Figure 7 (Right), when evaluated with GPT-4o-mini as a noisy human simulator in a fully online setting, APL — which appeared strong in the iterative batch setting (Figure 5, 1B scale) — learns fast initially but is eventually overtaken by the passive Online baseline. XPO, which showed minimal improvement over Online in the batch setting, delivers a better final performance after stabilizing its training online. SEA, which was designed from the start for the online regime, shows the best sample efficiency and asymptotic performance. These interactions between method and evaluation protocol would be invisible under the few-iteration batch evaluation that characterized prior work.

This is more than a "better benchmarking" contribution. It constitutes an **empirical refutation of a de facto evaluation standard** in the field. The paper's results imply that published sample efficiency gains from active exploration methods may not generalize to the online setting that these methods theoretically address. By providing the infrastructure to test methods in their intended regime, the paper enables a methodological reset — future work cannot claim online benefits without online verification.

Furthermore, the open-source release of `oat` transforms sample-efficient alignment from a theoretical ideal into an **empirically accessible research program**. The codebase handles the heterogeneous computational demands of online alignment (fast autoregressive generation, high-throughput preference labeling, memory-efficient distributed training, and rapid weight synchronization) that previously required custom distributed systems engineering. By lowering this barrier, the paper enables systematic comparison of exploration strategies, optimizers, and model scales under uniform, reproducible conditions — a prerequisite for cumulative scientific progress that the field of LLM alignment has, until now, largely lacked.

This is a **fundamental contribution** in the sense that it changes what counts as valid evidence in the subfield. If the community adopts fully online evaluation as the standard (as the paper argues it should), methods that report gains from batch-iterative experiments will need to be re-evaluated, and the design of future algorithms will be constrained by the requirements of genuine online operation — continuous policy updates, stable reward model learning from non-stationary data, and exploration mechanisms that function with streaming rather than batched data collection.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** All experiments use the TL;DR summarization dataset (Stiennon et al., 2020), which consists of Reddit posts as prompts where the agent must generate summaries aligned with human preferences. The training set is fixed at 50,000 prompts, and the query budget is also limited to 50,000 preference labels (Appendix D).

- **Base model(s).** Experiments use supervised fine-tuned (SFT) models from the Pythia family (Biderman et al., 2023) at three scales: 1B, 2.8B, and 6.9B parameters. These SFT starting points are taken directly from Huang et al. (2024), who tuned them on TL;DR. The 1B model is used for most ablation and analysis experiments to save computation, while the full scale sweep appears in the overall comparison (Figure 5).

- **Metrics.** The primary metric is **win rate** of the agent's responses against reference responses, judged by the preference oracle. In the BAI setting (the main experimental focus), "offline" win rate is measured by periodically evaluating the latest agent policy on a fixed set of holdout prompts using greedy decoding. In the E&E setting, "online" win rate is measured as the average quality of dueling responses executed during experience collection. When comparing within a single model scale, relative win rate against the initial SFT model is reported (Figures 5–7). When comparing across scales (Figure 1, Left), absolute win rate against ground truth reference responses in the dataset is reported.

- **Baselines.** The paper compares against several categories of methods:
  - **Offline DAP variants:** Offline DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023), offline IPO (Azar et al., 2024), and offline SLiC (Zhao et al., 2023) — each trained once on a fixed preference dataset without online interaction.
  - **Passively online DAP (Online):** The online DAP baseline from Guo et al. (2024), which samples dueling responses from the current policy `$\pi_\theta$` without active selection (satisfies Property 1 but not Property 2).
  - **Active exploration with fixed policies (Variant-7):** The method of Dwaracherla et al. (2024), which learns an ERM for exploration and Best-of-N inference but does not update the generative policy — responses are sampled from a fixed `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$`.
  - **Active exploration with DPO (APL):** Muldrew et al. (2024), which uses the implicit DPO reward margin to actively select dueling pairs.
  - **Optimistic DPO (XPO):** Xie et al. (2024), which adds an optimistic exploration bonus to the DPO loss. (Note: SELM by Zhang et al. (2024a) is omitted because of its "very similar algorithmic design" to XPO — Appendix D.)

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The unit of comparison is the **number of query steps** (preference labels requested from the oracle), up to a maximum budget of 50,000 queries. All methods are compared at identical query budgets — there is no separate accounting for the computational cost of maintaining the ERM ensemble or generating proposal sets, since the primary bottleneck being studied is human feedback volume, not GPU FLOPs. For SEA, each query step involves sampling `$M=20$` on-policy candidate responses (at temperature `$\eta = 0.7$`) and selecting a dueling pair, but the M=20 generation cost is shared across all methods that use on-policy sampling (including passively online baselines). The mixed preference learning component introduces additional policy updates on synthetic data, which do not consume oracle queries but do consume compute; this compute is not factored into the efficiency comparison, making the sample-efficiency metric purely about human label efficiency.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** Every experiment is launched with 3 independent runs using different random seeds. All results are reported with mean and standard error to indicate statistical significance (Appendix D). There is no train/validation/test split described for the TL;DR data — the 50,000 training prompts are used for online interaction, and a separate set of holdout prompts (size unspecified) is used for periodic offline evaluation.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Overall Comparison Across Scales and Optimizers (Figure 5)

The central result appears in Figure 5, which compares SEA against Offline and passively Online baselines across three model scales (1B, 2.8B, 6.9B) and three direct optimizers (DPO, IPO, SLiC). APL and XPO are compared only when DPO is the optimizer.

**Headline finding: Online agents consistently outperform their Offline counterparts.** Across all scales and optimizers, the Online (passive) win rate curves lie substantially above the Offline curves at nearly every query budget. At the 1B scale with DPO, Online reaches approximately 0.85 win rate at 50,000 queries compared to approximately 0.70 for Offline DPO. This validates Property 1 (online interaction) as necessary for sample efficiency.

**Headline finding: SEA outperforms all baselines across all configurations.** At the 1B scale with DPO, SEA achieves approximately 0.90 win rate at convergence — a gain of roughly +5 percentage points over passively Online DPO and roughly +20 points over Offline DPO. At the 2.8B scale, the gap between SEA and Online widens: SEA reaches approximately 0.92 win rate while Online plateaus near 0.78-0.82. At the 6.9B scale, SEA maintains a clear advantage, reaching approximately 0.95 win rate versus Online at roughly 0.88.

These gains are quantified further in Figure 1 (Right), which shows the number of queries required by different methods to reach various win rate thresholds. The passive Online method requires approximately 50,000 queries to reach a win rate that SEA achieves in roughly 10,000-20,000 queries (depending on the threshold), representing a **2–5× improvement in sample efficiency**. The paper states in the abstract that SEA achieves "up to 2-5× better sample efficiency" — the exact multiplier depends on the win rate target and model scale.

**Headline finding: Prior active exploration baselines show inconsistent scaling.** APL demonstrates significant improvement over Online at the 1B scale (reaching roughly 0.88 win rate) but this advantage "diminishes when scaling up and it performs almost the same as Online at 6.9B scale" (Section 6.1, Figure 5, bottom row). XPO gives a small improvement in final performance over Online at 1B (roughly +2 percentage points) but "falls short for larger scales." In contrast, SEA's advantage over Online is preserved or even widens as scale increases — at 2.8B with DPO, SEA reaches approximately 0.92 versus Online at approximately 0.78, a gap of ~14 points.

**Headline finding: The choice of optimizer matters for online learning and active exploration.** At the 1B scale (Figure 5, first column), all three Offline agents (DPO, IPO, SLiC) demonstrate comparable learning efficiency and reach similar final performance around 0.70 win rate. However, when moving to online interaction, SLiC Online delivers "slightly less improvement than DPO and IPO Online agents." Furthermore, when incorporating active exploration (SEA), the agent using DPO shows "much larger improvement than the other two" — SEA-DPO reaches approximately 0.90+, while SEA-IPO and SEA-SLiC reach roughly 0.84-0.86. This suggests a **synergistic interaction** between the optimizer and the exploration mechanism: DPO's implicit reward parameterization may align particularly well with the ERM-based Thompson sampling, though the paper does not mechanistically explain this interaction.

#### Ablation Analysis: Decomposing SEA's Components (Figure 6, Table 1)

The paper decomposes SEA into three axes (Table 1): inference method (policy vs. Best-of-N sampling), exploration strategy (passive vs. active), and learning components (learn policy only vs. learn policy + ERM vs. learn policy + ERM with synchronization). Seven variants are constructed to isolate the contribution of each component. All are evaluated at 1B scale with DPO.

**Variant-1 (Online DAP baseline):** Uses `$\pi_\theta$` for both inference and passive exploration (two responses sampled from the policy). This is the Guo et al. (2024) baseline.

**Variant-2 (SEA without ERM sync):** Active exploration using `$(\pi_\theta, \mathcal{R}_\Phi)$` — Thompson sampling selects dueling pairs, and the ERM is learned, but the policy is trained only on real oracle data without synthetic labels from the ERM (Section 4.2.3's mixed preference learning disabled). Compared to Variant-1, this isolates the contribution of active exploration alone.

**Variant-3 (Full SEA with policy inference):** Active exploration with `$(\pi_\theta \leftrightarrow \mathcal{R}_\Phi)$` — the ERM is both learned and used to generate synthetic preference labels for policy training. Compared to Variant-2, this isolates the contribution of mixed preference learning.

**Variant-4 (Best-of-N with passive exploration):** Uses Best-of-N sampling with the ERM for inference (selecting the best response from `$N$` candidates via ERM scoring), but passive exploration (random dueling pairs from the policy). Policy and ERM are both learned.

**Variant-5 (Best-of-N with active exploration, no sync):** Like Variant-4 but with active Thompson sampling exploration. ERM sync disabled.

**Variant-6 (Full SEA with Best-of-N inference):** Best-of-N inference with active exploration and ERM synchronization. This is the complete SEA agent when inference-time compute (Best-of-N) is employed.

**Variant-7 (Dwaracherla et al., 2024 baseline):** Best-of-N inference with active exploration and ERM learning, but the policy is NOT updated — all responses are sampled from a fixed proposal `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$`. Only the ERM is trained online.

**Results (Figure 6, Left — policy inference):** When using the policy directly for inference (no Best-of-N), Variant-2 (active exploration) clearly outperforms Variant-1 (passive): at 50,000 queries, Variant-2 reaches approximately 0.86 win rate versus Variant-1 at roughly 0.83. Variant-3 (full SEA, with ERM sync) further improves to approximately 0.89 win rate. The progression Variant-1 → Variant-2 → Variant-3 shows monotonic improvement, confirming that both active exploration and mixed preference learning contribute independently to the final performance.

**Results (Figure 6, Right — Best-of-N inference):** With Best-of-N sampling at inference time, the same pattern holds: Variant-5 (active exploration) outperforms Variant-4 (passive), and Variant-6 (full SEA with sync) outperforms Variant-5. Variant-6 reaches approximately 0.91-0.92 win rate at 50,000 queries, the highest of any variant. Critically, Variant-7 (Dwaracherla et al., 2024 — active exploration but fixed policy) "ceases to improve after ERM converges due to the limited performance of its fixed policy" (Section 6.2). At roughly 10,000-15,000 queries, Variant-7 plateaus near 0.72 win rate, while Variant-6 continues to improve, nearly 20 points higher by 50,000 queries. This empirically validates the paper's critique that active exploration without policy updates hits a data coverage ceiling.

#### Choice of Exploration Strategies: E&E vs. BAI vs. Uncertainty (Figure 7, Left and Middle)

The paper investigates three exploration strategies at 1B scale with DPO:

- **Uncertainty (pure exploration):** Selects the dueling pair that exhibits the largest epistemic uncertainty — the pair whose preference logit difference has the highest variance across ERM ensemble members. This is a pure information-maximization strategy (similar to Das et al., 2024).

- **E&E-TS (Algorithm 1, Line 5):** Both responses are selected to maximize sampled reward functions, with resampling until they differ. Prioritizes reward maximization.

- **BAI-TS (Algorithm 1, Lines 4 + 6):** First response via standard TS, second response maximizes preference variance over the first.

**Online performance (E&E setting, Figure 7 Left):** E&E-TS achieves the best online performance, reaching approximately 0.88 win rate at 50,000 queries. BAI-TS reaches approximately 0.84, and Uncertainty shows the worst online performance at roughly 0.78. This is expected: E&E-TS deliberately selects both responses to be high-quality (greedy under sampled rewards), maximizing the average quality of responses shown during data collection. Uncertainty, which may select low-quality responses if they are informative, naturally produces worse online experience.

**Offline performance (BAI setting, Figure 7 Middle):** The ranking reverses. BAI-TS achieves the best offline improvement, reaching approximately 0.90 win rate at 50,000 queries. Uncertainty reaches roughly 0.88, and E&E-TS reaches roughly 0.86 at 50,000 queries. The paper explains: "BAI-TS ≻ Uncertainty indicates exploration with both reward and information maximization is better than exploration with only information maximization" (Section 6.3). That is, BAI-TS's first response via standard TS ensures that exploration is directed toward high-reward regions (increasing relevance), while the second response's uncertainty maximization ensures informativeness. Uncertainty alone explores arbitrary directions that may be informative about `$r^\star$` in regions irrelevant to policy improvement.

The paper further notes that E&E-TS, by always choosing two responses with similarly high quality, can lead to "less efficient exploration" and "less efficient policy learning due to smaller DAP loss gradients" (Section 6.3) — when both responses are similarly good, the preference margin is small, and the contrastive DAP loss provides a weaker training signal. This is a subtle but important mechanism: exploration that generates larger quality gaps between dueling responses produces stronger gradients for policy improvement.

#### Alignment with a Noisy Human Simulator (GPT-4o-mini) (Figure 7, Right)

To test robustness beyond the deterministic scalar reward oracle (Skywork-Reward-Llama-3.1-8B), the paper uses `gpt-4o-mini-2024-07-18` as a stochastic judge via the OpenAI API, following the LLM-as-a-judge prompt template from Li et al. (2023). This introduces realistic noise and variability into the preference feedback.

**Headline finding: SEA remains the most sample-efficient and achieves the best asymptotic performance.** The results (Figure 7, Right) show higher variance across all methods (wider shaded regions) compared to the oracle RM setting, consistent with the noisier feedback.

The relative ranking of prior baselines changes: APL "learns fast initially but is eventually outperformed by Online," while XPO "improves over Online after stabilizing its training and delivers a better final performance" (Section 6.4). This is notable because it reverses the pattern observed with the oracle RM at 2.8B and 6.9B scales (Figure 5), where APL held an advantage over XPO. The paper attributes this to "randomness introduced in the feedback process, which puts more stringent requirements for learning algorithms" (Section 6.4), suggesting that XPO's optimistic exploration bonus may be more robust to stochastic feedback than APL's margin-based selection.

SEA maintains the best sample efficiency and asymptotic performance, reaching approximately 0.88 win rate at 50,000 queries, versus roughly 0.83-0.84 for the best alternative (XPO). The gap between SEA and the Online baseline is roughly +8 percentage points at convergence, smaller than in the deterministic oracle setting but still substantial.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Inference method (policy vs. Best-of-N):** Both inference methods benefit from active exploration and ERM synchronization (Figure 6). Best-of-N provides a consistent ~3-4 point boost over direct policy inference at equivalent win rates (compare Variant-3 to Variant-6), but the relative gains from exploration components are preserved. This suggests that the exploration mechanism complements, rather than substitutes for, inference-time compute.

**ERM synchronization (mixed preference learning):** Ablated by comparing Variant-2 to Variant-3 (policy inference) and Variant-5 to Variant-6 (Best-of-N inference) in Figure 6. The ERM sync provides roughly a +3 percentage point improvement at convergence in both settings. The `$\gamma$` schedule (1.0 → 0.7 after 1,000 samples) is critical — starting with `$\gamma=1.0$` prevents the policy from being misled by a poorly calibrated ERM early in training. No ablation over the `$\gamma$` value or burn-in length is reported.

**Fixed policy vs. learnable policy (Variant-7 vs. Variant-4/5/6 in Figure 6, Right):** The performance gap between Variant-7 (Dwaracherla et al., 2024 — fixed `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$`, active exploration) and Variant-6 (SEA — learnable `$\pi_\theta$`, active exploration) demonstrates that policy learning is essential even with active exploration. Variant-7 plateaus around 0.72 after ~15,000 queries, while Variant-6 continues improving to ~0.92. This 20-point gap quantifies the data coverage bottleneck that the paper identifies as the key limitation of prior active exploration works.

**Choice of direct optimizer:** Ablated implicitly through the overall comparison (Figure 5, three rows). SEA's gains over Online are largest with DPO (~+10 points at 1B), smaller but still significant with IPO (~+5 points) and SLiC (~+4 points). This interaction between optimizer and exploration method is not explained mechanistically but is consistent with DPO's implicit reward parameterization aligning with the ERM's explicit reward modeling.

**Exploration strategy for different objectives:** Ablated in Figure 7 (Left, Middle). The same algorithm (TS) with different second-response selection criteria produces markedly different online vs. offline performance curves, validating the paper's claim that E&E and BAI require different exploration strategies. No method performs best on both metrics simultaneously — E&E-TS wins online, BAI-TS wins offline — confirming the fundamental tradeoff.

**Oracle type (deterministic scalar RM vs. stochastic LLM judge):** Ablated by comparing Figure 5 (Skywork-Reward oracle) to Figure 7 Right (GPT-4o-mini judge). Performance is lower and variance is higher with the stochastic judge, as expected. The relative ranking of prior baselines changes (APL falls behind Online, XPO catches up), suggesting that active exploration methods optimized on deterministic oracles may not transfer robustly to stochastic human feedback. SEA's relative advantage narrows but remains positive.

**Model scale (1B → 2.8B → 6.9B):** Ablated across the three columns of Figure 5, top row (DPO). SEA's advantage over Online widens or is preserved at larger scales, while APL's advantage vanishes. This suggests that SEA's sample efficiency gains are not an artifact of small-scale experimentation.

### Critical Assessment

**Do the experiments demonstrate that SEA achieves sample-efficient alignment?** Yes, but with important qualifications. The results consistently show that SEA requires fewer oracle queries than baselines to reach a given win rate — the "2–5×" improvement claimed in the abstract is supported by Figure 1 (Right) and Figure 5, though the exact multiplier varies substantially across scales, optimizers, and target win rates. At 1B with DPO, the improvement is roughly 2–3× (SEA reaches 0.85 at ~15K queries vs. Online DPO at ~40K). At 2.8B, the improvement is larger, plausibly 3–5× at certain thresholds. The claim should be understood as a range, not a fixed ratio, and is most compelling at moderate win rate targets (0.80-0.90) rather than at convergence where all methods approach similar asymptotes.

**Do the experiments demonstrate that Thompson sampling specifically, rather than exploration in general, drives the improvement?** The evidence is suggestive but not definitive. The comparison in Figure 7 (Middle) shows that BAI-TS outperforms pure Uncertainty-based exploration, supporting the claim that reward-directed exploration (Thompson sampling's probability-matching property) is more effective than information-maximization alone. However, there is no comparison against other exploration heuristics such as UCB, Boltzmann exploration, or `$\epsilon$`-greedy. The paper's theoretical motivation for Thompson sampling is sound, but the empirical demonstration that TS outperforms Uncertainty does not constitute evidence that TS is optimal — only that it is better than a pure exploration baseline. The absence of comparisons against other exploration strategies is a genuine gap.

**Do the experiments demonstrate that the CDB formulation is necessary or merely descriptive?** The CDB formalism serves as an analytical lens and motivation, but the experiments do not directly test the CDB formulation — they test SEA, which is one algorithm inspired by it. The paper does not, for example, show that algorithms optimized for the CDB regret bound outperform algorithms that ignore the CDB structure but also use ensembles and active selection. The CDB framing is best understood as a design principle that guides algorithm construction, not as a hypothesis tested by experiments.

**Do the experiments demonstrate that mixed preference learning (Dyna-style model-based RL) is necessary?** The ablation in Figure 6 shows it helps (~+3 points), but the benefit is relatively modest compared to the gain from active exploration alone (Variant-2 vs. Variant-1, ~+3 points for active exploration; Variant-3 vs. Variant-2, ~+3 points for ERM sync). The paper's theoretical argument — that ERM sync maintains policy diversity for Thompson sampling — is plausible but not directly verified; there is no measurement of policy diversity or ensemble exploitation in the paper. An experiment tracking proposal set entropy or the variance of selected responses across ensemble members would strengthen this claim.

**Genuine weaknesses:**

1. **No comparison against iterative RLHF with PPO.** The paper positions active exploration as a sample-efficiency solution for alignment broadly, but all experiments compare against DAP methods (DPO, IPO, SLiC). A comparison against online RLHF with PPO — the dominant paradigm in production systems (Ouyang et al., 2022) — is absent. It is possible that PPO with a learned reward model achieves comparable sample efficiency without explicit ensemble-based exploration, since PPO's stochastic policy and value function bootstrapping provide a different form of exploration. Without this baseline, the paper's claim to be a general-purpose sample-efficiency solution for "LLM alignment" (rather than specifically for DAP-based alignment) is unvalidated.

2. **Single benchmark (TL;DR).** All results are on a single summarization dataset. Summarization has specific properties — responses are relatively short, the quality criterion (accuracy, conciseness) is fairly objective — that may not generalize to open-ended dialogue, instruction following, or tasks where preferences are more subjective. The paper does not discuss dataset-specific limitations.

3. **Simulated oracle, not real human feedback.** The preference oracle is a strong reward model (Skywork-Reward-Llama-3.1-8B) or GPT-4o-mini. Both are approximations of human preferences with unknown fidelity. Active exploration strategies that work well against a deterministic, noise-free oracle may exploit regularities in the oracle that do not exist in real human judgments. The GPT-4o-mini experiment partially addresses this by introducing stochasticity, but LLM judges have their own systematic biases that may differ from human biases.

4. **Computational cost of ERM not factored into efficiency.** The paper measures efficiency purely in oracle queries, but the ERM ensemble (20 MLP heads on a frozen 0.4B backbone, trained with `$m=5$` replay batches per round) and the `$M=20$` on-policy samples per prompt consume significant GPU compute. For practitioners whose bottleneck is GPU hours rather than annotation budget, the "sample efficiency" gains may be offset by increased computational cost. The paper does not report wall-clock time or GPU-hours for any experiment, making it impossible to assess the compute-query tradeoff.

5. **No ablation over ensemble size K=20.** The choice of `$K=20$` ensemble members is stated without justification. Larger ensembles capture more uncertainty but cost more to train; smaller ensembles are cheaper but may provide poor posterior approximations. No experiment varies `$K$`. If `$K=5$` worked nearly as well, the computational cost argument would be significantly weaker. The L2 regularization coefficient `$\lambda$` was set to 0.5 "after a coarse hyperparameter search" (Appendix D) — no sweep results are shown.

6. **No results on the data coverage trap claim without SEA.** The paper argues that methods with fixed policies (Variant-7, Dwaracherla et al., 2024) hit a data coverage ceiling. This is shown in Figure 6 (Right) for the specific fixed policy `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$`. However, the claim that active exploration *without policy updates* fundamentally cannot escape the behavior policy's support is a structural argument, not an empirical one — it should hold for any fixed policy. The paper only tests one fixed policy (the SFT model). Showing that even a moderately improved fixed policy (e.g., the converged Offline DPO model) still plateaus would strengthen the argument.

7. **Missing experiment: SEA without Thompson sampling, with only the ERM for Best-of-N inference.** This would isolate whether the sample efficiency gain during training (from Thompson sampling dueling pair selection) is separable from the asymptotic performance gain (from ERM-based Best-of-N inference). The current design conflates these — SEA uses the ERM both for exploration during training and for Best-of-N selection at evaluation (in some variants). A condition where training uses passive exploration but evaluation uses Best-of-N with the ERM would clarify the contribution of each phase.

8. **Small number of seeds (3 runs).** While standard for LLM fine-tuning experiments, 3 seeds may not capture the full variance of online learning with stochastic exploration. The standard error bands in Figures 5–7 are visible but not wide, suggesting reasonable stability, but outlier runs (especially with noisy oracles) could change the relative ranking of closely-matched methods.

9. **Limited scale range.** The largest model tested is 6.9B parameters (Pythia 6.9B). Modern production LLMs are 70B–405B+. It is unknown whether the ERM ensemble (0.4B backbone) can adequately model the preference structure of much larger policies, or whether the exploration dynamics change at scale. The paper's finding that APL's advantage vanishes at 6.9B while SEA's is preserved is suggestive but does not guarantee extrapolation to 70B+.

10. **The BAI setting experiments use a mixture of real and synthetic data for the policy (γ=0.7), but the synthetic data does not count toward the query budget.** This means that at 50,000 oracle queries, the policy has effectively seen ~71,000 training examples (50,000 real + ~21,000 synthetic at 30%). The passive Online baseline sees exactly 50,000. This is not an apples-to-apples comparison of *total training signal*, even though it is an apples-to-apples comparison of *human effort*. The paper is transparent about this mechanism, but it means that SEA's improvement comes partly from *amplifying* human data (the Dyna effect) rather than purely from *selecting better* human data (the Thompson sampling effect). The ablation comparing Variant-2 to Variant-3 helps disentangle these, but neither Variant-2 nor the Online baseline gets the benefit of synthetic data amplification, so SEA has a structural advantage in total training examples seen.

**What experiments would have strengthened the paper:**

- An ablation sweeping ensemble size `$K \in \{2, 5, 10, 20, 50\}$` to determine the point of diminishing returns.
- A comparison against online RLHF with PPO + learned RM to test whether active exploration advantages are specific to DAP.
- Measuring and reporting proposal set diversity (e.g., average pairwise distinct n-grams in `$\mathcal{S}_t$`) to verify the claim that ERM sync maintains exploration diversity.
- A condition where the Online baseline is also allowed to train on synthetic data from its own (single, non-ensemble) reward model, to isolate the ensemble-vs-single effect from the real-vs-synthetic effect.
- Results on at least one additional dataset (e.g., Anthropic helpfulness/harmlessness, or a dialogue dataset) to assess domain generalization.
- A wall-clock time comparison showing the total GPU-hours consumed by SEA versus Online to reach a given win rate, to assess whether the sample-efficiency gain justifies the computational overhead.

The central claim — that SEA achieves sample-efficient alignment through online Thompson sampling with an epistemic reward model — is **well-supported for DAP-based summarization alignment with simulated oracles at 1B–6.9B scale**, with the caveat that "sample efficiency" refers specifically to human label efficiency, not computational efficiency, and that the gains are most pronounced at intermediate query budgets (10K–30K) rather than at convergence. The paper's more ambitious framing — that this demonstrates a general principle for sample-efficient LLM alignment — is plausible but requires validation on human feedback, other tasks, and larger models before generalizing.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Cost of Difficulty Estimation Is Not Accounted For in the Headline Efficiency Numbers

**The assumption or constraint.** The entire SEA framework depends on a learned epistemic reward model (ERM) to drive exploration — posterior sampling, uncertainty computation for BAI-TS, and synthetic label generation for mixed preference learning all require an accurate ensemble of reward models trained on *real human feedback*. The ERM itself is not free: it consists of `K = 20` independent MLP heads on a frozen 0.4B-parameter transformer backbone, trained continuously online using `m = 5` random replay batches per round (Appendix A), and it must be queried for every dueling pair selection (`M = 20` candidates per prompt). The paper explicitly measures efficiency only in terms of **oracle queries** — the number of human preference labels consumed. All computation required to train and query the ERM, generate on-policy proposals, and perform mixed preference learning is omitted from the efficiency accounting. The paper does not report wall-clock time, GPU-hours, or FLOPs for any experiment.

**The consequence.** A practitioner deciding whether to deploy SEA must contend with a fundamentally different efficiency picture than the one presented. The headline "2–5× sample efficiency improvement" (Figure 1 Right) refers exclusively to *human label efficiency* — SEA reaches a given win rate with 2–5× fewer oracle queries than the passive Online baseline. But if the practitioner's bottleneck is **GPU budget** rather than annotation budget, SEA may actually be *less* efficient than the baseline. The ERM ensemble (20 × MLP heads, 0.4B backbone), the `M = 20` on-policy proposals generated per prompt (at temperature `η = 0.7`, then scored by all ensemble members for BAI-TS variance computation), the `m = 5` replay batches used for ERM updates per round, and the additional policy updates on synthetic data (30% of training batches after burn-in) all consume substantial compute that the passive Online baseline does not incur. The passive baseline only needs to generate two responses per prompt and train on the resulting pair — no ensemble, no uncertainty computation, no synthetic data generation.

There is also a **latency concern** not reflected in any metric. The BAI-TS dueling pair selection (Algorithm 1, Line 6) requires computing the preference probability variance across all `K = 20` ensemble members for each candidate `b ∈ S_t` relative to the first selected response `y_t`. This involves 20 × `M` = 400 forward passes through the ERM backbone for a single prompt. In contrast, passive exploration requires zero ERM queries for pair selection — it simply samples two responses from the policy. The paper's distributed architecture (Figure 4) masks this cost through parallelism, but for latency-sensitive applications (interactive chat systems), the ERM computation may add unacceptable delay between receiving a prompt and presenting the dueling pair to a user.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides no measurements of computational cost. Appendix C benchmarks the `oat` system's training throughput (Figure 9) but reports *batch latency for online DPO*, not for SEA with the full ERM ensemble and Thompson sampling overhead. The benchmark compares `oat` against HuggingFace `trl` on standard online DPO, not on SEA-specific workloads. There is no ablation varying ensemble size `K` to understand the cost-accuracy tradeoff — `K = 20` is stated without justification, and no experiment tests whether `K = 5` or `K = 10` would yield similar sample efficiency at lower computational cost.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not attempt to mitigate this limitation — it is not framed as a limitation at all. The authors are transparent that their efficiency metric is oracle queries (Section 5.2, Appendix D: "we limit the query budget to 50k"), but they do not discuss the computational overhead of the active exploration machinery. This is a significant omission because the paper's central claim — "sample-efficient alignment" — is ambiguous: efficient in what resource? The abstract and introduction use "sample" to refer to human preference labels, but a practitioner reading the paper might reasonably assume that the method also improves overall resource efficiency. The paper's silence on compute cost and latency makes it impossible to assess whether SEA's active exploration is worth its overhead in any given deployment scenario.


### Single Benchmark, Single Task Family, Single Model Architecture

**The assumption or constraint.** All experiments in the paper use exactly one benchmark: the TL;DR summarization dataset (Stiennon et al., 2020), consisting of Reddit posts where the agent generates summaries. All models are from a single architecture family: Pythia (Biderman et al., 2023) at three scales (1B, 2.8B, 6.9B). The reward oracle used in the main experiments is a single strong scalar reward model (Skywork-Reward-Llama-3.1-8B; Liu et al., 2024a). The paper makes no attempt to evaluate on other alignment tasks (e.g., helpfulness, harmlessness, instruction following, dialogue) or other model families (e.g., Llama, Mistral, Qwen).

**The consequence.** The generalizability of every finding in the paper — the 2–5× sample efficiency gain, the superiority of BAI-TS over E&E-TS for offline performance, the diminishing returns of APL at larger scales, the interaction between optimizer choice and exploration efficacy — is unknown beyond the specific combination of TL;DR summarization + Pythia models + Skywork-Reward oracle.

This matters because summarization has properties that may favor SEA's exploration mechanism in ways that do not transfer to other tasks. Summarization responses are relatively short, grounded in a source text (the Reddit post), and evaluated on fairly objective criteria (accuracy, conciseness, coverage). The action space `Y` — the set of possible summaries — is constrained by the source text, meaning that the policy's output distribution is relatively concentrated. Thompson sampling over reward functions works well when the optimal response is somewhere in the support of the current policy's proposals; if the proposal set `S_t` (sampled from `π_{θ_{t-1}}`) contains near-optimal responses with non-trivial probability, greedy selection approximates `arg max` effectively. For more open-ended tasks — creative writing, long-form dialogue, multi-step reasoning — the action space is vastly larger, the policy's output distribution may be more diffuse, and `M = 20` proposals may be insufficient to contain a near-optimal response. The policy-guided search approximation could break down, and the Thompson sampling exploration strategy would need to generate proposals from a much broader distribution.

Similarly, Skywork-Reward-Llama-3.1-8B is a deterministic, noise-free oracle — it always produces the same preference label for the same pair of responses. Real human annotators are stochastic, exhibiting rating noise, attention lapses, and systematic biases. The GPT-4o-mini experiment (Section 6.4, Figure 7 Right) partially addresses noise, but even LLM judges have different noise characteristics than humans. The paper's results show that the relative ranking of baselines changes under the stochastic oracle (APL falls behind Online, XPO catches up), demonstrating that exploration strategies interact with oracle noise in ways that are not predictable from the deterministic setting. Whether SEA's advantage generalizes to genuinely human feedback — which introduces correlated errors, annotator disagreement, and distribution shift over time — is an open question.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides no multi-task or multi-family evaluation. The only variation in oracle comes from the GPT-4o-mini experiment (Figure 7 Right), which is conducted at a single scale (1B) with a single optimizer (DPO). The paper does not report results for IPO or SLiC with the stochastic oracle, nor does it test at 2.8B or 6.9B. There is no ablation studying how the ERM ensemble's uncertainty calibration changes when the oracle is stochastic versus deterministic — a critical question because Thompson sampling's effectiveness depends on the posterior being well-calibrated.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not acknowledge TL;DR-specificity or Pythia-specificity as a limitation. The closing statements (Section 7) express hope that "positive empirical results, along with the open-sourced codebase, will encourage future research in this direction," implicitly acknowledging that validation on other settings is future work, but the paper does not frame the single-benchmark evaluation as a limitation that constrains the strength of its claims.


### The ERM Ensemble Size and Architecture Are Not Ablated

**The assumption or constraint.** The ERM design — `K = 20` ensemble members, each an MLP with 2 hidden layers of 128 nodes, on a frozen 0.4B transformer backbone, trained with L2 regularization coefficient `λ = 0.5` toward random initializations — is presented as a fixed configuration without empirical justification. The paper states that the ERM "is chosen to be much smaller than the reward oracle following Dwaracherla et al. (2024), which reflects the fact that human preferences can be more complex than what the agent can model" (Appendix D), and that `λ` was selected "after a coarse hyperparameter search" (Appendix D). No ablation over `K`, `λ`, the depth or width of the MLP heads, or the choice of frozen backbone is reported.

**The consequence.** It is impossible to assess whether `K = 20` is near-optimal or whether the same sample efficiency could be achieved with dramatically less compute (e.g., `K = 5`). Ensemble size directly controls the computational cost of ERM training and inference — each forward pass through the 0.4B backbone must be replicated (or batched) for all ensemble members. The quadratic relationship between `K` and BAI-TS variance computation (`K × M` evaluations per prompt) means that reducing `K` from 20 to 5 would cut ERM inference cost by 4×. If `K = 5` achieves comparable sample efficiency, the computational overhead objection (limitation #1) weakens considerably.

Conversely, `K = 20` may be *insufficient* for some settings. The paper's theoretical motivation relies on the deep ensemble approximating a Bayesian posterior over reward functions (Section 4.2.1). The quality of this approximation depends on ensemble size, ensemble member diversity (which the L2 penalty encourages), and the expressiveness of the individual models. If the ensemble underestimates epistemic uncertainty — for instance, if all 20 MLP heads converge to similar functions despite the L2 penalty — Thompson sampling degenerates toward greedy selection, and active exploration provides no benefit over passive sampling. The paper never measures ensemble diversity (e.g., disagreement rate, predictive variance on held-out data, calibration of the posterior), so it is unknown whether `K = 20` with `λ = 0.5` actually yields a well-calibrated uncertainty estimate.

The frozen backbone choice also matters. The ERM shares a 0.4B transformer across all ensemble members, which is **different from the Pythia models being aligned** (1B, 2.8B, 6.9B). The ERM's feature representations are therefore learned from a different pretraining distribution than the policy's, and may not capture the fine-grained differences between responses that the policy generates as it improves. If the ERM's frozen backbone becomes a bottleneck on representation quality, the ensemble members will have systematically limited accuracy, and the uncertainty they express will reflect model misspecification rather than genuine epistemic uncertainty about human preferences.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. There is no ablation over `K`, `λ`, MLP architecture, or backbone choice. No calibration analysis is reported.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper treats the ERM design as a fixed implementation detail rather than a hyperparameter to be optimized or analyzed. For a method whose central contribution is active exploration via posterior sampling, the quality of the posterior approximation is a first-order concern that deserves systematic investigation.


### No Comparison Against RLHF with PPO — the Dominant Production Paradigm

**The assumption or constraint.** All experiments in the paper compare SEA against DAP methods: DPO, IPO, and SLiC. No comparison is made against **online RLHF with PPO**, which remains the dominant alignment paradigm in production LLM systems (Ouyang et al., 2022; Bai et al., 2022; Touvron et al., 2023). The paper's literature review discusses RLHF as an offline or iteratively online method (Section 3, Figure 3a) and argues that PPO-based RLHF is "difficult due to the complexity and instability of RL optimizers" (Section 3). But this difficulty does not justify omitting RLHF as an empirical baseline — if SEA claims to be a sample-efficient alternative for LLM alignment broadly (not just for DAP-based alignment), it must be compared against the method that practitioners actually use.

**The consequence.** RLHF with PPO has an exploration mechanism that DAP methods lack: PPO's stochastic policy gradient and learned value function provide a form of exploration through action-space noise and bootstrapped value estimates. In online RLHF, the policy is updated continuously from on-policy data, and the reward model can be retrained iteratively — satisfying Property 1 (online interaction). While PPO does not employ explicit epistemic uncertainty estimation for dueling pair selection (failing Property 2 in the CDB framework), its stochastic policy may explore the response space sufficiently that the marginal benefit of Thompson sampling over PPO's implicit exploration is small.

The paper's argument that DAP simplifies alignment is valid — DPO is indeed simpler to implement than PPO+RM — but this is a practical convenience argument, not a sample-efficiency argument. If online RLHF with PPO achieves comparable sample efficiency to SEA without the ERM ensemble overhead, then SEA's primary contribution would be in simplifying the training pipeline (replacing PPO with supervised DAP losses) rather than in improving sample efficiency per se. Conversely, if SEA significantly outperforms online RLHF in oracle queries to reach a given win rate, that would strengthen the paper's claim to be a sample-efficiency innovation independent of optimizer choice.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. The word "PPO" appears only in the context of describing RLHF in the literature review (Section 3). No experiment includes PPO as a baseline, and no discussion acknowledges this absence as a limitation.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper contributes an open-source codebase (`oat`) that "could inspire future research in sample-efficient online LLM alignment" (Section 7). It is plausible that the infrastructure could support PPO experiments, but the paper does not run them or discuss the challenges of doing so. Given that the paper's central claim is about sample efficiency for LLM alignment, the absence of the industry-standard alignment method as a baseline is a significant gap that weakens the practical relevance of the reported gains.


### No Evidence That Mixed Preference Learning Does Not Eventually Lead to ERM Exploitation

**The assumption or constraint.** Mixed preference learning (Section 4.2.3) trains the policy `π_θ` on a mixture of `γ = 0.7` real oracle data and `1-γ = 0.3` synthetic labels from randomly sampled ERM members. The paper argues that this amplifies the policy's training signal and maintains proposal diversity for Thompson sampling. The synthetic labels are generated by individual ERM members, which are (by design) imperfect approximations of the true preference oracle `r^*`. The paper states that "synthetic experience is not added into `D_t` to ensure reward learning always uses ground truth environment data" (Appendix A), preventing the ERM from training on its own errors. However, the *policy* trains on synthetic data throughout — and as the ERM converges, its synthetic labels become more confident (the ensemble members agree more), but not necessarily more accurate.

**The consequence.** There is a risk that the policy **overfits to the ERM's systematic errors**. While the ERM is trained on real human feedback, it is a finite-capacity model (0.4B backbone, 2-layer MLP heads) that will make correlated mistakes across ensemble members. If these mistakes consistently assign high reward to certain response patterns that humans actually disprefer, training the policy on synthetic labels from the ERM will reinforce those patterns. This is the classic **model exploitation** failure in model-based RL: the agent learns to exploit inaccuracies in the learned world model rather than optimizing the true objective (Janner et al., 2019).

The paper's `γ = 0.7` schedule is intended to prevent this — the majority of training data comes from the real oracle, keeping the policy grounded. But as training progresses, the ratio of synthetic-to-real data seen by the policy grows without bound. After 50,000 oracle queries at `γ = 0.7`, the policy has seen approximately 71,400 total training examples (50,000 real + ~21,400 synthetic). After 100,000 queries (beyond the paper's budget), it would see ~142,800 examples. The cumulative synthetic data continues to accumulate, and if the ERM's errors are systematic, the policy will eventually overfit to them — the 70% real data may not be sufficient to prevent this asymptotic drift.

This limitation is particularly concerning because the paper's experiments use a **deterministic, highly accurate oracle** (Skywork-Reward-Llama-3.1-8B, top-ranked on RewardBench). The ERM trained on this oracle's labels will have relatively small errors to begin with, so the risk of exploitation is minimized. Against real human feedback — which is noisier, less consistent, and more complex — the ERM's errors will be larger, and the exploitation risk grows correspondingly.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides no analysis of ERM accuracy over time, no measurement of policy-ERM agreement rates, and no test of whether the policy starts to exploit ERM errors at longer training horizons. The win rate curves in Figures 5–7 appear to be approaching an asymptote by 50,000 queries, but it is unclear whether the asymptote is determined by the true oracle or by the ERM's limitations. The only relevant signal is the comparison between Variant-3 (with ERM sync) and Variant-2 (without) in Figure 6 — Variant-3 consistently outperforms Variant-2, suggesting that synthetic data helps within the 50K-query budget. But this does not rule out eventual overfitting at larger budgets.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper acknowledges (Appendix B) that "online model-based RL algorithms could suffer from catastrophic forgetting in the face of nonstationary data... we leave it for future work," but this refers to nonstationary data distributions, not model exploitation. There is no discussion of how to detect or prevent policy overfitting to ERM errors in the mixed preference learning framework. A natural mitigation — periodically evaluating the policy on pure real data to detect divergence — is not proposed or tested.


### Difficulty of Deployment with Real Human Annotators

**The assumption or constraint.** The entire SEA framework assumes that the preference oracle can be queried **online** — at each round, the agent selects a dueling pair, presents it to the oracle, receives an immediate label, and updates both the ERM and the policy. The experiments simulate this using a reward model (Skywork-Reward-Llama-3.1-8B) or an LLM judge (GPT-4o-mini) that can be queried programmatically with negligible latency. Real human annotation does not work this way. Human annotators have latency measured in minutes to hours, not milliseconds; they have limited availability (you cannot query them 50,000 times in a continuous stream); they exhibit fatigue, drift, and inter-annotator disagreement; and they are expensive — each query has a real monetary cost that may dominate any computational overhead.

**The consequence.** The online interaction loop that SEA assumes — continuous, low-latency, unlimited-capacity oracle access — does not exist in realistic human annotation pipelines. In practice, human feedback is collected in **batches**: a model generates a large set of candidate pairs, the pairs are sent to annotators (often through a crowdsourcing platform), and labeled data returns hours or days later. This batch-collection paradigm breaks the assumptions of both Thompson sampling (which expects immediate posterior updates after each query to guide the next dueling pair selection) and the Dyna-style mixed preference learning (which relies on the ERM being continuously updated from a stream of real data to provide useful synthetic labels).

If SEA is deployed in a batched human annotation setting, it faces two problems:

1. **Stale ERM.** Between batches, the ERM is frozen. Thompson sampling during batch generation cannot adapt to the data being collected — all dueling pairs in a batch are selected using the same ERM posterior, which may repeatedly query similar pairs that are informative under the stale posterior but redundant after the first few labels. This defeats the purpose of active exploration: the strategy should adapt after each observation to avoid redundant queries.

2. **Delayed policy improvement.** The policy cannot be updated until the batch returns, meaning that potentially thousands of prompts are "wasted" on generating responses from a suboptimal policy. In the fully online setting, each oracle query immediately improves the policy (and ERM) for the next prompt. In the batched setting, the policy stagnates between batches, reducing the effective sample efficiency — the same number of oracle queries produces less policy improvement because they are not interleaved with policy updates.

The paper's E&E setting (online user feedback, as in ChatGPT's comparison interface, Figure 10) partially escapes this problem because users *are* online — they provide immediate feedback. But the paper's primary experimental focus is the BAI setting (crowdsourcing), which is precisely the setting where batched collection is standard and where the online assumption is most violated.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. All experiments run in a fully online loop with a simulated oracle that responds instantly. There is no experiment where oracle queries are batched with delayed feedback, no analysis of how ERM staleness affects Thompson sampling efficiency, and no comparison of online vs. batched versions of the same algorithm.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper contributes a distributed learning system (`oat`) designed for "experimenting with online LLM alignment using simulated human preferences" (Section 5.1, emphasis added). The word "simulated" acknowledges that the setup is not realistic for human annotation, but the paper does not discuss how SEA would need to be adapted for real deployment. The authors could have included a batched variant of SEA (e.g., select a batch of dueling pairs using the current ERM, collect labels for the entire batch, then update) and compared its sample efficiency to the fully online version, quantifying the cost of the online assumption. This experiment is absent.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper shifts the LLM alignment conversation from **optimizing within a fixed data regime** toward **optimizing the data collection process itself**. Before this work, the dominant framing treated alignment as a supervised learning problem: you have a preference dataset (offline or iteratively collected), you choose an optimizer (PPO, DPO, IPO, SLiC), and you train until convergence. Sample efficiency was implicitly about the optimizer's ability to extract signal from a given dataset — how quickly the loss decreases, how few epochs are needed, how well the method regularizes. The CDB formulation reframes alignment as an **online decision-making problem under budgeted feedback**, where the *choice of which data to collect* is at least as important as *how you train on it*. This is a genuine conceptual shift: the agent is no longer a passive recipient of data but an active participant in its own education.

The magnitude of this shift is more **reframing plus new diagnostic** than paradigm shift. The individual components — online learning, Thompson sampling, model-based RL, deep ensembles — are not new in their respective fields. What is new is the **synthesis into a unified framework** that explains *why* prior alignment methods are sample-inefficient in a common language (Properties 1 and 2) and provides a recipe for addressing both failures simultaneously. The shift is methodological: future alignment work can no longer report only final win rate at a fixed data budget; it must contend with *how* the data was collected and whether the collection strategy itself could be improved. This is analogous to how the Chinchilla scaling laws (Hoffmann et al., 2022) didn't invent model scaling but changed what questions the field asked about it.

**Reconciling prior contradictions.** The paper resolves a puzzle that had been accumulating silently in the literature. Several recent works reported gains from active exploration for LLMs — APL (Muldrew et al., 2024), XPO (Xie et al., 2024), SELM (Zhang et al., 2024a) — but their experiments were limited to a few online iterations with large batches, leaving it unclear whether the gains came from active selection or simply from online interaction. Meanwhile, Dwaracherla et al. (2024) showed that Thompson sampling improves reward model learning but didn't update the policy, leaving the data coverage problem unsolved. The CDB framework provides a unified diagnosis: the iterative batch methods satisfied Property 2 (active exploration) weakly and Property 1 (online interaction) barely; Dwaracherla et al. satisfied Property 2 strongly but failed Property 1 entirely. SEA is the first method to satisfy both strongly in a fully online setting, and its empirical superiority (Figures 5 and 7) validates that **both properties are necessary and their combination is synergistic**. This doesn't just reconcile contradictions — it explains them as partial satisfactions of a common set of requirements.

**Research directions that become more attractive:**

- **Verifier/reward model design for exploration rather than evaluation.** The paper demonstrates that a reward model's primary value during alignment is not scoring final responses for Best-of-N selection, but **driving exploration through uncertainty quantification**. This redirects research on reward models away from raw accuracy on RewardBench (Lambert et al., 2024) and toward calibration, ensemble diversity, and efficient posterior approximation — properties that matter for Thompson sampling but are invisible to standard accuracy metrics. A reward model that is slightly less accurate but much better calibrated could produce *better* alignment outcomes than a highly accurate but overconfident one.

- **Model-based RL for LLMs.** The Dyna connection (Appendix B) opens a broad research direction. SEA's mixed preference learning is a simple form of model-based RL — the ERM serves as a world model, and synthetic rollouts augment real experience. More sophisticated model-based techniques — uncertainty-aware planning, lookahead search with the ERM, conservative model exploitation (Kidambi et al., 2020; Yu et al., 2021), or model-based policy gradients — could be productively adapted to LLM alignment. The paper's demonstration that even simple Dyna helps (+3 points in the ablation, Figure 6) suggests substantial headroom.

- **Fully online experimental methodology.** The open-source `oat` system makes it possible to run hundreds or thousands of online interaction rounds without custom distributed systems engineering. This enables a **reproducibility and benchmarking standard** for online alignment that didn't exist before. Methods claiming sample-efficiency gains must now be tested under genuine online conditions, not batch approximations. This raises the bar for the field and will likely lead to the retirement of methods that only work in the batch-iterative regime.

**Research directions that become less attractive:**

- **Optimistic DPO variants that embed exploration in the loss.** XPO and SELM showed modest gains in iterative batch settings but "fell short" at larger scales in the paper's fully online evaluation (Figure 5, 2.8B and 6.9B). The paper's results suggest that exploration and exploitation should be handled by **separate components** (the ERM explores, the policy exploits) rather than conflated in a single loss function. The tight coupling to DPO further limits these methods — they cannot be used with IPO or SLiC. Unless future work demonstrates that optimistic loss bonuses scale better with more online iterations, this line of research seems less promising than modular architectures that separate uncertainty estimation from policy optimization.

- **Active reward model learning without policy updates.** Dwaracherla et al. (2024) showed that Thompson sampling improves reward model learning, but the policy was frozen. The paper's Variant-7 (Figure 6, Right) empirically demonstrates the ceiling: active reward model learning with a fixed policy plateaus at ~0.72 win rate, while SEA with an updated policy reaches ~0.92. The data coverage bottleneck is real and severe — a 20-point gap in win rate at convergence. Pure active reward modeling without policy updates is unlikely to be a productive direction for end-to-end alignment, though it may remain useful for reward model benchmarking.

- **Offline-only alignment for sample-constrained settings.** The paper's Figure 5 shows that offline DAP consistently underperforms passively online DAP across all scales and optimizers — confirming that Property 1 alone provides substantial gains. In a world where human feedback is the bottleneck (the setting the paper targets), offline learning leaves enormous sample efficiency on the table. Offline methods will remain important when a fixed preference dataset already exists (e.g., using public data), but for new alignment tasks where annotation budget must be spent, purely offline approaches are dominated by even the simplest online baseline.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Scalable difficulty estimation for query selection.** The paper's ERM ensemble must be trained on real human feedback to provide useful uncertainty estimates, and Thompson sampling requires the ensemble to be well-calibrated — if the posterior is poorly estimated, the exploration strategy may waste queries. The current design uses `K = 20` ensemble members on a 0.4B backbone, but no experiment measures calibration quality or ensemble diversity. A direct follow-up would measure the **expected calibration error (ECE)** of the ERM ensemble's preference probability predictions against held-out human labels as a function of ensemble size `K`, backbone capacity, and the L2 regularization coefficient `λ`. If `K = 5` achieves comparable calibration to `K = 20`, the computational overhead of SEA drops by 4×, significantly improving its practical deployability. If calibration degrades quickly below `K = 10`, that establishes a minimum ensemble size for reliable Thompson sampling. This experiment requires no new algorithmic components — it's purely an evaluation of the existing ERM design.

**Batched Thompson sampling for realistic human annotation pipelines.** The paper's fully online loop assumes instantaneous oracle feedback, which is unrealistic for crowdsourced human annotation (oracles have hours-to-days latency and are queried in batches). A natural extension is **batched Thompson sampling**: generate a batch of prompts, select dueling pairs for all of them using the current ERM posterior (without updating between selections within the batch), collect all labels, then update both the ERM and policy. The key question is: how much sample efficiency is lost relative to the fully online version? A direct comparison — fully online SEA vs. batched SEA with batch sizes of 128, 512, 2048, and 4096 — would quantify the **sample efficiency cost of realism**. If batched SEA with moderate batch sizes (128–512) retains most of the gain over passive online baselines, the method is deployable with real annotators. If large batches destroy the Thompson sampling advantage, then SEA requires a different deployment model (e.g., synthetic oracles during training, with periodic human recalibration). This experiment can be run entirely with simulated oracles by adding artificial delay between batch generation and label return.

**Cross-task and cross-model-family generalization.** The paper evaluates exclusively on TL;DR summarization with Pythia models. To establish generality, a replication study should evaluate SEA on at least two additional alignment tasks with different structural properties: **instruction following** (e.g., AlpacaEval or MT-Bench, where responses are longer and more diverse than summaries) and **harmlessness** (e.g., Anthropic's helpfulness/harmlessness data, where preferences involve nuanced ethical judgments). Additionally, testing with a different model family (e.g., Llama-3 or Mistral) would verify that the ERM's frozen 0.4B backbone (from a different architecture) does not create a representation gap that limits exploration quality. The key measurements: (a) whether active exploration (SEA vs. Online) provides gains on tasks beyond summarization, (b) whether the optimal exploration strategy (E&E-TS vs. BAI-TS vs. Uncertainty) differs by task, and (c) whether the ERM ensemble trained on Pythia outputs transfers to a different policy family without retraining the backbone. Negative results — e.g., SEA failing to improve over Online on harmlessness tasks because preferences are too noisy for the ERM to model — would be as informative as positive ones, bounding the method's applicability.

**SEA with PPO: disentangling optimizer from exploration.** The paper compares SEA only against DAP methods (DPO, IPO, SLiC), not against RLHF with PPO, which is the production-standard alignment method. A critical follow-up would implement **SEA-PPO**: an agent that uses the same ERM-based Thompson sampling for dueling pair selection (Properties 1 and 2), but trains the policy using PPO with a KL penalty on the ERM's (or a separately trained) reward model rather than a DAP loss. This disentangles the contribution of active exploration from the contribution of the optimizer. If SEA-PPO matches or exceeds SEA-DPO in sample efficiency, the exploration mechanism is the dominant factor and optimizer choice is secondary. If SEA-PPO underperforms SEA-DPO, the implicit reward parameterization in DPO may be synergistically interacting with the ERM's explicit reward model. Additionally, comparing SEA-PPO against standard online RLHF (PPO with a single reward model, no active dueling pair selection) would isolate the benefit of Thompson sampling in the RLHF setting, providing evidence for whether active exploration helps regardless of optimizer or is specific to DAP.

**Adversarial evaluation of ERM exploitation.** The paper acknowledges the risk of model exploitation in mixed preference learning (training the policy on synthetic ERM labels) but provides no empirical analysis of whether it occurs within the 50K-query budget or would occur at larger budgets. A deliberate stress test would run SEA for 200K+ oracle queries (well beyond the paper's budget) and track: (a) the ERM's accuracy on held-out oracle labels over time, (b) the policy's win rate when evaluated by the *oracle* vs. by the *ERM* (a growing gap indicates exploitation), and (c) the diversity of the policy's proposal set `S_t` over time (collapse indicates overfitting to ERM modes). To accelerate exploitation, one could use a deliberately weakened ERM (e.g., trained on only 10% of the oracle data, or with a smaller backbone). If the policy's oracle win rate eventually *decreases* while its ERM win rate increases, model exploitation is confirmed and the `γ` schedule or ERM capacity needs rethinking. If no exploitation is observed even with a weak ERM and long training, the 70/30 real/synthetic split may be inherently robust. This experiment establishes safety boundaries for mixed preference learning.

**Uncertainty quantification beyond deep ensembles.** The ERM uses a deep ensemble of MLP heads as an approximate posterior. This is one of many possible uncertainty quantification methods. A systematic comparison against alternatives — **Monte Carlo dropout** on a single reward model head, **Laplace approximation** for the MLP weights, or **evidential deep learning** (which parameterizes a distribution over rewards directly) — would establish whether the ensemble is merely a convenient choice or genuinely necessary for effective Thompson sampling. The key metric is **regret**: does a cheaper uncertainty method achieve comparable sample efficiency to `K = 20` ensembles? If MC dropout with a single head achieves 80% of the gain at 5% of the ensemble cost, it may be preferable for practitioners. This is a purely diagnostic experiment that can be run by swapping the ERM implementation in the `oat` codebase.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Cost-efficient annotation campaigns for custom alignment.** Organizations that need to align an LLM for a domain-specific application (e.g., medical summarization, legal document review, customer support for a SaaS product) typically face a tradeoff: hire expensive domain-expert annotators to label thousands of preference pairs, or use a weaker, cheaper alignment method and accept lower quality. SEA's 2–5× sample efficiency improvement (Figure 1, Right) translates directly to a 2–5× reduction in annotation budget for the same alignment quality. For a medical summarization task where domain-expert annotators cost $50/hour and a standard alignment run might require 50,000 labels at roughly $1–2 per label (including overhead), SEA could reduce the annotation budget from ~$75,000 to ~$15,000–$25,000, making custom alignment economically viable for smaller organizations. The BAI-TS variant is purpose-built for this scenario — it deliberately queries informative (not necessarily high-quality) pairs to maximize policy improvement per dollar spent on annotators.

**Online LLM serving with integrated preference collection.** Production LLM systems (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini) already solicit preference feedback from users through side-by-side comparison interfaces (Figure 10). However, the dueling pairs shown to users are typically generated through passive sampling — two random responses from the current model. SEA's E&E-TS strategy provides a drop-in improvement: both responses are selected via Thompson sampling to be greedily optimal under plausible reward functions, maintaining high response quality for the user while actively exploring the preference landscape. Because both responses in E&E-TS are near-optimal (each maximizes a sampled reward from the posterior), the user experience is preserved or improved relative to passive sampling, and the collected preference data is more informative for policy improvement. The paper's Figure 7 (Left) shows that E&E-TS achieves the best online performance among all exploration strategies — meaning it simultaneously improves the *current* user experience and accelerates *future* alignment. This is a rare case where exploration and exploitation are aligned rather than in tension.

**Amplified training data for self-improvement loops.** In self-improvement pipelines (e.g., STaR, ReST-EM, or iterative fine-tuning where a model generates training data for itself), the bottleneck is often the cost of verifying or labeling the generated data. SEA's mixed preference learning — where 30% of policy training data comes from synthetic ERM labels after a burn-in period — amplifies each human verification into approximately 1.4 training examples (at `γ = 0.7`). This amplification compounds over multiple rounds of self-improvement. If a self-improvement pipeline runs 5 rounds, each consuming 10,000 human verifications, SEA's amplification generates roughly 7,000 additional synthetic training examples per round (at 30% synthetic), or 35,000 additional examples across the pipeline — effectively getting 1.4 rounds of training data for free. The caveat is that the ERM must be well-calibrated and must not produce systematic errors that compound over rounds. The paper's finding that the ERM is trained only on real data (synthetic labels not added to the ERM's training buffer) provides a partial safeguard, but monitoring for exploitation drift would be essential in production self-improvement loops.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper positions SEA against two named alternatives — passively online DAP (Guo et al., 2024) and active exploration with fixed policies (Dwaracherla et al., 2024) — and the experiments provide clear decision boundaries. The scenario-dependent E&E-TS vs. BAI-TS distinction also provides internal guidance on which SEA variant to use.

**Prefer SEA over passively online DAP when:**
- Human annotation is the primary bottleneck (not GPU compute or wall-clock time)
- The alignment task involves a substantial query budget (10,000+ labels, where SEA's sample efficiency gains — Figure 1 Right — are maximized)
- The model scale is moderate to large (2.8B+, where the paper shows SEA's advantage over Online is preserved or widens, while APL's advantage vanishes — Figure 5)
- The deployment setting matches either BAI (crowdsourcing with paid annotators, use BAI-TS) or E&E (online user-facing system, use E&E-TS), and you are willing to run the appropriate TS variant

**Prefer passively online DAP over SEA when:**
- GPU compute is the bottleneck and annotation is cheap (e.g., using synthetic oracles or existing labeled data) — the ERM ensemble and Thompson sampling overhead may not be justified
- Latency is critical and the ERM's uncertainty computation for dueling pair selection (400 forward passes per prompt for BAI-TS with `K = 20`, `M = 20`) is unacceptable
- The model scale is very small (sub-1B), where the ERM's frozen 0.4B backbone may be too large relative to the policy, causing the ERM to dominate compute costs

**Prefer SEA over active exploration with fixed policies (Dwaracherla et al., 2024) when:**
- The initial policy's output distribution is far from the optimal policy (as is typical for SFT models) — the fixed-policy approach plateaus when the ERM converges (~0.72 win rate in Figure 6, Right), while SEA continues improving to ~0.92
- You need end-to-end alignment of the generative model, not just a good reward model for reranking — fixed-policy methods only improve candidate selection, not candidate generation

**Prefer active exploration with fixed policies when:**
- You already have a strong, well-aligned policy and only need to improve Best-of-N reranking (e.g., improving a production model's output selection without fine-tuning)
- You cannot afford to fine-tune the policy online (e.g., due to stability concerns, regulatory constraints on model updates, or inference-only deployment)

**Use BAI-TS over E&E-TS when:**
- Annotators are paid and response quality during data collection is irrelevant — BAI-TS achieves better offline asymptotic performance (Figure 7, Middle, reaching ~0.90 vs. ~0.86 for E&E-TS at 50K queries)
- Information gain per query is the primary objective, and you can tolerate showing low-quality responses to annotators

**Use E&E-TS over BAI-TS when:**
- The dueling responses are shown to real users whose experience matters — E&E-TS achieves the best online performance (Figure 7, Left, reaching ~0.88 vs. ~0.84 for BAI-TS at 50K queries)
- You need both responses to maintain minimum quality thresholds (e.g., avoiding obviously bad outputs that would degrade trust in the system)
