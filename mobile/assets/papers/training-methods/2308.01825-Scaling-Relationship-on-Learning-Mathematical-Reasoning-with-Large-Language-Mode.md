# Scaling Relationship on Learning Mathematical Reasoning with Large Language Models

**ArXiv:** [2308.01825](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.01825)

## 🎯 Pitch

Pre-training loss, not model size, is the real driver of math reasoning ability—and when you let LLMs teach themselves via rejection sampling, the weakest models gain the most, because what matters isn't just more data but more *distinct reasoning paths*.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper empirically studies how pre-training loss, supervised data amount, and augmented data amount influence the mathematical reasoning performance of supervised LLMs on the GSM8K benchmark using LLaMA and LLaMA2 models. The core contribution is the proposal of **Rejection sampling Fine-Tuning (RFT)**—a data augmentation method where a supervised model generates multiple reasoning paths via rejection sampling, deduplicates them to retain only distinct reasoning calculation processes, and uses these as additional training data—which leverages the finding that reasoning performance scales log-linearly with supervised data amount but that distinct reasoning path diversity, rather than raw sample count, governs improvement from augmentation. RFT with combined rejection samples from multiple models pushes LLaMA-7B to 49.3% accuracy on GSM8K, a +13.4 point improvement over the 35.9% SFT baseline, and LLaMA-13B to 52.1% (+9.1 points). A central finding is that RFT brings more improvement for less performant LLMs while better models benefit less, establishing that augmentation is most effective when pre-training has not already saturated reasoning ability—and that reducing pre-training loss through better base model training remains the fundamental driver of mathematical reasoning performance.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Gap: We Don't Understand How Mathematical Reasoning Scales with Data and Model Quality

This paper addresses a fundamental gap in our understanding of how mathematical reasoning ability in LLMs scales. While the scaling laws community has established relatively clear relationships between model size, data quantity, and pre-training loss for generic language modeling (Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022), and some work has explored scaling laws for downstream task transfer (Hernandez et al., 2021), there has been **no systematic empirical characterization** of how mathematical reasoning—one of the most practically important and intellectually challenging capabilities of LLMs—responds to changes in model quality, supervised data quantity, and augmented data quantity.

This gap matters for two distinct reasons:

**Practical decision-making in LLM deployment.** Organizations and researchers building math-capable LLMs face concrete resource allocation questions: Should they invest in pre-training a better model (more parameters, more tokens, lower loss), or should they instead invest in curating larger supervised fine-tuning datasets? If they augment training data through the model's own generations (a cheap, scalable source of additional supervision), how much improvement can they expect, and does this vary with the quality of the underlying model? Without empirical answers, these decisions are made blindly—teams might spend enormous resources on data augmentation when pre-training improvement would have yielded more benefit, or conversely, might leave cheap performance gains on the table by not augmenting.

**Theoretical understanding of reasoning acquisition.** Mathematical reasoning is qualitatively different from the language modeling capabilities typically studied in scaling laws work. It requires multi-step logical deduction, manipulation of abstract quantities, and often the ability to recognize when a particular reasoning strategy is appropriate—all skills that are not obviously captured by next-token prediction objectives. Understanding how these capabilities emerge and scale as a function of pre-training quality and supervision signal offers insight into what LLMs are actually learning during pre-training versus what must be explicitly taught through fine-tuning. The paper's finding that better pre-trained models (lower loss) show diminishing returns from both supervised and augmented data provides concrete evidence that pre-training and fine-tuning are partially substitutable—something that was speculated but not empirically measured at this scale of analysis.

### Where Prior Approaches Fall Short

The paper identifies several categories of prior work and explains why each leaves the core scaling relationships unresolved.

**Test-time intervention approaches dominate the literature but avoid the supervision question.** A large fraction of recent work on improving LLM math reasoning focuses on what happens at inference time: chain-of-thought prompting (Wei et al., 2022b), ensembling multiple reasoning paths through majority voting or verifier-based selection (Cobbe et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2023; Lightman et al., 2023), or search over reasoning trajectories (Yao et al., 2023). The paper acknowledges the effectiveness of these approaches but explicitly positions itself differently in Section 1:

> "While in-context learning (ICL) and performing multiple inferences can improve performance, it is computationally expensive and not suitable for online deployment scenarios. Therefore, we focus on the performance of the supervised LLMs with inference only once which is a setting closer to online deployment."

This is a crucial distinction. Test-time strategies that require generating 40 or 100 samples per question and then selecting the best answer are fundamentally different from improving the model itself so that its first, greedy-decoded answer is more likely to be correct. The former consumes computation proportional to the number of inference requests; the latter amortizes the cost across all future inferences. For high-volume production systems (the "online deployment" scenario), improving the model's single-inference accuracy directly reduces serving costs and latency. The paper's focus on single-inference (maj1@1) performance is thus motivated by practical deployment considerations that prior test-time-heavy approaches do not address.

**Data augmentation for LLM reasoning exists but the scaling relationships are uncharacterized.** Several prior works have applied rejection sampling or related techniques to augment training data for reasoning. Zelikman et al. (2022) proposed STaR, which uses rejection sampling to generate chain-of-thought rationales for fine-tuning. Uesato et al. (2022) used both outcome-based and process-based reward models to filter reasoning paths. Zhu et al. (2023) developed CoRE, which combines rejection sampling with Monte Carlo Tree Search and requires training a separate verifier model. Ni et al. (2023) used both fully-correct and partially-correct solutions for data augmentation.

The paper acknowledges these as effective methods but identifies a specific limitation: none of them systematically studied **how the benefit of augmentation scales**—with the amount of augmented data, with the number of distinct reasoning paths, with the quality of the sampling model, or with the quality of the model being fine-tuned. The paper states this explicitly:

> "Compared to previous works (Zelikman et al., 2022; Uesato et al., 2022; Zhu et al., 2023; Ni et al., 2023), we are using a simpler way of generating augmented samples without any trained process-level reward models and we are focusing on researching the scaling relationship between LLMs and math reasoning ability."

The phrase "simpler way" is important: RFT does not require training a separate verifier (unlike CoRE), collecting human process-level annotations (unlike Lightman et al., 2023), or using reinforcement learning methods. It is purely rejection sampling from the SFT model's own generations, followed by deduplication based on the equation structure of reasoning paths. This simplicity is a strength because it isolates the scaling behavior—any observed relationships are attributable to the data characteristics (amount, diversity) and model quality, not to the complexity of the augmentation pipeline.

**Scaling laws for downstream tasks are under-studied.** While Kaplan et al. (2020), Hoffmann et al. (2022), and Muennighoff et al. (2023) established scaling laws for pre-training loss, relatively little work has characterized how downstream task performance scales with pre-training quality, supervised data, or augmented data. Hernandez et al. (2021) studied scaling laws for transfer from pre-training to downstream tasks, and Henighan et al. (2020) and Caballero et al. (2022) investigated scaling for math problems specifically. The paper positions itself within this lineage but notes that none of this prior work provides the systematic empirical characterization—across model sizes, pre-training qualities, data quantities, and augmentation strategies—that the paper undertakes for mathematical reasoning on GSM8K.

**Pre-training loss as a performance indicator is under-utilized.** A key conceptual contribution of the paper is demonstrating that pre-training loss is a better predictor of downstream math reasoning performance than either model parameter count or pre-training token count. The paper observes in Section 3.1 that LLaMA models outperform GPT-3 on math reasoning despite having fewer parameters, which suggests parameter count alone is insufficient as a performance predictor. By showing a consistent negative linear relationship between pre-training loss and both SFT and ICL accuracy across GPT-3, LLaMA, LLaMA2, and GPT-4 (Figure 2), the paper provides an empirical basis for using pre-training loss—already a standard metric in scaling laws research—as the primary independent variable when analyzing how model quality affects downstream capability. This is not a theoretical claim about why loss predicts performance; it is an empirical observation that loss serves as a stable, model-family-agnostic proxy for the capability level the model has reached, which the paper then uses to organize its analysis.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper positions itself as a **scaling relationship investigation**, not a method proposal. RFT is presented not as a novel technique but as a simple, well-defined method that enables controlled experiments on how augmented data influences reasoning performance. The key contribution is the set of empirical findings about scaling behavior, summarized in Figure 1:

- Pre-training loss is approximately linearly correlated with both SFT and ICL accuracy within the studied range, and SFT performance improves more slowly with lower loss than ICL performance does.
- Model performance under SFT scales **log-linearly** with supervised data amount—doubling supervised data yields a constant accuracy improvement, but this constant is smaller for better pre-trained models.
- RFT performance scales with **distinct reasoning path count**, not raw sample count, and the improvement is larger for less performant models.
- Combining rejection samples from multiple models of different sizes substantially increases distinct reasoning path diversity, leading to further gains that degrade gracefully as models get larger.

The paper's framing places it at the intersection of three concerns: **practical deployment efficiency** (improving single-inference accuracy rather than relying on expensive multi-sample inference), **resource allocation guidance** (helping practitioners decide between improving pre-training versus augmenting data), and **scientific understanding** (characterizing when and why data augmentation works, including the diversity mechanism and the model-quality-dependent benefit). This is explicitly articulated in the introduction:

> "It is of interest to understand, predict, and improve an LLM's math reasoning ability based on different pre-trained LLMs and supervised datasets. With this knowledge, we can better decide the effort we put into improving the LLM or augmenting the dataset."

The paper does not compete with prior augmentation methods—it analyzes the scaling behavior underlying a simplified version of them. The comparison with CoRE and other prior work (Table 3) shows that RFT achieves competitive or better performance with a simpler approach, but this is presented as validation of the scaling insights rather than as a claimed methodological innovation. The paper's contribution is the **characterization of the scaling relationships themselves**, which the authors argue should inform how the field thinks about improving mathematical reasoning in LLMs.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

This paper constructs a systematic empirical framework for understanding how mathematical reasoning performance in LLMs scales as a function of three factors: the quality of the pre-trained base model (measured by pre-training loss), the quantity of human-annotated supervised fine-tuning data, and the quantity of model-generated augmented data (produced via rejection sampling). The problem it solves is not proposing a new method but rather characterizing the functional relationships that govern when and how much each of these investments improves a model's ability to produce correct reasoning chains on previously unseen math problems—with the ultimate practical goal of helping practitioners decide where to allocate limited resources (better pre-training, more human annotation, or cheap model-generated augmentation) for maximum single-inference accuracy gain.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The experimental framework consists of four major components arranged in a pipeline that studies scaling behavior at increasing levels of data availability:

1. **Base Pre-trained LLMs (the capability substrate):** A collection of pre-trained language models (LLaMA and LLaMA2 at 7B, 13B, 33B, 65B, and 70B parameter scales, plus GPT-3 and GPT-4 for cross-family comparison) whose pre-training loss serves as the independent variable representing model quality. These models provide the starting distribution from which all reasoning behavior emerges.

2. **Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) Pipeline (establishing baseline reasoning):** The process of training each base LLM on the GSM8K training set (7,473 human-annotated question–reasoning–answer triples) to produce an SFT model that can perform zero-shot chain-of-thought reasoning. This pipeline also supports controlled downsampling experiments (using 1/32, 1/16, 1/8, 1/4, 1/2, and full fractions of the training set) to measure how performance scales with supervised data quantity.

3. **Rejection Sampling Augmentation Pipeline (generating cheap supervision):** Starting from an SFT model, this component generates `$k$` candidate reasoning paths per training question (using temperature 0.7 decoding), filters out those with incorrect final answers, deduplicates paths sharing identical equation structures, and produces an augmented dataset `$D'$` that combines the original human-annotated data with the model-generated correct reasoning paths. The key configurable parameter is `$k$` (the number of candidate samples per question), varied among `$\{1, 3, 6, 12, 25, 50, 100\}$`.

4. **Rejection Sampling Fine-Tuning (RFT) Training and Evaluation (measuring augmentation benefit):** The augmented dataset `$D'$` is used to fine-tune the original base LLM from scratch (not the SFT model), producing an RFT model. The performance of this model is evaluated via greedy-decoded maj1@1 accuracy on the GSM8K test set (1,319 questions). Additionally, a multi-model aggregation variant combines rejection samples from multiple SFT models (e.g., `$D'_{\text{U13B}} = D'_{\text{7B}} \oplus D'_{\text{7B2}} \oplus D'_{\text{13B}} \oplus D'_{\text{13B2}}$`) to create higher-diversity augmented datasets.

Information flows through the system as follows: pre-trained base models → SFT on varying fractions of human data → measurement of SFT scaling curves → rejection sampling from SFT models at varying `$k$` → deduplication by equation structure → construction of augmented datasets → RFT training from base models on augmented datasets → measurement of RFT scaling curves → cross-model aggregation → measurement of multi-model RFT performance.

- **First,** I explain the formalization of the problem: what constitutes a "supervised LLM" in this paper, how performance is measured, and why maj1@1 is the chosen metric. This establishes the experimental unit before we examine how variables influence it.
- **Second,** I detail the relationship between pre-training loss and math reasoning performance (SFT and ICL), including how pre-training loss is measured, why it serves as a model quality proxy, and the linear correlation observed across model families. This sets up pre-training loss as the primary independent variable used throughout the paper.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is a **scaling relationship investigation paper** whose core idea is that mathematical reasoning performance of supervised LLMs can be understood as a function of three variables—pre-training loss, supervised data amount, and augmented data amount—and that these relationships can be empirically characterized through controlled experiments, enabling principled resource allocation decisions without proposing fundamentally new training algorithms.

---

#### Formalizing the Problem: What Is Being Studied and Measured

The paper's experimental unit is a **supervised LLM**: a pre-trained language model `$\rho$` that has been fine-tuned on a supervised reasoning dataset `$D$` to produce a model `$\pi$` capable of solving math word problems through chain-of-thought reasoning. The dataset `$D$` is defined as a set of triples `$\{q_i, r_i, a_i\}_i$`, where:

- `$q_i$` is a natural language math word problem (the question),
- `$r_i$` is a human-annotated chain-of-thought reasoning path (the step-by-step solution showing work), and
- `$a_i$` is the correct numerical answer.

The performance metric is **maj1@1** (also called "accuracy" in the paper): the fraction of test set questions for which `$\pi$` produces a correct final answer when decoding greedily (temperature 0, always selecting the highest-probability token). This is distinguished from **maj1@K** (majority voting over `$K$` sampled reasoning paths), which the paper reports as a secondary metric but explicitly states is not the focus because it is "computationally expensive and not suitable for online deployment scenarios" (Section 1). The single-inference accuracy directly measures what the model has internalized from training, not what can be extracted through expensive test-time ensembling.

The pre-trained model `$\rho$` is characterized by its **pre-training loss**, measured as cross-entropy on the model's pre-training corpus (reported in the original LLaMA and LLaMA2 papers). The paper uses this loss value as the primary proxy for model quality, rather than parameter count or pre-training token count, because the empirical results (Figure 2) show that loss correlates more consistently with downstream math performance across model families. This is a **descriptive empirical choice**, not a theoretical claim: the paper does not propose a formula connecting loss to accuracy, but rather uses loss as a scalar that captures whatever quality differences exist between models, whether those differences arise from parameter count, training tokens, architecture, or training recipe.

The experimental protocol is to vary one factor at a time—pre-training loss (by using different base models), supervised data amount (by downsampling), or augmented data amount (by varying `$k$` in rejection sampling)—while measuring accuracy on a fixed test set (the 1,319 problems in the GSM8K test split), thereby isolating the scaling relationship for each factor.

---

#### Pre-Training Loss as a Performance Indicator (Section 3.1)

The paper's first scaling analysis establishes that pre-training loss is a reliable predictor of downstream math reasoning performance, enabling the use of loss as the independent variable in subsequent analyses rather than having to reason about model size, architecture, and training data separately.

**What is measured:** For each model in the study (GPT-3, LLaMA 7B/13B/33B/65B, LLaMA2 7B/13B/70B, and GPT-4 as a reference point), the paper collects or computes two quantities:

1. **SFT accuracy:** The maj1@1 performance after supervised fine-tuning on the full GSM8K training set with 3 epochs, batch size 128, peak learning rate `$2 \times 10^{-5}$` with 3% warmup, using the final epoch checkpoint.

2. **ICL accuracy:** The 8-shot in-context learning performance (with chain-of-thought exemplars provided in the prompt but no weight updates), taken from the original LLaMA and LLaMA2 papers for those models and from Cobbe et al. (2021) for GPT-3.

**The observed relationship (Figure 2):** The paper plots these accuracies against the pre-training losses reported in each model's original publication and finds:

> "The pre-training losses are approximately negatively linear correlated to the SFT and ICL accuracy during the given pre-training loss interval."

In plain language: as pre-training loss decreases (the model gets better at language modeling), both SFT and ICL math accuracy increase in an approximately straight-line fashion. The paper explicitly notes that this linear relationship is expected to hold only within the observed range because accuracy is bounded between 0 and 1 (it must eventually saturate), but across the loss values studied—approximately 1.90 (GPT-3 175B) down to presumably below 1.62 (GPT-4, though GPT-4's exact loss is not publicly reported)—the linear approximation is serviceable.

**Asymmetric improvement rates:** The paper observes a second, subtler relationship:

> "SFT outperforms ICL consistently, while the improvements diminish when the pre-training loss is lower."

The SFT line in Figure 2 sits above the ICL line (supervised fine-tuning always beats in-context learning), but the SFT line's slope is shallower than the ICL line's slope. This means that as models improve (loss decreases), the **absolute gap between SFT and ICL narrows**—better models extract relatively more reasoning ability from in-context examples and relatively less additional benefit from explicit fine-tuning. The paper interprets this as evidence that better pre-trained models have already acquired more reasoning ability during pre-training, so supervised data provides less marginal signal. This interpretation echoes through every subsequent experiment: the central narrative of the paper is that pre-training quality and supervised data are partially fungible, and the relative benefit of adding data depends on how much reasoning ability the model already possesses.

**Why loss rather than parameter count?** The paper provides an empirical justification rather than a theoretical one: LLaMA models outperform GPT-3 on math reasoning despite having fewer parameters, showing that parameter count alone is an unreliable predictor. Loss aggregates the effects of parameter count, training data quantity, architecture quality, and training recipe into a single scalar, and Figure 2 shows this scalar correlates well with downstream math performance across model families. The paper does not claim this correlation is universal—it may break for models with radically different architectures or pre-training data distributions—but within the studied regime (decoder-only transformers trained on web-scale corpora), it holds.

#### SFT Scaling: The Log-Linear Relationship with Supervised Data Amount (Section 3.2)

The paper's second scaling analysis quantifies how SFT performance depends on the quantity of human-annotated supervised data. This establishes the baseline scaling behavior that the paper later compares against augmentation-based improvements.

**Experimental design:** The paper downsamples the GSM8K training set (7,473 questions) to fractions of `$\{1, 1/2, 1/4, 1/8, 1/16, 1/32\}$` by random subset selection. For each fraction, the base LLaMA or LLaMA2 model is fine-tuned with the same hyperparameters (3 epochs, batch size 128, peak learning rate `$2 \times 10^{-5}$`, 3% warmup). However, a critical experimental detail is that for very small data fractions, training for 3 epochs produces poor results due to overfitting. The paper addresses this by searching over two epoch settings: either 3 epochs or `$3/(\text{data fraction})$` epochs (which keeps the total number of gradient updates roughly constant). For example, with 1/16 of the data, the model is trained for either 3 epochs or `$3 / (1/16) = 48$` epochs, and the better test result is reported. This ensures that each data fraction is evaluated at the training duration that produces the best test performance, preventing underfitting from confounding the data-quantity signal.

**The observed log-linear relationship (Figure 3):** The paper reports:

> "The model performance has a log-linear relation versus data amount. When the data amount doubles, the performance increases by a unit."

Formally, this means that accuracy `$\text{acc}$` is approximately linear in `$\log_2(\text{data amount})$`. Let `$N_d$` be the number of supervised training samples. Then:

$$\text{acc} \approx \alpha \cdot \log_2(N_d) + \beta$$

where `$\alpha$` (the slope) represents the accuracy gain per doubling of data, and `$\beta$` (the intercept) represents the accuracy at `$N_d = 1$` (extrapolated). The paper finds this relationship is "stable during `$\{1, 1/2, 1/4, 1/8\}$` amount of the training data," meaning it holds across roughly an order of magnitude of data scaling before showing signs of curvature or saturation.

**Why log-linear?** The paper does not provide a theoretical derivation but presents this as an empirical regularity. Log-linear scaling in data amount is a common pattern in machine learning—it implies that each doubling of data provides a constant absolute improvement, meaning the marginal value of each additional sample decays as `$1/N_d$`. This is less optimistic than linear scaling (where each sample provides equal value) but more optimistic than a saturation curve that flattens quickly.

**Model-dependent benefit:** A key finding, echoed in every subsequent experiment, is that the benefit of adding supervised data depends on the pre-training quality of the base model:

> "Better model benefits less when supervised data amount doubles."

In Figure 3, the slope of the log-linear fit is steeper for LLaMA-7B (higher pre-training loss, worse base model) than for LLaMA2-7B (lower pre-training loss, better base model), and steeper for LLaMA2-7B than for LLaMA2-13B, etc. Quantitatively, moving from the full dataset to 1/32 of the data causes a much larger accuracy drop for weaker models than for stronger ones. The paper interprets this as evidence that better pre-trained models have already internalized more reasoning patterns during pre-training, so they need fewer explicit demonstrations to achieve a given level of performance—and conversely, they have less room to improve from additional demonstrations because they are already closer to whatever ceiling exists for the task given the model's fundamental capability.

**Practical implication for resource allocation:** If you have a weak base model, investing in larger supervised datasets yields substantial returns. If you have a strong base model, supervised data provides diminishing returns, and further improvements must come from better pre-training (or, as the next section explores, from augmentation that provides something qualitatively different from additional human-annotated examples).

**ICL crossover point:** The paper notes that "better model needs more amount of data to outperform its ICL performance." This means that for very strong models, the 8-shot ICL accuracy can be higher than the SFT accuracy when only a small amount of supervised data is available—the model extracts more reasoning ability from carefully chosen in-context examples than from fine-tuning on a handful of training samples. As supervised data increases, SFT eventually overtakes ICL, but the crossover point occurs at a higher data fraction for stronger models. This is another manifestation of the same principle: strong models already possess substantial latent reasoning ability that can be elicited without weight updates.

#### Rejection Sampling Fine-Tuning (RFT): The Core Augmentation Method (Section 3.3)

RFT is the paper's primary mechanism for augmenting training data without human annotation effort. It consists of three stages: (1) generating candidate reasoning paths from an SFT model, (2) filtering and deduplicating to select high-quality, diverse reasoning paths, and (3) fine-tuning the base model on the augmented dataset. I walk through each stage in detail, explaining the design choices and the scaling behavior that emerges.

---

##### Stage 1: Generating Candidate Reasoning Paths via Rejection Sampling

**What happens:** Starting from an SFT model `$\pi$` (trained on the full 7,473-question GSM8K training set), the paper generates `$k$` candidate reasoning paths for each training question `$q_i$`. The generation uses temperature 0.7 (following Cobbe et al., 2021), which introduces randomness into the token selection process: at each step, the model samples from the softmax distribution over next tokens scaled by temperature, producing diverse outputs rather than deterministic greedy sequences. The value 0.7 was chosen based on prior work showing it provides a useful trade-off between diversity and coherence for math reasoning.

**Filtering by correctness:** Each generated output is checked against the ground-truth answer `$a_i$`. Outputs with wrong final answers are discarded immediately—this is the "rejection sampling" mechanism. Additionally, outputs containing calculation errors detectable through Python evaluation are also filtered out. The paper does not elaborate on the Python evaluation step in detail, but this presumably means extracting the arithmetic expressions from the generated chain-of-thought, evaluating them in Python, and checking whether the intermediate results match what the reasoning path claims they are. This catches errors where the model writes a correct final answer but makes arithmetic mistakes in intermediate steps—such paths are internally inconsistent and would be poor training examples.

**What remains:** After filtering, each question `$q_i$` has a set of `$c_i \leq k$` correct reasoning paths. The average number of correct paths per question depends on both `$k$` and the quality of the SFT model. Table 1 reports these values for `$k = 100$`: LLaMA-7B-SFT generates 53.3 correct paths per question on average, LLaMA2-13B-SFT generates 71.6, and LLaMA-33B-SFT generates 88.7. The fact that stronger models generate more correct paths is unsurprising—they are more capable overall—but the next stage reveals a critical non-monotonicity.

**Why temperature 0.7?** The paper does not provide an extensive temperature sweep but notes that for LLaMA-33B, increasing temperature to 1.0 produces 82.4 correct paths per question (slightly fewer than the 88.7 at temperature 0.7) but 4.77 distinct paths per question (higher diversity than the 2.78 at temperature 0.7). This suggests a trade-off: higher temperature increases diversity at the cost of some correctness, and the optimal temperature for RFT likely depends on the model and the desired diversity-correctness balance. The paper acknowledges that a systematic temperature grid search might yield better results for larger models but was not conducted due to computational cost.

---

##### Stage 2: Equation-Based Deduplication (Algorithm 1)

This is the most technically distinctive component of RFT and the one the paper identifies as the key to its effectiveness.

**The core problem:** Many of the correct reasoning paths generated by the SFT model are essentially the same solution expressed with minor surface-form variations. Training on multiple copies of the same logical reasoning path provides limited additional supervision signal—the model already sees that pattern once. The goal is to select a set of reasoning paths that are **structurally distinct** in their mathematical reasoning, not just lexically different in their wording.

**What constitutes a "distinct" reasoning path:** The paper defines distinctness at the level of the **equation list**—the sequence of mathematical operations extracted from the chain-of-thought text. Specifically, the process is:

1. **Extract equations:** From each reasoning path `$r$`, locate all text wrapped in `<<...>>` delimiters (the chain-of-thought format used in GSM8K training data). These contain arithmetic expressions like `<<12/60=0.2>>`. Remove all whitespace from each equation.

2. **Form the equation string:** Join the extracted equations by a special separator symbol (not specified in the paper, but functionally this produces an ordered sequence of mathematical operations).

3. **Define distinctness:** Two reasoning paths are considered to have the same **calculation process** if their equation strings are identical. This means that paths with different natural language phrasing but the same sequence of arithmetic operations are treated as duplicates, while paths that differ in the order of operations (e.g., computing the hourly rate before the minutes vs. after) or in the specific formulas used (e.g., `$12 \times 50 / 60$` vs. `$12 \times (50/60)$`) are treated as distinct.

**The paper's justification for using equation lists:** The paper includes a revealing detail about why equation-order matters:

> "Different order of elements (e.g. 3 + 4 = 7 and 4 + 3 = 7) or different order of equations (e.g. 1 + 2 = 3, 3 + 4 = 7 and 1 + 4 = 5, 2 + 5 = 7) are considered different. It is helpful for models to know these orders can be exchanged and is hard for models to learn this with only one reasoning path each problem."

This insight drives the deduplication design: the goal is not just to avoid redundant training data but to actively teach the model that the same problem can be solved through multiple valid sequences of operations. A single human-annotated reasoning path for each problem (the SFT data) only shows one solution trajectory; RFT with deduplication preserves multiple structurally different trajectories.

**Algorithm 1 in operational detail:** The paper's Algorithm 1 proceeds as follows for each question `$q$`:

1. **Initialize:** An empty list `$R_q^s$` for selected reasoning paths and an empty set `$E_q^s$` for tracking equation strings that have already been seen.

2. **Iterate through generated paths:** For each candidate reasoning path `$r \in R_q$` (the pool of correct paths generated by the SFT model):
   - Compute `get_equation(r)` to obtain the equation string.
   - If this equation string has not been seen before, add `$r$` to the selected list and record the equation string in the seen set.
   - **If the equation string HAS been seen before:** Compare the new path `$r$` against the previously selected path `$r_s$` that shares the same equation string. Compute the total Levenshtein edit distance (character-level string similarity) between each path and all OTHER selected paths. Keep whichever path is **more dissimilar** to the rest of the selected set—i.e., the path whose average edit distance to other selected paths is larger.

**Why the edit distance refinement?** The paper explains:

> "We are trying to find the most dissimilar reasoning paths based on Levenshtein distances. The idea comes from we want diverse reasoning paths for better generalization."

Even among paths that share the same sequence of mathematical operations, the natural language framing can differ substantially (e.g., verbose explanations vs. concise calculations, different ways of stating the same operation in words). When forced to choose between two paths with identical equation structures, Algorithm 1 selects the one that is lexically most different from the rest of the selected set, maximizing the linguistic diversity of the training data in addition to the structural diversity already enforced by equation-level deduplication.

**The deduplication effect in practice (Table 2):** The paper quantifies how many distinct reasoning paths remain after deduplication for different values of `$k$` and different models. For example, with `$k = 100$` on LLaMA-7B, the SFT model generates an average of 53.3 correct paths per question (Table 1), but after deduplication, only **5.25 distinct reasoning paths per question** remain (Table 2). This is a ~90% reduction, showing that the vast majority of generated correct solutions are minor variants of the same few reasoning strategies. The number of distinct paths grows sublinearly with `$k$`: doubling `$k$` from 50 to 100 increases distinct paths from 3.94 to 5.25 (only a 33% increase), and further increasing to `$k = 400$` (via the U13B multi-model combination) yields 12.84 distinct paths.

**The critical implication for scaling:** The paper's central finding about RFT scaling is that performance depends on **distinct reasoning path count**, not raw sample count. The "no dedup" condition (where all `$k = 100$` generated correct paths are included, without filtering for distinctness) produces similar performance to the deduplicated condition despite having roughly 10× more training samples (Figure 4). This demonstrates that the model saturates on the information content of repeated similar solutions—adding more near-duplicates provides marginal benefit, while adding structurally novel solutions (even if fewer in total count) drives improvement. This is why RFT performance improves sub-log-linearly with `$k$`: because distinct path count grows much more slowly than `$k$`, and it is the distinct path count that matters.

---

##### Stage 3: RFT Training—Fine-Tuning on the Augmented Dataset

**Dataset construction:** The augmented dataset `$D'_\pi$` is the union of the original human-annotated training set `$D$` and the selected model-generated reasoning paths:

$$D'_\pi = D \cup \{q_i, r_{ij}, a_i\}_{i,j}$$

where `$r_{ij}$` is the `$j$`-th selected reasoning path for question `$i$` after rejection sampling and deduplication, and the subscript `$\pi$` indicates which SFT model generated the augmentations.

**Training procedure:** RFT fine-tunes the **original base pre-trained LLM** `$\rho$` on `$D'_\pi$`, not the SFT model `$\pi$` used for generation. This is a deliberate design choice: by starting from the same pre-trained weights that SFT started from, RFT isolates the effect of the augmented data from any effects of sequential fine-tuning. The training hyperparameters are not explicitly detailed in Section 3.3 but are presumably the same as SFT (3 epochs, batch size 128, learning rate `$2 \times 10^{-5}$` with 3% warmup), based on the appendix descriptions.

**RFT vs. SFT performance (Table 1):** For `$k = 100$`, RFT improves maj1@1 over SFT by:
- LLaMA-7B: 35.9 → 41.7 (+5.8)
- LLaMA2-7B: 41.6 → 47.5 (+5.9)
- LLaMA-13B: 43.0 → 49.1 (+6.1)
- LLaMA2-13B: 50.0 → 54.8 (+4.8)
- LLaMA-33B: 54.6 → 54.5 (−0.1, essentially flat)

The improvement is substantial for 7B and 13B models but **disappears completely for the 33B model**. The paper attributes this to the diversity collapse in the 33B-SFT model's generations: it produces many correct paths but they are all structurally similar (only 2.78 distinct paths per question at `$k = 100$`, compared to ~5.2 for the smaller models), so the augmented dataset adds negligible new information beyond what the original SFT data already contains. The 33B model has effectively memorized the human-annotated solution for each training question and cannot generate novel solution strategies through sampling—it overfits the training set's specific reasoning patterns.

---

##### Stage 4: RFT Scaling with `$k$` (Figure 4)

The paper varies `$k$` in `$\{1, 3, 6, 12, 25, 50, 100\}$` and plots the resulting RFT accuracy. The key observations are:

**Diminishing returns with `$k$`:** Every doubling of `$k$` increases accuracy by a decreasing amount. From `$k = 1$` to `$k = 3$`, the improvement is roughly 1.4–1.9 points. From `$k = 50$` to `$k = 100$`, the improvement shrinks to 0.3–1.0 points. This is because distinct reasoning path count grows sublinearly with `$k$` (Table 2), and performance is driven by distinct paths, not raw samples.

**Sub-log-linear scaling for RFT vs. log-linear scaling for SFT:** Recall that SFT accuracy scales log-linearly with supervised data amount—each doubling of human-annotated data provides a roughly constant accuracy increment. By contrast, RFT accuracy gains diminish as `$k$` increases, because the effective information content (distinct reasoning paths) grows much more slowly than the raw sampling budget. This reflects a fundamental asymmetry: adding a new human-annotated problem introduces both a new question and a new reasoning strategy, while rejection sampling from existing problems can only introduce new reasoning strategies (same questions, different paths). The paper explicitly notes:

> "Doubling reasoning paths should improve less than doubling training samples since obtaining different reasoning paths does not obtain any new questions."

**Model quality determines augmentation benefit:** The weaker models (higher pre-training loss) show larger absolute improvements from RFT. LLaMA-7B gains 5.8 points at `$k = 100$`; LLaMA2-7B gains 5.9 points; LLaMA-13B gains 6.1 points; but LLaMA2-13B gains only 4.8 points and LLaMA-33B gains essentially zero. This pattern is consistent with the paper's overarching thesis: augmentation helps more when the model has not already saturated its reasoning ability through pre-training. Better models are closer to their performance ceiling given available data diversity, so adding more diverse reasoning paths provides less marginal benefit.

---

#### Multi-Model Aggregation: Combining Rejection Samples Across Models (Section 3.3, Figure 5)

The paper's most effective RFT variant aggregates rejection samples from multiple differently-sized or differently-trained models to create augmentation datasets with substantially higher reasoning path diversity than any single model can produce alone.

**Why single-model diversity is limited:** The paper hypothesizes—and the 33B results confirm—that SFT models generate reasoning paths that are biased by their training. A model that has overfit the human-annotated solutions (like LLaMA-33B) produces correct answers through a narrow set of strategies; a model with weaker memorization (like LLaMA-7B) may explore a broader range of strategies during sampling, albeit with lower overall correctness. Different models, due to differences in parameter count, training data, or training duration (LLaMA vs. LLaMA2), may have learned different reasoning heuristics, so their rejection samples may cover complementary regions of the space of valid reasoning paths.

**How aggregation works:** The paper constructs two aggregated datasets:

- **`$D'_{\text{U13B}}$`:** The union of rejection samples (at `$k = 100$`) from four SFT models: LLaMA-7B-SFT, LLaMA2-7B-SFT, LLaMA-13B-SFT, and LLaMA2-13B-SFT. The samples are first combined across all models for each question, and then Algorithm 1 (equation-based deduplication) is applied to the combined pool.

- **`$D'_{\text{U33B}}$`:** The union of `$D'_{\text{U13B}}$` with rejection samples from LLaMA-33B-SFT (also at `$k = 100$`), deduplicated again.

**Resulting diversity (Table 2):** `$D'_{\text{U13B}}$` contains an average of **12.84 distinct reasoning paths per question**—more than double the 5.25 from any single 7B/13B model and more than quadruple the 2.78 from the 33B model. Adding the 33B samples increases this to only 13.65 distinct paths per question, a marginal gain of 0.81. This quantitative result is explained by the Venn diagram in Figure 6: each of the 7B/13B models contributes roughly 15% **unique** reasoning paths (paths found only in that model's samples and not in any other model's), while LLaMA-33B-SFT contributes only 6.5% unique paths—most of its correct generations duplicate reasoning strategies already produced by the smaller models.

**Performance gains from multi-model RFT (Figure 5 and Table 3):** Training on `$D'_{\text{U13B}}$` yields substantial improvements over single-model RFT at `$k = 100$`:

- LLaMA-7B: 41.7 (RFT k=100) → **49.3** (RFT-U13B), a +7.6 point jump
- LLaMA2-7B: 47.5 → **50.3** (+2.8)
- LLaMA-13B: 49.1 → **52.1** (+3.0)
- LLaMA2-13B: 54.8 → **55.4** (+0.6)
- LLaMA-33B: 54.5 (flat from SFT) → **56.5** (+2.0 over RFT k=100, +1.9 over SFT)

Adding the 33B samples (`$D'_{\text{U33B}}$`) provides essentially zero additional benefit over `$D'_{\text{U13B}}$` for most models, and in some cases slightly reduces performance (e.g., LLaMA-13B drops from 52.1 to 51.4). This is consistent with the finding that `$D'_{\text{U33B}}$` adds only 0.81 additional distinct paths on average—the marginal diversity gain is too small to improve training.

**Equalizing effect of multi-model RFT:** The paper notes that training on `$D'_{\text{U13B}}$` reduces performance gaps between models of the same size but different pre-training quality. For example, the gap between LLaMA-7B and LLaMA2-7B under SFT is 5.7 points (35.9 vs. 41.6); under RFT-U13B, it shrinks to 1.0 point (49.3 vs. 50.3). Similarly, the 13B gap shrinks from 7.0 points under SFT to 3.3 points under RFT-U13B. The paper interprets this as evidence that the augmented dataset provides enough reasoning supervision to compensate for differences in pre-training quality—the weaker base model "catches up" when given sufficiently diverse reasoning demonstrations. The paper speculates:

> "We can assume with sufficient supervised data amounts, the performance indicator should be the model size but not the pre-training losses."

This means that if you have enough high-quality, diverse supervised data, the advantage conferred by better pre-training diminishes, and raw model capacity (parameter count) becomes the primary differentiator.

**The 65B and 70B boundary:** For the largest models studied (LLaMA-65B and LLaMA2-70B), RFT-U13B provides minimal or no improvement over SFT. LLaMA-65B SFT achieves 59.3%; RFT-U13B yields 59.0% (essentially unchanged). LLaMA2-70B SFT achieves 63.2%; RFT-U13B yields 62.3% (slightly worse). The paper suggests the same explanation as for the 33B model: "better models benefit less from the supervised sample amounts while it has learnt more reasoning ability during pre-training." At sufficient scale, the pre-training data has already covered the space of reasoning strategies that the augmented dataset would provide.

#### Alternative Augmentation Methods Explored and Abandoned (Appendix D)

The paper's design choices for RFT are informed by negative results from two alternative augmentation approaches that were tried and found to be ineffective or only marginally beneficial. These negative results justify RFT's simple, diversity-focused design.

**Self-query augmentation (Appendix D.1):** This approach attempts to generate new training questions by reversing the question-answer relationship. The idea, inspired by hindsight experience replay in reinforcement learning, is:

1. Fine-tune a LLaMA model on the **reversed** task: given a reasoning path and answer, predict the original question.
2. Use a standard SFT model to generate (potentially incorrect) reasoning paths on training questions.
3. For incorrect reasoning paths, use the reversed model to generate a new question that would make this reasoning path correct.
4. Add the (generated question, generated reasoning path, answer) triple as a new training sample.

The paper reports that this approach produces "the worst results"—"Fine-tuning with self query augmentation data leads to the worst results, and the performance of mixing the original data with self query augmented data still falls short of that of the original data." Two defects are identified: (1) some generated reasoning paths are internally inconsistent (they contain calculation errors or logical gaps despite producing a correct final answer), making them poor training targets regardless of the question they're paired with; (2) the question generation model sometimes produces questions that are semantically mismatched with the reasoning path, confusing the model rather than teaching it.

**Self-revising augmentation (Appendix D.2):** This approach attempts to teach the model to correct its own mistakes:

1. Sample `$K$` reasoning paths from an SFT model for each training question.
2. Create training pairs: concatenate the question with one sampled reasoning path (using a template) as input, and pair with the ground-truth reasoning path as target.
3. Fine-tune a "revising model" on these pairs.
4. At inference time, first generate a reasoning path with the standard SFT model, then feed it to the revising model to produce an improved version.

With `$K = 1$`, the revising model marginally improves accuracy (36.09% vs. 35.90% for LLaMA-7B). With larger `$K$`, performance degrades. The paper attributes this to a **distribution discrepancy**: on the training set, the sampled reasoning paths are lexically similar to the ground-truth (since the model has seen these questions during training), making the revision task artificially easy. On the test set, the sampled paths are more dissimilar to any ground-truth the model could produce, and the revising model—trained on easy revision cases—fails to generalize. Two attempted fixes (selecting the most lexically dissimilar sampled path for training, and using N-fold cross-validation to reduce overfitting) provide marginal improvements but do not make the approach competitive with RFT.

**Why RFT succeeded where these failed:** The negative results highlight what makes RFT effective. Self-query augmentation attempted to increase question diversity but introduced noise. Self-revising augmentation attempted to teach error correction but suffered from distribution shift. RFT, by contrast, simply adds more diverse correct reasoning paths for existing questions—a clean, low-noise signal that improves generalization without requiring the model to learn complex meta-reasoning skills (like question generation or error correction) that may be beyond its current capability.

#### Computational Cost Analysis: Why RFT Is Cheap Relative to Pre-Training (Section 4.2)

The paper includes a FLOPs and GPU-hour analysis (Table 4) that contextualizes the cost of RFT relative to pre-training, SFT, and ICL. The key numbers:

- **Pre-training** a 7B model costs `$4.2 \times 10^{22}$` FLOPs and ~82,000 A100 GPU-hours.
- **SFT** on the 7,473-sample GSM8K training set costs `$1.7 \times 10^{17}$` FLOPs and ~0.6 GPU-hours for a 7B model—**five orders of magnitude less** than pre-training.
- **RFT inference** (generating `$k = 100$` samples per training question from the SFT model) costs `$1.4 \times 10^{18}$` FLOPs and ~10 GPU-hours for a 7B model—still **four orders of magnitude less** than pre-training.
- **RFT training** on the augmented dataset is not separately cost-estimated in the FLOPs table, but the GPU hours for "RFT inference + RFT" total roughly 10 GPU-hours for 7B, meaning the training step adds minimal overhead beyond inference.

The paper's cost narrative is: "the cost times of SFT (~1 × 10⁻⁵) and RFT (~1 × 10⁻⁴) are negligible compared to pre-training. One can always use SFT and RFT to improve models' performance." The practical implication is that for any given pre-trained model, RFT represents an extremely cheap way to extract additional math reasoning performance—the cost is dominated by the inference needed to generate augmentation data, not by the fine-tuning step itself.

**The FLOPs calculation methodology (Appendix E):** The paper follows the standard approximations from Kaplan et al. (2020). Training FLOPs are estimated as `$C_{\text{train}} \approx 6 N n_{\text{ctx}} N_s$`, where `$N$` is the number of non-embedding model parameters, `$n_{\text{ctx}}$` is the average sequence length, and `$N_s$` is the number of training samples. For inference, the paper accounts for the KV cache during autoregressive decoding, deriving a per-token forward-pass FLOPs approximation of `$1.66 N + 2 n_{\text{layer}} n_{\text{ctx}} d_{\text{model}}$`. The total inference FLOPs integrate over both the prompt encoding and the autoregressive generation of the response, accounting for the growing sequence length as each new token is generated. The average lengths on GSM8K are `$n_q \approx 66$` tokens (question) and `$n_r \approx 130$` tokens (reasoning path + answer).


## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Pre-Training Loss as a Unifying Performance Metric Across Model Families

The most conceptually significant reframing in this paper is the elevation of pre-training loss—rather than parameter count or pre-training token count—as the primary independent variable for analyzing how model quality affects downstream reasoning capability. This is not merely a convenience choice; it is a diagnostic insight with implications for how the field should think about model comparison.

**What the field did before:** The dominant framing in scaling laws research and model capability analysis used parameter count as the standard proxy for model "size" or "quality" (Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022). When comparing models, papers would control for number of parameters and sometimes training tokens, but these variables interact with architecture and training recipe in ways that make cross-family comparison unreliable. The observation that LLaMA models outperform GPT-3 on math reasoning despite having fewer parameters (Section 3.1) is a concrete demonstration of why parameter count alone is insufficient: it captures capacity but not the realized quality of training. The field lacked a simple, architecture-agnostic scalar that could serve as a common currency for model quality when studying downstream task scaling.

**What the paper contributes conceptually:** The paper demonstrates empirically—across GPT-3, LLaMA, LLaMA2, and GPT-4—that pre-training loss is approximately linearly correlated with both SFT and ICL math reasoning accuracy within the studied range (Figure 2). This is not a theoretical claim about why loss predicts performance (the paper does not provide a mechanistic model); it is an **empirical diagnostic** that enables systematic analysis. By using loss as the independent variable, the paper can ask questions that would be scrambled by parameter-count-based analysis: Does a model with lower loss benefit more or less from supervised data? Does augmentation close the gap between models at different loss levels? These questions become tractable precisely because loss collapses multiple quality dimensions (capacity, data, training recipe) into a single observable that directly tracks the information-theoretic quality of the model's learned distribution.

**Why this is significant beyond performance:** The loss-as-metric insight is a conceptual tool, not a method. It enables the rest of the paper's scaling analysis by providing a stable coordinate system: instead of saying "bigger models benefit less from data," which is ambiguous (bigger in parameters? tokens? both?), the paper can make precise claims like "lower-loss models show smaller slope in the log-linear SFT scaling curve." This precision matters because it separates two hypotheses that are often conflated: (1) larger models benefit less from data because they have more capacity to absorb it (a capacity story), versus (2) better-trained models benefit less from data because they've already internalized reasoning patterns during pre-training (a quality story). The fact that LLaMA2-7B (lower loss, same parameters as LLaMA-7B) shows a shallower SFT scaling slope than LLaMA-7B supports hypothesis (2): parameter count is not the driver, pre-training quality is.

**Tie to evidence:** Figure 2 shows the linear correlation directly. The asymmetry in SFT vs. ICL slopes (ICL improves faster with lower loss than SFT does) provides additional evidence that loss captures something about latent reasoning ability that manifests differently under different elicitation methods—a subtlety that parameter-count-based analysis would miss entirely.

---

### Innovation 2: The Log-Linear Relationship Between Supervised Data and Math Reasoning Performance

The paper provides the first systematic empirical characterization—at scale, across multiple model qualities—of how mathematical reasoning performance scales with the quantity of human-annotated supervised data. This is a **descriptive law**, not a theoretical derivation, but its practical and conceptual implications are substantial.

**What the field did before:** Prior work on data scaling for LLMs focused almost exclusively on pre-training data scaling (Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022; Muennighoff et al., 2023), establishing that pre-training loss follows power-law scaling with dataset size. For supervised fine-tuning, the dominant assumption was implicit: more data helps, but the functional form—and whether it depends on the base model's quality—was uncharacterized. Practitioners making decisions about data annotation budgets had no empirical basis for predicting the return on investment. Would doubling annotation effort double the gain? Would the benefit taper off quickly? Would a stronger base model need more or less annotation to reach a given accuracy?

**What the paper contributes:** The finding that SFT accuracy scales log-linearly with data amount—"when the data amount doubles, the performance increases by a unit"—is deceptively simple. Its significance lies in what it enables: **extrapolation**. Given accuracy at two data fractions, one can estimate accuracy at any other fraction within the stable regime. This transforms data annotation from a blind investment into a predictable one. The paper demonstrates this concretely by plotting SFT accuracy from 1/32 to the full GSM8K training set and showing the log-linear relationship holds stably (Figure 3).

**Why this is fundamental rather than incremental:** The log-linear form is not a foregone conclusion. Many plausible scaling relationships—power-law, sigmoidal saturation, even linear in data amount—would have been consistent with the prior literature. The specific finding that *doubling* provides *constant absolute improvement* (rather than constant relative improvement or diminishing absolute improvement) has concrete implications: (a) there is no "saturation point" within the studied range where adding data stops helping; (b) the absolute benefit of adding data is largest when starting from small datasets (the first doubling is as valuable as the fifth); (c) the slope—how many accuracy points per doubling—is model-quality-dependent. This last point is the key diagnostic: the slope is steeper for higher-loss (worse) models and shallower for lower-loss (better) models (Figure 3), which directly supports the paper's central thesis that pre-training and supervised data are partially substitutable—better pre-training reduces the need for supervised data, and vice versa.

**Tie to evidence:** Figure 3 shows the log-linear fits for LLaMA and LLaMA2 at 7B and 13B scales. The diminishing slope with decreasing pre-training loss is visually apparent and consistent across model families. The paper's empirical claim is bounded—it holds for the studied range of data fractions and may break down at extremes—but within that range, the regularity is clear and actionable.

---

### Innovation 3: Distinct Reasoning Path Diversity as the Governing Variable in Data Augmentation

This is the paper's deepest conceptual contribution: the identification that what matters for augmentation-based improvement is not the raw quantity of generated training samples but the **diversity of structurally distinct reasoning paths** they contain. This reframes data augmentation from a quantity problem to a coverage problem, with significant implications for how augmentation methods should be designed and evaluated.

**What the field did before:** Prior work on reasoning data augmentation (Zelikman et al., 2022; Uesato et al., 2022; Zhu et al., 2023; Ni et al., 2023) focused on generating more correct reasoning paths and using them for training, with the implicit assumption that more data—especially more *correct* data—translates to better performance. Methods were distinguished by *how* they generated and filtered samples (verifier-guided, MCTS, RL-based), not by their ability to produce *structurally diverse* reasoning paths. The diversity question, when addressed at all, was handled implicitly through sampling temperature or ensembling, without a clear operationalization of what diversity means or how it scales with sampling effort.

**What the paper contributes:** The paper operationalizes reasoning path diversity through a specific, measurable proxy: the number of **distinct equation lists** (ordered sequences of mathematical operations, extracted from chain-of-thought text) that survive deduplication. This is not presented as a theoretically optimal diversity measure but as a pragmatically effective one that captures the dimension of variation that matters for training signal: different ways of sequencing arithmetic operations to solve the same problem.

The critical finding is that **distinct reasoning path count, not raw sample count, predicts RFT performance**. The "no dedup" condition in Figure 4—which includes all ~53 correct paths per question (7B model, `k = 100`)—performs similarly to the deduplicated condition with only ~5 distinct paths, despite having ~10× more training samples. This is a **strong negative result**: adding near-duplicate correct solutions provides negligible marginal benefit. The model saturates on the information content of a reasoning strategy after seeing it a few times; what drives further improvement is exposure to *structurally different* strategies for solving the same problem.

**Why this is a conceptual advance rather than just a method trick:** The diversity-as-governing-variable insight has cascading implications. First, it explains why RFT scaling is sub-log-linear with sampling budget `k`: because distinct path count grows sublinearly with `k` (Table 2 shows that doubling `k` from 50 to 100 increases distinct paths by only ~33%), and performance tracks distinct paths, not `k`. Second, it explains why the 33B model fails to benefit from RFT: it produces many correct paths (88.7 per question) but almost no *distinct* paths (2.78), revealing that larger models overfit the training set's specific reasoning patterns and lose the ability to generate diverse solution strategies through sampling. Third, it motivates multi-model aggregation as a way to increase diversity beyond what any single model can produce—different models, due to differences in training, overfit to different subsets of reasoning strategies, so their combined rejection samples cover a broader space of valid reasoning paths (Figure 6 shows each 7B/13B model contributes ~15% unique paths to the combined pool).

**Tie to evidence:** Table 2 quantifies distinct path counts across `k` and models. Figure 4 shows RFT performance scaling with `k` and the "no dedup" comparison. Figure 6 visualizes the unique-path contributions across models. The convergence of these results on the diversity explanation is the paper's strongest empirical argument.

### Innovation 4: The Diminishing Returns of Augmentation as a Diagnostic of Pre-Training Capability Saturation

The paper's most subtle but potentially most consequential finding is not a method or a scaling law but a **diagnostic pattern**: the benefit of data augmentation (RFT) decreases monotonically with pre-training quality, eventually reaching zero for sufficiently capable models. This pattern serves as a probe for understanding what pre-training actually contributes to reasoning ability.

**What the field did before:** The relationship between pre-training quality and fine-tuning data requirements was largely uncharacterized for reasoning tasks. The dominant narrative was that "bigger models are more sample-efficient"—they need fewer examples to learn a task—which would predict that better models should benefit *more* from a given amount of additional data (since they can extract more signal from each sample). The paper's findings point in the opposite direction: better models benefit *less* from additional data, whether it's human-annotated (SFT scaling, Figure 3) or model-generated (RFT scaling, Figure 4 and Table 1). This challenges the simple sample-efficiency narrative and suggests a more nuanced relationship.

**What the paper contributes:** The pattern is consistent across all experiments. In SFT downsampling (Figure 3), lower-loss models show shallower log-linear slopes—they lose less accuracy when data is reduced, which also means they gain less when data is increased. In RFT with `k = 100` (Table 1), the improvement over SFT is ~5.8 points for LLaMA-7B but drops to ~4.8 for LLaMA2-13B and essentially zero for LLaMA-33B. In multi-model RFT (Figure 5), the gain from `D'_U13B` is +7.6 points for LLaMA-7B but only +1.9 for LLaMA-33B and essentially zero for LLaMA-65B and LLaMA2-70B. The pattern is monotonic and robust: **the better the base model, the less it gains from any form of additional supervised data**.

**Why this is a diagnostic contribution:** The paper interprets this not as a failure of augmentation but as evidence that pre-training and supervised fine-tuning are partially fungible—they teach overlapping capabilities. A model with very low pre-training loss has already internalized, from its massive pre-training corpus, much of the reasoning ability that supervised data would otherwise provide. The supervised data is "less informative" not because it's lower quality but because the model already knows much of what it contains. Conversely, a higher-loss model has more "room to learn" from supervised data because its pre-training left more reasoning capability latent or unformed.

This fungibility interpretation has a testable consequence that the paper verifies: multi-model RFT with sufficiently diverse data can nearly close the performance gap between models with different pre-training losses. The gap between LLaMA-7B and LLaMA2-7B shrinks from 5.7 points under SFT to 1.0 point under RFT-U13B. The gap between LLaMA-13B and LLaMA2-13B shrinks from 7.0 to 3.3 points. This means that *if you have enough diverse supervised data, pre-training quality matters less*—the data can compensate for what pre-training didn't provide. The paper's aphoristic summary captures this: "Pre-training is all you need!" for ultimate capability, but for a given pre-training budget, cheap data augmentation can recover much of the gap.

**Tie to evidence:** The monotonic decline in RFT benefit with model quality is visible across Table 1 (single-model RFT), Figure 5 (multi-model RFT), and the 65B/70B results (Table 5, where RFT-U13B provides zero or negative improvement). The gap-narrowing under RFT-U13B is quantified in Section 3.3 and in the detailed results table (Table 5).

### Innovation 5: The Computational Asymmetry Between Pre-Training and Augmentation as a Resource Allocation Principle

The paper's cost analysis (Section 4.2, Table 4) is not merely an engineering footnote—it establishes a **resource allocation principle** that reframes how organizations should think about improving math reasoning in LLMs. The principle is that augmentation (RFT) is so cheap relative to pre-training that it should be applied *by default* to any pre-trained model before deciding whether further pre-training investment is warranted, but that augmentation cannot substitute for pre-training at the frontier of capability.

**What the field did before:** The cost of various training strategies was typically discussed in isolation or at the level of rough orders of magnitude. There was no systematic FLOPs-matched comparison that would allow practitioners to answer the question: "I have a fixed compute budget. Should I spend it on better pre-training or on data augmentation?" The paper does not perform this comparison formally (unlike the FLOPs-matched analysis in the prior summary example, which compared pretraining vs. test-time compute), but the cost data in Table 4 makes the comparison implicit.

**What the paper contributes:** The numbers are stark. Pre-training a 7B model costs ~82,000 A100 GPU-hours. RFT—including both the inference to generate augmentation data and the subsequent fine-tuning—costs ~10 GPU-hours. That's a factor of ~8,000×. Even multi-model RFT (aggregating samples from four models, with correspondingly higher inference costs) costs only ~62 GPU-hours for a 7B model. The ratio is so extreme that the paper can legitimately claim:

> "the cost times of SFT (~1 × 10⁻⁵) and RFT (~1 × 10⁻⁴) are negligible compared to pre-training. One can always use SFT and RFT to improve models' performance."

**Why this is a principle rather than just a number:** The cost asymmetry establishes a decision rule: for any pre-trained model you have access to, the economically rational first step is always to apply RFT (or similar augmentation) before considering expensive pre-training improvements. The marginal cost is so low relative to the potential gain (e.g., +13.4 points on LLaMA-7B to reach 49.3% with RFT-U13B) that *not* doing it leaves performance on the table at negligible opportunity cost. Only after exhausting what augmentation can provide should one consider whether the remaining performance gap justifies pre-training investment.

However, the paper is careful to establish the boundary of this principle: augmentation has diminishing returns as model quality improves (Innovation 4), and for the largest models (65B, 70B), it provides essentially zero benefit. For these models, "pre-training is all you need" is literally true—further improvement requires better pre-training, because the models have already saturated what supervised and augmented data can teach them about GSM8K-level math reasoning. This suggests a **two-phase strategy**: use augmentation aggressively for small-to-medium models to maximize their capability per parameter, and reserve pre-training investment for pushing the capability frontier beyond what current models can achieve.

**Tie to evidence:** Table 4 provides the FLOPs and GPU-hour numbers. The performance gains that correspond to these costs are in Table 5 (SFT to RFT-U13B: 35.9 → 49.3 for LLaMA-7B, etc.). The boundary condition is established by the 65B and 70B results where RFT provides no improvement despite similarly negligible relative cost.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** All experiments use the **GSM8K** benchmark (Cobbe et al., 2021), a dataset of grade-school math word problems requiring multi-step arithmetic reasoning. The training set contains 7,473 questions, each with a human-annotated chain-of-thought reasoning path and a final numerical answer; the test set contains 1,319 questions. The paper reports training on the full training set and on random downsampled fractions of {1, 1/2, 1/4, 1/8, 1/16, 1/32} to study data-quantity scaling.

- **Base model(s).** The primary model families are **LLaMA** (Touvron et al., 2023a) at 7B, 13B, 33B, and 65B parameter scales, and **LLaMA2** (Touvron et al., 2023b) at 7B, 13B, and 70B scales. For cross-family comparison of pre-training loss as a performance indicator (Figure 2), the paper also includes GPT-3 175B (Brown et al., 2020) and GPT-4 (OpenAI, 2023) as reference points, though only LLaMA-family models are used in the RFT experiments. The models were chosen because their pre-training losses are publicly reported and they span a range of capabilities suitable for observing scaling behavior.

- **Metrics.** The primary metric is **maj1@1** (also called "accuracy"): the fraction of test set questions for which the model produces a correct final answer under greedy decoding (temperature 0). This measures single-inference performance, which the paper argues is the relevant metric for online deployment where multiple sampling is computationally prohibitive. A secondary metric, **maj1@K**, reports accuracy under majority voting over `$K$` sampled reasoning paths (typically `$K = 100$`) with temperature 0.7 decoding. The paper notes maj1@K results as supplementary context but explicitly focuses its analysis on maj1@1.

- **Baselines.** The paper uses several comparison points throughout:
  - **SFT (Supervised Fine-Tuning):** Standard fine-tuning on the human-annotated GSM8K training set, serving as the primary baseline that RFT aims to outperform. This is explicitly not a prior-work method but the paper's own implementation, trained for 3 epochs with batch size 128 and learning rate `$2 \times 10^{-5}$`.
  - **ICL (In-Context Learning):** 8-shot chain-of-thought prompting without weight updates, with performance numbers taken from the original LLaMA and LLaMA2 papers for those models and from Cobbe et al. (2021) for GPT-3.
  - **CoRE (Zhu et al., 2023):** A prior reasoning augmentation method that combines rejection sampling with Monte Carlo Tree Search and requires training a separate verifier model. Compared in Table 3.
  - **FCS + PCS (Ni et al., 2023):** A prior method using fully-correct and partially-correct solutions for data augmentation. Compared in Table 3.
  - **RFT without deduplication ("no dedup"):** An internal baseline where all correct rejection-sampled paths are included without equation-based filtering, used to isolate the effect of diversity-based deduplication (Figure 4).

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper measures generation budget in terms of `$k$`, the number of candidate reasoning paths sampled per training question during rejection sampling, varied among {1, 3, 6, 12, 25, 50, 100}. The total number of augmented samples depends on both `$k$` and the correctness rate of the SFT model used for generation. For the multi-model aggregation experiments, the generation budget is the sum of `$k = 100$` per contributing model (e.g., `$D'_{\text{U13B}}$` requires 400 total samples per question across four models). The paper estimates computational costs in FLOPs and NVIDIA A100 80GB GPU-hours (Table 4), using the FLOPs approximation formulas from Kaplan et al. (2020) with KV-cache-aware inference FLOPs accounting (Appendix E).

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper conducts an epoch count search for SFT on downsampled data: because training for 3 epochs on very small data fractions leads to severe overfitting, the paper evaluates both 3 epochs and `$3 / (\text{data fraction})$` epochs (keeping total gradient updates approximately constant) and reports the better test result (Appendix A.2). For RFT, all training uses 3 epochs on the augmented dataset. There is no explicit cross-validation or statistical significance testing reported.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Pre-Training Loss as a Predictor of Math Reasoning Performance (Section 3.1, Figure 2)

The paper establishes that pre-training loss is approximately negatively linear correlated with both SFT and ICL accuracy across model families. This is not a quantitative claim with regression statistics but a visual pattern in Figure 2, which plots the following data points:

- **GPT-3 175B:** ICL 11.0% (8-shot, from Touvron et al., 2023a), SFT 34.0% (from Cobbe et al., 2021), pre-training loss 1.90 (estimated from Brown et al., 2020).
- **LLaMA-7B through LLaMA-65B:** ICL values of 11.0%, 17.8%, 35.6%, and 50.9% (from Touvron et al., 2023a); SFT values of 35.9%, 43.0%, 54.6%, and 59.3%; pre-training losses of 1.80, 1.73, 1.62, and 1.58 (estimated).
- **LLaMA2-7B through LLaMA2-70B:** ICL values of 14.6%, 28.7%, and 56.8%; SFT values of 41.6%, 50.0%, and 63.2%; pre-training losses of 1.75, 1.68, and 1.53 (estimated).
- **GPT-4:** Placed as an approximate reference point (the paper notes GPT-4 "states they use some part of the GSM8K data in pre-training, and suggest others consider its performance between SFT and ICL").

The key empirical observations directly from Figure 2 and Table 5:
- The SFT accuracy for LLaMA-7B is 35.9% while for LLaMA-65B it is 59.3%—a 23.4 point spread across the LLaMA family that correlates with pre-training loss decreasing from 1.80 to 1.58.
- SFT consistently outperforms ICL for every model. The SFT advantage is largest for weaker models: LLaMA-7B shows 35.9% (SFT) vs. 11.0% (ICL), a +24.9 point gap, while LLaMA-65B shows 59.3% vs. 50.9%, only a +8.4 point gap. This narrowing gap demonstrates that "SFT outperforms ICL consistently, while the improvements diminish when the pre-training loss is lower."
- The ICL line in Figure 2 has a visibly steeper slope than the SFT line, meaning ICL performance improves faster with decreasing pre-training loss. The paper does not provide numerical slope estimates but this visual relationship drives the interpretation that better models extract relatively more from in-context examples.

The paper explicitly acknowledges that "pre-training losses correspond to different pre-training datasets and different tokenizers which means they could not be compared strictly," and that the linear relationship is expected to hold only within the observed loss interval because accuracy is bounded between 0 and 1. There is no formal regression or confidence interval reported.

#### SFT Scaling with Supervised Data Amount (Section 3.2, Figure 3, Table 5)

The paper studies how SFT performance scales with the amount of human-annotated supervised data by training models on random downsampled fractions of the GSM8K training set. The headline finding is a log-linear relationship between data fraction and accuracy, with the slope (improvement per doubling) decreasing as pre-training loss decreases.

**Numerical results from Table 5 (3-epoch results unless otherwise noted for small fractions):**

| Data Fraction | LLaMA-7B | LLaMA2-7B | LLaMA-13B | LLaMA2-13B | LLaMA-33B | LLaMA-65B | LLaMA2-70B |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1/32 | ~8.7 | 14.2 | 7.4 | 5.9 | 25.3 | 28.9 | 15.8 |
| 1/16 | 12.7 | 16.2 | 14.2 (48 ep) | 27.7 | 29.2 | 39.5 | 52.8 |
| 1/8 | 16.5 | 21.8 | 19.5 | 33.4 | 39.3 | 46.0 | 57.8 |
| 1/4 | 22.7 | 28.1 | 27.4 | 37.5 | 44.6 | 50.4 | 57.8 |
| 1/2 | 30.9 | 34.6 | 36.1 | 45.3 | 50.8 | 55.6 | 61.0 |
| Full (1) | 35.9 | 41.6 | 43.0 | 50.0 | 54.6 | 59.3 | 63.2 |

Note: Some data points use the alternative epoch setting (`$3 / \text{fraction}$`) when it outperforms 3 epochs on small data fractions. The paper reports the best result for each fraction.

From these numbers:
- **Log-linear behavior is visible:** For LLaMA-7B, each doubling from 1/32 to 1/16 (12.7% vs. ~9.5% where available), 1/16 to 1/8 (12.7% → 16.5%, +3.8), 1/8 to 1/4 (16.5% → 22.7%, +6.2), 1/4 to 1/2 (22.7% → 30.9%, +8.2), and 1/2 to full (30.9% → 35.9%, +5.0) shows roughly consistent absolute improvements across several doublings, though the pattern is somewhat irregular given the small fractions and epoch selection.
- **Diminishing benefit for better models:** The gap between full data and 1/2 data is 5.0 points for LLaMA-7B (35.9% − 30.9%) but only 2.2 points for LLaMA2-70B (63.2% − 61.0%). Similarly, going from 1/4 to full data gains LLaMA-7B 13.2 points but LLaMA-65B only 8.9 points. This pattern is monotonic across the model quality spectrum.
- **ICL crossover:** The paper notes that "better model needs more amount of data to outperform its ICL performance." For LLaMA-7B, SFT with 1/32 of data already roughly matches ICL (~9.5% vs. 11.0%), while for LLaMA2-70B, SFT with 1/32 data (15.8%) is substantially below ICL (56.8%), indicating that stronger models require more supervised examples to surpass what they can already do via in-context learning.

The paper does not fit explicit regression lines or report R² values, so the "log-linear" claim is qualitative. However, Figure 3 uses a log-scale x-axis and the points visually align along straight lines for each model, supporting the log-linear characterization.

#### RFT with Single-Model Rejection Sampling (Section 3.3, Table 1, Figure 4, Table 2)

The paper evaluates RFT by varying the sampling budget `$k$` and measuring the resulting maj1@1 accuracy after fine-tuning on the augmented (deduplicated) dataset.

**Headline RFT results at `$k = 100$` (Table 1, Figure 4):**

| Model | SFT Accuracy | RFT k=100 Accuracy | Improvement | Distinct Paths/Question |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| LLaMA-7B | 35.9 | 41.7 | +5.8 | 5.25 |
| LLaMA2-7B | 41.6 | 47.5 | +5.9 | 5.19 |
| LLaMA-13B | 43.0 | 49.1 | +6.1 | 5.26 |
| LLaMA2-13B | 50.0 | 54.8 | +4.8 | 5.29 |
| LLaMA-33B | 54.6 | 54.5 | −0.1 | 2.78 |

For 7B and 13B models, RFT yields improvements of approximately 5–6 points in maj1@1 and roughly 4 points in maj1@100 (Table 6: LLaMA-7B goes from 35.9/48.7 for maj1@1/maj1@100 to 41.7/52.7). The 33B model shows essentially no improvement, which the paper attributes to the low distinct reasoning path count (2.78 per question vs. ~5.2 for smaller models) indicating that the 33B SFT model overfits the training set and cannot generate diverse reasoning strategies through sampling.

**RFT scaling with `$k$` (Figure 4, Table 5 detailed numerical results):**

The paper varies `$k$` across {1, 3, 6, 12, 25, 50, 100} and reports RFT accuracy for each. The numerical results from Table 5:

| k | Approx. Total Samples | LLaMA-7B | LLaMA2-7B | LLaMA-13B | LLaMA2-13B |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | ~12K | 37.6 | 43.4 | 42.7 | 52.1 |
| 3 | ~15K | 39.0 | 45.3 | 45.2 | 51.9 |
| 6 | ~18K | 39.5 | 45.6 | 46.8 | 52.2 |
| 12 | ~22K | 41.6 | 45.3 | 48.0 | 53.1 |
| 25 | ~28K | 40.9 | 46.5 | 46.0 | 52.6 |
| 50 | ~35K | 40.7 | 47.0 | 49.4 | 54.5 |
| 100 | ~47K | 41.7 | 47.5 | 49.1 | 54.8 |

Key observations:
- **Diminishing returns:** From `$k = 1$` to `$k = 3$`, LLaMA-7B gains 1.4 points; from `$k = 25$` to `$k = 50$`, it gains −0.2 (actually a slight decrease); from `$k = 50$` to `$k = 100$`, it gains 1.0 point. The improvement per doubling of `$k$` clearly decreases as `$k$` grows. For LLaMA2-13B, the improvement from `$k = 1$` to `$k = 100$` is only 2.7 points (52.1 → 54.8), indicating near-saturation.
- **Sub-log-linear scaling:** The paper explicitly contrasts this with SFT scaling: "Doubling reasoning paths should improve less than doubling training samples since obtaining different reasoning paths does not obtain any new questions." Table 2 confirms that distinct reasoning path count grows sublinearly with `$k$`—for LLaMA-7B, distinct paths increase from 2.20 at `$k = 12$` to 2.93 at `$k = 25$` (+33%) to 3.94 at `$k = 50$` (+34%) to 5.25 at `$k = 100$` (+33%), showing approximately square-root-like growth rather than linear.
- **The "no dedup" comparison (Figure 4):** RFT with `$k = 100$` and no deduplication (using all ~47K augmented samples per the 7B model) achieves approximately the same accuracy as the deduplicated version (~43.6% for LLaMA-7B vs. 41.7% with dedup—actually slightly higher, though the paper notes "the performance is similar and shows that it is better to estimate RFT performance based on distinct reasoning path amount instead of RFT augmented sample counts"). The critical point is that using ~10× more training data (no dedup) yields only marginal improvement over the deduplicated version, demonstrating that raw sample count is not the driver of performance.

**Correct paths and distinct paths as a function of `$k$` (Table 1, Table 2):** For `$k = 100$`, the average correct paths generated per question are 53.3 (7B), 60.8 (7B-2), 62.5 (13B), 71.6 (13B-2), and 88.7 (33B). Better models generate more correct paths. However, distinct paths per question after deduplication are 5.25 (7B), 5.19 (7B-2), 5.26 (13B), 5.29 (13B-2), and only 2.78 (33B)—the 33B model, despite producing the most correct paths, yields the fewest distinct ones. The paper interprets this as evidence that the 33B model has memorized the human-annotated solutions and cannot generate novel reasoning strategies through sampling.

#### Multi-Model RFT: Aggregating Rejection Samples Across Models (Section 3.3, Figure 5, Table 3, Table 2)

The paper constructs two aggregated augmented datasets: `$D'_{\text{U13B}}$` (combining `$k = 100$` rejection samples from LLaMA-7B, LLaMA2-7B, LLaMA-13B, and LLaMA2-13B SFT models) and `$D'_{\text{U33B}}$` (adding LLaMA-33B samples to `$D'_{\text{U13B}}$`), with Algorithm 1 deduplication applied to the combined pool.

**Headline results (Table 5, Figure 5):**

| Model | SFT | RFT k=100 | RFT-U13B | RFT-U33B | Gain vs. SFT |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| LLaMA-7B | 35.9 | 41.7 | **49.3** | 49.1 | +13.4 |
| LLaMA2-7B | 41.6 | 47.5 | **50.3** | 51.2 | +8.7 |
| LLaMA-13B | 43.0 | 49.1 | **52.1** | 51.4 | +9.1 |
| LLaMA2-13B | 50.0 | 54.8 | **55.4** | 55.3 | +5.4 |
| LLaMA-33B | 54.6 | 54.5 | **56.5** | 57.9 | +1.9 |
| LLaMA-65B | 59.3 | — | 59.0 | 59.7 | −0.3 |
| LLaMA2-70B | 63.2 | — | 62.3 | 64.8 | −0.9 |

The multi-model aggregation provides substantial further gains over single-model RFT for 7B and 13B models: LLaMA-7B gains an additional 7.6 points beyond RFT k=100 (41.7 → 49.3), and LLaMA-13B gains an additional 3.0 points (49.1 → 52.1). For LLaMA-33B, which showed essentially zero improvement from single-model RFT (54.6 → 54.5), the multi-model dataset provides a modest 1.9-point improvement over SFT (54.6 → 56.5). For 65B and 70B models, RFT-U13B provides zero or negative improvement compared to SFT.

**Diversity analysis (Table 2, Figure 6):** `$D'_{\text{U13B}}$` contains an average of **12.84 distinct reasoning paths per question**, more than double the ~5.25 from any single 7B/13B model and more than quadruple the 2.78 from the 33B model. Adding the 33B samples (`$D'_{\text{U33B}}$`) increases this to only 13.65 distinct paths per question—a marginal gain of 0.81. Figure 6's Venn diagram shows that each of the four 7B/13B models contributes approximately 15% unique reasoning paths (paths found only in that model's samples), while LLaMA-33B-SFT contributes only 6.5% unique paths. This explains both why multi-model aggregation helps (diverse sources cover complementary reasoning strategies) and why adding 33B samples helps little (the 33B model's generations largely overlap with what the smaller models already produce).

**Gap-narrowing effect:** The gaps between same-size models with different pre-training losses shrink substantially under RFT-U13B. The LLaMA-7B vs. LLaMA2-7B gap is 35.9 vs. 41.6 (5.7 points) under SFT, but narrows to 49.3 vs. 50.3 (1.0 point) under RFT-U13B. The LLaMA-13B vs. LLaMA2-13B gap narrows from 43.0 vs. 50.0 (7.0 points) under SFT to 52.1 vs. 55.4 (3.3 points) under RFT-U13B. The paper interprets this as evidence that "with sufficient supervised data amounts, the performance indicator should be the model size but not the pre-training losses."

**Effect of 33B samples (Figure 5, Table 5):** Adding `$D'_{\text{33B}}$` to form `$D'_{\text{U33B}}$` provides marginal or slightly negative benefit for most models. LLaMA-7B goes from 49.3 (U13B) to 49.1 (U33B); LLaMA-13B drops from 52.1 to 51.4. Only LLaMA-33B and LLaMA-65B show small improvements (+1.4 and +0.7 respectively). The paper concludes that "including `$D'_{\text{33B}}$` in aggregation barely influences the performance," attributed to the 6.5% unique path contribution and the marginal 0.81 increase in average distinct paths.

**Comparison to prior methods (Table 3):** RFT-U13B on LLaMA-7B achieves 49.3% maj1@1, compared to 34.9% for CoRE (Zhu et al., 2023) on GPT-J-6B and 19.5% for FCS+PCS (Ni et al., 2023) on GPT-Neo-2.7B. The paper notes that RFT is "simpler compared to CoRE, since RFT does not require training verifier models and decoding with Monte Carlo Tree Search." However, the comparison is across different base models and different parameter counts, so the numbers are not directly comparable—they establish that RFT achieves competitive performance but not that it strictly dominates prior methods. The paper also compares against several open-source aligned models (ChatGLM2, InternLM) which achieve 28–38% on GSM8K, noting that their performance is "very similar to SFT performances of LLaMA-7B" (35.9%) and that "using our augmented dataset `$D'_{\text{U13B}}$` to replace the original GSM8K can significantly boost their 7B models' performances."

#### RFT Scaling vs. SFT Scaling: A Comparison of Data Efficiency

The paper's experiments allow comparison of how efficiently different types of data improve performance. This comparison is implicit in the numbers but not aggregated into a single figure.

**Human-annotated data efficiency (SFT, Figure 3):** Starting from LLaMA-7B with 1/32 of GSM8K (~234 samples), SFT achieves roughly 8.7% accuracy. Each doubling of supervised data to reach the full 7,473 samples provides roughly 5–6 points of improvement, reaching 35.9%. The total improvement from ~234 to 7,473 samples (a 32× increase, 5 doublings) is ~27.2 points.

**Model-generated data efficiency (RFT, Figure 4):** Starting from LLaMA-7B SFT (35.9%), RFT with k=1 (~12K augmented samples, mostly the same as SFT data plus minimal additions) achieves 37.6%. Increasing to k=100 (~47K augmented samples) reaches 41.7%, a gain of only 4.1 points despite adding ~35K new samples. The paper explains this by noting that the augmented samples cover the same questions with different reasoning paths, so they provide less new information per sample than adding entirely new questions would.

**Multi-model augmentation efficiency:** RFT-U13B (~104K augmented samples) reaches 49.3%, a 7.6-point gain over single-model RFT k=100 and a 13.4-point gain over SFT. This gain per augmented sample is higher than single-model RFT because the multi-model samples contain more structurally distinct reasoning paths (12.84 per question vs. 5.25).

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

The paper does not present formal ablation studies in the traditional sense of systematically removing components and measuring degradation. However, several experimental conditions serve as implicit ablations that test the importance of specific design choices:

**Deduplication (Equation-Based Filtering) vs. "No Dedup" (Figure 4, Table 5):** The comparison between RFT with deduplication at `$k = 100$` and RFT without deduplication (keeping all correct generated paths) tests whether filtering for structurally distinct reasoning paths matters. For LLaMA-7B, no-dedup RFT achieves 43.6% vs. 41.7% with dedup—a +1.9 point difference despite using approximately 10× more training samples. For LLaMA-13B, the difference is 46.9% (no dedup) vs. 49.1% (dedup)—dedup actually *outperforms* despite using fewer samples. The paper's conclusion that "the performance is similar and shows that it is better to estimate RFT performance based on distinct reasoning path amount instead of RFT augmented sample counts" is supported by the rough equivalence of the two conditions despite massive sample count differences. The deduplication achieves comparable or better performance with substantially less training time.

**Single-Model vs. Multi-Model Rejection Sampling (Figure 5, Table 1 vs. Table 5):** The comparison between RFT k=100 (single model) and RFT-U13B (four models) isolates the effect of combining samples from diverse models. The large performance jumps (e.g., +7.6 points for LLaMA-7B) demonstrate that model diversity in the sampling pool substantially increases the effective diversity of the augmented dataset beyond what any single model can achieve by increasing `$k$`. This is corroborated by the distinct path counts in Table 2: single-model k=100 yields ~5.25 distinct paths per question, while U13B yields 12.84 (+144%).

**U13B vs. U33B (Figure 5, Table 5):** Adding 33B model samples to the U13B pool tests whether larger models contribute complementary reasoning strategies. The near-zero performance difference for 7B and 13B models (e.g., LLaMA-7B: 49.3 U13B vs. 49.1 U33B) and the Venn diagram in Figure 6 (33B contributes only 6.5% unique paths) demonstrate that the 33B model's rejection samples add negligible diversity beyond what the four smaller models already cover. This is a finding about model behavior rather than a method ablation: larger models overfit the training set and lose the ability to generate diverse reasoning strategies through sampling.

**The `$k = 1$` Baseline for RFT (Figure 4, Table 5):** RFT with `$k = 1$` represents minimal augmentation—only a single generated sample per question, which, after correctness filtering and possible deduplication, adds very few novel reasoning paths. For LLaMA-7B, RFT k=1 achieves 37.6% vs. SFT at 35.9%, a +1.7 point improvement. This establishes a baseline for what RFT contributes beyond SFT when augmentation is minimal and confirms that the method provides gains even at low sampling budgets.

**Model-Scale Boundary (65B and 70B results, Table 5):** The inclusion of 65B and 70B models serves as a robustness check on the claim that RFT benefits are model-quality-dependent. For these largest models, RFT-U13B provides zero or negative improvement over SFT (LLaMA-65B: 59.3% SFT vs. 59.0% RFT-U13B; LLaMA2-70B: 63.2% vs. 62.3%). This confirms that the benefit of augmentation does not simply scale with model size but rather diminishes as pre-training quality improves, eventually reaching zero.

**Self-Query Augmentation and Self-Revising Augmentation (Appendix D, Figure 8):** These negative results—where alternative augmentation strategies either degrade performance or provide only marginal gains—serve as implicit ablations validating RFT's design. Self-query augmentation produces results that are "the worst" and underperforms standard SFT. Self-revising augmentation provides at most +0.19 points improvement (35.90% → 36.09% for LLaMA-7B at `$K = 1$`) and degrades as `$K$` increases. The only revision variant that outperforms SFT is the Levenshtein-distance-based path selection, which provides uniformly better results across K but still with modest absolute gains. These negative results demonstrate that simply generating more data is insufficient—the data must be both correct and structurally diverse, as RFT's rejection sampling + equation-based deduplication provides.

**Training Epoch Count for Downsampled Data (Appendix A.2, Table 5):** For SFT on small data fractions, the paper finds that the standard 3-epoch training produces "very poor results." The reported numbers use a search over {3, 3/(data fraction)} epochs and take the better result. This is explicitly noted as a practical experimental detail, not an ablation, but it affects the reliability of the downsampling results: if the epoch search were not conducted, the apparent scaling behavior with data quantity would be confounded by undertraining on small fractions. The fact that some very small fractions (1/32) still show low performance despite the epoch search (e.g., 7.8% for LLaMA-7B at 3 epochs vs. ~9.5% at 96 epochs) indicates that extremely small datasets may be fundamentally insufficient regardless of training duration.

### Critical Assessment

The paper's central claims, as outlined in the executive summary and Figure 1, are examined against the experimental evidence below. I assess each claim's support, identify what was and was not tested, and note where the experiments demonstrate something narrower than what is claimed.

**Claim 1: "Pre-training loss is a better indicator of the model's performance than the model's parameter count."**

**What was tested:** Figure 2 plots SFT and ICL accuracy against pre-training loss for models across GPT-3, LLaMA, LLaMA2, and GPT-4 (as a reference point). The visual correlation is clear and spans model families. The paper's specific evidence that parameter count is insufficient comes from the observation that "LLaMA outperforms GPT-3" on SFT accuracy (LLaMA-7B at 35.9% vs. GPT-3 175B at 34.0%) despite having 25× fewer parameters.

**Assessment:** The claim is qualitatively supported but the evidence is incomplete in several ways. First, the paper does not actually plot accuracy against parameter count to show that loss is *better*—it only shows that loss *works* as a predictor. A direct comparison (e.g., R² for loss vs. R² for log-parameters in predicting SFT accuracy) would strengthen the claim. Second, the pre-training losses are from different corpora and tokenizers, which the paper acknowledges: "pre-training losses correspond to different pre-training datasets and different tokenizers which means they could not be compared strictly." This caveat is significant—the cross-family comparison may conflate genuine model quality differences with tokenizer efficiency differences (models with more efficient tokenizers can achieve lower loss on the same underlying text). Third, the linear relationship is only observed in a specific loss interval (~1.50–1.90) and must break at extremes (accuracy is bounded), but the paper does not characterize when linearity breaks down. Fourth, the sample size is small (7 distinct models plotted, some with estimated losses). This is a suggestive correlation, not a well-characterized functional relationship. The claim is best understood as an empirical diagnostic that works within the studied regime, not as a universal law.

**Claim 2: "SFT improves in a log-linear manner with the increase of supervised data amount, and the benefits of increasing data amount diminish as the pre-trained model gets better."**

**What was tested:** Figure 3 plots SFT accuracy against data fraction (log scale) for LLaMA and LLaMA2 at 7B and 13B scales. The points approximately align along straight lines in log-space. The slopes are visibly shallower for lower-loss models.

**Assessment:** The log-linear pattern is visible but the evidence is less rigorous than it could be. The paper does not report regression fits, R² values, or confidence intervals. The data points for very small fractions (1/32, 1/16) use a different number of training epochs than the standard 3-epoch protocol (searched over two options), which means they are not sampled from identical training procedures—the data-quantity signal is confounded with training-duration optimization. The claim about diminishing benefits for better models is robust across the four plotted models and consistent with the RFT results, so this aspect is well-supported even if the exact functional form of the scaling relationship is not rigorously characterized. A stronger test would include intermediate model sizes (e.g., 1.3B, 3B, 6B from the LLaMA family) to establish the monotonicity of the slope-pretraining-loss relationship, but the four-model comparison is sufficient to establish the qualitative pattern.

**Claim 3: "RFT improves mathematical reasoning performance more for LLMs, and the key factor influencing RFT performance is the distinct reasoning path amount which can be increased by sampling more times or combining samples from multiple models."**

**What was tested:** Table 1 and Figure 4 compare RFT k=100 to SFT across five model sizes, and Table 2 tracks distinct reasoning path counts across `$k$` and models. Figure 5 compares single-model RFT to multi-model RFT.

**Assessment:** The evidence for this claim is the strongest in the paper. Three converging lines of evidence support the distinct-paths-as-driver hypothesis: (1) the "no dedup" condition (Figure 4) achieves similar performance to the deduplicated version despite ~10× more samples, showing raw sample count is not the driver; (2) distinct path count grows sublinearly with `$k$` (Table 2) and RFT performance gains diminish correspondingly; (3) multi-model aggregation (Figure 5) increases distinct path count to 12.84 per question (from ~5.25 for single models) and yields substantial additional performance gains. The failure case—LLaMA-33B, which produces many correct paths (88.7) but few distinct ones (2.78) and shows essentially zero RFT improvement—provides a clean natural experiment that isolates diversity from correctness.

However, the evidence supports a more specific claim than the paper's broader framing. The paper demonstrates that distinct path count within the specific operationalization (equation-list-based deduplication) predicts performance. Whether this operationalization captures all relevant diversity—or whether other definitions of "distinctness" would yield different scaling behavior—is not tested. The paper also does not disentangle the effects of distinct path count from total supervised sample count in a controlled way (e.g., by fixing distinct path count and varying total samples, or vice versa). The "no dedup" comparison is suggestive but not perfectly controlled, since the no-dedup condition includes ~10× more samples with the *same* distinct paths plus additional near-duplicates.

**Claim 4: "The combination of rejection sampling samples from multiple models further enhances the RFT performance, resulting in an accuracy of 49.3 for LLaMA-7B (+13.4 compared to SFT)."**

**What was tested:** Figure 5 and Table 5 show RFT-U13B results across model scales. The 49.3% number for LLaMA-7B is clearly the headline result, representing a 13.4-point gain over SFT.

**Assessment:** The result is numerically impressive and well-documented. The mechanism—multi-model aggregation increases distinct reasoning path diversity (12.84 vs. 5.25 per question) by covering complementary reasoning strategies from differently-trained models—is supported by the Venn diagram in Figure 6. The finding that adding 33B samples (U33B) provides essentially zero additional benefit further supports the mechanism: the 33B model contributes only 6.5% unique paths.

However, the claim's framing as "enhances the RFT performance" is somewhat misleading as a method contribution. Multi-model aggregation works because *different models of different sizes and training runs produce different reasoning strategies*. This is a finding about model behavior (incomplete overlap in the reasoning strategies learned by different training runs), not a novel method. The practical cost of multi-model RFT is also higher than single-model RFT—it requires sampling from four different SFT models—and this cost is noted in Table 4 but not prominently discussed in the performance claims. Additionally, the comparison to prior methods (Table 3) uses different base models (GPT-J-6B vs. LLaMA-7B; GPT-Neo-2.7B vs. LLaMA-7B), so the claim that RFT achieves "better performance than two recent state-of-the-art reasoning augmentation methods" is partially confounded by base model quality differences.

**Claim 5: "RFT brings more improvement for less performant LLMs."**

**What was tested:** Table 1 shows RFT k=100 improvements of +5.8 (LLaMA-7B, highest loss), +5.9 (LLaMA2-7B), +6.1 (LLaMA-13B), +4.8 (LLaMA2-13B), and −0.1 (LLaMA-33B, lowest loss). Figure 5 shows RFT-U13B improvements of +13.4 (7B), +8.7 (7B-2), +9.1 (13B), +5.4 (13B-2), +1.9 (33B), −0.3 (65B), −0.9 (70B).

**Assessment:** The monotonic decline in benefit with model quality is strongly supported by the data. However, the 7B-to-13B comparison introduces a confound: 7B and 13B models differ not only in pre-training loss but also in parameter count. The paper's narrative attributes the declining benefit to pre-training quality ("better models benefit less"), but parameter count is an alternative explanation: larger models have more capacity to absorb the original SFT data, leaving less room for augmentation to improve. The paper partially addresses this confound by comparing LLaMA-7B (loss 1.80) to LLaMA2-7B (loss 1.75, same parameters)—the LLaMA2-7B shows slightly smaller RFT-U13B gain (+8.7 vs. +13.4), which supports the loss-based explanation. But the dramatic drop from 13B (+9.1 for LLaMA) to 33B (+1.9) conflates a large loss decrease with a large parameter increase, making it impossible to cleanly attribute the decline to one factor versus the other.

An experiment that would strengthen this claim is comparing the same architecture trained to different loss values (e.g., intermediate checkpoints of the same model) to isolate loss from parameter count. This experiment is not run.

**Claim 6: "The most important thing is to pre-train a better language model towards excellent math reasoning abilities."**

**What was tested:** The paper demonstrates that lower pre-training loss correlates with higher SFT and ICL accuracy (Figure 2), that better models benefit less from both SFT data scaling (Figure 3) and RFT (Table 1, Figure 5), and that for 65B and 70B models, RFT provides zero or negative improvement (Table 5). The cost analysis (Table 4) shows that pre-training is ~10⁴–10⁵× more expensive than RFT in FLOPs, but RFT's effectiveness diminishes to zero while pre-training improvements continue to yield gains.

**Assessment:** The claim is supported as a high-level strategic conclusion: at the capability frontier, pre-training quality is the primary bottleneck, and augmentation cannot substitute indefinitely. However, the paper does not provide a direct FLOPs-matched comparison of pre-training vs. augmentation (unlike the scaling laws literature or the test-time compute paper in the summary example), which weakens the prescriptive force of the claim. The paper's cost data shows that moving from LLaMA-7B to LLaMA2-7B requires ~4.2 × 10²² additional pre-training FLOPs and yields +2.1 points in RFT-U33B accuracy. RFT-U13B on LLaMA-7B costs ~10¹⁸ FLOPs and yields +13.4 points. This suggests that, at least for 7B-scale models, augmentation provides dramatically better FLOPs-normalized returns than improved pre-training. The "most important thing is pre-training" conclusion reflects an asymptotic argument (you can't augment your way past the capability ceiling of the base model) rather than a FLOPs-efficiency argument for practitioners with fixed budgets. The paper does not fully reconcile these two perspectives.

**Genuine weaknesses in experimental design:**

- **Single benchmark:** All experiments are on GSM8K. The paper provides no evidence that the scaling relationships (log-linear SFT scaling, diminishing RFT returns with model quality, distinct-paths-as-driver) generalize to other math reasoning benchmarks (e.g., MATH, SVAMP, MultiArith) or other reasoning domains entirely. GSM8K has specific characteristics—grade-school arithmetic, relatively narrow problem types, single-step answer extraction—that may not represent mathematical reasoning more broadly.
- **Test set size of 1,319 questions:** While larger than some reasoning benchmarks, this test set provides limited statistical resolution for comparing models that differ by 1–2 percentage points. The paper does not report confidence intervals, standard errors, or statistical significance tests for any comparisons. Several important comparisons (e.g., U13B vs. U33B, dedup vs. no dedup for some models) involve differences of <2 points, which may not be statistically reliable.
- **No explicit regression or quantitative scaling law fitting:** The paper describes relationships as "log-linear," "linear," or "sub-log-linear" but does not fit explicit functional forms or report goodness-of-fit measures. This leaves the claimed relationships qualitatively suggestive rather than quantitatively characterized. For a paper whose title promises "scaling relationship," the absence of fitted scaling law parameters is a notable gap.
- **Ghost of the epoch count search confound:** The SFT downsampling experiments use different epoch counts for different data fractions (3 epochs for larger fractions, up to 96 epochs for 1/32 fraction). This means the data points in Figure 3 do not represent pure data-quantity scaling—they conflate data quantity with training duration optimization. The paper's log-linear conclusion could be partly an artifact of the epoch search (which allows smaller fractions to partially compensate through more training iterations) rather than a pure property of data scaling. An experiment that fixes total optimization steps (varying data while keeping batch size × steps constant) would isolate data quantity from training duration, but this was not run.
- **Temperature sensitivity not systematically studied:** The paper uses temperature 0.7 for rejection sampling following Cobbe et al. (2021) but notes that for LLaMA-33B, temperature 1.0 produces 4.77 distinct paths per question vs. 2.78 at temperature 0.7. This is a 72% increase in diversity from a single hyperparameter change. The paper acknowledges that "there should be a temperate (or generation config) that can produce more distinct paths and generate good results for RFT in 33B and even larger models while it does need more computation resources for inference." This means the observed failure of RFT for large models might be partially a temperature-tuning artifact rather than a fundamental limitation—a different temperature could potentially generate sufficient diversity to make RFT work for 33B+ models. The paper does not run a systematic temperature sweep, so this hypothesis remains untested.
- **Multi-model aggregation uses models that were all fine-tuned on the same GSM8K data:** The four models contributing to U13B (LLaMA-7B, LLaMA2-7B, LLaMA-13B, LLaMA2-13B) differ in pre-training but all underwent SFT on the identical GSM8K training set. Their reasoning path diversity may partly reflect random seed effects or subtle pre-training differences rather than fundamentally complementary reasoning capabilities. An experiment that aggregates samples from models fine-tuned on different (possibly disjoint) subsets of the data, or from models with different fine-tuning hyperparameters, would test whether diversity arises from difference in training experience rather than from inherent model differences.
- **The 33B model result may not generalize to all large models:** The paper attributes the 33B model's poor RFT performance to overfitting the training set (manifested as low distinct path diversity). This is a property of the specific 33B model trained on the specific dataset, not necessarily a general property of all sufficiently large models. A 33B model trained with different hyperparameters (lower learning rate, more regularization, longer training with early stopping) might show different overfitting behavior. The paper does not explore whether this overfitting is contingent on training choices or is a fundamental property of the model scale.

**Missing experiments that would strengthen the paper:**

- **FLOPs-matched pretraining vs. augmentation comparison:** The paper argues that pre-training is ultimately more important but provides cost data showing augmentation is ~10⁴× cheaper. A controlled experiment comparing, say, a 7B model with heavy augmentation against a 13B model without augmentation (matched for total FLOPs including pre-training) would directly test whether pre-training investment beats augmentation investment.
- **Difficulty-stratified analysis:** The paper does not analyze whether SFT scaling, RFT benefit, or distinct path diversity effects vary by problem difficulty. If easy problems saturate quickly while hard problems continue to benefit from augmentation, the aggregate numbers might obscure important heterogeneity—analogous to the difficulty-dependent findings in the test-time compute paper summarized in the example.
- **Ablation on the deduplication criterion:** The paper uses equation-list equality as the deduplication criterion. Alternative criteria (e.g., final answer computation steps, semantic similarity of reasoning, abstract computation graph isomorphism) could yield different distinct path counts and different RFT performance. An ablation comparing equation-based deduplication to semantic deduplication or to random subsampling would test whether the specific diversity operationalization matters or whether any diversity-maximizing filtering would work.
- **Transfer to other math reasoning benchmarks:** Evaluating the RFT-augmented models on MATH, SVAMP, or MultiArith would test whether the diversity-driven improvements transfer or are GSM8K-specific. Since GSM8K is the augmentation source, there is risk of overfitting to the benchmark's specific reasoning patterns.
- **Inference-time diversity analysis:** The paper shows (Figure 7) that RFT-U13B trained models generate more diverse reasoning paths at inference time (more distinct calculation processes per question) than SFT or single-model RFT models. This is presented as a histogram of per-question distinct path counts. A quantitative correlation between training-time distinct path diversity and inference-time answer diversity (and the relationship of both to accuracy) would strengthen the causal argument that training diversity drives generalization.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Cost of Diversity Estimation Is Not Accounted for in the Headline Gains

**The assumption or constraint:** RFT's effectiveness depends critically on the availability of structurally diverse reasoning paths for each training question. However, the paper provides no method for estimating diversity *before* running the full RFT pipeline—the number of distinct reasoning paths per question (Table 2) is only measurable after generating and deduplicating `k` samples. This means practitioners must commit to the generation cost before knowing whether it will yield sufficient diversity for RFT to help. The paper acknowledges this obliquely in the context of larger models:

> "There may be a generation config or training config for large enough LMs not to overfit on the training dataset while it is not trivial to find them."

More fundamentally, the paper's difficulty estimation for whether RFT will work on a given model requires running rejection sampling at scale first.

**The consequence:** In a deployment setting, the decision to apply RFT to a new model involves a gamble: invest the inference budget to generate `k` samples per question, deduplicate them, and only then discover whether the distinct path count is high enough to justify RFT training. For models like LLaMA-33B (which yields only 2.78 distinct paths per question at `k = 100`, Table 2), the entire generation investment produces negligible training benefit. The paper's cost analysis (Table 4) shows that RFT inference for a 33B model at `k = 100` costs ~1.8 × 10¹⁹ FLOPs and ~4,500 GPU-hours—a substantial waste if the resulting diversity proves insufficient. There is no early-stopping or progressive assessment mechanism that would allow aborting the process cheaply once low diversity is detected.

**What evidence exists in the paper:** The 33B failure case (Table 1, Table 2) is the clearest evidence: for `k = 100`, the 33B SFT model generated 88.7 correct paths per question on average but only 2.78 distinct paths after deduplication—a 97% redundancy rate. The RFT accuracy for LLaMA-33B was 54.5%, essentially flat from SFT at 54.6%. The cost of discovering this null result was approximately 4,500 GPU-hours. The paper does not measure or discuss the ratio of wasted-to-useful generation for any model.

**Mitigation status:** Not addressed. The paper does not propose predictors of diversity that could be computed cheaply (e.g., from a small pilot sample, from model confidence metrics, or from pre-training loss thresholds). The relationship between pre-training loss and generated diversity is documented but is purely descriptive and post-hoc—there is no actionable decision rule provided (e.g., "models with loss above threshold `X` should use RFT; below, skip it"). The temperature observation for LLaMA-33B (temperature 1.0 yields 4.77 distinct paths vs. 2.78 at temperature 0.7) suggests that generation hyperparameters can significantly affect diversity, but the paper does not provide a systematic methodology for finding diversity-maximizing configurations without full-scale sampling.

---

### Single Benchmark, Single Task Family: No Evidence of Generality

**The assumption or constraint:** All experiments in the paper use the GSM8K benchmark exclusively—7,473 training questions and 1,319 test questions, all comprising grade-school math word problems requiring multi-step arithmetic reasoning. The paper's claims about scaling relationships (log-linear SFT scaling, distinct reasoning paths as the driver of RFT improvement, diminishing returns with better pre-trained models) are derived entirely from this single dataset. The paper does not claim generality beyond GSM8K, but the abstract and introduction frame the findings in terms of "mathematical reasoning" broadly, without restricting to grade-school arithmetic:

> "we investigate how the pre-training loss, supervised data amount, and augmented data amount influence the reasoning performances of a supervised LLM."

**The consequence:** GSM8K has specific properties that may not generalize to other mathematical reasoning tasks or other reasoning domains. Its problems are relatively short (average ~66 tokens for the question, ~130 for the reasoning path), are templated in structure (narrative word problems with a single numerical answer), draw from a limited set of arithmetic operations, and have exact-match answer verification. The finding that equation-list-based deduplication effectively captures reasoning diversity may not transfer to domains where reasoning paths cannot be decomposed into discrete equation steps—such as geometric reasoning, proof generation, or multi-step logical deduction in natural language. The log-linear scaling of SFT with data amount may saturate differently on more difficult benchmarks (e.g., MATH from Hendrycks et al., 2021, where problems require significantly more complex reasoning). The "diminishing returns with better models" pattern may interact with problem difficulty in ways that GSM8K alone cannot reveal—perhaps better models *do* benefit from RFT on tasks where their pre-training has not already saturated relevant reasoning patterns.

**What evidence exists in the paper:** Zero. The paper does not evaluate any model or method on any benchmark other than GSM8K. There is no transfer experiment (e.g., training on GSM8K augmented data and evaluating on SVAMP or MultiArith to test whether diversity-driven improvements generalize to related but distinct problem distributions). There is no difficulty-stratified analysis *within* GSM8K that would test whether easy and hard GSM8K problems show different scaling behavior (the paper's approach to problem variation is entirely at the level of distinct reasoning paths, not problem difficulty). The paper makes no claim about, and provides no evidence for, the generality of its findings.

**Mitigation status:** Not addressed. The paper does not discuss the single-benchmark limitation, does not suggest future work on cross-benchmark validation, and does not qualify its claims with benchmark-specific caveats. A reader interested in applying RFT to code generation, scientific reasoning, or even higher-difficulty math benchmarks (MATH, AMC) has no guidance from this paper on whether the scaling relationships will hold. This is a significant practical gap, because the paper's central resource-allocation argument—that RFT is cheap and should be applied by default—depends implicitly on the assumption that the diversity-driven benefit observed on GSM8K will materialize on other tasks. If RFT provides zero benefit on MATH (where reasoning path diversity may matter less or more, or where correctness filtering is harder), the "always apply RFT" decision rule breaks.

---

### Multi-Model Aggregation Requires Access to Multiple Trained SFT Models, Not Just a Single Base Model

**The assumption or constraint:** The paper's strongest results—LLaMA-7B reaching 49.3% with RFT-U13B, a +13.4 point gain over SFT—depend on multi-model aggregation: combining rejection samples from four separately trained SFT models (LLaMA-7B, LLaMA2-7B, LLaMA-13B, and LLaMA2-13B). This assumes the practitioner has access to, or can produce, multiple distinct supervised models of varying sizes and training runs. The paper does not explore whether the same diversity can be achieved from a single model by varying generation hyperparameters (temperature, nucleus sampling parameters, prompt formats) or training configurations (different random seeds, different data subsets, different learning rates).

**The consequence:** The computational and engineering cost of RFT-U13B is substantially higher than single-model RFT. Producing the U13B dataset requires: (1) pre-training or acquiring four distinct base models, (2) running SFT on each separately (four fine-tuning jobs), (3) running rejection sampling at `k = 100` from each of the four SFT models (four inference jobs, each generating 7,473 × 100 = 747,300 reasoning paths), and (4) aggregating and deduplicating across all four pools. The GPU-hour cost in Table 4 reflects this: RFT inference for U33B (which subsumes U13B work) totals ~3.0 × 10¹⁸ FLOPs for the 7B target model, with GPU hours for inference alone ranging from 10 (7B SFT model) to 4,500 (33B SFT model) depending on which models contribute samples. For a practitioner with access to only a single base model—a common scenario in industry where models are licensed or served through APIs—the multi-model aggregation strategy is infeasible, and the performance ceiling is the single-model RFT k=100 result (e.g., 41.7% for LLaMA-7B rather than 49.3%). The paper's headline numbers thus represent an upper bound that requires infrastructure and model access beyond what many deployment scenarios provide.

**What evidence exists in the paper:** The single-model RFT results in Table 1 show that the gain from RFT k=100 alone is +5.8 points for LLaMA-7B (35.9% → 41.7%). The multi-model RFT-U13B provides an additional +7.6 points (41.7% → 49.3%)—meaning roughly 57% of the total RFT gain over SFT comes from multi-model aggregation, not from the base RFT method. The Venn diagram in Figure 6 quantifies the complementarity: each 7B/13B model contributes roughly 15% unique reasoning paths to the combined pool. The paper does not report single-model RFT at `k = 400` (matching the total sampling budget of U13B but from a single model), which would test whether the diversity gains from multi-model aggregation could be matched by simply sampling more from one model. The sublinear growth of distinct paths with `k` in Table 2 (e.g., for LLaMA-7B, distinct paths increase from 5.25 at `k = 100` to likely well below 12.84 at any feasible `k`) suggests they could not, but this is not experimentally verified.

**Mitigation status:** Not addressed. The paper does not discuss the practical requirements for multi-model aggregation, does not explore cheaper alternatives (single-model with hyperparameter variation), and does not provide guidance on the minimum number of contributing models needed to achieve meaningful diversity gains. The paper's cost narrative ("SFT and RFT are negligible compared to pre-training") is true per-model but understates the multiplicative cost of running the pipeline for four separate models. The suggestion that "one can always use SFT and RFT to improve models' performance" (Section 4.2) applies to single-model RFT, but the paper's strongest results require more than "always available" infrastructure.

### No Confidence Intervals or Statistical Significance Testing for Any Claimed Comparison

**The assumption or constraint:** The paper reports all results as point estimates—single accuracy percentages on the 1,319-question GSM8K test set—without any measure of uncertainty. There are no confidence intervals, standard errors, statistical significance tests, or even train/test split variation reported. All comparisons between methods (SFT vs. RFT, RFT k=1 vs. k=100, single-model vs. multi-model RFT, different model scales) are interpreted based on the raw point estimate differences, with no assessment of whether those differences are statistically reliable.

**The consequence:** Several of the paper's key claims rest on accuracy differences that may not be statistically distinguishable given the test set size. For example, the claim that RFT with deduplication is comparable to RFT without deduplication relies on differences like 43.6% (no dedup) vs. 41.7% (dedup) for LLaMA-7B—a 1.9-point gap. With 1,319 test questions, a 1.9-point difference represents approximately 25 questions. Whether this difference would replicate on a different train/test split or is within the noise floor of model training variance is unknown. Similarly, the claim that RFT-U33B provides no benefit over RFT-U13B rests on comparisons like LLaMA-7B: 49.3% (U13B) vs. 49.1% (U33B)—a 0.2-point difference (~3 questions). The paper interprets this as "including D'33B in aggregation barely influences the performance," but with no variance estimates, this could equally be interpreted as sampling noise.

More concerning, the SFT downsampling experiments (Figure 3) use an epoch count search per data fraction (3 epochs vs. 3/(data fraction) epochs) and select the better result. This introduces an optimization bias: the reported accuracy for each data fraction is the maximum of (at least) two training runs, which inflates the apparent scaling curve relative to what a practitioner would achieve with a fixed training protocol. The extent of this inflation cannot be assessed because only the winning result is reported, not the distribution of outcomes across the two epoch settings.

**What evidence exists in the paper:** The absence of uncertainty reporting is systematic and acknowledged nowhere in the paper. The test set size is stated (1,319 questions), and the training set size is stated (7,473 questions), but the statistical implications of these sizes for the reliability of reported differences are not discussed. The paper does not report results across multiple random seeds for SFT or RFT training, does not use cross-validation for hyperparameter selection or evaluation, and does not provide error bars on any figure. The two-fold cross-validation mentioned in some experimental descriptions (e.g., for self-revising augmentation in Appendix D.2) is limited to that specific negative-result experiment and is not used in the main results.

**Mitigation status:** Not addressed. The paper provides no discussion of statistical methodology, no rationale for why point estimates without uncertainty are sufficient, and no suggestion that future work should address reliability. This is a significant weakness for a paper whose primary contribution is empirical scaling characterization. Scaling relationships are only actionable if the fitted slopes can be trusted—without variance estimates, a practitioner cannot determine whether the log-linear SFT scaling claim (which is used to extrapolate what would happen with more supervised data) is a robust regularity or a fragile fit to a specific downsampling of a specific dataset.

### Large Models' Failure to Benefit From RFT May Be a Hyperparameter Artifact, Not a Fundamental Capability Ceiling

**The assumption or constraint:** The paper's central narrative arc—that RFT benefits diminish as pre-training loss decreases, reaching zero for sufficiently capable models—rests heavily on the LLaMA-33B, 65B, and 70B results. For 33B, RFT k=100 provides essentially zero improvement (54.6% → 54.5%). For 65B and 70B, RFT-U13B provides zero or negative improvement (LLaMA-65B: 59.3% → 59.0%; LLaMA2-70B: 63.2% → 62.3%). The paper attributes this to insufficient diversity in the generated reasoning paths: the 33B model produces only 2.78 distinct paths per question at temperature 0.7. However, the paper itself provides evidence that this diversity collapse is temperature-dependent:

> "We have tried using a larger temperate 1.0 for decoding LLaMA-33B-SFT models, it generates 82.4 correct paths and 4.77 distinct paths per question which is more diverse than using temperate 0.7 but still less diverse than 7B and 13B models."

**The consequence:** The 33B model's distinct path count nearly doubles (2.78 → 4.77) simply by raising temperature from 0.7 to 1.0. At 4.77 distinct paths, the 33B model would have diversity approaching that of the 7B and 13B models (~5.2 distinct paths), which yielded +5–6 point RFT improvements at `k = 100`. The paper does not report RFT results for 33B using temperature 1.0 or any other temperature, nor does it conduct a systematic temperature sweep for any model. This means the observed failure of RFT for large models is confounded with the choice of sampling temperature—a hyperparameter that was fixed at 0.7 based on prior work (Cobbe et al., 2021) without verification that it is appropriate for larger models or for diversity-maximizing generation.

Furthermore, the paper notes that LLaMA-33B at temperature 0.7 produces 88.7 correct paths per question on average (Table 1)—a very high correctness rate that suggests the model is sampling near its mode. Higher temperatures would trade some correctness for greater diversity, potentially pushing the distinct-path count into the regime where RFT becomes effective. The paper acknowledges this possibility ("there should be a temperate or generation config that can produce more distinct paths and generate good results for RFT in 33B and even larger models") but treats it as a computational burden rather than a limitation of the central claim.

**What evidence exists in the paper:** The temperature 1.0 result for LLaMA-33B is mentioned in passing in Section 3.3, with the numbers 82.4 correct paths and 4.77 distinct paths per question. No RFT training is reported for this condition. No other model sizes have temperature variation reported, despite the paper using a single temperature (0.7) for all RFT experiments. The cost of a temperature sweep is cited as prohibitive ("We admit there should be a temperate... while it does need more computation resources for inference compared to sampling using 7B and 13B models"), but this is a practical constraint, not evidence that the null result at temperature 0.7 represents a fundamental capability limit.

**Mitigation status:** Not addressed. The paper's conclusion that "better models benefit less from RFT" and that "pre-training is all you need" for large models is stated without qualification, despite the temperature evidence suggesting that the failure of RFT for 33B+ models may be a generation-hyperparameter artifact rather than an inherent property of model scale. A practitioner reading the paper would reasonably conclude that RFT is not worth attempting on models above 13B parameters; the paper provides no guidance on whether temperature tuning could change that assessment. This is a significant practical limitation because the decision of whether to invest in augmentation vs. pretraining for a large model deployment hinges on whether the "RFT doesn't work at scale" claim is robust or merely a consequence of the specific generation configuration used.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper changes the conversation around improving LLM math reasoning from a **method-centric to a data-centric diagnostic** orientation. The field has invested heavily in developing ever-more-sophisticated training and inference techniques—process reward models, Monte Carlo Tree Search, reinforcement learning, verifier ensembling—with each new method evaluated primarily by whether it outperforms prior methods on benchmark accuracy. This paper steps back from that arms race to ask a different question: given a fixed pre-trained model and a fixed human-annotated dataset, how much improvement can you expect from simply augmenting training data through the model's own generations, and what governs that improvement?

The conceptual reframing is that **reasoning path diversity, not raw sample quantity, is the bottleneck variable** in data augmentation for mathematical reasoning. This is not an incremental finding—it recasts augmentation from a sampling problem (how do I generate more correct solutions?) to a coverage problem (how do I generate structurally novel solution strategies?). The evidence that deduplicated RFT with ~5 distinct paths per question matches or exceeds the performance of undeduplicated RFT with ~53 samples per question (Figure 4, Table 5) demonstrates that the model saturates on repeated instances of the same reasoning pattern and that **only structurally novel reasoning paths provide marginal training signal**. This finding suggests that many prior augmentation methods—which focused on generating more samples, filtering for correctness, or optimizing the generation process—may have been operating on the wrong axis. The returns from those investments are bounded by the diversity ceiling of the sampling model, not by the sophistication of the filtering or generation algorithm.

The paper also provides a **unifying diagnostic for model-quality-dependent benefit from supervision**. Prior work offered conflicting guidance: larger models are more sample-efficient at learning from a few examples (the few-shot learning literature) but this paper shows they benefit *less* from large supervised datasets than smaller models (Figure 3, Table 5). These are not contradictory—they describe different regions of the data-quantity curve—but the field lacked a framework for reconciling them. The paper's demonstration that the slope of the SFT log-linear scaling curve decreases monotonically with pre-training loss (Figure 3) provides this framework: better models are closer to their performance ceiling given available reasoning patterns, so adding supervised data (whether human-annotated or model-generated) provides diminishing marginal return. This reframes the "bigger models need less data" intuition into a more precise claim: bigger (specifically, better-pre-trained) models *saturate faster* on additional reasoning demonstrations.

The practical consequence for resource allocation is a decision rule that the field had not previously articulated clearly: **for any pre-trained model below a certain capability threshold, data augmentation through rejection sampling is so cheap (~10⁻⁴ the FLOPs of pre-training, Table 4) that it should be applied by default before considering expensive pre-training improvements.** This is not a recommendation to abandon pre-training—the paper is explicit that pre-training loss is the fundamental performance driver and that augmentation effectiveness drops to zero for sufficiently capable models (65B, 70B in Table 5). Rather, it establishes a two-phase strategy: use cheap augmentation to extract near-maximum performance from whatever pre-trained model you have, and invest pre-training compute only when augmentation returns have been exhausted. This is a practical reframing that changes how organizations should sequence their ML investments.

The work also resolves a latent tension in the self-training and self-improvement literature. Methods like STaR (Zelikman et al., 2022) and ReST^EM (Singh et al., 2024) showed that models can improve from their own generations, but the mechanisms and boundary conditions were unclear. This paper identifies **reasoning path diversity as the specific mechanism**—not just exposure to correct answers, but exposure to structurally distinct ways of arriving at them—and **model overfitting as the specific failure mode** that prevents large models from benefiting. The 33B model's inability to generate diverse reasoning paths (2.78 distinct paths vs. 5.25 for 7B models at k=100, Table 2) explains why prior work may have found diminishing or negative returns from self-training on larger models. This transforms self-training from a "try it and see" heuristic into a diagnostically grounded practice: measure generation diversity first, and only apply self-training if diversity exceeds a threshold.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Direct measurement of the diversity ceiling as a function of model scale and training data.** The paper documents that LLaMA-33B produces only 2.78 distinct reasoning paths per GSM8K question at temperature 0.7 (Table 2), compared to ~5.25 for 7B and 13B models, and suggests that temperature tuning can partially recover diversity (4.77 at temperature 1.0). A systematic study that measures distinct reasoning path count as a function of model scale (from 1B to 70B+), temperature, nucleus sampling parameters, and training data size would characterize the **diversity collapse curve**—the point at which models stop generating novel solution strategies and merely reproduce memorized patterns. This is directly enabled by the paper's equation-based deduplication method (Algorithm 1), which provides an operational diversity metric. A strong follow-up would sweep Llama-2 and Llama-3 model scales on multiple math benchmarks (GSM8K, MATH, SVAMP), measure distinct path count per question at varying sampling budgets, and test whether the diversity ceiling is a function of model parameters, pre-training data quantity, or both. The practical output would be a predictive model: given model size and benchmark, estimate the maximum achievable reasoning path diversity and thus the expected benefit from RFT before running generation.

**Temperature-tuned RFT for large models to resolve whether the null result is fundamental or a hyperparameter artifact.** The paper's most consequential finding for practitioners—that RFT provides zero benefit for models above 33B parameters—rests on experiments at a single temperature (0.7). The paper's own evidence that temperature 1.0 nearly doubles the 33B model's distinct path count (2.78 → 4.77, Section 3.3) suggests that the null result may be a generation-configuration artifact. A direct follow-up experiment would run RFT on LLaMA-33B, 65B, and 70B using rejection sampling at multiple temperatures (0.3, 0.5, 0.7, 0.9, 1.0, 1.2) and measure both distinct path counts and downstream RFT accuracy. If a higher temperature recovers diversity to near-7B levels (~5 distinct paths) and RFT yields the expected ~5–6 point improvement, then the paper's "diminishing returns with model quality" claim needs qualification: the returns diminish only when generation configuration is fixed, not when it is optimized for diversity. If, conversely, even temperature-optimized large models cannot generate sufficient diversity to benefit from RFT, then the paper's claim is confirmed as a fundamental scaling property rather than a hyperparameter artifact. This experiment would cost more than the paper's reported compute (since it requires multiple full RFT pipelines), but it directly addresses the paper's most uncertain conclusion and is therefore high-value.

**Diversity-aware curriculum design for self-training loops.** The paper shows that RFT benefits derive from structurally distinct reasoning paths, but the RFT process is uniform—all distinct paths are included without regard for their difficulty, abstractness, or complementarity. A natural extension is to design a **diversity-aware curriculum** for self-training: cluster the generated reasoning paths by their abstract computational structure (e.g., the computation graph or the dependency structure of equations), and add paths to the training set in order of decreasing structural novelty relative to what has already been added. This could achieve the same diversity benefit with fewer total samples by explicitly maximizing marginal structural information per added path, rather than relying on the implicit diversity accumulation from random deduplication. A strong experiment would compare: (1) standard RFT with equation-based deduplication, (2) RFT with random selection from the distinct-path pool (keeping sample count fixed to isolate diversity effects), and (3) RFT with a curriculum that orders paths by structural dissimilarity to the existing training set. If the curriculum matches or exceeds standard RFT performance with fewer samples, it would establish that diversity can be achieved more efficiently than the paper's all-samples-considered approach. The experiment would use the same GSM8K setup as the paper for comparability, with extension to MATH to test generalization of the curriculum concept to harder problems.

**Cross-benchmark transfer and the specificity of reasoning diversity.** All of the paper's experiments use GSM8K for both augmentation generation and evaluation. A critical open question is whether the diversity acquired through RFT on one math benchmark transfers to other math benchmarks or to unrelated reasoning tasks. The specific hypothesis is that exposure to structurally diverse reasoning paths teaches the model **generalizable reasoning strategies** (e.g., "solve for the unknown by working backwards," "decompose a multi-step problem into sub-goals") rather than just benchmark-specific heuristics. A direct test would RFT-train a LLaMA-7B model on GSM8K augmented data (RFT-U13B, achieving 49.3% on GSM8K) and evaluate it zero-shot on: (a) other math word problem benchmarks (SVAMP, MultiArith, ASDiv) to test within-domain transfer; (b) more difficult math benchmarks (MATH, GSM8K-hard) to test difficulty generalization; (c) non-math reasoning benchmarks (StrategyQA, Date Understanding, Coin Flip from BIG-bench) to test whether diverse arithmetic reasoning strategies incidentally improve other reasoning capabilities. If RFT improvements transfer to SVAMP and MultiArith but not to MATH or StrategyQA, that would characterize the boundary of what "reasoning diversity" provides—it would suggest the strategies learned are specific to the problem format (word problems with arithmetic solutions) rather than to abstract reasoning patterns. If transfer is broad, it would strengthen the paper's implicit claim that diversity-driven augmentation teaches genuine reasoning rather than pattern matching.

**FLOPs-matched pretraining vs. augmentation for mathematical reasoning.** The paper provides cost data (Table 4) showing that RFT costs ~10⁻⁴ the FLOPs of pre-training but offers essentially no direct comparison of where a fixed FLOP budget is better spent. A controlled FLOPs-matched experiment would compare: (a) LLaMA-7B with heavy augmentation (RFT-U13B, achieving 49.3% on GSM8K) against (b) a model trained from scratch with the same total FLOP budget—perhaps a LLaMA architecture at 8B or 9B parameters trained on the same number of tokens as LLaMA-7B (since the additional FLOPs are consumed by increased parameter count) and then SFT-ed on GSM8K without augmentation. The total FLOPs for (a) would include: LLaMA-7B pretraining (~4.2 × 10²²) + SFT (~1.7 × 10¹⁷) + RFT inference for U13B (~3.0 × 10¹⁸, covering four models) + RFT training (~negligible). The total FLOPs for (b) would be matched, with the pretraining compute increased to consume the FLOPs that (a) spent on augmentation. This experiment would directly answer the paper's implicit resource-allocation question: for a given total FLOP budget, is it better to train a slightly larger model or to augment a smaller one? The paper provides all the necessary cost numbers and baseline performances to make this comparison calculable, but does not run the experiment. Running it would transform the paper's qualitative cost narrative into a quantitative prescriptive tool.

**Why do different models generate complementary reasoning paths?** Figure 6 shows that each model (LLaMA-7B, LLaMA2-7B, LLaMA-13B, LLaMA2-13B) contributes roughly 15% unique reasoning paths to the U13B combined pool. This is a striking empirical regularity, but the paper provides no explanation for *why* models generate complementary strategies rather than converging on the same ones. Potential hypotheses include: (a) random initialization and training stochasticity cause models to settle in different regions of the solution space; (b) differences in pre-training data (LLaMA vs. LLaMA2) lead to different reasoning heuristics; (c) parameter count differences (7B vs. 13B) enable different levels of abstract reasoning; or (d) the deduplication algorithm itself amplifies apparent complementarity through its edit-distance refinement step (Algorithm 1). A follow-up study that isolates these factors would train multiple models from the *same* pre-trained checkpoint with different random seeds for SFT, measure the overlap in their generated reasoning paths (using the same Venn diagram methodology as Figure 6), and compare this to the cross-model complementarity observed in the paper. If different SFT seeds from the same base model produce complementary paths at similar rates to different base models, then the complementarity is primarily a stochastic training artifact rather than a pre-training-driven phenomenon—which would mean single-model RFT with multiple training runs could match multi-model RFT without requiring multiple base models. If complementarity requires pre-training differences, then multi-model RFT is genuinely necessary for diversity gains. This experiment directly addresses the multi-model aggregation cost concern identified in the limitations.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Cost-efficient fine-tuning of open-source LLMs for mathematical reasoning APIs.** The most immediate practical use case is for organizations deploying math reasoning capabilities through open-source models like Llama-2 or Llama-3. A team starting with a 7B or 13B base model and wanting to maximize single-inference GSM8K accuracy on a fixed budget can follow the paper's recipe directly: (1) SFT on available human-annotated data (costing ~0.6 GPU-hours, Table 4); (2) rejections sampling at k=100 from the resulting SFT model (~10 GPU-hours); (3) RFT training on the deduplicated augmented data (~negligible additional cost); (4) optionally, if multiple base model variants are available, aggregate rejection samples across them for an additional ~7–8 percentage point gain (e.g., LLaMA-7B going from 35.9% SFT to 41.7% single-model RFT to 49.3% RFT-U13B, Table 5). The total cost for steps (1)–(3) is ~10–11 GPU-hours on A100 hardware—negligible compared to any pre-training investment. For a production math reasoning API serving millions of queries, improving single-inference accuracy from 35.9% to 49.3% (a ~37% relative reduction in error rate) directly translates to higher customer satisfaction and potentially lower costs if the model can handle queries that would otherwise require expensive fallback to larger proprietary models.

**Data augmentation bootstrapping for domain-specific math tutoring systems.** Educational technology companies building math tutoring systems for specific curricula (e.g., state standards-aligned problem sets, corporate training assessments) often face the challenge that their target problems are similar in structure to public benchmarks like GSM8K but involve domain-specific terminology, constraints, or problem formats. The paper's RFT approach requires no human annotation beyond the initial SFT dataset and no training of auxiliary verifier models—it can be applied directly to a small collection of domain-specific training problems. A tutoring company with, say, 500 annotated problems for their specific curriculum could: (1) SFT a Llama-7B model on those 500 problems; (2) run rejection sampling (k=100) to generate diverse solution strategies for each problem; (3) RFT on the augmented dataset; and (4) deploy the resulting model for hint generation, solution explanation, or automated grading. The paper's SFT scaling results (Figure 3) suggest that with only 500 initial problems, the model would benefit substantially from augmentation (the log-linear curve is steepest at small data fractions), and RFT could close much of the gap to what a fully-annotated dataset of 7,473 problems would provide. This is directly actionable today with the paper's released code and datasets.

**Quality filtering for large-scale synthetic reasoning data generation.** The paper's finding that the "no dedup" RFT variant performs similarly to deduplicated RFT despite using ~10× more training data (Figure 4, LLaMA-7B: 43.6% vs. 41.7%) has direct implications for organizations generating synthetic training data at scale. When producing millions of reasoning examples through model sampling, it is common practice to filter for correctness but retain all correct samples. The paper's results suggest this is wasteful—most of the retained samples are near-duplicates that provide negligible additional training signal. The equation-based deduplication (Algorithm 1) provides a cheap, automated filter that can reduce training dataset size by ~90% (from 53.3 correct paths to 5.25 distinct paths per question for LLaMA-7B, Table 1 and Table 2) without sacrificing downstream model quality. For a pipeline generating 10 million synthetic reasoning examples, reducing to 1 million through deduplication saves ~90% on training compute, storage, and data processing costs while preserving essentially all the benefit. This is a practical engineering optimization that any team doing large-scale synthetic data generation can implement immediately.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper articulates a clear tradeoff between investing in better pre-training (lower pre-training loss, which may require larger models, more tokens, or longer training) versus investing in data augmentation through RFT. The decision rule emerges directly from the paper's empirical findings:

- **Prefer RFT (data augmentation) over pre-training investment when:**
  - You are working with a base model below ~33B parameters (or, more precisely, a model with pre-training loss above approximately 1.62, based on the LLaMA-33B loss value in Table 1 where RFT benefit drops to zero). At 7B and 13B scales, RFT provides +5–13 accuracy points on GSM8K (Table 5) at negligible cost relative to pre-training.
  - You have access to any reasonable amount of human-annotated supervised data (even a few hundred examples—RFT improves over SFT even at small data fractions, Appendix A.2), and the diversity ceiling (distinct reasoning paths per question) is above approximately 3–4, as indicated by the single-model RFT results (Table 2 shows that k=25 yields ~2.9 distinct paths and still provides +5.1 points over SFT for LLaMA-7B).
  - You can tolerate the inference cost of generating k=100 samples per training question (~10 GPU-hours for 7B, ~0.1k for 13B, ~4.5k for 33B, Table 4) to construct the augmented dataset.
  - Your goal is single-inference accuracy (maj1@1) in a deployment setting where multiple sampling is prohibitively expensive—this is explicitly the paper's focus and the scenario where RFT's improvements are most impactful.

- **Prefer pre-training investment (training a better base model) over RFT when:**
  - You are working with a model at 65B+ parameters where pre-training loss is low enough that the model already generates reasoning paths with low structural diversity (Tables 1, 5), and RFT provides zero or negative benefit regardless of the augmentation dataset used. The paper's 65B and 70B results show that even multi-model RFT-U13B provides at best flat accuracy (59.0% vs. 59.3% SFT for LLaMA-65B, 62.3% vs. 63.2% for LLaMA2-70B), meaning augmentation cannot substitute for pre-training at this scale.
  - You need to push performance substantially beyond ~55% on GSM8K (the approximate ceiling of RFT-U13B for 13B models in Table 5). The paper's pre-training loss vs. accuracy relationship (Figure 2) shows that moving to lower-loss models continues to yield improvements, while augmentation returns have been exhausted.
  - Your task requires reasoning capabilities that go beyond grade-school arithmetic word problems—the paper provides no evidence that RFT's diversity benefit transfers to harder math (MATH) or non-math reasoning, so pre-training remains the only demonstrated path to capability improvements outside the GSM8K distribution.

- **Prefer RFT with multi-model aggregation over single-model RFT when:**
  - You have access to, or can produce, multiple SFT models from different base pre-trained models (different sizes, different training runs, or different families like LLaMA vs. LLaMA2). The paper shows that four-model aggregation (U13B) provides +7.6 additional points over single-model RFT (k=100) for LLaMA-7B (49.3% vs. 41.7%), and that this gain comes specifically from complementary reasoning strategies (each model contributes ~15% unique paths, Figure 6).
  - The additional inference cost of sampling from multiple models (~62 GPU-hours total for RFT-U13B inference, Table 4) is acceptable relative to your total compute budget.

- **Prefer single-model RFT when:**
  - You have access to only one base model and need to optimize within that constraint. Single-model RFT at k=100 still provides +5–6 points over SFT for 7B/13B models (Table 1), which may be sufficient for your use case without the engineering complexity of multi-model aggregation.
