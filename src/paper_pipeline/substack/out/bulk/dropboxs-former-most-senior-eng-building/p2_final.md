Here are the takeaways from this James Cowling interview (CTO at Convex, formerly Dropbox's most senior engineer, built their exabyte-scale storage migration from AWS, and was an MIT PhD on distributed transactions):

**Systems work during his PhD**

James Cowling’s career has two parallel threads: abstractions and large-scale transactional systems. His doctoral work produced Granola, an algorithm for **one-shot transactions**—sending code to a server to run reads and writes across multiple nodes atomically. The key insight was about eliminating points of coordination. "What really matters to performance in a large-scale system is eliminating points of coordination," shifting from serial throughput to parallel throughput. Granola introduced the concept of an **independent transaction**, where two entities processing pure functions on independent state both arrive at the same conclusion; they only need to agree on a timestamp to serialize atomically. The paper was about exchanging those timestamps efficiently. His master's thesis focused on Byzantine fault tolerance in the presence of malicious nodes, but his most cited work was a paper called **View Stamp Replication Revisited**, a consensus protocol that predated Paxos and later influenced companies like Tiger Beetle.

Testing distributed systems during a PhD in that era meant camping next to a desk. With Amazon Web Services in its infancy, Cowling relied on a rack of MIT servers and **Planet Lab**, a communal academic testbed that was "continually having problems." He'd sleep next to his desk, wake up in the middle of the night, check Planet Lab's status, and kick off benchmarks. This was the cusp of what he describes, with affectionate respect, as Google "ruining" systems research: before, papers traded in ideas; afterwards, program committees expected realistic benchmarks from industrial-scale systems that grad students couldn't produce. He mourns the loss of idea-first papers like the one simply called *Leases*, where someone invented the time-based lock.

A PhD, Cowling stresses, is not college—it's training to be a researcher. For most engineers, it's the wrong path. But he values one developmental experience uniquely: reaching a point where you face a problem no one else in the world knows the answer to. "You can't ask your advisor because your advisor doesn't know either." Being forced into that discomfort, with no large language model crutch, trains your mind for uncertainty. The difference between academia and industry crystallized for him: in academia, your goal is to advance knowledge; in industry, your goal is to solve problems. "I gravitate more towards just solving problems."

**Dropbox technical deep dive**

Multi-homing—having data in two locations—came in two flavors at Dropbox. **Primary-secondary multi-homing** replicated all writes from an authoritative region to a secondary region for business continuity, with a window of vulnerability for data loss. **Active-active multi-homing** externalized writes only after they'd landed in all regions, allowing them to take down an entire region (say, Ashburn, Virginia) with zero downtime and no data loss. Cowling is emphatic that for most companies, this is a mistake: "If Amazon's down that day, your company will be down. That's a shame, right? But by avoiding that complexity, you're going to be able to move much faster." For Dropbox, storing exabytes for hundreds of millions of customers, the trade-off made sense.

The durability model was staggering. A file stored in Dropbox targeted "at least 12 nines of durability internally, with models showing around 24 nines"—meaning, per the model, the universe would be extinct before data loss occurred. The mechanism combined **erasure coding** with a custom Vandermonde matrix, spreading encoded fragments across racks, rows, power feeds, hard drive manufacturers, and eras to guard against correlated failures. Reading back was actually faster: to reconstruct a file, you might need six out of nine fragments, so you'd ask all nine and return data as soon as the first six responded. Cowling cautions that building your own infrastructure is almost certainly more expensive than the cloud unless you have "very small requirements, very fixed requirements, or very heavy investment"—you need supply chain teams negotiating with Western Digital and Seagate, capacity planning, and data center teams. Magic Pocket, as the storage system was called, was roughly a billion-dollar project and likely the largest data migration in history at that time.

**Why Dropbox migrated from AWS**

The migration from S3 was a strategic desire from before Cowling joined in 2010, driven by a need to control the company's destiny. Ultimately it saved huge money and drove massive cost efficiencies before the company went public. But "it was hard, in a way that would be very difficult to emulate without a huge investment." There's a subtler benefit to having such hard problems: they attract engineers who love hard technical problems, and after solving them, those engineers cycle onto different systems. Post-migration, Jamie redesigned the sync protocol and Cowling worked on the file system and distributed databases.

Dropbox had negotiated very aggressive S3 pricing ("very, very, very good discounted rates"), but at a certain point Amazon couldn't match their cost efficiency because they'd designed a system specifically for their own workload. They used experimental **Shingled Magnetic Recording** disks at scale and exploited tight workload knowledge: files are accessed heavily right after upload (for sharing or preview generation), then decay. So they built two clusters—a temporary one optimized for cheap reads and writes but inefficient storage, and a cold storage system that was far more static, bulk-written, and far more efficient. The system could tolerate the cold side going down for writes because it wasn't in the live path. This wasn't abstract: at one point they got a bad batch of hardware causing disks to fail too rapidly, and the data center team messaged that racks were running way too hot from re-replication traffic.

The implementation language journey was telling. The prototype was in Python, "pretty efficient for I/O," but hard to refactor and weak on concurrency. They migrated to Go before it was in general availability—great for proxies and concurrency, but with unpredictable memory usage from its runtime. An out-of-memory error in a storage node looks indistinguishable from a disk failure, triggering re-replication. When a band (possibly De La Soul) released an album on Dropbox, a spike in load caused nodes to OOM, and the system turned one fire hose of traffic into seven fire hoses of reconstruction load—a cycle that can lead to **congestion collapse**, "one of the hardest parts of Magic Pocket." The solution was rewriting the storage nodes in Rust, also pre-GA, and using a new instruction set to directly address disk heads, eliminating the filesystem entirely. The combined project, called **Discotech**, shifted the cost pie chart to be almost entirely disks.

The lived reality of physical infrastructure was brutal. In one week, two trucks delivering servers both crashed; the drivers were okay, but they lost capacity for six weeks. "What happens is the equivalent of your disk filling up on your laptop, except it's a million disks, and you can't tell the customers to go away." The antidote was formal threat modeling—an FMEA spreadsheet cataloging every possible failure mode, from fire in the data center to market dynamics, then designing around each. He describes loving "that rubber-hits-the-road stuff" where there's congestion collapse and no one can help you. When Ryan asks about defcon knobs for gracefully degrading, Cowling is absolute: "Never. When it came to durability, we had absolute zero, non-negotiable standards." They did have a system called **Trampoline** that, when capacity ran close to the edge, would dump 30 petabytes onto S3 as an escape hatch, then pull it back later.

**How to do massive migrations**

The Magic Pocket initial build was done by a handful of engineers—"three, four, five, six"—not a thousand. The secret was simplicity and a "very thick layer of validation." During the S3 migration, they conducted a **dark launch**: moving data off S3 but keeping it in both locations, with a requirement to run six months with no incidents, downtime, or data loss before deleting *any* data from S3. Halfway through, a bug made it through to production. Nothing bad happened, but Cowling went to the VP and said they were resetting the launch clock, costing the company double-digit millions. "They were like, great, thank you, that's good, I trust you. That was the cool thing." The culture reinforced user safety over schedule; "no one was mad about that. It was almost like they were proud."

Simplicity for him isn't just an aesthetic—it's a defensive design principle. A concrete example: the block-to-location mapping was a cluster of a thousand MySQL nodes, indexed by block ID, listing which disks held the data. "That's pretty simple. It's not sophisticated." Every new hire from academia or other companies would suggest a Patricia trie or a distributed hash table. But Cowling's counter is that the MySQL table is in one location: to validate the whole system, you walk the table and check. A distributed hash table is nearly impossible to validate end-to-end. "It's not about getting a system to work. It's what do you do when it doesn't work." **Simplicity is the hardest thing in systems.** "The best compliment you could ever get about anything you design is when people say, 'Oh, isn't that the obvious way of doing it?'" Simplicity is scalable—not just in queries per second, but in surviving five years of feature additions and requirement shifts. A complex, over-optimized system "will not stand the test of time."

**Simplicity vs. complexity in promotions**

Cowling's voice rises here: the promotion incentive system that rewards complexity "almost angers me." He contrasts it with the startup model, where the goal is to build the system, have users like it, and everyone gets rewarded for solving the problem. At large companies, layers of organization create alternative incentive structures: "I'm so far away from whatever the hell we're trying to do over here that my goal now is to get all green check marks on my OKR plan." He's seen engineers who, when asked why they built something, say "the VP told me to," and when asked how it's used, say "I think ads use it." "You can do competent engineering, but the best engineering comes from a deep understanding of why." His advice to people stuck in these environments: "Try to fight the system a little bit." Find a team of folks who just want to do the right thing. "I felt good when I went home, not trying to get promoted, just trying to do the right thing and then assuming I'm going to get promoted. If not, go somewhere else."

**What technical teams should be focused on**

One of his most impactful jobs at Dropbox was shutting down projects. He'd find a team of 60 people working on something for two years that didn't make sense, ask them privately if they believed in it, and often hear, "I don't really know." Inertia is so strong. His antidote is orienting teams around missions, not systems. When the storage system shipped, he renamed the team from **Magic Pocket Team** to **Storage Team**—renaming email addresses, channels, repos. His argument: the storage team's responsibility is to solve storage needs for the organization, not advocate for Magic Pocket. "If there was a point in time where S3 was a better idea, it would make sense to move back. It's the job of the storage team to advocate for moving back." Otherwise, suggesting a move back to S3 feels threatening to a team whose identity is the system. The heuristic question he'd ask across the company: **"If we could be spending these resources working on any project at the company right now, would this still be the best use of time?"** Reframing everything as a prioritization question, not a yes/no, makes conversations easier.

He earned the capital to do this by visibly caring. "This is a guy over here that really wants to do the right thing and cares about the company." When engineers complained about inefficiencies, his response was: "Do you think we should solve this problem right now? Because if we should, let me know the team to take some engineers off and the product to shut down, and I can redirect resources." Usually the answer was that nothing else was lower priority—and then the answer is just acceptance. On the mindset shift from a tiny early team: when someone joined from Google and said, "Someone needs to build this logging framework," the response was, "Well, who is someone? Because it's just us. We build it or we don't build it. There's no other idiots out there. We're the idiots."

**Doing the right thing vs. promotion hypothetical**

Presented with the two-by-two matrix—do the right thing and not get promoted, or do the wrong thing and get promoted—Cowling picks the former without hesitation. His argument is that at a certain experience level, "you've made it, that's it, you're done, the money's fine." Junior engineers over-optimize for promotion and salary rather than investing in themselves. He once was the tech lead making the least money on his team. "If you have two choices—making 20% more money now or working with the best people in the world—just be around the best people because that's going to set you on the ship to success." There's a correlation between talent, compensation, and growth. The real privilege of engineering is getting to work on something cool.

When Ryan asks what motivates doing the right thing when the machine doesn't incentivize it, Cowling pushes back: the machine does incentivize it, just not immediately. People who job-hop less grow more. "If you're not in a job for three years, you're not going to see whether your decisions were good. It's like playing a basketball game and leaving before the game's over. You're just not learning." He left academia because he hated having to convince people an idea was good via a paper—he just wanted to build it and see. "I don't know, you could have a fancier car or you could enjoy what you do every day. I promise that enjoying what you do every day is going to have a much bigger impact on your life."

**Why he dipped into management sometimes**

"I feel like most people should not want to be managers, and I sometimes think it's a bit of a red flag if someone wants to be a manager too much." He went into management because it was necessary—someone needed to manage the team. As soon as someone else could come in, he'd bounce back to engineering. Management is tremendously educational, revealing how complicated companies and people really are. But he strongly cautions against going into management too early: "You should ideally get to staff engineer before you do that." Without deep technical skills, managers can't evaluate their team's work, can't push toward excellence, and risk becoming coordinators rather than organizational leaders.

**Why you shouldn't lead by example**

Leading by example is passive and doesn't work because engineers don't interpret it as intended. When he responded first to every page, wrote all the reports, and jumped on all the bugs, his team thought, "Maybe that's James's job" or "Maybe he likes doing those things." At a certain point, leadership is about understanding human psychology. He describes a universal trajectory: a new tech lead cares so much they become a micromanager, reviewing every line of code, becoming the bottleneck. Then a manager says "let your team own more," and the tech lead just takes their hands off the wheel—and the team falls apart. The solution is a **slider between oversight and accountability**. When someone is new, they're in oversight mode. But dialing down oversight requires explicitly dialing up accountability: "You've got this project. Let me know when it's going to get done. Next Thursday. What's the plan? How are you going to know it's correct? Great. It's on you." Most people genuinely like accountability.

His leadership philosophy centers on alignment around **why**. Most organizational conflict is well-intentioned people debating *how* while disagreeing on *why*. One team thinks the priority is shipping features; another thinks it's reliability. Both are valid, but they'll do very different things. "Everyone needs to have 100% alignment on why." If someone listened to him at Dropbox, it wasn't because they were scared of him—it was because he took the time to figure out their motivations and explain in a way that resonated. "If you are leading through authority, you're not going to have a culture where good ideas arise from within the organization."

**How to mentor senior staff+ engineers**

At the senior principal level, there's no single archetype—"you've got to be your own brand of engineer." His brand is strategic collaboration, simplicity, and abstraction; other engineers are the deep science-fiction problem-solvers. Mentoring at this level involves two things: helping them become more spiky in their unique strengths, and almost always working on the personal, organizational side. He finds himself telling senior engineers that **you can't change someone's mind in a meeting**. "To change someone's mind involves going through a complex series of neurological processes that don't happen live in front of ten people." If you force agreement, they'll go along but their ego gets bruised. His technique: identify the disagreement, provide enough information, then "let it sit. Let them go and reflect on it and come back a week later." He invokes a kind of capitalist interpersonal realism: "It doesn't matter how smart you are, it doesn't matter how right you are; are you effective? A lot of being effective at that level is about interpersonal dynamics because most hard problems happen with a team."

**Career advice for the AI era**

The industry has shifted: incredible demand for senior engineers, harder for juniors. Large language models can't do architecture, simplicity, or design—"despite what you might hear on Twitter." Junior tasks are commoditizing, which worries him, because wisdom requires synthesis through struggle. He rejects the ludicrous claim that "not knowing engineering will be an advantage because you won't have biases." "The best software engineering is not about knowing syntax; it's about being really good at conceptualizing problems, breaking them down into building blocks, and coming up with clean solutions." That requires doing the reps. **"Find a way to stress your brain every day."** He observes in himself that it's getting harder to read a book—the stimulation economy has fried his attention—and similarly, it's getting harder to face a difficult problem without reaching for Claude. He advises thinking of a solution yourself first, then checking with the LLM.

Against the "permanent underclass" fear, his framing is practical: if you believe AGI is coming in a year, the person who's been training their mind will be better placed than the person who gave up and just fed the machine. He notes that Anthropic still does whiteboard coding interviews—"candidates are getting worse at coding"—because solving an engineering problem is still the best vehicle for evaluating intellectual capacity. He uses the analogy of a math degree, where students prove theorems already proven, not because the theorems need proving, but because the act improves the mind. "I hate this talk about how AI means humans don't have a role anymore. What I like is, 'Hey, look at this—all this new cool stuff we can build.'"

On the general noise: "I see that with all the 996 stuff and this kind of performative photos of being in a bar with a laptop, and I'm like that's not real." New models drop, and he doesn't even remember the names. "There was this thing called RALPH. I haven't heard anyone mention RALPH in the past few weeks, but it was the biggest thing on Twitter for like a month. This is noise." He calls it **tech tabloidism**—the nerd equivalent of reading about Beyoncé. "Just be building stuff. Just do real work."

**Why he started his own company**

After eight years at Dropbox as the most senior engineer, he left amicably to grow. Convex started pre-agentic era from an observation: the real differentiator in project success is the quality of abstractions, design, and architecture, and the hardest challenge in engineering is distributed state management. Convex is a transactional database where transactions are written in TypeScript as stored procedures with serializable isolation and automatic reactivity. The coding agent era has only strengthened the thesis: "What humans find hard is also what agents find hard." Agents aren't good with large codebases, action at a distance, or race conditions across services—exactly the problems Convex makes go away. Most Convex users now use a coding agent for their front end but need the backend abstraction that eliminates state management, concurrency, scheduling, and polling concerns.

Behind the scenes, Convex is developing a lower-level API with primitives like **fork**, inspired by operating systems, for efficient background processes, singletons, and condition-variable-style wakeups. "I still find Convex very hard. I struggle every day. I don't find my job easy." He finds this edge—"I'm really liking this, but also it's a bit tricky"—the most exciting place to be.

**The most technically challenging work of his career**

The most stimulating work wasn't the formal protocol modeling in grad school (though reasoning through consensus protocols on weekend phone calls with Barbara Liskov was fun). It was "working on a very large storage system with a team where things were going wrong. Where the rubber hits the road." He defines engineering as **science with constraints**—solving problems in the presence of resource limitations. "I'm not particularly interested in constraint-free environments. That's art. I like craft and engineering. The more visceral and difficult the constraints, the more fun that is."

**How he got involved in Silicon Valley**

After season one of *Silicon Valley* was a hit, the writers needed an expert on compression and storage to consult on what happens after a compression algorithm is built. Cowling was that expert. Mike Judge (who had been a software engineer at Lockheed) and the writers cared obsessively about accuracy—that's partly why the show is so cringy to actual engineers: "those stories are real." They farmed real stories from companies and wove them in, and Cowling consulted on everything from storylines to what a rack in a garage data center should look like. He made $400 total (required by Hollywood union rules, though he didn't want to get paid for visa reasons).

**Career regrets**

His regret isn't a missed financial opportunity—everyone who's been in the industry long enough has been offered the chance to co-found multiple billion-dollar companies. The regret is personal: "I underinvested in my personal life." He's been on call his entire career, carried a laptop almost every day, skipped countless dinners and parties. "There are people in my personal life who have suffered as a result." If he could go back, he'd take more vacations and have more balance. The 996 performative hustle culture isn't real; "the cool stuff is enjoying doing important things." To his younger self, he'd say: "It'll be okay. Don't sweat the small stuff. Careers are long. Any story about a 22-year-old billionaire—just ignore that story. That's not normal. And it's not that healthy." You can't max out your growth in three months. "All you gotta do is orient your career around learning every day, getting better at what you're doing, and trying to solve things in the most simple ways."

---

Cowling's career is a long argument for a single, hard-won conviction: that **simplicity is the hardest thing in systems** and the only thing that truly scales across years of feature churn and organizational drift. His **slider between oversight and accountability** is the management corollary—just as you design systems for validation, you design teams for ownership by explicitly dialing up responsibility when you dial down review. Both feed his rejection of **tech tabloidism**, the performative noise that distracts from the real work of training your mind on hard problems with real constraints. Whether pouring over an FMEA spreadsheet for congestion collapse or shutting down a 60-person project that lost its **why**, his through-line is the same: do the simple, obvious thing that solves the problem, not the complex thing that impresses a promotion committee. If the abstraction floor keeps rising, the people who keep doing the reps—stressing their brains every day, not outsourcing the struggle to an agent—will be the ones ready to build whatever comes next.
