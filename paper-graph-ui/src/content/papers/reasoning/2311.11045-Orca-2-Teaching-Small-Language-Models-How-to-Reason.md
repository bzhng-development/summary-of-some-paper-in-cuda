# Orca 2: Teaching Small Language Models How to Reason

**ArXiv:** [2311.11045](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.11045)

## 🎯 Pitch

Small language models can match or beat models 5–10× larger on tough reasoning tasks—not by parroting teacher outputs, but by learning *which* reasoning strategy to use per problem. Orca 2 teaches 7B and 13B models a toolbox of techniques (step-by-step, recall-then-generate, etc.) and trains them to choose the right tool for the job, yielding a 47.54% relative jump over LLaMA-2-Chat-13B.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces a method for teaching small language models to reason by training them on tailored synthetic data that demonstrates a variety of solution strategies, then teaching the model to choose among them based on the task. Using LLaMA-2 7B and 13B as base models, the authors develop **Cautious Reasoning**—training the model to select the most effective reasoning technique for each problem (step-by-step, recall-then-generate, direct answer, etc.)—and couple it with **Prompt Erasure**, a technique where the detailed system instructions used to elicit strategic behavior from the teacher (e.g., GPT-4) are replaced with a generic instruction during student training, forcing the model to learn the underlying strategy itself rather than merely imitating the teacher's output style. Orca-2-13B achieves a 47.54% relative improvement over LLaMA-2-Chat-13B and matches or exceeds models 5–10× larger (e.g., WizardLM-70B, ChatGPT) on zero-shot reasoning benchmarks, establishing that small models can compete with much larger ones when equipped with diverse reasoning strategies—but primarily when the required knowledge already exists in the base model's pretraining corpus, since post-training teaches reasoning procedures rather than new factual knowledge.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Small Models Don't Reason Well, But We Don't Know How to Teach Them

The fundamental question this paper tackles is straightforward: **how can we endow small language models (roughly 7–13B parameters) with the reasoning capabilities that have emerged in the largest models, without simply scaling up the model size?** This matters because frontier models like GPT-4 (OpenAI, 2023) and PaLM-2 (Anil et al., 2023) exhibit remarkable zero-shot reasoning abilities—solving multi-step math problems, answering complex medical licensing exam questions (Singhal et al., 2023), and engaging in logical deduction—that were once considered beyond the reach of AI. However, these capabilities come at enormous computational cost: the largest models require datacenter-scale hardware for inference, making them impractical for many deployment scenarios (on-device, edge computing, latency-sensitive applications, or cost-constrained environments).

This gap is significant for several practical reasons the authors highlight (Section 1):

- **Deployment flexibility**: A 7B or 13B parameter model that can reason competently opens the door to on-device deployment, private inference, and applications where sending data to cloud APIs is infeasible for privacy or latency reasons.
- **Cost efficiency**: Smaller models consume orders of magnitude less compute per inference, making them viable for high-throughput or budget-constrained use cases.
- **Democratization of AI**: Open-source smaller models that can compete with proprietary giants enable broader research access and downstream innovation without dependence on API providers.
- **Specialization potential**: A small model that serves as a "reasoning engine" can be paired with retrieved knowledge or specialized to narrow domains, potentially outperforming a larger generalist model on specific tasks.

### The Imitation Learning Trap: Why Existing Approaches Fall Short

The dominant paradigm for improving small language models has been **imitation learning**—also called behavior cloning—where a "student" model is fine-tuned to replicate the outputs of a larger, more capable "teacher" model. This approach has been adopted by numerous projects: Alpaca (Taori et al., 2023), Vicuna (Chiang et al., 2023), WizardLM (Xu et al., 2023), Baize (Xu et al., 2023), and Koala (Geng et al., 2023), among others. The recipe is consistent: collect outputs from a powerful model like GPT-4 or ChatGPT, then fine-tune a smaller model (typically LLaMA) on those outputs via instruction tuning (Section 2.1).

The problem, as the authors argue, is that **imitation learning teaches style but not substance**. The resulting models can produce outputs that *look* like they came from a capable teacher—the formatting, the confident tone, the structure—but often fall short on actual reasoning and comprehension (Gudibande et al., 2023, cited in Section 1). The authors identify a critical failure mode in Section 2.2:

> "One of the known weaknesses of instruction tuning is that a resulting student model could learn to generate stylistically correct, but ultimately wrong, outputs."

This is not merely an aesthetic concern. When a small model imitates a larger one, it learns to mimic the *surface patterns* of the teacher's reasoning—phrases like "Let's think step by step" or "Therefore, the answer is"—without necessarily internalizing the cognitive process that produced the correct answer. The model becomes a convincing but unreliable approximator, particularly on tasks requiring genuine logical deduction rather than pattern matching.

### The Explanation Tuning Advancement—and Its Limits

Orca 1 (Mukherjee et al., 2023) addressed this imitation learning weakness with **Explanation Tuning**, described in Section 2.2. Rather than simply training on teacher outputs, Orca 1 used carefully crafted system instructions to elicit detailed explanations from GPT-4—step-by-step reasoning traces, not just final answers. The key insight was that exposing the student to the teacher's *reasoning process*, not just its conclusions, would produce better generalization. System instructions like "think step-by-step" or "generate detailed answers" were combined with user prompts from the FLAN collection (Longpre et al., 2023) to produce a dataset of (system instruction, user prompt, detailed answer) triplets. Training on these richer signals produced substantial improvements over conventional instruction-tuned models on complex zero-shot reasoning tasks.

However, Explanation Tuning as practiced in Orca 1 has a fundamental limitation that this paper identifies: **it naively assumes that the teacher's reasoning strategy is the right one for the student to learn**. The teacher (GPT-4) can deploy different strategies—direct answers, step-by-step reasoning, explanation-then-answer—and the quality of its output varies depending on whether the right strategy was prompted for the right task. But even when the teacher produces a correct answer, the *optimal* strategy for a 13B model may differ from the optimal strategy for a model orders of magnitude larger.

Consider the example in Figure 3. Given a story reordering task, GPT-4 produces four different answers depending on the system instruction:
- **Default answer**: wrong
- **Chain-of-thought prompt**: better but missing crucial details
- **Explain-your-answer prompt**: wrong answer but correct explanation
- **Specialized multi-step prompt** (identify theme, find cause-effect, locate starting sentence, rearrange, final answer): the only correct answer

This illustrates two critical observations:
1. **Strategy matters enormously**—the same model on the same task can succeed or fail based solely on which reasoning procedure it follows, even with identical underlying capability.
2. **The best strategy for GPT-4 may not be the best strategy for a smaller model**—GPT-4 can sometimes produce correct direct answers (the default behavior), but a smaller model likely cannot, and would benefit more from structured step-by-step decomposition.

### The Missing Piece: Teaching Strategy Selection

This paper positions itself at the intersection of two observations that prior work failed to connect:

**Observation 1 (from Orca 1)**: Training on diverse reasoning traces improves small model performance beyond simple instruction tuning. The richness of the training signal—explanations, not just answers—is what drives the improvement.

**Observation 2 (novel to this paper)**: Not all reasoning strategies are equally appropriate for all tasks, and more importantly, the optimal strategy for a teacher model may differ from the optimal strategy for a student model. A smaller model has different cognitive constraints—less memorized knowledge, weaker in-context learning, lower capacity for holding multiple constraints simultaneously—and therefore should use different reasoning approaches.

The paper's core contribution is to bridge these observations via **Cautious Reasoning**: teaching the student model not just *how* to execute various reasoning techniques, but *when* to use which one. This is a meta-cognitive skill—the ability to look at a problem, assess its nature, and select an appropriate solution strategy—that the paper argues is essential for smaller models to compensate for their reduced capacity.

The authors frame this explicitly in Section 3:

> "The optimal strategy for a smaller model may differ from that of a more powerful one. For instance, while a model like GPT-4 may easily generate a direct answer, a smaller model might lack this capability and require a different approach, such as thinking step-by-step. Therefore, naively teaching a smaller model to 'imitate' the reasoning behavior of a more powerful one may be sub-optimal."

This is a subtle but profound shift in perspective. Prior approaches treated the teacher model as an oracle whose behavior should be replicated as faithfully as possible. This paper treats the teacher as a *reservoir of possible behaviors*, from which the most student-appropriate strategies should be selectively extracted and taught.

### The Prompt Erasure Mechanism: Learning Strategy, Not Script

How do you teach a model to *choose* a strategy rather than just execute a predetermined one? The paper introduces **Prompt Erasure** (Section 3) to address this. The teacher (GPT-4) receives detailed, task-specific system instructions designed to elicit a particular reasoning strategy—for example, a multi-step instruction for story reordering that specifies: "Identify the main theme, look for cause-effect relationships, find the starting sentence, rearrange in correct order, output final answer." The teacher produces a response that demonstrates that strategy in action.

But here is the critical design choice: **during training, the student model does NOT see those detailed system instructions**. Instead, the detailed instruction is replaced with a generic, strategy-agnostic system message (the "cautious system instruction": "You are Orca, an AI language model created by Microsoft. You are a cautious assistant. You carefully follow instructions..."). The student sees only: (generic system message, task description, teacher's strategy-demonstrating answer).

What does this force the student to learn? Since the detailed recipe for how to approach the task is absent from the input, the model must infer—from the structure of the answer alone—what reasoning procedure generated it. And when the model encounters a new task at inference time, it must decide *which* procedure to apply, because no procedure is specified in the prompt. The authors summarize:

> "In the absence of the original system instruction which detailed how to approach the task, the student model will be encouraged to learn that underlying strategy as well as the reasoning abilities it entailed."

This is genuinely clever. It transforms the training data from a demonstration of "here is how the teacher solved this problem given explicit instructions to use method X" into a demonstration of "here is a problem and a solution that exhibits the structure of method X—figure out what X is and when to use it." The model learns both the *mechanics* of various reasoning strategies (from the structure of the answers) and the *meta-decision* of which strategy to deploy (because it must infer the appropriate strategy from the task description alone, without the teacher's prompting).

### Where Prior Approaches Specifically Fall Short

The paper identifies concrete limitations in existing methods that motivate its approach:

**Standard instruction tuning (Alpaca, Vicuna, WizardLM)**: These methods train on (instruction, output) pairs where the output is generated by a teacher model. The problem, as demonstrated by Gudibande et al. (2023), is that "imitation models mostly learn the style of the teacher rather than its reasoning capabilities." The outputs look convincing but fail under close evaluation, especially on reasoning-intensive tasks where correctness depends on logical validity rather than surface fluency.

**Explanation Tuning (Orca 1)**: This improves on instruction tuning by providing reasoning traces rather than just answers, but it has two limitations this paper addresses. First, it uses a cross-product approach: M task categories × N system instructions producing M×N different training instances, where many of those combinations are *inappropriate*—asking for step-by-step reasoning on a simple classification task, or asking for a direct answer on a complex multi-step problem. The resulting training data contains examples of suboptimal strategy choices that the student may learn from. Second, and more fundamentally, even the correct strategy-to-task mappings may not be optimal for the student model. GPT-4's ability to directly answer questions that require a 13B model to reason step-by-step means that mimicking GPT-4's strategy choices is potentially harmful.

**Evaluation limitations in prior small-model work**: The paper notes (Section 1) that many previous studies on small model training rely on narrow evaluation protocols—small numbers of tasks, or using other LLMs as judges via pairwise comparison prompts ("which of these two responses is better?"). As demonstrated by Wang et al. (2023), Zheng et al. (2023), and others, LLM-as-judge evaluations suffer from position bias, length bias, and preference for their own model family's outputs. This means the apparent performance of imitation-trained small models may be inflated relative to their actual capabilities, making the gap that Orca 2 addresses even larger than commonly reported.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper situates itself explicitly as a successor to Orca 1 (Section 1):

> "Like Orca 1, we utilize more capable LLMs to demonstrate various reasoning strategies across various tasks. However, in Orca 2, the reasoning strategies are carefully tailored to the task at hand, bearing in mind whether a student model is capable of the same behavior."

This is not merely an incremental improvement in data quality. It represents a shift from "teach the student everything the teacher knows" to "teach the student what the student needs to know, using strategies appropriate to the student's capacity." The teacher becomes a tool for generating strategically diverse demonstrations, not a behavior to be cloned.

The paper also positions itself relative to the broader question of what instruction tuning can and cannot accomplish (Section 2.1). It explicitly acknowledges a fundamental constraint:

> "Instruction tuning, while very beneficial for teaching the model how to solve a task, does not necessarily teach the model new knowledge. Hence instruction tuned models will be always limited by the knowledge learned during pre-training."

This is an important honesty about the scope of the contribution. Orca 2 can make a 13B model a better *reasoner* over the knowledge it already possesses, but it cannot inject new factual knowledge. The model will still fail on tasks requiring facts it didn't encounter during pretraining. This both bounds the claims the paper makes and points to the appropriate use case: "smaller language models with enhanced reasoning are perhaps best used as reasoning engines over knowledge provided to the model in its context window, or when specialized to narrower domains."

This grounding in the limits of post-training is a mature acknowledgment that distinguishes the paper from approaches that imply instruction tuning can create capabilities from nothing. It also connects to Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) and domain-specialization paradigms where a small reasoning-capable model is the ideal engine.

In summary, this paper addresses the gap between large models' emergent reasoning abilities and small models' practical deployability by teaching small models *how to think*—not just what to say—and crucially, *how to decide which way of thinking is appropriate* for a given problem. It does so by using large models as a source of strategically diverse demonstrations rather than as behavioral targets, and by structuring training so that the student must learn *strategy selection* as a first-class skill rather than as an incidental byproduct of imitation.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

This paper presents a training methodology—not a single model architecture or algorithm—for teaching small (7B–13B parameter) language models to reason more effectively by exposing them to a diversity of solution strategies during fine-tuning, then forcing the model to learn *when* to deploy each strategy by removing the strategy-specifying instructions from the training data. The core problem it solves is the imitation learning trap: existing methods teach small models to replicate the surface style of larger teacher models without learning the underlying decision process for choosing *which* reasoning approach fits a given problem, especially when the optimal strategy for a small model differs from what the teacher would naturally do.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The system has five major components, arranged as a data generation and training pipeline:

1. **Task Categorization**: A manual grouping of 1,448 FLAN-v2 tasks into 23 categories and 126 sub-categories, where each sub-category represents tasks that should share the same reasoning strategy.

2. **Teacher Model (GPT-4)**: The more capable LLM used to generate training data. It receives carefully crafted, task-specific system instructions designed to elicit particular reasoning strategies—not just any strategy, but the one deemed most appropriate for the student model's capacity on that task type.

3. **Strategy-Specific Prompt Engineering**: For each sub-category, the authors write detailed system instructions that guide GPT-4 through a specific reasoning procedure (step-by-step decomposition, recall-then-generate, explain-then-answer, direct answer extraction, etc.). These instructions may involve multiple internal steps, explicit checks, or particular output formatting.

4. **Prompt Erasure**: Before training, the detailed system instruction—which explicitly told the teacher *how* to reason—is replaced with a generic, strategy-agnostic system message. The student model sees only (generic system message, task description, teacher's strategically-structured answer).

5. **Progressive Training Pipeline**: The student model (LLaMA-2 7B or 13B) is fine-tuned in three sequential stages, each using data from progressively higher-quality sources: (1) FLAN-v2 for broad instruction following, (2) ChatGPT-generated data from Orca 1 for explanation-rich responses, (3) GPT-4-generated data from Orca 1 and the new Orca 2 dataset for strategy-diverse, high-quality reasoning demonstrations.

Information flows: Tasks are categorized → strategy-appropriate prompts are written for each sub-category → GPT-4 generates strategically-structured answers using those prompts → the detailed prompts are erased and replaced with a generic system message → the student model is trained in stages on this data, learning both the mechanics of each reasoning strategy (from the answer structure) and the meta-decision of which strategy fits which task (because the prompt no longer specifies it).

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the **formal definition of Cautious Reasoning**—what it means for a model to select a reasoning strategy, why the strategy must be task-dependent and model-size-dependent, and how Prompt Erasure operationalizes the meta-decision.
- **Second**, the **Prompt Erasure mechanism** in detail—what exactly is erased, what remains, what the student must infer, and why this produces strategy-selection capability rather than blind imitation.
- **Third**, the **dataset construction pipeline**—the four data sources (FLAN, Few-Shot, Math, Synthetic), the manual categorization of 1,448 tasks into strategy-sharing sub-categories, the filtering rationale, and the specific synthetic data generation procedures.
- **Fourth**, the **progressive training procedure**—the three training stages, their data mixtures, epoch counts, loss computation, sequence packing, and hardware configuration, plus the rationale for training in stages rather than all at once.
- **Fifth**, the **answer parsing and evaluation infrastructure**, since the paper's results depend heavily on robust extraction of final answers from free-form model outputs, and the design choices here affect all reported numbers.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **training methodology paper** whose core technical contribution is a data generation and curation pipeline that teaches a small language model to select among reasoning strategies by (1) constructing strategically diverse teacher demonstrations and (2) removing the strategy-specifying instructions so the student must infer the strategy from structure alone.

---

#### The Formal Definition of Cautious Reasoning

The paper defines **Cautious Reasoning** (Section 3) as the act of deciding which solution strategy to apply to a given task, where the set of possible strategies includes direct answer generation as well as various "Slow Thinking" strategies (Kahneman, 2011): step-by-step processing, guess-and-check, explain-then-answer, recall-then-generate, extract-generate, and others. This is not merely a taxonomy of answer formats—it is a claim that different strategies impose different computational demands on the model, and that the *choice of strategy* is itself a learnable skill separable from the *execution* of any individual strategy.

The key insight that distinguishes this from prior work is **strategy relativity to model capacity**. The paper states this explicitly in Section 3:

> "The optimal strategy for a smaller model may differ from that of a more powerful one. For instance, while a model like GPT-4 may easily generate a direct answer, a smaller model might lack this capability and require a different approach, such as thinking step-by-step."

What does "lack this capability" mean concretely? A direct answer on a complex reasoning task requires the model to perform multiple inferential steps *implicitly* within a single forward pass—the attention mechanism must route information across multiple logical dependencies without explicit intermediate tokens to scaffold the computation. Larger models, with more layers and wider attention patterns, can do this more reliably. A smaller model may need to externalize the reasoning into explicit intermediate steps (the "chain of thought") to manage the same inferential complexity. Teaching a small model to always imitate a large model's direct answers is therefore teaching it to use a strategy that its architecture does not support well.

The paper also observes that even for the *same* model, strategy effectiveness varies by task. Figure 3 demonstrates this with GPT-4 on story reordering: four different system instructions produce four different answers of varying quality (two wrong, one partially correct, one fully correct). Even a model capable of executing multiple strategies will produce *different outcomes* depending on which strategy it deploys. Cautious Reasoning is therefore the decision process that maximizes the probability of a correct answer by selecting the strategy best suited to both the task's demands and the model's capacity.

**Why not just always use the best strategy?** If step-by-step reasoning improves accuracy across the board, why would a model ever choose a direct answer? The paper's implicit answer (visible in the diversity of strategies included in training data) is twofold. First, different tasks benefit from different strategies—a simple classification task does not need step-by-step decomposition and forcing one would waste inference compute and potentially introduce error through unnecessary intermediate steps. Second, in multi-turn or interactive settings, different strategies produce outputs with different verbosity and structure; a model that can flexibly adapt its reasoning style to the context is more useful than one that always produces the longest possible chain of thought.

The Cautious Reasoning training process is formalized in Section 3 as a four-step procedure:

1. **Start with a collection of diverse tasks** (the 1,448 tasks from FLAN-v2, plus math, few-shot, and synthetic data).

2. **Decide which tasks require which solution strategy**, guided by the observed performance of Orca 1—essentially, use empirical evidence from the previous generation to determine which strategies work best for a 13B model on which task types, rather than assuming the teacher's default behavior is optimal.

3. **Write task-specific system instructions** corresponding to the chosen strategy to obtain teacher responses. This step "has a broad mandate" and can involve "multiple calls, very detailed instructions, etc."—the teacher is treated as an engine for generating strategically-structured data, not as a behavioral template.

4. **Prompt Erasing**: at training time, replace the detailed system instruction with a generic one that contains no information about *how* to approach the task. The student sees the strategic output but not the strategic recipe.

The generic system instruction used for Prompt Erasing is:

> "You are Orca, an AI language model created by Microsoft. You are a cautious assistant. You carefully follow instructions. You are helpful and harmless and you follow ethical guidelines and promote positive behavior."

This message conveys identity and behavioral tone (cautious, helpful, harmless) but contains zero information about what reasoning procedure to apply. The model must therefore learn to map directly from the task description to an appropriate strategic response.

#### The Prompt Erasure Mechanism in Detail

**Prompt Erasure** (Section 3) is the central technical mechanism that distinguishes Orca 2's training from standard instruction tuning and from Orca 1's Explanation Tuning. Understanding it requires distinguishing three entities present in the data generation process:

1. **The teacher's system instruction**: A detailed, task-specific prompt given to GPT-4 that specifies *how* to reason about the problem. For example, the story reordering instruction in Figure 3 specifies: identify the main theme, look for cause-effect relationships, find the starting sentence, go through each answer choice, rearrange in correct order, output final answer as a digit string. This is a procedural recipe.

2. **The teacher's response**: The answer GPT-4 produces when conditioned on that detailed system instruction and the task. This answer exhibits the *structure* of the specified strategy—it contains sections corresponding to each step of the recipe, it uses particular formatting conventions, it shows intermediate reasoning.

3. **The substitute student system instruction**: The generic cautious system message quoted above, which replaces the detailed recipe during training.

The training instance seen by the student model is therefore: (generic system message, task description, teacher's strategically-structured response). Crucially, the detailed recipe that *caused* the response to have that structure is absent.

What must the student learn from this data? Since the input does not specify a reasoning procedure, the model has two learning problems:

- **Learning the strategies themselves**: The model must learn to produce responses with particular structural properties (step-by-step enumeration, intermediate calculations, explicit recall of relevant facts) by observing examples of those structures in the training data. This is similar to standard instruction tuning, except the structures are diverse rather than uniform.

- **Learning strategy selection**: Since no explicit instruction says "use step-by-step reasoning" or "give a direct answer," the model must learn to infer, from the task description alone, which strategic approach is appropriate. If the training data consistently shows math tasks receiving step-by-step answers and simple classification tasks receiving direct answers, the model can learn this mapping—not because it was told the mapping, but because the statistical association between task type and response structure is present in the data.

The authors describe the effect of Prompt Erasure as making Orca 2 a **Cautious Reasoner**:

> "In the absence of the original system instruction which detailed how to approach the task, the student model will be encouraged to learn that underlying strategy as well as the reasoning abilities it entailed."

This framing—"encouraged to learn"—is worth unpacking. The model is not explicitly trained to predict which strategy to use; there is no auxiliary classification loss for strategy selection. Instead, the model's standard next-token prediction objective must account for the strategic structure of responses. If the model incorrectly chooses a direct-answer strategy for a complex math problem, the tokens it generates will have low probability under the training distribution (which contains step-by-step answers for such problems), and the gradient will push the model toward strategies that match the training data's task-strategy associations. Strategy selection emerges as an implicit meta-skill from the statistical structure of the training data.

**What Prompt Erasure is NOT**: It is not data augmentation or noising. It is not removing all system instructions—the generic cautious message remains. It is a deliberate information asymmetry between the data generation process (which used detailed strategies) and the training process (which hides those strategies), designed to force the model to internalize strategy selection as part of its generation policy.

**Connection to the cautious behaviors observed in results**: The paper reports (Section 6) that using the cautious system message at inference time provides a small boost on reasoning tasks (e.g., AGIEval average goes from 49.93% to 48.18% with empty system message vs. 48.18% for the cautious variant on Orca-2-13B—actually a slight decrease, but the effect is mixed across tasks; on GSM8K, cautious system message improves from 59.14% to 65.73%). This is consistent with Prompt Erasure during training: since the model was trained with the cautious system message as the input prefix for all strategically-diverse examples, the cautious message becomes associated with the strategy-selection capability. Using the empty system message at inference time is a mild distribution shift, while the cautious message matches the training distribution and therefore elicits the learned strategy-selection behavior more reliably.

#### Dataset Construction Pipeline

The Orca 2 dataset contains approximately 817,000 training instances drawn from four sources, described in Section 4.1. The construction process is not simply a collection of existing datasets—it involves significant manual curation, filtering, and strategic labeling of which tasks receive which reasoning strategies.

##### Source 1: Cautious-Reasoning FLAN (~602K instances)

The primary data source is the FLAN-v2 Collection (Longpre et al., 2023), which contains five sub-collections: CoT, NiV2, T0, Flan 2021, and Dialogue. Following Orca 1, the authors use only CoT, NiV2, T0, and Flan 2021—excluding Dialogue—for a total of 1,913 tasks. Each task is a collection of queries with an associated answer.

From these 1,913 tasks, the authors filter to retain 1,448 "high quality" tasks, specifically removing many synthetically generated tasks. Synthetically generated tasks are those created by inverting another task—for example, converting a question-answering task into a question-generation task by swapping inputs and outputs. The paper does not provide detailed criteria for which synthetic tasks were excluded, but the filtering reduces the task count by approximately 24% (from 1,913 to 1,448), suggesting a significant curation effort. From these 1,448 tasks, approximately 602,000 zero-shot user queries are sampled from the training split.

This is where the strategic categorization occurs—the step that links tasks to reasoning strategies. The 1,448 retained tasks are **manually grouped into 23 categories**, and each category is further divided into sub-categories, creating a total of 126 sub-categories. The paper lists example categories: Text Classification, Claim Verification, Data2Text, Text Generation, Logic, Math, Multiple Choice Questions, Open Ended Question Answering, Reading Comprehension, etc. The sub-categories are designed such that **all tasks within a sub-category share the same system instruction**—meaning they are judged to require the same reasoning strategy.

This manual grouping is labor-intensive (1,448 tasks examined by humans) and is critical to the method's success. If tasks requiring different strategies were grouped together and assigned the same system instruction, the teacher would produce suboptimal responses for some tasks, and the student would receive noisy training signals about which strategy maps to which task type. The paper does not report inter-annotator agreement or the time required for this categorization, but the granularity (126 sub-categories for 1,448 tasks, averaging ~11.5 tasks per sub-category) suggests a fine-grained classification.

For each of the 126 sub-categories, the authors write a task-specific system instruction designed to elicit the optimal reasoning strategy for a ~13B model. This instruction is used to query GPT-4, producing the strategically-structured teacher responses. The response data is then subjected to Prompt Erasure: all system instructions are replaced with the generic cautious system instruction before training.

##### Source 2: Few-Shot Data (~55K instances)

The FLAN-derived data described above contains zero-shot prompts only—no in-context demonstrations. To teach the model to leverage few-shot examples when they are provided, the authors construct an additional 55,000 samples by re-purposing the zero-shot data from the Orca 1 dataset.

The construction procedure (Section 4.1):

1. Structure the Orca 1 data into (task, system instruction, user prompt, answer) tuples.
2. Group the tuples by task and system instruction.
3. For each group and each user prompt, randomly select 3–5 (user prompt, answer) pairs from the rest of the group to serve as in-context examples.
4. Format the prompt to include these examples before the target query.

This teaches the model a different meta-skill: when examples are provided in context, use them as templates for the expected response format and reasoning approach, rather than relying solely on internal strategy selection.

##### Source 3: Math Data (~160K instances)

The paper acknowledges that math reasoning is a distinct skill requiring explicit training. The math data is collected from multiple sources:

- **Deepmind Math dataset** (Saxton et al., 2019): Approximately 160K problems are sampled, but only from the arithmetic sub-tasks (addition, subtraction, multiplication, division).
- **Training splits of existing datasets**: GSM8K (Cobbe et al., 2021), AquaRat (Ling et al., 2017), MATH (Hendrycks et al., 2021), AMPS (Hendrycks et al., 2021), FeasibilityQA (Gupta et al., 2022), NumGLUE (Mishra et al., 2022), AddSub (Hosseini et al., 2014), GenArith (Koncel-Kedziorski et al., 2015), and Algebra (Kushman et al., 2014). For NumGLUE, AddSub, GenArith, and Algebra, the training splits are sourced from the LILA benchmark (Mishra et al., 2022).

The paper explicitly notes (Section 4.1) that including prompts from the training split of datasets like GSM8K renders those datasets **in-domain for evaluation**—a point of transparency that matters for interpreting the GSM8K results (59.14% for Orca-2-13B, 65.73% with cautious system message). The authors also note that "datasets like GSM8K are considered in-domain for many of our baselines too," acknowledging that the comparison remains fair even though absolute numbers may be higher than true zero-shot generalization would produce.

##### Source 4: Fully Synthetic Data (2,000 Doctor-Patient Conversations)

To assess whether the training methodology enables learning of specialized skills from small amounts of data, the authors create 2,000 synthetic doctor-patient conversations using GPT-4. The procedure:

1. Generate 2,000 doctor-patient dialogues covering various medical scenarios.
2. For each dialogue, instruct GPT-4 to produce a clinical note with four structured sections: HISTORY OF PRESENT ILLNESS, PHYSICAL EXAM, RESULTS, ASSESSMENT AND PLAN.
3. Use **two different system prompts** for the summarization: one with high-level task instruction only, and another with detailed instructions that explicitly encourage the model to avoid omissions and fabrications.

The inclusion of two different prompts for the same task is notable because it creates within-task strategic diversity: the model sees summaries generated under different levels of instructional detail, which may help it learn that the *content* of the note (accurate, grounded, complete) matters independently of the *procedure* specified in the prompt. This connects to the grounding evaluation in Section 6.5, where Orca-2-13B achieves the lowest hallucination rate among all 13B and 70B models tested.

##### Training Data Volume and Composition

The final Orca 2 dataset consists of approximately 817,000 instances. Combined with the Orca 1 dataset (1 million GPT-4 examples and 5 million ChatGPT examples used in progressive training), the total training data across all stages is substantial. The breakdown of the 817K Orca 2-specific dataset:
- ~602K from FLAN (cautious-reasoning zero-shot)
- ~55K few-shot
- ~160K math
- 2K synthetic doctor-patient conversations

This totals approximately 819K (the paper states ~817K, likely due to rounding or minor exclusion of some samples).

---

#### Progressive Training Procedure

The training process (Section 4.2) uses **progressive learning**—training in sequential stages on data of increasing quality—following the approach established in Orca 1. The intuition is that the model should first acquire broad instruction-following ability on diverse but lower-quality data, then refine its reasoning capabilities on higher-quality, more carefully curated data.

The three training stages, in order:

**Stage 1: FLAN-v2 Fine-Tuning.** Starting from the LLaMA-2-7B or LLaMA-2-13B base checkpoint, the model is fine-tuned on the training split of the FLAN-v2 dataset for one epoch. This dataset contains both zero-shot and few-shot problems and covers a broad range of tasks. The purpose is to establish basic instruction-following ability on a wide task distribution.

**Stage 2: ChatGPT Data from Orca 1.** The model is then trained on 5 million ChatGPT-generated examples from the Orca 1 dataset for 3 epochs. This data contains explanation-rich responses—reasoning traces rather than just answers—produced by ChatGPT (GPT-3.5-Turbo) under diverse system instructions. At 3 epochs, the model sees each example multiple times, suggesting this stage focuses on deepening the explanation-generation capability established in Orca 1.

**Stage 3: GPT-4 Data (Orca 1 + Orca 2).** Finally, the model is trained on a combination of 1 million GPT-4-generated examples from Orca 1 and the 817K examples from the new Orca 2 dataset, for 4 epochs. This is the highest-quality data, with strategically structured responses produced by GPT-4 under task-appropriate system instructions, then subjected to Prompt Erasure. The higher epoch count (4) on this data may reflect its greater diversity (it contains examples spanning many distinct reasoning strategies) and the paper's emphasis on strategy-selection learning.

The progression is therefore: FLAN (broad instruction following) → ChatGPT explanations (rich reasoning traces) → GPT-4 strategic data (diverse strategies + strategy selection via Prompt Erasure). Each stage builds on capabilities acquired in the previous stage.

##### Tokenization

The authors use the LLaMA Byte Pair Encoding (BPE) tokenizer (Touvron et al., 2023) with two modifications:

1. **Padding token**: The LLaMA tokenizer does not include a native padding token. The authors add a `[[PAD]]` token to handle variable-length sequences in packed training.

2. **ChatML special tokens**: The tokens `<|im_start|>` and `<|im_end|>` are added to support the ChatML dialogue interface, which separates system messages, user messages, and assistant responses with role-specific delimiters.

The resulting vocabulary contains 32,003 tokens. The use of LLaMA's BPE tokenizer means that **all numbers are split into individual digits**—"123" becomes "1", "2", "3"—which has implications for math reasoning since the model must learn to compose multi-digit numbers from individual digit tokens.

##### Packing

To optimize GPU utilization, the paper employs **sequence packing** (Krell et al., 2022). Rather than padding each training example to the maximum sequence length independently (which wastes computation on padding tokens), multiple examples are concatenated into a single sequence of length exactly `max_len = 4096` tokens. The procedure:

1. Shuffle all input examples.
2. Partition examples into groups such that the total length of the concatenated sequence in each group is at most `max_len`.
3. Add padding tokens to the concatenated sequence to reach exactly `max_len`.

This maximizes the number of tokens receiving meaningful gradient signal per training step. The authors note that the packing is performed "such that the total length of the concatenated sequence does not exceed max_len = 4096 tokens"—the 4,096 token context window of LLaMA-2. Importantly, packing multiple examples may cause the model to see unrelated tasks adjacent in the same sequence, which could be seen as a form of regularization or could introduce gradient interference; the paper does not discuss this tradeoff.

##### Loss Computation

The training loss is computed only on the tokens generated by the teacher model—that is, the assistant's response in the ChatML format. Tokens corresponding to the system message and user prompt are not included in the loss. This is standard practice in instruction tuning: the model learns to generate responses conditioned on instructions, and penalizing the model for "predicting" the instruction tokens (which are given as input) would be counterproductive.

The paper describes this as ensuring that "the model focuses on learning from the most relevant and informative tokens, improving the overall efficiency and effectiveness of the training process."

The loss function itself is not explicitly stated but is the standard causal language modeling objective (next-token prediction cross-entropy), since the training is supervised fine-tuning without any auxiliary objectives.

##### Compute and Training Duration

Training is conducted on 32 NVIDIA A100 GPUs with 80GB memory each, using bfloat16 precision. Training times per stage for the 13B model:

- **Stage 1** (FLAN-v2, 1 epoch): ~17 hours
- **Stage 2** (5M ChatGPT data, 3 epochs): ~40 hours
- **Stage 3** (~1.8M GPT-4 data, 4 epochs): ~23 hours

Total training time: approximately 80 hours (3.3 days) on 32 A100s. Training hyperparameters (learning rate, batch size, optimizer settings) are not explicitly stated for Orca 2; the paper references Orca 1 for the progressive learning approach but does not provide the specific numerical values used.

##### The Cautious System Instruction at Training Time

Crucially, during all three training stages, any system instruction from the data generation process is replaced with the generic cautious system instruction (quoted in Section 3.4 under Prompt Erasure). This means the model never sees detailed strategy-specifying instructions during training—it only sees the generic cautious message. The Prompt Erasure is applied uniformly across all data sources in the Orca 2 dataset, not just the FLAN-derived portion. This ensures the model's strategy-selection capability is the *only* mechanism for producing appropriately-structured responses; it cannot rely on parsing explicit "use step-by-step reasoning" instructions because it was never trained on them.

---

#### Answer Parsing and Evaluation Infrastructure

The evaluation protocol (Section 5.3) is essential to understanding the paper's results because all numbers depend on robust extraction of final answers from free-form model responses. The authors divide evaluation tasks into three categories:

1. **MCQ (Multiple-Choice Questions)**: Tasks requiring extraction of the selected option from the model's response. This includes classification tasks reformatted as multiple choice. The prompt includes the question followed by answer choices, and often includes formatting guidelines like "At the end, output ###Final answer: {answer choice}."

2. **Exact Match/Span Extraction**: Tasks requiring extraction of an exact answer or a span from the provided context. This includes DROP (discrete reasoning over paragraphs), GSM8K (math word problems), and LAMBADA (word completion).

3. **No extraction required**: Open-ended generation tasks where the response is evaluated as-is, such as abstractive summarization and multi-turn conversation.

For categories requiring extraction, the authors compile "an extensive set of patterns and delimiters" to locate the answer within the model's response. These patterns include phrases like "Final answer", "So, the answer is", "Final option:", and similar concluding statements. Regular expressions are then used to extract the option ID or exact answer text. The same parsing logic is applied to all models' responses for consistency.

The paper also calculates a **format-OK metric**: the percentage of samples from which the parsing logic successfully extracted an answer. This matters because models may not always adhere to formatting guidelines—if one model has a 95% format-OK rate and another has 70%, the reported accuracy numbers are not directly comparable (the lower-format-OK model's "real" accuracy on extractable answers might be higher than reported). The paper acknowledges that "performance of all models could be improved with a better parsing logic."

**Why this matters for interpreting results**: If Orca 2's training on strategically-structured responses makes it better at following formatting guidelines (producing clear "Final answer:" statements), its format-OK rate might be higher than baselines, inflating its relative performance. Conversely, if Orca 2's diverse training causes it to sometimes produce answers in unexpected formats, its format-OK rate might be lower, deflating its relative performance. The paper does not report format-OK rates per model, which is a limitation in transparency.

##### Domain Overlap Transparency

The paper explicitly lists which benchmarks include training data, making them in-domain for zero-shot evaluation: DROP, ARC, RACE, Hellaswag, Lambada, MS Marco, and GSM8K (Section 5.3). The remaining benchmarks (AGIEval, BBH, CRASS, MMLU, TruthfulQA, ToxiGen, HHH, MT-Bench, QMSum, ACI-BENCH) are considered out-of-domain. This transparency is commendable, though the authors note they "cannot completely rule out further data leakage" from LLaMA-2's pretraining data, which is not fully documented.

##### System Messages at Evaluation

For evaluation, two system message configurations are tested for Orca 2 models:
- **Empty system message**: No special instruction, just the task prompt.
- **Cautious system message**: The same generic message used during training.

For baseline models, an empty system message is used. The authors state they use "simple prompts for all models to avoid variations in quality due to prompt engineering, except for general guidelines around answer formats for some tasks." Example formatting guidelines include "At the end, output ###Final answer: {answer choice}" and "select the answer from the provided options." These guidelines are applied uniformly across all models to maintain comparability.

**Greedy decoding** (temperature = 0, top_p = 1) is used for all evaluations to ensure deterministic outputs. This is important because it means any variation in response structure (e.g., whether the model chooses step-by-step vs. direct answer) is a result of the model's learned policy, not sampling noise.

---

#### Summary of Key Design Choices and Their Justifications

- **Prompt Erasure over explicit strategy instruction**: The alternative would be to include the strategy-specifying instructions in training data and rely on the model to execute strategies when prompted at inference time. The paper argues this would be suboptimal because (a) the optimal strategy for a small model may differ from what the teacher was told to use, and (b) providing explicit strategy instructions at inference time shifts the burden to the user (who must know which strategy to request), whereas Cautious Reasoning makes strategy selection automatic.

- **Manual task categorization (1,448 tasks into 126 sub-categories) rather than automated clustering**: Automated clustering by task embedding might group tasks with similar surface features but different reasoning requirements. Manual categorization allows the authors to apply their judgment about which tasks benefit from which strategies *for a 13B model specifically*, which is knowledge that embedding similarity alone would not capture.

- **Progressive training (three stages of increasing data quality) over single-stage training on all data**: The paper argues (by following Orca 1's precedent) that broad instruction-following should be established before fine-grained reasoning capabilities are refined. Training directly on the highest-quality but narrower-distribution GPT-4 data might produce a model that reasons well on in-distribution tasks but fails to follow instructions on out-of-distribution tasks.

- **Loss only on response tokens over loss on full sequence**: Standard for instruction tuning; prevents the model from being penalized for "predicting" the instruction, which would waste capacity and potentially cause the model to assign lower probability to novel instructions at inference time.

- **Packing to 4,096 tokens over dynamic batching**: Maximizes GPU utilization and ensures consistent per-step computational cost, important for efficient training on 32 GPUs.

- **BPE tokenization with digit splitting over alternative tokenizers**: Inherited from LLaMA-2; the digit splitting has known implications for math reasoning (the model must learn multi-digit number composition), but changing the tokenizer would require training from scratch rather than fine-tuning from the LLaMA-2 checkpoint.

- **Generic cautious system message at both training and (optionally) inference time**: Creates a consistent distribution between training and the recommended inference configuration. Using a different system message at inference time is a distribution shift that may degrade the model's strategy-selection behavior.

- **Two different prompts for synthetic medical data**: Creates within-task strategic diversity, potentially teaching the model that output quality (accuracy, groundedness) can vary depending on the care taken during generation, which connects to the hallucination reduction observed in grounding benchmarks.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Reframing the Teacher from Behavioral Target to Strategic Resource Depot

The dominant paradigm in small model training treats the larger teacher model as an oracle whose behavior should be replicated as faithfully as possible. Alpaca, Vicuna, WizardLM, and even Orca 1 all operate under this assumption: the teacher produces outputs, and the student learns to imitate those outputs. The intellectual contribution of Orca 2 is to break this assumption and replace it with a fundamentally different relationship between teacher and student.

The key conceptual move is treating the teacher model not as a *target to be cloned*, but as a *reservoir of possible behaviors* from which the most student-appropriate strategies should be selectively extracted. This shift is articulated in Section 3:

> "Rather than naively imitating powerful LLMs, we treat them as a reservoir of behaviors from which we carefully select those best suited for the task at hand."

This is not a superficial reframing—it changes every downstream design decision. Under the imitation paradigm, the goal is fidelity: make the student's output distribution match the teacher's as closely as possible. Under the reservoir paradigm, the goal is *pedagogical optimization*: select teacher behaviors that will maximally improve the student's capability, even if those behaviors differ from what the teacher would naturally produce. The teacher can be prompted to use a strategy that it doesn't need—GPT-4 can solve many problems with direct answers, but it can be instructed to produce step-by-step reasoning anyway, because that's what the student needs to learn.

This reframing resolves a tension that was latent in prior work but never explicitly addressed. Orca 1's Explanation Tuning showed that training on detailed reasoning traces improves small model performance beyond training on answers alone. But Orca 1 treated the teacher's behavior as given—it collected explanations from GPT-4 under diverse system instructions without systematically asking whether each explanation strategy was *the right one for a 13B model to learn*. The cross-product approach (M task categories × N system instructions) produced many training instances where the strategy was suboptimal for the student, and the student learned from them anyway because imitation was the objective.

The reservoir paradigm introduces an explicit optimization: for each task type, select the reasoning strategy that maximizes the *student's* expected correctness, not the teacher's. If the teacher can produce correct direct answers but the student needs step-by-step decomposition to achieve the same accuracy, then the training data should demonstrate step-by-step decomposition—even though the teacher would never use that strategy on its own. The teacher becomes a controllable engine for generating pedagogically-optimized demonstrations, evaluated by their effect on the student, not by their fidelity to the teacher's default behavior.

This is a fundamental shift rather than an incremental refinement. It redefines the learning problem from "approximate the teacher's conditional distribution p_teacher(answer | prompt)" to "find a student policy that maximizes correctness, using the teacher as a tool to explore the space of possible reasoning strategies." The second formulation is strictly more general and acknowledges that the optimal policy for a small model may lie outside the support of the teacher's natural behavior.

The evidence supporting this innovation's impact is indirect but pervasive: the 47.54% relative improvement of Orca-2-13B over LLaMA-2-Chat-13B on reasoning benchmarks (Figure 4, Table 1) cannot be attributed solely to data volume or teacher quality, since Orca 1 used the same teacher (GPT-4) and a similar data scale. The distinguishing factor is the strategic curation—carefully selecting which reasoning strategies the teacher demonstrates for each task type, rather than collecting demonstrations indiscriminately. The story reordering experiment (Figure 12) provides a microcosm: GPT-4's default answer is wrong, its step-by-step answer is wrong, and only a specifically-engineered multi-step strategy produces correct answers. Training on that strategy—and only that strategy—for this task type produces dramatic improvements (74% for Orca 2 vs. 1.4% for LLaMA-2-Chat-13B), demonstrating the value of selecting from the teacher's behavioral repertoire rather than imitating its default behavior.

---

### Innovation 2: Prompt Erasure as an Implicit Meta-Learning Mechanism for Strategy Selection

The second conceptual innovation is Prompt Erasure—a training technique that creates an information asymmetry between data generation and model training to force the student to learn strategy selection as an emergent capability. This is more subtle than it first appears, and its significance lies in what it reveals about how language models can acquire meta-cognitive skills without explicit meta-cognition objectives.

The typical approach to teaching a model to use different strategies would be to include the strategy specification in the input—for instance, training on examples like "Solve this math problem step by step: [problem]" and "Answer this classification question directly: [question]." At inference time, the user would specify which strategy to use. This approach has two problems: it requires the user to know which strategy is appropriate (shifting the cognitive burden to the human), and it only works if the strategy specification is explicit in the prompt.

Prompt Erasure inverts this. By *removing* the strategy-specifying system instruction during training—replacing "You will be given a task. Use the following steps to solve it: 1. Identify the main theme... 5. Final answer..." with the generic "You are Orca, an AI language model created by Microsoft. You are a cautious assistant."—the model must infer the appropriate strategy from the task description and the structural properties of the answer alone. The strategy is present in the training signal (the teacher's answer exhibits the strategy's structure) but absent from the conditioning information (the prompt doesn't specify it).

What makes this intellectually distinctive is that it demonstrates a form of **implicit meta-learning through standard next-token prediction**. The model is never told "this is a step-by-step reasoning problem" or "choose the right strategy for each task." There is no auxiliary loss, no reinforcement learning signal for strategy selection, no explicit strategy labels. Yet the model learns to deploy different strategies for different tasks because the training data exhibits task-strategy correlations, and the next-token prediction objective incentivizes the model to reproduce those correlations at test time. If math problems in the training data consistently receive step-by-step answers, and classification problems consistently receive direct answers, the model's conditional distribution p(answer | task_description) will naturally encode these associations.

This is a conceptual advance in understanding how instruction tuning works. Prior work treated instruction tuning as teaching the model a mapping from explicit instructions to outputs. Prompt Erasure shows that the instruction is not necessary—the model can learn to *generate its own implicit instructions* (in the form of strategy selection) from the statistical structure of the training data alone. The system message becomes not a command to execute a specific strategy, but a context that activates the model's learned strategy-selection capability.

The evidence for this innovation's effectiveness is the consistent, though modest, improvement from using the cautious system message at inference time. On GSM8K, Orca-2-13B improves from 59.14% (empty system message) to 65.73% (cautious system message)—a 6.6 percentage point gain (Table 1). On DROP, the improvement is even larger: from 57.97% to 70.88%, a 12.9 percentage point gain. These gains occur because the cautious system message matches the training distribution under which strategy selection was learned. The empty system message is a distribution shift—the model was never trained to reason strategically from an empty system message—and performance degrades accordingly.

However, the effect is not uniform. On AGIEval, the cautious message produces a slight *decrease* (49.93% → 48.18%), and on Hellaswag, the decrease is more substantial (81.56% → 79.60%, Figure 5). This inconsistency suggests that Prompt Erasure's benefits are task-dependent and that the mechanism for strategy selection is imperfectly learned. The paper does not deeply analyze when and why the cautious message helps versus hurts, which limits the strength of the innovation claim.

---

### Innovation 3: Strategy Relativity—The Optimal Reasoning Approach Depends on Model Capacity, Not Just Task Type

This insight is the paper's most philosophically significant contribution and the one with the deepest implications for how the field thinks about knowledge distillation and model training. The paper argues that the optimal reasoning strategy for a given task is not a property of the task alone—it is a function of both the task and the model's capacity. A strategy that works for GPT-4 may be inappropriate for a 13B model, and vice versa.

This is articulated explicitly in Section 3:

> "The optimal strategy for a smaller model may differ from that of a more powerful one. For instance, while a model like GPT-4 may easily generate a direct answer, a smaller model might lack this capability and require a different approach, such as thinking step-by-step."

The underlying mechanism is a capacity-strategy interaction. Direct answer generation on complex reasoning tasks requires the model to perform multiple inferential steps implicitly—the attention mechanism must route information across logical dependencies without intermediate tokens to externalize the reasoning. Larger models, with more layers and wider representational capacity, can do this more reliably. Smaller models have less capacity for implicit multi-step computation and therefore benefit from externalizing the reasoning into explicit intermediate tokens (chain of thought), which provides additional computational steps and reduces the per-step representational burden.

This insight challenges a widespread but rarely-examined assumption in the knowledge distillation literature: that the teacher's behavior is always an appropriate learning target for the student. If the teacher uses direct answers because that strategy works for its capacity regime, and the student is trained to imitate those direct answers, the student is being taught to use a strategy that its architecture may not support. The resulting performance gap is not a failure of the student to learn—it is a failure of the *teaching strategy* to be appropriate for the learner.

The intellectual contribution here is to make **strategy relativity** a first-class consideration in model training. Prior work on chain-of-thought prompting (Kojima et al., 2022; Wei et al., 2022) showed that step-by-step reasoning improves model performance, but treated it as a universal intervention—"more reasoning is better"—without asking whether the *magnitude* of the benefit depends on model size. Orca 2 inverts this: rather than asking "what strategy maximizes the teacher's performance?", it asks "what strategy maximizes the student's performance, given the student's capacity constraints?"

This is a fundamental shift rather than an incremental refinement because it changes the optimization objective of the training process. Under imitation learning, the objective is to minimize divergence between the student's and teacher's output distributions. Under strategy relativity, the objective is to maximize the student's task performance, using the teacher as a tool to explore the space of possible training strategies. The second formulation is strictly more general and opens the door to systematic optimization over training strategies as a function of student capacity—a research direction that the paper initiates but does not fully explore.

The evidence for this innovation is the central result: Orca-2-13B matches or exceeds WizardLM-70B and LLaMA-2-Chat-70B on reasoning benchmarks (Figure 4, Table 1) despite being 5–10× smaller. This is not simply "better training data"—it is evidence that strategic training can compensate for capacity gaps in a way that imitation learning cannot. The 13B model with strategy-appropriate training outperforms the 70B model with strategy-agnostic training because the 13B model learned to use reasoning strategies suited to its capacity, while the 70B model may have been taught strategies that don't fully exploit its greater implicit computation ability. The result is not that Orca 2 is "better" than larger models in an absolute sense—it is that strategic training can narrow the capacity gap substantially, which has direct implications for where to invest compute budgets (training better small models vs. deploying larger ones).

---

### Innovation 4: Strategy Diversity as a Training Objective—Not Just a Means to Data Augmentation

Prior work on data diversity in instruction tuning (e.g., FLAN, Super-NaturalInstructions) treated diversity as a means to improve generalization: train on many different tasks, and the model will generalize better to unseen tasks. The diversity was in the *tasks*, not in the *strategies*. Orca 2 introduces a orthogonal dimension of diversity: for the same task type, demonstrate *different reasoning strategies*, and teach the model to select among them.

This is a conceptual shift from "diverse data → better generalization" to "diverse strategies → better meta-reasoning." The former aims to expand the support of the training distribution; the latter aims to teach the model a higher-order skill (strategy selection) that improves performance even on tasks within the training distribution. The two forms of diversity are complementary but serve different purposes.

The significance of this shift becomes apparent when comparing Orca 2 to Orca 1. Orca 1 used Explanation Tuning, which produced strategically diverse training data by combining M task categories with N system instructions to produce M×N training instances. But this diversity was an *incidental byproduct* of the data collection process, not a deliberate training objective. Many of those M×N combinations were suboptimal—asking for step-by-step reasoning on trivial classification tasks, or direct answers on complex multi-step problems—and the model learned from these suboptimal examples because imitation was the objective.

Orca 2 makes strategy diversity a *deliberate curriculum design choice*. The manual categorization of tasks into 126 sub-categories, with a single strategy assigned to each sub-category, is an explicit optimization over the strategy-task mapping. The goal is not to maximize the number of strategy-task combinations (as in Orca 1) but to maximize the *appropriateness* of each combination. The resulting training data is less diverse in the combinatorial sense (1 strategy per sub-category rather than N strategies per category) but more diverse in the pedagogically useful sense—the model sees multiple distinct strategies, each applied to the tasks where it is most effective.

This connects to the broader literature on curriculum learning and pedagogical content knowledge. The innovation is not just that diverse strategies help—it's that *selective strategic diversity*, where each strategy is demonstrated only on tasks where it is appropriate for the student model, is fundamentally different from *indiscriminate diversity*, where strategies are demonstrated across all tasks regardless of appropriateness. The former teaches strategy selection as a skill; the latter may teach that strategy is irrelevant (since the same task appears with multiple strategies, implying any strategy works).

The evidence is the improvement of Orca 2 over Orca 1. Orca-2-13B achieves 59.14% on GSM8K vs. Orca-1-13B's 26.46% (Table 1)—more than doubling the score. On BBH, the improvement is from 47.84% to 50.18% (smaller but consistent). These gains cannot be attributed solely to using GPT-4 instead of ChatGPT for data generation, since Orca 1 already used GPT-4 for a portion of its data. The distinguishing factor is the strategic curation: teaching the model *which* reasoning approach to use for math problems (step-by-step calculation with explicit intermediate results) rather than exposing it to diverse but sometimes inappropriate strategies.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The paper evaluates on 15 benchmarks spanning approximately 100 tasks and over 36,000 unique prompts (Section 5.2). These cover reasoning (AGIEval, BBH, DROP, CRASS, RACE, GSM8K), knowledge and language understanding (MMLU, ARC-Easy, ARC-Challenge), text completion (HellaSwag, LAMBADA), multi-turn conversation (MT-Bench), grounding and abstractive summarization (ACI-BENCH, MS-MARCO, QMSum), and safety/truthfulness (ToxiGen, HHH, TruthfulQA, plus a proprietary automated RAI measurement framework from Magooda et al., 2023). All evaluations use the test split of each dataset and are conducted in a zero-shot setting without exemplars or chain-of-thought prompting, with the exception of GSM8K, DROP, ARC, RACE, Hellaswag, Lambada, and MS-MARCO, whose training portions overlap with the FLAN-v2 data used during training and are therefore considered in-domain (Section 5.3).

- **Base model(s).** All Orca 2 variants are fine-tuned from LLaMA-2 (Touvron et al., 2023) at two scales: 7B and 13B parameters. The choice of LLaMA-2 is pragmatic—it is a widely available, open-weight foundation model with strong baseline performance—and ensures that all comparisons against LLaMA-2-Chat and WizardLM (which share the same LLaMA-2 base) isolate the effect of the fine-tuning methodology rather than the pretraining corpus or architecture. The authors note that the Orca 2 training procedure could be applied to any base LLM (Section 1).

- **Metrics.** The primary metric varies by task category (Section 5.3). For multiple-choice and classification tasks (AGIEval, MMLU, ARC, BBH, RACE, CRASS, HellaSwag, TruthfulQA, HHH, ToxiGen), the metric is exact match accuracy: the percentage of test instances where the extracted answer matches the ground-truth label. For exact match and span extraction tasks (DROP, GSM8K, LAMBADA), accuracy is computed by matching the extracted answer against the gold answer using task-specific normalization. For open-ended generation tasks (MT-Bench, summarization), the paper uses model-based evaluation: GPT-4 as a judge for MT-Bench (scoring 1–10 per turn, following Zheng et al., 2023) and for hallucination rate in grounding tasks (following the framework of Magooda et al., 2023 and Jones et al., 2023), plus standard automatic metrics (BLEU, ROUGE-L, Perplexity) for summarization quality. A format-OK metric—the percentage of samples from which the parsing logic successfully extracts an answer—is computed for tasks requiring extraction, though per-model format-OK rates are not reported numerically.

- **Baselines.** The paper benchmarks against five instruction-tuned model families (Section 5.1):
  - **LLaMA-2-Chat-13B** and **LLaMA-2-Chat-70B** (Touvron et al., 2023): the official chat-tuned versions of LLaMA-2, using reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). These represent the strongest publicly available models sharing the same base architecture as Orca 2.
  - **WizardLM-13B** (V1.2) and **WizardLM-70B** (V1.0) (Xu et al., 2023): LLaMA-2 models fine-tuned via Evol-Instruct, which autonomously generates diverse and complex instruction data. These represent the state of the art in imitation-based instruction tuning at these scales.
  - **Orca-1-13B** (Mukherjee et al., 2023): the predecessor model, fine-tuned from LLaMA-1 with Explanation Tuning. Included to isolate the improvement from Prompt Erasure and strategic curation over the Orca 1 methodology.
  - **ChatGPT (GPT-3.5-Turbo)** and **GPT-4** (OpenAI, 2023): accessed via the Azure OpenAI API version "2023-03-15-preview". These serve as upper-bound references for what much larger, proprietary models achieve, though exact parameter counts for ChatGPT and GPT-4 are not disclosed.
  All baseline models are evaluated under the same prompts and parsing logic as Orca 2, using empty system messages and greedy decoding (temperature = 0, top_p = 1). Inference precision is fp32 for LLaMA-2 and Orca models, and fp16 for WizardLM models (matching their training precision).

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper does not report inference FLOPs or latency. All evaluations use a single greedy decoding pass per prompt—there is no sampling, no beam search, no majority voting, and no test-time compute scaling. The "budget" comparison is entirely about model size: a 13B model vs. a 70B model at a single deterministic output per prompt. This means the paper's claims about efficiency ("matching or exceeding models 5–10× larger") are based on parameter count ratios, not on actual inference cost comparisons. Wall-clock inference time, hardware requirements, and FLOPs-per-token are not measured or reported.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not employ cross-validation for model selection or hyperparameter tuning, nor does it report confidence intervals, standard deviations, or statistical significance tests for any of the reported benchmark scores. All numbers are single-point estimates evaluated once on the test split of each dataset. The evaluation protocol focuses on making answer extraction consistent across models (same prompts, same parsing logic, same regular expressions) to ensure comparability, but does not account for the variance introduced by the stochasticity of training or the finite size of test sets. For the MT-Bench evaluation, the authors note that they used the same GPT-4 endpoint and version for all models to avoid version-dependent variation in judge scores.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Reasoning Benchmarks

The central result is Figure 4 and Table 1: Orca-2-13B achieves a macro-average of 64.49% across six reasoning benchmarks (AGIEval, BBH, DROP, CRASS, RACE, GSM8K), representing a **47.54% relative improvement over LLaMA-2-Chat-13B** (43.71%) and a **28.15% relative improvement over WizardLM-13B** (50.32%). Orca-2-13B also surpasses LLaMA-2-Chat-70B (56.85%) and performs comparably to WizardLM-70B (66.92%) and ChatGPT (67.65%), despite the latter models having approximately 5×, 5×, and an undisclosed but substantially larger number of parameters, respectively. GPT-4 achieves 79.03%.

Breaking this down by individual benchmark (Table 1):

- **GSM8K**: Orca-2-13B with cautious system message achieves 65.73%, more than doubling Orca-1-13B's 26.46% and dramatically outperforming LLaMA-2-Chat-13B (25.09%) and WizardLM-13B (48.60%). The cautious message provides a notable boost over the empty message (59.14% → 65.73%, +6.59 points). This result must be interpreted with the caveat that GSM8K's training split was included in the Orca 2 training data (Section 4.1), making it in-domain. However, the authors note that GSM8K is also in-domain for many baselines, and the gap (65.73% vs. 25.09% for LLaMA-2-Chat-13B) is large enough that data leakage alone cannot explain it. ChatGPT achieves 79.38% and GPT-4 reaches 85.52%.

- **DROP**: Orca-2-13B with cautious system message achieves 70.88%, a 13-point gain over the empty system message (57.97%) and substantially higher than LLaMA-2-Chat-13B (40.73%), WizardLM-13B (45.97%), and even LLaMA-2-Chat-70B (54.11%). The cautious system message appears to be particularly beneficial for this task, which requires discrete reasoning over paragraphs and extracting precise spans. GPT-4 achieves 71.59%, meaning Orca-2-13B with cautious message (70.88%) effectively matches GPT-4 on this benchmark.

- **AGIEval**: Orca-2-13B achieves 49.93% (empty) and 48.18% (cautious), compared to 38.85% for LLaMA-2-Chat-13B and 38.25% for WizardLM-13B. Notably, Orca-2-13B surpasses LLaMA-2-Chat-70B (46.70%) and WizardLM-70B (48.73%), and is competitive with ChatGPT (53.13%). GPT-4 leads at 70.40%. The per-subtask breakdown (Appendix A, Table 6) shows Orca-2-13B's strongest advantages on LSAT-RC (60.97% vs. 38.66% for LLaMA-2-Chat-13B, a 22.31-point gap) and Gaokao-EN (79.41% vs. 64.71%, a 14.70-point gap), with smaller margins on logic and math subtasks.

- **BBH**: Orca-2-13B achieves 50.18% (empty) and 50.01% (cautious), compared to 33.60% for LLaMA-2-Chat-13B and 38.47% for WizardLM-13B. The model surpasses LLaMA-2-Chat-70B (44.68%) and approaches WizardLM-70B (51.08%). ChatGPT achieves 55.38%, GPT-4 reaches 69.04%. The per-task breakdown (Appendix B, Tables 7–10) shows substantial variation: Orca-2-13B excels on Logical Deduction (3 objects) at 72.00% (vs. 44.00% for LLaMA-2-Chat-13B) and Movie Recommendation at 65.60% (vs. 40.40%), but struggles on Formal Fallacies at 10.80% (vs. 25.20% for LLaMA-2-Chat-13B) and Hyperbaton at 58.80% (vs. 55.60%). This task-level heterogeneity suggests that Cautious Reasoning works well for some reasoning subtypes but not others.

- **CRASS**: Orca-2-13B achieves 86.86–87.59%, substantially above LLaMA-2-Chat-13B (61.31%) and WizardLM-13B (67.88%), and comparable to WizardLM-70B (86.13%) and ChatGPT (85.77%). GPT-4 reaches 94.53%. This counterfactual reasoning benchmark appears to be one where Orca 2's strategic training provides particularly large gains.

- **RACE**: Orca-2-13B achieves 82.87% (empty) and 79.16% (cautious), compared to 62.69% for LLaMA-2-Chat-13B and 62.77% for WizardLM-13B. The model surpasses LLaMA-2-Chat-70B (68.79%) and WizardLM-70B (78.96%). ChatGPT scores 67.87% and GPT-4 reaches 83.08%. The paper notes that its ChatGPT run on RACE is approximately 9 points lower than reported in Laskar et al. (2023), attributed to different API endpoints or evaluation prompts.

The cautious system message effect is mixed: it helps substantially on DROP (+12.91 points) and GSM8K (+6.59 points), but provides small or negative effects on AGIEval (−1.75), BBH (−0.17), and RACE (−3.71). The paper does not offer a systematic explanation for when the cautious message helps versus hurts, though it notes (Section 6.1) that "using the cautious system message with both the 7B and 13B models provides small gains over the empty system message" on average.

The 7B variant shows a similar pattern at lower absolute performance: Orca-2-7B achieves 62.62% macro-average (cautious message: 60.60%), which is comparable to LLaMA-2-Chat-70B (56.85%) and exceeds both 13B baselines (LLaMA-2-Chat-13B at 43.71%, WizardLM-13B at 50.32%). On GSM8K, Orca-2-7B with cautious message reaches 55.72%, more than double LLaMA-2-Chat-13B's 25.09%. On DROP, the cautious message again shows a large boost: 69.09% vs. 60.26%.

#### Knowledge and Language Understanding

Table 2 reports results on MMLU, ARC-Easy, and ARC-Challenge:

- **MMLU**: Orca-2-13B achieves 57.73% (59.32% with cautious message), compared to 49.14% for LLaMA-2-Chat-13B and 42.81% for WizardLM-13B. This is a 17.5–38.6% relative improvement. Orca-2-13B is comparable to LLaMA-2-Chat-70B (58.54%) and WizardLM-70B (55.00%). ChatGPT reaches 68.92%, GPT-4 reaches 80.61%. The cautious message provides a small boost (+1.59 points), unlike in reasoning where the effect was mixed. The paper notes that publicly reported few-shot results for LLaMA-2 on MMLU are higher (54.8% for 13B, 68.9% for 70B; Touvron et al., 2023), making the zero-shot comparison more favorable to Orca 2.

- **ARC-Easy**: Orca-2-13B achieves 92.85% (85.31% with cautious), compared to 76.26% for LLaMA-2-Chat-13B and 68.98% for WizardLM-13B. This is a 21.7–34.7% relative improvement. The model surpasses both 70B baselines (LLaMA-2-Chat-70B at 82.20%, WizardLM-70B at 80.68%). ChatGPT achieves 93.73%. The cautious message here produces a substantial *decrease* (−7.54 points), the largest negative effect observed, suggesting that on easy tasks where the answer is straightforward, the cautious message may induce unnecessary complexity.

- **ARC-Challenge**: Orca-2-13B achieves 83.36% (79.95% with cautious), compared to 61.18% for LLaMA-2-Chat-13B and 50.43% for WizardLM-13B. The model surpasses both 70B baselines (67.66% and 71.93%) and approaches ChatGPT (84.73%). GPT-4 reaches 93.26%. The cautious message again decreases performance (−3.41 points).

The consistent pattern across ARC tasks (cautious message hurts) contrasts with DROP and GSM8K (cautious message helps), suggesting that the cautious system message's effect is task-dependent in ways the paper does not fully characterize.

#### Text Completion

Figure 5 reports results on HellaSwag and LAMBADA:

- **HellaSwag**: Orca-2-13B achieves 85.52% (81.19% with cautious), compared to 58.40% for LLaMA-2-Chat-13B and 48.01% for WizardLM-13B—a 46.4–78.1% relative improvement. The model surpasses both 70B baselines (61.35% and 67.96%) and even GPT-4 (77.75%). The cautious message decreases performance by 4.33 points. The paper notes that LLaMA-2-Chat models sometimes refuse to answer HellaSwag questions citing safety concerns, which artificially depresses their scores relative to Orca 2.

- **LAMBADA**: Results are more compressed. Orca-2-13B achieves 63.69% (58.06% with cautious), compared to 38.21% for LLaMA-2-Chat-13B and 51.72% for WizardLM-13B. The model is comparable to LLaMA-2-Chat-70B (73.82%) and WizardLM-70B (72.93%). GPT-4 performs surprisingly poorly at 55.83%, which the paper analyzes: GPT-4 often claims insufficient context or proposes words that don't match the gold label. The paper concludes that "LAMBADA might need additional prompt engineering and may not be suitable for evaluating chat-optimized models."

#### Multi-Turn Open-Ended Conversations

Table 3 reports MT-Bench scores:

- Orca-2-13B achieves an average score of 6.15 (5.72 with cautious message), which is lower than LLaMA-2-Chat-13B (6.64) and WizardLM-13B (6.36). The second-turn scores drop more sharply for Orca 2: Turn 1 scores are 6.69 (vs. 7.17 for LLaMA-2-Chat-13B), while Turn 2 scores are 5.60 (vs. 6.11). The paper attributes this to "the absence of conversations in its training data" and notes that "packing multiple zero-shot examples into the same input sequence" during training does not substitute for genuine multi-turn dialogue data. GPT-4 achieves 9.04, ChatGPT achieves 8.01, and WizardLM-70B achieves 7.76.

This is the one benchmark category where Orca 2 underperforms same-size baselines, and it represents a clear limitation: Cautious Reasoning improves single-turn reasoning but does not transfer to multi-turn conversational ability, which likely requires explicit multi-turn training data.

#### Grounding and Abstractive Summarization

Figure 6 and Table 11 report hallucination rates evaluated by GPT-4 as a judge, averaged across ACI-BENCH, MS-MARCO, and QMSum:

- Orca-2-13B achieves an average hallucination rate of **10.97%**, the lowest among all 13B and 70B models tested. This represents a **76.92% relative reduction** compared to LLaMA-2-Chat-13B (47.53%) and a **61.71% relative reduction** compared to WizardLM-13B (28.65%). It also substantially outperforms LLaMA-2-Chat-70B (45.38%) and WizardLM-70B (15.67%). ChatGPT achieves 6.43%, GPT-4 achieves 2.80%.

- The cautious system message **dramatically increases hallucination** for Orca 2: from 10.97% to 29.00% for the 13B model, and from 19.68% to 37.41% for the 7B model. The paper diagnoses this through manual analysis (Section 6.5): "during the reasoning process led by cautious system message, Orca 2 might extrapolate the information available in the context, and uses the extrapolated content to create the summary. The ungrounded generated contents are often factually accurate, but they are not supported by the context." In other words, the cautious reasoning strategy causes the model to generate plausible-sounding details not present in the source text—a form of hallucination where the model's reasoning about what *should* be true overrides what is *actually stated*.

This finding is significant because it reveals a tension between the cautious reasoning strategy (which improves accuracy on reasoning benchmarks like GSM8K) and groundedness (where it increases hallucination). The paper does not explore whether this trade-off is fundamental or can be mitigated through training data adjustments.

Table 12 reports automatic metrics (BLEU, ROUGE-L, Perplexity) for summarization quality. Orca-2-13B achieves the best or near-best BLEU and ROUGE-L scores among 13B models across the three benchmarks, and is competitive with or exceeds 70B models. The cautious system message consistently reduces n-gram overlap with human references (lower BLEU and ROUGE-L), consistent with the finding that it causes the model to extrapolate beyond the provided context.

#### Safety and Truthfulness

**ToxiGen (discriminative evaluation):** Figure 7 shows that Orca-2-7B achieves the highest accuracy (87.62%) at classifying toxic statements, followed by WizardLM-13B (84.14%) and LLaMA-2-Chat-70B (79.73%). Orca-2-13B scores 76.83%. Figure 8 shows the opposite pattern for neutral (benign) statement classification: Orca-2-13B achieves the highest accuracy (77.80%), followed by WizardLM-70B (73.91%) and Orca-2-7B (65.13%). The paper notes an asymmetry in some models: "Orca-2-7B, WizardLM-13B, LLaMA-2-70B and Orca 1 models perform better at classifying toxic statements than classifying neutral statements," which may cause erasure—"filtering out the content related to specific groups of people even if it is not problematic." Orca-2-13B does not exhibit this asymmetry. Per-category breakdowns (Appendix D.1, Tables 13–14) show substantial variation: Orca-2-13B achieves 100% on Middle East neutral classification but only 43.90% on Latino neutral classification (vs. 43.90% for LLaMA-2-Chat-13B, indicating this category may be inherently difficult). Format-following rates are above 96% for all models in the Orca 2, LLaMA-2, and WizardLM families, but only 79% for Orca 1, which "does not follow task instruction as well."

**TruthfulQA (multiple-choice variant):** Figure 9 shows Orca-2-13B achieving 54.39% with cautious message (50.88% empty), compared to 37.43% for LLaMA-2-Chat-13B and 36.26% for WizardLM-13B. The model is comparable to LLaMA-2-Chat-70B (50.88%) and WizardLM-70B (54.97%). The paper notes that LLaMA-2's reported TruthfulQA numbers in Touvron et al. (2023) are higher because they used generative evaluation with GPT-3 as judge, rather than the multiple-choice variant used here.

**HHH:** Figure 10 shows Orca-2-7B with 80.09% accuracy, substantially above all other models (Orca-2-13B with cautious at 73.30%, WizardLM-70B at 66.06%). The paper notes that LLaMA-2-Chat models "occasionally avoid answering HHH questions" with responses like "I cannot fulfill your request," which may depress their scores because the evaluation expects a choice among options.

**Automated RAI measurement (generative evaluation):** Tables 4–5 report defect rates for Orca-2-13B vs. LLaMA-2-Chat-13B using the automated framework from Magooda et al. (2023):
- **Potentially Harmful Content**: Orca-2-13B shows higher defect rates for violent content (13.47% vs. 0.17%) and hate content (2.26% vs. 0.83%), but lower for sexual content (0.60% vs. 0.00%). The IP leakage rates are comparable or slightly higher for Orca 2 (18.47–21.85% vs. 15.49–18.18%).
- **Jailbreak**: Orca-2-13B shows slightly higher rates for adult content (4.55% vs. 1.00%) and illegal persuasion (7.58% vs. 6.00%), but substantially lower rates for leaking guidelines (24.24% vs. 70.00%).

The paper offers a qualitative explanation: "Orca 2 tends to counter the harmful positions more often (which is penalized by the metric), while models that have gone through RLHF safety training tend to decline to respond more often (which is rewarded by the metric)." This means the defect rate metric may penalize models that engage with harmful prompts even to argue against them, while rewarding models that refuse to answer entirely. The paper acknowledges that Orca 2 has not undergone RLHF safety training, and that these differences may reflect evaluation methodology rather than genuine safety differences.

**ToxiGen (generative evaluation):** Figure 11 reports HateBERT toxicity scores for text continuations. When prompted with toxic statements, Orca-2-7B generates the least toxic continuations (HateBERT score: 10.38), followed by Orca-1-13B (15.48) and Orca-2-13B (16.41). LLaMA-2-Chat-13B scores 18.84, and WizardLM-70B scores the highest at 22.63. When prompted with neutral statements, the ordering is similar: Orca-1-13B (5.55), Orca-2-13B (5.64), and Orca-2-7B (5.81) generate the least toxic continuations, while WizardLM-13B scores highest at 8.02.

#### Story Reordering: A Microcosm of the Method's Impact

Figure 12 demonstrates the method's effect on a single, controlled task. The base LLaMA-2-Chat-13B achieves 1.4% exact match accuracy; WizardLM-13B achieves 0.4%; GPT-4 with default prompt achieves 9.2% and with "explain" prompt achieves 7.8%. GPT-4 with the specialized multi-step strategy prompt (Answer 4 in Figure 3) achieves 74.0%. Orca-2-13B, trained on 5,000 examples generated using that specialized strategy (via GPT-4 under the detailed prompt, then Prompt Erasure to hide the strategy), achieves **51.8% with cautious system message** and **37.4% with empty system message**. Orca-2-7B achieves 57.6% and 26.0%, respectively.

This micro-experiment isolates the core mechanism: the teacher (GPT-4) can solve this task with 74% accuracy when given the right strategy, but its default behavior is poor (9.2%). By generating training data using the optimal strategy and then erasing the strategy specification, the student learns to deploy something close to that strategy at inference time—achieving 51.8% accuracy, or roughly 70% of the teacher's optimal performance—without any explicit instruction to do so. The gap between 74% (teacher with optimal prompt) and 51.8% (student with cautious prompt) represents the loss from model capacity + imperfect strategy inference. The gap between 51.8% (cautious) and 37.4% (empty) represents the value of matching the training distribution's system message.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Prompt Erasure (strategy-specifying vs. generic system message):** The paper does not contain a clean ablation where Prompt Erasure is turned off—i.e., training with the detailed strategy-specifying system instructions included in the student's input and comparing to training with them erased. However, the story reordering experiment (Figure 12) provides indirect evidence. GPT-4 with the detailed strategy prompt achieves 74%. Orca 2, trained on GPT-4's outputs from that prompt but with Prompt Erasure applied, achieves 51.8% with cautious system message. If Prompt Erasure were not applied and the model were trained to reproduce the strategy *when given the detailed prompt*, we would expect the student to perform similarly when given that prompt at inference time—but the paper does not test this configuration. The ablation that is present is the system message at inference time: for story reordering, the cautious message (matching training) yields 51.8%, while the empty message (distribution shift) yields 37.4%—a 14.4-point gap demonstrating that the strategy-selection behavior is indeed conditioned on the system message matching the training distribution.

**Cautious vs. empty system message at inference:** This is tested across nearly all benchmarks (Tables 1–3, Figures 4–12) and serves as a robustness check on whether Prompt Erasure successfully binds strategy-selection to the training system message. The results are task-dependent:
- Strong positive effect: DROP (+12.91 points for 13B), GSM8K (+6.59 points), TruthfulQA (+3.51 points), MMLU (+1.59 points)
- Negligible effect: AGIEval (−1.75 points), BBH (−0.17 points)
- Negative effect: ARC-Easy (−7.54 points), ARC-Challenge (−3.41 points), RACE (−3.71 points), HellaSwag (−4.33 points), LAMBADA (−5.63 points), MT-Bench (−0.43 points on average)
- Large negative effect on hallucination: grounding average hallucination rate increases from 10.97% to 29.00% (+18.03 points)

The paper does not systematically characterize what task properties predict the direction and magnitude of the cautious message effect, which is a significant analytical gap.

**Model scale (7B vs. 13B):** The paper trains and evaluates both sizes, allowing a limited scale comparison (Figures 4, 5; Tables 1–3). The 13B model consistently outperforms the 7B model, with gaps ranging from small (BBH: 50.18% vs. 45.93%, a 4.25-point gap) to large (GSM8K: 59.14% vs. 47.23%, an 11.91-point gap). The 7B model with cautious reasoning still matches or exceeds LLaMA-2-Chat-70B on several benchmarks (e.g., DROP: 69.09% for 7B cautious vs. 54.11% for 70B; RACE: 80.79% vs. 68.79%). This suggests that the benefits of Cautious Reasoning are present at both scales and that even a 7B model with strategy training can outperform a 5× larger model without it.

**Comparison to Orca 1 (effect of strategic curation + Prompt Erasure over Explanation Tuning):** Orca-1-13B is included as a baseline in all major tables. The improvements are substantial: on GSM8K, Orca-2-13B more than doubles Orca-1-13B's accuracy (59.14% vs. 26.46%, +32.68 points). On DROP, the improvement is smaller (57.97% vs. 53.63%, +4.34 points with empty message, but +17.25 points with cautious message). On BBH, the improvement is modest (50.18% vs. 47.84%, +2.34 points). On HellaSwag, the improvement is dramatic (85.52% vs. not directly reported for Orca 1 in Figure 5, but the bar chart shows a large gap). These improvements cannot be attributed solely to the base model change (LLaMA-1 for Orca 1 vs. LLaMA-2 for Orca 2) or the teacher model used for data generation, since Orca 1 also used GPT-4 for a portion of its data. The distinguishing factor is the strategic curation and Prompt Erasure methodology.

**Teacher model quality (ChatGPT vs. GPT-4 data in progressive training):** The progressive training stages use ChatGPT data (Stage 2) followed by GPT-4 data (Stage 3). The paper does not ablate this ordering—e.g., training only on GPT-4 data, or reversing the order—so the marginal contribution of each data source cannot be isolated. The progressive training design is inherited from Orca 1 and is not specifically justified for Orca 2's methodology.

**Data quantity (817K new data vs. millions of existing data):** The paper adds 817K Orca 2-specific examples to the 6 million examples from Orca 1 (5M ChatGPT + 1M GPT-4). No ablation studies vary the amount of Orca 2 data to measure its marginal value, nor is there an experiment that trains on Orca 2 data alone to isolate its contribution. The relative importance of data quantity vs. data quality (strategic curation) is therefore unknown.

**Task-specific data (story reordering):** The 5,000 example story reordering experiment (Figure 12) serves as a limited ablation on the effect of task-specific strategically-generated data. Adding 5,000 examples (approximately 0.6% of the total Orca 2 dataset) produces a 50+ point improvement on this specific task (from 1.4% for LLaMA-2-Chat-13B to 51.8% for Orca-2-13B with cautious message), demonstrating that strategically structured data has disproportionately large effects on in-distribution task performance.

**Safety training (effect of no RLHF):** The paper repeatedly notes that Orca 2 has not undergone RLHF safety training, unlike LLaMA-2-Chat. This is not an ablation but a design choice. The safety evaluation results (Section 6.6, Tables 4–5) show that Orca 2's safety characteristics are qualitatively different from RLHF-trained models—it tends to counter harmful positions rather than refuse to answer—but the paper does not train an RLHF-augmented Orca 2 to test whether RLHF on top of Cautious Reasoning would improve or harm reasoning performance.

### Critical Assessment

#### Claim 1: "Orca 2 significantly surpasses models of similar size"

This claim is strongly supported by the evidence. On the six reasoning benchmarks (Table 1, Figure 4), Orca-2-13B achieves 64.49% macro-average vs. 43.71% for LLaMA-2-Chat-13B and 50.32% for WizardLM-13B—a 47.54% and 28.15% relative improvement, respectively. On MMLU (Table 2), Orca-2-13B scores 57.73% vs. 49.14% and 42.81%. On Hellaswag (Figure 5), the gaps are even larger (85.52% vs. 58.40% and 48.01%). These are not marginal improvements; they are transformative differences that move a 13B model from well below 70B-model performance to parity or superiority.

However, there are important caveats. First, the LLaMA-2-Chat baselines were trained with RLHF for safety, which is known to sometimes reduce benchmark performance (the "alignment tax"). A more informative baseline might be LLaMA-2 fine-tuned on the same FLAN data without RLHF, to isolate the effect of Cautious Reasoning from the effect of removing RLHF. Second, the evaluation protocol (same prompts, same parsing logic) may favor models that are better at following formatting instructions—Orca 2 was trained to produce strategically-structured outputs, which might make its answers easier to parse. The paper reports a format-OK metric but does not provide per-model numbers, so this potential confound cannot be assessed.

#### Claim 2: "Orca 2 attains performance levels similar or better to those of models 5–10× larger"

This claim is supported with qualifications that depend on the benchmark. On reasoning (Figure 4), Orca-2-13B at 64.49% exceeds LLaMA-2-Chat-70B at 56.85% (+7.64 points) and approaches WizardLM-70B at 66.92% (−2.43 points) and ChatGPT at 67.65% (−3.16 points). On MMLU (Table 2), Orca-2-13B at 57.73% is comparable to LLaMA-2-Chat-70B at 58.54% and WizardLM-70B at 55.00%. On ARC-Challenge, Orca-2-13B at 83.36% exceeds both 70B baselines.

But the claim does not hold uniformly. On multi-turn conversation (MT-Bench, Table 3), Orca-2-13B (6.15) underperforms LLaMA-2-Chat-13B (6.64) and WizardLM-13B (6.36), and is substantially below LLaMA-2-Chat-70B (6.82) and WizardLM-70B (7.76). On text completion (LAMBADA, Figure 5), Orca-2-13B (63.69%) is 10 points below LLaMA-2-Chat-70B (73.82%). The "5–10× larger" claim is accurate for reasoning and knowledge benchmarks, but not for conversation or text completion.

Moreover, the comparison is not to the best possible 70B model that could be trained with similar techniques—it is to existing 70B models trained with different methodologies. A 70B model trained with Cautious Reasoning and Prompt Erasure might substantially outperform Orca-2-13B, making the "matching 5–10× larger" claim a statement about specific existing models rather than a fundamental capacity-for-capacity equivalence. The paper does not train a 70B Orca 2 variant, so the scaling properties of the method are unknown.

#### Claim 3: "Prompt Erasure makes Orca 2 a Cautious Reasoner that learns to determine the most effective solution strategy for each task"

This is the paper's central mechanistic claim, and the evidence for it is indirect. The strongest evidence is the story reordering experiment (Figure 12): GPT-4 achieves 74% with the optimal strategy prompt, and Orca 2 achieves 51.8% after training on those strategically-generated outputs with Prompt Erasure—without ever seeing the strategy specification. The model has clearly learned something about how to approach this task that goes beyond surface-level imitation, since the baseline models (which did not receive this training) score near 0–1%.

However, the paper does not provide direct evidence that the model is actually *selecting among strategies* as opposed to simply learning a fixed strategy per task type from the statistical associations in the training data. The term "Cautious Reasoning" implies a meta-cognitive decision process—the model considers the task, evaluates which strategy would be best, and then deploys it. But the observed behavior is equally consistent with a simpler mechanism: the model has learned that certain task features (e.g., math problems, story reordering tasks) are statistically associated with certain output structures (e.g., step-by-step reasoning, structured multi-step analysis) in the training data, and it reproduces those structures. This is "cautious" in the sense of being task-appropriate, but it does not necessarily involve the reflective strategy selection that the term implies.

The paper could have strengthened this claim with experiments that directly test strategy selection: for example, giving the model a task and asking it explicitly to state which strategy it will use before producing the answer; or evaluating whether the model's chosen strategy changes appropriately when the same task is presented in different formats (e.g., a math problem formatted as multiple choice vs. open-ended); or testing whether the model can follow an explicit instruction to use a *different* strategy than the one it would naturally select (demonstrating that strategy choice is controllable rather than fixed). None of these experiments are performed.

The mixed effect of the cautious system message further complicates this claim. If the cautious message genuinely activates a strategy-selection capability, why does it *reduce* performance on ARC-Easy and HellaSwag? One interpretation is that on easy tasks, the model's "cautious" strategy selection causes it to overthink—applying step-by-step reasoning where a direct answer would be more reliable—but the paper does not test this by comparing the actual output structures produced under empty vs. cautious messages.

#### Claim 4: "Excessive emphasis on imitation may restrict the potential of smaller models"

This is more of a philosophical position than a directly testable claim, but the paper provides circumstantial evidence. Orca 2, which explicitly does not imitate the teacher's default behavior but selectively extracts student-appropriate strategies, substantially outperforms Orca 1, which used a less selective approach, and WizardLM, which uses an imitation-based Evol-Instruct method. The story reordering experiment (Figure 12) is particularly instructive: GPT-4's default behavior on this task is wrong (9.2%), and imitating that default behavior would teach the student to be wrong. By instead extracting a strategy that GPT-4 can execute but doesn't naturally choose, and teaching only that strategy, the student becomes competent (51.8%).

However, the comparison is again confounded by model architecture differences (Orca 1 uses LLaMA-1), data quantity differences, and teacher model quality differences. To cleanly test the claim, one would need an experiment where the same base model is trained on the same data generated by the same teacher, with the only variable being whether the teacher's default strategy or a student-optimized strategy is used. This experiment is not performed.

#### Weaknesses in the Experimental Design

**No statistical reporting.** The paper reports single-point accuracy numbers with no confidence intervals, standard deviations, or significance tests. With test sets ranging from ~100 questions (ARC-Challenge has 1,172 in the full set, but the paper does not specify the exact test size used) to 500 (MATH, GSM8K) to thousands (MMLU has ~14K questions), the precision of these point estimates varies substantially. Differences of 1–2 percentage points—which the paper often interprets as meaningful (e.g., the cautious message effect on AGIEval)—may be within sampling noise for smaller test sets. The absence of any uncertainty quantification weakens the reliability of small-effect claims and makes it impossible to assess whether the mixed cautious-message effects are real or noise.

**Format-OK metric not reported.** The paper acknowledges that "models may not always adhere to these formatting guidelines" and that "performance of all models could be improved with a better parsing logic," but never reports the format-OK rates per model. If Orca 2 has a 98% format-OK rate while WizardLM-13B has 85%, the reported accuracy gap overstates the true capability gap (because WizardLM's lower reported accuracy partly reflects parsing failures rather than incorrect answers). Without these numbers, the comparison is not fully controlled.

**In-domain vs. out-of-domain contamination.** The paper transparently lists which benchmarks overlap with training data (Section 5.3): DROP, ARC, RACE, Hellaswag, Lambada, MS Marco, and GSM8K. The largest improvements are indeed on some of these: GSM8K (25.09% → 59.14%), Hellaswag (58.40% → 85.52%), and DROP (40.73% → 57.97%). But large improvements are also seen on benchmarks claimed to be out-of-domain: CRASS (61.31% → 86.86%), BBH (33.60% → 50.18%), and ARC-Challenge (61.18% → 83.36%). The paper notes that "we do not have detailed information about the data used for training the base model (LLAMA-2) and hence we cannot completely rule out further data leakage." This is an honest acknowledgment, but it means the zero-shot generalization claims should be interpreted cautiously—some of the out-of-domain performance may reflect contamination through the base model's pretraining rather than genuine zero-shot reasoning ability.

**Single base model family.** All experiments use LLaMA-2 (7B and 13B). The method's effectiveness may depend on LLaMA-2's specific pretraining characteristics—its instruction-following ability, its in-context learning capacity, its knowledge coverage. Applying the same Cautious Reasoning methodology to a different base model (e.g., Mistral, Falcon, or a non-LLaMA architecture) would test whether the improvements are tied to LLaMA-2's particular strengths. The paper acknowledges this as a limitation (Section 7: "Orca 2's performance is likely to correlate strongly with the distribution of the tuning data") but does not test it.

**The cautious system message effect is unexplained.** The paper reports the cautious-vs-empty system message difference across many benchmarks but never systematically characterizes when it helps, when it hurts, or why. This is a significant analytical gap because the cautious message is supposed to be the mechanism that activates the strategy-selection capability learned through Prompt Erasure. If its effect is negative on several benchmarks, that raises questions about whether Prompt Erasure successfully taught strategy selection or whether the cautious message simply induces a generic response pattern (e.g., more verbose, more structured) that happens to improve some tasks and degrade others.

**No comparison to chain-of-thought prompting of baselines.** The paper evaluates all models in zero-shot settings without chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting. But prior work (Kojima et al., 2022; Wei et al., 2022) has shown that simply adding "Let's think step by step" to the prompt substantially improves LLM reasoning. A comparison of Orca 2 (which uses learned strategy selection) against LLaMA-2-Chat-70B with explicit CoT prompting would test whether learned strategy selection offers benefits beyond what explicit prompting can achieve. If LLaMA-2-Chat-70B with CoT matches Orca-2-13B, then the contribution is not better reasoning per se but automation of CoT-like behavior. The paper briefly discusses this in the limitations (Section 7: "while the model demonstrates very strong performance in zero-shot setting, it does not show the same gains of using few-shot learning compared to other, specially larger, models") but does not report the explicit comparison.

**Missing experiments that would strengthen the paper:**
- Training a 70B Orca 2 variant to test whether Cautious Reasoning is complementary to or redundant with scale.
- An ablation where Prompt Erasure is turned off (training with strategy-specifying instructions included) to isolate its contribution.
- An experiment where the model is explicitly asked to state its chosen strategy before answering, to verify that strategy selection is conscious rather than statistical.
- Systematic measurement of output structure (e.g., what fraction of outputs exhibit step-by-step vs. direct-answer formatting) under empty vs. cautious system messages.
- Evaluation of Orca 2 with explicit CoT prompting to test whether learned strategy selection outperforms explicit prompting.
- Evaluation on a completely held-out task category (not in FLAN, not in any training data) to test genuine zero-shot generalization of strategy selection.
- Human evaluation of grounding/hallucination, since the GPT-4-as-judge framework has known biases.

#### Assessment Summary

The experiments convincingly demonstrate that Orca 2 models substantially outperform comparably-sized instruction-tuned models on reasoning benchmarks, and that they can match or exceed certain 70B models on these tasks. The strategic curation of training data and the inclusion of diverse reasoning strategies are clearly beneficial relative to standard instruction tuning and even to Orca 1's Explanation Tuning. The story reordering experiment provides a clean demonstration of the method's mechanism at work.

However, the central claim about *Cautious Reasoning* as a learned meta-cognitive capability remains more asserted than demonstrated. The evidence is consistent with a simpler interpretation—the model learns statistical associations between task types and output structures—and the paper does not provide the experiments that would distinguish these interpretations. The mixed and often negative effect of the cautious system message further complicates the narrative. The paper's value lies more in its practical demonstration that strategic data curation can dramatically improve small model reasoning and in its identification of Prompt Erasure as a useful technique, rather than in establishing a precise mechanistic account of how strategy selection is learned.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### Hard Problems Remain Unsolved: Test-Time Compute Amplifies Existing Capability But Cannot Create It

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper operates under the implicit assumption that the base model possesses the necessary knowledge to solve the task, and that the bottleneck is reasoning procedure rather than factual knowledge. This is stated explicitly in Section 2.1:

> "Instruction tuning, while very beneficial for teaching the model how to solve a task, does not necessarily teach the model new knowledge. Hence instruction tuned models will be always limited by the knowledge learned during pre-training."

And again in Section 7:

> "post-training, while significantly beneficial in teaching the model how to solve a task, it does not necessarily teach the model new knowledge."

**The consequence.** On tasks requiring knowledge the base model did not acquire during pretraining, Cautious Reasoning offers no benefit regardless of strategy quality. The model can reason flawlessly about facts it doesn't possess and will still produce wrong answers. This creates a hard ceiling: no amount of strategic training can compensate for missing knowledge. A 13B model simply stores fewer facts than a 70B model or GPT-4, and Orca 2 inherits LLaMA-2's knowledge boundaries. The paper's positioning of Orca 2 as a "reasoning engine over knowledge provided to the model in its context window, or when specialized to narrower domains" (Section 7) is an acknowledgment that the method is only effective when the required knowledge is either (a) already in the pretraining corpus, (b) provided in-context, or (c) injected via domain-specific fine-tuning. For open-domain question answering, fact verification, or any task requiring broad world knowledge, the knowledge gap between a 13B and 70B model likely dominates any reasoning strategy improvements.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper does not directly measure a knowledge-vs-reasoning decomposition—it cannot distinguish whether a wrong answer came from a reasoning failure or a knowledge gap. However, the pattern of results is suggestive: the largest improvements are on tasks that are reasoning-intensive but knowledge-light (DROP, CRASS, BBH logical deduction, story reordering), while improvements on knowledge-heavy tasks like MMLU are more modest (57.73% vs. 49.14% for LLaMA-2-Chat-13B, a 17.5% relative gain vs. 47.5% on reasoning benchmarks). The explicit inclusion of the GSM8K training split (Section 4.1) also means that strong math performance (65.73% with cautious) partly reflects memorization of problem structures rather than pure reasoning generalization. The paper does not evaluate on a held-out math dataset with structurally novel problems to isolate reasoning from memorization.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges this limitation transparently and suggests the appropriate deployment model: pair Orca 2 with retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) where knowledge is supplied in context, or specialize it to narrow domains via fine-tuning. No mitigation at the training level is proposed or tested—the limitation is fundamental to the post-training paradigm and cannot be addressed without modifying pretraining.

---

### The Cautious System Message Is Unreliable: It Helps on Some Tasks, Actively Harms on Others, and Dramatically Increases Hallucination in Grounded Generation

**The assumption or constraint.** Prompt Erasure is designed to bind the model's strategy-selection capability to the generic cautious system message used during training. The paper assumes that using this message at inference time will reliably activate the learned strategic reasoning behavior, producing better or equivalent outputs compared to an empty system message.

**The consequence.** In practice, the cautious system message produces highly inconsistent effects across tasks. On DROP, it adds 12.91 points (57.97% → 70.88%). On GSM8K, it adds 6.59 points (59.14% → 65.73%). But on ARC-Easy, it *subtracts* 7.54 points (92.85% → 85.31%). On ARC-Challenge, it subtracts 3.41 points. On RACE, it subtracts 3.71 points. On grounded summarization, it more than *doubles* the hallucination rate from 10.97% to 29.00% (Table 11). A practitioner deploying Orca 2 cannot know in advance whether the cautious message will improve or degrade performance on their specific task distribution—the effect is unpredictable without per-task evaluation. This undermines the core usability claim that the model has learned to autonomously select appropriate strategies: if strategy selection were working correctly, the cautious message (which activates it) should not produce *worse* outputs than the empty message (which represents a distribution shift from training).

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The effect is documented across nearly every benchmark in Sections 6.1–6.5 (Tables 1–3, Figures 4–8, Table 11). The paper provides a partial diagnosis for the hallucination increase (Section 6.5): "during the reasoning process led by cautious system message, Orca 2 might extrapolate the information available in the context, and uses the extrapolated content to create the summary. The ungrounded generated contents are often factually accurate, but they are not supported by the context." But no equivalent diagnosis is offered for the ARC, RACE, or HellaSwag degradations. The paper does not analyze whether the cautious message causes the model to switch strategies inappropriately (e.g., using step-by-step reasoning where a direct answer would be more accurate) or whether it introduces some other behavioral change (e.g., increased verbosity that confuses the answer parser).

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper reports the numbers without systematically characterizing when the cautious message helps vs. hurts, and provides no guidance for practitioners on when to use it. The implicit recommendation appears to be "evaluate both configurations on your task and pick the better one," which reduces the method from "the model has learned strategy selection" to "try both system messages and see which works." This is a significant weakness in the deployability story.

---

### Multi-Turn Conversational Ability Is Not Improved and May Be Degraded Relative to Baselines

**The assumption or constraint.** The training data for Orca 2 consists almost entirely of single-turn, zero-shot or few-shot task prompts. There is no multi-turn dialogue data in the Orca 2 dataset, and the paper states that "packing multiple zero-shot examples into the same input sequence" during training is the only mechanism that could simulate multi-turn interaction (Section 6.4). The method implicitly assumes that improved single-turn reasoning will transfer to, or at least not harm, multi-turn conversational settings.

**The consequence.** On MT-Bench (Table 3), Orca-2-13B scores 6.15 average vs. 6.64 for LLaMA-2-Chat-13B and 6.36 for WizardLM-13B—meaning it *underperforms* same-size baselines on the only multi-turn benchmark tested. The second-turn degradation is sharper: Turn 1 score of 6.69 (vs. 7.17 for LLaMA-2-Chat-13B) drops to 5.60 at Turn 2 (vs. 6.11). The cautious message makes this worse (5.72 average, 5.31 Turn 2). This means that Orca 2's reasoning improvements come at the cost of conversational coherence and follow-up handling. For applications that require multi-turn interaction—chatbots, tutoring systems, interactive assistants—Orca 2 may be a worse choice than standard instruction-tuned models despite its superior single-turn reasoning.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 3 provides the only evidence. The paper acknowledges the limitation explicitly: "The average second turn score of Orca-2-13B is lower than the first turn score, which can be attributed to the absence of conversations in its training data." The paper does not test whether the degraded multi-turn performance is caused by (a) the absence of dialogue data, (b) the Cautious Reasoning training causing the model to treat each turn as an independent reasoning problem rather than part of a conversation, or (c) some other mechanism.

**Mitigation status.** The paper notes that "this ability can be enhanced by packing multiple zero-shot examples into the same input sequence" (Section 6.4), but this is precisely what was done during training and it produced the reported degradation. The paper suggests that "it is part of our future work to improve Orca 2's multi-turn conversational ability," acknowledging that the current method does not address this capability and that explicit multi-turn training data would likely be needed.

---

### The Comparison to 5–10× Larger Models Is Not FLOPs-Matched and Does Not Account for Inference Cost, Pretraining Cost, or the Cost of Data Generation

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's headline claim—that Orca 2 "matches or exceeds models 5–10× larger"—is a parameter-count comparison. It treats model size as the relevant cost metric while ignoring the substantial compute invested in (a) pretraining the 70B baseline models, (b) generating the Orca 2 training data (GPT-4 API calls for ~1.8M examples, ChatGPT calls for ~5M examples), and (c) training Orca 2 itself (~80 hours on 32 A100s). The implicit assumption is that the cost of data generation and fine-tuning is negligible compared to the cost of deploying a larger model—but this is not quantified and may not hold for all deployment scenarios.

**The consequence.** For a practitioner deciding between deploying a 70B open-weight model (e.g., LLaMA-2-Chat-70B) or fine-tuning a 13B model into Orca 2, the relevant comparison is total cost, not parameter count. The cost of generating 1.8M GPT-4 responses for training data, plus ~80 GPU-hours for fine-tuning, must be amortized over the number of inference queries the model will serve. If the model will serve millions of queries, the upfront training cost is negligible and Orca 2's 5× lower per-query inference cost is a clear win. But if the model will serve thousands of queries, the training cost may dominate, making the larger off-the-shelf model more economical. The paper provides no cost analysis, no FLOPs comparison, and no guidance on the breakeven point.

Furthermore, the comparison is not FLOPs-matched in the inference sense: a 70B model could be given additional test-time compute (chain-of-thought prompting, majority voting, best-of-N sampling) that would improve its performance. The paper compares Orca 2 (which uses learned reasoning strategies) against larger models with greedy decoding and no test-time compute. A fairer comparison would give the larger model an equivalent inference compute budget—for example, Orca-2-13B produces step-by-step outputs that are longer and more computationally expensive than the direct answers the 70B model might produce. If both models were allocated the same inference FLOPs budget, the larger model might close or reverse the gap.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper does not measure or report inference FLOPs, wall-clock latency, or total training cost breakdown. It does not compare against larger models with chain-of-thought prompting. The only efficiency metric is parameter count. The story reordering experiment (Figure 12) inadvertently reveals the scale of the data generation cost: GPT-4's best strategy achieves 74% on this task, but to train Orca 2 to reach 51.8%, the authors generated 5,000 GPT-4 responses (likely costing hundreds of dollars in API fees) for a single narrow task. Scaling this to 1,448 tasks and 817K examples implies a substantial data generation budget that is not accounted for in any cost comparison.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper focuses on capability comparisons and does not engage with cost analysis. For research purposes, this is acceptable—the goal is to demonstrate what is possible, not to optimize for cost. But for practitioners, the absence of cost analysis is a significant gap. The paper does not suggest that future work should quantify the cost-performance tradeoff.

---

### The Method Has Not Been Demonstrated on a Non-LLaMA Base Model, and the Reliance on LLaMA-2's Specific Properties Is Unknown

**The assumption or constraint.** All experiments use LLaMA-2 (7B and 13B) as the base model. The paper states that "Orca 2 training could be applied any base LLM" (Section 1), but this is an untested assertion. The method's effectiveness may depend on properties that vary across model families: LLaMA-2's instruction-following ability after pretraining, its in-context learning capacity, its tokenizer (which splits digits, affecting math reasoning), its knowledge coverage, and its sensitivity to system messages.

**The consequence.** A practitioner using a different base model (Mistral, Falcon, Phi, Gemma, or a custom pretrained model) cannot assume that applying the same Cautious Reasoning methodology will produce comparable improvements. The strategy-task mapping that was manually optimized for LLaMA-2-13B—which strategies are "appropriate for a 13B model" on which tasks—may not transfer to models with different capacity profiles. A 7B Mistral model might have different optimal strategies than a 7B LLaMA-2 model, and the categorization of 1,448 tasks into 126 sub-categories with specific strategy assignments would need to be revalidated or redone. The progressive training schedule (FLAN → ChatGPT → GPT-4 data) was also designed for LLaMA-2 and may not be optimal for a base model with different pretraining characteristics.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Only LLaMA-2 results are reported. There is no experiment applying Orca 2 training to a different base model family, nor any analysis of which LLaMA-2 properties are essential to the method's success. The paper does report both 7B and 13B scales, which provides limited evidence that the method works across model sizes within the same architecture family—but this is a weak test of generalization compared to cross-architecture evaluation.

**Mitigation status.** The paper explicitly notes the limitation in Section 7: "Orca 2's performance is likely to correlate strongly with the distribution of the tuning data. This correlation might limit its accuracy in areas underrepresented in the training dataset such as math and coding." But this acknowledgment is about data distribution, not base model architecture. The suggestion that the method "could be applied any base LLM" remains an untested hypothesis. The release of Orca 2 weights (which the paper announces) partially mitigates the limitation for the LLaMA-2 family—practitioners using LLaMA-2 can directly adopt the released model—but provides no evidence for other architectures.

---

### The Safety Profile Is Qualitatively Different from RLHF-Trained Models and Not Adequately Characterized

**The assumption or constraint.** Orca 2 has not undergone RLHF safety training, unlike LLaMA-2-Chat. The paper assumes that the safety properties of the teacher models (ChatGPT, GPT-4), combined with Azure OpenAI content filtering during data generation, will transfer to the student via instruction tuning, producing a model that is reasonably safe for research use. The paper states: "We hypothesize that since the teacher models (ChatGPT, GPT-4) have gone through various steps of safety tuning, and the use of content filtering in the data generation process, Orca 2 might have learned some aspects of those through instruction tuning" (Section 6.6).

**The consequence.** The safety evaluation in Tables 4–5 reveals that Orca 2's safety behavior is qualitatively different from RLHF-trained models—and the evaluation metrics may not capture which behavior is actually safer. For violent content, Orca-2-13B shows a 13.47% defect rate vs. 0.17% for LLaMA-2-Chat-13B. But the paper's analysis reveals that this is partly a metric artifact: "Orca 2 tends to counter the harmful positions more often (which is penalized by the metric), while models that have gone through RLHF safety training tend to decline to respond more often (which is rewarded by the metric)." In other words, Orca 2 may engage with harmful prompts to argue against them—a behavior that could be either more helpful (persuading the user) or more dangerous (amplifying harmful content by discussing it), depending on context—while LLaMA-2-Chat refuses to engage entirely. The existing evaluation framework cannot distinguish these outcomes.

For practitioners, this creates uncertainty. Deploying Orca 2 in a user-facing application requires understanding whether "counters harmful positions" is actually safer, less safe, or context-dependent relative to "refuses to answer." The paper does not resolve this. Furthermore, Orca 2 lacks the refusal training that RLHF provides, meaning it may comply with harmful requests that an RLHF-trained model would decline—a failure mode the generative ToxiGen evaluation (Figure 11) partially captures but does not fully characterize.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Tables 4–5, Figures 7–11, and the per-category ToxiGen breakdowns (Appendix D.1). The paper is transparent about the limitations: "Orca 2 models have not undergone RLHF training for safety" (Section 1), and "any model used as annotator... is a proxy and may come with its own weaknesses and biases" (Section 6.6). However, the safety evaluation does not include human evaluation, red-teaming, or adversarial testing beyond the automated framework, leaving significant gaps in the safety characterization.

**Mitigation status.** The paper explicitly recommends that "additional analysis is needed to assess potential harm or bias in the proposed application" and states that Orca 2 "is solely designed for research settings" (Section 7). The authors "hope for better regulations and standards from government and technology leaders around content harms for AI technologies in future" (Section 7). The mitigation is therefore scoping—the model is for research, not production—rather than technical. The paper does not propose specific safety training augmentations or evaluate whether RLHF could be applied to Orca 2 without degrading its reasoning improvements (the "alignment tax" question).

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper shifts the conversation around small-model training from "how closely can we approximate a larger model's output distribution?" to "what reasoning strategies should we teach, given that the student has different computational constraints than the teacher?" This is a reframing of the knowledge distillation problem rather than a paradigm shift—the tools (instruction tuning, synthetic data from large models, progressive training) are the same as in prior work—but the objective function is different. Instead of minimizing KL divergence between student and teacher output distributions, the implicit objective becomes maximizing student task accuracy by selecting teacher behaviors appropriate to the student's capacity, even when those behaviors differ from what the teacher would naturally produce.

The magnitude of this reframing matters because it changes which research directions appear promising. Under the imitation paradigm, natural next steps are better teacher models, more teacher data, and architectural improvements that close the capacity gap. Under the strategy-relativity paradigm, the next steps are systematic characterization of which strategies work for which model capacities on which task types, automated strategy-to-task mapping, and training objectives that explicitly optimize over strategy selection. The paper initiates this shift without fully realizing it—the manual categorization of 1,448 tasks into 126 sub-categories with hand-chosen strategies is a proof of concept, not a scalable solution—but the conceptual foundation is laid.

The work also partially reconciles the tension in the literature between results showing that small models can approximate large ones through imitation (Alpaca, Vicuna, WizardLM) and results showing that imitation models fail on genuine reasoning tasks (Gudibande et al., 2023). Orca 2 suggests that both can be true: imitation works for style transfer but fails for reasoning because it teaches the *what* (the teacher's output structure) without the *why* (the strategy selection that generated that structure). By teaching strategy selection explicitly—through diverse strategic demonstrations and Prompt Erasure to force the model to infer the strategy—Orca 2 narrows the reasoning gap substantially. The 47.54% relative improvement over LLaMA-2-Chat-13B on reasoning benchmarks (Figure 4, Table 1) is evidence that the reasoning gap was partly a *teaching gap* rather than purely a capacity gap.

This reframing makes certain research directions more attractive:

- **Strategy optimization as a function of model capacity** becomes a first-class research problem. Rather than assuming chain-of-thought is universally beneficial, the field should ask: for a 1B, 7B, 13B, and 70B model, what is the optimal reasoning strategy for each task type? The answer likely forms a spectrum—smallest models need extensive externalized reasoning, mid-size models benefit from mixed strategies, and largest models can internalize more computation.

- **Automated strategy-to-task mapping** becomes a necessary scaling step. The paper's manual categorization of 1,448 tasks is informative but expensive and brittle. Research on training a meta-model to predict which strategy will maximize a given model's accuracy on a given task becomes newly tractable using the paper's framework (generate data with multiple strategies per task, train on each, measure which produces the best student performance, train a predictor).

- **Verifier-guided strategy selection** becomes a natural extension. Rather than relying on the model's implicit strategy selection (which the paper shows is unreliable—the cautious message hurts on ARC-Easy, RACE, and Hellaswag), an explicit verifier could score candidate strategies at inference time and select the one expected to maximize correctness, analogous to how the Orca 1/2 teacher models are prompted with different strategies and the best output is chosen.

Certain research directions become less attractive:

- **Blind scaling of imitation data** (more teacher outputs, more diverse prompts) without attention to *which* teacher strategies are being imitated. The paper's story reordering experiment (Figure 12) demonstrates that GPT-4's default behavior is wrong (9.2% accuracy), yet that is what a naive imitation pipeline would collect. Quantity without strategic curation can actively teach incorrect approaches.

- **Training on a single reasoning strategy for all tasks.** The paper shows that different strategies benefit different tasks, and that the cautious system message—which activates learned strategy selection—can degrade performance on tasks where the selected strategy is inappropriate (e.g., ARC-Easy: −7.54 points). Uniform strategy application is provably suboptimal.

- **Assuming that what works for GPT-4 works for smaller models.** The paper explicitly demonstrates strategy relativity (Section 3) and provides evidence through the Orca-2-13B vs. GPT-4 comparison: the 13B model needs step-by-step decomposition where GPT-4 can answer directly, and teaching the 13B to imitate GPT-4's direct answers would be counterproductive.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Systematic characterization of the strategy-capacity interaction surface.** The paper demonstrates that optimal reasoning strategy depends on model capacity (Section 3) but does not characterize *how*. A natural follow-up would train Orca 2-style models at a range of scales (1B, 3B, 7B, 13B, 30B, 70B) from the same base model family, using the same training data and strategies, and measure which strategies maximize accuracy at each scale for each task type. The hypothesis is that smaller models benefit more from externalized reasoning (step-by-step, recall-then-generate), while larger models can internalize more computation and benefit less. Measuring the shape of this interaction—does the benefit of step-by-step reasoning decrease monotonically with model size? Is there a threshold where direct answers become optimal?—would provide practical guidance for strategy selection and theoretical insight into how reasoning capability relates to model depth and width. The paper's 7B vs. 13B comparison provides two data points; a full sweep would produce the first scaling law for reasoning strategy effectiveness.

**Automated strategy-to-task assignment via meta-learning.** The manual categorization of 1,448 FLAN tasks into 126 sub-categories with hand-assigned strategies (Section 4.1) is the most labor-intensive and least scalable component of the method. A follow-up could treat strategy assignment as a learnable function: for each task type, generate training data under multiple candidate strategies (direct answer, step-by-step, extract-then-answer, recall-reason-generate), fine-tune a small student model under each strategy, measure the resulting accuracy, and train a lightweight classifier to predict the best strategy from task features (e.g., task description embedding, task type label, average prompt length). If such a classifier generalizes to held-out task categories, it would automate the strategy selection step and make the method applicable to arbitrary new task collections without human categorization. The paper's finding that the optimal strategy is model-specific (Section 3) implies this predictor would need to be conditioned on model capacity as well, adding a second input dimension.

**Prompt Erasure with explicit strategy supervision.** The paper's Prompt Erasure technique relies on implicit learning of strategy selection through statistical associations in the training data, and the mixed effect of the cautious system message (helps on DROP and GSM8K, hurts on ARC-Easy and RACE) suggests this implicit learning is imperfect. A strong follow-up would augment Prompt Erasure with an explicit auxiliary loss: during training, in addition to the next-token prediction objective, the model predicts which strategy was used to generate each response (a classification head over strategy types). This would make strategy recognition an explicit supervised task rather than an emergent property. The test would be whether the augmented model shows more consistent benefits from the cautious system message—i.e., whether the negative effects on ARC-Easy, ARC-Challenge, and RACE are eliminated or reduced. This would also enable interpreting the model's internal strategy representations by probing the classification head.

**Testing Cautious Reasoning on a non-LLaMA base model family.** All experiments use LLaMA-2 (7B and 13B). The paper claims the method "could be applied any base LLM" (Section 1), but this is untested. A direct replication on Mistral-7B, Falcon-7B, or Gemma-7B—keeping the training pipeline, data mixture, and strategy assignments identical—would test whether the benefits of Cautious Reasoning are architecture-dependent or general. Because LLaMA-2 has particular properties (digit-splitting tokenizer affecting math, specific pretraining data mixture, specific instruction-following ability), the method's effectiveness might vary substantially. A negative result—Orca 2 training producing smaller or no gains on a different base model—would be highly informative, suggesting that the method interacts with pretraining properties in ways the paper does not characterize. A positive result would substantially strengthen the generality claim.

**Combining Cautious Reasoning with chain-of-thought prompting of baselines.** The paper evaluates all models in zero-shot settings without explicit chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting, yet prior work (Kojima et al., 2022) shows that adding "Let's think step by step" to the prompt substantially improves reasoning. The critical open question is: does Orca 2's learned strategy selection outperform explicit CoT prompting of a larger model? Concretely, compare Orca-2-13B with cautious system message against LLaMA-2-Chat-70B with the prompt "Let's think step by step. [task description]" on AGIEval, BBH, and GSM8K. If LLaMA-2-Chat-70B + CoT matches or exceeds Orca-2-13B, then the paper's contribution is automation of CoT-like behavior rather than a fundamentally new reasoning capability—still valuable, but less transformative. If Orca-2-13B substantially outperforms LLaMA-2-Chat-70B + CoT, it would demonstrate that diverse strategic training produces capabilities beyond what explicit CoT prompting can extract from a larger model.

**Grounding-aware reasoning strategy training to resolve the hallucination tension.** Section 6.5 reveals a tension: the cautious system message, which improves reasoning accuracy on DROP and GSM8K, dramatically increases hallucination in grounded summarization (from 10.97% to 29.00% average hallucination rate, Table 11). The paper's diagnosis—that cautious reasoning causes the model to extrapolate factually-plausible-but-ungrounded content—suggests that strategy selection learned through Prompt Erasure does not distinguish between open-ended reasoning (where extrapolation can be useful) and grounded generation (where it produces hallucinations). A follow-up would augment the training data with explicit grounding annotations: for summarization and extraction tasks, include examples where the cautious strategy is *not* used (direct extraction from context) and where strategy selection is explicitly conditioned on whether the task requires groundedness. The test would be whether this targeted training eliminates the hallucination increase under the cautious message while preserving the reasoning improvements. This would also require a more nuanced set of strategies that includes "grounded-extract" and "grounded-summarize" variants that constrain the model to the provided context.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**On-device reasoning engines for privacy-sensitive domains.** Orca-2-7B achieves 60.60% macro-average on reasoning benchmarks with cautious system message (Figure 4), matching or exceeding LLaMA-2-Chat-70B (56.85%) while being 10× smaller. For applications requiring local inference—medical note summarization where patient data cannot leave the device, legal document analysis under data residency requirements, or enterprise knowledge base querying where proprietary information must stay on-premises—a 7B model that can reason at the level of a 70B cloud-hosted model directly enables deployment scenarios that were previously infeasible. The grounding evaluation (Table 11) shows Orca-2-13B achieving a 10.97% hallucination rate—lower than any 13B or 70B baseline—which is particularly relevant for clinical and legal settings where factual accuracy is paramount. The caveat that the cautious message doubles hallucination (29.00%) means deployment should use the empty system message for summarization tasks, accepting the modest accuracy reduction on reasoning in exchange for groundedness.

**Cost-efficient batch inference for reasoning-heavy workloads at scale.** For organizations running large-scale batch evaluation—curriculum publishers scoring student answers, testing services evaluating exam responses, or research groups annotating datasets—the inference cost difference between a 13B and 70B model is approximately 5× per query (based on parameter count scaling, though the paper does not measure this directly). Orca-2-13B's performance parity with WizardLM-70B on reasoning benchmarks (64.49% vs. 66.92%, Figure 4) and superiority on grounding (10.97% hallucination vs. 15.67%, Table 11) means the cost savings come with no accuracy penalty and in some cases an improvement. The upfront cost of data generation (~1.8M GPT-4 calls) and training (~80 GPU-hours on 32 A100s, Section 4.2) must be amortized, but for high-volume batch pipelines processing millions of queries, the 5× per-query savings dominate. A concrete deployment: an educational technology company evaluating student free-response answers against rubrics could use Orca-2-13B instead of a 70B model, achieving similar reasoning quality at one-fifth the inference cost, with the 10.97% hallucination rate providing acceptable reliability for non-diagnostic assessment.

**Specialization to narrow domains via the synthetic data recipe.** The paper's story reordering experiment (Figure 12) demonstrates that adding just 5,000 strategically-generated task-specific examples (approximately 0.6% of the total Orca 2 dataset) improves performance from 1.4% (LLaMA-2-Chat-13B baseline) to 51.8% (Orca-2-13B with cautious message)—a 50+ point gain. This implies that organizations with domain-specific reasoning tasks (medical diagnosis from case descriptions, legal analysis from case law, engineering design from specifications) can use the paper's recipe—identify the optimal reasoning strategy for the domain by testing multiple GPT-4 prompts, generate 5,000–10,000 strategically-structured examples, apply Prompt Erasure, and fine-tune Orca 2—to create a specialized model that dramatically outperforms generic instruction-tuned models on that domain. The cost is modest (5,000 GPT-4 API calls plus fine-tuning on a single GPU for a few hours) relative to the performance gain. This is the paper's most directly actionable finding for practitioners: the synthetic data pipeline scales down to individual tasks and does not require the full 817K dataset.

**Retrieval-augmented reasoning over external knowledge bases.** The paper explicitly positions Orca 2 as a "reasoning engine over knowledge provided to the model in its context window" (Section 7), acknowledging that post-training does not add factual knowledge. Paired with a retrieval system, Orca 2's strong performance on DROP (70.88% with cautious message, matching GPT-4's 71.59%) and CRASS (87.59%, approaching GPT-4's 94.53%)—tasks that require reasoning over provided context—makes it well-suited for RAG applications where the model must synthesize, compare, and deduce from retrieved documents. A deployment where a small on-device model queries a knowledge base and reasons over the results could use Orca-2-7B, which at 62.62% on reasoning benchmarks (Figure 4) provides strong reasoning at the smallest practical scale, keeping both retrieval and inference local. The grounding improvements (low hallucination with empty system message) are particularly valuable for RAG, where fabricating information not present in the retrieved context directly undermines the system's reliability.
