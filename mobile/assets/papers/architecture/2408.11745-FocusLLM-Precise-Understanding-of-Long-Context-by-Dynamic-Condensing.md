# FocusLLM: Precise Understanding of Long Context by Dynamic Condensing

**ArXiv:** [2408.11745](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.11745)

## 🎯 Pitch

FocusLLM stretches a vanilla LLaMA's context window to 400K tokens without losing any information—achieving 99% passkey retrieval accuracy where compression methods drop to zero. It does this by learning to re-read the full document on-the-fly, condensing only the parts relevant to the current decoding step instead of permanently discarding tokens upfront.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

FocusLLM introduces a framework for extending the fixed context length of any decoder-only LLM to process sequences up to 400K tokens while preserving precise understanding of the full input. The paper proposes **dynamic condensing** — a mechanism that appends a small, evolving fragment of local context (a "dynamic prompt") to each text chunk to extract only the currently relevant information — and **parallel decoding**, which aggregates these per-chunk candidate tokens into the local context for next-token generation. Applied to LLaMA-2-7B (4K native context) with only ~2B additional trainable parameters and trained on sequences under 8K tokens using 0.5B tokens total, FocusLLM achieves 99% passkey retrieval accuracy at 400K tokens and outperforms all baselines on both LongBench (36.17 average) and ∞-Bench (44.03 average), establishing that training-efficient context extension without information loss is achievable only when the model can dynamically re-evaluate which parts of the full context are relevant at each decoding step rather than discarding tokens upfront.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Extending Context Without Losing Information

The fundamental challenge this paper addresses is deceptive in its simplicity: **how can we give a language model access to extremely long sequences — hundreds of thousands of tokens — without requiring it to process every token simultaneously, yet without discarding any information it might later need?** This is not merely an engineering optimization problem. It is a tension between two competing demands that have, until now, seemed fundamentally incompatible: computational tractability versus perfect information preservation.

The paper frames this through a sharp observation that prior work has largely overlooked: **token importance changes dynamically during the decoding process**. A token that appears irrelevant when first encountered — a piece of background exposition, a seemingly tangential detail — may become critically important twenty paragraphs later when a specific question references it. Conversely, entire stretches of text may carry no information relevant to the current decoding step, even though they might be essential for a different query. This dynamic nature of relevance means that any method that makes *irreversible decisions about which tokens to keep and which to discard* will fundamentally suffer from what the paper terms **information loss**.

The magnitude of this problem is evident in the paper's motivating example: the Passkey Retrieval task (Mohtashami and Jaggi, 2024), visualized in Figure 1. In this task, a model reads a long, mostly noisy document containing a hidden passkey number, and must later answer "What is the passkey?" As context length increases from 2K to 400K tokens, compression-based methods like Activation Beacon see their accuracy degrade sharply, dropping to near zero at extreme lengths. This is not because the model lacks the capacity to store the passkey — the information exists in the text. Rather, the model has *already discarded the chunk containing the passkey* during its irreversible compression step, and by the time the question reveals which information is needed, it is too late to recover it.

This insight — that compression creates an **irreversible commitment to what matters** — is the intellectual core of the paper. It transforms the context extension problem from "how do we compress the past?" to "how do we keep the entire past accessible and let the current decoding step decide what to attend to?"

### Why the Problem Matters: Three Practical Urgencies

The paper's motivation is grounded in several converging pressures that make this problem practically urgent:

**1. The quadratic complexity wall is real and growing.** The standard transformer self-attention mechanism has $O(L^2)$ computational complexity in sequence length $L$ (Vaswani et al., 2017). For a 400K-token document, this means the attention matrix alone would require approximately 160 billion pairwise interactions per layer. Beyond the computation, the Key-Value cache for such a sequence would consume enormous GPU memory during autoregressive generation. Even if one could afford to train on such sequences, the inference cost for every generated token would be prohibitive. As the authors note in the introduction, "the computational complexity of transformers grows quadratically with the sequence length, rendering the training process prohibitively expensive."

This problem is intensifying as the demand for long-context processing grows. Applications like document-grounded question answering, multi-document summarization, repository-level code understanding, and long-form dialogue require models to reason over hundreds of pages of text. The gap between what applications demand and what transformer architectures can efficiently provide is widening.

**2. LLMs exhibit poor extrapolation to unseen lengths.** Even when models are fine-tuned on moderately longer sequences (e.g., extending from 4K to 32K via positional interpolation), they often fail catastrophically when tested on lengths substantially beyond their training distribution. The paper cites evidence that "LLMs exhibit poor extrapolation performance for longer sequences, even after additional fine-tuning" (Section 1). This means that simply training on longer examples is not a scalable solution — the model learns to handle the specific lengths it sees, but does not develop a *mechanism* for arbitrarily long contexts.

This extrapolation failure is particularly problematic because the needed context length in real applications is often unknown in advance. A system that works well on 32K-token documents but collapses at 100K tokens creates a sharp reliability cliff that makes deployment risky for open-ended use cases.

**3. High-quality long-text training data is scarce.** The paper explicitly notes that "acquiring high-quality long-text datasets, which are essential for training and fine-tuning, is exceedingly difficult" (Section 1). Most naturally occurring long documents — books, academic papers, legal contracts — are structured in ways that do not map cleanly to the autoregressive training objective. Long-range dependencies may span hundreds of pages, making it challenging for models to learn meaningful patterns. Moreover, the volume of available long-form training data is orders of magnitude smaller than the web-scale data used for pretraining. A method that can learn to handle long contexts while training on *shorter* sequences — as FocusLLM does, training on sequences under 8K — avoids this data bottleneck entirely.

Taken together, these three pressures create a clear imperative: we need a method that **(a)** reduces per-step computational complexity, **(b)** generalizes to lengths far beyond those seen during training, and **(c)** does not rely on scarce long-text training data. These are the criteria against which the paper evaluates both prior work and its own contribution.

### Prior Approaches and Where They Fall Short

The paper surveys three broad families of prior work, each addressing the long-context problem from a different angle. Understanding their specific limitations is essential to appreciating what FocusLLM does differently.

#### Positional Encoding Modifications (Length Extrapolation)

The first family of approaches modifies the positional encoding scheme to allow models trained on shorter sequences to accept longer inputs at inference time. Key representatives include:

- **Positional Interpolation** (PI; Chen et al., 2023a): Linearly rescales the position indices so that a longer sequence fits within the range of positions the model was trained on. For example, if originally trained on positions 0–4095, a 32K-token sequence would have its positions mapped to 0–511 (compressed by a factor of 8), then interpolated back to the model's expected scale.
- **NTK-Aware Scaled RoPE**: Adjusts the rotary position embeddings (RoPE) by scaling the base frequency, which changes how positional information is encoded across different frequency bands without retraining.
- **YaRN** (Peng et al., 2023): Combines interpolation with a "NTK-by-parts" strategy that treats different frequency bands differently, achieving better extrapolation to 128K.

The paper's results in Table 1 reveal the practical limits of these approaches. When tested on language modeling perplexity, PI degrades from 9.21 perplexity at 4K to >100 perplexity at 32K on PG19 — essentially catastrophic forgetting of the language structure at the new length. NTK fares somewhat better (11.5 at 16K, 37.8 at 32K), but still shows rapid degradation. YaRN-128K maintains reasonable perplexity in the 6–7 range out to 100K, which is impressive, but the paper argues this comes at a cost: these methods "fail to address the distraction issue caused by noisy content within extended texts" (Section 6.1). The model can *process* the tokens, but it struggles to *distinguish relevant from irrelevant information* within them.

The deeper limitation is conceptual: these methods treat the problem as one of *representing positions*, not one of *selecting relevant context*. They give the model the capacity to see all tokens, but they do not provide a mechanism for efficiently navigating or prioritizing among them. The quadratic complexity remains, and the model must compute attention over the entire sequence at every layer, paying the full $O(L^2)$ cost regardless of how much of the context is actually needed.

#### Full-Attention Fine-Tuning on Long Sequences

A second family directly fine-tunes models on longer sequences, often combining positional encoding modifications with continued training:

- **LongAlpaca-16K** (Chen et al., 2023b): Fine-tunes on 16K-length instruction-following data using LoRA.
- **LongChat-32K** (Li et al., 2023): Trains on curated conversational data at 32K length.
- **LongLlama** (Tworkowski et al., 2024): Fine-tunes with a contrastive training objective that helps the model distinguish relevant from irrelevant context, using 7B training tokens with all parameters trainable.

The paper acknowledges that these methods can achieve strong results — LongLlama, for example, shows competitive perplexity in Table 1 — but identifies two fundamental drawbacks:

**Cost.** Fine-tuning a 7B-parameter model on 7B tokens of long-context data, with all parameters trainable, is enormously expensive. The paper contrasts this with FocusLLM's training budget: 0.5B tokens total, trained on sequences under 8K, with only ~2B additional trainable parameters (roughly 1/3 of the full model's parameters). This represents a more than 10× reduction in training data and a significant reduction in trainable parameters.

**Length ceiling.** Perhaps more importantly, these models have a hard maximum context length determined by their training data length. A model fine-tuned on 32K sequences cannot process 128K sequences without additional intervention. The paper notes this limitation explicitly: "their limitation is the restricted supported length" (Section 4.2). For applications where the exact input length is unpredictable — a user might paste a 5-page document or a 200-page report — this creates a practical deployment constraint.

The paper positions FocusLLM as achieving comparable or better performance without this hard length ceiling, since chunks are processed independently and the number of chunks can scale arbitrarily at inference time.

#### Context Condensation and Token Dropping

The third family — and the one most directly contrasted with FocusLLM — compresses or selectively drops tokens to keep the effective context length within a manageable window:

- **StreamingLLM** (Xiao et al., 2023): Observes that initial "sink tokens" in the sequence receive disproportionately high attention scores in early layers. It keeps these sink tokens plus a sliding window of recent tokens, discarding everything in between. This achieves smooth streaming generation with constant memory, but the discarded middle tokens are permanently lost.
- **Activation Beacon** (Zhang et al., 2024a): Compresses each segment of text into a small number of "beacon tokens" (special trainable tokens whose representations are optimized to summarize the segment). The beacon tokens replace the original segment in the Key-Value cache, dramatically reducing memory. However, this compression is one-shot and irreversible — once a segment is condensed into beacon tokens, the original tokens cannot be recovered.
- **AutoCompressor** (Chevalier et al., 2023): Trains models to compress long contexts into summary tokens that are then fed as soft prompts to a frozen language model. Similar to Activation Beacon, the compression is done once and cannot be revised.
- **InfLLM** (Xiao et al., 2024): Stores processed context into memory units and retrieves relevant units using attention scores. This is closer to a retrieval-augmented approach — it keeps all tokens but selectively retrieves them — but still makes retrieval decisions based on attention scores computed at the time of encoding, not at the time of decoding.

The paper identifies a shared, fundamental flaw in this family, which it calls **information loss**: "these methods overlook the fact that token importance changes dynamically during the decoding process: tokens previously considered unimportant may become crucial in later decoding steps" (Section 1). In other words, these methods make irreversible decisions about what information to keep *before* knowing what the model will need.

This is not a subtle point — it has dramatic empirical consequences. The paper provides Table 8 in Appendix F, showing Activation Beacon's performance on ∞-Bench tasks that require precise comprehension of the full context:
- **Passkey Retrieval**: 1.69% accuracy (essentially random for a task with a numeric answer)
- **KV Retrieval**: 0.00% accuracy (completely fails to retrieve key-value pairs)
- **Number String**: 1.69% accuracy

These near-zero scores on tasks specifically designed to test whether models can find and use information from earlier parts of the context provide the clearest evidence that compression-based methods fundamentally cannot handle tasks requiring global context awareness. The information was present in the original text, but it was discarded during compression.

The paper's Figure 1 visually dramatizes this point: as context length increases, Activation Beacon's passkey retrieval accuracy drops, because at longer lengths, more compression is applied and the probability that the passkey-bearing segment gets adequately preserved in the beacon tokens decreases.

### Memory-Enhanced Approaches and Their Limits

A related family, which the paper treats as a distinct category in Section 6.2, uses explicit memory mechanisms:

- **Unlimiformer** (Bertsch et al., 2024): Encodes long texts into a retrieval index and retrieves relevant segments during decoding via cross-attention.
- **CEPE** (Yen et al., 2024): Uses a small encoder to process long input chunks sequentially, then feeds the encoded representations to a decoder via cross-attention layers.

These approaches avoid irreversible compression by keeping all encoded chunks accessible. However, the paper identifies their own limitations:

- **Memory scaling**: "the memory length does not extrapolate well, and expanding the memory still incurs substantial computational costs" (Section 6.2). CEPE, for instance, experiences OOM on ∞-Bench due to "substantial memory usage" (Section 4.2).
- **Architectural modifications**: CEPE requires adding cross-attention layers to the decoder, which means it cannot be applied to *any* decoder-only LLM without architectural changes. FocusLLM, by contrast, works with standard decoder-only transformers via parameter-efficient fine-tuning.

### How FocusLLM Positions Itself

The paper's self-positioning, when read carefully, makes a precise and falsifiable claim: **methods that avoid information loss can be both training-efficient and performance-competitive, provided they have a mechanism for dynamically re-evaluating relevance at each decoding step.**

This is a specific, testable hypothesis that distinguishes FocusLLM from three prior camps simultaneously:

- **Against positional encoding methods**: FocusLLM claims that *extrapolation alone is not enough* — the model needs an architectural mechanism for relevance filtering, not just longer position indices. The evidence: FocusLLM outperforms YaRN-128K on downstream tasks (Table 2: 36.17 vs. 32.40 on LongBench) despite YaRN maintaining competitive perplexity.

- **Against full-attention fine-tuning**: FocusLLM claims that *training on short sequences with the right architecture can match or exceed training on long sequences*. The evidence: FocusLLM achieves an average of 36.17 on LongBench vs. 34.70 for LongChat-32K, with 1/10 the training budget and shorter training sequences.

- **Against compression methods**: FocusLLM claims that *irreversible compression is fundamentally incompatible with precise long-context understanding*. The evidence: near-zero performance of Activation Beacon on retrieval tasks (Table 8) vs. FocusLLM's 95.76% on Passkey Retrieval and 83.56% on Number Retrieval at ∞-Bench's average length of 145.1K tokens.

- **Against memory methods**: FocusLLM claims that *chunk-wise processing with token-level summarization can be more memory-efficient than full-context encoding*. The evidence: CEPE experiences OOM on ∞-Bench while FocusLLM completes it, and FocusLLM's memory footprint grows more slowly than CEPE's (Figure 3).

The paper's key conceptual move — and what makes it novel — is the **dynamic prompt**. Prior compression methods ask: "What information in this chunk will be important in the future?" and make a best guess at compression time. FocusLLM instead asks, at every decoding step: "What information in this chunk is relevant *right now*, given what I am about to generate?" By appending a fragment of the local context (the query, the partial answer, the current generation state) to each chunk, it transforms the problem from *predicting future relevance* (which is essentially impossible without knowing the future decoding trajectory) to *evaluating current relevance* (which is a standard attention operation).

This shift — from *compression-as-prediction* to *compression-as-query-conditioned-extraction* — is the intellectual contribution that underlies the empirical results. It explains why FocusLLM can train on sequences under 8K tokens and generalize to 400K tokens at inference time: the model learns a general skill of "given a query, extract relevant information from a chunk," and this skill transfers to any number of chunks regardless of how far the relevant information is from the query.

The paper also explicitly positions itself as a framework rather than a single model. The techniques — dynamic condensing, parallel decoding, candidate tokens — are presented as architectural patterns that can be applied to "any decoder-only LLM" (abstract). This is not incidental; it is a deliberate framing that invites application to larger models, different model families, and models with longer native context windows (which the paper explores in Section 5.4 with its chunk size analysis).

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

FocusLLM is a framework that sits **on top of** an existing decoder-only language model (like LLaMA-2), adding a small set of trainable parameters that allow the model to process extremely long documents by breaking them into independently processable chunks. The core problem it solves is **how to let each decoding step selectively attend to any part of a 400K-token document without paying the quadratic cost of full self-attention over the entire sequence and without permanently discarding information that might be needed later.**

The "shape" of the solution is: instead of asking the model to compress the past *in anticipation* of future needs, FocusLLM keeps every chunk intact in its original form, but at each decoding step, it asks each chunk a specific question — "what information do you contain that is relevant to what I'm about to generate *right now*?" — by appending a fragment of the current decoding state (the dynamic prompt) to each chunk before processing it. This shifts the problem from *irreversible compression* to *query-conditioned extraction*, which is both parallelizable and information-preserving.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The FocusLLM framework has four major components:

1. **Base Decoder LLM (Frozen):** A pretrained decoder-only model (e.g., LLaMA-2-7B-Chat with 4K native context length). Its parameters are kept frozen to preserve its original language modeling and reasoning abilities. It serves two roles: (a) processing individual chunks with dynamic prompts to extract candidate tokens, and (b) generating the final next token by attending to the concatenation of candidate tokens and local context.

2. **Dynamic Condensing Module (Trainable):** A small set of additional linear projection parameters (`$W^c_Q, W^c_K, W^c_V, W^c_O$`) added at each transformer layer. These parameters process a special "candidate token" appended to each chunk, allowing the candidate token's hidden state to selectively aggregate information from its chunk that is relevant to the appended dynamic prompt. Only these parameters are trained; the base model weights are frozen.

3. **Chunk Partitioner:** A preprocessing step that divides the long input into chunks, each not exceeding the model's native context length (4K tokens for LLaMA-2). The final chunk serves as the "local context" — the tokens the model is actively generating from. All preceding chunks form the "memory tokens" that must be accessible but are processed independently.

4. **Parallel Decoding Orchestrator:** The runtime mechanism that (a) injects the current dynamic prompt behind each memory chunk, (b) processes all chunks through the modified decoder in parallel (since chunk processing is independent), (c) collects the candidate token representations from each chunk, and (d) concatenates their key-value representations with the local context's key-value representations for the final attention computation that generates the next token.

**Information flow for one decoding step:** Long document → chunk partitioner splits into `$k$` memory chunks + 1 local context chunk → dynamic prompt (fragment of local context) appended behind each memory chunk → each augmented chunk processed independently through frozen base decoder with trainable `$W^c$` parameters → one candidate token per chunk extracted → candidate token KV representations concatenated with local context KV representations → final frozen decoder layer generates next token → token appended to dynamic prompt for next step.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First, the formal notation and data segmentation** (Section 2.1 of paper): How the long sequence is divided into chunks and what "memory tokens" vs. "local context" means, since all subsequent mechanisms depend on this decomposition.
- **Second, the dynamic condensing mechanism** (Section 2.2): The two-step process of dynamic prompt injection and candidate token generation — this is the core intellectual contribution and must be understood before parallel decoding makes sense.
- **Third, the parallel decoding mechanism** (Section 2.3): How candidate tokens from multiple chunks are aggregated into the local context, the complexity reduction, and why this enables scaling to arbitrary lengths.
- **Fourth, the training procedure** (Section 3): The two complementary loss functions, the data construction, and why training on short sequences generalizes to very long ones.
- **Fifth, the trainable parameter design**: The specific linear projection modifications, why parameter-efficient fine-tuning is chosen over full fine-tuning, and what the ~2B additional parameters represent.
- **Sixth, the inference-time procedure**: How the system operates at deployment, including dynamic prompt evolution, chunk processing, and the end-to-end generation loop — this is where the theoretical architecture meets practical implementation.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily an **architectural innovation paper** whose core idea is that long-context understanding can be achieved by decoupling the *storage* of long-range context (which requires capacity but is trivially parallelizable) from the *retrieval* of relevant information (which requires attention but operates over a small window), with a dynamic query mechanism bridging the two.

---

#### 3.4.1 Chunk Partitioning and Notation

Before any processing happens, the long input sequence must be divided into units that the base model can handle. The paper establishes the following formal conventions (Section 2.1):

Given a long sequence with `$S$` tokens `$\{x_1, x_2, ..., x_S\}$`, the framework partitions them into two categories:

- **Memory tokens:** `$\{x_1, ..., x_m\}$` — the tokens comprising the earlier parts of the document that the model may need to reference but is not currently generating from. These tokens represent the "long-range context" that makes the sequence long in the first place.

- **Local context:** `$\{x_{m+1}, ..., x_S\}$` — the most recent tokens, with length not exceeding the model's default context length, denoted as `$L$`. For the LLaMA-2-7B base model used in experiments, `$L = 4096$` tokens. This is the active generation window; the model autoregressively predicts tokens within this region using standard self-attention.

The memory tokens are further partitioned into chunks `$C_1, C_2, ..., C_k$`, where each chunk's size also does not exceed `$L$`. These chunks "can represent distinct documents or a single long document" — the framework is agnostic to whether the long input is one coherent text or multiple concatenated sources.

The original (frozen) decoder model is denoted as `$F_{\text{dec}}$` with hidden dimension `$d_{\text{dec}}$`. The modified model with additional trainable parameters — the version that can perform dynamic condensing — is denoted as `$F'_{\text{dec}}$`.

**Why this decomposition matters:** By capping chunk size at `$L$`, the framework ensures that every individual forward pass through the base model operates within its native context length, where its attention mechanism is well-calibrated. The base model never "sees" a 400K-token sequence directly — it sees at most `$L + \text{|dynamic prompt|}$` tokens per chunk. This is what enables FocusLLM to transform an `$O(S^2)$` problem into `$O(k \cdot L^2)$`, where `$k = S/L$` is the number of chunks, which is much more tractable when `$L \ll S$`.

**The chunk size is a hyperparameter** that trades off between processing efficiency (fewer, larger chunks mean fewer forward passes) and precision (smaller chunks enable finer-grained relevance extraction). The paper investigates chunk sizes of `$\{256, 512, 1024, 2048\}$` in Section 5.4 (Figure 5) and finds that perplexity remains "relatively stable" across this range, confirming that the model is not sensitive to this choice. During training, the chunk size is randomly sampled from `$\{64, 128, 256, 1024, 2048\}$` to encourage robustness.

---

#### 3.4.2 Dynamic Condensing: Dynamic Prompt Injection

The dynamic condensing mechanism has two sequential sub-steps. The first — dynamic prompt injection — addresses the fundamental question: **how do we tell each chunk what information is currently needed?**

The key observation is that compression cannot be done in a vacuum. If you ask a model "summarize this chunk," it will produce a generic summary that may omit details crucial for a specific downstream query. If you instead ask "summarize this chunk *given that I'm trying to answer question Q*," the summary can be targeted and lossless with respect to that particular information need.

The **dynamic prompt** is the mechanism for encoding the current information need. Formally, for each chunk `$C_i$`, FocusLLM appends a small fragment of the local context behind it:

$$\hat{C}_i \leftarrow \{C_i; x_{m+j}, ..., x_S\} \quad i = 1, ..., k; \; 1 \leq j \leq S-m$$

where `$C_i$` is the original `$i$`-th chunk, `$\hat{C}_i$` is the augmented chunk, and `$\{x_{m+j}, ..., x_S\}$` is a suffix of the local context (the dynamic prompt). The hyperparameter `$j$` determines where in the local context the dynamic prompt begins — equivalently, how many of the most recent local context tokens are appended.

**Operational meaning:** The dynamic prompt is literally a text fragment — the last several hundred tokens of what the model has generated or processed so far — appended to the end of each chunk before it is processed. For example, if the model is answering a question about a long document, the dynamic prompt might contain the question itself, the partially-generated answer, or whatever tokens the model is currently attending to in its local context.

**Default length:** The paper adopts a default dynamic prompt length of 512 tokens for inference, which it argues is "sufficient to encapsulate the necessary local contextual information." During training, to avoid having the dynamic prompt exceed the chunk size (which would break the native context length constraint), the injected length is set to `$\min(512, \text{chunk size})$`.

**Why 512 tokens?** The paper does not provide an explicit ablation for dynamic prompt length, but the reasoning is implicit: 512 tokens is long enough to contain a complete query, instruction, or partial answer — the kind of information that would distinguish which parts of a long document are relevant. It is also short relative to the typical chunk size (which can be up to 2048 tokens), leaving most of the chunk's capacity for the original content.

**The dynamic prompt evolves at each decoding step:** This is what makes the condensing "dynamic" rather than static. When FocusLLM generates the next token (say, at position `$S+1$`), that new token is appended to the dynamic prompt. The updated dynamic prompt is then used to generate new candidate tokens in the *next* decoding step. Specifically:

> "After each decoding step, when FocusLLM generates the next token, this token will be appended to the dynamic prompt. This updated dynamic prompt is then used to generate new candidate tokens in the next decoding step."

If the dynamic prompt reaches its maximum length (512 tokens by default), the first token of the dynamic prompt can be dropped to maintain the fixed length — a sliding window over the recent generation history.

**What this achieves:** The dynamic prompt serves as a **query** that conditions the information extraction from each chunk. In the Passkey Retrieval task, for instance, the dynamic prompt would eventually contain the question "What is the passkey?" or tokens that implicitly encode that retrieval intent. When appended to a chunk containing the passkey number, the model can attend to the passkey in the chunk's context because the dynamic prompt provides the relevance signal. When appended to a chunk containing irrelevant filler text, the model can learn to ignore it because the dynamic prompt creates no meaningful connection.

This is the critical difference from static compression: the same chunk may produce different candidate token representations at different decoding steps because the dynamic prompt changes. At step 5, when the model hasn't yet formulated the retrieval intent, the candidate token from the passkey chunk might carry little information. At step 50, after the question is fully in context, the same chunk produces a candidate token rich with passkey information.

---

#### 3.4.3 Dynamic Condensing: Candidate Token Generation

Once the dynamic prompt is appended, the augmented chunk `$\hat{C}_i$` is processed through the modified decoder `$F'_{\text{dec}}$`. But how does the model *extract* a condensed representation from this chunk? This is where candidate tokens enter.

**What is a candidate token?** It is a **trainable hidden state** corresponding to the last token of the dynamic prompt (which is the last token of the augmented chunk `$\hat{C}_i$`). Concretely, each augmented chunk ends with the dynamic prompt, and the dynamic prompt ends with the most recent token in the local context. The hidden representation of *this specific token*, after passing through the transformer layers with the modified projection parameters, becomes the candidate token for that chunk.

The paper is explicit: "The candidate token is denoted as the trainable hidden states corresponding to the last local token `$x_S$` in each chunk `$\hat{C}_i$`." So the mechanism piggybacks on the standard transformer forward pass — it doesn't add new token positions or special separator tokens. It simply designates the representation of an existing token (the last one in the augmented chunk) as the summary vector for that chunk.

**Why the last token of the dynamic prompt?** Because this token has, via standard causal self-attention, attended to all preceding tokens in the augmented chunk — including both the original chunk content `$C_i$` and the dynamic prompt `$\{x_{m+j}, ..., x_S\}$`. Its hidden state at layer `$l$` is therefore a function of the entire augmented chunk. The trainable projection parameters `$W^c$` then transform this hidden state to emphasize the dimensions that are useful for downstream aggregation.

**The trainable parameters for dynamic condensing** are new linear projection matrices added at each transformer layer:

$$\{W^c_Q, W^c_K, W^c_V, W^c_O\}_l$$

where `$l$` indexes the transformer layer. `$W^c_Q, W^c_K, W^c_V$` are the query, key, and value projection matrices specific to the candidate token, and `$W^c_O$` is the output projection matrix. The superscript `$c$` distinguishes these from the original (frozen) projection matrices of the base model.

These are **additional** parameters — they do not replace the original projections. The base model's original projections continue to process all tokens normally. The candidate-specific projections are applied *only* to the candidate token's hidden state, allowing it to extract information in a way that is optimized for the dynamic condensing task without disturbing the base model's general language capabilities.

**The computation in each self-attention module** for the candidate token follows this sequence (the paper provides Equations 3–5):

**Step 1: Project the candidate token's hidden state** using the candidate-specific matrices:

$$Q^c \leftarrow H^c W^c_Q$$
$$K^c \leftarrow H^c W^c_K$$
$$V^c \leftarrow H^c W^c_V$$

where `$Q^c, K^c, V^c$` are the query, key, and value representations for the candidate token, and `$H^c \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{dec}}}$` is the candidate token's input hidden state at the current layer.

**What these projections do:** They transform the candidate token's representation into query, key, and value spaces that are *different* from the spaces used by normal tokens. This is crucial because the candidate token has a fundamentally different role than a regular content token — it's not trying to predict the next word; it's trying to *summarize what is relevant in this chunk*. The separate projection spaces allow it to learn attention patterns optimized for information extraction rather than next-token prediction.

**Step 2: Compute attention scores** between the candidate token's query and the concatenation of all keys (normal tokens + candidate token):

$$A^c \leftarrow \text{softmax}\left(Q^c (K \oplus K^c)^T\right)$$

where `$K$` represents the key representations of all normal tokens in the augmented chunk `$\hat{C}_i$`, `$K^c$` is the candidate token's key representation, and `$\oplus$` denotes concatenation along the sequence dimension.

**What this attention computes:** For the candidate token, how much it should attend to each token in the augmented chunk (including itself). The candidate token's query `$Q^c$` is projected through `$W^c_Q$`, which has been trained to ask questions like "where in this chunk is the information relevant to the dynamic prompt?" The dot product `$Q^c(K \oplus K^c)^T$` measures the relevance of each chunk token's key to this query. The softmax normalizes these scores into a probability distribution over the chunk's tokens.

**Step 3: Aggregate values using the attention weights:**

$$V^c \leftarrow A^c (V \oplus V^c)^T$$

where `$V$` represents the value representations of all normal tokens and `$V^c$` is the candidate token's own value representation.

**What this aggregation computes:** A weighted sum of all value vectors in the chunk, where the weights are the attention scores from Step 2. If the attention scores concentrate on tokens containing passkey information, the resulting vector `$V^c$` will be dominated by those tokens' values — the candidate token has effectively "read" the relevant information from its chunk and encoded it into a single vector.

**Step 4: Apply output projection:**

$$O^c \leftarrow V^c W^c_O$$

where `$W^c_O$` is the candidate-specific output projection matrix, and `$O^c$` becomes the candidate token's output hidden state for this layer, which feeds into the next layer's input.

**Why separate projection matrices at every layer?** The paper follows the design of Activation Beacon (Zhang et al., 2024a) in adding trainable projections at each layer rather than only at the embedding or output layer. The motivation (discussed in Section 2.2) is that information extraction is a hierarchical process — early layers might extract local syntactic patterns relevant to the dynamic prompt, while later layers might build higher-level semantic abstractions. Having trainable parameters at each layer allows the model to learn layer-specific extraction strategies.

**The total number of additional parameters** is approximately 2 billion for the LLaMA-2-7B base model. This represents roughly 1/3 of the base model's 7B parameters. The paper notes this explicitly in Section 5.3: "The additional trainable parameters mentioned in Section 2 amount to only 2B approximately." This is **parameter-efficient** in the sense that most of the model's capacity (the frozen base weights) is reused, but it is not as lightweight as adapter-based methods that add only a few million parameters. The tradeoff is justified by the complexity of the task — condensing arbitrary chunks conditioned on arbitrary queries likely requires substantial representational capacity.

**What happens to the normal tokens in the chunk?** They are processed through the base model's original frozen projections exactly as they would be in standard inference. The candidate-specific projections are applied *only* to the candidate token. The normal tokens produce their usual key-value pairs, which are stored in the chunk's KV cache but are not carried forward into the local context processing. Only the candidate token's KV representations are retained and passed to the parallel decoding stage.

**A subtle but important detail:** The candidate token occupies the position of the last token in the augmented chunk. In the causal attention mask, this means the candidate token can attend to *all preceding tokens* in the chunk (both the original content and the dynamic prompt), but no token in the chunk can attend to it — it is the final position. This is by design: the candidate token is a *consumer* of information (it aggregates), not a *producer* (nothing depends on it within the chunk).

#### 3.4.4 Parallel Decoding Mechanism

Once each chunk has been processed through dynamic condensing, the framework has produced one candidate token per chunk — a total of `$k$` vectors, each representing the most relevant information from its respective chunk for the current decoding step. The parallel decoding mechanism now **integrates** these candidate tokens into the local context to generate the next token.

**Why "parallel"?** The processing of each augmented chunk `$\hat{C}_i$` is **independent** — chunk `$C_1$` does not need to know anything about chunk `$C_2$` to produce its candidate token, because the candidate token only depends on its own chunk and the shared dynamic prompt. This means all `$k$` chunks can be processed simultaneously in a single batched forward pass. The paper emphasizes this in Section 2.3: "the process of obtaining the candidate token from each chunk is independent, enabling parallel forwarding for all chunks."

**The aggregation procedure** works layer by layer through the frozen decoder:

1. For each layer `$l$` of the frozen decoder, the key-value representations of the `$k$` candidate tokens are extracted. Specifically, these are the `$K^c$` and `$V^c$` representations (the key and value projections of the candidate tokens' hidden states at layer `$l$`).

2. These candidate token KV pairs are **concatenated** with the KV pairs of the tokens in the local context `$\{x_{m+1}, ..., x_S\}$`. The concatenation is along the sequence dimension, so the local context tokens see an extended key-value store containing both their own tokens and the candidate tokens from all chunks.

3. The local context tokens then perform standard causal self-attention over this extended KV store. The candidate tokens are treated as *read-only* context — the local context tokens can attend to them, but the candidate tokens themselves do not get updated during this pass (they've already been fully processed through all layers during dynamic condensing).

**What this enables:** The local context, when generating the next token, can attend not only to its own preceding tokens (the standard autoregressive context) but also to `$k$` summarized representations of distant chunks. If chunk 14 contained the answer to the current question, its candidate token's key representation should have a high dot product with the local context's query, and the corresponding value representation carries the information needed to answer.

**The candidate tokens are integrated layer by layer**, meaning that at layer 1 of the local context processing, candidate tokens' layer-1 KV representations are concatenated; at layer 2, candidate tokens' layer-2 KV representations are concatenated; and so on through all layers. This preserves the hierarchical structure — the local context's layer-`$l$` queries can match with candidate tokens' layer-`$l$` keys, which encode information at the same abstraction level.

**The final token generation** happens at the last layer of the frozen decoder, which produces a probability distribution over the vocabulary for the next token. This is standard autoregressive generation — the only difference is that the attention context has been augmented with candidate tokens.

**Complexity analysis.** The standard transformer has `$O(L^2)$` complexity for a sequence of length `$L$`. In FocusLLM, let `$n$` be the number of chunks (including the local context chunk). The complexity becomes:

- **Chunk processing:** `$n$` independent forward passes, each operating on a sequence of length `$\leq L + 512$` (chunk + dynamic prompt), giving `$O(n \cdot (L + 512)^2) \approx O(n \cdot L^2)$`.
- **Local context processing with candidate tokens:** The local context attends to its own tokens (length `$\leq L$`) plus `$k$` candidate tokens, giving `$O((L + k)^2) \approx O(L^2)$` when `$k \ll L$`.

The paper provides an equivalent formulation in Appendix A: "By dividing the sequence into `$n$` chunks, the complexity within each chunk becomes `$O((L/n)^2)$`." This is because if the original sequence has total length `$S$` and is divided into `$n$` equal chunks, each chunk has length `$S/n$`, and the per-chunk attention complexity is `$O((S/n)^2)$`. With parallel processing across chunks, the time complexity is `$O((S/n)^2)$` per chunk, and the space complexity across all chunks is `$O((S/n)^2 \cdot n) = O(S^2/n)$`.

Compared to a standard transformer's `$O(S^2)$`, this represents a reduction by a factor of `$n$` in space and effectively `$n$` in time (with parallelism). The paper states: "FocusLLM can reduce the computational complexity to a fraction, 1/n or even more of the original theoretically."

**In practice, the efficiency gain depends on hardware parallelism.** Figure 3 shows that with parallel processing enabled, FocusLLM's memory usage grows more slowly than both the standard approach (PI/NTK) and CEPE. For instance, at 32K context length, FocusLLM with parallelism uses roughly 40GB GPU memory compared to over 70GB for the standard approach (which would OOM at longer lengths). Without parallelism (processing chunks sequentially), FocusLLM still has lower peak memory than full-attention methods because only one chunk is in GPU memory at a time, but inference time increases linearly with the number of chunks (Figure 4).

**The key design choice here** is the **separation of chunk processing from local context processing**. The rationale — implicit in the architecture but worth articulating — is that this separation creates an **information bottleneck**: the only way information flows from distant chunks to the current generation is through the candidate tokens' key-value pairs. This forces the model to learn to encode *useful* information in the candidate tokens (since anything not encoded is lost) and to learn to *query* the candidate tokens effectively from the local context. It is a form of learned compression where the compression objective — what should be preserved — is defined dynamically by the current decoding needs rather than statically by the chunk content alone.

#### 3.4.5 Training Procedure: Data and Objectives

FocusLLM is trained to perform next-token prediction, but with a training setup that carefully constructs the relationship between memory chunks and local context to teach the model when and how to extract information.

**Training data.** The paper uses **RedPajama** (Together, 2023b), an open-source reproduction of the LLaMA-1 pretraining dataset. From this corpus, 80,000 sequences are randomly sampled with lengths varying between 3K and 8K tokens. The length distribution (Table 5, Appendix B) is:
- 3K–4K tokens: 30,000 sequences (38%)
- 4K–6K tokens: 16,000 sequences (20%)
- 6K–8K tokens: 34,000 sequences (42%)

The total training budget is approximately `$80,000 \times 6,000 \approx 0.5$`B tokens (using the average length), which the paper contrasts with LongLlama's 7B-token budget and full-parameter training.

**Why RedPajama?** It is an open-source dataset widely used in prior context-extension work (Zhang et al., 2024a; Yen et al., 2024), ensuring fair comparison. It also provides naturally long documents (books, articles, code) that exhibit the kind of long-range dependencies FocusLLM needs to learn. Critically, the training sequences are all **under 8K tokens** — the model never sees a 128K or 400K document during training. The learned skill of query-conditioned extraction is what generalizes to arbitrary lengths.

**Training objective: Auto-regressive loss on local context only.** The training loss is defined as:

$$\min_{F'_{\text{dec}}} -\sum_{i=2}^{S-m} \log p(x_{m+i} \mid c_1, \ldots, c_k, x_{m+1}, \ldots, x_{m+i-1})$$

**Symbol definitions:**
- `$F'_{\text{dec}}$` is the modified decoder model (frozen base + trainable candidate token parameters). The minimization is only over the trainable parameters `$\{W^c_Q, W^c_K, W^c_V, W^c_O\}_l$`; the base model parameters remain frozen.
- `$S$` is the total sequence length, `$m$` is the number of memory tokens (chunked content), so `$S-m$` is the number of local context tokens.
- `$x_{m+i}$` is the `$i$`-th token of the local context being predicted.
- `$c_1, ..., c_k$` are the candidate tokens generated from the `$k$` memory chunks, conditioned on the dynamic prompt (which consists of tokens `$\{x_{m+j}, ..., x_{m+i-1}\}$` — the local context tokens up to position `$i-1$`).
- `$x_{m+1}, ..., x_{m+i-1}$` are the preceding tokens in the local context that the model can attend to via standard causal self-attention.

**Operational meaning:** For each position `$i$` in the local context, the model predicts the next token `$x_{m+i}$` given (a) the candidate tokens from all memory chunks and (b) the preceding local context tokens. The loss is computed **only on local context tokens** — the memory chunks are not themselves targets for prediction. This is by design: "the loss is only applied to tokens in the local context, which encourages the candidate token to aggregate useful information from each chunk" (Section 3).

**Why this restricted loss?** If the model were also trained to predict tokens within the memory chunks, it would learn to produce good continuations of those chunks, which is not the goal. The goal is for the candidate tokens to be *useful context* for predicting the local continuation. By only backpropagating loss from local context predictions, the training signal flows through the candidate tokens and tells them: "whatever representations you produce should improve the model's ability to predict what comes next in the local context." This aligns the candidate tokens' optimization objective with their downstream use: providing relevant context to the current generation.

**Two complementary loss constructions (implicit in the training setup).** The paper describes two ways of defining the relationship between memory tokens and local context, each yielding a different loss term:

**1. Continuation Loss:** The local context is a **natural continuation** of the memory tokens. In this setup, the entire sequence is one long document, and the split point `$m$` is chosen so that `$\{x_{m+1}, ..., x_S\}$` follows naturally from `$\{x_1, ..., x_m\}$`. The model learns: "when generating text that follows naturally from the preceding content, use the candidate tokens to carry forward relevant information from that preceding content."

**2. Reconstruction Loss:** A segment of `$L$` consecutive tokens is randomly selected from *within* the memory tokens and designated as the local context. The model must reconstruct these tokens given the surrounding memory chunks. This forces the model to learn: "when you need to reproduce tokens that appeared earlier (or are related to earlier content), extract that information from the candidate tokens."

**Operationally, both loss types are implemented jointly** by constructing training examples in two ways and summing the losses. The paper's ablation (Section 5.5, Table 4) demonstrates that both are essential:

- **Continuation Loss only:** The model achieves decent performance on forward-continuation tasks (NarrativeQA: 17.36, vs. 18.53 with both losses) but fails catastrophically on tasks requiring retrieval of specific information from the past (Retrieve.PassKey: 1.69 vs. 99.32 with both losses). Without Reconstruction Loss, the model never learns to "look back" and extract precise details — it only learns to use the preceding context for smooth continuation.

- **Reconstruction Loss only:** The model can retrieve past information reasonably well (Retrieve.PassKey: 91.19) but loses general language modeling capability, performing worse on open-ended tasks (TREC: 62.0 vs. 65.5 with both losses, NarrativeQA: 17.05 vs. 18.53). Without Continuation Loss, the model overfits to the reconstruction pattern and doesn't generalize to generating novel tokens.

**Why both losses together work:** The Continuation Loss teaches the model the *general skill* of language modeling with augmented context — how to generate coherent text when candidate tokens provide supplementary information. The Reconstruction Loss teaches the model the *specific skill* of information extraction — that candidate tokens should encode *precise, retrievable* information from their chunks, not just vague gist. Together, they produce a model that both understands language broadly and can access specific details when the dynamic prompt signals that they're needed.

**Training hyperparameters** (Appendix C):
- Hardware: 8×A100 40GB GPUs
- Training steps: 10,000 (one epoch over the 80K training set)
- Batch size: 8 per GPU (effective batch size 64 across 8 GPUs)
- Learning rate: `$5 \times 10^{-5}$` with linear scheduler
- Optimizer: Not explicitly specified, but standard for LLaMA fine-tuning (likely AdamW, consistent with the broader fine-tuning literature the paper operates within)
- Memory optimization: DeepSpeed ZeRO-2 offload
- Training time: approximately 20 hours
- Chunk size during training: randomly sampled from `$\{64, 128, 256, 1024, 2048\}$`
- Dynamic prompt length during training: `$\min(512, \text{chunk size})$`

**The chunk size randomization during training** is a noteworthy design choice. By exposing the model to chunks ranging from very small (64 tokens) to moderately large (2048 tokens), the training encourages the candidate token extraction mechanism to work across a wide range of information densities. At inference time, when chunks might be up to 4096 tokens (the full native context length), the model has learned to handle both fine-grained and coarse-grained information aggregation.

**Inference-time configuration** (distinct from training):
- Chunk size: Fixed at the original model context length (up to 4096 for LLaMA-2-7B), chosen to minimize the number of chunks while staying within native limits.
- Dynamic prompt length: Fixed at 512 tokens.
- Local context size: For LongBench, the paper uses 3,500 tokens for the local context, "consistent with the official setting." For other evaluations, the local context size can be up to `$L$` (the model's native context length).

#### 3.4.6 Trainable Parameter Design and Parameter Efficiency

The trainable parameters in FocusLLM are specifically the linear projection matrices `$\{W^c_Q, W^c_K, W^c_V, W^c_O\}_l$` added at each transformer layer. To understand the parameter count, consider:

- LLaMA-2-7B has 32 transformer layers, each with hidden dimension `$d_{\text{dec}} = 4096$`.
- For each layer, four projection matrices are added: `$W^c_Q, W^c_K, W^c_V$` (each `$4096 \times 4096$`, but likely with a smaller head dimension in practice, since LLaMA uses multi-head attention) and `$W^c_O$` (also `$4096 \times 4096$`).

The paper states this amounts to approximately 2 billion additional parameters. This is roughly 29% of LLaMA-2-7B's 7 billion parameters, consistent with adding about 1/3 additional projection capacity at each layer.

**Why add full projection matrices rather than lightweight adapters?** The paper does not explicitly justify this design choice, but the reasoning can be inferred from the task complexity. The candidate token must learn an entirely different attention pattern than normal tokens — it is not fine-tuning an existing capability; it is learning a new capability (query-conditioned information extraction) from scratch. Full projection matrices provide sufficient capacity for this. Lightweight adapters (e.g., LoRA with rank 8 or 16) might not have enough representational capacity to transform a token's hidden state from "next-word prediction mode" to "chunk summarization mode."

**Why keep the base model frozen?** The paper states this explicitly: "The original model parameters are kept frozen to maintain generalization capabilities." Fine-tuning all parameters would risk catastrophic forgetting of the base model's language understanding and reasoning abilities. By freezing the base model and adding new parameters, FocusLLM adds long-context capability *on top of* the existing general-purpose language model without degrading it.

**Initialization of the new parameters:** The paper does not specify the initialization scheme, which is a minor gap. In practice, the projection matrices are likely initialized randomly (e.g., Xavier or Kaiming initialization) and trained from scratch, since they represent a new functionality not present in the base model.

**At inference time**, only one copy of the trainable parameters exists — they are shared across all chunks. All `$k$` augmented chunks are processed through the same `$F'_{\text{dec}}$` with the same `$W^c$` parameters, which is why the batch dimension can be used for parallelization.

#### 3.4.7 End-to-End Inference Procedure

Putting all components together, here is what happens step by step when FocusLLM processes a long document at inference time:

**Phase 0: Preprocessing.**
1. The long input sequence of `$S$` tokens (where `$S$` can be up to 400K) is segmented into `$k+1$` chunks, each of size `$\leq L = 4096$` tokens. The last chunk is designated as the initial local context; the first `$k$` chunks are memory chunks `$C_1, ..., C_k$`.
2. The local context initially contains the end of the document. The dynamic prompt is initialized as a suffix of the local context — typically the last 512 tokens.

**Phase 1: Dynamic condensing (per decoding step).**
3. For each memory chunk `$C_i$`, the current dynamic prompt is appended: `$\hat{C}_i = [C_i; \text{dynamic\_prompt}]$`.
4. All `$k$` augmented chunks are processed in parallel through the modified decoder `$F'_{\text{dec}}$`. Within each chunk, the candidate token (the last token of the dynamic prompt) attends to all tokens in `$\hat{C}_i$` via standard causal self-attention, using the trainable `$W^c$` projections at each layer.
5. For each chunk `$C_i$`, the candidate token's key-value representations `$\{K^c_{i,l}, V^c_{i,l}\}_{l=1}^{32}$` are extracted at every layer.

**Phase 2: Parallel decoding (per decoding step).**
6. At each layer `$l$` of the frozen decoder, the local context tokens are processed with their KV cache extended by the candidate tokens' KV pairs from all chunks. Specifically, the attention key-value store becomes `$[K_{\text{local},l}; K^c_{1,l}; ...; K^c_{k,l}]$` and `$[V_{\text{local},l}; V^c_{1,l}; ...; V^c_{k,l}]$`.
7. The local context tokens perform causal self-attention over this extended KV store. They can attend to all preceding local context tokens and all candidate tokens, but candidate tokens do not attend to each other across chunks (they were processed independently).
8. At the final layer, the last local context token produces the next-token logits. The token is sampled (or greedily decoded) to produce the next output token `$x_{\text{new}}$`.

**Phase 3: State update.**
9. The new token `$x_{\text{new}}$` is appended to the local context. If the local context exceeds `$L$` tokens, the oldest tokens are evicted (standard sliding window).
10. The dynamic prompt is updated: `$x_{\text{new}}$` is appended, and if the dynamic prompt exceeds 512 tokens, the oldest token is dropped. The updated dynamic prompt encodes the latest generation state.
11. Return to Step 3 for the next decoding step, with the updated dynamic prompt conditioning a new round of candidate token extraction.

**Key property: No KV cache for memory chunks needs to be maintained across decoding steps.** At each step, the memory chunks are re-processed from scratch with the updated dynamic prompt. This might seem wasteful — why not cache the chunk computations? The answer is that the dynamic prompt changes at each step, which means the candidate token's attention distribution over its chunk changes. A cached candidate token from step `$t$` would be conditioned on step `$t$`'s dynamic prompt, which may be irrelevant to step `$t+1$`'s needs. Re-processing ensures the candidate tokens always reflect the current information need.

The computational cost of re-processing all chunks at every step is the main efficiency tradeoff of FocusLLM. The paper acknowledges this implicitly through the parallel decoding mechanism — chunks can be re-processed in parallel, so wall-clock time depends on the slowest chunk's processing time plus the local context processing, not on the sum of all chunk processing times. For very long documents (many chunks), the inference time is dominated by the parallelism overhead and the constant factor of re-processing, which is why Figure 4 shows FocusLLM as slightly slower than the standard approach at shorter lengths but dramatically more efficient than other memory methods at longer lengths.

**An important implementation detail not fully specified:** How does the framework handle the *first* decoding step, when the dynamic prompt hasn't yet been populated with generated tokens? The paper implies that the dynamic prompt initially contains the end of the provided local context — typically, for a question-answering task, this would include the question and any initial instructions. As the model generates answer tokens, these are appended, gradually shifting the dynamic prompt's content from "what is being asked" to "what is being answered." This evolving query is what allows the model to first locate relevant information (when the dynamic prompt contains the question) and then verify or reference it (when the dynamic prompt contains partial answers).

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Reframing Context Extension from "Compress the Past" to "Query the Past"

The dominant mental model in prior context-extension work — spanning StreamingLLM, Activation Beacon, AutoCompressor, and InfLLM — is that long contexts must be condensed, compressed, or filtered *before* the model knows what it will need. These methods share a common architectural assumption: information density should be reduced upfront to fit within a computational budget, and the model should learn to produce summaries or select tokens that preserve as much future utility as possible. This is fundamentally a **prediction problem**: given only the past, predict what will be relevant to an unknown future.

FocusLLM inverts this framing entirely. Instead of asking "what in this chunk will matter later?", it asks, at every decoding step, "what in this chunk matters *right now*, given what I am about to generate?" The mechanism for this — appending the dynamic prompt to each chunk before processing — is described in Section 3. The **conceptual move** is what distinguishes this paper: it recasts context extension from a *compression-as-anticipation* problem into a *compression-as-query-conditioned-retrieval* problem.

Why is this reframing significant? Because it eliminates the fundamental impossibility that prior methods implicitly accepted. Predicting future relevance from past content alone is, in the general case, intractable — the model cannot know which tokens in paragraph 3 will be needed to answer a question that appears in paragraph 47. Prior methods addressed this intractability by making heuristic compromises (keep attention sinks, compress evenly, select based on global importance scores) that work on average but fail catastrophically when the needed information is precisely what the heuristic discards. The empirical evidence for this failure is the near-zero performance of Activation Beacon on Passkey Retrieval (1.69%) and KV Retrieval (0.00%) in Table 8, Appendix F — tasks deliberately designed to require information that compression heuristics would not preserve.

FocusLLM's reframing sidesteps the intractability entirely. It does not try to predict what will be needed. It waits until the current decoding state *reveals* what is needed (via the dynamic prompt), then extracts it. This transforms the problem from one requiring foresight into one requiring only the ability to match a query against stored content — a capability that transformer attention already implements natively.

The paper's Passkey Retrieval results (Figure 1: 99% at 400K tokens, nearly flat across all lengths) are the empirical signature of this reframing. They demonstrate not just better performance but a qualitatively different scaling behavior: information-preserving methods can maintain constant accuracy as context length grows, while compression-based methods degrade monotonically as the probability of discarding the critical information increases with length. This is not a metric improvement — it is evidence of a different failure mode being avoided entirely.

The significance of this reframing extends beyond the specific architecture. It suggests that the long-context problem is not fundamentally about *capacity* (can the model store everything?) but about *access* (can the model find what it needs when it needs it?). FocusLLM separates storage (which is trivially parallelizable and scales linearly with document length) from access (which requires attention but operates over a small query-conditioned window). This decomposition was not present in prior work, which conflated the two by compressing storage to reduce access cost. It is a conceptual framework that future context-extension methods can adopt regardless of their specific architectural choices.

This is a **fundamental** contribution rather than an incremental refinement, because it changes the problem definition itself — from "how do we compress a long sequence into a short one?" to "how do we let a short query retrieve from a long sequence?"

---

### Innovation 2: The Dynamic Prompt as a Mechanism for Shifting Relevance Evaluation from Encoding Time to Decoding Time

Prior work that compresses context does so at **encoding time** — when the text is first processed, before any generation begins. Activation Beacon, for example, condenses each segment into beacon tokens once and stores those compressed representations in the KV cache. InfLLM retrieves relevant memory units using attention scores computed during the initial forward pass. In all these methods, the decision about what information to preserve or surface is made *before generation starts* and is based solely on the text's internal structure.

FocusLLM shifts this evaluation to **decoding time** by making the compression conditional on the dynamic prompt, which evolves with each generated token. The dynamic prompt is described mechanically in Section 3; its conceptual significance is that it **encodes the model's current cognitive state** — what question it is trying to answer, what partial answer it has produced so far, what tokens it is about to generate — and uses this state as the query that determines which information gets extracted from each chunk.

This means the same chunk produces different candidate token representations at different decoding steps. At step 5, when the model has only seen the instruction "Please read the following document and answer the question," the candidate token from a chunk containing the answer might be relatively generic — it carries the chunk's general semantic content but nothing specifically targeted. At step 50, after the model has read the question "What year was the company founded?" and begun generating the answer, the same chunk now produces a candidate token rich with the specific founding year, because the dynamic prompt now contains tokens that create high attention scores with the year-encoding tokens in the chunk.

This is not a minor efficiency improvement — it is a **fundamentally different computational primitive**. Prior methods compute a function of the past: `f(past) → compressed_past`. FocusLLM computes a function of the past *conditioned on the present*: `f(past | current_state) → relevant_information`. The second function is strictly more expressive than the first. It can represent any compression that the first can (by making the dynamic prompt empty or constant), but it can also adapt to changing information needs that the first fundamentally cannot anticipate.

The evidence that this shift matters comes from the ablation on training loss in Table 4. When trained with Continuation Loss only, the model never learns that the dynamic prompt should be used for targeted retrieval — it only learns to use candidate tokens for smooth forward continuation. The result is catastrophic failure on retrieval tasks (Retrieve.PassKey: 1.69%). The Reconstruction Loss is necessary precisely because it teaches the model that the dynamic prompt *should change the information extracted* — when the local context needs to reconstruct earlier content, the dynamic prompt encodes that reconstruction intent, and the candidate token must respond by extracting the specific information needed.

This innovation is **fundamental** because it introduces a new axis of variation (the dynamic prompt's evolution) that prior methods did not exploit. It redefines the role of context not as a *repository* that is indexed once, but as a *resource* that is re-queried at every step with a potentially different question. The attention heatmap visualizations (Figures 7 and 8, Appendix H) provide direct evidence: in the Passkey task, a single candidate token receives high attention (the chunk containing the answer), while in NarrativeQA, multiple candidate tokens receive attention (because multiple chunks contain relevant information). This demonstrates that the same architecture, with the same frozen chunks, produces qualitatively different extraction patterns depending on what the dynamic prompt signals — exactly the decoding-time adaptation that prior methods cannot achieve.

---

### Innovation 3: Empirical Proof That Information-Loss-Free Context Extension Is Achievable With Short-Sequence Training

A persistent assumption in the long-context literature is that to handle sequences of length `N`, models must be trained on sequences approaching length `N`. LongLlama trains on 7B tokens with full context lengths. LongChat-32K is fine-tuned on 32K-length conversational data. LongAlpaca-16K trains on 16K sequences. The implicit belief is that the model must *experience* long-range dependencies to learn to reason over them — that the attention patterns, positional encodings, and information routing needed for long contexts can only emerge from long-context training data.

FocusLLM provides a strong counterexample: it is trained entirely on sequences under 8K tokens (using only 0.5B tokens total, roughly 1/14 of LongLlama's training budget), yet it generalizes to 400K tokens at inference time with 99% Passkey Retrieval accuracy (Figure 1) and maintains competitive language modeling perplexity at 128K (Table 1: 10.59 on PG19, 2.57 on Proof-Pile, 3.02 on CodeParrot). This generalization is not marginal — it substantially outperforms methods like YaRN-128K that *were* trained or designed for 128K contexts on downstream benchmarks (LongBench average: 36.17 vs. 32.40).

Why is this possible? Because the skill FocusLLM learns during training is not "how to attend over a 128K-token sequence" but rather "how to extract query-relevant information from a chunk of text." This skill is **length-independent**. Whether there are 4 chunks or 100 chunks at inference time, the per-chunk operation is identical — take the chunk, append the dynamic prompt, run it through the modified decoder, extract the candidate token. The model was trained on this exact operation with chunk sizes up to 2048 tokens. At inference time, the chunks are 4096 tokens — a length the base model already handles natively — and the number of chunks scales without bound because each is processed independently.

This is not merely an efficiency claim; it is a **conceptual finding about the nature of long-context understanding**. It suggests that long-range reasoning may be decomposable into two sub-skills: (1) the ability to extract relevant information from a local window given a query (which can be learned from short sequences), and (2) the ability to aggregate information from multiple sources (which the frozen base model already possesses, since it was pretrained on documents with paragraph-level dependencies). If this decomposition holds, then the data bottleneck for long-context training is not actually about long documents per se — it is about teaching the model to use queries to selectively extract information, which can be practiced on short sequences.

The evidence supporting this decomposition comes from the generalization behavior: FocusLLM's perplexity on PG19 remains reasonable at 400K (Figure 6 in Appendix G: approximately 45, compared to >100 for PI and NTK at 32K in Table 1), even though the model was never trained with more than a few dozen candidate tokens and at 400K the number of candidate tokens is approximately 200. The per-chunk extraction operation learned during training transfers without modification, and the base model's attention mechanism — which was designed for up to 4096 tokens of context — can handle the concatenation of local context + 200 candidate tokens because the total attended sequence is still within the native length.

This innovation is **fundamental** in its implications for how the field thinks about training for long-context tasks, though the finding itself — that chunk-wise query-conditioned extraction generalizes to arbitrary lengths — is specific to the architectural decomposition that FocusLLM introduces. It challenges the prevailing assumption that long-context training data is the bottleneck for long-context models and opens the possibility that the right architectural inductive bias can substitute for long-context training experience.

### Innovation 4: Diagnosing "Information Loss" as the Unifying Failure Mode of Compression-Based Methods

Prior work on context compression — StreamingLLM, Activation Beacon, AutoCompressor — reported aggregate performance on benchmarks like LongBench or language modeling perplexity, where the effects of information loss can be masked by task composition. LongBench, for example, includes tasks like TREC (few-shot classification with short contexts) and SAMSum (dialogue summarization with moderate-length contexts) where the full long context is not actually needed. A model that discards 90% of tokens might still perform adequately on these tasks because the answer is recoverable from the remaining 10%.

FocusLLM performs a diagnostic move that sharpens the evaluation: it tests compression methods on tasks **specifically designed to require information that compression would likely discard**. The ∞-Bench benchmark includes three retrieval tasks — PassKey, Number String, and KV Retrieval — where the target information is a small piece of data (a single number, a key-value pair) embedded in a long, mostly irrelevant document. These tasks are adversarial to compression heuristics: the relevant information has no distinguishing surface features (it looks like any other number or string in the text), so any compression strategy that does not preserve *every* token has a non-trivial probability of discarding the target.

The results in Table 8 (Appendix F) are stark: Activation Beacon achieves 1.69% on Passkey, 0.00% on KV Retrieval, and 1.69% on Number String at ∞-Bench's average context length of 145K tokens. These are not degraded performance — they are effectively total failure. In contrast, FocusLLM achieves 95.76% on Passkey and 83.56% on Number String (Table 2) at the same lengths.

This diagnostic contribution is **significant beyond the raw performance numbers** because it provides a clear, operational definition of information loss and a benchmark for testing whether a method suffers from it. Prior work did not have this — information loss was a theoretical concern, but it was difficult to point to a specific result and say "this model failed because it discarded the information, not because it lacked reasoning capability." The ∞-Bench retrieval tasks serve this diagnostic function: if a model gets near-zero on these tasks while performing adequately on other long-context tasks, the failure mode is specifically information loss, not a general inability to process long contexts.

This innovation is best characterized as a **framing contribution with empirical teeth**. The concept of information loss is not entirely new — the paper's introduction discusses it as a drawback of compression methods — but prior work treated it as a tradeoff (lose some information, gain efficiency) rather than a diagnosable failure mode with sharp boundaries. FocusLLM shows that the boundary is not gradual; it is binary in tasks where the target information is sparse. A model either preserves the information (and can retrieve it) or it does not (and fails completely). This binary characterization changes how context-extension methods should be evaluated: aggregate benchmark scores that average over easy and hard retrieval tasks may mask the presence of catastrophic information loss on the subset of tasks that genuinely require full-context access.

The innovation is **incremental** in the sense that it refines an existing concern rather than introducing a new one, but it is practically important because it provides a clear evaluation protocol that future work can adopt. The Passkey Retrieval task, which the paper uses prominently (Figure 1, Table 2), serves as a minimal, easily-implementable litmus test: if a method cannot achieve near-perfect accuracy on Passkey Retrieval as context length scales, it necessarily suffers from information loss on some fraction of inputs, and that fraction will grow with context length.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The paper evaluates on three categories of benchmarks: (1) Long-context language modeling: PG19 (Rae et al., 2019), Proof-Pile (Azerbayev et al., 2023), and CodeParrot (Tunstall et al., 2022), encompassing 100 test cases from books, arXiv papers, and code repositories respectively; (2) Downstream long-context understanding: LongBench (Bai et al., 2023) with 14 English tasks spanning question answering, summarization, few-shot learning, and code completion, with average lengths from 5K–15K tokens; (3) Extreme-length comprehension: ∞-Bench (Zhang et al., 2024b) with an average length of 145.1K tokens, designed specifically to test whether models can precisely understand full contexts without information loss. For language modeling, following Yen et al. (2024), perplexity is calculated on the last 256 tokens of each sequence; for 128K evaluation, documents exceeding 128K are filtered and 10 samples are tested due to data scarcity and computational cost.

- **Base model(s).** All experiments use LLaMA-2-7B-Chat (Touvron et al., 2023b) as the base decoder, which has a native context length of 4K tokens. The authors argue this model is "representative of the capabilities of many contemporary LLMs" and sits at a scale (7B parameters) where context extension is both practically valuable and computationally feasible for academic research. For fair comparison on LongBench against baselines built on Vicuna-7B-v1.5 (which is fine-tuned from LLaMA-2-7B on conversational data), a Vicuna version of FocusLLM is also trained.

- **Metrics.** Three categories of metrics are used: (1) **Perplexity** for language modeling evaluation, computed on the last 256 tokens of test sequences (consistent with the setting from Yen et al., 2024); (2) **Task-specific metrics** for LongBench, following the official benchmark: F1 score for question answering tasks (NarrativeQA, Qasper, MultiFieldQA, HotpotQA, 2WikiMQA, Musique, TriviaQA), ROUGE-L for summarization tasks (GovReport, QMSum, MultiNews, SAMSum), accuracy for few-shot tasks (TREC) and synthetic tasks (PassageRetrieval), and Edit Similarity for code tasks (LCC, RepoBench-P); (3) **Accuracy** for ∞-Bench tasks, which include Math.Find, En.MC, Code.Debug, Retrieve.KV, Retrieve.Number, and Retrieve.PassKey, all graded by exact match or task-specific scoring functions from the official benchmark release.

- **Baselines.** The paper compares against three categories of baselines: (1) **Training-free positional encoding modifications**: PI (Chen et al., 2023a), NTK-Aware Scaled RoPE (community-developed, cited via URL), and StreamingLLM (Xiao et al., 2023); (2) **Fine-tuned full-attention methods**: LongAlpaca-16K (Chen et al., 2023b), LongChat-32K (Li et al., 2023), YaRN-128K (Peng et al., 2023), and LongLlama (Tworkowski et al., 2024); (3) **Compression and memory-based methods**: AutoCompressor-6K (Chevalier et al., 2023), Activation Beacon (Zhang et al., 2024a), InfLLM (Xiao et al., 2024), and CEPE (Yen et al., 2024). For LongBench (Table 3), the comparison focuses on Llama-2-7B-based models (CEPE, LongLlama, Activation Beacon) and includes an "Original" Vicuna-7B-v1.5 baseline. For ∞-Bench (Table 2), additional baselines include the original Vicuna-7B-v1.5 (4K), LongChat-32K, Vicuna-16K, Yarn-128K (based on Mistral-7B-inst-v0.2), PI, NTK, StreamingLLM, and InfLLM. Several baselines could not be evaluated on ∞-Bench: PI and NTK encounter OOM; CEPE and LongLlama experience OOM "due to their substantial memory usage."

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** For language modeling experiments, the "budget" is implicit in the model's ability to process a given context length — models are tested on 4K, 16K, 32K, and 100K (or 128K) sequences, with perplexity measured at the last 256 tokens. For downstream tasks, methods process the full input length (if they support it) or truncate inputs to their maximum supported length, "only preserving the system prompts and the tail of inputs to simulate real-world applications with streaming inputs" (Appendix E). The paper does not use a normalized FLOPs budget for comparisons; instead, it compares methods based on their *training efficiency* (FocusLLM trains on 0.5B tokens vs. LongLlama's 7B tokens, with ~2B trainable parameters vs. all 7B) and *inference memory/time* (Figures 3 and 4). For the memory and inference time analysis (Section 5.3), GPU memory (GB) and per-token generation time (seconds) are measured at context lengths 4K, 8K, 16K, and 32K on a single setting, comparing FocusLLM (with and without parallel processing) against Standard (PI/NTK), CEPE, and LongLlama.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not employ cross-validation or statistical significance testing. The 500-question MATH test set split used in the reference example is not present here; FocusLLM evaluates on the full test sets of LongBench (varying task sizes: 150–500 samples per task, Table 6) and ∞-Bench (229–590 samples per task, Table 7). Results are reported as single-run performance without confidence intervals or multiple random seeds. The training uses one epoch over 80K sampled sequences from RedPajama (10,000 steps at batch size 8), with no held-out validation set mentioned for model selection.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Language Modeling Perplexity (Table 1)

The headline finding is that FocusLLM maintains reasonable perplexity on sequences up to 100K tokens while training on sequences under 8K, and does so with competitive or better efficiency than fine-tuned full-attention methods.

At **4K context length** (the native length for LLaMA-2-7B), FocusLLM achieves 9.21 on PG19, 3.47 on Proof-Pile, and 2.55 on CodeParrot — identical to the original LLaMA-2-7B, confirming that the frozen base model's language modeling capability is preserved (the trainable parameters do not degrade native-length performance).

At **100K context length** on PG19, FocusLLM achieves 10.59 perplexity. The comparative landscape:
- StreamingLLM: 9.32 (lower perplexity, but "unable to recover the previous context information, which severely affects their capabilities in downstream tasks" — Section 4.1)
- Activation Beacon: 8.68 (lower perplexity, same information-loss caveat)
- YaRN-128K: does not reach 100K — "OOM" at this length
- LongChat-32K: "OOM" at 100K
- LongLlama: "OOM" at 100K
- PI, NTK: "OOM" at 100K

At **32K context length** on PG19, FocusLLM achieves 9.17 vs. Activation Beacon's 8.56, YaRN-128K's 6.38, and LongChat-32K's 8.81. The pattern is consistent: full-attention fine-tuned methods (YaRN, LongChat) achieve lower perplexity where they can operate, but they have hard length ceilings. Compression methods (Activation Beacon, StreamingLLM) achieve even lower perplexity by discarding tokens, which the paper argues makes them unsuitable for tasks requiring precise context retrieval.

A non-obvious observation: FocusLLM's perplexity on CodeParrot at 16K (2.01) is *lower* than at 4K (2.55), and at 32K (2.27) remains below the 4K baseline. This suggests that for structured text (code), having a longer context improves the model's ability to predict the next token — the candidate tokens from earlier chunks are providing useful conditioning information that outweighs any noise introduced by the dynamic condensing mechanism. At 100K, CodeParrot perplexity rises to 3.02, indicating some degradation at extreme lengths, but this is still vastly better than the OOM failures of most baselines.

On Proof-Pile (mathematical texts, arXiv papers), the pattern is similar: FocusLLM achieves 2.57 at 100K vs. 3.35 for Activation Beacon and 3.55 for StreamingLLM, and all full-attention methods have already OOM'd. The lower absolute perplexity on Proof-Pile compared to PG19 reflects the more formulaic nature of mathematical text.

The paper explicitly notes the tension: "Although models like StreamingLLM and Activation Beacon can still achieve lower perplexity by compressing tokens, they are unable to recover the previous context information, which severely affects their capabilities in downstream tasks." This is the core tradeoff — lower perplexity through compression comes at the cost of information loss for retrieval tasks. The paper uses downstream benchmarks (LongBench, ∞-Bench) to evaluate whether FocusLLM's slightly higher perplexity is a worthwhile price for information preservation.

#### LongBench Results (Table 2, Table 3)

The primary downstream evaluation is on LongBench. FocusLLM achieves an **average score of 36.17** across all 16 tasks (Table 2, bottom row), which is the highest among all compared models. The next best performers are CEPE (36.31, Table 3) and LongLlama (37.50 on a subset of tasks, Table 3), though neither is directly comparable across all tasks due to missing evaluations.

**Task-by-task decomposition reveals where FocusLLM excels and where it is merely competitive.** The paper's presentation in Tables 2 and 3 uses slightly different baselines (Table 2 compares against Vicuna-based models; Table 3 compares against Llama-2-based models), which makes direct cross-table comparison somewhat involved. Here I focus on the patterns:

**On tasks requiring precise retrieval from long documents**, the advantages are clearest:

- **HotpotQA** (multi-document QA, avg 9,151 tokens): FocusLLM achieves 40.65 — a substantial margin over LongChat-32K (33.19), InfLLM (22.53), and StreamingLLM (22.17). This is a 22% relative improvement over the next-best model in Table 2. Notably, the original Vicuna-7B-v1.5 scores only 12.71 on this task, so FocusLLM nearly quadruples the base model's performance through context extension.

- **Musique** (multi-document QA, avg 11,214 tokens): FocusLLM achieves 14.20 vs. LongChat-32K's 14.71 (statistically similar). The original Vicuna scores 4.81, so the improvement is substantial but FocusLLM does not uniquely dominate.

- **2WikiMQA** (multi-document QA, avg 4,887 tokens): FocusLLM's 20.30 is competitive with LongChat-32K (24.33) and CEPE (32.39 from Table 3) but not dominant.

**On single-document QA**, FocusLLM shows strong but not uniformly best performance:

- **NarrativeQA** (avg 18,409 tokens): FocusLLM achieves 21.14, outperforming LongChat-32K (20.35), InfLLM (15.53), and the original model (11.19). CEPE achieves 22.14 in Table 3 — slightly higher.

- **Qasper** (avg 3,619 tokens): FocusLLM achieves 31.07, a clear win over LongChat-32K (29.35), InfLLM (23.57), and CEPE (26.34 in Table 3).

- **MultiFieldQA** (avg 4,559 tokens): FocusLLM's 36.73 is comparable to LongChat-32K (42.55) and CEPE (31.56 in Table 3) but lower than the best.

**On summarization tasks**, FocusLLM is competitive but not exceptional:

- **GovReport** (ROUGE-L, avg 8,734 tokens): FocusLLM achieves 26.66 vs. LongChat-32K's 30.83, InfLLM's 26.79, and Activation Beacon's 26.79 (Table 3). All are in a narrow band.

- **QMSum** (ROUGE-L, avg 10,614 tokens): FocusLLM's 20.50 is within 1-2 points of all baselines in Table 2. CEPE scores 20.30 in Table 3.

- **MultiNews** (ROUGE-L, avg 2,113 tokens): FocusLLM's 27.45 is competitive with Activation Beacon (26.43 in Table 3) and LongChat (26.63). CEPE scores anomalously low at 3.10.

**On few-shot tasks**, FocusLLM is strong:

- **TREC** (accuracy): FocusLLM achieves 68.00, essentially tied with CEPE (68.50 in Table 3) and better than LongChat (66.50), InfLLM (67.50), and the original (69.00). The margins are small because this task does not heavily stress long-context capabilities.

- **TriviaQA** (F1): FocusLLM's 81.63 is lower than CEPE's 87.90 (Table 3) but comparable to LongChat (83.99) and InfLLM (84.36). The original model scores 81.94 — FocusLLM essentially maintains performance without improvement, which is acceptable since TriviaQA does not require extremely long contexts (avg 8,209 tokens).

- **SAMSum** (ROUGE-L): FocusLLM achieves 35.36, outperforming LongChat (12.83 — notably poor), InfLLM (31.89), and CEPE (32.38 in Table 3). The original model scores 35.12 — FocusLLM essentially preserves the base performance.

**On code tasks**:

- **LCC** (Edit Similarity): FocusLLM's 62.79 is competitive with CEPE (66.21 in Table 3) and LongLlama (not available). StreamingLLM scores 63.70.

- **RepoBench-P**: FocusLLM achieves 53.72, which is lower than CEPE (58.94 in Table 3) but higher than Activation Beacon (47.52 in Table 3) and LongLlama (not available in Table 3 for this task).

**On PassageRetrieval** (avg 9,289 tokens): FocusLLM's 15.67 is substantially better than StreamingLLM (6.00), InfLLM (9.00), and Activation Beacon (unreported, likely low), but far below LongChat (30.50). CEPE reports 13.90 (Table 3). This synthetic task, which requires finding a specific passage in a long document, tests information preservation directly, and FocusLLM's middling performance suggests that the candidate token mechanism has room for improvement on precise span retrieval.

**The average of 36.17** conceals significant task-level variance, which is expected for a benchmark with heterogeneous tasks. The key pattern is that FocusLLM's advantage is most pronounced on tasks where **(a)** the context is genuinely long (HotpotQA at 9K, NarrativeQA at 18K) and **(b)** the answer depends on information scattered across the document (multi-document QA) rather than concentrated in a single retrievable passage. On tasks where the answer is in the most recent ~4K tokens (which fit in the local context), FocusLLM's architecture provides no advantage and performance is similar to the base model. On tasks requiring very precise token-level retrieval (PassageRetrieval), the candidate token's vector representation may lack the granularity to pinpoint exact spans.

#### ∞-Bench Results (Table 2)

∞-Bench is the more diagnostic benchmark because its tasks are specifically designed to test "whether the model has a precise understanding of long contexts without information loss" at an average length of 145.1K tokens. FocusLLM achieves an **average of 44.03**, the highest among all models capable of processing inputs at this length.

**On retrieval tasks** — the core test of information preservation:

- **Retrieve.PassKey** (avg 122.4K tokens): FocusLLM achieves **95.76%**, compared to InfLLM's 99.15%, Yarn-128K's 92.71%, and StreamingLLM's 4.92%. The original Vicuna (4K) scores 5.08% (essentially random, since the passkey is buried far from the question). This is the key result: FocusLLM preserves passkey information almost perfectly at lengths where compression methods collapse to near-zero (Activation Beacon: 1.69% per Table 8, Appendix F). InfLLM's 99.15% is slightly higher, likely because its retrieval-based memory mechanism can directly fetch the relevant segment, whereas FocusLLM's candidate token must learn to encode the passkey into a vector — a slightly lossier process.

- **Retrieve.Number** (avg 122.4K tokens): FocusLLM achieves **83.56%**, second only to InfLLM's 81.69% (Table 2 reports both; InfLLM's is actually 81.69, so FocusLLM slightly edges it out). Yarn-128K scores 56.61%. StreamingLLM: 4.41%. The Number String task is harder than PassKey because the target is a multi-digit number rather than a simple key-value association — the candidate token must encode the entire number string accurately, and small encoding errors propagate to the local context's decoding.

- **Retrieve.KV** (avg 89.9K tokens): FocusLLM achieves **12.40%** — substantially better than InfLLM (0.60%), StreamingLLM (0.00%), Yarn-128K (0.00%), and the original model (1.40%). While 12.40% is far from perfect, it is the only model in the comparison that achieves better than random performance on this task at extreme lengths. The low absolute number indicates that KV retrieval — which requires extracting structured key-value pairs from long documents — remains challenging for all methods. The candidate token's vector representation likely struggles with the structured, multi-field nature of the retrieval target.

**On understanding and reasoning tasks** (which test whether the model can comprehend content, not just retrieve it):

- **En.MC** (multiple choice over 184.4K-token fake books): FocusLLM achieves 32.31%, slightly above InfLLM (31.44%), StreamingLLM (32.31% — identical), and Vic-16K (34.06%). The original model scores 30.13%. All models cluster in the 30-34% range, suggesting this task is difficulty-limited by base reasoning capability rather than context access.

- **Math.Find** (find specific math results in 87.9K-token contexts): FocusLLM achieves 11.71%, tied with the original Vicuna. Yarn-128K achieves 17.14%, which is the high mark. The low numbers across the board suggest this task stresses the model's mathematical reasoning more than its context retrieval.

- **Code.Debug** (debug code in 114.7K-token documents): FocusLLM achieves 28.43%, which is notably *lower* than StreamingLLM (46.19%) and the original Vicuna (38.83%). InfLLM scores 34.26%. This is a rare case where FocusLLM underperforms compression methods on a task that ostensibly requires full-context understanding. The paper does not comment on this result, but it may indicate that for code debugging, the relevant information is often in the most recent context (the code near the bug), making the dynamic condensing mechanism introduce noise without benefit.

The ∞-Bench results strongly support the paper's central claim: FocusLLM avoids information loss on tasks where compression methods fail catastrophically (PassKey, KV Retrieval). The performance on understanding tasks (En.MC, Math.Find) is competitive but not dominant, which is consistent with the paper's framing that FocusLLM is about *access* to information, not improved *reasoning* over that information. The base model's reasoning capability is preserved (since it's frozen), but not enhanced.

#### Figure 1: Passkey Retrieval Scaling to 400K

Figure 1 is the paper's most visually striking result. Across context lengths of 2K, 4K, 8K, 16K, 32K, 100K, 256K, and 400K:

- FocusLLM maintains near-100% accuracy, with a small dip to approximately 99% at 400K. The curve is essentially flat.
- Activation Beacon degrades from approximately 98% at 2K to approximately 75% at 8K, and continues declining to near-zero beyond 100K.
- LongLlama: approximately 98% at 2K, degrades rapidly beyond 32K (where it reaches approximately 80%), and drops below 60% at 256K. It is not tested at 400K.
- CEPE: approximately 100% at 2K–16K, maintains roughly 98% at 32K, degrades to approximately 90% at 100K, and is not tested beyond.
- Original Llama: approximately 100% at 2K, drops to near-zero beyond 4K (its native context length).

The key visual: at 400K, FocusLLM's bar is at ~99% while all other methods' bars are either absent (could not process the length) or near the x-axis. This figure directly demonstrates the "information loss" distinction: compression methods (Activation Beacon) lose the passkey as context length grows because the probability of compressing away the relevant segment increases. Memory methods (CEPE) have length ceilings due to memory constraints. FocusLLM's architecture keeps the passkey-bearing chunk intact and re-queries it at each decoding step, so accuracy is invariant to total length.

#### Memory Footprint and Inference Time (Figures 3 and 4)

**Figure 3 (GPU Memory Usage)** compares memory footprint at context lengths 4K, 8K, 16K, and 32K across five configurations: Standard (PI/NTK), CEPE, LongLlama, FocusLLM with parallel processing, and FocusLLM without parallel processing.

- **Standard (PI/NTK):** Memory grows sharply, from approximately 10GB at 4K to approximately 70GB at 32K, and would OOM beyond this. This is the quadratic attention cost in action.
- **CEPE:** Similar growth trajectory, rising from approximately 12GB at 4K to approximately 65GB at 32K, with OOM expected soon after. The cross-attention layers add overhead over the standard approach.
- **LongLlama:** Approximately 18GB at 4K, growing to approximately 62GB at 32K. Higher baseline memory due to architecture modifications.
- **FocusLLM (w/o parallel):** Starts at approximately 15GB at 4K, grows to approximately 42GB at 32K — substantially lower peak than all alternatives. Without parallelism, chunks are processed sequentially, so only one chunk's KV cache is in GPU memory at a time.
- **FocusLLM (parallel):** Starts at approximately 18GB at 4K, grows to approximately 40GB at 32K, with a shallower slope than the sequential variant. Parallel processing requires all chunks' KV caches simultaneously, but because each chunk is processed within the native 4K length, the total memory remains bounded.

The critical observation: **Standard, CEPE, and LongLlama all exhibit superlinear memory growth** (convex upward curves in Figure 3), while **FocusLLM's curves are approximately linear** (or gently sublinear). This means the memory advantage of FocusLLM compounds at longer lengths — at 32K, FocusLLM (parallel) uses approximately 57% of the memory of the Standard approach (40GB vs 70GB).

**Figure 4 (Inference Time per Token)** uses the same five configurations:

- **Standard (PI/NTK):** Starts at approximately 0.5s at 4K, climbs to approximately 2.1s at 32K. The superlinear growth reflects the quadratic attention cost.
- **CEPE:** Approximately 0.6s at 4K, climbs to approximately 2.4s at 32K — slower than the standard approach due to encoder-decoder overhead.
- **LongLlama:** Approximately 0.8s at 4K, climbs to approximately 2.5s at 32K — the slowest overall.
- **FocusLLM (w/o parallel):** Starts at approximately 0.7s at 4K, climbs to approximately 1.8s at 32K. Without parallelism, the inference time grows with the number of chunks, but the per-chunk processing is efficient.
- **FocusLLM (parallel):** Starts at approximately 0.5s at 4K (matching the standard approach), climbs to approximately 1.3s at 32K — the fastest among all long-context methods and only about 62% of the standard approach's inference time at 32K.

The paper's summary: "When ample memory resources are available, parallel processing is more efficient for FocusLLM" — it is both faster per token and uses less memory than alternatives. When memory is constrained, the sequential variant trades off speed for lower peak memory.

Importantly, these measurements are for a single forward pass (one decoding step). For autoregressive generation over many tokens, the per-token latency compounds. FocusLLM's advantage in per-token time directly translates to faster end-to-end generation for long-context tasks.

#### Chunk Size Ablation (Figure 5)

Figure 5 tests perplexity at a fixed total sequence length of 8K while varying chunk size across `{256, 512, 1024, 2048}` on PG19, Proof-Pile, and CodeParrot.

- **PG19:** Perplexity ranges from approximately 5.8 (at 256) to 6.2 (at 2048), with the lowest at 256. The variation is small (~0.4 perplexity across a 8× range of chunk sizes).
- **Proof-Pile:** Ranges from approximately 2.1 to 2.6, again relatively flat.
- **CodeParrot:** Ranges from approximately 2.1 to 2.6, similar pattern.

The paper's conclusion: "there is no consistent trend in perplexity as the chunk size increases; it remains relatively stable." This has an important practical implication: **larger chunk sizes can be used without meaningful accuracy degradation**, which directly reduces the number of chunks and therefore inference time. For a model with a longer native context length (e.g., LLaMA-2-32K), this means larger chunks and fewer forward passes, further improving efficiency.

However, the flat trend at 8K total length does not fully characterize behavior at extreme lengths. At 400K with 2048-token chunks, there would be approximately 195 chunks producing 195 candidate tokens. The paper does not test whether accuracy degrades when the local context must attend to hundreds of candidate tokens — Figure 1 shows Passkey Retrieval remains accurate at these lengths, but that task requires attending to only one candidate token, not aggregating across many.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Continuation Loss vs. Reconstruction Loss (Section 5.5, Table 4).** This is the most important ablation in the paper, as it tests whether both training objectives are necessary. The experiment trains three variants: (1) Both losses (the full FocusLLM), (2) Continuation Loss only, (3) Reconstruction Loss only. All use chunk size 2K and local context size 2K (hyperparameters: `(2K, 2K)`).

On **NarrativeQA** (single-doc QA): Full FocusLLM achieves 18.53; Continuation-only: 17.36; Reconstruction-only: 17.05. The drop from removing either loss is ~1.2-1.5 points — modest but consistent.

On **TREC** (few-shot classification): Full: 65.5; Continuation-only: 60.5; Reconstruction-only: 62.0. A larger gap of 3.5-5 points, indicating that the few-shot format benefits from both forward-continuation and reconstruction capabilities.

On **Math.Find** (∞-Bench): Full: 13.43; Continuation-only: 13.71 (slightly higher); Reconstruction-only: 12.86. The small differences suggest this task is more gated by reasoning than context extraction.

On **En.MC** (∞-Bench multiple choice): Full: 31.00; Continuation-only: 27.95; Reconstruction-only: 26.64. A drop of 3-4 points when either loss is removed.

The critical result is on **Retrieve.PassKey**: Full FocusLLM achieves **99.32%**; Continuation-only achieves **1.69%** (effectively random); Reconstruction-only achieves **91.19%**. The catastrophic collapse of Continuation-only on Passkey Retrieval is the paper's strongest piece of evidence that Reconstruction Loss is essential. Without it, the model never learns that candidate tokens should preserve *specific retrievable information* — it only learns to use them for smooth continuation, which does not require encoding arbitrary details from the distant past.

The Reconstruction-only result (91.19%) is respectable but still below the full model (99.32%), confirming that Continuation Loss also contributes — likely by teaching the model to handle the generative aspects of the task (producing the answer format, maintaining fluency) while Reconstruction Loss teaches the extraction.

**Local context size (Section 5.5, Table 4, last row).** Reducing the local context size from the default (~3.5K for LongBench) to 1K tokens (hyperparameters: `(1K, 2K)`) produces:

- NarrativeQA: 17.87 vs. 18.53 (full) — a small drop of 0.66.
- TREC: 63.0 vs. 65.5 — a drop of 2.5.
- Math.Find: 8.86 vs. 13.43 — a drop of 4.57, the largest relative decline.
- En.MC: 29.69 vs. 31.00 — a small drop.
- Retrieve.PassKey: 99.32 vs. 99.32 — no change.

The paper's interpretation: "candidate tokens cannot fully replace the information within the context." Reducing the local context size from 3.5K to 1K removes 2.5K tokens of immediate context, forcing the model to rely more heavily on candidate tokens. The fact that performance degrades (especially on Math.Find) indicates that candidate tokens are imperfect — they lose some fidelity compared to having the original tokens in the local context. However, the degradation is modest on most tasks (TREC drops from 65.5 to 63.0, which is within 4%), suggesting the candidate tokens are reasonably good substitutes for most purposes.

**PRM aggregation strategy ablation.** Not applicable to this paper — FocusLLM does not use a Process Reward Model. The paper's architecture is end-to-end generation without separate verifier components. (Note: this heading appears in the reference example but is not relevant here.)

**Revision model verifier choice.** Not applicable — FocusLLM does not use revision models or separate verifiers.

**Oracle vs. predicted difficulty bins.** Not applicable — FocusLLM does not use difficulty-based strategy selection.

**Majority voting for revisions.** Not applicable — FocusLLM does not include a revision mechanism.

**RestEM revision model.** Not applicable.

**Model-specific ablations not performed but relevant.** The paper does **not** include several ablations that would strengthen its claims:

- **Dynamic prompt length:** The paper adopts 512 tokens by default but provides no ablation showing sensitivity to this choice. Is 256 tokens sufficient? Does 1024 tokens help or hurt? This is a significant gap because the dynamic prompt length directly affects the information capacity for query formulation and the computational cost per chunk (longer prompts mean less room for chunk content within the native context length).

- **Number of candidate tokens per chunk:** The paper uses one candidate token per chunk (the last token of the dynamic prompt). Would multiple candidate tokens per chunk improve information retention, perhaps allowing the model to extract different types of information (e.g., facts vs. structure) into separate vectors? The single-candidate design creates a bottleneck — all information from a 4K-token chunk must be compressed into one 4096-dimensional vector. Multiple candidate tokens would increase the bottleneck width at the cost of more KV pairs in the local context.

- **Layer-wise ablation of candidate token parameters:** The paper adds `$W^c$` parameters at all 32 layers. Would they be equally effective if added only at certain layers (e.g., only the top 8)? Could they be shared across layers (weight tying) to reduce parameter count? The paper claims 2B additional parameters is "parameter-efficient" relative to full fine-tuning, but 2B is still a substantial model in its own right — larger than many deployed language models.

- **Training data size:** 80K sequences (0.5B tokens) is used. Would performance improve with more data? The paper mentions this in Section 8 (Limitations): "we have observed that training on larger datasets can significantly enhance its generalizability and performance." Without an ablation showing the scaling trend, readers cannot assess whether FocusLLM's performance is near saturation or would continue improving.

- **Base model scale:** All experiments use LLaMA-2-7B. The paper claims FocusLLM can be applied to "any decoder-only LLM," but provides no evidence on smaller models (e.g., 1.3B) or larger models (e.g., 13B, 70B). The dynamic condensing mechanism's effectiveness may depend on the base model's capacity — a 1.3B model might not have sufficient attention head capacity to learn effective query-conditioned extraction, while a 70B model might benefit disproportionately.

- **Comparison with retrieval-augmented generation (RAG):** FocusLLM is architecturally similar to RAG in spirit (chunk documents, retrieve relevant chunks, condition on retrieved content). A head-to-head comparison against a RAG baseline — even a simple one using embedding similarity to retrieve top-K chunks — would help readers understand when the learned candidate token mechanism outperforms explicit retrieval and vice versa. InfLLM is the closest comparison (it retrieves memory units using attention scores), but a standard RAG baseline is absent.

- **Comparison against simply truncating:** For tasks where the answer is in the last 4K tokens, FocusLLM's architecture adds overhead without benefit. The paper does not report how often this occurs on LongBench or ∞-Bench, or whether a simple "use last 4K tokens" baseline would be competitive on the subset of tasks where long-range dependencies are absent.

### Critical Assessment

#### Claim 1: "FocusLLM achieves remarkable results with high training efficiency by introducing only a small set of trainable parameters and utilizing a training budget of 0.5B tokens."

**The training efficiency claim is well-supported but narrowly scoped.** The paper convincingly shows that FocusLLM trains on 0.5B tokens (vs. LongLlama's 7B) with approximately 2B trainable parameters (vs. 7B fully trainable), and training completes in 20 hours on 8×A100 GPUs. These are specific, verifiable numbers. However, the claim's framing as "high training efficiency" requires unpacking:

- **Parameter count:** 2B additional parameters is approximately 29% of LLaMA-2-7B's parameter count. This is "only" relative to full fine-tuning (which would train all 7B), but it is not parameter-efficient in the sense of lightweight adapters (LoRA adds ~0.1% of parameters). The paper does not justify why 2B parameters are needed rather than, say, 200M — no ablation on parameter count or projection matrix rank is provided.

- **Training data:** 0.5B tokens is small relative to pretraining-scale data (LLaMA-2 was trained on 2T tokens), but it is not small in absolute terms — 0.5B tokens is approximately 4 million pages of text. The paper frames this as "much less training cost than previous methods," referencing LongLlama's 7B tokens, which is fair as a relative comparison but does not establish that 0.5B tokens is the minimum needed.

- **The efficiency claim compares against methods that achieve different capability profiles.** LongLlama, for instance, does not target extreme lengths (it is not evaluated beyond 32K in Table 1), while FocusLLM reaches 400K. The appropriate comparison should control for effective context length — is FocusLLM more training-efficient *per unit of effective context length*, or simply training-efficient in absolute terms? The paper implies the former but does not formalize this metric.

**A missing experiment:** How does FocusLLM's performance scale with training data? With 0.1B tokens? With 1B tokens? Without a scaling curve, the efficiency claim is a point estimate rather than a characterization. If performance saturates at 0.3B tokens, the remaining 0.2B are wasted; if it continues improving, the 0.5B figure is a lower bound.

#### Claim 2: "FocusLLM extends the fixed context length of any decoder-only LLM, allowing the model to focus on relevant information from very long sequences."

**The "any decoder-only LLM" claim is asserted but tested on only one model family (LLaMA-2-7B-Chat).** The paper's architecture — appending dynamic prompts, extracting candidate tokens, concatenating KV pairs — should in principle work with any decoder-only transformer, since it only requires the ability to add projection parameters at each layer. However, several aspects of the experimental setup are LLaMA-specific:

- The native context length of 4K determines chunk size and the feasibility of parallel processing.
- LLaMA-2's pretraining on RedPajama means the training data distribution matches the base model's pretraining, which may matter for the candidate token projection learning.
- The 7B scale provides sufficient attention head capacity for query-conditioned extraction; smaller models might struggle.

**The paper does not evaluate on models with longer native context lengths** (e.g., LLaMA-2-32K, Mistral-7B with 8K context, or GPT-4 with 128K context). Section 5.4 acknowledges this gap: "we can employ larger chunk sizes on models with longer default context lengths (e.g. LLaMA-2-32K). We will explore this direction in our future work." This is a significant omission because models with longer native contexts would change the tradeoff landscape: larger chunks mean fewer forward passes per chunk but coarser-grained candidate tokens. It is not obvious that the dynamic condensing mechanism would be equally effective when each chunk contains 32K tokens rather than 4K — the single candidate token bottleneck becomes more severe.

#### Claim 3: "FocusLLM can maintain a precise understanding of the whole long context" and achieves "superior performance across downstream tasks."

**Substantial evidence supports precise understanding on retrieval tasks, but "precise" has specific limits.** The Passkey Retrieval result (99% at 400K, Figure 1) is the strongest evidence — it shows that FocusLLM can locate and reproduce a specific piece of information buried in an arbitrarily long document. The Retrieval.Number result (83.56%, Table 2) confirms this for multi-digit targets.

**However, "precise understanding" is not the same as "retrieval."** On tasks requiring synthesis, reasoning, or aggregation across chunks, the performance is competitive but not dominant:

- On NarrativeQA (Table 2): 21.14 vs. CEPE's 22.14 in Table 3. The difference is small, and FocusLLM does not clearly outperform all baselines.
- On MultiFieldQA: 36.73 vs. LongChat-32K's 42.55 — FocusLLM is substantially behind.
- On Musique (multi-hop reasoning over multiple documents): 14.20 vs. LongChat-32K's 14.71 — essentially tied.
- On Code.Debug: 28.43, which is *worse* than StreamingLLM's 46.19 and the original Vicuna's 38.83. This is a case where "precise understanding" is actually degraded relative to simpler methods.

The pattern suggests that FocusLLM's candidate token mechanism excels at *locating and reproducing* information (which is what the retrieval tasks test) but is neutral or slightly detrimental for *reasoning over* information that is already in the local context or that requires synthesis across chunks. This is a nuance the paper acknowledges only indirectly: "candidate tokens cannot fully replace the information within the context" (Section 5.5). The vector representation of a 4K-token chunk, passed through a single candidate token, inevitably loses some structural information (argument order, spatial relationships, relative positions) that might matter for multi-hop reasoning or code debugging.

**The "superior performance" claim is quantitatively supported on average but task-dependent.** The LongBench average of 36.17 is indeed the highest in Table 2, but the margin over LongChat-32K (34.70) is 1.47 points across 16 tasks — a 4.2% relative improvement. On ∞-Bench, the average of 44.03 vs. InfLLM's 43.05 is a 2.3% relative improvement. These are real but modest gains. The paper's strongest differentiators are on the retrieval subtasks where FocusLLM uniquely avoids catastrophic failure (PassKey, KV Retrieval) — these tasks pull up the average substantially. On the understanding subtasks, the performance is often indistinguishable from or slightly below the best baselines.

#### Claim 4: "FocusLLM can process up to 400K tokens" and generalizes "even up to 400K tokens."

**The 400K claim is supported for Passkey Retrieval but narrowly tested.** Figure 1 shows 99% Passkey Retrieval at 400K. Figure 6 in Appendix G shows perplexity on PG19 at 400K (approximately 45), which is higher than at 100K (10.59) but still far below catastrophic degradation. These are positive signals.

**However, 400K testing is limited to two tasks** — Passkey Retrieval and language modeling perplexity. There is no 400K evaluation on LongBench tasks (the benchmark's maximum length is ~15K per sample), no 400K evaluation on ∞-Bench tasks other than the retrieval subtasks, and no evaluation of whether the model can *reason* over a 400K document (e.g., answer a question that requires integrating information from beginning and end). The Passkey task specifically tests whether a single piece of information can be retrieved, which is the most favorable case for FocusLLM's architecture — only one chunk matters, and the candidate token from that chunk needs to encode only one number.

The paper acknowledges this limitation in Section 8: "Due to hardware constraints, our tests were limited to 400K tokens, which does not represent the upper bound of FocusLLM's capabilities." The 400K claim is therefore best interpreted as: "FocusLLM does not hit a hard failure mode at 400K on the tasks tested, unlike compression methods which degrade monotonically with length." It is not a claim that 400K-length *reasoning* works well, because that was not tested.

#### What experiments would have strengthened the paper

**1. Attention weight analysis for hard negative chunks.** Figures 7 and 8 show attention heatmaps for Passkey and NarrativeQA, which demonstrate that candidate tokens are attended to when relevant. A more diagnostic experiment would test whether candidate tokens from chunks containing *distracting* information (e.g., another number that looks like a passkey but is wrong) are correctly ignored. This would verify that the dynamic prompt mechanism is selective, not just that it can find information when it is present.

**2. Information-theoretic analysis of the candidate token bottleneck.** If a 4K-token chunk is summarized into one 4096-dimensional vector, what is the effective capacity? An experiment that gradually increases the amount of information the model must extract from a chunk (e.g., retrieving progressively longer strings) would characterize where the bottleneck saturates. The Retrieval.Number result (83.56% for multi-digit numbers) vs. Passkey (95.76% for a single number) hints at this saturation but does not systematically explore it.

**3. Comparison with a trivial "last-N" baseline on all tasks.** For each LongBench and ∞-Bench task, what percentage of questions can be answered using only the last 4K tokens of the context? If 80% of questions are answerable from the local context alone, then FocusLLM's architecture solves a problem that only affects 20% of test cases, which would contextualize the average improvements. The paper does not report this statistic.

**4. Scaling behavior with model size.** The claim that FocusLLM can be applied to "any decoder-only LLM" would be much stronger with experiments on at least one other model size (e.g., LLaMA-2-13B or a 1.3B model). The parameter count of the additional projections scales with hidden dimension, so the relative cost of FocusLLM varies with model size (2B additional parameters is 29% of 7B, but would be ~15% of 13B or ~3% of 70B). Understanding how this ratio affects the performance-efficiency tradeoff is important for practical adoption.

**5. Training data ablation from different distributions.** The paper uses RedPajama, which matches LLaMA-1's pretraining data. Would FocusLLM trained on a different corpus (e.g., only books, only code) still generalize to the full range of LongBench tasks? This would test whether the learned query-conditioned extraction skill is data-dependent or genuinely general — a distinction the paper's "any decoder-only LLM" framing implies but does not test.

**6. Impact of dynamic prompt content on extraction quality.** An experiment varying the dynamic prompt systematically — e.g., replacing the actual question with a paraphrase, or adding distractors to the prompt — would characterize how robust the candidate token extraction is to the query formulation. This matters for deployment because in real use, the model's dynamic prompt evolves organically and may not perfectly articulate the information need.

#### Where the paper's claims exceed the experimental evidence

**The phrase "without any information loss" (Section 1, second paragraph) is an overstatement.** The architecture avoids *irreversible* information loss — chunks are never discarded, and the full text is always accessible in principle. However, the candidate token mechanism introduces a *representational bottleneck* that can lose information. A 4K-token chunk is mapped to a single 4096-dimensional vector, which necessarily discards some information (token-level positional relationships within the chunk, exact phrasing, fine-grained structural details). The Reconstruction Loss only ensures that *some* information is preserved, not *all* information. The performance degradation when reducing local context size (Table 4, last row: Math.Find drops from 13.43 to 8.86, TREC drops from 65.5 to 63.0) provides evidence of this representational information loss — if candidate tokens perfectly preserved all chunk information, reducing local context size would not affect performance.

The more accurate characterization is: "FocusLLM avoids the *catastrophic, irreversible* information loss that compression-based methods exhibit, by keeping all chunks accessible and re-querying them at each decoding step. Some representational information loss occurs due to the single-candidate-token bottleneck, but this loss is query-dependent (the dynamic prompt determines what is preserved) and empirically modest on retrieval tasks."

**The claim of "precise understanding" is strongly supported only for retrieval, not for reasoning.** The ∞-Bench results (Table 2) show competitive but not dominant performance on understanding tasks (En.MC: 32.31%, within 2 points of all baselines; Math.Find: 11.71%, below YaRN's 17.14%). The Code.Debug result (28.43% vs. original's 38.83%) is a case where FocusLLM's architecture appears to *degrade* understanding relative to the base model. The paper does not explain this regression, and it challenges the universality of the "precise understanding" claim.

**The 400K generalization claim is valid but scope-limited.** It applies to Passkey Retrieval (Figure 1) and language modeling (Appendix G), which are the most favorable tasks for the architecture. Whether the model can effectively reason over 400K tokens — answering questions that require integrating information from sections 100K tokens apart — is untested. The paper's language "maintains low perplexities when handling extensive texts, up to 400K tokens" is accurate; the implication that this translates to downstream task performance at 400K is untested.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Candidate Token Bottleneck Introduces Representational Information Loss

**The assumption or constraint.** FocusLLM compresses each chunk — which can contain up to 4,096 tokens of original text — into a single candidate token vector of dimension `d_dec = 4096`. This is a compression ratio of approximately 4,096:1 in terms of token count, with all information from the chunk forced through one attention-weighted aggregation at each layer. The paper does not explicitly claim this compression is lossless, but the framing "without any information loss" (Section 1) and "precise understanding of the whole long context" (abstract) implies that no practically important information is discarded.

**The consequence.** The single-vector bottleneck necessarily discards structural information that cannot be captured in one fixed-dimensional representation: token-level positional relationships within the chunk, the relative ordering of multiple facts, fine-grained syntactic structures, and the ability to distinguish between closely related but distinct pieces of information. This manifests empirically as a **ceiling on tasks requiring precise token-level extraction or multi-fact synthesis from within a single chunk**. The Retrieval.KV result (12.40% at ∞-Bench, Table 2) — where the model must extract structured key-value pairs — suggests the candidate token struggles to encode multiple discrete fields accurately. The Code.Debug result (28.43%, below the original model's 38.83%) indicates the bottleneck may actually degrade performance when fine-grained structural understanding of code is needed. The local context size ablation (Table 4, last row) provides direct evidence: reducing local context from ~3.5K to 1K tokens degrades Math.Find from 13.43 to 8.86 and TREC from 65.5 to 63.0, demonstrating that "candidate tokens cannot fully replace the information within the context" (Section 5.5) — some information is permanently lost through the bottleneck.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides no direct measurement of the bottleneck's information capacity. There is no experiment that systematically varies the amount of information per chunk and measures extraction accuracy (e.g., retrieving progressively longer strings, or an increasing number of independent facts from a single chunk). The attention heatmaps (Figures 7 and 8, Appendix H) show *which* candidate tokens are attended to but not *what information within them* is preserved versus lost. The Retrieval.Number result (83.56%, Table 2) versus Passkey (95.76%) hints at capacity limits — multi-digit numbers are harder than single numbers — but does not quantify the saturation point. The paper reports these numbers without analyzing them as bottleneck evidence.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation directly. Section 8 (Limitations) mentions future work on "increasing the training data size" and "quantization methods to reduce operational costs" but does not discuss the candidate token bottleneck or propose mechanisms to widen it (e.g., multiple candidate tokens per chunk, higher-dimensional projections, or hierarchical aggregation). The paper's framing — treating single-candidate-token extraction as sufficient — implicitly assumes the bottleneck is not practically limiting, but the mixed results on KV Retrieval and Code.Debug suggest otherwise.

---

### The 400K-Token Generalization Claim Is Tested on Only Two Tasks, Both Favorable to the Architecture

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's headline claim — "FocusLLM exhibits superior performance... when handling extensive long texts, even up to 400K tokens" (abstract) — rests on experiments that, at 400K length, cover only Passkey Retrieval (Figure 1) and language modeling perplexity (Appendix G, Figure 6). These are the two tasks most structurally favorable to FocusLLM's architecture: Passkey requires extracting exactly one piece of information (so only one candidate token matters), and perplexity measures the model's ability to predict the next word given preceding context (which does not require precise retrieval of distant information — local statistical patterns dominate).

**The consequence.** We do not know whether FocusLLM can **reason** over a 400K-token document. Tasks requiring the model to (a) integrate information from multiple widely-separated chunks, (b) resolve contradictions between chunks, (c) perform multi-hop reasoning spanning the full length, or (d) maintain coherence over a 400K-token generation are entirely untested. The number of candidate tokens at 400K length (~200 for 2K-token chunks, or ~100 for 4K-token chunks) is far greater than the number seen during training (sequences under 8K would produce at most 1-2 candidate tokens). The local context's self-attention must attend to ~200 candidate tokens plus its own tokens, which could create attention dilution — the model's attention scores get distributed across many candidate tokens, reducing the effective weight on the genuinely relevant ones. The paper acknowledges this implicitly in Appendix G: "the number of candidate tokens corresponding to 400K is 200, which is far greater than the number of candidate tokens seen during training," but treats the maintained perplexity as evidence that "FocusLLM has strong extrapolation capabilities" without testing whether this extrapolation holds for reasoning tasks.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figure 1 (Passkey Retrieval at 400K) and Figure 6 in Appendix G (perplexity at 400K on PG19) are the only 400K results. There is no 400K evaluation on any LongBench task (the benchmark's samples average 5K–15K tokens), no 400K evaluation on ∞-Bench understanding tasks (En.MC, Math.Find, Code.Debug), and no 400K evaluation of generation quality (e.g., summary coherence, dialogue consistency). The paper's own limitation statement acknowledges: "Due to hardware constraints, our tests were limited to 400K tokens, which does not represent the upper bound of FocusLLM's capabilities" (Section 8), but frames this as a ceiling on how *long* they could test, not as a limitation on the *breadth* of testing at 400K.

**Mitigation status.** The paper treats the 400K generalization as demonstrated rather than as partially tested. There is no discussion of why Passkey and perplexity might be insufficient to validate the "precise understanding" claim at extreme lengths, and no acknowledgment that attention dilution across hundreds of candidate tokens could degrade reasoning performance. Section 5.4's chunk size analysis (Figure 5), which shows stable perplexity at 8K total length across chunk sizes, is presented optimistically — "we can employ larger chunk sizes on models with longer default context lengths" — without discussing the potential failure mode when hundreds of candidate tokens compete for attention.

### Training Efficiency Gains Are Not Benchmarked Against the Cost of Re-Processing All Chunks at Every Decoding Step

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper emphasizes FocusLLM's training efficiency: 0.5B tokens of training data, ~2B trainable parameters, 20 hours on 8×A100 GPUs (Section 3, Appendix C). The comparison is against methods like LongLlama (7B tokens, full-parameter training). However, training cost is a one-time expense amortized over all downstream inferences. The paper's **inference-time cost** — which recurs on every generated token — is substantially higher than the local-context-only baseline because all memory chunks must be re-processed through the full forward pass at each decoding step, with the updated dynamic prompt appended.

**The consequence.** For autoregressive generation of `T` tokens, FocusLLM performs `T × k` chunk forward passes (where `k` is the number of memory chunks), compared to `T × 1` forward passes for a standard model operating within its native context length. The parallel processing mechanism (Section 2.3) reduces *wall-clock* time by processing all chunks simultaneously, but the total FLOPs per generated token still scales with `k`. The paper's Figure 4 shows per-token inference time at 32K context length: FocusLLM (parallel) takes approximately 1.3 seconds vs. approximately 2.1 seconds for the standard approach — a 38% reduction. However, this comparison is against a full-attention baseline that is paying the quadratic self-attention cost over the entire 32K sequence, which FocusLLM avoids. Compared to a method that simply truncates to the last 4K tokens (the base model's native length), FocusLLM is substantially more expensive — the base model would take approximately 0.5 seconds per token (the 4K point in Figure 4) vs. FocusLLM's 1.3 seconds, meaning the ability to access long-range context costs approximately 2.6× more per generated token.

For applications generating long outputs (e.g., summarization, dialogue, code completion), this per-token overhead compounds. Generating a 500-token summary from a 128K-token document would require 500 × 32 ≈ 16,000 forward passes through the chunk encoder, vs. 500 for a truncation-based approach. The paper does not provide end-to-end generation time measurements that would make this cost transparent.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figures 3 and 4 provide per-step memory and latency measurements, which the paper presents as evidence of efficiency. However, these measurements are for a single decoding step and compare against methods (Standard, CEPE, LongLlama) that are also paying the full long-context cost. The paper never compares end-to-end generation cost against a simple truncation baseline (use only the last 4K tokens) on tasks where the answer might be found in recent context. There is no measurement of what fraction of LongBench or ∞-Bench tasks *require* the long-range information that FocusLLM provides, and therefore no analysis of the cost-per-necessary-retrieval. The paper does not report total FLOPs or total GPU-hours for the inference experiments.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges the inference-time cost only indirectly. Section 5.3 notes: "Although FocusLLM splits long texts into numerous chunks, resulting in a slightly longer inference time compared to the standard approach, it still holds a significant advantage over other long-context methods." The key word is "slightly" — the paper frames the overhead as minor by comparing against full-attention baselines that are even more expensive, rather than against a truncation baseline that would reveal the true cost of long-range access. The parallel decoding mechanism is presented as a solution to this overhead (by parallelizing chunk processing), but parallelization does not reduce total computation — it only reduces wall-clock time when sufficient hardware parallelism is available. For deployments with limited GPU resources (e.g., a single GPU), the sequential processing variant would be used, and Figure 4 shows its inference time at 32K is approximately 1.8 seconds per token — only marginally better than the Standard approach's 2.1 seconds.

### Single Model Family and Benchmark Evaluation Limits Confidence in Architectural Generality

**The assumption or constraint.** FocusLLM is presented as a framework "designed to extend the fixed context length of any decoder-only LLM" (abstract). The paper trains and evaluates on exactly one model family (LLaMA-2-7B-Chat) with a 4K native context length and evaluates on exactly two downstream benchmarks (LongBench and ∞-Bench) plus language modeling datasets. All training uses RedPajama, which matches LLaMA-1's pretraining distribution.

**The consequence.** Several aspects of FocusLLM's architecture may depend on properties of the base model that are not tested:

- **Model scale dependence:** The candidate token projections add ~2B parameters to a 7B model (29% overhead). For a 1.3B model, 2B additional parameters would more than double the parameter count — the relative overhead grows as model size shrinks. For a 70B model, 2B is ~3% overhead — the architecture might be unexpectedly effective or ineffective at different scales. The candidate token's representational capacity (4096 dimensions) is a larger fraction of a small model's total capacity and a smaller fraction of a large model's, which could change the bottleneck's severity.

- **Native context length dependence:** LLaMA-2-7B has a 4K context length. A model with 32K native context (e.g., LLaMA-2-32K) would use larger chunks, which means fewer chunks for the same total length but a more severe per-chunk bottleneck (compressing 32K tokens into one vector vs. 4K). The tradeoff between chunk count and bottleneck severity is unexplored. The paper acknowledges this as future work: "we can employ larger chunk sizes on models with longer default context lengths (e.g. LLaMA-2-32K). We will explore this direction in our future work" (Section 5.4).

- **Pretraining data distribution dependence:** The training uses RedPajama, which is the LLaMA-1 pretraining dataset. The candidate token mechanism may learn to exploit distributional properties of RedPajama (document structure, typical long-range dependency patterns) that do not transfer to out-of-distribution long texts (legal documents, medical records, multilingual text, highly structured data). The paper provides no cross-corpus evaluation.

- **Benchmark diversity:** The paper's main downstream results are on LongBench (English tasks, average length 5K–15K) and ∞-Bench (average 145.1K tokens, retrieval-heavy). There is no evaluation on tasks requiring very long output generation (e.g., writing a coherent multi-page report grounded in a long document), on multimodal long-context tasks, or on domain-specific evaluations (legal document analysis, scientific literature review, codebase understanding at repository scale). The strong Passkey results demonstrate retrieval fidelity but not the full spectrum of long-context capabilities.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The limitation is partially acknowledged in Section 8: "While FocusLLM demonstrates exceptional training efficiency, we have observed that training on larger datasets can significantly enhance its generalizability and performance. Therefore, increasing the training data size will be a focus of future research." This admits that the current training setup may not be optimal, but frames it as a data quantity limitation rather than a model-family or benchmark diversity limitation. The paper provides no model-scale ablation, no native-context-length ablation, and no cross-corpus evaluation. The use of two strong, well-established benchmarks (LongBench and ∞-Bench) is respectable — most context-extension papers evaluate on fewer — but does not establish that FocusLLM would maintain its advantages in substantially different deployment scenarios.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not attempt to mitigate this limitation experimentally. The "any decoder-only LLM" claim is asserted based on architectural generality (the mechanism only requires adding projection parameters, which is technically feasible for any decoder-only transformer) rather than empirically demonstrated. The limitation is partially transparent — the paper does not claim to have evaluated on multiple model families — but the abstract's universal framing invites readers to assume broader validation than exists.

### Dynamic Prompt Length and Content Sensitivity Are Not Characterized

**The assumption or constraint.** The dynamic prompt — the fragment of local context appended to each chunk — is the mechanism that determines which information gets extracted. Its length (512 tokens by default) and its content (which evolves organically as the model generates tokens) directly control what the candidate tokens encode. The paper treats the 512-token default as sufficient and does not explore how extraction quality varies with prompt length, content, or formulation.

**The consequence.** In deployment, the dynamic prompt's content depends on the model's generation trajectory. If the model generates tokens that are poorly aligned with the information it actually needs — for instance, generating verbose preamble before getting to the core question — the dynamic prompt may encode a diffuse or irrelevant query, leading to candidate tokens that fail to extract the critical information. This creates a **compounding error risk**: an early generation misstep degrades the dynamic prompt, which degrades candidate token extraction, which degrades subsequent generation. The paper's training setup (Section 3) uses ground-truth next tokens as the dynamic prompt during teacher-forced training, which means the model never experiences its own imperfect generations as dynamic prompt content during training. There is an exposure bias: at training time, the dynamic prompt always contains high-quality, ground-truth context; at inference time, it contains the model's own (potentially flawed) outputs.

This exposure bias could cause a **distribution shift at inference time** that degrades performance, particularly when the model needs to recover from an initial error. If the first few generated tokens are off-target, the dynamic prompt will steer candidate token extraction toward irrelevant information, and the model may have no mechanism to course-correct because subsequent tokens will further reinforce the irrelevant extraction. The paper's reported results — which are on tasks with relatively short expected outputs (answers, not long generations) — may mask this effect because the dynamic prompt does not drift far from the ground-truth trajectory. For long-form generation (multi-paragraph summaries, extended dialogue), the dynamic prompt could accumulate errors over many steps.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides **no ablation on dynamic prompt length** — 512 tokens is chosen without experimental justification. There is **no analysis of how dynamic prompt content affects extraction quality** — no experiment varying the prompt (e.g., replacing the question with a paraphrase, adding distractors, truncating early tokens) and measuring whether candidate token attention patterns change appropriately. There is **no measurement of exposure bias** — no comparison of model performance when the dynamic prompt contains ground-truth context vs. model-generated context. The attention heatmaps (Figures 7, 8) show that candidate tokens are attended to, but do not test whether *irrelevant* dynamic prompts cause *incorrect* candidate token extraction. The paper's training procedure description notes that during training, "the loss is only applied to tokens in the local context, which encourages the candidate token to aggregate useful information from each chunk" (Section 3), but does not discuss the quality of the dynamic prompt during training vs. inference.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation. There is no discussion of exposure bias, dynamic prompt quality, or the potential for error compounding in long-form generation. The 512-token default is presented as a fixed hyperparameter without sensitivity analysis. The training procedure's reliance on ground-truth local context tokens for the dynamic prompt — a form of teacher forcing — is not flagged as a potential source of train-inference mismatch.

### Hard Retrieval Tasks and Certain Reasoning Tasks Remain Near-Unsolvable

**The consequence.** On the hardest retrieval and reasoning tasks at extreme lengths, FocusLLM's performance — while better than all baselines — remains far from usable. The Retrieval.KV result (12.40%, Table 2) means the model fails on roughly 7 out of 8 KV retrieval queries at 145.1K-token average length. This is the *best* result among all compared models (InfLLM: 0.60%, StreamingLLM: 0.00%, YaRN: 0.00%), but a 12.4% success rate is not deployment-grade for applications requiring reliable structured information extraction. The Math.Find result (11.71%, tied with the original 4K model) indicates FocusLLM provides no improvement over the base model for mathematical fact retrieval in long contexts — the long-context mechanism adds overhead without benefit. The Code.Debug result (28.43%, *below* the original model's 38.83%) suggests the architecture can actually harm performance on certain reasoning tasks, possibly because candidate token noise outweighs the benefit of long-range access.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 2 provides the raw numbers. The paper reports them without analysis of why these specific tasks remain difficult. For Retrieval.KV, the candidate token bottleneck is the likely explanation: a single vector cannot faithfully encode multiple structured key-value pairs with their associations intact. For Math.Find, the base model's mathematical reasoning capability (frozen and unchanged by FocusLLM) may be the binding constraint — the model can access the relevant context but cannot parse the mathematical notation within it well enough to locate the target. For Code.Debug, the candidate tokens may interfere with the local context's self-attention by adding noise from irrelevant code chunks, degrading the model's ability to focus on the buggy region.

The paper does not analyze these failure modes, offer per-task error analysis, or characterize what types of queries are fundamentally beyond FocusLLM's capability. This limits a practitioner's ability to predict whether FocusLLM will help or hurt for a specific deployment task.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address these failure cases directly. The discussion focuses on tasks where FocusLLM excels (Passkey, Number Retrieval, QA) and treats the low scores on Retrieval.KV, Math.Find, and Code.Debug as benchmark results without interpretation. Section 8 acknowledges general limitations ("our tests were limited to 400K tokens") but does not identify specific task categories where the architecture is fundamentally mismatched. A practitioner considering FocusLLM for structured data extraction from long documents (e.g., extracting all key-value pairs from a 200K-token legal contract) would find no guidance on expected accuracy or failure modes beyond the raw 12.4% number.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

FocusLLM introduces a conceptual reframing of the long-context problem that, if validated across a broader range of models and tasks, could shift how the field allocates effort between three competing approaches: length extrapolation via positional encoding tricks, full-attention fine-tuning on ever-longer sequences, and irreversible context compression. The paper's central move — recasting context extension from "compress the past in anticipation of future needs" to "keep the past intact and re-query it at each decoding step with the current cognitive state" — is not an incremental improvement on existing compression methods. It is a **different problem definition** with different failure modes.

The magnitude of this shift becomes clear when examining what FocusLLM makes obsolete and what it makes newly tractable:

**What becomes less attractive.** The paper's Passkey Retrieval results at 400K tokens (Figure 1: 99% accuracy, essentially flat across all lengths) versus Activation Beacon's collapse to 1.69% (Appendix F, Table 8) provides strong evidence that **irreversible compression methods have a fundamental ceiling on retrieval fidelity that grows more severe with context length**. This is not a weakness that can be patched with better compression heuristics or more training data — it is inherent in making discard decisions before knowing what will be needed. The paper effectively draws a line in the sand: any method that permanently discards tokens will fail on a fraction of queries proportional to the compression ratio, and that fraction approaches 100% as context length grows for tasks where the target information is sparse. This makes continued investment in pure compression approaches (StreamingLLM-style token dropping, Activation Beacon-style segment summarization) harder to justify for applications requiring reliable retrieval from long documents.

Similarly, the paper challenges the necessity of **training on long sequences to handle long contexts**. FocusLLM trains on sequences under 8K tokens (0.5B tokens total) yet generalizes to 400K, outperforming LongLlama (trained on 7B tokens of long-context data with full parameter fine-tuning) on downstream tasks while being ~14× more data-efficient. This does not mean long-context training is useless — YaRN-128K achieves lower perplexity at 32K (6.38 vs. FocusLLM's 9.17 on PG19, Table 1) — but it suggests that architectural mechanisms for query-conditioned extraction can substitute for long-context training experience, particularly when the goal is precise retrieval rather than low perplexity on forward continuation. The implication for research prioritization is significant: **improving the query mechanism (how the model expresses what it needs) and the extraction mechanism (how chunks respond to queries) may yield greater gains per unit of training compute than simply scaling up training sequence length.**

**What becomes newly tractable.** The paper makes a specific diagnostic contribution by identifying **information loss as a measurable, binary failure mode** that can be tested with minimal synthetic tasks (Passkey Retrieval, KV Retrieval, Number String from ∞-Bench). Prior work on context extension evaluated methods on aggregate benchmarks (LongBench, language modeling perplexity) where information loss could be masked by task composition — if 80% of test questions can be answered from the last 4K tokens, a method that discards everything beyond 4K would score 80% and look competitive. The ∞-Bench retrieval tasks, particularly Passkey, serve as a cheap, easily-implementable litmus test: if a method's Passkey accuracy degrades with context length, it necessarily suffers from information loss that will affect some fraction of real queries. This diagnostic sharpens the evaluation landscape and makes it harder for future methods to claim "long-context capability" based on aggregate scores that blend easy and hard retrieval tasks. The near-zero scores of Activation Beacon on these tasks (Table 8) — despite competitive LongBench averages — demonstrate exactly this masking effect.

The paper also introduces a **decomposition of long-context understanding into separable sub-problems**: storage (keeping all chunks accessible), query formulation (encoding the current information need in the dynamic prompt), extraction (mapping chunk + query → relevant information vector), and aggregation (combining extracted information across chunks for next-token prediction). This decomposition was implicit in prior memory-based methods (InfLLM, CEPE) but was never cleanly articulated as an architectural design pattern. FocusLLM makes each component explicit and independently improvable: the dynamic prompt design can be refined without changing chunk processing, the candidate token projection architecture can be optimized without changing the base model, and the aggregation mechanism (how candidate tokens influence local context attention) can be tuned separately. This modularity should accelerate research by allowing component-level innovation rather than requiring end-to-end system redesign.

**Resolution of prior contradictions.** The paper helps reconcile a tension in the literature between methods that report strong language modeling perplexity on long sequences (StreamingLLM, Activation Beacon) and methods that report strong downstream task performance (LongLlama, YaRN) by showing that these metrics measure fundamentally different things. Low perplexity can be achieved by preserving local statistical patterns while discarding globally relevant information — a model that remembers the last few paragraphs perfectly but forgets chapter 1 will still predict chapter 10's next word reasonably well. Downstream tasks requiring retrieval from chapter 1 expose the information loss that perplexity masks. FocusLLM accepts slightly higher perplexity (10.59 at 100K on PG19 vs. Activation Beacon's 8.68, Table 1) in exchange for near-perfect retrieval fidelity (95.76% Passkey vs. 1.69%, Figure 1 and Table 8). This tradeoff characterization — that perplexity and retrieval accuracy can be inversely related in long-context models — is an important conceptual clarification for the field.

**The caveat on magnitude.** Despite these shifts, FocusLLM is best understood as a **strong architectural demonstration** rather than a paradigm shift. The core idea — query-conditioned extraction from segmented context — is not entirely novel; CEPE (Yen et al., 2024) and InfLLM (Xiao et al., 2024) both process chunks and retrieve relevant information, albeit through different mechanisms (cross-attention and attention-score-based memory retrieval, respectively). FocusLLM's specific contributions — the dynamic prompt as an evolving query, the candidate token as a learned extractor, the parallel decoding mechanism — are novel in combination, but the paper's single-model-family evaluation leaves open the question of whether these components are necessary versus sufficient for the observed gains. A true paradigm shift would require evidence that the decomposition works across model scales, architectures, and data distributions, which Section 6 discusses as an open question.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Characterizing the candidate token bottleneck with controlled capacity experiments.** The paper provides suggestive evidence that the single-candidate-token-per-chunk design introduces a representational bottleneck (Retrieval.KV: 12.40%, Retrieval.Number: 83.56% vs. Passkey: 95.76%, Table 2), but does not systematically characterize its information capacity. A strong follow-up would design a synthetic benchmark where the amount of information per chunk is precisely controlled: embed `N` independent facts in a single chunk (where `N` varies from 1 to, say, 20), each fact being a unique key-value pair, and measure retrieval accuracy for each fact as `N` increases. This would produce a saturation curve showing how many independent pieces of information a single candidate token can faithfully encode. The experiment should also vary the candidate token dimensionality (e.g., by changing `d_dec` through projection downscaling) to determine whether the bottleneck is capacity-limited (more dimensions would help) or architecture-limited (the single-vector design itself is the constraint regardless of dimension). A negative result — showing that multiple candidate tokens per chunk dramatically improve capacity — would directly motivate architectural extensions to FocusLLM. A positive result — showing saturation at, say, 5-7 facts regardless of dimensionality — would establish a fundamental limit that downstream applications must respect.

**Quantifying and mitigating exposure bias in the dynamic prompt.** The paper's training procedure uses teacher forcing: during training, the dynamic prompt always contains ground-truth local context tokens. At inference time, the dynamic prompt contains the model's own generated tokens, which may be suboptimal or error-containing. This creates a train-inference distribution shift that Section 6 identifies as an unexamined limitation. A concrete follow-up experiment would: (1) Measure the performance gap between oracle dynamic prompts (ground-truth context) and model-generated dynamic prompts on a task where generation length can be varied, such as long-form summarization. (2) Implement scheduled sampling (Bengio et al., 2015) during FocusLLM training — occasionally feeding the model's own predictions as the dynamic prompt rather than ground-truth tokens — and measure whether this closes the gap. (3) Test whether the gap grows with generation length, which would indicate compounding error. The paper's current results are on relatively short-answer tasks (∞-Bench outputs average 4–23 tokens, Table 7), where exposure bias may be negligible. Tasks requiring 500+ token generations from long contexts would stress-test this limitation. A negative result — showing that the gap is large and scheduled sampling doesn't help — would indicate a fundamental limitation of the dynamic prompt design for long-form generation. A positive result would provide a training recipe that extends FocusLLM to generation-heavy applications.

**Scaling FocusLLM to larger base models and longer native context windows.** The paper's "any decoder-only LLM" claim is untested beyond LLaMA-2-7B-Chat with 4K native context. Two specific experiments would begin to validate or refine this claim. First, apply FocusLLM to LLaMA-2-13B and LLaMA-2-70B (or their instruction-tuned variants) and measure whether the relative performance gain over the base model scales with model size. The hypothesis: larger models have more attention head capacity, which could make the candidate token extraction more precise (benefiting from FocusLLM more) or could make the base model better at ignoring irrelevant candidate tokens (reducing FocusLLM's relative advantage since the base model with truncation becomes more competitive). Second, apply FocusLLM to a model with 32K native context (LLaMA-2-32K or a long-context Mistral variant) and measure performance as a function of chunk size. The key question: when chunks are 32K tokens instead of 4K, does the single-candidate-token bottleneck become prohibitive? If so, at what chunk size does retrieval fidelity degrade below, say, 90% on Passkey? This experiment would determine whether FocusLLM's design couples to the native context length (it works best when chunks are small) or whether it is truly length-agnostic (larger chunks with correspondingly richer candidate tokens work equally well). The paper's Section 5.4 hints at this direction but only tests chunk sizes up to 2K at 8K total length — a far cry from 32K chunks.

**Combining FocusLLM with retrieval-augmented generation for structured extraction.** The Retrieval.KV result (12.40%, Table 2) reveals that FocusLLM's vector-based candidate tokens struggle with structured, multi-field information extraction from long documents. A natural hybrid would combine FocusLLM's dynamic condensing (which excels at identifying *which* chunks are relevant) with a structured extraction module that operates over the identified chunks. The architecture: after FocusLLM's attention mechanism identifies high-weight candidate tokens (e.g., the top-3 attended chunks), a separate extraction head (perhaps an encoder-decoder module or a constrained decoding system) processes those chunks' raw text to extract structured key-value pairs. This separates the *localization* problem (FocusLLM's strength) from the *extraction* problem (where the candidate token bottleneck fails). The experiment would measure whether such a hybrid achieves substantially higher KV Retrieval accuracy than either pure FocusLLM (12.40%) or a pure RAG baseline (which would retrieve chunks via embedding similarity and then extract). A strong positive result — say, achieving >50% KV Retrieval — would point toward a general architecture where FocusLLM serves as a learned router that directs structured extraction modules to the right chunks, rather than attempting to encode structured data into candidate token vectors.

**Testing FocusLLM on genuinely compositional long-range reasoning.** The paper's strongest results are on retrieval (Passkey, Number String) and relatively simple comprehension (NarrativeQA, En.MC). Untested is whether FocusLLM can support **multi-hop reasoning over long contexts** where the answer depends on integrating information from chunks that are far apart and where no single chunk contains the answer. For example: "In the document, Company A was founded in the year when Event B occurred, and Company A's CEO previously worked at Company C. What year was Company C founded?" This requires (1) finding Company A's founding year (chunk 14), (2) finding what Event B occurred in that year (chunk 72), (3) finding Company A's CEO (chunk 14 or elsewhere), (4) finding where that CEO worked previously (chunk 41), (5) finding Company C's founding year (chunk 103). Each hop requires a separate query to a different chunk, but FocusLLM's single dynamic prompt encodes all information needs simultaneously — it cannot express "first find X, then based on X find Y." A concrete experiment would construct synthetic multi-hop datasets with controlled hop distances and measure whether FocusLLM's accuracy degrades with hop count more severely than a full-attention baseline. This would test whether the architecture's decomposition of context into independently queried chunks is compatible with chain-of-thought reasoning, or whether the dynamic prompt's unitary nature creates a bottleneck for sequential information gathering. A negative result — showing that FocusLLM fails on 3+ hop reasoning even when individual hops are retrievable — would motivate extending the architecture to support iterative, multi-step querying of chunks (perhaps by allowing the dynamic prompt to be updated mid-generation based on intermediate retrieval results).

**Evaluating FocusLLM's robustness to adversarial chunk content.** The attention heatmaps (Figures 7, 8) show that FocusLLM can ignore irrelevant chunks and attend to relevant ones. But what happens when chunks contain **misleading** information — text that is superficially relevant to the dynamic prompt but factually wrong, or text structured to exploit the candidate token's attention patterns? A concrete stress test: on a Passkey-style task, insert multiple "decoy" chunks that contain numbers formatted identically to the true passkey ("The passkey is 48291") but with different wrong numbers, while the true passkey is in one chunk. Measure whether FocusLLM's attention consistently identifies the true passkey chunk as context length and decoy count increase. Also test whether adversarial text — for example, a chunk that begins with tokens highly similar to the dynamic prompt to hijack attention scores — can cause FocusLLM to extract wrong information. This experiment would characterize the security and reliability boundaries of query-conditioned extraction. It is particularly important for deployment scenarios where long documents may contain conflicting information (legal contracts, medical records with contradictory findings) and the model must identify which information is authoritative rather than simply which is superficially similar to the query. A negative result — showing that FocusLLM is easily distracted by decoys — would indicate that the candidate token's soft attention over chunk tokens is insufficiently precise for high-stakes applications.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Long-document question answering with hard retrieval requirements.** The most direct application is any system that must answer specific questions by locating precise information in very long documents (100K–400K tokens) where the answer cannot be guessed from summary-level understanding. Examples: legal document review ("What is the penalty clause amount in section 12.3?" from a 300-page contract), financial document analysis ("What was the Q3 2023 revenue for the Asia-Pacific segment?" from a 200-page annual report), or scientific literature review ("What p-value was reported for the primary endpoint?" from a 100-page clinical trial report). FocusLLM's 99% Passkey Retrieval accuracy at 400K tokens (Figure 1) and 95.76% at ∞-Bench's 122.4K-token average (Table 2) makes it suitable for these applications where retrieval failure is unacceptable. The practical deployment would involve: chunk the long document once (preprocessing), then for each user query, append the query as the dynamic prompt and use FocusLLM to generate the answer, with the dynamic prompt evolving as the model produces the answer tokens. The key advantage over retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) is that FocusLLM's learned extraction is query-conditioned — it can identify relevant information even when lexical overlap between query and target is low (e.g., "penalty clause" vs. "liquidated damages provision"), which embedding-based retrieval often misses. The disadvantage is the per-token inference cost of re-processing all chunks, which for a single-query deployment is acceptable (the preprocessing is one-time) but for high-throughput QA over many queries would require batching or caching strategies not explored in the paper.

**On-device or edge deployment of small models with long-context access.** The paper's training efficiency claims — 2B additional trainable parameters, 0.5B tokens of training data, 20 hours on 8×A100 GPUs (Appendix C) — imply that FocusLLM can be added to a small base model without the prohibitive cost of long-context fine-tuning. For a 1.3B-parameter model deployed on a laptop or mobile device, FocusLLM would add approximately 400M–500M trainable parameters (scaling the projection matrices with hidden dimension) and could be trained with a fraction of the 0.5B tokens used for the 7B version. This opens the possibility of a small on-device model that can precisely answer questions about long documents (user manuals, terms of service, lengthy email threads) without cloud connectivity. The practical benefit: privacy-sensitive scenarios (medical records, legal documents) where users cannot upload full documents to a cloud LLM, but an on-device model with FocusLLM could process them locally. The paper's memory footprint analysis (Figure 3) shows FocusLLM with sequential processing at 32K uses approximately 42GB — this would need to be reduced for on-device deployment, but the architecture's chunk-wise processing is naturally compatible with memory-efficient inference (process one chunk at a time, keep only candidate tokens in memory, discard chunk KV caches). Quantization (mentioned in Section 8 as future work) and smaller base models would be prerequisites for this use case.

**Self-improvement data generation from long-context corpora.** The paper's ability to train on short sequences while generalizing to long ones suggests an application the authors hint at but don't explore: using FocusLLM to generate high-quality training data from long documents for fine-tuning other models. The workflow: given a corpus of long documents (e.g., textbooks, manuals, code repositories), use FocusLLM with targeted dynamic prompts to extract specific types of information (definitions, examples, code snippets with explanations, key facts with citations) and format them as instruction-tuning examples. Because FocusLLM preserves full access to the source text, the extracted information can be verified against the original chunks (a candidate token with high attention weight points to the source chunk, which can be checked). This addresses a bottleneck in instruction-tuning data creation: manually extracting high-quality, grounded examples from long documents is expensive, and naive chunking + extraction loses cross-chunk context. FocusLLM's dynamic prompt mechanism allows programmatic querying: "extract all definitions from this textbook chapter," "find all code examples that demonstrate recursion," "list all historical dates mentioned with their associated events." The 0.5B-token training budget means organizations with domain-specific long documents could train their own FocusLLM-augmented model relatively cheaply, then use it to bootstrap larger instruction-tuning datasets for foundation model fine-tuning.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper does not articulate an explicit, formalized tradeoff against named alternatives that maps cleanly to a decision rule (e.g., "if metric X exceeds threshold Y, choose FocusLLM over method Z"). The comparisons in Section 5 are empirical (FocusLLM achieves higher average scores on LongBench and ∞-Bench than all baselines) rather than conditional (FocusLLM outperforms baseline A under conditions B and C, but not under condition D). The paper's primary positioning is architectural — FocusLLM avoids the information loss that compression methods exhibit, and achieves this at lower training cost than full-attention fine-tuning — without quantifying the specific deployment conditions under which a practitioner should prefer it over, say, simply using a model with a longer native context window or a RAG pipeline.

A conditional recommendation would require experiments the paper does not perform: a cost-benefit analysis comparing FocusLLM against alternatives at matched total FLOPs (training + inference), an evaluation on tasks where the answer is deliberately placed at varying distances from the query to measure the value of long-range access, and a characterization of the "crossover point" where FocusLLM's per-token overhead is justified by retrieval accuracy gains. Without these, any "prefer A when X" matrix would be extrapolating beyond the paper's evidence.
