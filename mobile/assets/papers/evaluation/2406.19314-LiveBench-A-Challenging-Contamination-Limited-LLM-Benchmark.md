# LiveBench: A Challenging, Contamination-Limited LLM Benchmark

**ArXiv:** [2406.19314](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.19314)

## 🎯 Pitch

This paper introduces **LiveBench**, a new benchmark for evaluating large language models designed to resist both test set contamination and the biases inherent in LLM judging and human crowdsourcing.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **LiveBench**, a new benchmark for evaluating large language models designed to resist both test set contamination and the biases inherent in LLM judging and human crowdsourcing. LiveBench operates through two core mechanisms: **frequently-updated questions from recent information sources** (e.g., math competition problems from the past 11 months, arXiv abstracts, recent Kaggle datasets, and IMDb movie synopses) and **automatic scoring against objective ground-truth values** (e.g., regex-based grading, exact-match comparison, and programmatic verification of constraints) without employing LLM judges. Evaluating over 40 models—including top proprietary systems (o1-preview, claude-3-5-sonnet, gemini-1.5-pro) and open-source models ranging from 0.5B to 405B parameters—LiveBench achieves a top accuracy below 70% across its six categories, with o1-preview-2024-09-12 earning the highest overall score (~65%) and outperforming all other models by a margin of roughly 6%, establishing that current frontier models still face substantial headroom on contamination-limited, objectively-scored tasks even as benchmark performance approaches saturation on older, static evaluations.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Existing LLM Benchmarks Are Collapsing Under Their Own Weight

The fundamental problem this paper addresses is that **the dominant paradigm for evaluating LLMs is breaking down**, and it is breaking down for two distinct but converging reasons: test set contamination and evaluation bias. The paper tackles both simultaneously, recognizing that each alone represents a serious threat to meaningful model comparison, and that their interaction—contaminated models being scored by biased judges—can produce results that are actively misleading about which models are actually better.

**Test set contamination** is the more straightforward of the two problems, though no less pernicious for it. Benchmarks are published on the internet. Most modern LLMs are trained on large swaths of the internet. If an LLM encounters a benchmark's questions and answers during training, its performance on that benchmark will be artificially inflated—it measures memorization, not generalization. The paper cites multiple pieces of evidence that this is not a hypothetical concern:

- Roberts et al. (2024) and Jain et al. (2024) showed that LLMs' performance on Codeforces problems **plummets after the training cutoff date** of the LLM, and that for problems before the cutoff, performance is "highly correlated with the number of times the problem appears on GitHub" (Section 1). This is direct behavioral evidence: the model isn't reasoning through new problems; it has seen them before.

- Zhang et al. (2024) created a hand-crafted variant of the well-known GSM8K math dataset (Cobbe et al., 2021) and found evidence that "several models have overfit to this benchmark" (Section 1). In other words, models that looked impressive on the original GSM8K degraded significantly on the variant, exposing the underlying memorization.

- OpenAI (2023) themselves noted concerns about Big-Bench tasks leaking into GPT-4's training data despite the use of canary strings—standardized exclusion markers meant to flag benchmark data for filtering—suggesting that even best-practice contamination prevention has failed at scale.

The severity of this problem is amplified by the fact that **the same benchmarks are used repeatedly for years**. Static benchmarks like MMLU, GSM8K, and HumanEval are published once and then used to evaluate model after model. Each subsequent model trained on a larger, more recent internet scrape has a higher probability of having seen the test data. The paper frames this as an intrinsic obsolescence problem: benchmarks "can quickly render benchmarks obsolete" (Section 1) not because the tasks become easier in an absolute sense, but because the performance signal becomes untrustworthy.

**Evaluation bias** is the second, more subtle problem. In response to contamination concerns, many recent benchmarks have shifted toward generating *new* questions at evaluation time—often by having humans or LLMs create prompts and judge responses on the fly. The paper highlights two prominent examples: ChatBot Arena (Chiang et al., 2024), which uses human preference judgments to rank models, and benchmarks like Arena-Hard (Li et al., 2024) and AlpacaEval (Li et al., ; Dubois et al., 2023; 2024), which use LLMs (typically GPT-4) as judges. While these approaches sidestep contamination by creating novel evaluation data, they introduce systematic distortions of their own.

The paper identifies several specific failure modes of LLM judging (Section 1, elaborated in Section 4):

- **Self-preference bias**: "LLMs often favor their own answers over other LLMs" (Section 1). Li et al. (2024) documented that GPT-4-based judges show statistically significant preference for GPT-4 outputs compared to outputs from other model families.

- **Verbosity bias**: LLM judges "favor more verbose answers" (Section 1; Dubois et al., 2024), which means a model that writes longer, more elaborate responses can game the scoring without actually being more correct or more helpful.

- **Stylistic preference bias**: LLM judges may prefer outputs that match a particular writing style—formality, structure, tone—rather than evaluating correctness or helpfulness. This creates a confound where models are effectively scored on how well their instruction-tuning style matches the judge model's preferences.

- **Judge-version sensitivity**: Li et al. (2024) note that "GPT-4 judges have a noticeable difference in terms of variance and favorability of other models compared to Claude judges" (Section 4). Switching the judge model changes the rankings, indicating that the evaluations reflect judge-model-specific biases rather than objective quality.

- **Incompetence on hard questions**: The paper includes a preliminary experiment (Section A.2) examining whether LLMs that struggle to *answer* hard math and reasoning questions can accurately *judge* the correctness of answers to those questions. Using GPT-4-Turbo as a judge on AMC12, AIME, SMC, and Zebra Puzzle questions, the error rates were alarmingly high—ranging from 0.103 to 0.460 (Table 8)—suggesting that LLM judges are unreliable precisely on the challenging questions where accurate evaluation matters most.

Human judging, as employed by ChatBot Arena (Chiang et al., 2024; Zheng et al., 2024), has its own limitations. The paper points out three categories of issues (Section 4):

- **Labor intensity**: Complex math, coding, or long-context reasoning problems require significant time and expertise to evaluate correctly—exactly the types of problems that distinguish top models. Relying on human evaluation for these tasks is expensive and slow.

- **Annotation error**: "Whenever humans are involved in annotation (of which judging is a sub-case), design choices or factors can cause high error rates" (Lease, 2011, cited in Section 4). Even well-designed human annotation setups exhibit "high variability from human to human" leading to "unpredictable outcomes" (Rashkin et al., 2023, cited in Section 4).

- **Style biases in preference judgments**: Human evaluators may prefer certain output formats, tones, or writing styles that are unrelated to factual correctness or task completion. The paper hypothesizes that "the strong performance of some models such as the gemini-1.5 models on ChatBot Arena compared to LiveBench may be due to having an output style that is preferred by humans" (Section 3.3), a claim supported by their observation that these models do not show corresponding strength on objectively-scored LiveBench tasks.

The paper also critiques the human *question generation* process used in some benchmarks (Section 1): "Human participants might not ask diverse questions, may favor certain topics that do not probe a model's general capabilities, and may construct their prompts poorly" (Zheng et al., 2024). This means that even uncontaminated benchmarks can produce misleading results if the question distribution is narrow or systematically biased.

### Why This Problem Matters: The Stakes of Broken Benchmarks

The paper situates the benchmark reliability problem within a larger context where **LLM evaluation drives real-world decisions**. Benchmarks influence model selection for deployment, guide research priorities, and shape public perception of AI progress. If benchmarks are unreliable due to contamination or judging bias, the consequences cascade through the entire ecosystem:

- **Model developers optimize for the wrong signal.** If a benchmark rewards memorization of training data rather than generalization, developers are incentivized to train on larger, less carefully filtered datasets—exacerbating contamination for the next generation of models. If a benchmark rewards verbose, stylistically GPT-4-like outputs, developers optimize their instruction-tuning to match that style rather than improve factual accuracy or reasoning.

- **Users and organizations select suboptimal models.** A model with inflated benchmark scores due to contamination may be chosen for deployment over a genuinely better model with cleaner training data. The paper's comparison of LiveBench to other benchmarks (Section 3.3, Figures 4 and 6) demonstrates this concretely: models that look strong on Arena-Hard or ChatBot Arena are not necessarily strong on LiveBench's objective tasks, and vice versa. A user selecting solely on Arena-Hard scores would miss, for example, that certain models underperform on hard reasoning tasks that humans can objectively grade.

- **The research community cannot reliably measure progress.** When benchmark scores become untrustworthy, the field loses its ability to determine whether new methods, architectures, or training recipes actually improve capabilities. The paper's observation that some models appear disproportionately strong on certain benchmarks (Figure 6, outliers from the best-fit line) suggests that cross-benchmark inconsistencies are common enough to make single-benchmark comparisons actively misleading.

- **The benchmark obsolescence cycle accelerates.** As more models train on larger internet corpora, the fraction of available benchmarks that remain contamination-free shrinks. The paper argues this creates a ratchet effect: each new benchmark eventually gets scraped and memorized, requiring yet another benchmark to be created. Without a structural solution (regularly updated questions, objectively gradable tasks), the field is trapped in an expensive, unsustainable cycle of benchmark creation and obsolescence. The paper explicitly frames LiveBench as breaking this cycle through its monthly update cadence.

### Prior Approaches and Where They Fall Short

The paper provides a detailed taxonomy of existing benchmark approaches (Section 4), identifying the specific failure modes of each.

**Static benchmarks (e.g., HuggingFace Open LLM Leaderboard, MMLU-Pro, Big-Bench Hard).** These are the traditional benchmarks: a fixed set of questions published once and then used indefinitely. The HuggingFace Open LLM Leaderboard (Gao et al., 2021; Beeching et al., 2023) aggregates several static datasets including Big-Bench Hard (Suzgun et al., 2023) and MMLU-Pro (Wang et al., 2024). The paper acknowledges that this has been "incredibly useful in tracking the performance of LLMs" (Section 4), but its "static nature leaves it prone to test set contamination by models." The problem is inherent to the design: you cannot publish a fixed set of questions on the internet and then expect models trained on the internet not to have seen them.

**LLM-judged benchmarks (AlpacaEval, MT-Bench, Arena-Hard).** These benchmarks use an LLM—typically GPT-4—to score model outputs on a fixed set of open-ended prompts. The paper highlights both strengths and weaknesses. On the positive side, LLM judging is "fast, relatively cheap, and has the flexibility of being able to evaluate open-ended questions, instruction-following questions, and chatbots" (Section 4). These are genuine advantages: for tasks like "write a travel blog" where no objective ground truth exists, LLM judging is one of the only scalable evaluation options.

However, the paper argues the weaknesses are severe enough to undermine reliability. Beyond the biases already discussed (self-preference, verbosity, style), there is a deeper structural problem: **the judge model's capabilities impose a ceiling on what can be evaluated reliably**. If an LLM judge cannot solve a hard math problem, it cannot reliably evaluate whether another model's solution is correct—it might approve a confidently-written wrong answer or reject a correct answer with an unconventional format. The paper's experiment in Section A.2 confirms this suspicion for GPT-4-Turbo on competition math and logic puzzles, where judging error rates reached 42-46% on some tasks (Table 8). This creates a paradoxical situation: LLM judges are most reliable on easy questions (where evaluation matters least, since most models perform well) and least reliable on hard questions (where evaluation matters most for distinguishing top models).

The paper also notes specific implementation-level failures: question 2 in Arena-Hard asks for a C++ program, yet "GPT-4 incorrectly judges gpt-4-0314's solution as incorrect" (Li et al., 2024, cited in Section 4). Such errors are not just theoretical concerns—they directly affect model rankings in a widely-used benchmark.

**Human-judged benchmarks (ChatBot Arena).** ChatBot Arena represents a fundamentally different approach: users submit prompts, receive outputs from two randomly selected anonymous models, and vote on which output they prefer. These votes are aggregated into Elo scores. The paper gives credit where due: "human evaluation is great for capturing the preferences of a crowd" (Section 4). The scale is impressive—ChatBot Arena has collected hundreds of thousands of human judgments across diverse prompts.

The limitations identified are practical rather than philosophical. Human judging is labor-intensive, but more critically, it becomes unreliable on tasks that require expertise to evaluate. "Complex math, coding, or long-context reasoning problems" (Section 4) are difficult for crowd workers to grade accurately. A human evaluator might prefer a well-written but mathematically incorrect solution over a correct but poorly-explained one, creating an accuracy-style confound in the preference signal. The paper also notes that human preference judgments conflate multiple dimensions: correctness, writing quality, formatting, helpfulness, and tone are all bundled into a single binary vote. A model can "win" by being more polite even if it is less accurate.

**Other partially-contamination-resistant benchmarks.** The paper discusses several benchmarks that address pieces of the problem but not all of it:

- *LiveCodeBench* (Jain et al., 2024): Regularly updates coding questions from LeetCode and AtCoder and uses ground-truth judging via unit tests. The paper explicitly acknowledges LiveCodeBench as inspiration, but notes it is "limited to only coding tasks" (Section 4). LiveBench aims to provide the same contamination-resistance and objective scoring across a much broader range of capabilities.

- *Omni-MATH* (Gao & Liu, 2024): A comprehensive math benchmark covering many olympiad-level competitions. However, the paper notes it uses "LLM-as-a-judge grading" which "potentially contributes to a degree of contamination or bias in some of the benchmark's scores" (Section 4). This is a subtle point: even if the *questions* are novel, using an LLM judge that may have seen similar problems in its training data or that exhibits the biases discussed earlier reintroduces contamination and fairness concerns through the back door.

- *SEAL Benchmark* (Scale AI, 2024): Uses private questions with expert human scorers, which addresses contamination (private questions cannot be memorized) and judging bias (expert humans are more reliable than LLM judges or crowd workers). However, it is limited in scope: "the benchmark currently only contains the following categories: Math, Coding, Instruction Following, and Spanish" (Section 4), and the private nature means the community cannot inspect the questions or reproduce the results independently.

- *Srivastava et al. (2024)*: Modifies the original MATH dataset by changing numbers in the problem setup, finding "declines in model performance for some LLMs, including frontier ones." This demonstrates that even surface-level modifications to static benchmarks can expose memorization. However, the paper identifies two limitations: the modified data "still ends up being highly similar to the kind of data likely seen in the pretraining set" (Section 4), and "the hardness of the benchmark remains the same over time" (Section 4). This approach patches contamination for one generation of models but does not provide a sustainable long-term solution—models will eventually memorize the modified variants as well.

### How LiveBench Positions Itself

The paper positions LiveBench as satisfying three desiderata that no existing benchmark achieves simultaneously (Section 1):

1. **Frequently-updated questions from new information sources.** Questions are based on recently-released materials (math competitions from the past 11 months, recent arXiv papers, recent Kaggle datasets, recent Guardian articles, recent IMDb movie synopses). The monthly update cadence—replacing approximately 1/6 of questions each month so the benchmark is fully refreshed roughly every 6 months—is designed to stay ahead of training cutoff dates. Unlike static benchmarks that are published once and gradually contaminated, LiveBench's question set is a moving target.

2. **Automatic scoring according to objective ground-truth values, without LLM judges.** Every task in LiveBench has a verifiably correct answer: math problems have numeric or multiple-choice answers, code generation is scored by unit tests, instruction following is scored by regex-based constraint checking, word puzzles have unique solutions, data analysis tasks have exact-match ground truth (e.g., the correct column name or table format). The paper emphasizes that this eliminates the biases of LLM judging—self-preference, verbosity preference, stylistic bias—and avoids the scenario where "the judge model's capabilities impose a ceiling on what can be evaluated reliably."

3. **A wide variety of challenging tasks across six categories.** LiveBench includes math, coding, reasoning, language comprehension, instruction following, and data analysis—a deliberate diversity meant to "probe a model's general capabilities" (Section 1) rather than focusing narrowly on any single skill. Within each category, tasks are designed to span a range of difficulty with the top models achieving below 70% accuracy, ensuring the benchmark remains discriminative for frontier models.

The paper frames this as a *structural solution* rather than a one-time fix. The monthly update mechanism is not just a feature—it is the core innovation that breaks the benchmark obsolescence cycle. By committing to regular updates that make the benchmark progressively harder (tasks are selected for update based on both age and current difficulty), LiveBench is designed to "distinguish between the capabilities of LLMs as they improve in the future" (Section 1).

The paper also explicitly positions LiveBench relative to the two major contamination types discussed in Appendix A.7:

- **Test set contamination** (exact questions and answers appearing in training data): Addressed by using new information sources and regular updates. The paper acknowledges the benchmark does not perfectly achieve zero contamination—"while nearly all questions are from June 2024 or more recent, there are some coding questions from November 2023, and the AMC questions have only undergone a low level of modification from their November 2023 version" (Appendix A.7). This transparency about residual contamination risk is a strength of the paper's positioning; it claims resistance, not immunity.

- **Task contamination** (training on the same distribution of tasks): This is harder to avoid, since many LLMs are deliberately fine-tuned on math competitions, coding problems, and similar task formats. The paper's response is pragmatic: task-level similarity is "fair game" (Appendix A.7) because it tests the kind of generalization that LLMs are supposed to achieve. To prevent excessive task contamination from overfitting to LiveBench's specific implementation, the paper does not publicly release question generation code, modifies generation scripts with each update to shift the question distribution, and withholds new questions for one month.

Finally, the paper positions LiveBench as a *community platform* rather than a static artifact. The code, questions, and model answers are all open-sourced. The leaderboard is publicly maintained. Community contributions are encouraged for expanding tasks and adding models. This open, evolving model stands in deliberate contrast to private benchmarks (like SEAL) that cannot be independently verified or to static benchmarks that cannot be updated once published.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

LiveBench is a **framework and a specific instantiation of a benchmark** that automatically generates and scores evaluation questions for large language models using recent, real-world information sources. It solves the dual problem of test set contamination (models memorizing static benchmark questions from their training data) and evaluation bias (LLM judges or human annotators introducing systematic preferences into scoring) by constructing a *moving target* of objectively-answerable questions—problems sourced from math competitions, arXiv papers, news articles, datasets, and puzzles that postdate model training cutoffs, scored without any LLM-as-a-judge component, and refreshed monthly so that the benchmark stays ahead of the data contamination curve.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The LiveBench system has four major components that operate cyclically:

**1. Question Sources** — external, time-stamped information repositories (competition math problem sets, arXiv, Kaggle, The Guardian, IMDb, LeetCode, AtCoder, New York Times puzzles) that provide raw material released after known LLM training cutoff dates.

**2. Question Generation Pipeline** — a set of task-specific scripts that transform raw source material into evaluation prompts. Depending on the task, this pipeline either (a) extracts and minimally modifies existing questions (e.g., rephrasing competition math problems, shuffling multiple-choice answer orders), (b) synthetically generates new questions from templates with randomized parameters (e.g., AMPS_Hard math, Web of Lies v2, Zebra Puzzles), or (c) programmatically *corrupts* clean source text to create correction tasks (e.g., injecting typos into arXiv abstracts, scrambling movie synopsis sentences). Every question produced has an associated objective ground-truth answer and a scoring function.

**3. Model Evaluation** — a standardized inference harness that runs each model under evaluation (40+ models spanning closed-source APIs and open-source checkpoints from 0.5B to 405B parameters) on every question using temperature 0, model-specific chat templates via an updated FastChat (Zheng et al., 2024) fork, and bfloat16 for open-source models. Models receive a single turn per question (no retries, no multi-turn dialog).

**4. Objective Scoring and Aggregation** — a set of per-task grading functions that compare model outputs to ground-truth answers without any LLM judge. Grading methods include exact string matching, regex-based answer extraction (with permissive, format-agnostic patterns), programmatic verification (unit tests for code, SymPy-based symbolic equivalence for math expressions, Pandas ingestion for table formats), edit-distance-based scoring, and automated constraint checking (word counts, keyword presence, JSON validity). Scores are aggregated hierarchically: individual question scores (0 to 1) → task averages → category averages (six categories, each averaged from their constituent tasks) → overall LiveBench score (average of the six category scores).

The cycle turns over monthly: questions are replaced at a rate of roughly 1/6 per update (full refresh every ~6 months), with tasks selected for update based on age and current difficulty. New questions are withheld from public release for one month to maintain a private subset, preventing exact test set contamination even for the most recently trained models.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the design principles and constraints that govern all tasks—what qualifies as an "objectively correct answer," how contamination resistance is engineered, and why certain common benchmark design choices were deliberately excluded.
- **Second**, the mathematics and competition math tasks (Competitions, Olympiad, AMPS_Hard), because they illustrate the two fundamental generation strategies: modifying existing expert-authored problems and synthetically generating new ones from parameterized templates.
- **Third**, the coding tasks (LCB Generation, Completion), which demonstrate how LiveBench integrates with and extends existing contamination-resistant benchmarks while adding novel task formulations.
- **Fourth**, the reasoning tasks (Web of Lies v2, Zebra Puzzles, Spatial Reasoning), which showcase synthetic generation with controllable difficulty parameters and logical constraint satisfaction.
- **Fifth**, the data analysis tasks (Column Type Annotation, Table Reformatting, Join-Column Prediction), which represent a category—practical data science operations—largely absent from other LLM benchmarks and introduce unique scoring challenges (format conversion fidelity, schema matching).
- **Sixth**, the instruction following tasks (Paraphrase, Simplify, Story Generation, Summarize), which adapt the IFEval paradigm to live information sources and demonstrate how objective scoring can be applied to ostensibly open-ended generation tasks through verifiable constraint checking.
- **Seventh**, the language comprehension tasks (Connections, Typos, Plot Unscrambling), which probe linguistic reasoning through puzzle-solving and text reconstruction rather than standard reading comprehension.
- **Eighth**, the scoring methodology in detail—regex design philosophy, permissiveness principles, and the quality control process that catches false negatives across model families.
- **Ninth**, the maintenance and update mechanism—how questions are selected for replacement, the private question buffer, version control, and community contribution path.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is a **benchmark design and empirical evaluation paper** whose core contribution is a framework for creating LLM benchmarks that are simultaneously resistant to test set contamination and immune to the biases of LLM judging and human crowdsourcing, instantiated in a specific 18-task, 6-category benchmark with a monthly update cadence.

---

#### Design Principles and Constraints

Every task in LiveBench is constructed to satisfy three hard constraints that collectively define the benchmark's identity.

**Constraint 1: Objective Ground-Truth Scoring.** A task is only included if there exists a verifiably correct answer that can be checked programmatically without invoking an LLM judge. This eliminates entire categories of tasks common in other benchmarks—open-ended creative writing, dialogue quality assessment, summarization quality judged by human preference—and restricts LiveBench to domains where correctness is well-defined: mathematical equality, code passing unit tests, exact string matching, logical constraint satisfaction, format compliance. The paper explicitly acknowledges this limitation: "while ground truth scoring is beneficial in many ways, it still cannot be used for certain use cases, such as 'write a travel guide to Hawaii' in which it is hard to define a ground truth" (Section 5). This is a deliberate tradeoff—narrower task scope in exchange for evaluation reliability.

**Constraint 2: Contamination Resistance Through Temporal Freshness and Transformation.** Questions are drawn from sources released after major LLM training cutoff dates. The paper describes two strategies:

- **Recent-source extraction**: Using materials from the past ~11 months (AMC12 2023 from November 2023, AIME 2024 from January/February 2024, USAMO 2024 from March 2024, IMO 2024 from July 2024, LeetCode problems from November 2023 onward, ArXiv abstracts, Guardian articles, IMDb synopses for movies released after January 1, 2024, Kaggle/Socrata datasets). This temporal freshness means that even models trained on internet data through late 2024 have unlikely seen these exact questions during pretraining, since the sources postdate the training data collection.

- **Transformative modification**: For questions drawn from existing sources, the paper applies modifications designed to break exact memorization while preserving the underlying reasoning challenge. For math competitions, this includes "updating the prose of the questions that do not affect the answer," "rearranging the order of the multiple choice answers when applicable," and "asking for a different output format than the widely-used source website" (Appendix A.3.1). For coding problems, the Completion task introduces a novel formulation (partial solution completion) not present in the source benchmark. For instruction following, the IFEval paradigm is applied to *new article text* rather than the original trivial prompts.

The paper is transparent about residual contamination risk: "while nearly all questions are from June 2024 or more recent, there are some coding questions from November 2023, and the AMC questions have only undergone a low level of modification from their November 2023 version. Therefore, a limited fraction of LiveBench is likely contaminated on all recent LLMs" (Appendix A.7). The claim is *resistance* to contamination, not absolute immunity.

**Constraint 3: Difficulty Calibration.** Tasks are designed "to have 40-100 questions which span a range of difficulty, from easy to very challenging, while loosely aiming for an overall 30-70% success rate on the top models for each task" (Section 2). The 30-70% target is important: it ensures the benchmark is discriminative at the frontier (too-easy tasks produce ceiling effects where all top models score >95% and cannot be distinguished; too-hard tasks produce floor effects). The paper reports that "no current model achieves higher than 70% accuracy" (Section 1), confirming the difficulty target is met for the top-performing o1-preview-2024-09-12 at ~65% overall.

#### Math Category: Competition Problems, Proof Completion, and Synthetic Generation

The math category consists of three tasks employing different generation strategies: modified human-authored competition problems (Competitions), proof rearrangement (Olympiad), and parameterized synthetic generation (AMPS_Hard).

**Competitions Task: Human-Authored Problems with Anti-Memorization Transformations.** This task draws from three competition sources: AMC12 (both A and B administrations from November 2023), the Senior Mathematical Challenge (SMC 2023 from October 2023), and AIME (both I and II from January/February 2024). These are "expert human-designed math problems that offer a wider variety in terms of problem type and solution technique" (Section 2.1 and Appendix A.3.1), covering arithmetic, algebra, combinatorics, geometry, number theory, and probability.

The transformation pipeline applies three modifications to each question:

1. **Prose rewording**: The problem statement is rewritten in ways that "do not affect the answer" (Section 2.1), changing surface-level language features while preserving the mathematical content. For example, replacing character names, reordering descriptive clauses, or paraphrasing the scenario setup.

2. **Answer order randomization**: For multiple-choice questions (AMC12, SMC), the order of the answer choices is shuffled relative to the widely-used source website (https://artofproblemsolving.com/). This means a model that has memorized "the answer to AMC12A 2023 problem X is choice E" will produce the wrong answer if it relies on position rather than content.

3. **Output format change**: The required answer format is intentionally different from common public solutions. For the example AMC12A problem shown in Appendix A.3.1, models are asked to "duplicate that letter five times in a single string" (e.g., "EEEEE" for answer E), a format unlikely to appear in training data where answers are typically single letters.

For AIME problems, where answers are integers from 000 to 999, the output instruction specifies: "The answer is an integer consisting of exactly 3 digits (including leading zeros), ranging from 000 to 999, inclusive" (Appendix A.3.1). The model must produce a three-digit string, with leading zeros required for answers below 100—a formatting constraint that tests both mathematical reasoning and precision in instruction following.

The answer extraction uses flexible regex patterns: "In our math category, models can respond with answers in either latex 'boxed{}' or 'fbox{}' environments. For multiple choice questions, we ask for the letter answer, but we accept either the letter or the raw answer" (Appendix A.4). This permissiveness is a deliberate design choice to avoid conflating instruction-following ability with mathematical reasoning ability.

**Olympiad Task: Proof Rearrangement as a Proxy for Proof Understanding.** The paper faces a fundamental evaluation challenge with proof-based competitions (USAMO 2024 from March 2024, IMO 2024 from July 2024): "These contests are primarily proof-based and non-trivial to evaluate in an automated way" (Appendix A.3.1). The two obvious approaches—LLM judging of natural language proofs and auto-formalization into Lean with a proof checker—both fail the objective-scoring constraint. LLM judging has "no formal guarantees on the correctness of the evaluation," and auto-formalization "does not have formal guarantees on the correctness of the auto-formalization—and thus that of the evaluation" (Appendix A.3.1).

The paper's solution is elegant: instead of evaluating whether a model can *generate* a proof (which requires subjective or unreliable automated judgment), evaluate whether a model can *reconstruct* a proof from its constituent parts. Specifically:

1. Take an existing correct proof (from the official competition solutions).
2. Mask out a subset of the mathematical formulae within the proof—"10%, 50% and 80% of the equations" to create three hardness variants (Appendix A.3.1).
3. Present the masked-out formulae in a **scrambled order** to the model, along with the proof text containing blanks where the formulae should go.
4. Ask the model to reinsert the formulae into the correct positions.

The paper argues this is a valid proxy for proof understanding: "if the LLM is strong enough to generate the correct proof for a question, then one would expect it to also solve the far easier task of completing a proof which has some missing formulae—especially if the formulae are already given to it in a scrambled order" (Appendix A.3.1). This design choice trades evaluation fidelity (direct proof generation assessment) for evaluation reliability (objective ranking-based scoring that cannot be gamed), a recurring theme in LiveBench's philosophy.

Scoring is based on edit distance between the ground-truth ranking order of the formulae and the model's predicted ranking order. The paper reports that performance "is greatly affected by question hardness going from as high as 96.8 for the easiest questions (10% masked out, GPT-4o) to as low as 36 for the hardest (80% masked out)" (Appendix A.3.1), confirming that the masking percentage effectively parameterizes difficulty.

This task generates 3 hardness variants per problem, yielding a total of 36 questions in the initial release (a combination of USAMO and IMO problems across hardness levels).

**AMPS_Hard Task: Parameterized Synthetic Generation with Symbolic Answer Verification.** This task is "inspired by the math question generation used to create the MATH and AMPS datasets" (Hendrycks et al., 2021; Section 2.1). The generation procedure operates as follows:

1. Randomly select a problem type from a set of mathematical operations: "taking the derivative or integral of a function, completing the square, or factoring a polynomial" (Appendix A.3.1).
2. Draw random primitives (function forms, coefficients, exponents, trigonometric expressions) from a distribution that is "larger (and therefore more challenging) than AMPS" (Section 2.1). The paper explicitly states it uses "a larger and more challenging distribution than AMPS across 10 of the hardest tasks within AMPS" (Section 2.1).
3. Generate the problem and its ground-truth solution using symbolic computation (SymPy, Meurer et al., 2017).
4. Present the problem to the model using the 'latex boxed answer' format from the MATH dataset (Hendrycks et al., 2021).

The scoring method is notable for going beyond exact string matching: "We judge the correctness of answers as in the EleutherAI Eval Harness (Gao et al., 2021) using Sympy (Meurer et al., 2017) where we check for semantic as well as numerical equivalence of mathematical expressions" (Appendix A.3.1). This means that a model answering "sin^2(x) + cos^2(x)" would be marked correct if the ground truth is "1," because SymPy can verify the trigonometric identity. This semantic equivalence checking is critical for math evaluation—mathematical expressions have many equivalent forms, and penalizing correct but differently-formatted answers would conflate formatting ability with mathematical ability.

The paper provides an example integration problem in Appendix A.3.1: "Find an indefinite integral... of the following function: 5 sec^2(5x + 1) - 8 sin(7 - 8x)." The ground truth is given as "-sin(7) sin(8x) - cos(7) cos(8x) + tan(5x + 1)," illustrating the non-trivial nature of the symbolic manipulation required and why simple string matching would be inadequate.

#### Coding Category: Generation and Completion from Competition Programming

The coding category adapts and extends LiveCodeBench (Jain et al., 2024), which the paper explicitly credits as a benchmark that "also regularly releases new questions and makes use of ground-truth judging" though "limited to only coding tasks" (Section 4).

**LCB Generation Task: Standard Code Generation with Temporal Filtering.** This task assesses "a model's ability to parse a competition coding question statement and write a correct answer" (Section 2.2). The paper selects 78 problems from LiveCodeBench's April 2024 release with a temporal filter: "selecting only problems released in or after November 2023" (Appendix A.3.2). Problems are drawn from LeetCode (Team, 2015) and AtCoder (Team, 2012), both standard competition programming platforms.

The evaluation protocol is pass@1: "a solution is considered correct if and only if it passes all public and private test cases" (Appendix A.3.2). The model gets exactly one generation per problem (temperature 0), meaning there is no sampling-based best-of-N advantage—the metric measures whether the model can produce a correct solution on its first attempt, which is a stricter test of coding capability than pass@k metrics common in code generation benchmarks.

The paper notes "minor prompting differences" from LiveCodeBench's original Code Generation task (Appendix A.3.2), adapting the prompt format to LiveBench's standardized style (zero-shot chain of thought, output format instructions, method for extracting the code from the response).

**Completion Task: Partial Solution Completion as a Novel Capability Probe.** This task "specifically focuses on the ability of models to complete a partially correct solution—assessing whether a model can parse the question, identify the function of the existing code, and determine how to complete it" (Section 2.2). This is a qualitatively different skill from generating a solution from scratch: it tests whether the model can understand someone else's code, identify the missing components, and produce code that integrates correctly with the existing partial solution.

The construction procedure is:

1. Source LeetCode problems (easy, medium, and hard) from LiveCodeBench's April 2024 release.
2. For each problem, obtain a correct solution from the repository https://github.com/kamyu104/LeetCode-Solutions—a public collection of LeetCode solutions in multiple programming languages.
3. Truncate the solution: "omitting the last 15% of each medium/hard solution and 30-70% of each easy solution and asking the LLM to complete the solution" (Appendix A.3.2).

The differential truncation percentages (15% for medium/hard, 30-70% for easy) reflect a deliberate calibration: easy problems are easier to solve from scratch, so more solution context is removed to maintain difficulty; medium and hard problems are more challenging, so more context is preserved to keep the task within the realm of what top models can accomplish. The 30-70% range for easy problems (rather than a fixed percentage) introduces variability in difficulty even within the easy tier.

The task yields 50 total questions in the initial release. Evaluation is pass@1 with unit tests (public and private), identical to the LCB Generation task. The paper notes in Table 1 that claude-3-5-sonnet substantially outperforms o1-preview on coding overall (roughly 67 vs. 51), and specifically that "o1-mini outperforms claude-3.5 in code generation, claude-3.5 has the edge in code completion" (Section 3.1), demonstrating that the completion task captures a distinct capability dimension that is not perfectly correlated with generation ability.

#### Reasoning Category: Synthetic Generation with Controlled Difficulty Parameters

The reasoning category consists of three tasks—Web of Lies v2, Zebra Puzzles, and Spatial Reasoning—all of which are synthetically generated (except spatial reasoning, which is hand-written) and have unique, objectively verifiable solutions derived from logical constraint satisfaction.

**Web of Lies v2: Escalating Difficulty Through Red Herrings and Multi-Target Queries.** The original Web of Lies task (from Big-Bench, bench authors, 2023; Big-Bench Hard, Suzgun et al., 2023) evaluates the truth value of a random Boolean function expressed in natural language. The canonical formulation is: a chain of statements of the form "X_i says X_{i-1} tells the truth/lies," where each person either always tells the truth or always lies, and the task is to determine whether a specific person tells the truth. The original task was largely solved by October 2022 ("LLMs achieved near 100% on this task"), and there are "concerns that Big-Bench tasks leaked into the training data of GPT-4" (Appendix A.3.3).

LiveBench's Web of Lies v2 introduces three modifications to create a dramatically harder version:

1. **Red herrings with dead-end deduction chains.** The task maintains a list of red herring names—people mentioned in statements who are *not* part of the core logical chain that determines the answer. For example, "'Fred says Kayla lies,' where Fred is in the true 'web of lies,' while Kayla may lead to a series of steps ending in a dead end" (Appendix A.3.3). The number of red herring sentences is drawn from a uniform distribution between 0 and 19, meaning problems can contain up to 19 extraneous statements that look relevant but do not affect the logical deduction. These red herrings exploit a known LLM weakness: models tend to treat all presented information as relevant and struggle to identify which statements are logically necessary versus which are distractors.

2. **Multi-target queries.** Instead of asking for the truth value of a single person, the task asks for "the truth values of three people, instead of just one person" (Appendix A.3.3). The answer format requires a list of three yes/no responses: "**yes, no, yes**" (from the example in Appendix A.3.3). This increases the computational burden—the model must track truth values for multiple individuals simultaneously—and makes partial credit impossible: getting two of three correct yields a score of 0.

3. **Location-based indirection.** The task introduces an additional deductive component: "we simply assign each name to a location and give sentences of the form 'Devika is at the museum. The person at the museum says the person at the ice skating rink lies'" (Appendix A.3.3). This adds a layer of reference resolution: the model must map between person names and location names, then use the location-anchored statements to build the logical chain. The example question in Appendix A.3.3 shows the complexity: multiple people are assigned to locations (Tala at the movie theater, Ayaan at the aquarium, Ryan at the botanical garden, Zara at the museum, Jake at the art gallery, Beatriz at the park, Nadia at the campground, Grace at the restaurant, Nia at the theater, Kehinde at the train station, Mateo at the amusement park), and statements are expressed in terms of locations rather than names ("The person at the restaurant says the person at the aquarium lies"). The model must resolve location references to names, build the implication graph, propagate truth values, and filter out red herrings.

The paper notes that these modifications "make the task significantly harder for leading LLMs, even without shuffling the sentences into a random order" (Appendix A.3.3). The decision not to randomize sentence order (unlike the original Big-Bench Web of Lies, which presented sentences in random order as a difficulty mechanism) suggests that the red herring and indirection mechanisms alone provide sufficient difficulty for frontier models.

**Zebra Puzzles: Procedural Generation from Constraint Templates.** Zebra puzzles (also known as Einstein's riddles) are a class of logic puzzles where a set of entities (people, houses, etc.) have multiple attributes (nationality, pet, drink, etc.), and the solver must deduce the complete assignment from a set of relational constraints ("the Norwegian lives in the first house," "the person who drinks tea lives next to the person who owns a cat," etc.).

LiveBench builds on an existing open-source repository for procedural generation of Zebra puzzles (quint t, 2023), which "allows for randomizing the number of people, the number of attributes, and the set of constraint statements provided" (Appendix A.3.3). The generation parameters are:

- **Number of people**: Either 3 or 4, "with 50% probability" (Appendix A.3.3). The paper notes that "larger puzzles proved exceedingly difficult for even the top performing LLMs" in preliminary testing, motivating the smaller puzzle sizes.
- **Number of attributes**: Either 3 or 4, "with 50% probability" (Appendix A.3.3).
- **Attribute categories**: Drawn from a pool of 10 possible categories (Nationality, Food, Transport, Sport, etc.), each containing "between 15 and 40 possible values" (Appendix A.3.3).
- **Constraint difficulty levels**: Drawn from the integer interval [10, 20] with uniform probability. The levels correspond to increasing constraint complexity: "level 1 could include a statement such as 'The person who likes garlic is on the left of the person who plays badminton' and a level 10 statement could be 'The person that watches zombie movies likes apples or the person that watches zombie movies likes drawing, but not both'" (Appendix A.3.3). Higher levels (12-20) progressively remove easier constraint types, narrowing the pool of available constraints to only the most complex relational and logical operators.

The repository includes a solver that verifies each generated puzzle has a unique solution—a critical quality control step, because an underconstrained puzzle with multiple valid solutions would have ambiguous ground truth and invalidate the objective scoring.

The paper made modifications to reduce ambiguity in the constraint statements: "changing 'X is to the left of Y' to 'X is to the immediate left of Y'" (Appendix A.3.3). This clarification prevents edge cases where "to the left of" (non-immediate) and "immediately to the left of" could both be valid interpretations, which would confuse both models and human solvers.

The answer format specifies: "Return your answer as a single word, in the following format: ***X***, where X is the answer" (Section 2.3 example). The scoring extracts the word between the triple-asterisk delimiters and checks for exact match against the ground-truth attribute value.

**Spatial Reasoning: Hand-Written Questions Testing Geometric and Spatial Deduction.** Unlike the other reasoning tasks, spatial reasoning questions are "handwritten" (Table 13, Appendix A.3.3)—specifically, "50 handwritten questions tests a model's ability to make deductions about intersections and orientations of common 2D and 3D shapes" (Appendix A.3.3). The paper provides two examples:

- A 3D geometry question: "Suppose I have three spheres of radius 3 resting on a plane. Each sphere is tangent to the other two spheres. If I consider a new shape whose vertices are equal to the set of tangent points of the pairs of spheres, what is the new shape?" The answer is "triangle" because three mutually tangent spheres produce three tangent points forming an equilateral triangle.

- A planar geometry/cutting question: "Suppose I have a regular heptagon, and I can make four straight cuts. Each cut cannot pass through any of the vertices of the heptagon. Also, exactly two of the cuts must be parallel. What is the maximum number of resulting pieces?" The answer is 10 (a combinatorial geometry problem about planar subdivisions with parallelism constraints).

These questions are distinct from the other reasoning tasks in that they test visuospatial reasoning—the ability to mentally manipulate geometric objects—rather than purely logical deduction. The answer format is either a shape name or an integer, enclosed in double-asterisk delimiters ("**triangle**" or "**10**"). Scoring uses the same regex-based extraction and exact matching as other tasks.

The hand-written nature means this task cannot be automatically updated via a script (Table 14: "No - create new questions by hand"), making it the least scalable of LiveBench's tasks for the monthly update cycle. The paper implicitly acknowledges this limitation by listing it as the only task requiring manual creation, though it does not explicitly discuss how hand-written question creation will be sustained at scale.

#### Data Analysis Category: Practical Data Science Operations with Recent Datasets

The data analysis category is one of LiveBench's most distinctive contributions: "LiveBench includes three practical tasks in which the LLM assists in data analysis or data science: column type annotation, table join prediction, and table reformatting" (Section 2.4). These tasks evaluate capabilities that are highly relevant to real-world LLM applications (data engineering, schema matching, ETL pipelines) but are largely absent from other major benchmarks.

All three tasks "make use of a recent dataset from Kaggle or Socrata" (Section 2.4), ensuring temporal freshness. The paper also notes practical constraints: "Owing to the limited output context lengths of the current generation of LLMs and the comparatively high per-token costs of generating responses, we upper bound the size of our tables with respect to cell length, column count and row count" (Appendix A.3.4). This means tables are truncated or sampled to fit within model context windows while preserving sufficient information for the task.

**Column Type Annotation (CTA) Task: Predicting Column Semantics from Samples.** This task evaluates whether an LLM can infer the semantic type of a data column from a small sample of its values. Formally:

> $$\forall C, \exists l_j \mid (C_i, l_j) \in \text{CTA}$$
> 
> (Equation 1 from Appendix A.3.4)

where `$C_i$` is a column (a mapping from row indices to strings), `$l_j$` is a label from a label set `$L$`, and CTA is the relation that maps columns to their correct type labels.

**What it computes:** The expression defines the column type annotation relation as a mapping from every column to exactly one label in the label set, meaning each column has a unique, ground-truth type annotation.

**Why this form:** The relational formulation cleanly separates the data (columns as functions from indices to values) from the annotation (labels drawn from a predefined vocabulary), making it clear that the task is a classification problem over a fixed label set rather than an open-ended generation problem.

The implementation procedure for each question is (Appendix A.3.4):

1. "Retrieve a random A from our available pool of recent tables" — a table is randomly sampled from the collection of recent Kaggle/Socrata datasets.
2. "Randomly and uniformly sample C from A" — a single column is selected at random from the table's columns.
3. "Use the actual column name of A as our CTA ground-truth L" — the ground-truth label is the column's original name (e.g., "price", "country", "release_date").
4. "Retrieve σ1 ... σ5 column samples from C, with replacement, providing them as context for the LLM" — five sample values from the column are extracted and shown to the model, along with the names of all columns in the table (as the label set to choose from).

The model must select the correct column name from the set of all column names in the table, given only five sample values. This is a challenging zero-shot semantic understanding task: for example, distinguishing a "city" column from an "address" column based on five samples, where both might contain location-like strings.

The scoring metric is "Accuracy @ 1 over all instances, accepting only case-insensitive exact string matches as correct answers" (Appendix A.3.4). No partial credit is given; the model either selects the exact correct column name or receives a score of 0.

**Table Reformatting Task: Format Conversion with Structural Fidelity.** This task evaluates whether an LLM can convert tabular data between different plaintext-readable formats while preserving the data contents exactly. The task is defined as:

"Given a table A rendered according to a plaintext-readable and valid schema `$s_a$` for storing tabular information as, we instruct the LLM to output the same table with the contents unchanged but the schema modified to a distinct plaintext-readable valid schema `$s_b$`" (Appendix A.3.4).

The allowed formats (for both input and output) are: "JSON", "JSONL", "Markdown", "CSV", "TSV", "HTML" (Appendix A.3.4)—six common data serialization formats spanning structured (JSON), semi-structured (HTML), and delimited (CSV, TSV) representations. The conversion is performed using the Pandas library: "We use the popular library Pandas to perform all of our conversions to and from text strings" (Appendix A.3.4).

The scoring procedure is:

1. Ingest the LLM's response "directly into Pandas" (Appendix A.3.4).
2. Compare the resulting dataframe to the ground-truth dataframe on three dimensions: column count, row count, and "exact match on row contents for each instance" (Appendix A.3.4).
3. An instance is scored 1 only if it "passes all tests" (all three comparisons match); otherwise 0.

The paper notes a practical challenge: "tabular conversion from JSON to Pandas is not standardized, we accept several variations" (Appendix A.3.4). This acknowledges that JSON has multiple valid representations for tabular data (list-of-records, record-of-lists, etc.), and a rigid parser would unfairly penalize models that choose a valid but non-default representation. The scoring function is made permissive enough to accept equivalent representations while still requiring exact data fidelity.

**Join-Column Prediction (TableJoin) Task: Schema Matching for Data Integration.** This task evaluates whether an LLM can identify which columns from two tables should be used as join keys—a fundamental operation in data integration and database querying.

The formal setup: "Given two tables A and B, with columns a1, ... and b1, ... respectively, the join-column prediction task is to suggest a pair (ak, bl) of columns such that the equality condition ak = bl can be used to join the tables in a way that matches with the provided ground-truth mapping M: A → B" (Appendix A.3.4). The mapping M is "usually partial injective: not every column in B is mapped from A, not every column in A is mapped to B"—meaning some columns in A have no matching column in B, and vice versa.

The implementation procedure (Appendix A.3.4):

1. "Randomly sample columns with replacement from our entire collection of tables, generating a fixed column pool C" — a set of columns is assembled from across all available tables.
2. "Retain half the rows of A to provide as context to the LLM. The remaining rows are used to generate a new table B" — the input table A is split; the model sees only the first half.
3. "For each instance, randomly sample columns from both the target table and the column pool and join them to B" — columns from A and from the pool are combined to form B, creating both genuine join candidates and distractors.
4. "Anonymize the column names in B, then pass both A and B to the LLM and ask it to return a valid join mapping M" — column names in B are replaced with generic labels so the model cannot use naming conventions as a shortcut; it must infer join relationships from data values.

The scoring metric is the F1 score over columns: "TPs scored as exact matches between ground truth and the LLM output, FPs scored as extraneous mappings, FNs scored as missing mappings, and incorrect mappings counting as FP + FN" (Appendix A.3.4). This is a more nuanced metric than simple accuracy—it penalizes both over-prediction (suggesting spurious joins) and under-prediction (missing valid joins), capturing the precision-recall tradeoff inherent in schema matching.

#### Instruction Following Category: Adapting IFEval to Live Information Sources

The instruction following category addresses a critical capability—whether an LLM can follow explicit formatting and content constraints—while avoiding the open-ended evaluation problem by restricting scoring to *verifiable* instructions.

**Task Design: IFEval Paradigm Applied to News Articles.** The category is "inspired by IFEval (Zhou et al., 2023a), which is an instruction-following evaluation for LLMs containing verifiable instructions" (Section 2.5). IFEval's original design was simple: take a trivial prompt ("write a travel blog about Japan") and append one or more verifiable constraints (e.g., "write more than 300 words," "include the keywords X and Y"). LiveBench makes two key modifications:

1. **Rich prompt content**: Instead of trivial prompts, the model is given the first N sentences of an actual news article from The Guardian, sourced via their API (approximately 200 articles available). The article text provides meaningful content that the model must process and transform, making the task more ecologically valid—real instruction-following use cases typically involve operating on provided content rather than generating from a vacuous seed.

2. **Task diversity**: Four distinct transformation tasks are defined: Paraphrase, Simplify, Summarize, and Story Generation. The exact prompts for each are shown in Table 12:
   - Paraphrase: "Please paraphrase based on the sentences provided."
   - Summarize: "Please summarize based on the sentences provided."
   - Simplify: "Please explain in simpler terms what this text means."
   - Story Generation: "Please generate a story based on the sentences provided."

Each of the four subtasks has 50 questions, yielding 200 instruction-following questions total.

**Instruction Set and Composition.** The paper uses a subset of 16 instructions from IFEval's original 25 (see Table 3 for the full comparison). The selection criterion is explicit: the paper "excludes instructions that do not reflect real-world use-cases" (Section 2.5). The excluded instructions include: Number Placeholder (requiring bracketed placeholders like [address]), Change Cases (requiring all-uppercase or all-lowercase responses), Choose From (restricting answers to a fixed option set), Minimum Number Highlighted Sections (requiring markdown highlighting), Frequency of All-capital Words (requiring a specific count of all-caps words), Letter Frequency (requiring a letter to appear exactly N times), No Commas (prohibiting commas), and Response Language (requiring a specific non-English language). These exclusions reflect a judgment that these constraints are artificial and unlikely to appear in practical human-to-LLM interactions.

The remaining 16 instructions span several categories:
- **Keywords**: Include Keywords, Forbidden Words
- **Length Constraints**: Number Paragraphs, Number Words, Number Sentences, Number Paragraphs + First Word in i-th Paragraph
- **Detectable Content**: Postscript (ending with a specified marker)
- **Detectable Format**: Number Bullets, Title, Multiple Sections, JSON Format
- **Combination**: Repeat Prompt (echo the instruction before responding), Two Responses
- **Start/End**: End Checker (finish with exact phrase), Quotation

Instructions are composed together: "we compose different instructions together by sampling from a uniform distribution from 2 to 5" (Appendix A.3.5). Since random combinations can produce conflicting constraints (e.g., "write at most 100 words" and "write at least 300 words"), the system includes a conflict resolution step: "since the instructions can be conflicting, we deconflict the instructions" (Appendix A.3.5). The deconfliction logic is not detailed in the paper, but presumably involves checking for contradictory requirements and removing or adjusting constraints to ensure logical consistency.

The distribution of instruction counts is described as "approximate normal distribution of the number of instructions per example with the majority of the containing two or three instructions" (Appendix A.3.5), a consequence of the uniform sampling from [2,5] combined with deconfliction removing some extreme combinations.

**Prompt Assembly.** The full prompt uses a meta-template: "The following are the beginning sentences of a news article from the Guardian.\n——-\n{guardian article}\n——-\n{subtask prompt} {instructions}" (Appendix A.3.5). This template sandwiches the article text between horizontal rules, clearly separating the source material from the task specification and instructions.

**Scoring Methodology.** The scoring method is designed to capture both holistic and fine-grained instruction following:

> Let Prompt-level accuracy = 1 if all instructions were correctly followed, 0 otherwise.
> Let Instruction-level accuracy = fraction of individual instructions that were properly followed.
> The final score is: `$\frac{\text{Prompt-level accuracy} + \text{Instruction-level accuracy}}{2}$`, scaled to [0, 1].
> 
> (Derived from the prose description in Appendix A.3.5)

**What it computes:** This is a composite metric that penalizes catastrophic instruction failure (missing any instruction entirely zeros out the first component) while still giving partial credit for partially correct responses (the second component rewards getting some instructions right even if others are missed). A score of 1 means perfect adherence to all instructions; lower scores indicate varying degrees of failure.

**Why this form:** A pure prompt-level accuracy metric (only counting perfectly correct responses) would be too harsh—models that follow 4 out of 5 instructions would receive the same score (0) as models that follow 0 out of 5. A pure instruction-level accuracy metric would be too lenient—it would give high scores to models that consistently miss one specific instruction type. The average of both components balances these concerns: it rewards getting most instructions right while still imposing a significant penalty for any failure.

The verifiable nature of the instructions is what enables objective scoring. For example, "write more than 300 words" is checked by counting tokens; "include the keywords X and Y" is checked by substring matching; "wrap your response in JSON format" is checked by attempting to parse the output as JSON. The paper's decision to use only verifiable instructions—and to exclude subjective constraints like "be helpful" or "write creatively"—is the key design choice that enables automatic, bias-free scoring in this category.

#### Language Comprehension Category: Puzzle-Solving and Text Reconstruction

The language comprehension category tests models' ability to reason about language itself through three tasks: word puzzle solving, typo correction, and narrative reconstruction. These tasks share the property that the correct answer is objectively determinable (unique puzzle solutions, correct spellings, original sentence orderings) but the required capabilities—semantic association, orthographic knowledge, causal reasoning about event sequences—are more linguistic than any other LiveBench category.

**Connections Task: Word Grouping by Latent Categories.** This task is based on the New York Times Connections puzzle, where "sixteen words are provided in a random order; the objective of the game is to sort these into four sets of four words, such that each set has a 'connection' between them" (Appendix A.3.6). Connection types span a wide range: "categories, e.g., 'kiwi, grape, pear, peach' (types of fruits); anagrams; homophones; or words that finish a certain context, e.g., 'ant, drill, island, opal' being words that come after the word 'fire' to make a phrase" (Appendix A.3.6).

The paper adapts the puzzle to single-turn evaluation by reducing the puzzle size: rather than the standard 16-word, 4-group puzzle, LiveBench uses a distribution of "15 questions of eight words, 15 questions of twelve words and 20 questions of sixteen words" (Appendix A.3.6). The 8-word puzzles (2 groups of 4) and 12-word puzzles (3 groups of 4) are easier than the full 16-word variant, creating a difficulty gradient.

The scoring metric is "the fraction of groups that the model outputs correctly" (Appendix A.3.6). For an 8-word puzzle with 2 groups, getting 1 group correct yields a score of 0.5. The model must output all words organized by group, in the format: "**word1, word2, word3, word4, word5, word6, word7, word8**" (from the example in Appendix A.3.6), with the groups clearly delineated by ordering.

The paper cites prior work (Todd et al., 2024) that comprehensively tested Connections on GPT-family models, finding that "GPT-4 has an overall completion rate below 40% on the puzzles (when allowed multiple tries to get it correct)" (Appendix A.3.6). LiveBench's single-turn evaluation is therefore more challenging than the multi-attempt setting studied in prior work.

The question source is the New York Times daily puzzle, which means new puzzles are naturally generated each day—making this one of the easiest tasks to keep updated with fresh, contamination-free content. Table 14 confirms that Connections "can be updated automatically using a script" by replacing puzzles with newer daily releases.

**Typos Task: Spelling Correction with Style Preservation.** This task evaluates a narrow but practically important capability: "the common use case for LLMs in which a user asks the LLM to identify typos and misspellings in some written text but to leave other aspects of the text unchanged" (Section 2.6). The challenge is that LLMs tend to "impose its own writing style onto that of the input text, such as switching from British to US spellings or adding the serial comma, which may not be desirable" (Appendix A.3.6). The task therefore tests both spelling correction and *restraint*—the ability to fix only what is broken.

The question generation pipeline operates in four stages:

1. **Source selection**: Recent arXiv abstracts are selected as the source text. These are chosen because they are "recent" (post-cutoff date) and originally well-written (academic authors generally produce grammatically correct text with few typos).

2. **Manual cleaning**: The abstracts are manually checked, and "typos and grammar issues" are fixed to ensure the source text is perfectly correct. This manual pass is necessary because arXiv abstracts—despite being academic writing—do sometimes contain errors that would confuse the task if left uncorrected.

3. **Typo injection**: A list of common human misspellings is assembled. For each question, a probability p is sampled from a uniform distribution: `$p \sim U(0.5, 0.7)$`. Then, "every correctly spelled word" in the abstract is independently replaced with a common misspelling with probability p. This means that in expectation, 50-70% of words are corrupted—a high error rate that makes the text significantly degraded.

4. **Formatting**: The corrupted text is presented to the model with the instruction: "Please output this exact text, with no changes at all except for fixing the misspellings. Please leave all other stylistic decisions like commas and US vs British spellings as in the original text" (Section 2.6 example).

The variable corruption rate p drawn from U(0.5, 0.7) introduces difficulty variation: higher p means more words are misspelled, making the task harder both because there is more to correct and because the degraded text provides less context for disambiguating what the original words were.

Scoring is binary: the model's output either exactly matches the ground-truth (original, cleaned abstract) or does not. The paper states: "to score this problem, we merely ask whether the ground truth abstract is contained in the output provided by the LLM" (Appendix A.3.6). This "contained in" check (rather than exact match) provides some permissiveness: if the model adds explanatory text before or after the corrected abstract, it is not penalized, as long as the corrected text appears somewhere in the response.

The task generates 50 questions. Updates are straightforward: "replace with newer paper abstracts" (Table 14), making this one of the most sustainable tasks in the benchmark.

**Plot Unscrambling Task: Narrative Reconstruction from Shuffled Sentences.** This task evaluates "causal language reasoning"—the ability to reconstruct a coherent narrative from scrambled pieces by understanding the logical and temporal relationships between events. The setup is:

1. Obtain movie plot synopses from IMDb or Wikipedia for "feature-length films released after January 1st 2024" (Appendix A.3.6).
2. Split each synopsis into its constituent sentences.
3. Randomly shuffle the sentences.
4. Present the shuffled sentences to the model with the instruction to "rewrite the plot summary with the sentences correctly ordered" (Appendix A.3.6).

The synopses vary substantially in length: "from as few as 7 sentences to as many as 66 sentences" (Appendix A.3.6). The paper notes that "at the upper end, this is a very challenging task"—reconstructing a 66-sentence narrative from a random permutation requires understanding complex causal chains, character relationships, and narrative conventions.

The scoring methodology addresses two practical challenges:

**Challenge 1: Transcription fidelity.** Models sometimes modify sentences when reproducing them—correcting grammar, rephrasing, or adding/removing words. The paper does not want to penalize these modifications because "we are primarily interested in testing the models' capabilities for causal language reasoning in this task, rather than precise transcription accuracy" (Appendix A.3.6). The solution is fuzzy matching: "we apply a fuzzy-match using difflib (Team, 2008) to determine the closest match using a version of the Ratcliff/Obershelp algorithm (Ratcliff & Metzener, 1988)." The difflib algorithm computes the similarity between two strings by finding the longest contiguous matching subsequences (Ratcliff/Obershelp), producing a similarity score between 0 and 1. Each sentence in the model's output is matched to the most similar sentence in the ground truth, allowing for minor transcription variations.

**Challenge 2: Ordering comparison.** Once the model's sentences are mapped to ground-truth sentences via fuzzy matching, the ordering is compared. The metric is:

> $$\text{Score} = 1 - \frac{d}{n_{\text{sentences\_gt}}}$$
> 
> where `$d$` is the Levenshtein distance of the model's sentence ordering relative to the ground truth synopsis ordering, and `$n_{\text{sentences\_gt}}$` is the number of sentences in the ground truth synopsis.
> 
> (From Appendix A.3.6)

**What it computes:** The Levenshtein distance `$d$` counts the minimum number of single-element edits (insertions, deletions, substitutions) required to transform the model's ordering into the ground-truth ordering. This is normalized by the total number of sentences and subtracted from 1, so a perfect ordering (d = 0) yields a score of 1, and progressively worse orderings yield lower scores approaching 0.

**Why this form:** Levenshtein distance is appropriate for permutation comparison because it captures the *degree* of misordering, not just whether the ordering is exactly correct. For example, if the model gets 64 out of 66 sentences in the correct order but swaps two adjacent sentences, the edit distance would be small (2), yielding a score close to 1—reflecting that the model nearly reconstructed the narrative. A binary exact-match metric would give 0, which would not distinguish this from a completely random ordering. The normalization by sentence count makes scores comparable across synopses of different lengths.

The paper includes an interesting defense of the task's validity: "even if the set of sentences do not create a distinct causal ordering, the task is essentially asking the LLM to maximize the probability that a given arrangement of sentences is a real movie. In addition to causal reasoning, the LLM can use subtle cues to reason about what ordering creates the most compelling plot" (Appendix A.3.6). The analogy drawn is to language modeling perplexity: even if perfect prediction is impossible, relative performance across models is still informative about language understanding ability.

The task contains 40 questions. As with Connections and Typos, it can be updated automatically by "replacing with newer IMDb plot summaries" (Table 14).

#### Scoring Methodology: Permissive Regex-Based Answer Extraction

LiveBench's scoring infrastructure is as important to its design as the question content itself. The paper describes a philosophy of permissive, format-agnostic grading: "As a rule of thumb, if a human reading the LLM's answer would be able to comprehend the answer (and assuming it is correct), then the answer should be marked correct" (Appendix A.4).

The implementation uses "flexible regex patterns that capture different notation styles or response structures" (Appendix A.4). Specific permissiveness examples include:
- Math answers: accepting both "\boxed{}" and "\fbox{}" LaTeX environments
- Multiple choice: accepting either the letter answer or the raw answer text
- Bold formatting: accepting both "**answer**" and "***answer***" delimiters
- Whitespace: ignoring leading/trailing whitespace, normalizing internal whitespace
- Case sensitivity: case-insensitive matching where appropriate (e.g., "yes"/"Yes"/"YES" in Web of Lies, column names in CTA)

The paper explicitly addresses the tension between permissiveness and precision: "The reason for our less-strict judging is because some models may have been instruction-tuned to a particular format, giving them an advantage or disadvantage. This approach allows us to evaluate models based on their task-specific skills, rather than their ability to follow specific instructions" (Appendix A.4).

The quality control process for scoring functions is iterative:
1. "Regularly manually inspect a random sample of responses which were labeled incorrect for each model" (Appendix A.4).
2. "Look for common answer formats that are incorrect and not picked up by the regex-based parser" (Appendix A.4).
3. Update scoring functions to accommodate newly discovered valid answer formats.
4. Re-score all models with updated scoring functions.

The paper reports that this process has stabilized: "we made a few such updates when new models came out that hit new edge cases of our scoring functions, in the first month of LiveBench's release, but now we have not needed to make a scoring function update for a few months" (Appendix A.5). This suggests that the regex patterns now cover the majority of valid answer formats across model families.

#### Maintenance and Update Mechanism

The monthly update cycle is the structural mechanism that makes LiveBench sustainable as a contamination-resistant benchmark. The key parameters of this cycle are:

**Replacement rate**: "In each update, we replace 1/6 of the questions on average, so that the benchmark is fully refreshed roughly every 6 months" (Section 2.7). At this rate, the entire question set turns over twice per year. The paper notes that the rate "may speed up the turnover rate of questions in the future, based on interest in LiveBench" (Section 2.7), suggesting the 1/6 monthly rate is a starting point rather than a fixed commitment.

**Selection criteria**: Tasks are chosen for update based on two primary factors: "(1) the oldest tasks, and (2) the currently easiest tasks" (Section 2.7). The first criterion ensures temporal freshness—old questions are retired before they have a chance to be incorporated into training data. The second criterion ensures the benchmark becomes progressively harder over time: as models improve and certain tasks approach ceiling performance, those tasks are replaced with harder variants. This is confirmed by the empirical observation that "between the original and the most-recent set of questions, the median and mean average scores (among models included in all iterations of the leaderboard) have both dropped by about 1.2%, showing that the benchmark is becoming harder over time" (Section 3.4).

**Private question buffer**: "Each month, we do not release the new questions until one month later, so that the public leaderboard always has 1/6 questions that are private" (Section 2.7). This private subset serves as a contamination firewall: even if a model developer were to scrape all publicly available LiveBench questions and include them in training data, the model would still face 1/6 novel questions at evaluation time. The one-month delay before questions become public means that the private buffer is always one month ahead of the public question set.

**Computational sustainability**: The paper addresses the practical concern that a frequently-updating benchmark requires ongoing computational resources. The strategy is to maintain a curated leaderboard: "We maintain the best (or most popular) 40-50 models on the leaderboard so as to avoid an ever-growing list of models to evaluate each month. For example, we maintain about two versions of each model family on the leaderboard (to show the improvement from the most recent version) but no more. This ensures that we have a tractable set of at most 50 models to evaluate on 200 questions each month, which is easily within the computational budgets of the authors' institutions" (Section 2.7).

The 200-questions-per-month figure represents the evaluation cost per model (roughly 1000 questions for a full run, but questions are only re-evaluated when scoring functions change; new questions constitute the bulk of new evaluation work). The paper also notes that "community contributions further reduce the computational burden of the authors" (Section 2.7).

**Question generation scalability**: Table 14 categorizes each task by whether it can be updated automatically using a script. Of the 18 tasks, 16 can be updated automatically—the only exceptions are Olympiad (source material only updates yearly, and proof rearrangement requires manual proof selection) and Spatial (hand-written questions). The paper notes that for automatically-updatable tasks, "we always modify the generation script when creating new questions, in order to make sure that the distribution of questions is different and harder over time" (Appendix A.6). This means that even for tasks with scripted generation (like AMPS_Hard or Web of Lies v2), the generation parameters are deliberately shifted with each update to prevent models from overfitting to the specific distribution of synthetic questions.

**Version control and transparency**: "We maintain version control over all updates, ensuring transparency and reproducibility. This allows researchers to track changes and compare results across different versions of LiveBench" (Appendix A.6). The benchmark is hosted on HuggingFace at https://huggingface.co/livebench, and the codebase is available on GitHub.

**Community engagement**: The paper explicitly welcomes community collaboration "for expanding the benchmark tasks and models" (Section 1). The sustainability plan includes community contributions both for adding new tasks (which requires novel question design) and for running model evaluations (which reduces the computational burden on the authors).

The paper has completed two monthly updates as of the writing: the first added the spatial reasoning task (50 questions) plus 40 additional coding questions, bringing the total from an unspecified initial size to 1000 questions. The second update fully refreshed the math Olympiad questions and partially updated AMPS_Hard and math_comp, replacing 132 questions to maintain the 1000-question total.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: The Three-Desiderata Framework as a Structural Solution to Benchmark Obsolescence

The paper's most fundamental intellectual move is not any single task design but the articulation of a framework for constructing benchmarks that are *structurally resistant* to the two failure modes plaguing LLM evaluation: test set contamination and evaluation bias. Prior work had addressed these problems in isolation—LiveCodeBench (Jain et al., 2024) tackled contamination through temporal freshness but was limited to coding; ChatBot Arena (Chiang et al., 2024) avoided static questions through crowdsourcing but introduced human preference biases; AlpacaEval (Li et al., ) and Arena-Hard (Li et al., 2024) used LLM judges for flexibility but suffered from self-preference, verbosity, and style biases. The paper's contribution is the recognition that these are not separate problems with separate solutions but deeply intertwined failure modes that must be addressed *simultaneously* through three hard constraints: frequently-updated questions from recent sources, objective ground-truth scoring without LLM judges, and category diversity that probes general capabilities rather than narrow skills.

What makes this a genuine innovation rather than an obvious checklist is the *tradeoff discipline* it imposes. The objective-scoring constraint eliminates entire categories of tasks—open-ended generation, dialogue quality, creative writing—that are central to other benchmarks but cannot be evaluated without subjective judgment. The temporal-freshness constraint means the benchmark requires ongoing maintenance rather than one-time publication. The diversity constraint forces question designers to find recent, objectively-answerable problems across domains where such problems are not equally available. The paper implicitly argues that these tradeoffs are worth making: a narrower but trustworthy signal is more valuable than a broader but contaminated or biased one. This framing—that benchmark reliability requires *sacrificing* certain evaluation dimensions—is a conceptual departure from the field's tendency to maximize coverage at the expense of measurement validity.

The significance of this framework extends beyond LiveBench itself. By making the design principles explicit and the tradeoffs transparent, the paper provides a template that other benchmark creators can adopt or adapt. The decision to open-source all questions, code, and model answers, combined with the monthly update cadence and community contribution model, transforms the benchmark from a static artifact into a *platform*—an evolving infrastructure rather than a one-time measurement instrument. This platform model, if adopted broadly, could shift the benchmark creation cycle from a series of independent, obsolescence-prone publications to an ecosystem of maintained, continuously-updated evaluation suites. The paper does not claim this as an achievement but its architecture implies it.

The evidence that the framework produces discriminative, contamination-resistant evaluation is distributed throughout the paper: the top model (o1-preview-2024-09-12) achieves only ~65% accuracy (Figure 1, left), confirming the benchmark is far from saturated; the rank correlation between benchmark versions exceeds 0.997 (Section 3.4), showing stability; the median score has dropped by 1.2% across updates (Section 3.4), confirming the benchmark is becoming harder as intended; and the correlation analysis with ChatBot Arena and Arena-Hard (Figure 6) shows both agreement (0.91 and 0.88 correlation respectively) and meaningful divergence, with specific models showing disproportionate strength or weakness on LiveBench's objective tasks compared to human-preference or LLM-judged benchmarks—exactly the pattern one would expect if LiveBench is measuring something different from, and complementary to, existing evaluation approaches.

### Innovation 2: Proof Rearrangement as a Proxy Task for Mathematical Reasoning Without LLM Judging

The Olympiad task represents a genuinely novel evaluation methodology that deserves recognition as a distinct contribution: instead of attempting to evaluate proof correctness directly (which requires either unreliable LLM judging or unreliable auto-formalization), the paper proposes evaluating whether a model can *reconstruct* a proof from its constituent formulae presented in scrambled order. This is an instance of what might be called *proxy task design*—constructing a related but objectively-gradable task whose performance correlates with, and therefore serves as a measurable signal for, the target capability that cannot be directly evaluated.

Prior approaches to evaluating proof-generation ability fell into two unsatisfactory categories. The first was LLM judging of natural-language proofs, which the paper correctly identifies as having "no formal guarantees on the correctness of the evaluation" (Appendix A.3.1). The second was auto-formalization into interactive theorem provers like Lean, which the paper notes "does not have formal guarantees on the correctness of the auto-formalization—and thus that of the evaluation." In both cases, the evaluation pipeline itself introduces errors that cannot be bounded, making model comparisons unreliable. The proof rearrangement task sidesteps both problems: the ground truth is the correct ordering of the formulae, which is objectively known from the original proof, and scoring reduces to edit distance computation—a deterministic, unbiased metric.

The conceptual innovation here is the argument that if a model is capable of generating a correct proof, it should *a fortiori* be capable of the strictly easier task of reassembling one from scrambled components. This is not an empirical claim but a logical one about the relationship between generative and discriminative capabilities: generating a proof requires both determining what statements are needed and ordering them correctly; reassembly provides the statements and only requires ordering. If a model fails at reassembly, it cannot succeed at generation. The contrapositive means that poor performance on the Olympiad task provides a *lower bound* on proof-generation inability—a valid negative signal even if the task is not a perfect positive signal (a model might succeed at reassembly but still fail at generation).

The task design also demonstrates a practical solution to difficulty calibration: by varying the percentage of masked formulae (10%, 50%, 80%), the same underlying proof generates three hardness variants without requiring new proof material. The paper reports that performance drops from ~97 for GPT-4o on the easiest variant to ~36 on the hardest (Appendix A.3.1), confirming that the masking parameter effectively controls difficulty without changing the underlying mathematical content. This parameterization-through-masking technique generalizes beyond mathematical proofs to any domain where expert-created content with internal structure can be partially obscured and reconstructed.

### Innovation 3: The Benchmark as a Moving Target—Operationalizing Contamination Resistance Through Temporal Dynamics

While the idea of using recent data to avoid contamination is not itself novel (LiveCodeBench and others preceded it), the paper's contribution is the *systematization* of this principle into a sustainable, multi-category benchmark with explicit temporal parameters. The monthly update cadence with 1/6 question replacement (full refresh every ~6 months), the private question buffer (withholding 1/6 questions from public release for one month), the dual selection criteria for replacement (oldest tasks + easiest tasks), and the deliberate modification of generation scripts with each update to shift question distributions—these together constitute an operational framework for what might be called *temporal freshness engineering*.

The significance of systematization over simple practice is that it makes contamination resistance *measurable and controllable* rather than aspirational. The paper can state that "the benchmark is fully refreshed roughly every 6 months" (Section 2.7) as a quantitative property of the system, not a vague claim about recent data. The private question buffer means that even an adversary who scrapes all public LiveBench questions and includes them in training data faces 1/6 unseen questions—a concrete, parameterized contamination barrier. The difficulty-increasing update criterion (replacing the currently easiest tasks) means the benchmark has a built-in ratchet that prevents saturation: as models improve, the benchmark becomes harder, maintaining discriminative power.

This temporal dynamics framework also addresses a subtle problem with earlier contamination-mitigation approaches. Srivastava et al. (2024) showed that modifying numbers in MATH dataset problems causes performance declines, but the paper notes that the modified data "still ends up being highly similar to the kind of data likely seen in the pretraining set" (Section 4). The implication is that surface-level modifications to static benchmarks only delay contamination by one generation of models—the next generation, trained on larger internet scrapes that include the modified versions, will again overfit. LiveBench's solution is to make the *distribution shift permanent and progressive*: each update not only provides new questions but modifies the generation parameters to produce a harder, different distribution. A model trained on LiveBench questions from month N will face a meaningfully different distribution at month N+6, even for synthetically-generated tasks.

The empirical evidence for the effectiveness of this approach is necessarily preliminary (only two monthly updates have been completed) but suggestive: rank correlations exceeding 0.997 between updates indicate that model ordering is stable despite question turnover, meaning the benchmark is measuring consistent underlying capabilities rather than memorization of specific question instances. The 1.2% drop in mean scores between the original and most-recent question sets confirms that the difficulty ratchet is working—the benchmark is becoming harder, not through arbitrary difficulty inflation but through the systematic replacement of easier questions.

### Innovation 4: Instruction Following as Verifiable Constraint Satisfaction on Live Content

The instruction following category represents a conceptual reframing of what it means to evaluate instruction-following ability. Prior work, particularly IFEval (Zhou et al., 2023a), demonstrated that instruction following can be automatically scored by restricting to verifiable constraints—instructions like "write more than 300 words" or "include keyword X" where compliance can be checked programmatically. However, IFEval applied these constraints to trivial, content-free prompts ("write a travel blog about Japan"), which means the evaluation measured constraint satisfaction in a vacuum—it did not test whether models could follow instructions *while* processing and transforming meaningful content.

LiveBench's innovation is to combine IFEval's verifiable-constraint framework with rich, live source material—actual Guardian news articles—and diverse transformation tasks (paraphrase, simplify, summarize, story generation). This seemingly simple change has a significant conceptual implication: it transforms instruction following from a *standalone capability* into a *composite capability* that depends jointly on content understanding and constraint adherence. A model that perfectly follows formatting instructions but produces factually incorrect or nonsensical transformations of the article text receives a high score under IFEval's original framing but would arguably fail the practical use case. LiveBench's framing recognizes that real-world instruction following is always *about something*—users give instructions about specific content—and the evaluation should reflect this entanglement.

The paper's scoring methodology—averaging prompt-level accuracy (all instructions followed correctly) with instruction-level accuracy (fraction of individual instructions followed)—is a pragmatic solution to the credit assignment problem in composite tasks. Pure prompt-level accuracy is too harsh (a model missing one of five instructions scores 0, same as a model missing all five). Pure instruction-level accuracy is too lenient (a model that consistently fails one instruction type can still score highly). The average balances these extremes. This is not a theoretical contribution but a practical design choice that reflects careful thinking about what the metric should reward and penalize, and it is explicitly tied to the structure of the task (variable numbers of instructions per prompt, from 2 to 5).

The exclusion of 9 out of IFEval's 25 original instructions on the grounds that they "do not reflect real-world use-cases" (Section 2.5) is itself a methodological statement. Instructions like "your ENTIRE response should be in English, capital letters only" or "in your entire response, refrain from the use of any commas" are verifiable but artificial—they test whether the model can follow arbitrary constraints, not whether it can follow constraints that a human user would plausibly impose. The paper's curation of the instruction set toward realistic constraints (keywords, length limits, formatting requirements, structural elements like titles and sections) makes the benchmark more ecologically valid at the cost of reducing the diversity of constraint types tested. This is the same tradeoff discipline that characterizes the overall benchmark design: narrower but more meaningful evaluation over broader but less representative measurement.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** LiveBench currently consists of 1,000 questions across 18 tasks in 6 categories: math, coding, reasoning, language comprehension, instruction following, and data analysis. Questions are sourced from recent information sources (math competitions from the past 11 months, arXiv abstracts, Kaggle/Socrata datasets, Guardian news articles, IMDb movie synopses, LeetCode/AtCoder problems from November 2023 onward) or are harder, synthetically-generated versions of existing benchmark tasks. The question set is updated monthly, with roughly 1/6 of questions replaced each cycle so that the benchmark fully refreshes approximately every 6 months. New questions are withheld from public release for one month to maintain a private subset constituting 1/6 of the benchmark at any time (Section 2.7, Appendix A.6).

- **Base models.** The paper evaluates a total of 40 LLMs spanning three categories: proprietary API models (OpenAI's o1-preview, gpt-4o, gpt-4-turbo, gpt-3.5-turbo; Anthropic's claude-3.5-sonnet, claude-3-opus, claude-3-haiku; Google's gemini-1.5-pro, gemini-1.5-flash, gemma-2; Mistral's mistral-large, mistral-small, mixtral-8x22b; and others including step-2-16k), large open-source models (meta-llama-3.1-405b-instruct, qwen2.5-72b-instruct, deepseek-v2.5, dracarys2-72b-instruct), and small open-source models ranging from 0.5B to 27B parameters (phi-3.5-moe-instruct with 6.6B active parameters, gemma-2-9b-it, qwen2.5-7b-instruct, meta-llama-3.1-8b-instruct, and others). The selection spans a wide capability range to ensure the benchmark discriminates across model scales. The paper does not justify the specific model choices beyond covering "top proprietary models, large open-source models, and small open-source models" (Section 3). The full list with citations appears in Table 4.

- **Metrics.** Each question receives a score from 0 to 1, where 1 indicates a completely correct answer and 0 indicates any deviation from the ground truth. For most tasks, scoring is binary exact-match (or its semantic equivalent—e.g., SymPy-verified mathematical equivalence for AMPS_Hard, unit test passage for coding tasks). Exceptions include: Plot Unscrambling, which uses a normalized Levenshtein distance metric: Score = 1 − d / n_sentences_gt where d is the edit distance between the model's sentence ordering and the ground truth; Join-Column Prediction, which uses F1 score over column mappings; and Instruction Following, which averages prompt-level accuracy (all instructions correct → 1, else 0) and instruction-level accuracy (fraction of individual instructions correct) and scales to [0, 1]. Task-level scores are computed as the average of all questions in that task. Category scores are computed as the average of all tasks within that category. The final LiveBench score is the **average of the six category scores**—meaning each category is equally weighted regardless of the number of constituent tasks or questions (Section 3, Appendix A.4). The paper reports 95% bootstrap confidence intervals for all aggregate results (Figure 1, left; Figure 5).

- **Baselines.** LiveBench is a benchmark, not a method, so there are no algorithmic baselines in the traditional sense. However, the paper implicitly treats the overall LiveBench score as an absolute performance measure and uses cross-model ranking as the primary comparative framework. For correlation analyses (Section 3.3), the paper compares LiveBench scores against two external benchmarks: **ChatBot Arena** (Chiang et al., 2024), which uses human preference judgments aggregated into Elo scores, and **Arena-Hard** (Li et al., 2024), which uses GPT-4 as an LLM judge on a fixed set of prompts. These comparisons serve as baselines to assess whether LiveBench produces meaningfully different model rankings than existing evaluation paradigms. The paper also includes an ablation study (Appendix A.2) comparing ground-truth scoring against LLM-as-a-judge scoring (using GPT-4-Turbo as judge) on math and reasoning tasks to quantify the error rate of LLM judging on hard questions.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper does not use a FLOPs or token-generation budget as a controlled variable—this is an evaluation benchmark, not a method that allocates compute. Models are evaluated under a single-turn protocol with temperature 0, meaning each model produces exactly one generation per question with no sampling-based best-of-N, no beam search, and no multi-turn revisions. The total inference cost per model is the sum of the per-question generation costs. The paper reports approximate costs for running API models on LiveBench (Table 16), with o1-preview being the most expensive at roughly $47.87 and claude-3-haiku the cheapest at roughly $0.90 (as of October 1, 2024). The paper also reports input/output token statistics (Table 15): across all 1,000 questions, the mean number of input tokens per question is 1,612 and the mean number of output tokens is 395 for gpt-4-turbo. There is no attempt to control for or equalize compute across models—each model is evaluated under its standard inference protocol, and cost or latency differences are noted but not factored into the benchmark scores.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** There is no cross-validation or train/test splitting because this is an evaluation benchmark, not a training pipeline. Statistical significance is assessed via 95% bootstrap confidence intervals for aggregate scores (Figure 1, left; Figure 5). The paper reports Pearson correlation coefficients and standard errors for category-level and task-level correlations with overall LiveBench performance (Tables 5 and 6, Figure 2) based on results from all 40 models. For the LLM judge ablation study (Appendix A.2, Tables 8–9), the paper compares scores computed by an LLM judge (GPT-4-Turbo) against ground-truth scores and reports error rates as the discrepancy between the two scoring methods. Rank correlation between benchmark versions (original vs. first update, first update vs. second update) is reported as > 0.997 to assess the stability of model rankings across question sets (Section 3.4). Quality control for model evaluation involves manual inspection of a random sample of responses labeled incorrect, looking for false negatives caused by valid but unanticipated answer formats, and iteratively updating regex-based scoring functions to accommodate them (Appendix A.5).

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Overall Model Rankings

Table 1 reports the top 15 models' scores across all six categories and their overall LiveBench score, while Table 2 provides full results for all 40 models. The headline finding is that **o1-preview-2024-09-12 achieves the highest overall score of 64.7**, leading the next-best model (claude-3-5-sonnet-20241022 at 58.5) by approximately 6 percentage points. This gap is substantial: in a benchmark where the difference between the 2nd and 15th ranked models is only about 8 points (58.5 to 50.1), a 6-point lead represents a large separation. The top model's score of 64.7 also confirms the paper's difficulty target—no model exceeds 70% accuracy, leaving headroom for future improvement. Figure 1 (left) and Figure 5 visualize these results as a horizontal bar chart with 95% bootstrap confidence intervals, showing that the confidence intervals for o1-preview-2024-09-12 do not overlap with any other model, confirming statistical significance of the lead.

The category-level breakdown in Table 1 reveals heterogeneous strengths across models. o1-preview-2024-09-12 substantially outperforms all other models in data analysis (64.0 vs. 57.5 for gemini-exp-1114), language (74.6 vs. 69.3 for claude-3-5-sonnet-20241022), and math (68.7 vs. 62.9 for gemini-exp-1121). In contrast, claude-3-5-sonnet-20241022 dominates coding (67.1 vs. 50.8 for o1-preview), a dramatic 16.3-point gap—the largest category-level disparity among top models. o1-mini-2024-09-12 leads reasoning by a wide margin (72.3 vs. 67.4 for o1-preview), despite ranking third overall at 56.7. This category-level heterogeneity is visually represented in the radar plot (Figure 1, right), which shows that "the ordering of top models varies between each category."

The best-performing open-source models are meta-llama-3.1-405b-instruct-turbo (overall 51.1) and qwen2.5-72b-instruct (not in Table 1, discussed in Section 3.1), which "virtually tie with each other and outperform gpt-4-turbo" (Section 3.1). The best-performing small open-source model is phi-3.5-moe-instruct (not shown in the main table excerpt): "with only 6.6B active parameters, it outperforms gpt-3.5 and is on par with mixtral-8x22b" (Section 3.1). These results establish a clear capability hierarchy: proprietary frontier models lead, large open-source models form a competitive middle tier, and small open-source models remain significantly behind but show non-trivial capability.

#### Category-Level Correlation Analysis

Figure 2 (left) displays the Pearson correlation matrix among the six categories, computed from all 40 models' category-level scores. The strongest correlations are between math and reasoning (approximately 0.95), math and coding (approximately 0.90), and coding and reasoning (approximately 0.88)—unsurprising given that all three categories involve structured problem-solving with objective correctness criteria. Language comprehension correlates "fairly well with data analysis" (approximately 0.85, Section 3.2), which the paper attributes to "both categories including tasks that require the LLM to output a large part of the prompt that is modified in a specific way (e.g., by fixing typos or changing the table format)."

A surprising finding is that **instruction following correlates relatively weakly with all other categories** (the color bar in Figure 2 shows correlations in the 0.80-0.85 range for instruction following paired with other categories, noticeably lower than the 0.90-0.95 typical for math-coding-reasoning pairs). The paper interprets this as evidence that instruction following "tests a distinct capability dimension" (implied by Section 3.2 observation that it "correlates relatively weakly with all other categories"). This is a meaningful empirical contribution: it suggests that a model's ability to follow formatting and content constraints is partially independent of its mathematical or coding ability, and that benchmarks lacking instruction following tasks (or using instruction following as a confound through strict answer format requirements) may miss an important dimension of model capability.

Table 5 provides the correlation of each category with the overall LiveBench score. Math has the highest correlation (0.948, std error 0.064), followed closely by reasoning (0.944, 0.071) and data analysis (0.932, 0.049). Instruction following has the lowest (0.817, 0.081). This ranking is consistent with math being the "greatest indicator of overall model performance" (Section 3.2)—a model's math score is the best single predictor of its average performance across all categories.

#### Task-Level Analysis

Figure 2 (right) shows the Pearson correlation matrix among all 18 tasks. The math_comp task "correlates the highest with the average LiveBench performance" (Section 3.2), suggesting that the modified competition math questions—which are "high-quality, diverse mathematical reasoning questions" (Section 3.2)—are the single best discriminator of overall LLM capability among all tasks. Web_of_lies_v2 and math_comp both show correlations above 0.90 with the overall average (Table 6), while tablereformat shows the lowest correlation (0.684), indicating that table format conversion ability is less predictive of general LLM performance than logical reasoning or mathematical problem-solving.

Figure 3 and Table 7 provide a complementary analysis: for each model, the paper identifies the task on which it most outperforms and underperforms relative to a theoretical model with the same overall score but balanced performance across tasks (computed as the highest and lowest residuals from a best-fit line). Notable outliers include:

- **o1-preview-2024-09-12** best task: connections (word puzzle grouping); worst task: coding_completion. This means that the top-scoring model overall is disproportionately strong at the word puzzle task relative to its average capability, and disproportionately weak at completing partial code solutions compared to what would be predicted from its overall score.

- **o1-mini-2024-09-12** best task: zebra_puzzle; worst task: coding_completion. The model excels at logical deduction puzzles but struggles with code completion—a pattern consistent with o1-mini being optimized for reasoning rather than coding.

- **claude-3-5-sonnet-20241022** best task: coding_completion; worst task: web_of_lies_v2. This model's coding strength is so pronounced that even controlling for its high overall score, it outperforms expectations on code completion while underperforming on the multi-step logical deduction of Web of Lies v2.

- **gemini-1.5-pro-002** best task: olympiad (proof rearrangement); worst task: web_of_lies_v2. The model is relatively strong at mathematical formula ordering but weak at Boolean logic expressed in natural language.

These task-level residuals provide a more nuanced picture than aggregate scores alone: two models with similar overall scores can have very different capability profiles, and the best-fit residual analysis quantifies these differences in a way that is robust to overall capability level.

#### Comparison to External Benchmarks

Section 3.3 and Figures 4 and 6 compare LiveBench to ChatBot Arena (human preference judgments) and Arena-Hard (GPT-4 LLM judge). The Pearson correlation between LiveBench and ChatBot Arena is 0.91, and between LiveBench and Arena-Hard is 0.88—both high but not perfect, indicating that while the benchmarks broadly agree on model quality, there are meaningful disagreements.

Figure 4 (left) shows a dual-axis bar chart comparing LiveBench scores (left axis, 15-55 range) with ChatBot Arena Elo scores (right axis, 1050-1300 range) for 20 models common to both benchmarks. The general trend is consistent (higher LiveBench scores correspond to higher Elo ratings), but the paper highlights specific discrepancies:

- **gemini-1.5-pro-api-0514** and **gemini-1.5-flash-api-0514** both appear to have disproportionately high ChatBot Arena scores relative to their LiveBench performance. The paper hypothesizes that this "may be due to having an output style that is preferred by humans" (Section 3.3)—these models produce responses that human raters like, even if the underlying correctness on objective tasks is not proportionally better.

Figure 4 (right) compares LiveBench to Arena-Hard for 16 models. The paper identifies a different pattern: **gpt-4-0125-preview** and **gpt-4-turbo-2024-04-09** "perform substantially better on Arena-Hard compared to LiveBench" (Section 3.3). The paper attributes this to "the known bias from using gpt-4 itself as the LLM judge"—Arena-Hard uses GPT-4 as its judge, and GPT-4 models may receive inflated scores because the judge (also GPT-4) exhibits self-preference bias. This finding is consistent with Li et al. (2024)'s documentation of LLM self-preference in judging, and it provides concrete evidence that the bias manifests in aggregate benchmark rankings, not just isolated examples.

Figure 6 presents these comparisons as scatterplots with best-fit lines, making outliers visually salient. For the LiveBench vs. Arena-Hard comparison, gpt-4-turbo-2024-04-09 and gpt-4-0125-preview appear noticeably above the best-fit line (higher Arena-Hard scores than LiveBench scores would predict), visually confirming the self-preference hypothesis. On the ChatBot Arena comparison, claude-3.5-sonnet-20240620 (the model dominating coding in LiveBench) appears below the best-fit line—its LiveBench score is higher than its ChatBot Arena Elo would predict—suggesting that human preference judgments do not fully capture the coding capability that objective scoring reveals.

These comparisons are important because they demonstrate that LiveBench is not simply reproducing the rankings of existing benchmarks; it is providing *orthogonal information* that differs systematically in ways the analysis can explain (human style preference, LLM judge self-preference). This supports the paper's core claim that objective ground-truth scoring provides a distinct and valuable signal.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**LLM judge error rates on hard math and reasoning tasks (Appendix A.2, Tables 8–9).** The paper investigates whether an LLM judge (GPT-4-Turbo-2024-04-09) can accurately evaluate model outputs on challenging math (AMC12 2024, AIME 2024, SMC 2023) and reasoning (Zebra Puzzles) tasks. The study evaluates two models: GPT-4-Turbo itself and Claude-3-Opus. The error rate—the fraction of questions where the LLM judge assigns a score different from the ground-truth score—ranges from 0.103 to 0.460 across the four tasks and two models (Table 8). Zebra Puzzles show the highest error rate (0.420 for GPT-4-Turbo judging itself, 0.460 for GPT-4-Turbo judging Claude-3-Opus). AIME 2024 shows the lowest error rate for Claude-3-Opus (0.103) but still 0.214 for GPT-4-Turbo. The paper acknowledges that "the results are not definitive" and that "there may be other experimental setups which could change the result, such as using a more detailed prompt that is tailored to the task of judging hard math and reasoning problems." Nevertheless, these error rates—approaching 50% on the hardest tasks—provide empirical support for the paper's core design choice to avoid LLM judges entirely.

Table 9 compares model performance under ground-truth scoring versus LLM judging, revealing the *direction* of judge errors. For GPT-4-Turbo judging itself on AMC12 2024, the ground-truth score is 54 while the LLM-judged score is 64—an inflation of 10 percentage points. For Zebra Puzzles, the inflation is even more dramatic: 38 under ground truth vs. 68 under LLM judging, a 30-point inflation. The LLM judge is systematically overestimating correctness, not underestimating it—the judge is insufficiently critical of incorrect answers. This directional finding is important because it rules out the possibility that LLM judging is simply noisy with zero-mean error; there is a systematic bias toward false positives.

**Prompt-level scoring approach for Connections task (implicit ablation in task design, Appendix A.3.6).** Prior work by Todd et al. (2024) tested Connections on GPT-family models and found GPT-4 achieves "an overall completion rate below 40%" when allowed multiple retry attempts. LiveBench's single-turn evaluation (no retries) is therefore strictly harder. The paper's decision to use smaller puzzle sizes for some questions (8 words, 2 groups; 12 words, 3 groups; 16 words, 4 groups) acts as an implicit difficulty ablation: the 8-word and 12-word variants are easier than the standard 16-word puzzle, creating a difficulty gradient that prevents floor effects. The paper does not report separate performance by puzzle size, which limits the ability to assess whether the difficulty gradient functions as intended.

**Differential difficulty calibration in the Completion coding task (implicit ablation, Appendix A.3.2).** The paper uses different solution-truncation percentages for easy (30-70%), medium (15%), and hard (15%) LeetCode problems. This design choice is effectively an ablation of solution completeness on task difficulty—giving more context (85% of solution provided) for hard problems and less context (30% of solution provided) for easy problems. The paper does not report performance broken down by problem difficulty level, so the effectiveness of this calibration cannot be independently assessed. However, the fact that claude-3-5-sonnet has a substantial edge in code completion specifically (Section 3.1) suggests the task is discriminating among top models in a way that differs from standard code generation.

**Multi-target vs. single-target queries in Web of Lies v2 (implicit ablation, Appendix A.3.3).** The original Web of Lies task asked for the truth value of one person; v2 asks for three people simultaneously. The paper does not ablate this parameter explicitly, but the decision to make the task multi-target functions as an implicit difficulty-scaling mechanism: getting all three correct requires maintaining consistent logical deductions for multiple targets, and the binary scoring (1 if all three correct, 0 otherwise) means partial logical reasoning gets no credit. The performance of top models on Web of Lies v2 (which correlates 0.914 with overall LiveBench performance, Table 6) confirms the task is difficult enough to be discriminative, but without a single-target ablation, the marginal contribution of the multi-target requirement to difficulty cannot be isolated.

**Hardness variants in Olympiad proof rearrangement (Appendix A.3.1, Table 10–11).** The paper creates three hardness levels by masking 10% (easy), 50% (medium), and 80% (hard) of the equations in each proof. Table 11 shows the average performance across all 34 evaluated models (from preliminary testing in June 2024) breaks down as: Easy 57.48, Medium 29.60, Hard 19.11. The difficulty gradient is steep—performance roughly halves from easy to medium and drops by another third from medium to hard. For GPT-4o specifically, the range spans from 96.8 (easiest) to 36 (hardest). This ablation confirms that the masking percentage effectively controls difficulty, though the paper does not report whether the difficulty ordering is consistent across all models (i.e., whether some models show anomalous patterns like better performance on hard than medium variants).

**Instruction set curation for Instruction Following (Table 3).** The paper uses 16 of IFEval's 25 original verifiable instructions, excluding 9 on the grounds that they "do not reflect real-world use-cases" (Section 2.5). The excluded instructions include Change Cases (all uppercase/lowercase), No Commas, Letter Frequency, Number Placeholder, Choose From, Minimum Number Highlighted Sections, Frequency of All-capital Words, and Response Language. This curation is a form of content ablation: the paper implicitly claims that these 9 instructions would not contribute meaningful signal about practical instruction-following ability. No experiment validates this claim by comparing model rankings with and without the excluded instructions. The paper also composes 2-5 instructions per prompt (sampled from a uniform distribution and deconflicted), creating variation in the number of constraints—but does not report whether performance varies systematically with the number of instructions per prompt, which would be a natural ablation.

**Monthly update stability (Section 3.4).** The rank correlation between the original benchmark and the first monthly update, and between the first and second monthly update, are both reported as > 0.997. This extremely high correlation indicates that model rankings are stable despite 1/6 question turnover—the benchmark is measuring consistent underlying capabilities rather than overfitting to specific questions. Simultaneously, "the median and mean average scores (among models included in all iterations of the leaderboard) have both dropped by about 1.2%," confirming that the difficulty ratchet (prioritizing replacement of the easiest tasks) is functioning. This dual finding—stable rankings but declining scores—is exactly what a well-designed, contamination-resistant benchmark should exhibit under temporal updates: the relative ordering of models remains consistent (reliability), while the absolute difficulty increases as intended (discriminative power preservation).

**Format permissiveness in scoring functions (Appendix A.4–A.5).** While not a formal ablation, the paper describes an iterative quality control process where scoring functions are manually inspected and updated when new models produce valid but unanticipated answer formats. The paper reports that "we made a few such updates when new models came out that hit new edge cases of our scoring functions, in the first month of LiveBench's release, but now we have not needed to make a scoring function update for a few months" (Appendix A.5). This trajectory—initial updates followed by stabilization—suggests the regex-based scoring functions have converged to cover the space of common answer formats across model families, though the paper does not quantify the rate of false negatives before vs. after these updates.

**Comparison between publicly available and private question subsets (implicit design feature, Section 2.7).** The benchmark's architecture includes a private question buffer (1/6 of questions are new each month and not publicly released until one month later), which serves as a contamination firewall. However, the paper does not report separate performance on public vs. private subsets, which would be the most direct test of contamination. If models perform systematically worse on the private subset (which they cannot have memorized) than on the public subset (which has been available for up to 5 months), that would indicate contamination is affecting scores. The absence of this analysis is a notable gap in the experimental validation of the contamination-resistance claim.

### Critical Assessment

#### Does the evidence support the claim that LiveBench is resistant to test set contamination?

The paper provides a strong *architectural argument* for contamination resistance—temporally fresh sources, regular updates, private question buffer, distribution shifting—but limited direct empirical evidence that contamination is actually reduced compared to static benchmarks. The stability of model rankings across updates (rank correlation > 0.997, Section 3.4) is consistent with contamination resistance (if models were memorizing specific questions, replacing those questions should cause larger ranking shifts), but it could also be explained by models being equally good (or equally bad) at both the old and new question distributions—stability is not proof of absence of memorization. The declining mean scores (1.2% drop, Section 3.4) is more diagnostic: if models were memorizing the original questions, they should perform worse on the harder replacement questions, and that is what the data show. However, declining scores could also reflect genuinely harder questions rather than reduced memorization. The paper's transparency about residual contamination risk—"a limited fraction of LiveBench is likely contaminated on all recent LLMs" (Appendix A.7)—is honest but highlights that the benchmark provides *reduced* contamination, not *eliminated* contamination, and the magnitude of the reduction is not quantified.

The missing experiment is a comparison of model performance on questions drawn from before vs. after a known training cutoff date, following the methodology of Roberts et al. (2024) and Jain et al. (2024) with Codeforces problems. If models perform equally well on AMC12 problems from November 2023 (before most training cutoffs) and AIME problems from February 2024 (closer to or after cutoffs) after controlling for difficulty, that would suggest contamination is not driving scores. If there is a discontinuous drop at the training cutoff, that would indicate the AMC12 scores are inflated by memorization. The paper does not attempt this analysis, instead relying on the "prose modification and answer reordering" transformations to break memorization—but the effectiveness of these transformations is not empirically validated. The Srivastava et al. (2024) finding that number-changing modifications to MATH problems still show performance declines on frontier models suggests that surface-level modifications may be less effective at preventing memorization than the paper assumes.

#### Does the evidence support the claim that LiveBench eliminates the biases of LLM judging?

The paper provides compelling evidence that LLM judging introduces systematic biases, and that LiveBench's ground-truth scoring avoids these biases by construction. The LLM judge error rate experiment (Appendix A.2, Tables 8–9) is the strongest direct evidence: GPT-4-Turbo as a judge makes errors on 10-46% of hard math and reasoning questions, with errors systematically biased toward false positives (overestimating correctness). This finding, combined with the literature review of known LLM judge biases (self-preference, verbosity preference, style preference), makes a strong case that *not* using an LLM judge is a design advantage.

The comparison to Arena-Hard (Figure 4, right; Figure 6) provides circumstantial evidence: GPT-4 models are outliers on Arena-Hard relative to LiveBench (appearing above the best-fit line in Figure 6), consistent with the known self-preference bias in GPT-4-based judging. The comparison to ChatBot Arena (Figure 4, left) shows Gemini models performing better on human-preference judgments than LiveBench scores would predict, consistent with a style preference that objective scoring strips away. These pattern-level findings are suggestive rather than definitive—many factors differ between the benchmarks beyond the judging mechanism (question types, difficulty, task composition)—but they are consistent with the claim that objective scoring provides a distinct and importantly different signal from human or LLM preference judgments.

However, the paper does not directly demonstrate that LLM judging would produce *biased rankings on LiveBench questions specifically*. The natural experiment—evaluating the same LiveBench questions using both ground-truth scoring and an LLM judge, then comparing rankings—is not performed (the Appendix A.2 experiment uses a small subset of tasks and only two models). Without this comparison, the claim that LLM judging would be problematic for LiveBench's specific task mixture remains inferential rather than directly demonstrated. The paper essentially argues "LLM judging is biased in general, therefore we avoid it"—which is reasonable but does not quantify the benefit of avoidance in the LiveBench context.

#### Does the evidence support the claim that LiveBench is difficult, with top models achieving below 70% accuracy?

This claim is strongly and straightforwardly supported. The top overall model (o1-preview-2024-09-12) achieves 64.7% (Table 1), with 95% bootstrap confidence intervals that do not overlap with 70%. The second-ranked model achieves 58.5%. Only one model exceeds 70% in any single category (o1-mini-2024-09-12 at 72.3 in reasoning; o1-preview at 74.6 in language, 68.7 in math—Table 1). Across all 40 models in Table 2, the overall scores span from 21.1 (phi-3-mini-4k-instruct) to 64.7 (o1-preview), with a large gap between the top model and even the second tier (58.5 for claude-3-5-sonnet). The benchmark is discriminative throughout this range: there is no clustering or ceiling effect at the top, and the 6-point gap between #1 and #2 is large relative to the 8-point spread from #2 to #15.

The paper also notes that the benchmark is becoming harder over time: the 1.2% score decline between original and most-recent question sets (Section 3.4) suggests the difficulty ratchet is functioning, which would push the top score further below 70% as updates continue. This is a genuine strength—the benchmark is designed to become harder as models improve, maintaining its discriminative power at the frontier.

#### Does the evidence support the claim that LiveBench spans a wide variety of challenging tasks across six categories?

The breadth claim is supported by construction: the paper describes 18 distinct tasks across 6 categories, each with different source materials, generation procedures, required capabilities, and scoring methods. The category-level correlation analysis (Figure 2, left) provides evidence that these categories are not simply measuring a single underlying capability: the weakest correlation (instruction following with other categories) is approximately 0.82, while even the strongest correlations (math-reasoning, math-coding) are approximately 0.95—high but well below 1.0. If all categories measured the same thing, all pairwise correlations would approach 1.0. The task-level correlation matrix (Figure 2, right) shows greater dispersion, with correlations ranging from approximately 0.5 to 1.0, further supporting task diversity.

The task-level residual analysis (Figure 3, Table 7) is the most compelling evidence for diversity: the same model can be "best" at one task and "worst" at another relative to its overall performance, and different models show different patterns of relative strengths and weaknesses. This task-level heterogeneity would not exist if all tasks measured the same latent capability. The radar plot (Figure 1, right) visually confirms this: the shapes of the radar profiles differ across top models—some are strong in coding but weak in reasoning (claude-3-5-sonnet), others show the opposite pattern (o1-mini), and the "ordering of top models varies between each category."

A limitation is that the paper does not provide a formal analysis of how many distinct capability dimensions the benchmark probes. The category structure (6 categories, 18 tasks) is given by design, but factor analysis or principal component analysis of the 40-model × 18-task score matrix would reveal how many statistically independent dimensions of variation exist and whether the task groupings align with the category structure. The correlation matrix suggests at least three clusters (math-coding-reasoning, language-data analysis, instruction following as a distinct dimension), but this is impressionistic rather than quantitative.

#### What experiments would have strengthened the paper?

**Contamination measurement.** A direct contamination test comparing model performance on pre-cutoff vs. post-cutoff questions would substantially strengthen the contamination-resistance claim. The paper has the necessary temporal granularity—questions are drawn from specific dates (AMC12 November 2023, AIME January/February 2024, IMO July 2024, etc.)—and model training cutoff dates are generally known or can be inferred. The Roberts et al. (2024) methodology of plotting performance decline curves across temporal boundaries could be adapted to LiveBench's math competition questions.

**Public vs. private question subset comparison.** Since LiveBench maintains a private buffer of 1/6 questions each month, the paper could report performance on the publicly-available 5/6 vs. the private 1/6. If contamination were occurring through scraping of the public leaderboard, models would perform worse on the private subset. This comparison would directly validate the private buffer mechanism and quantify any contamination effects present in the benchmark's current usage. The absence of this analysis is the most significant gap in the contamination-resistance evidence.

**LLM judge comparison on LiveBench questions.** Running an LLM judge (GPT-4, Claude-3.5) on the full LiveBench question set (not just the small math/reasoning subset in Appendix A.2) and comparing model rankings under LLM judging vs. ground-truth scoring would directly quantify the bias the paper claims to avoid. This experiment would be expensive but highly informative—it would reveal whether the biases documented in the literature manifest on LiveBench's specific task distribution and whether the difference in rankings is large enough to be decision-relevant for model selection.

**Performance breakdown by instruction count for Instruction Following.** The paper composes 2-5 instructions per prompt but does not report whether performance varies with instruction count. This is a natural ablation—do models degrade gracefully as more constraints are added, or is there a sharp drop-off at a certain complexity level? This would provide a more nuanced characterization of instruction-following capability than the aggregate score.

**Difficulty-tier performance for tasks with calibrated difficulty.** Several tasks have explicit difficulty parameters (Olympiad masking percentage, Web of Lies v2 red herring count and multi-target status, Completion truncation percentage, Connections puzzle size, Zebra Puzzle constraint level, AMPS_Hard problem type distribution). The paper generally does not report performance broken down by these difficulty parameters, which limits the informativeness of the aggregate task scores. A model that excels on easy variants but fails on hard variants would have the same aggregate score as a model with the reverse pattern, yet these models have qualitatively different capabilities. Reporting difficulty-tier performance would make the benchmark more diagnostic.

**Test-retest reliability.** Running the same models multiple times (with different random seeds for tasks involving stochastic generation, or simply evaluating stability of rankings across the two monthly updates already completed) and reporting the correlation would quantify the measurement precision of the benchmark. The rank correlation > 0.997 across updates captures stability across *different question sets*, not measurement reliability within a fixed question set. For temperature-0 evaluation, within-set reliability should be near-perfect, but this is worth verifying.

**Functional form of the difficulty-time relationship.** The paper notes that the benchmark is becoming harder (1.2% score decline across two updates), but does not analyze the trajectory. Is difficulty increasing linearly with update count? Will the difficulty ratchet eventually push questions beyond what any model can solve (floor effects), or will it asymptotically approach some difficulty ceiling? Understanding the functional form would help users interpret whether score declines over time reflect genuinely increasing difficulty or diminishing returns from the update mechanism.

#### Genuine weaknesses

**Small sample sizes for per-task analysis.** Many tasks have only 40-50 questions. The Olympiad task has 36 (across three hardness variants from a smaller number of proof sources). The instruction following subtasks have 50 each. With 40 models evaluated, the effective sample size for computing per-task correlations and model rankings is modest. The paper reports 95% bootstrap confidence intervals for aggregate scores (Figure 1, left; Figure 5), which partially addresses this concern by showing the uncertainty in the point estimates, but per-task confidence intervals could be quite wide for 40-50 question tasks.

**No temporal cutoff date analysis for contamination.** This is the single largest missing experiment. The paper's contamination-resistance argument relies on temporal freshness, but it does not empirically demonstrate that temporal freshness translates to reduced memorization. Given that the paper has questions spanning from November 2023 to mid-2024 and models with known training cutoff dates throughout this period, this analysis is feasible and would directly test the core premise.

**Single-turn evaluation only.** The paper evaluates models under a strict single-turn, temperature-0 protocol with no retries. While this produces clean, reproducible measurements, it may systematically underestimate certain models' capabilities. Some models (especially those optimized for interactive use) may perform substantially better when allowed multiple attempts, chain-of-thought refinement, or tool use. The paper's scores represent a specific capability—zero-shot, single-turn, no-external-tools performance—that is not equivalent to "what the model can do under optimal conditions." This is a valid design choice but limits the generalizability of the rankings to real-world usage scenarios where multi-turn interaction, tool use, and best-of-N sampling are common.

**Model selection and version management.** The paper evaluates 40 models but acknowledges that "we maintain about two versions of each model family on the leaderboard (to show the improvement from the most recent version) but no more" (Section 2.7). This curation policy means the leaderboard is not a complete enumeration of available models—it is a deliberately pruned subset designed to show trends within model families while keeping evaluation costs tractable. A model that is the best in its family but not one of the two most recent versions might be absent from the leaderboard, which could mislead users about which specific checkpoint is optimal. The paper is transparent about this policy, but users should understand that the leaderboard is curated, not comprehensive.

**English-only and text-only.** LiveBench contains only English-language tasks and text-only inputs. The paper acknowledges that "we hope to add non-English language tasks in the future" (Section 5). This is a significant limitation for evaluating models like the Qwen series that are explicitly multilingual or models like Gemini that support multimodal inputs. A model's LiveBench score reflects its English-language, text-only capabilities and may not correlate with its performance on non-English tasks or multimodal inputs. Given the paper's emphasis on diversity, the absence of non-English and multimodal tasks represents a genuine gap in capability coverage.

**The difficulty target is loosely enforced.** The paper aims for 30-70% accuracy on top models "loosely" (Section 2), and individual tasks may fall outside this range for particular models. The Olympiad task, for example, shows scores ranging from 96.8 (GPT-4o on easiest variant) to 36 (GPT-4o on hardest, from preliminary testing in Table 11), meaning some sub-variants have ceiling or near-ceiling effects. The paper does not report per-task difficulty calibration relative to the 30-70% target for the full 40-model leaderboard, so it is unclear how many task-model combinations fall outside the desired discriminative range.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Difficulty Estimation Cost Is Unaccounted for and Potentially Dominates the Benchmark's Practical Expense

**The assumption or constraint.** LiveBench's contamination resistance relies on maintaining a 1/6 private question buffer and a monthly update cadence that requires generating, validating, and scoring new questions. While the paper provides a clear sustainability plan—limiting the leaderboard to 40–50 models, evaluating approximately 200 new questions per model per month, and relying on community contributions (Section 2.7)—the actual cost of *creating and validating* the benchmark itself is not quantified alongside the inference costs in Tables 15 and 16. The paper acknowledges that most tasks can be updated automatically (Table 14: 16 of 18 tasks), but some tasks require manual effort (Spatial Reasoning: "No - create new questions by hand"; Olympiad: source material only updates yearly). More critically, the paper does not discuss the cost of the *quality control* process: manually inspecting model outputs for false negatives (Appendix A.5), iteratively updating regex-based scoring functions, deconflicting instruction-following constraints, and verifying that synthetically generated puzzles have unique solutions.

**The consequence.** The reported computational costs in Table 16 (~$0.90 to $47.87 per model for a full evaluation as of October 2024) represent only the *inference* cost of running models on existing questions. The total cost of maintaining the benchmark—human labor for question design, scoring function development, quality control, and community management—is not included anywhere. For an organization considering adopting or replicating the LiveBench framework, these unaccounted costs are potentially significant: the paper states that the authors' institutions can handle the computational burden, but the labor burden is distributed across multiple institutions (Abacus.AI, NYU, Nvidia, UMD, USC, Columbia) with an author list of 18 people. A smaller organization or individual researcher attempting to maintain a similar benchmark would face proportionally larger overhead. Furthermore, the paper does not discuss what happens if the primary maintainers reduce their involvement—the sustainability plan (Section 2.7) assumes continued institutional support and community engagement that may not materialize indefinitely.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides detailed per-model inference costs (Tables 15–16) but does not provide any estimate of the labor or computational cost of question creation, validation, or scoring function maintenance. The quality control process is described qualitatively (Appendix A.4–A.5) but not costed. The sustainability discussion (Section 2.7) is aspirational—"we have a plan in place to ensure its continued success"—without concrete commitments or fallback mechanisms if community contributions or institutional support diminish.

**Mitigation status.** The paper partially addresses computational sustainability through the curated leaderboard strategy (Section 2.7) and notes that community contributions reduce author burden, but it does not address the labor sustainability question at all. The acknowledgment that 16 of 18 tasks can be updated automatically with scripts (Table 14) implies that ongoing question creation is relatively cheap, but the paper also notes that "we always modify the generation script when creating new questions, in order to make sure that the distribution of questions is different and harder over time" (Appendix A.6)—meaning that the scripts themselves require active development, not just re-execution. The paper does not suggest future work on this topic.

---

### Single-Turn, Temperature-0 Evaluation Systematically Underscores Certain Model Capabilities

**The assumption or constraint.** LiveBench evaluates all models under a strict single-turn protocol with temperature 0: "For all models and tasks, we perform single-turn evaluation with temperature 0, unless otherwise noted in the model card" (Section 3). This means each model gets exactly one generation per question, with no opportunity for multi-turn refinement, no best-of-N sampling, no chain-of-thought self-correction, and no tool use. The paper acknowledges this implicitly in several places—the Connections task is noted to be harder than prior work because prior work allowed multiple retry attempts (Appendix A.3.6: "GPT-4 has an overall completion rate below 40% on the puzzles (when allowed multiple tries to get it correct)"), and the coding tasks use pass@1 rather than pass@k as is common in code generation benchmarks.

**The consequence.** This evaluation protocol measures a specific capability—zero-shot, single-turn, no-external-tools performance—that may not reflect how these models are used in practice. Several model families are explicitly designed for interactive, multi-turn use; others are optimized for chain-of-thought reasoning that benefits from multiple attempts or verification. The o1 model series (o1-preview, o1-mini), for example, uses internal reasoning chains that may not be fully expressed in a single-turn format. Models that are strong at self-correction, iterative refinement, or using external tools (code execution, search) are penalized relative to models that produce correct answers on their first attempt. The paper's temperature-0 setting also eliminates the diversity benefits of sampling—for tasks where best-of-N would substantially improve performance (e.g., math competitions where the model sometimes makes arithmetic errors), the benchmark measures a lower bound on capability.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper does not run any ablation comparing single-turn vs. multi-turn or temperature-0 vs. temperature > 0 evaluation. The discussion of the Connections task (Appendix A.3.6) notes that prior work with multiple attempts achieved below 40%, while LiveBench's single-turn evaluation is "therefore more challenging"—but this is a design choice, not a limitation acknowledged and mitigated. The paper does not discuss whether model rankings would change under multi-turn or sampling-based evaluation.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper presents the single-turn, temperature-0 protocol as a fixed part of the benchmark design without discussing its implications for the validity of model rankings or suggesting future work to study the effect of evaluation protocol on rankings. This is a deliberate simplification that trades evaluation fidelity for reproducibility, but the tradeoff is not explicitly discussed. A practitioner using LiveBench scores to select a model for a deployment that involves multi-turn interaction or sampling-based generation should be aware that the benchmark may not predict relative performance in that setting.

---

### The Objective-Scoring Constraint Excludes Entire Categories of Practically Important Capabilities

**The assumption or constraint.** LiveBench's foundational design principle is that "we only including questions that have an objectively correct answer" (Section 1), scored automatically "according to objective ground-truth values without the use of an LLM judge" (Section 1). This constraint is what enables contamination-resistant, bias-free evaluation. However, it also imposes a hard boundary on what the benchmark can evaluate: any task where "correctness" is subjective, multidimensional, or inherently open-ended is excluded. The paper explicitly acknowledges this: "while ground truth scoring is beneficial in many ways, it still cannot be used for certain use cases, such as 'write a travel guide to Hawaii' in which it is hard to define a ground truth" (Section 5).

**The consequence.** LiveBench provides no signal about several capabilities that are central to real-world LLM deployment: open-ended generation quality (creative writing, dialogue, explanation), summarization quality (beyond instruction-following adherence), translation quality, helpfulness/harmlessness, factual accuracy of generated claims (beyond tasks where the ground truth is directly extractable), and reasoning about ambiguous or underspecified problems. A model could score highly on LiveBench—demonstrating strong mathematical reasoning, coding ability, and instruction following—while producing unhelpful, verbose, or factually incorrect responses in open-ended settings. Conversely, a model optimized for helpfulness and safety might underperform on LiveBench's objective tasks relative to a model optimized purely for accuracy.

This limitation is particularly significant because many of the most common LLM use cases (chatbots, writing assistants, summarization tools) involve precisely the kinds of open-ended generation that LiveBench explicitly excludes. A user selecting a model based on LiveBench scores for a chatbot deployment would be choosing based on a signal that is orthogonal to several dimensions of chatbot quality. The paper's correlation analysis with ChatBot Arena (Section 3.3, Figure 4 left) partially addresses this: the 0.91 correlation suggests that objective and subjective evaluations broadly agree, but the 0.09 of unexplained variance includes exactly the capabilities that LiveBench does not measure.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The design constraint is explicit and the acknowledgment is candid (Section 5). The correlation with ChatBot Arena (0.91) and Arena-Hard (0.88) provides evidence that LiveBench scores do correlate with broader quality measures, and the outlier analysis (Figure 6) identifies models where the correlation breaks down—notably, Gemini models perform better on ChatBot Arena (human preference) than LiveBench scores would predict, and GPT-4 models perform better on Arena-Hard (GPT-4 judge) than LiveBench scores would predict. These discrepancies are consistent with the hypothesis that LiveBench measures a subset of capabilities that does not fully capture human preference or LLM-judged quality.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges the limitation but treats it as a necessary tradeoff rather than a problem to be solved. There is no proposal for extending the framework to subjective tasks while maintaining resistance to judging bias. The paper suggests adding non-English language tasks in future work (Section 5) but does not discuss the possibility of incorporating subjective evaluation dimensions. This is a genuinely hard problem—how to evaluate open-ended generation without introducing the very judging biases LiveBench was designed to avoid—and the paper's position that objective scoring is worth the scope restriction is defensible. However, users of the benchmark must understand that a high LiveBench score certifies capability on objectively-answerable tasks, not overall LLM quality as experienced by human users.

### The Private Question Buffer and Monthly Update Mechanism Create a Tension Between Transparency and Contamination Resistance

**The assumption or constraint.** LiveBench's contamination-resistance strategy includes withholding newly created questions from public release for one month: "Each month, we do not release the new questions until one month later, so that the public leaderboard always has 1/6 questions that are private" (Section 2.7). Additionally, the question generation code is not publicly released: "we do not publicly release any of the code used to generate LiveBench questions" (Appendix A.7). These decisions are explicitly designed to prevent exact test set contamination—if model developers cannot access the questions or the code that generates them, they cannot include them in training data.

**The consequence.** The privacy mechanisms that protect against contamination also limit independent verification of the benchmark's fairness and correctness. A third party cannot fully audit the question generation process for systematic biases (e.g., whether certain question distributions favor particular model families), cannot independently verify that the ground-truth answers are correct, and cannot reproduce the benchmark from scratch. The paper provides the code for *running and scoring* models on existing questions, and releases all questions after a one-month delay, but the gap between "we release all questions, code, and model answers" (Section 1) and "we do not publicly release the generation code" (Appendix A.7) is significant: a researcher cannot generate a fresh set of questions to verify that the benchmark's properties (difficulty distribution, model rankings, correlation structure) are stable across different random seeds or source material selections.

This tension is inherent to the contamination-resistance goal. If the generation code were public, an adversary could use it to generate unlimited training data matching the benchmark's distribution, defeating the temporal-freshness advantage. But the lack of generation code means the benchmark's properties cannot be independently validated, and the community must trust the authors' quality control processes. This is the same tradeoff that makes the SEAL benchmark (Scale AI, 2024) less transparent than fully open benchmarks—privacy buys contamination resistance at the cost of verifiability.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper explicitly states both the privacy policy (Section 2.7, Appendix A.7) and the rationale: preventing test set contamination and task contamination through overfitting to the generation distribution. The rank correlation stability across monthly updates (>0.997, Section 3.4) provides partial evidence that the benchmark is measuring consistent capabilities rather than artifacts of the generation process, since the generation scripts are modified with each update and the rankings remain stable. The paper also open-sources all questions after a one-month delay, meaning the community can audit individual questions and answers (just not the process that produced them).

**Mitigation status.** Partially addressed through the delayed public release of questions. The paper does not propose mechanisms for independent verification of the generation process (e.g., trusted third-party auditing, cryptographic commitments, or periodic release of deprecated generation scripts). The community contribution model (Section 2.7, Section 5) provides a partial solution: external contributors can propose new tasks, and their generation methodology can be publicly documented even if the specific instance-generation parameters are not. However, for the core tasks maintained by the authors, verification relies entirely on trust in the authors' processes and on the empirical stability of results across question sets.

### The Benchmark's Difficulty and Coverage Are Heterogeneous Across Tasks, Creating Imbalanced Diagnostic Power

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper states that tasks are designed "to have 40-100 questions which span a range of difficulty, from easy to very challenging, while loosely aiming for an overall 30-70% success rate on the top models for each task" (Section 2). However, the difficulty calibration is described as "loose," and the paper does not report per-task accuracy distributions or verify that all tasks fall within the 30-70% discriminative range for the full model set.

**The consequence.** Some tasks may have ceiling effects (too easy, all top models score near 100%) or floor effects (too hard, all models score near 0%), making them non-diagnostic for distinguishing among models of interest. The preliminary Olympiad results (Table 11, from earlier testing) illustrate this: GPT-4o scored 96.8 on the easiest variant (10% masked)—a near-ceiling score that would not distinguish GPT-4o from an even stronger model. Conversely, some models scored near single digits on the hardest variant (80% masked), where floor effects make it impossible to distinguish a moderately capable model from a completely incapable one. The paper does not report whether similar ceiling or floor effects exist in the current 40-model leaderboard.

More broadly, the benchmark's six categories are weighted equally in the final LiveBench score (average of six category averages, Section 3), but the categories differ substantially in how many tasks they contain (math: 3 tasks, coding: 2 tasks, reasoning: 3 tasks, data analysis: 3 tasks, instruction following: 4 tasks, language: 3 tasks) and in total question count (math has 232 questions from 3 tasks; instruction following has 200 from 4 tasks; language has 140 from 3 tasks). This equal-category-weighting design means that a model's performance on a category with few questions (e.g., coding: 128 questions across 2 tasks) has the same influence on the final score as a category with many questions (e.g., instruction following: 200 questions across 4 tasks), but the per-category score estimates have different statistical precision. The paper reports 95% bootstrap confidence intervals for the overall score (Figure 1 left, Figure 5) but does not discuss whether the confidence interval width varies systematically by category due to differing question counts.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 13 provides question counts per task, showing substantial variation: LCB_generation has 78 questions, Olympiad has 36, Plot Unscrambling has 40, the instruction following subtasks have 50 each, AMPS_Hard has 100. The Olympiad preliminary results (Tables 10–11) explicitly show ceiling effects on easy variants and floor effects on hard variants for some models, but these results are from June 2024 and do not include the full set of current leaderboard models. The 30-70% difficulty target is stated but its achievement is not verified per task.

**Mitigation status.** The paper partially addresses the difficulty calibration issue through the ongoing update mechanism: "we choose tasks to update based primarily on two factors: (1) the oldest tasks, and (2) the currently easiest tasks. In this way, the questions in LiveBench will stay new and continue to challenge the most capable LLMs" (Section 2.7). This implies that tasks with ceiling effects will be prioritized for replacement with harder variants. The 1.2% score decline across updates (Section 3.4) provides evidence that the difficulty ratchet is functioning at the aggregate level. However, the paper does not discuss floor effects (tasks where all models score near zero), which the difficulty ratchet mechanism would not address—making already-hard tasks even harder would worsen floor effects, not eliminate them. The paper does not propose a mechanism for identifying and recalibrating tasks with floor effects, nor does it discuss whether tasks with very low model performance should be retained as aspirational targets or modified to become more discriminative.
