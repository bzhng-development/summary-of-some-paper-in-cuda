# TAPS: Task Aware Proposal Distributions for Speculative Sampling

**ArXiv:** [2603.27027](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.27027)

## 🎯 Pitch

Speculative decoding speedup collapses when your draft model was trained on generic data but your workload is math—or vice versa. This paper shows that matching the draft’s training domain to the target task doubles acceptance length over mismatched pairs, and that keeping specialized drafters separate and combining them at inference time wildly beats merging their weights.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper studies how the training distribution of lightweight draft models affects speculative decoding quality, evaluating HASS and EAGLE-2 drafters trained on MathInstruct, ShareGPT, and mixed-data variants against a fixed Llama-3-8B-Instruct verifier on MT-Bench, GSM8K, MATH-500, and SVAMP. The core finding is that **task-specific training** produces clear domain specialization—MathInstruct-trained drafts dominate reasoning benchmarks while ShareGPT-trained drafts dominate conversational benchmarks—and that when multiple specialists are available, **inference-time composition** via confidence-based routing or **merged-tree verification** substantially outperforms naive weight-space averaging, with merged-tree verification achieving the highest acceptance length overall (5.11 for HASS at temperature 0, compared to 2.59 for checkpoint averaging). The paper establishes that draft training distribution matters at least as much as draft architecture for proposal quality, and that specialized drafters should be kept separate and composed at inference time rather than merged in parameter space.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: We Don't Know How Draft Training Data Affects Speculative Decoding

Speculative decoding (Leviathan et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2023) addresses one of the central bottlenecks in large language model inference: the autoregressive generation process where each token must be produced sequentially because it depends on all previously generated tokens. The standard approach—running the full target model once per token—is computationally expensive and latency-bound, particularly for long sequences. Speculative decoding circumvents this by introducing a smaller, faster **draft model** that proposes several future tokens at once, which the larger **target model** then verifies in a single parallel pass. The key theoretical property is that this procedure is **lossless**: the acceptance rule (Equation 1 in the paper) guarantees that the final output distribution is identical to what the target model would produce under standard autoregressive decoding, regardless of the draft model's quality.

However, the practical effectiveness of speculative decoding depends entirely on **acceptance length**—how many consecutive draft tokens the target model approves per verification call. A draft model that proposes tokens the target model would likely disagree with produces short accepted sequences, yielding minimal speedup. The draft model's job, therefore, is to approximate the target model's next-token behavior as closely as possible, subject to being substantially smaller and faster.

The gap this paper identifies is straightforward but has been largely overlooked: **the draft model is almost always trained on broad generic corpora like ShareGPT**, which contains diverse conversational data, with little systematic investigation of how this training distribution choice affects acceptance length on specific downstream workloads. Prior work has focused overwhelmingly on improving the draft architecture (EAGLE, EAGLE-2, EAGLE-3, HASS; Li et al., 2024a,b, 2025; Zhang et al., 2025) or the verification procedure (tree verification, self-speculative decoding, hierarchical drafting, cascaded drafters, retrieval-assisted proposals; Miao et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024; Elhoushi et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024; He et al., 2024), treating the drafter as a generic component whose training data is incidental rather than a design choice in its own right.

The paper argues this gap matters for two concrete reasons (Section 1):

1. **Alignment sensitivity is unmeasured.** If a draft model trained on conversational data is deployed alongside a verifier on a mathematical reasoning workload, acceptance length may degrade substantially even though the speculative decoding algorithm and architecture are unchanged. The system designer has no principled guidance on whether this degradation occurs, how large it is, or how to mitigate it.

2. **The ecosystem increasingly offers multiple specialized checkpoints.** The open-weight model landscape (Sun et al., 2025) now provides multiple specialized model variants—some fine-tuned for math, others for chat, others for code. A practitioner with access to several such checkpoints faces a practical question with no established answer: should they combine these specialists by mixing their training data, merging them in weight space (e.g., via model averaging; Ilharco et al., 2022), or composing them dynamically at inference time?

### Why This Problem Matters

The practical significance of this question is tied to the economics of LLM deployment. Speculative decoding is one of the most widely adopted lossless acceleration techniques, but its speedup is multiplicative: the wall-clock improvement is proportional to average acceptance length. If a mismatched draft training distribution reduces acceptance length from, say, 5.0 to 3.0 tokens per verifier call, that represents a ~40% throughput reduction that architecture improvements alone cannot recover. Understanding this dependency is therefore directly relevant to deployment efficiency.

Beyond throughput, the question has implications for how we think about draft models as system components. If draft training distribution strongly affects acceptance length, then the drafter is not a fixed auxiliary module that can be trained once on generic data and deployed everywhere. Instead, it becomes a **task-dependent component** that should be selected or adapted based on the deployment workload—analogous to how retrieval-augmented generation systems select different retrieval corpora per domain, or how mixture-of-experts models route inputs to different expert subsets (Mu and Lin, 2026). This reframing has downstream consequences for model training pipelines, checkpoint management, and inference serving infrastructure.

The theoretical significance is more subtle: the paper provides some of the first systematic evidence that **proposal quality in speculative decoding is a joint function of draft architecture and draft training distribution**, not architecture alone. This is conceptually parallel to findings in other areas of ML—for example, that data quality and distribution matter at least as much as model architecture for downstream task performance—but the connection had not been empirically established for speculative decoding specifically.

### Where Prior Approaches Fall Short

The paper identifies specific limitations in the existing speculative decoding literature along three dimensions:

**Architecture-centric development.** The dominant research trajectory in speculative decoding has been improving the draft model's representational capacity and inference efficiency. EAGLE (Li et al., 2024a) introduced feature-level drafting where the draft model predicts the target model's hidden states rather than tokens directly, achieving better alignment. EAGLE-2 (Li et al., 2024b) replaced fixed draft trees with context-dependent dynamic trees that adapt the tree structure per input. EAGLE-3 (Li et al., 2025) further scaled the approach with training-time test. HASS (Zhang et al., 2025) addressed objective mismatch (via Top-K distillation that focuses learning on the verifier's most likely tokens, Equation 7) and context mismatch (via harmonized context alignment where later draft predictions are trained on imperfect draft features rather than clean target features, Equation 8). These are substantial architectural innovations, but they all train drafters on generic data (typically ShareGPT) and evaluate on similarly generic benchmarks, leaving the training distribution as an uncontrolled variable.

**Verification-centric development.** Another line of work has focused on making verification more efficient or more capable. Tree verification (Miao et al., 2024) allows the verifier to check multiple draft paths in parallel, increasing proposal diversity per call. Self-speculative decoding (Zhang et al., 2024; Elhoushi et al., 2024) eliminates the separate draft model entirely by using early exits from the target model itself. Hierarchical drafting (Sun et al., 2024) uses multiple drafters at different scales. Cascaded drafters (Chen et al., 2024) chain multiple speculative stages. Retrieval-assisted proposals (He et al., 2024) use a retrieval corpus to inform the drafter. These methods improve the verification pipeline, but they do not address the question of what the draft model should be trained on.

**No systematic study of training distribution effects.** Prior to this paper, no published work had systematically varied the draft training distribution while holding architecture and verifier fixed, measured the effect on acceptance length across workloads of different types, and characterized how the mismatch manifests (e.g., which benchmark families are affected, at what speculative depths, and whether the effect interacts with decoding temperature). The paper's research questions (RQ1–RQ5) are designed to fill exactly this gap.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper positions itself not as proposing a new drafting architecture or verification procedure, but as **characterizing a previously uncontrolled variable** in the speculative decoding pipeline: the draft training distribution. It does this through a controlled experimental design that holds fixed the verifier (Llama-3-8B-Instruct), the draft architectures (HASS and EAGLE-2 with identical lightweight backbones: 1 transformer layer, hidden size 4096, ~0.8B parameters), the training hyperparameters (20 epochs, learning rate $3 \times 10^{-5}$, batch size 8), the evaluation benchmarks (MT-Bench for conversational, GSM8K/MATH-500/SVAMP for mathematical reasoning), and the acceptance metric (average acceptance length under lossless verification).

What varies across experiments is only the **draft training data** (MathInstruct for mathematical specialization, ShareGPT for conversational specialization, or mixtures of both) and the **composition strategy** for combining multiple specialized drafts (weight averaging, confidence routing, merged-tree verification).

This controlled setup allows the paper to make causal claims about the effect of training distribution that would be confounded in a less systematic comparison. For example, observing that MathInstruct-trained drafts outperform ShareGPT-trained drafts on GSM8K under both HASS and EAGLE-2 (Table 1, RQ1) provides convergent evidence that the effect is driven by training data, not by idiosyncratic interactions with a particular architecture.

The paper's relationship to prior work on model merging (Ilharco et al., 2022) is explicitly contrastive. Model averaging techniques have been shown to combine capabilities from different fine-tuned checkpoints in weight space, and a natural hypothesis would be that averaging a math-specialized and chat-specialized draft model yields a single draft model that performs well on both workloads. The paper tests this hypothesis directly (RQ3, Figure 6) and finds it false: checkpoint averaging performs poorly (average acceptance length 2.34–2.62), while inference-time composition via confidence routing or merged trees is substantially stronger (4.63–5.11 at temperature 0). This negative result is practically important because it tells practitioners that the straightforward approach they might reach for does not work for this problem.

The paper also positions its merged-tree verification approach (Section "Inference-Time Composition") as distinct from prior tree verification work. Standard tree verification packs multiple candidate paths from a single draft model into a verifier call. Merged-tree verification instead packs candidates from **two independently trained specialists** into a single verifier call, while preserving each subtree's internal ancestry and preventing cross-subtree attention (Algorithm 1, Figure 4, Figure 5). The key insight is that the verifier can evaluate both specialists' proposals in one parallel pass without either specialist's candidates influencing the other's, and the lossless guarantee is preserved because the verifier sees exactly the same conditional distributions it would see for each subtree independently (Proposition A.2 in Appendix A.4). This tests whether increased proposal diversity from multiple specialists outweighs the cost of processing a larger tree.

Finally, the paper explicitly acknowledges the boundaries of its contribution, noting that it evaluates one target model, two source domains, two speculative backbones, and four benchmarks, with acceptance length as the primary metric—not end-to-end latency or throughput. This scoping is appropriate for an initial characterization of a previously unstudied variable, and it defines the space where future work can extend the findings to more models, domains, and deployment metrics.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

This paper is primarily an **empirical analysis** that systematically characterizes how a draft model's training distribution affects speculative decoding quality, and evaluates three strategies for combining multiple specialized drafters at inference time. The core idea is that speculative decoding performance—measured by acceptance length—is a joint function of the draft architecture *and* the match between the draft's training data and the downstream workload, and that when multiple specialized drafts are available, keeping them separate and composing them at inference time (via confidence-based routing or merged-tree verification) is substantially more effective than combining them in weight space.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The system under study has four fixed components and one variable component:

1. **Target Model (Verifier)** — a fixed Llama-3-8B-Instruct model that performs lossless verification using the standard speculative acceptance rule. It never changes across any experiment.
2. **Draft Model Architecture** — one of two fixed backbones (HASS or EAGLE-2), each implemented as a lightweight LLaMA-style decoder (1 transformer layer, hidden size 4096, ~0.8B parameters). The architecture is fixed; only the training data and composition strategy vary.
3. **Training Data** — the variable input that defines domains: MathInstruct (mathematical reasoning), ShareGPT (conversational generation), or mixtures of both in varying proportions.
4. **Draft Checkpoints** — trained variants of the draft model produced by the training data. These include single-domain checkpoints (MathInstruct only, ShareGPT only), mixed-data checkpoints (35k+35k, 70k+70k), and an averaged checkpoint (equal-weight parameter interpolation between the two single-domain checkpoints).
5. **Inference-Time Composition Module** — two strategies (confidence routing and merged-tree verification) that operate on the single-domain checkpoints *after* training, selecting or combining their proposals before passing candidates to the verifier.

Information flows as follows: a prompt enters the system → the draft model (or composition module) generates a candidate tree of future tokens → the draft tree is presented to the target model → the target model computes its own next-token probabilities for each position in the tree → the standard speculative acceptance rule (Equation 1) accepts or rejects each token sequentially → accepted tokens are committed to the output sequence, and any rejected token triggers a correction sample from the residual distribution (Equation 2). The acceptance length—the number of consecutively accepted tokens per verifier call—is the primary metric.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the speculative decoding acceptance rule (Equations 1–2), since it is the fixed mechanism that converts draft proposals into verifier-approved output and defines what "acceptance length" means.
- **Second**, the two draft backbones (EAGLE-2 and HASS), covering their training objectives and how they produce candidate token trees, because the backbone determines *how* the draft model generates proposals and what training signals it responds to.
- **Third**, the draft training variants and data mixture configurations, since these are the independent variables that distinguish the seven main draft checkpoints studied.
- **Fourth**, the inference-time composition strategies—checkpoint averaging, confidence routing, and merged-tree verification—since these are the methods tested for combining specialized drafters without retraining.
- **Fifth**, the evaluation setup, metrics, and experimental design logic that connects the research questions to specific comparisons in Table 1.

#### The Speculative Decoding Acceptance Rule

Speculative decoding is a lossless acceleration technique that lets a lightweight draft model propose multiple future tokens which a larger target model then checks in parallel. The procedure guarantees that the final output distribution is identical to what the target model would produce under standard autoregressive decoding—regardless of how good or bad the draft model's proposals are. The acceptance rule is the mathematical mechanism that enforces this guarantee.

**The setup.** Given a prefix of already-generated tokens `$x_{1:n}$`, the draft model autoregressively generates `$K$` candidate future tokens `$\tilde{x}_{n+1}, \tilde{x}_{n+2}, \ldots, \tilde{x}_{n+K}$`. The target model then scores all `$K+1$` positions in one parallel forward pass, computing its own next-token probabilities `$q(\cdot \mid x_{1:n+t-1})$` for each position `$t = 1, \ldots, K$` (where the conditioning includes all previously *committed* tokens plus the draft tokens that were accepted at earlier positions in this verification step).

**The acceptance probability.** For each draft token `$\tilde{x}_{n+t}$` at position `$t$`, the verifier accepts it with probability:

$$\alpha_{n+t} = \min\left(1, \frac{q(\tilde{x}_{n+t} \mid x_{1:n+t-1})}{p(\tilde{x}_{n+t} \mid x_{1:n+t-1})}\right)$$

where `$q(\tilde{x}_{n+t} \mid x_{1:n+t-1})$` is the target model's probability for that token given the current prefix, and `$p(\tilde{x}_{n+t} \mid x_{1:n+t-1})$` is the draft model's probability for the same token under the same conditioning.

**What it computes:** the acceptance probability is the ratio of the target model's probability to the draft model's probability, capped at 1. If the draft model assigned a *lower* probability than the target model to that token (the target is more confident), the ratio exceeds 1 and the token is accepted with probability 1. If the draft model assigned a *higher* probability (the draft was over-confident relative to the target), the ratio is less than 1 and the token may be rejected with probability `$1 - \alpha_{n+t}$`. The key property is that tokens the target model finds more likely than the draft did are always accepted, while tokens the draft was too confident about are stochastically filtered.

**Why this form:** the `$\min(1, q/p)$` rule is the standard rejection sampling correction that transforms samples from the draft distribution `$p$` into samples from the target distribution `$q$`. The mathematical guarantee is that the marginal probability of accepting a particular token `$x$` at position `$n+t$`, averaged over the draft's sampling and the acceptance randomness, equals `$q(x \mid \text{prefix})$`. This holds regardless of how far `$p$` deviates from `$q$`—though larger deviations increase the rejection rate and reduce efficiency.

**The rejection correction.** If a draft token is rejected at position `$n+t$`, the system does not simply fall back to the target model's most likely token. Instead, it samples a replacement token from the residual distribution:

$$r(x) \propto \max\left(0, q(x \mid x_{1:n+t-1}) - p(x \mid x_{1:n+t-1})\right)$$

where `$r(x)$` is the probability (after normalization) of sampling token `$x$` as the correction, and the `$\max(0, \cdot)$` operation ensures only tokens where the target model is *more* confident than the draft model are eligible. All subsequent draft tokens beyond position `$n+t$` are discarded, the corrected token is committed, and decoding resumes from the new prefix.

**What it computes:** the residual distribution `$r(x)$` measures, for each token in the vocabulary, how much *more* probability the target model assigns to it than the draft model did. Tokens where the target is less confident than the draft (`$q - p < 0$`) are excluded. The remaining positive mass is renormalized, and a token is sampled from this corrected distribution.

**Why this form:** the residual sampling correction is the mathematically necessary step to maintain the lossless guarantee after a rejection. Simply using the target model's distribution after rejection would be biased because the fact that rejection occurred already tells us the draft token was not accepted. The residual distribution correctly accounts for this conditioning: it is the target's distribution restricted to tokens where the draft *under*-estimated probability, which are exactly the tokens that could have caused the rejection.

**Acceptance length.** The paper's primary metric is acceptance length: the average number of consecutively accepted draft tokens per verifier call, measured over the evaluation distribution. Higher acceptance length means the draft model's proposals align more closely with what the target model would have generated, which translates directly to higher throughput (fewer verifier calls per output token). The metric is reported as `$\mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}}[A(x; M_D, M_T)]$` where `$A(x; M_D, M_T)$` is the number of consecutively accepted tokens for input `$x$` using draft model `$M_D$` and target model `$M_T$`, as defined (implicitly) in Equation 10.

#### EAGLE-2 Drafting Backbone

EAGLE-2 (Li et al., 2024b) is a feature-level drafting architecture that operates differently from standard token-level autoregressive drafters. Instead of predicting tokens directly, EAGLE-2 predicts the target model's **hidden states**—the representations from the target model's second-to-last transformer layer—and then maps those predicted features to token probabilities through the target model's own language modeling head.

**Why feature-level drafting?** The intuition is that the target model's hidden states contain richer information about upcoming tokens than the tokens themselves. At position `$t$`, the target model's feature vector `$h_t$` encodes not just the most likely next token but the full distribution over the vocabulary, including second-choice alternatives, semantic constraints, and syntactic expectations. By predicting `$h_t$` rather than tokens, the draft model can leverage this richer signal and potentially achieve better alignment with the target model's behavior. The overhead is modest because the target's LM head (a single linear projection from hidden size to vocabulary size) is applied only to the predicted features, not to the full transformer stack.

**Draft model formulation.** Let `$h_t$` denote the target model's feature vector at position `$t$` (from the second-to-last layer). The draft model `$g_\phi$` predicts the feature vector at the next position:

$$\hat{h}_{t+1} = g_\phi(\hat{h}_{\leq t}, x_{\leq t+1})$$

where `$\hat{h}_{\leq t}$` are the predicted features from previous draft steps, `$x_{\leq t+1}$` are the corresponding tokens (including the newly drafted token at `$t+1$`), and `$\hat{h}_{t+1}$` is the draft model's prediction of what the target model's feature vector *would be* at position `$t+1$`. The predicted feature is then fed through the frozen target model's LM head `$W$` to produce token probabilities: `$\text{softmax}(W \hat{h}_{t+1})$`.

**Training objective.** EAGLE-2 trains the draft model with a combined loss that encourages both feature-level and token-level alignment:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{EAGLE}} = \sum_t \|\hat{h}_{t+1} - h_{t+1}\|_2^2 + \lambda \sum_t \text{CE}(\text{softmax}(W \hat{h}_{t+1}), x_{t+1})$$

where `$\|\hat{h}_{t+1} - h_{t+1}\|_2^2$` is the squared Euclidean distance between the predicted feature vector and the actual target feature vector at position `$t+1$`, `$\text{CE}(\cdot, x_{t+1})$` is the cross-entropy loss between the token distribution derived from the predicted feature and the ground-truth next token `$x_{t+1}$`, and `$\lambda$` is a hyperparameter balancing the two terms.

**What it computes (first term):** the feature regression loss is a direct distance between the draft model's predicted representation and the target model's actual representation. Minimizing this encourages the draft model to internalize the target model's internal geometry—how the target represents syntactic structures, semantic content, and reasoning patterns in its hidden states.

**What it computes (second term):** the token-level cross-entropy loss encourages the predicted features, when mapped through the target's LM head, to produce token distributions that assign high probability to the actual next token. This provides a direct behavioral signal: predicting features that lead to correct next-token predictions.

**Why this combined form:** training only on token cross-entropy would be standard but ignores the rich intermediate representations the target model produces. Training only on feature regression would align internal states but provides no guarantee about the final token distribution—small feature errors can compound through the LM head into large probability errors for high-likelihood tokens. The combined loss forces both representational alignment and behavioral alignment, with `$\lambda$` controlling the tradeoff.

**Dynamic draft trees.** A key EAGLE-2 innovation over the original EAGLE is context-dependent tree construction. Instead of a fixed tree topology, EAGLE-2 uses draft confidence to rank candidate expansions at each frontier node:

$$V_i \approx \prod_{v_j \in \text{Path}(\text{root}, v_i)} c_j$$

where `$V_i$` is the estimated value (quality score) of node `$v_i$`, `$\text{Path}(\text{root}, v_i)$` is the sequence of nodes from the root to `$v_i$` in the draft tree, and `$c_j$` is the draft confidence at node `$v_j$` (the draft model's predicted probability for the token at that node). The product of confidences along the path approximates the joint probability that all ancestors and the current node will be accepted.

**What it computes:** the path confidence product is a heuristic estimate of how likely the verifier is to accept all tokens along the path to `$v_i$`. Nodes with higher path confidence are prioritized for expansion—they get to propose additional child tokens in the tree.

**Why this form:** in speculative decoding, a token can only be verified if all its ancestors were accepted. A node whose ancestors have low draft confidence is unlikely to survive long enough to be useful, regardless of its own quality. The product-of-confidences heuristic focuses the limited tree budget (a fixed number of nodes per verifier call) on paths most likely to survive the acceptance process.

**Verification with EAGLE-2.** The acceptance rule for EAGLE-2 is identical to standard speculative decoding but expressed in terms of the draft model's predicted token probabilities rather than direct token predictions:

$$\alpha_{j+i} = \min\left(1, \frac{p_{j+i}(\hat{x}_{j+i})}{\hat{p}_{j+i}(\hat{x}_{j+i})}\right)$$

where `$p_{j+i}(\hat{x}_{j+i})$` is the target model's probability for the drafted token at that position, and `$\hat{p}_{j+i}(\hat{x}_{j+i})$` is the draft model's predicted probability for the same token (derived from the predicted feature `$\hat{h}_{j+i}$` through the LM head). The notation shift from `$q$`/`$p$` in Equation 1 to `$p_{j+i}$`/`$\hat{p}_{j+i}$` here reflects that EAGLE-2's draft model produces features, not tokens directly, but the acceptance mechanism is identical in structure.

**What it computes:** the same ratio-based acceptance rule as standard speculative decoding, but `$\hat{p}$` comes from mapping the draft model's predicted features through the target's LM head rather than from an independently parameterized token distribution.

**Why this form:** using the target model's own LM head for the draft side means the draft model doesn't need to learn a separate vocabulary-sized output layer. It only needs to predict features in the target's representation space, which is both parameter-efficient (no separate embedding or unembedding matrix) and ensures that any representational alignment directly translates to token-distribution alignment.

#### HASS Drafting Backbone

HASS (Zhang et al., 2025) uses the same lossless speculative decoding framework but improves the drafter by addressing what the authors identify as two mismatches between standard draft training and actual inference conditions: **objective mismatch** and **context mismatch**.

**Objective mismatch.** During standard training, a draft model is typically trained to minimize cross-entropy over the full vocabulary—all tokens matter equally. But during speculative decoding, only the verifier's most likely tokens actually matter for acceptance: if the draft model assigns vanishingly small probability to some rare token, that's irrelevant as long as it correctly captures the high-probability tokens the target model would produce. Standard training wastes capacity on accurate modeling of tokens that will never affect acceptance length.

**The HASS solution: Top-K distillation.** HASS addresses this with a distillation loss that focuses the draft model's learning on the verifier's highest-probability tokens. Let `$\hat{\Omega} \subset \Omega$` be the set of top-`$K$` tokens under the target model's distribution `$q(\cdot)$`, and let `$p(\cdot)$` be the draft model's next-token distribution. The Top-K distillation loss is:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{Top-K}} = -\sum_{x \in \hat{\Omega}} q(x) \log p(x)$$

where `$q(x)$` is the target model's probability for token `$x$` and `$p(x)$` is the draft model's predicted probability for the same token.

**What it computes:** cross-entropy between the target model's distribution and the draft model's distribution, but restricted to only the top-`$K$` tokens under the target model. Tokens outside `$\hat{\Omega}$` are ignored entirely—the draft model receives no gradient signal for them, positive or negative.

**Why this form:** standard distillation over the full vocabulary would force the draft model to allocate representational capacity to accurately modeling the target's distribution on low-probability tokens. In speculative decoding, those tokens will almost never be accepted (they're unlikely under the target) and even if proposed, the acceptance ratio `$q/p$` would be near zero. The Top-K restriction concentrates learning on the tokens that actually drive acceptance. The paper uses `$K = 10$` for all HASS experiments.

**Context mismatch.** During standard training, the draft model is trained on clean target-model features at every position—it sees the "ground truth" hidden states from the target model's autoregressive processing. At inference time, however, the draft model must predict features conditioned on its *own previous predictions*, not on clean target features. Errors compound: a small feature prediction error at position `$t$` affects the conditioning for position `$t+1$`, which produces a larger error, and so on. The training distribution (clean features) and inference distribution (noisy predicted features) are misaligned.

**The HASS solution: harmonized context alignment.** HASS progressively introduces imperfect draft features during training so that the model learns to predict from its own potentially noisy representations. At alignment step `$j$`, the draft model predicts:

$$P^{(s)}(x_{t+1} \mid x_{\leq t}) = \text{Head}(f^{(s_j)}_{t+1}) = \text{Head}(M^{(s)}(f^{(s_{j-1})}_t, f^{(l)}_1 \oplus \cdots \oplus f^{(l)}_{t-j+1} \oplus f^{(s_1)}_{t-j+2} \oplus \cdots \oplus f^{(s_{j-1})}_t))$$

where `$f^{(l)}_i$` are clean target-model features at position `$i$`, `$f^{(s_m)}_i$` are draft-model features from the `$m$`-th alignment step, `$\oplus$` denotes concatenation, `$M^{(s)}$` is the draft model, and `$\text{Head}$` is the target model's LM head.

**What it computes:** at alignment step `$j$`, the draft model's input is a mixture of clean target features (for positions far back in the context, `$1$` through `$t-j+1$`) and its own previously predicted features (for the most recent `$j-1$` positions). As `$j$` increases, the proportion of self-predicted features in the conditioning grows, making the training condition progressively closer to inference conditions.

**Why this form:** the standard approach—training only on clean features—creates a train-test mismatch where the model never learns to cope with its own errors. The progressive introduction of self-predicted features acts as a curriculum: early in training (small `$j$`), the model has mostly clean context and can learn strong representations; later (larger `$j$`), it must learn to predict accurately even when recent context is noisy. The paper uses three forward-alignment steps for all HASS experiments.

**Full HASS training objective.** The complete loss at alignment step `$j$` combines Top-K distillation with feature regression and standard cross-entropy:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{HASS}}^{(j)} = \sum_{t=1}^{T-1} \left[\text{CE}(P^{(l)}(x_{t+1} \mid x_{\leq t}), P^{(s)}(x_{t+1} \mid x_{\leq t})) + \mathcal{L}_{\text{aux}}\right]$$

where `$\text{CE}(P^{(l)}, P^{(s)})$` is cross-entropy between the target model's distribution `$P^{(l)}$` and the draft model's predicted distribution `$P^{(s)}$`, and `$\mathcal{L}_{\text{aux}}$` includes the Top-K distillation term (Equation 7) and a feature regression term. The paper's HASS runs use Top-K distillation with `$K = 10$`, loss weight `$1.0$`, and three forward-alignment steps throughout.

**What it computes:** the main distillation objective (the CE term) pushes the draft model's token distribution to match the target model's token distribution at each position. The auxiliary terms provide additional signals: Top-K distillation focuses the CE loss on the most important tokens, and feature regression (analogous to the first term in EAGLE-2's loss, Equation 4) provides a representational alignment signal.

**Why this combined form:** the HASS design philosophy is that the draft model needs to solve three related but distinct problems: produce a token distribution that matches the target's for the most relevant tokens (Top-K distillation), produce features that are close to the target's internal representations (feature regression), and do both under the noisy conditioning it will experience at inference time (harmonized context alignment). Each component addresses a different failure mode of simpler draft training.

#### Draft Training Variants and Data Configurations

The paper studies seven main draft variants for each backbone, distinguished entirely by their training data or composition strategy. These variants are designed to isolate the effect of training distribution (RQ1), data mixture scaling (RQ2), and composition method (RQ3).

**Single-domain checkpoints (RQ1).** Two variants are trained on single-domain data:

- **MathInstruct (70k):** Trained on 70,000 examples from MathInstruct, a dataset of mathematical reasoning problems and solutions. This checkpoint is expected to specialize on mathematical reasoning tasks, producing proposals that closely match the target model's behavior on math benchmarks.
- **ShareGPT (70k):** Trained on 70,000 examples from ShareGPT, a dataset of conversational interactions between users and AI assistants. This checkpoint is expected to specialize on conversational generation, producing proposals aligned with the target model on chat-style prompts.

These two checkpoints are trained with identical hyperparameters: 20 epochs, learning rate `$3 \times 10^{-5}$`, batch size 8, gradient accumulation 1. Only the training data differs.

**Mixed-data checkpoints (RQ2).** Two variants are trained on mixtures of MathInstruct and ShareGPT data:

- **Mixed 35k+35k:** Trained on 35,000 MathInstruct examples plus 35,000 ShareGPT examples, totaling 70,000 examples—the same total sample count as each single-domain checkpoint. This tests whether a *balanced* mixture at fixed total data volume can recover cross-domain robustness.
- **Mixed 70k+70k:** Trained on 70,000 MathInstruct examples plus 70,000 ShareGPT examples, doubling the total supervision to 140,000 examples. This tests whether *increasing* the total data volume in a balanced mixture further improves robustness, and whether the benefit extends across decoding temperatures.

The mixed-data checkpoints share the same training hyperparameters as the single-domain checkpoints.

**Averaged checkpoint (RQ3, baseline).** This variant is not trained from scratch but is constructed by **point-wise parameter averaging** of the MathInstruct and ShareGPT single-domain checkpoints:

$$\theta_{\text{merge}} = \lambda \theta_{\text{math}} + (1 - \lambda) \theta_{\text{chat}}$$

where `$\theta_{\text{math}}$` are the parameters of the MathInstruct-trained draft model, `$\theta_{\text{chat}}$` are the parameters of the ShareGPT-trained draft model, and `$\lambda \in [0, 1]$` controls the interpolation weight. The main experiments use `$\lambda = 0.5$` (equal weighting), and Figure 6 sweeps `$\lambda$` to characterize the interpolation behavior.

**What it computes:** for each parameter tensor in the draft model (attention weights, MLP weights, layer norms, embeddings), the averaged checkpoint takes a convex combination of the corresponding tensors from the two specialists. The result is a single set of parameters that lies on the linear interpolation path between the two checkpoints.

**Why this form:** linear interpolation in weight space has been shown effective for combining capabilities from different fine-tuned models in other contexts (Ilharco et al., 2022), under the hypothesis that when two models share a common pretrained initialization and are fine-tuned on different tasks, their parameter updates encode task-specific knowledge in roughly linear subspaces that can be additively combined. Testing this hypothesis for speculative decoding drafters is the purpose of this baseline.

**Inference-time composition strategies (RQ3).** These are not separate checkpoints but **procedures** that operate on the two single-domain checkpoints at inference time without modifying their parameters. Both strategies are applied within one backbone at a time—HASS drafts are combined only with HASS drafts, EAGLE-2 only with EAGLE-2—to isolate the effect of composition from cross-backbone differences.

#### Confidence-Based Routing

Given an input prefix, both the MathInstruct and ShareGPT draft models independently generate draft trees from the same root token. The system must decide which tree to submit to the verifier. **Confidence routing** makes this decision based on the draft models' own confidence scores.

**Tree-level scoring.** Each draft tree `$\mathcal{T}$` is scored by the mean confidence of its nodes:

$$\text{Score}(\mathcal{T}) = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}|} \sum_{v \in \mathcal{T}} c(v)$$

where `$|\mathcal{T}|$` is the number of nodes in the tree, `$v$` ranges over all nodes in `$\mathcal{T}$`, and `$c(v)$` is the draft confidence assigned to node `$v$` (the draft model's predicted probability for the token at that node under its own distribution).

**What it computes:** the average probability that the draft model assigns to its own proposed tokens across the entire tree. A higher score means the draft model is, on average, more confident in its proposals.

**Why this form:** draft confidence is a natural signal for routing because it is available at no additional cost (the draft model already produces these probabilities during tree generation) and does not require the verifier to be invoked. The hypothesis is that a draft model will tend to be more confident when operating in its domain of expertise—a math-specialized drafter should assign higher average confidence to its proposals on math problems than a chat-specialized drafter would, while the opposite should hold for conversational prompts.

**Selection rule.** The tree with the higher mean confidence is selected:

$$\mathcal{T}^* = \arg\max_{\mathcal{T} \in \{\mathcal{T}_{\text{math}}, \mathcal{T}_{\text{chat}}\}} \text{Score}(\mathcal{T})$$

Only `$\mathcal{T}^*$` is passed to the verifier; the other tree is discarded without consuming verifier computation. Figure 3 illustrates this process: two trees are generated from the same prefix, their node-level confidences are compared, and the higher-confidence tree is routed to the verifier.

**Why this design:** confidence routing is intentionally simple—it uses only draft-side information (no verifier calls for the decision), makes a hard binary choice (pick one specialist, discard the other), and uses a scalar summary statistic (mean confidence). This simplicity makes it a strong test of whether confidence alone contains enough signal about domain match to guide routing decisions. More sophisticated routing policies (learned functions, cost-aware selection, soft combination) could potentially outperform this baseline, but if confidence routing already works, it establishes a lower bound on what is possible.

**Correctness guarantee.** Proposition A.1 in Appendix A.4 proves that confidence routing preserves the target model's output distribution. The proof relies on the fact that the routing decision depends only on draft-side quantities available before verification (the draft trees and their confidences), not on any target-side information. Since each individual tree is valid under Assumption A.1, the randomly selected tree `$\mathcal{T}^*$` (random because the routing may be randomized, though the paper uses deterministic argmax) is itself a random valid tree, and Lemma A.1 guarantees that running speculative decoding on any distribution over valid trees preserves the target output law.

#### Merged-Tree Verification

Instead of selecting one specialist's tree and discarding the other, merged-tree verification submits both trees to the verifier simultaneously by **packing them under a shared root** while preserving each subtree's internal structure and preventing cross-subtree contamination.

**Tree construction (Algorithm 1).** The procedure, illustrated in Figure 4, works as follows:

1. **Generate draft trees:** Both the MathInstruct and ShareGPT draft models independently produce candidate trees `$\mathcal{T}_{\text{math}}$` and `$\mathcal{T}_{\text{chat}}$` from the same prefix (same root token).
2. **Merge the trees:** Concatenate the non-root nodes of both trees under a single shared root. The root token is shared; all other nodes are unioned. Node indices for the second subtree are offset by the size of the first subtree so that indices remain unique.
3. **Build ancestor-preserving attention masks:** Construct an attention mask where each node can attend only to the shared root and to its own ancestors within its source subtree. Crucially, nodes from the MathInstruct subtree cannot attend to nodes from the ShareGPT subtree, and vice versa. This ensures that the verifier's computation for each subtree is identical to what it would be if that subtree were verified independently.
4. **Assign depth-based position ids:** Each node receives the same depth-based position id it had in its source tree, with children placed one step deeper than their parents. This preserves the positional relationships within each subtree.
5. **Verify the merged tree in one parallel pass:** The target model processes the entire merged tree (tokens, position ids, attention mask) in a single forward pass, producing next-token logits for every node position.
6. **Apply standard speculative acceptance:** Extract candidate paths from the merged tree and apply the standard acceptance rule (Equation 1). The shared root has already been accepted (it was committed in a previous step); acceptance proceeds depth-by-depth from the root outward.

**Figure 5 (flattened merged-tree input)** shows the serialized representation: the tree is linearized into a sequence for the verifier's transformer, with the attention mask encoding the tree structure (which nodes can attend to which other nodes) and the position ids encoding depth. This is the standard tree attention mechanism used in prior work (Miao et al., 2024), extended to handle two independently generated subtrees packed together.

**What the merged tree provides.** The merged tree offers the verifier **increased proposal diversity** at each verification call because both specialists contribute candidate continuations. A math-specialized drafter might propose different high-level solution strategies than a chat-specialized one, and the verifier can evaluate both simultaneously. The cost is that the verifier must process a larger tree (roughly twice as many nodes), which increases the per-call computational cost even though the number of verifier calls remains the same.

**Correctness guarantee.** Proposition A.2 in Appendix A.4 proves that merged-tree verification preserves the target model's output distribution. The proof relies on Lemma A.2 (verifier invariance under masked concatenation), which establishes that for any transformer-style verifier, if two packed inputs agree on a subset of indices `$S$` in terms of tokens, position ids, and within-subset attention, and if indices in `$S$` do not attend to indices outside `$S$`, then the verifier's logits on `$S$` are identical under both packed inputs. By construction, each source subtree `$\mathcal{T}_{\text{math}}$` and `$\mathcal{T}_{\text{chat}}$` inside the merged tree satisfies these conditions relative to its standalone representation: tokens match, depth-based position ids match, within-subtree attention is preserved, and cross-subtree attention is fully masked. Therefore, every node receives exactly the same target-side conditional distributions it would receive under standalone verification of its source tree. Since each source tree is individually valid (Assumption A.1), the merged tree as a whole is also valid, and the lossless guarantee follows.

**Why this design over routing:** routing makes a hard per-input choice between specialists and discards the non-selected proposals. If the routing decision is correct, this is efficient (no wasted verifier computation). If the routing decision is wrong—for example, routing a math problem to the ShareGPT specialist—acceptance length degrades. Merged-tree verification avoids this all-or-nothing risk by letting the verifier see both specialists' proposals. The tradeoff is between proposal diversity (merged trees provide more candidates but cost more per verifier call) and routing accuracy (routing costs less per call but can make mistakes). The paper reports acceptance length for both strategies but explicitly does not claim end-to-end latency improvement for merged trees without a separate systems analysis.

#### Evaluation Setup and Experimental Design Logic

**Verifier and draft model specifications.** Across all experiments, the verifier is Meta-Llama-3-8B-Instruct (Llama Team, 2024), an 8-billion parameter instruction-tuned language model. The draft model is a lightweight LLaMA-style decoder with **one transformer layer, hidden size 4096, and approximately 0.8B parameters**. Draft and verifier share the same tokenizer and vocabulary—this is critical because tokenization mismatch would confound the acceptance metric: differences in token boundaries between draft and target would cause spurious rejections unrelated to distributional alignment.

**Training protocol.** All draft checkpoints are trained for **20 epochs** with **learning rate `$3 \times 10^{-5}$`, batch size 8, gradient accumulation 1**. HASS runs use the same auxiliary settings throughout: **Top-K distillation with `$K = 10$`, loss weight 1.0, and three forward-alignment steps**. These hyperparameters are fixed across all variants to isolate the effect of training data.

**Benchmarks.** The paper evaluates on four benchmarks chosen to span conversational and mathematical reasoning domains:

- **MT-Bench:** Multi-turn conversational benchmark with 80 multi-turn questions covering writing, roleplay, reasoning, math, coding, extraction, STEM, and humanities. Represents conversational/chat workloads.
- **GSM8K:** 1,319 grade-school math word problems requiring multi-step arithmetic reasoning. Represents mathematical reasoning.
- **MATH-500:** 500 competition-level mathematics problems from the MATH dataset (Hendrycks et al., 2021), covering algebra, geometry, number theory, counting, and precalculus. Represents harder mathematical reasoning.
- **SVAMP:** 300 elementary math word problems with varying structures, designed to test mathematical reasoning robustness. Represents mathematical reasoning with linguistic variation.

**Decoding temperatures.** All experiments are run at two temperatures: **temperature 0** (greedy decoding, where the verifier always selects the most likely token) and **temperature 1** (standard sampling, where the verifier samples from its full distribution). Temperature 0 isolates the effect of training distribution on the mode of the target distribution; temperature 1 tests whether the effects generalize to stochastic decoding where the target model may produce different tokens on different runs and the draft model must match a broader distribution.

**Primary metric.** The paper's primary metric is **average acceptance length**, defined as the number of consecutively accepted draft tokens per verifier call, averaged over all verifier calls across the evaluation set:

$$\mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}}[A(x; M_D, M_T)]$$

where `$\mathcal{D}$` is the evaluation distribution (one of the four benchmarks), `$M_D$` is the draft model or composition strategy, `$M_T$` is the fixed verifier, and `$A(x; M_D, M_T)$` counts how many draft tokens are accepted before the first rejection for input `$x$`.

**What it measures:** acceptance length is a direct proxy for the efficiency of speculative decoding. Each verifier call processes multiple positions in parallel, so the throughput improvement over standard autoregressive decoding is roughly proportional to acceptance length (minus overhead for draft generation, tree construction, and the verifier's parallel pass). An acceptance length of 1 means the draft model provides zero benefit: the first token is always accepted (the root token was already committed), but all draft tokens beyond the first are rejected, so each verifier call produces exactly one output token—identical to standard decoding. An acceptance length of 5 means each verifier call produces an average of 5 committed tokens, roughly a 5× reduction in verifier calls.

**Why not end-to-end latency or throughput:** acceptance length abstracts away hardware-specific factors (GPU memory bandwidth, kernel launch overhead, draft model inference time, tree construction cost) that vary across deployment configurations. By measuring acceptance length—the algorithmic efficiency metric that directly determines the multiplicative speedup potential—the paper's findings are transferable across hardware platforms. The tradeoff is that the reported numbers should be interpreted as upper bounds on achievable speedup; actual throughput gains depend on the compute cost of draft generation and tree verification relative to the verifier's per-token cost.

**Experimental design logic.** The paper maps specific comparisons in Table 1 to research questions:

- **RQ1 (Does task-specific training improve matched-domain acceptance?):** Compare MathInstruct vs. ShareGPT on each benchmark. If specialization exists, MathInstruct should outperform ShareGPT on reasoning benchmarks (GSM8K, MATH-500, SVAMP) and ShareGPT should outperform MathInstruct on MT-Bench. The strength of the conclusion comes from the *crossover* pattern: each checkpoint being stronger in its own domain and weaker in the other, replicated across both HASS and EAGLE-2 backbones.

- **RQ2 (Can mixed-data training recover cross-domain robustness?):** Compare Mixed 35k+35k and Mixed 70k+70k against the single-domain checkpoints. The question is whether mixed training produces a single checkpoint that is competitive with the best single-domain checkpoint on each benchmark, without requiring per-benchmark selection. The comparison at two temperatures tests whether the robustness generalizes across decoding regimes.

- **RQ3 (How should multiple specialized drafters be combined?):** Compare checkpoint averaging, confidence routing, and merged-tree verification. The key comparison is weight-space combination (Averaged) versus inference-time composition (Confidence Routed, Merged Trees). If inference-time methods substantially outperform averaging, it indicates that the specialists' capabilities are not preserved by naive parameter interpolation and must be composed dynamically.

- **RQ4 (What do confidence, entropy, and depth reveal about acceptance behavior?):** Analyze routing decisions (Table 2), entropy distributions for accepted vs. rejected tokens (Figure 7), and acceptance rate by speculative depth (Figure 8). These analyses are diagnostic: they help explain *why* the composition strategies work or fail, not just that they do.

- **RQ5 (How does speculative depth affect the exploration-exploitation balance?):** Use the per-depth acceptance rate tables (Appendix A.5 and A.6) and Figure 8 to characterize how domain specialization evolves with increasing draft depth. The hypothesis is that shallower depths benefit from broad proposal coverage (exploration), while deeper acceptance increasingly favors the better-matched specialist (exploitation).

**Two-fold cross-validation.** The paper does not mention a separate holdout set for hyperparameter selection; all checkpoints are trained once with fixed hyperparameters and evaluated on the standard test sets of the four benchmarks. The routing policy is deterministic and non-learned (argmax of mean confidence), so there is no risk of overfitting the routing decisions to the evaluation set. Conclusions are drawn from consistency across both backbones and both temperatures rather than from fine-grained optimization of any single variant.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Draft Training Distribution as a First-Class Design Variable in Speculative Decoding

The paper's most fundamental conceptual move is recasting the draft model's training data—previously treated as an incidental, fixed choice—as a **first-class design variable** that independently controls speculative decoding quality, separable from and complementary to draft architecture. Before this work, the speculative decoding literature operated under an implicit assumption: draft models are trained on broad generic corpora (typically ShareGPT), and the primary lever for improving acceptance length is architectural innovation—better feature prediction (EAGLE, Li et al., 2024a), dynamic tree construction (EAGLE-2, Li et al., 2024b), or improved training objectives (HASS, Zhang et al., 2025). The training data was a background constant, not a variable to be manipulated.

This paper demonstrates that this assumption is not merely incomplete—it is **consequential**. The controlled experiment at the heart of RQ1 (Table 1, single-domain checkpoints) shows that holding architecture, verifier, and training hyperparameters fixed, swapping the training distribution from MathInstruct to ShareGPT can swing acceptance length by ~1.4 tokens on MATH-500 under HASS at temperature 0 (5.35 vs. 3.98), or by ~1.3 tokens on MT-Bench (3.98 vs. 2.90 for ShareGPT vs. MathInstruct). These are not small effects; they are comparable in magnitude to the gains reported from architectural innovations in prior work, but they come from changing only the data—no new loss function, no new tree construction algorithm, no change to the verifier.

What makes this insight intellectually distinctive is not the raw observation that training data matters (that would be unsurprising), but rather the **systematic characterization of the nature and boundaries of that dependence**. The paper establishes a crossover pattern that holds across two independent drafting backbones: MathInstruct-trained drafts dominate on reasoning benchmarks, ShareGPT-trained drafts dominate on conversational benchmarks, and the crossover is consistent at both temperature 0 (greedy) and temperature 1 (sampling). This pattern is not guaranteed a priori—one might have hypothesized that a conversational draft model, trained on diverse human interactions, would capture reasoning patterns incidentally, or that math data would transfer poorly to any non-math domain. The evidence instead shows clean, predictable specialization: the draft model becomes a **task-matched component** whose effectiveness depends on the alignment between its training distribution and the deployment workload.

This reframing has downstream consequences that the paper explicitly develops (RQ2, RQ3) but that extend beyond its immediate experiments. If draft training distribution is a design variable, then the draft model is no longer a fixed auxiliary component that can be trained once and deployed everywhere. Instead, it becomes a choice that should be made per-deployment-context, analogous to how retrieval-augmented generation systems select different retrieval corpora per domain, or how mixture-of-experts models (Mu and Lin, 2026) route inputs to different expert subsets. The paper's depth-wise analysis (Figure 8) reinforces this reframing: specialization is not just a top-level phenomenon but persists and often sharpens at deeper speculative depths, meaning the choice of training distribution affects not just whether early draft tokens are accepted but the entire trajectory of multi-step reasoning proposals.

**Comparison to prior work:** Prior speculative decoding papers uniformly trained drafters on generic data without questioning the choice. EAGLE-2 evaluated on conversational and code benchmarks using ShareGPT-trained drafts. HASS evaluated on similar distributions. The implicit assumption was that the drafter's job is to approximate the target model's behavior *in general*, and generic training data suffices. This paper provides the first controlled evidence that this assumption is empirically wrong for task-specialized workloads, and that the magnitude of the effect is large enough to matter for deployment decisions.

**Significance beyond performance:** This is primarily a **reframing contribution**, not a new method. The paper does not propose a novel drafting architecture or loss function; it demonstrates that a variable the field had been ignoring is in fact a major determinant of system quality. The value lies in changing how researchers and practitioners think about the speculative decoding pipeline: proposal quality should be studied as a joint function of draft architecture *and* draft training distribution, not architecture alone. The paper's closing sentence makes this reframing explicit: "proposal quality in speculative decoding should be studied as a function of both draft architecture and draft training distribution, not architecture alone."

### Innovation 2: The Failure of Weight-Space Averaging as a Strong Negative Result

The paper's second distinctive contribution is a **strong negative result**: naive weight-space averaging of specialized draft checkpoints performs poorly as a composition strategy, substantially underperforming both single-domain checkpoints and inference-time composition methods. This result is intellectually significant not because weight-space averaging was an obviously good idea, but because it was a **reasonable hypothesis given prior evidence**—and its failure reveals something fundamental about how specialization is encoded in draft model parameters.

**What prior work suggested.** Model averaging and task arithmetic (Ilharco et al., 2022) have been shown effective for combining capabilities from different fine-tuned checkpoints in other contexts. The standard narrative is that when two models share a common pretrained initialization and are fine-tuned on different tasks, their parameter updates encode task-specific knowledge in roughly linear subspaces that can be additively combined via simple interpolation. Under this hypothesis, averaging a math-specialized and chat-specialized draft model should yield a single draft model that performs reasonably well on both workloads—perhaps not as well as the best specialist on each domain, but acceptably close.

The paper tests this hypothesis directly and finds it **decisively false for speculative decoding drafters**. The Averaged checkpoint (Table 1, RQ3) achieves average acceptance lengths of 2.34–2.62 across backbones and temperatures—substantially below even the weaker single-domain checkpoint on most benchmarks. At temperature 0, the averaged HASS checkpoint achieves 2.29 on MT-Bench (vs. 3.98 for ShareGPT alone) and 2.80 on GSM8K (vs. 5.02 for MathInstruct alone). The interpolation sweep in Figure 6 shows that no choice of interpolation weight `$\lambda$` recovers performance close to the specialists: the acceptance length curve is not just low at `$\lambda = 0.5$` but unstable across the entire interpolation range, with no clear optimum.

**Why this negative result matters.** The failure of weight-space averaging tells us something non-obvious about how draft models encode specialization. It is not the case that the MathInstruct drafter learns "math capabilities" and the ShareGPT drafter learns "chat capabilities" in orthogonal parameter subspaces that can be linearly combined. Instead, the two checkpoints appear to learn **incompatible internal representations** that interfere destructively when averaged. The draft model's job—predicting the target model's next-token behavior—requires tightly coordinated feature representations, attention patterns, and output mappings. Small perturbations in any of these components, as introduced by averaging with a differently-specialized checkpoint, can degrade the entire system's coherence.

This insight is practically important because it tells practitioners what *not* to do. Given multiple specialized draft checkpoints, the straightforward approach of averaging them into a single model—which requires no additional training, no inference-time overhead, and no routing logic—is tempting. The paper shows that this temptation should be resisted: the resulting model will be substantially worse than either specialist on its own domain, potentially worse than a mixed-data trained checkpoint, and dramatically worse than inference-time composition.

**Comparison to prior work.** Ilharco et al. (2022) demonstrated successful task arithmetic for editing model behaviors (e.g., adding or removing specific capabilities) in the context of full-scale LLMs. The failure in the speculative decoding setting is notable because it suggests that the linearity assumption underlying task arithmetic does not transfer to draft models trained with feature-level objectives (EAGLE-2) or harmonized training procedures (HASS). The draft model's representational geometry may be more fragile than that of standard autoregressive LMs, perhaps because feature prediction and distillation objectives create sharper, less interpolatable loss landscapes.

**Significance beyond performance.** This is a **diagnostic contribution**—the negative result is more informative than a positive one would have been. If weight-space averaging had worked, it would have been a convenient engineering trick but not conceptually interesting. Its failure forces the field to confront the question of *why* draft specialization is not linearly composable in parameter space, which opens avenues for understanding the representational geometry of feature-level drafters, the interaction between distillation objectives and parameter interpolatability, and the conditions under which model averaging succeeds or fails for different model types and training procedures.

### Innovation 3: Inference-Time Composition as the Right Abstraction for Multi-Specialist Drafting

Building directly on Innovation 2, the paper establishes that **inference-time composition**—keeping specialists separate and combining their proposals dynamically at test time—is the correct abstraction for deploying multiple specialized drafters, and it tests two points on the design spectrum: **confidence-based routing** (hard selection of one specialist per input) and **merged-tree verification** (soft combination where both specialists' proposals are evaluated jointly).

What makes this intellectually distinctive is not the specific routing or merging mechanisms themselves (both are straightforward engineering choices), but the **architectural principle** they embody: when draft models encode incompatible specializations, composition must happen at the level of *proposals* (the candidate trees submitted to the verifier) rather than at the level of *parameters* (the weights that generate those proposals). This principle separates the draft model's internal representation—which may be fragile, non-interpolatable, and domain-specific—from its external interface—the draft tree, which is just a set of token hypotheses that the verifier can evaluate agnostically.

The paper provides converging evidence for this principle across two composition strategies with different tradeoffs. Confidence routing (Table 1, RQ3) achieves average acceptance lengths of 4.80 (HASS) and 4.63 (EAGLE-2) at temperature 0, substantially above both single-domain checkpoints on average (4.10–4.42) and dramatically above checkpoint averaging (2.42–2.59). Merged-tree verification pushes this further to 5.11 (HASS) and 5.03 (EAGLE-2) at temperature 0, the highest acceptance lengths in the table. This progression—averaging << single-domain < routing < merged trees—shows that adding more proposal diversity (merged trees provide both specialists' candidates) consistently improves acceptance length, and that the cost of making a wrong routing decision (sending a math problem to the chat specialist or vice versa) is real enough that avoiding it via merged verification pays off.

**Why the merged-tree result is conceptually interesting.** Merged-tree verification is not just a more expensive version of routing—it embodies a different philosophy about how to use multiple specialists. Routing makes a **hard, irreversible decision per input**: pick the specialist you think is better, discard the other, and live with the consequences if you're wrong. Merged-tree verification makes a **soft, reversible decision**: submit both specialists' proposals to the verifier and let the acceptance rule (Equation 1) naturally select the better-aligned tokens, regardless of which specialist proposed them. The verifier's acceptance probabilities act as an implicit arbitration mechanism: tokens from the better-matched specialist will have higher acceptance probabilities and survive more often, while tokens from the mismatched specialist will be rejected more often, but the mismatched specialist still gets a chance to contribute useful proposals on inputs where it happens to produce good candidates.

This is a form of **implicit ensembling at the proposal level**: the merged tree increases the coverage of the hypothesis space that the verifier evaluates, and the lossless acceptance rule ensures that only hypotheses the target model endorses survive. The correctness guarantees in Appendix A.4 (Proposition A.2) prove that this combination is mathematically sound—the target output distribution is preserved regardless of which specialist proposed which token, because the verifier evaluates each node under exactly the same conditional distributions it would use in standalone verification.

**Comparison to prior work.** Prior tree verification work (Miao et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024b) packed multiple candidate paths from a *single* draft model into a verifier call, increasing proposal diversity within one model's generation. Merged-tree verification extends this to packing candidates from *multiple independently trained specialists*, which is a qualitatively different source of diversity: the specialists may propose different high-level strategies, different reasoning chains, or different stylistic choices that no single model would produce. This connects to broader themes in ensemble methods and mixture-of-experts, but adapted to the specific constraints of lossless speculative decoding (where the verifier must see correct conditional distributions for every node).

**Significance beyond performance.** This is primarily an **architectural insight** about the right level of abstraction for composition. The paper shows that the draft model's parameter space is a poor locus for combination (Innovation 2), but its proposal space is a rich locus where specialists can contribute complementary value without requiring their internal representations to be compatible. This insight suggests a research direction: rather than trying to train a single universally-good draft model, invest in training multiple specialized drafters and developing more sophisticated inference-time composition strategies—learned routing policies, cost-aware tree packing, dynamic specialist selection, or hierarchical verification schemes that allocate verifier computation adaptively based on which specialist's proposals are being accepted.

### Innovation 4: Confidence as a Routing Signal and the Diagnostic Role of Entropy and Depth

The paper's fourth contribution is a **diagnostic analysis** of what signals are useful for understanding and predicting speculative decoding behavior when multiple specialists are available. This analysis separates signals into two categories: those useful for **routing** (deciding which specialist to use) and those useful for **diagnosis** (explaining *why* certain tokens, paths, or specialists succeed or fail).

**Confidence vs. entropy for routing.** Table 2 provides the clearest evidence. Under confidence-based routing, the MathInstruct drafter is selected for 90.8% of GSM8K examples, 97.0% of MATH-500 examples, and 93.0% of SVAMP examples, while ShareGPT is selected for 81.2% of MT-Bench examples. These are sharp, benchmark-level separations: confidence routing nearly always picks the right specialist for the domain. Under entropy-based routing—where selection is based on the draft model's uncertainty rather than its confidence—the decisions are near-balanced across all benchmarks (52.5% vs. 47.5% on MT-Bench, 54.6% vs. 45.4% on GSM8K), providing essentially no useful signal for domain discrimination.

This asymmetry is intellectually interesting because it reveals that **confidence and entropy encode different information about the draft-target relationship**. Entropy measures the draft model's uncertainty about its own predictions—how spread out its token distribution is. High entropy means the draft model considers many tokens plausible; low entropy means it concentrates probability on few tokens. Confidence measures the draft model's belief in the specific tokens it actually proposes. The finding that confidence discriminates domains while entropy does not suggests that domain specialization manifests not in how *uncertain* the draft model is in general (a math problem doesn't necessarily make the chat drafter more uncertain overall), but in whether the tokens the draft model *chooses to propose* align with what the target model would produce. A math-specialized drafter on a math problem assigns high confidence to math-appropriate tokens; a chat-specialized drafter on the same problem assigns lower confidence because its proposed chat-style tokens don't match what it "knows" about typical continuations.

**Entropy as a diagnostic of rejection.** Figure 7 and the appendix entropy tables (Tables 3–4) show a consistent pattern: rejected tokens have higher entropy than accepted tokens, across both backbones, all benchmarks, and all checkpoint types. The entropy gap (∆Draft and ∆Verifier columns) is positive in nearly every row, with draft-entropy gaps typically in the range of +0.1 to +1.5 nats. This makes intuitive sense: a token that the draft model was uncertain about is more likely to be a token the target model would not have produced, and therefore more likely to be rejected. But the finding is diagnostically useful because it provides a **post-hoc interpretability tool**: if a particular benchmark, checkpoint, or depth shows large entropy gaps between accepted and rejected tokens, it indicates that the draft model's uncertainty is a primary driver of rejection, which in turn suggests that reducing draft uncertainty (e.g., via better training data alignment, temperature scaling, or confidence calibration) would improve acceptance length.

**Depth-wise acceptance as a window into exploration vs. exploitation.** Figure 8 and the per-depth tables in Appendices A.5–A.6 show that acceptance rate declines with speculative depth for all variants—this is expected because deeper tokens are harder to predict and have accumulated more opportunities for rejection at shallower levels. But the paper identifies a more subtle pattern: at shallow depths, mixed-data checkpoints often perform best, suggesting an **exploration benefit** where broader proposal coverage from mixed training increases the chance of producing acceptable early branches. At deeper depths, the task-matched specialist becomes more dominant, suggesting **exploitation behavior** where sustained acceptance depends on deep alignment between the drafter's reasoning trajectory and the target model's. This depth-dependent shift from coverage-benefit to alignment-benefit is a novel characterization of how speculative decoding quality decomposes across the tree, and it provides a vocabulary for thinking about tradeoffs in draft tree construction: early nodes should prioritize diversity (many plausible first steps), while deeper nodes should prioritize precision (only high-confidence continuations from the right domain).

**Comparison to prior work.** Prior speculative decoding work has used confidence for tree construction (EAGLE-2's path-confidence heuristic, Equation 6) and has observed that acceptance length declines with depth. But these observations were made in the context of single-domain drafters, where the question of *which* specialist to use does not arise. The paper's contribution is to show that confidence, entropy, and depth have **differential diagnostic value** when multiple specialists are available: confidence distinguishes domains, entropy diagnoses rejection, and depth reveals how the exploration-exploitation balance shifts across the tree. This is not a new method but a **conceptual toolkit** for reasoning about multi-specialist speculative decoding that the field did not previously have.

**Significance beyond performance.** This is a **framework contribution**—it provides categories and distinctions (routing signal vs. diagnostic signal, exploration depth vs. exploitation depth) that enable more precise reasoning about speculative decoding behavior. The specific numerical findings (confidence routes correctly ~90%+ of the time on domain-typical benchmarks; entropy gap between accepted and rejected tokens is consistently positive) are less important than the conceptual structure they support: future work can build on this taxonomy to design more sophisticated routing policies (e.g., confidence-weighted tree selection), to diagnose failure modes in new draft architectures, or to develop depth-adaptive tree construction strategies that explicitly balance exploration at shallow depths with exploitation at deeper depths.

### Innovation 5: The Temperature-Dependent Fragility of Mixed-Data Training

The paper's fifth contribution is more subtle but has practical bite: **mixed-data training, while effective at improving cross-domain robustness, does not produce uniformly better drafters across decoding temperatures**. This insight emerges from the comparison of Mixed 35k+35k and Mixed 70k+70k in Table 1 (RQ2) and qualifies the seemingly straightforward conclusion that "more and more diverse training data is better."

At temperature 0 (greedy decoding), the pattern is monotonic: Mixed 70k+70k is the strongest trained checkpoint overall, with average acceptance length 5.18 for HASS and 4.48 for EAGLE-2, exceeding both single-domain checkpoints on most benchmarks. This suggests that doubling the mixture size while maintaining domain balance produces a broadly capable drafter whose proposals are well-aligned with the target model's most likely tokens across both conversational and mathematical domains.

At temperature 1 (stochastic sampling), this pattern **reverses**: Mixed 70k+70k drops to 3.69 for HASS (vs. 4.29 for Mixed 35k+35k) and 3.26 for EAGLE-2 (vs. 3.81 for Mixed 35k+35k). The larger mixture, which was strongest under greedy decoding, becomes the weakest mixed-data variant under stochastic decoding. Even the single-domain checkpoints sometimes outperform the larger mixture at temperature 1—for example, MathInstruct HASS achieves 4.63 on MATH-500 while Mixed 70k+70k HASS achieves only 3.42.

**Why this is intellectually interesting.** This temperature-dependent fragility reveals that the alignment between draft and target is **distribution-level, not just mode-level**. At temperature 0, the draft model only needs to match the target model's most likely token at each position—a relatively constrained problem where broader training data can help by exposing the model to more patterns. At temperature 1, the draft model must match the target's *full distribution*, because the target may sample different tokens on different runs and the acceptance rule (Equation 1) compares probabilities over the entire vocabulary. The larger mixture may introduce distributional breadth that helps with mode-matching (temperature 0) but diffuses the draft model's probability mass in ways that hurt full-distribution alignment (temperature 1). The smaller mixture (35k+35k) may strike a better balance, providing enough cross-domain coverage to be robust without diluting the distributional precision needed for stochastic matching.

This finding has direct implications for practitioners: if a deployment uses greedy decoding (common in latency-sensitive applications where determinism is desired), training on the largest available mixed dataset is likely optimal. If the deployment uses stochastic sampling (common in creative or diverse generation tasks), a smaller but well-balanced mixture may outperform a larger one. The paper's evidence on this point is not exhaustive—only two mixture sizes are tested—but the qualitative pattern is clear enough to warrant attention.

**Comparison to prior work.** The speculative decoding literature has largely evaluated at temperature 0 or with a single temperature setting, treating temperature as a fixed hyperparameter rather than a variable that interacts with training data choices. The observation that the optimal training data configuration depends on decoding temperature is, to the best of the paper's characterization, novel to this work.

**Significance beyond performance.** This is a **boundary condition insight**—it identifies a regime where a seemingly dominant approach (more data = better) fails. Boundary conditions are valuable in empirical ML because they prevent overgeneralization: without this finding, a practitioner might reasonably conclude from temperature-0 results that training on the largest available mixture is always best, and then be surprised when deployment at temperature 1 underperforms. The paper provides the evidence needed to avoid that mistake, and it opens a question for future work: what is the relationship between training data composition and temperature-dependent draft-target alignment, and can it be characterized more systematically across mixture ratios, domain types, and temperature schedules?

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The paper evaluates on four benchmarks. MT-Bench contains 80 multi-turn conversational questions spanning writing, roleplay, reasoning, math, coding, extraction, STEM, and humanities. GSM8K contains 1,319 grade-school math word problems requiring multi-step arithmetic reasoning. MATH-500 contains 500 competition-level mathematics problems from the MATH dataset (Hendrycks et al., 2021), covering algebra, geometry, number theory, counting, and precalculus. SVAMP contains 300 elementary math word problems with varying structures designed to test mathematical reasoning robustness. All benchmarks are used with their standard publicly available test splits.

- **Base model(s).** The verifier (target model) is Meta-Llama-3-8B-Instruct (Llama Team, 2024), an 8-billion parameter instruction-tuned language model. The draft model is a lightweight LLaMA-style decoder with **one transformer layer, hidden size 4096, and approximately 0.8B parameters**, sharing the same tokenizer and vocabulary as the verifier so that acceptance differences are not confounded by tokenization mismatch. The choice of a single verifier and two draft backbones (HASS and EAGLE-2) is designed to test whether training distribution effects generalize across drafting architectures, not to claim optimality of any specific backbone.

- **Metrics.** The primary metric is **average acceptance length**, defined as the number of consecutively accepted draft tokens per verifier call, averaged over the evaluation distribution under the lossless speculative decoding constraint. Formally, `$\mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}}[A(x; M_D, M_T)]$` where `$A(x; M_D, M_T)$` counts how many draft tokens are accepted before the first rejection for input `$x$`. Higher acceptance length indicates better draft-target alignment and translates directly to throughput improvement (fewer verifier calls per output token). The paper also reports acceptance rate by speculative depth (the fraction of nodes at a given tree depth that are accepted) and per-token entropy for both accepted and rejected tokens as diagnostic metrics.

- **Baselines.** The paper's comparisons are internal: no external prior methods are used as baselines because the contribution is characterizing a previously unstudied variable (draft training distribution), not proposing a new speculative decoding algorithm. The key comparisons are: (1) **single-domain checkpoints** (MathInstruct-only vs. ShareGPT-only) to establish specialization; (2) **mixed-data checkpoints** (Mixed 35k+35k and Mixed 70k+70k) to test robustness; (3) **checkpoint averaging** (equal-weight parameter interpolation between the two single-domain checkpoints with `$\lambda = 0.5$`, Equation 11) as the weight-space composition baseline; (4) **confidence routing** and **merged-tree verification** as inference-time composition strategies.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper measures acceptance length—the number of tokens accepted per verifier call—as the primary metric. All draft checkpoints are trained with identical computational budgets: 20 epochs, learning rate `$3 \times 10^{-5}$`, batch size 8, gradient accumulation 1. HASS runs use identical auxiliary settings (Top-K distillation with `$K = 10$`, loss weight 1.0, three forward-alignment steps). The composition strategies introduce no additional training: confidence routing doubles the draft-generation cost (two draft trees are generated per input) but uses only one verifier call; merged-tree verification generates two draft trees and processes one larger merged tree through the verifier. The paper explicitly does not report end-to-end latency or throughput, noting that merged-tree verification's acceptance length benefit does not automatically translate to wall-clock speedup without a separate systems analysis.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not employ cross-validation or statistical significance testing. All checkpoints are trained once with fixed hyperparameters and evaluated on the standard test sets of the four benchmarks. The routing policy is deterministic (argmax of mean confidence), so there is no risk of overfitting routing decisions to the evaluation set. Conclusions are drawn from consistency across two independent backbones (HASS and EAGLE-2), two decoding temperatures (0 and 1), and multiple benchmarks spanning different domains, rather than from statistical tests on a single configuration. All training and evaluation experiments were run on a single node with four NVIDIA A100 GPUs.

### Main Quantitative Results

The paper's results are organized by research question, with Table 1 serving as the primary evidence for RQ1–RQ3 and Table 2, Figure 7, and Figure 8 supporting RQ4–RQ5.

#### RQ1: Task-Specific Training Produces Clear Domain Specialization (Table 1, RQ1 Block)

The single-domain checkpoint comparison in Table 1 establishes the baseline specialization pattern. Under HASS at temperature 0:

- **ShareGPT** achieves 3.98 acceptance length on MT-Bench versus MathInstruct's 2.90, a **+1.08 advantage** for the conversational specialist on the conversational benchmark.
- **MathInstruct** achieves 5.02 on GSM8K versus ShareGPT's 4.09 (+0.93), 5.35 on MATH-500 versus 3.98 (+1.37), and 3.13 on SVAMP versus 4.44 (−1.31, the one reversal on SVAMP where ShareGPT is stronger even for the math specialist).

Under EAGLE-2 at temperature 0, the pattern replicates with slightly different magnitudes:

- **ShareGPT** achieves 3.57 on MT-Bench versus MathInstruct's 2.54 (+1.03).
- **MathInstruct** achieves 5.04 on GSM8K versus ShareGPT's 3.72 (+1.32), 5.28 on MATH-500 versus 3.81 (+1.47), and 4.81 on SVAMP versus 3.71 (+1.10).

At temperature 1, the specialization persists but with compressed gaps. HASS MathInstruct achieves 4.75 on GSM8K versus ShareGPT's 4.03 (+0.72), down from the +0.93 gap at temperature 0. EAGLE-2 MathInstruct achieves 4.71 on GSM8K versus ShareGPT's 3.72 (+0.99), down from +1.32. This compression is expected: stochastic decoding requires matching the target's full distribution rather than just its mode, making the alignment problem harder and reducing the advantage of domain specialization.

The crossover pattern—each specialist stronger in its own domain, weaker in the other—is consistent across both backbones and both temperatures on GSM8K, MATH-500, and MT-Bench. SVAMP is the outlier where ShareGPT often matches or exceeds MathInstruct, likely because SVAMP's word problems involve more natural language variation than the symbolic math in GSM8K and MATH-500, blurring the conversational/reasoning boundary.

#### RQ2: Mixed-Data Training Improves Robustness but Is Temperature-Dependent (Table 1, RQ2 Block)

The mixed-data checkpoints are evaluated against the single-domain baselines to test whether combining data sources produces a single checkpoint that performs well across all benchmarks.

At temperature 0, **Mixed 70k+70k is the strongest trained checkpoint overall**. Under HASS, it achieves 5.53 on GSM8K (vs. 5.02 for MathInstruct alone), 5.67 on MATH-500 (vs. 5.35), 5.38 on SVAMP (vs. 3.13), and 4.13 on MT-Bench (vs. 3.98 for ShareGPT alone), yielding an average acceptance length of **5.18**—higher than any single-domain checkpoint (4.10 for MathInstruct, 4.12 for ShareGPT). Under EAGLE-2, it achieves an average of **4.48**, also exceeding both single-domain baselines (4.42 and 3.70). Doubling the mixture size while maintaining domain balance produces a broadly capable drafter whose proposals are well-aligned with the verifier's most likely tokens across domains.

At temperature 1, this pattern **reverses**. Mixed 70k+70k HASS drops to 3.69 average, below Mixed 35k+35k at 4.29 and below both single-domain checkpoints on several benchmarks. On MATH-500 specifically, Mixed 70k+70k HASS achieves only 3.42 versus 4.63 for MathInstruct alone and 4.47 for Mixed 35k+35k. Under EAGLE-2, the same pattern: Mixed 70k+70k drops to 3.26 average versus 3.81 for Mixed 35k+35k.

The **Mixed 35k+35k** variant shows more consistent behavior across temperatures: 4.47 average at temperature 0, 4.29 at temperature 1 under HASS. It is not the strongest variant at either temperature, but it is the most **stable**—it avoids the temperature-1 collapse that affects the larger mixture. This suggests that keeping the total sample count at 70k (the same as single-domain training) while balancing domains provides a favorable tradeoff: enough cross-domain exposure to avoid catastrophic failure on mismatched benchmarks, without the distributional diffusion that appears to hurt full-distribution matching at temperature 1 in the 140k-example mixture.

As noted in Section 3's discussion of temperature effects, the larger mixture may help mode-matching (temperature 0, where matching the verifier's most likely token suffices) but hurt distribution-matching (temperature 1, where the draft must align its full token distribution with the target's). The 35k+35k mixture provides enough domain coverage for robustness without the distributional breadth that degrades stochastic matching.

#### RQ3: Inference-Time Composition Substantially Outperforms Weight-Space Averaging (Table 1, RQ3 Block)

The three composition strategies are compared at equal backbones and temperatures. The headline finding is a large and consistent performance gap between weight-space composition (Averaged) and inference-time composition (Confidence Routed, Merged Trees).

**Checkpoint averaging fails decisively.** The Averaged checkpoint (equal-weight interpolation, `$\lambda = 0.5$`) is the weakest variant in Table 1 across nearly all configurations:

- HASS temperature 0: average acceptance length **2.59** (vs. 4.80 for Confidence Routed, 5.11 for Merged Trees). On MT-Bench, it achieves 2.29 (vs. 3.98 for ShareGPT alone). On GSM8K, 2.80 (vs. 5.02 for MathInstruct alone).
- EAGLE-2 temperature 0: average **2.42** (vs. 4.63 for Confidence Routed, 5.03 for Merged Trees).
- HASS temperature 1: average **2.62** (vs. 4.37 for Confidence Routed, 4.75 for Merged Trees).
- EAGLE-2 temperature 1: average **2.34** (vs. 4.27 for Confidence Routed, 4.57 for Merged Trees).

The interpolation sweep in Figure 6 shows that no choice of `$\lambda$` recovers acceptable performance: the acceptance length curve is not just low at `$\lambda = 0.5$` but unstable across the entire interpolation range, with no clear optimum. This demonstrates that the two specialists' parameters are not linearly combinable—averaging them produces interference rather than blending of complementary capabilities.

**Confidence routing consistently improves over single-domain baselines.** At temperature 0 under HASS, confidence routing achieves 4.80 average—substantially above MathInstruct alone (4.10) and ShareGPT alone (4.12), and above Mixed 35k+35k (4.47) though below Mixed 70k+70k (5.18). The benefit comes from per-input specialist selection: on MT-Bench, where ShareGPT is stronger (3.98 vs. MathInstruct's 2.90), routing achieves 3.93—essentially matching the better specialist. On MATH-500, where MathInstruct is stronger (5.35 vs. ShareGPT's 3.98), routing achieves 5.37—essentially matching the better specialist. On benchmarks where the gap between specialists is large (MATH-500: +1.37 for MathInstruct), routing recovers nearly all of the better specialist's performance. On benchmarks where the gap is small or ambiguous (SVAMP, where ShareGPT actually outperforms MathInstruct), routing's 4.89 modestly exceeds both specialists (MathInstruct 3.13, ShareGPT 4.44), suggesting that in some cases the routing mechanism selects the better model even when the expected specialist is not obvious.

Under EAGLE-2 at temperature 0, confidence routing achieves 4.63 average, above MathInstruct alone (4.42), ShareGPT alone (3.70), and Mixed 35k+35k (4.02), though below Mixed 70k+70k (4.48). The pattern of per-benchmark performance closely tracking the better specialist holds: 3.63 on MT-Bench (ShareGPT achieves 3.57), 4.91 on GSM8K (MathInstruct achieves 5.04), 5.25 on MATH-500 (MathInstruct achieves 5.28), 4.71 on SVAMP (MathInstruct achieves 4.81). The small gap between routing and the better specialist on each benchmark (typically 0.1–0.2 acceptance length) reflects routing errors—inputs where the wrong specialist is selected.

**Merged-tree verification achieves the highest acceptance length overall.** At temperature 0, merged trees reach:

- HASS: **5.11** average, with 4.05 on MT-Bench, 5.42 on GSM8K, 5.65 on MATH-500, 5.31 on SVAMP.
- EAGLE-2: **5.03** average, with 3.93 on MT-Bench, 5.32 on GSM8K, 5.63 on MATH-500, 5.25 on SVAMP.

These numbers exceed not only the single-domain checkpoints and confidence routing, but also Mixed 70k+70k (5.18 for HASS, 4.48 for EAGLE-2) on both backbones. The merged-tree strategy benefits from increased proposal diversity without suffering from routing errors: both specialists contribute candidates, and the verifier's acceptance rule naturally favors tokens from the better-aligned specialist without requiring a hard upfront choice. On MATH-500 under HASS, merged trees' 5.65 exceeds MathInstruct alone (5.35) and Mixed 70k+70k (5.67 is the only comparison where merged trees do not clearly win—they are essentially tied with the largest mixed checkpoint on this benchmark). On MT-Bench, merged trees' 4.05 exceeds ShareGPT alone (3.98) and confidence routing (3.93), suggesting that even on conversational prompts, the math specialist occasionally contributes useful proposals that boost acceptance beyond what the chat specialist alone achieves.

At temperature 1, merged trees remain the strongest variant: 4.75 HASS average, 4.57 EAGLE-2 average. The advantage over confidence routing (4.37 HASS, 4.27 EAGLE-2) persists, though the gap narrows slightly from temperature 0 (0.31–0.40 difference at temperature 0 vs. 0.30–0.38 at temperature 1).

A notable quantitative relationship: the progression Averaged < single-domain < Confidence Routed < Merged Trees holds for **every combination** of backbone and temperature in Table 1. At temperature 0, the improvement from Averaged to Merged Trees is roughly 2× for HASS (2.59 → 5.11) and EAGLE-2 (2.42 → 5.03). At temperature 1, the improvement is similar: 2.62 → 4.75 for HASS, 2.34 → 4.57 for EAGLE-2. This consistency across backbones and temperatures strengthens the claim that inference-time composition is fundamentally the right abstraction for multi-specialist drafting—it is not sensitive to the specific draft architecture or decoding regime.

The paper reports that compared to the strongest single checkpoint, confidence routing reduces average speedup by 0.32× and 0.35× under EAGLE-2 and by 0.40× and 0.47× under HASS at temperatures 0 and 1 respectively, while merged-tree verification incurs a larger drop of 0.59× and 0.62× under EAGLE-2 and 0.72× and 0.78× under HASS. These overhead figures account for the additional draft-generation cost (two trees instead of one) and, for merged trees, the larger verifier tree. The paper notes that in deployments serving two distinct task families, this overhead may be partly or fully offset when the best single checkpoint is weak on one of the tasks—a scenario where the specialist that dominates average acceptance length performs poorly on a minority workload, and composition recovers acceptance that the single drafter would lose.

#### RQ4: Confidence Routes Effectively; Entropy Diagnoses Rejection (Table 2, Figure 7)

Table 2 provides the benchmark-level routing counts for EAGLE-2 under confidence-based and entropy-based selection. The MathInstruct drafter is selected by confidence routing for:

- **90.8%** of GSM8K examples (1198 of 1319)
- **97.0%** of MATH-500 examples (485 of 500)
- **93.0%** of SVAMP examples (279 of 300)

The ShareGPT drafter is selected for **81.2%** of MT-Bench examples (65 of 80). These are sharp, domain-discriminative splits: confidence routing nearly always selects the domain-appropriate specialist, with MT-Bench showing the most balanced split (18.8% routed to MathInstruct), which is sensible given that MT-Bench includes some math and reasoning questions within its multi-turn conversations.

Under entropy-based routing—where the specialist with lower average entropy (higher certainty) is selected—the decisions are near-balanced:

- MT-Bench: 52.5% MathInstruct, 47.5% ShareGPT
- GSM8K: 54.6% MathInstruct, 45.4% ShareGPT
- MATH-500: 62.4% MathInstruct, 37.6% ShareGPT
- SVAMP: 53.0% MathInstruct, 47.0% ShareGPT

These splits provide essentially no useful domain signal. The MathInstruct drafter is slightly preferred on math benchmarks, but the margin is small (~55/45), and the ShareGPT drafter is incorrectly preferred on ~40% of math examples. Entropy routing fails because the draft model's overall uncertainty about its predictions does not strongly correlate with domain match—a math problem does not necessarily make the chat drafter more uncertain overall, it just makes its *specific proposals* less aligned with what the target would produce. Confidence captures proposal-alignment; entropy captures general uncertainty, which is more strongly influenced by prompt complexity, ambiguity, and length than by domain.

Figure 7(a) and 7(b) show the per-token entropy distributions for accepted and rejected tokens across EAGLE-2 and HASS at temperature 0. The consistent pattern is that **rejected tokens have higher entropy than accepted tokens**, visible as a rightward shift in the rejected-token histograms. The appendix tables (Tables 3–4) quantify this: under EAGLE-2 MathInstruct, draft entropy for accepted tokens on GSM8K is 0.5284 versus 1.0756 for rejected tokens (∆Draft = +0.5473); on MATH-500, 0.4567 versus 0.8555 (∆Draft = +0.3988); on MT-Bench, 2.3867 versus 3.8516 (∆Draft = +1.4649). The entropy gap is consistently positive across all checkpoints, benchmarks, and both backbones. The verifier-side entropy (computed from the target model's distribution at each position) also shows a positive gap (∆Verifier columns), typically smaller in magnitude than the draft-side gap but consistently in the same direction.

This entropy pattern is intuitive—tokens the draft model was uncertain about are more likely to be tokens the target model would not have produced—but its consistency across configurations establishes entropy as a reliable **post-hoc diagnostic**: if a particular checkpoint, benchmark, or depth shows an unusually large entropy gap, it indicates that draft uncertainty is a primary driver of rejection for that configuration.

#### RQ5: Depth-Wise Acceptance Reveals Exploration-Exploitation Shift (Figure 8, Appendix Tables A.5–A.6)

Figure 8 plots acceptance rate by speculative depth for the main draft variants across benchmarks, backbones, and temperatures. The overall pattern is that **acceptance declines with depth for all variants**—this is expected because deeper tokens are harder to predict and have survived conditioning on acceptance at all shallower levels. But the paper's analysis identifies depth-dependent shifts in which variants perform best.

At shallow depths (depth 1–2), **mixed-data checkpoints often perform best**. Looking at Appendix Table 5 (EAGLE-2, temperature 0): at depth 1, Mixed 35k+35k achieves the highest average acceptance rate (93.7%) across benchmarks, compared to MathInstruct (90.2%), ShareGPT (91.4%), and Mixed 70k+70k (82.0%). At depth 2, Mixed 35k+35k (80.8%) and MathInstruct (80.2%) are essentially tied, both above ShareGPT (75.7%). This suggests an **exploration benefit** at shallow depths: broader proposal coverage from mixed training increases the chance of producing acceptable first and second branches, because the drafter has seen more diverse patterns for how solutions begin across both domains.

At deeper depths (depth 4–5), the **task-matched specialist becomes more dominant**. For GSM8K at depth 5 under EAGLE-2 temperature 0 (Appendix Table 5): MathInstruct achieves 76.4% acceptance versus ShareGPT's 57.5% and Mixed 35k+35k's 61.9%. On MATH-500: MathInstruct achieves 81.5% versus ShareGPT's 65.5% and Mixed 35k+35k's 70.6%. On SVAMP: MathInstruct achieves 75.0% versus ShareGPT's 58.8%. The gap between the domain-matched specialist and all other variants widens with depth on reasoning benchmarks—a pattern that holds across both backbones and both temperatures (compare Appendix Tables 6, 7, and 8 for temperature-1 and HASS variants).

This depth-dependent shift from coverage-benefit to alignment-benefit is the paper's characterization of an **exploration-exploitation balance** in speculative drafting: early proposal steps benefit more from diversity (many plausible ways to start a solution), while deeper accepted paths increasingly favor sustained alignment between the drafter's reasoning trajectory and the target's, which only the domain-matched specialist can maintain over many consecutive tokens.

The merged-tree and confidence-routing results are consistent with this depth-wise pattern. Merged trees, by preserving diversity from both specialists at all depths, achieve the highest acceptance across the full tree. Confidence routing, by selecting the likely-better specialist per input, avoids the penalty of using the mismatched specialist at depth but cannot benefit from both specialists' proposals simultaneously.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Checkpoint averaging interpolation sweep (Figure 6):** The paper sweeps the interpolation weight `$\lambda$` in Equation 11 from 0 (pure ShareGPT) to 1 (pure MathInstruct) and measures acceptance length on each benchmark. The resulting curves are **non-monotonic and unstable**—there is no `$\lambda$` that achieves consistently high performance across benchmarks, and the average acceptance length never approaches the best single-domain checkpoint, let alone the inference-time composition methods. This demonstrates that the failure of weight-space averaging is not an artifact of choosing `$\lambda = 0.5$` but a fundamental property of the parameter space: the two specialists' representations interfere destructively across the entire interpolation path.

**Single-domain vs. mixed-data at two mixture scales (Table 1, RQ1 vs. RQ2):** Comparing Mixed 35k+35k against the single-domain checkpoints tests whether balancing domains at fixed total data volume (70k examples) recovers robustness. Under HASS at temperature 0, Mixed 35k+35k achieves 3.92 on MT-Bench—essentially matching ShareGPT's 3.98—while achieving 4.77 on GSM8K and 5.02 on MATH-500—close to MathInstruct's 5.02 and 5.35. The slight degradation on math benchmarks relative to the math specialist (e.g., 4.77 vs. 5.02 on GSM8K, a 0.25 drop) is modest compared to the catastrophic failure of the mismatched specialist on each benchmark (ShareGPT achieves only 4.09 on GSM8K). This demonstrates that a balanced 70k-example mixture recovers most of the specialization benefit from both domains with a single checkpoint.

Comparing Mixed 70k+70k against Mixed 35k+35k tests whether doubling the mixture size helps. At temperature 0, it does: 5.18 average vs. 4.47 for HASS, 4.48 vs. 4.02 for EAGLE-2. But at temperature 1, as discussed under RQ2, the larger mixture degrades: 3.69 vs. 4.29 for HASS, 3.26 vs. 3.81 for EAGLE-2. This temperature-dependent reversal is the paper's key finding on mixture scaling and is replicated across both backbones.

**Backbone consistency (all results):** Every major finding—single-domain specialization, mixed-data robustness, failure of averaging, superiority of inference-time composition, confidence routing effectiveness, entropy as rejection diagnostic, depth-wise specialization sharpening—is demonstrated for **both HASS and EAGLE-2**. The numerical values differ (HASS typically achieves slightly higher acceptance lengths than EAGLE-2 on reasoning benchmarks, while EAGLE-2 is slightly stronger on SVAMP), but the qualitative patterns are identical. This two-backbone replication is the paper's primary robustness mechanism: it rules out the possibility that the observed effects are idiosyncratic to a particular draft architecture or training procedure.

**Temperature consistency (all results):** All experiments are run at both temperature 0 and temperature 1. The specialization pattern (RQ1), the superiority of inference-time composition over averaging (RQ3), the effectiveness of confidence routing (RQ4), and the depth-wise specialization sharpening (RQ5) all hold across both temperatures, with the expected magnitude compression at temperature 1 (stochastic decoding is harder to match). The only finding that interacts with temperature is the mixed-data scaling pattern (RQ2), where the relative ranking of Mixed 70k+70k and Mixed 35k+35k reverses between temperatures.

**Domain coverage across benchmarks:** The paper uses four benchmarks spanning two domain types (conversational: MT-Bench; mathematical reasoning: GSM8K, MATH-500, SVAMP at different difficulty levels). The consistency of findings across the three math benchmarks (with SVAMP as a partial exception where the math-chat boundary is blurrier) and the clear contrast with MT-Bench provides evidence that the effects are domain-driven rather than benchmark-idiosyncratic.

**Correctness proofs for composition strategies (Appendix A.4):** Propositions A.1 and A.2 provide formal proofs that confidence routing and merged-tree verification preserve the target model's output distribution (the lossless guarantee). These are not empirical ablations but theoretical checks that the composition strategies do not violate the fundamental property of speculative decoding. The proofs rely on the verifier invariance lemma (Lemma A.2), which establishes that a transformer verifier produces identical logits on a subset of indices when the packed inputs agree on tokens, positions, and within-subset attention, and when indices in the subset do not attend outside it. This lemma directly supports the merged-tree construction, where each specialist's subtree satisfies these isolation conditions.

### Critical Assessment

**Claim 1: Task-specific training produces clear domain specialization (RQ1).** Strongly supported. The crossover pattern—MathInstruct stronger on reasoning benchmarks, ShareGPT stronger on MT-Bench—holds for both backbones and both temperatures. The evidence is direct: changing only the training data (not architecture, hyperparameters, or verifier) produces a ~1.0–1.5 acceptance length swing per benchmark. The replication across HASS and EAGLE-2 rules out architecture-specific explanations.

However, the claim is demonstrated only for the specific MathInstruct/ShareGPT domain split with Llama-3-8B-Instruct as the verifier. The paper does not test whether specialization holds for other domain pairs (e.g., code generation vs. conversational, scientific QA vs. creative writing), other verifier families (e.g., Mistral, Qwen, Gemma), or other verifier scales (e.g., 70B parameter models). The finding that specialization exists is robust for the tested configuration; the generality of the finding to arbitrary domain pairs and verifiers is an extrapolation the paper does not test.

Additionally, SVAMP is a partial counterexample: ShareGPT often outperforms MathInstruct on this math benchmark (e.g., 4.44 vs. 3.13 for HASS at temperature 0), suggesting that the conversational/math boundary is not perfectly clean and that "matched domain" is a spectrum rather than a binary. The paper would be strengthened by a more detailed characterization of *which types* of math problems benefit from math-specific training versus which benefit from conversational training—perhaps SVAMP's linguistic variation makes conversational training more relevant, but this hypothesis is not tested.

**Claim 2: Mixed-data training improves robustness but does not uniformly dominate (RQ2).** Supported, with the temperature-dependent caveat well-characterized. The finding that Mixed 35k+35k provides stable cross-domain performance while Mixed 70k+70k is temperature-fragile is demonstrated quantitatively in Table 1. However, the paper tests only two mixture scales (70k total, 140k total) at one mixture ratio (1:1 MathInstruct:ShareGPT). The space of possible mixture configurations is large—varying the ratio (e.g., 3:1 math-to-chat for a math-heavy deployment), the total data volume, and the domain types. The paper's claim that "larger mixtures do not uniformly improve generalization across decoding temperatures" is supported for the specific 1:1 ratio tested, but the possibility that a different ratio or a larger mixture with temperature-aware data curation could recover temperature-1 robustness is not explored.

A missing ablation: training on 70k ShareGPT followed by 70k MathInstruct (or vice versa) in a staged curriculum rather than jointly. The paper's mixed training interleaves both domains throughout training; a curriculum approach might avoid the distributional diffusion that the paper hypothesizes hurts temperature-1 performance in the larger mixture.

**Claim 3: Inference-time composition substantially outperforms weight-space averaging (RQ3).** Very strongly supported. The performance gap between Averaged (2.34–2.62) and the inference-time methods (4.27–5.11) is large and consistent across all configurations. The interpolation sweep in Figure 6 demonstrates that the failure is not due to suboptimal `$\lambda$` choice. The paper provides both empirical evidence (Table 1) and a conceptual explanation (specialization is not linearly composable in parameter space) that together make a compelling case.

The primary limitation is that the paper tests only one weight-space composition method (linear interpolation). Other parameter-space combination techniques exist—task arithmetic with sign consensus (Ilharco et al., 2022), Fisher-weighted merging, learned linear combinations, low-rank adapter merging—and some of these might recover better performance. The paper's claim is specific to "naive weight-space averaging" (as stated in the abstract), not to all possible parameter-space composition methods. The failure of the simplest method is an important negative result, but it does not close the door on more sophisticated weight-space techniques.

A practical caveat: the paper reports that merged-tree verification reduces speedup by 0.59–0.78× relative to the strongest single checkpoint due to the increased cost of generating and verifying two trees. This overhead is substantial and means that merged trees' acceptance length advantage (e.g., 5.11 vs. 5.18 for HASS Mixed 70k+70k at temperature 0 on some benchmarks) does not automatically translate to end-to-end throughput improvement. The paper is transparent about this (Section "Discussion and Limitations": "we do not claim an end-to-end latency improvement without a separate systems analysis"), but readers may overinterpret the acceptance length numbers as speedup predictions. In practice, whether merged trees or confidence routing is preferable depends on the relative cost of draft generation, tree verification, and the frequency of routing errors in the deployment workload.

**Claim 4: Confidence is a useful routing signal; entropy is mainly diagnostic (RQ4).** Well-supported for the specific routing policy tested (mean-confidence argmax). Table 2 shows sharp benchmark-level discrimination from confidence routing (90%+ correct specialist selection on math benchmarks, 81% on MT-Bench) and near-chance discrimination from entropy routing. Figure 7 and the appendix entropy tables show the consistent accepted-vs-rejected entropy gap.

The paper does not report per-input routing accuracy—Table 2 shows benchmark-level counts, which measure how often each specialist is selected for inputs from each benchmark, but does not report whether those selections were *correct* (i.e., whether the selected specialist actually achieved higher acceptance length on that specific input than the alternative would have). It is possible that on some inputs, confidence routing selects the "wrong" specialist by benchmark label but that specialist actually performs better on that particular input (e.g., a math problem embedded in a conversational turn on MT-Bench). The paper's implicit assumption is that benchmark membership proxies for the optimal specialist, which is reasonable but imperfect. A stronger analysis would compare the acceptance length achieved by the routed specialist against the counterfactual acceptance length of the non-selected specialist on the same input—requiring running both specialists' proposals through the verifier for every input, which the current experimental setup likely does not do (routing only verifies the selected tree).

The claim that "entropy is a useful diagnostic of rejection" is well-supported by the consistent positive ∆Draft and ∆Verifier in Tables 3–4, but the paper does not explore whether this diagnostic signal can be used proactively—for example, by using high entropy as a signal to abort a draft tree early, or to adjust the draft model's sampling temperature on the fly. The finding is descriptive, not prescriptive.

**Claim 5: Speculative decoding is depth-aware, with exploration dominating early depths and exploitation dominating later depths (RQ5).** Supported by the per-depth tables and Figure 8, but the evidence is pattern-level rather than causal. The paper observes that mixed-data checkpoints often perform best at depth 1–2 and that the task-matched specialist dominates at depth 4–5. This is a characterization of the data, not a demonstration that exploration or exploitation *causes* the pattern. Alternative explanations are possible: perhaps mixed-data checkpoints perform well at shallow depths simply because they are better at predicting the first token (which is often a formatting or structural token that is less domain-specific), while the task-matched specialist's advantage at deeper depths reflects domain-specific vocabulary and reasoning patterns that only emerge after several tokens. The exploration/exploitation framing is an interpretation, not a directly tested mechanism.

**Overall strengths of the experimental design:**
- The two-backbone (HASS, EAGLE-2), two-temperature (0, 1), four-benchmark design provides strong internal replication.
- Holding verifier, architecture, and hyperparameters fixed while varying only training data and composition strategy isolates the variables of interest cleanly.
- The inclusion of a strong negative result (checkpoint averaging fails) adds credibility—the paper is not just reporting what works but systematically testing plausible alternatives and showing they don't work.
- The correctness proofs in Appendix A.4 are a nice touch that addresses a potential concern (do the composition strategies break the lossless guarantee?) that readers familiar with speculative decoding theory might have.

**Overall weaknesses and missing experiments:**
- **Single verifier, single scale.** All experiments use Llama-3-8B-Instruct. The paper does not test whether specialization patterns hold for verifiers of different scales (e.g., 70B), different families (Mistral, Qwen), or different training paradigms (base models vs. instruction-tuned). The interaction between verifier capability and draft specialization is unknown—a stronger verifier might be harder to draft for, potentially amplifying or attenuating the specialization effect.
- **No end-to-end latency measurements.** Acceptance length is an algorithmic metric; actual throughput depends on hardware, batching, draft inference time, and tree verification overhead. The paper explicitly scopes out latency analysis, but this means the practical significance of the acceptance length differences (e.g., 5.11 vs. 4.80 for merged trees vs. confidence routing) is not established in deployment-relevant terms.
- **Two-domain coverage only.** Math and conversational domains are tested; code generation, multilingual, retrieval-augmented, and other domain types are not. The claim that "draft training distribution matters" is demonstrated for math/chat but may not generalize to domain pairs with different relationships (e.g., code vs. conversational, where the token distributions might overlap more).
- **No adaptive or learned routing.** Confidence routing is a hard binary selection based on mean tree confidence. The paper does not test soft routing (e.g., confidence-weighted tree combination), learned routing (training a small classifier to predict which specialist will achieve higher acceptance), or cost-aware routing (selecting the cheaper specialist when confidence is close). These could close the gap between confidence routing and merged trees.
- **Small number of mixture configurations.** Two mixture scales at one ratio. Exploring the Pareto frontier of mixture composition (varying ratio, total volume, domain count) would strengthen the mixed-data robustness claims.
- **No statistical uncertainty quantification.** Table 1 reports point estimates of average acceptance length with no confidence intervals or standard errors. With 80 MT-Bench examples and 100-example difficulty bins (500 MATH-500 questions split into difficulty quintiles would give ~100 per bin, though the paper does not do this split), some of the acceptance length differences may not be statistically significant. The paper's reliance on pattern replication across configurations mitigates this concern but does not eliminate it.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### Acceptance Length as a Proxy Without End-to-End Latency Validation

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper uses average acceptance length—the number of consecutively accepted draft tokens per verifier call—as its primary and effectively sole evaluation metric. All comparisons, rankings, and claimed improvements across draft variants, training distributions, and composition strategies are based on this single algorithmic metric. End-to-end latency, throughput in tokens per second, memory consumption, and total generation time are explicitly excluded from measurement. The paper states this scope clearly in Section "Discussion and Limitations":

> "Acceptance length is our primary metric, so this paper does not establish end-to-end deployment trade-offs for routing or merged-tree verification."

This is a deliberate methodological choice—acceptance length abstracts away hardware-specific factors—but it means the paper's practical claims are about algorithmic efficiency, not realized system performance.

**The consequence.** Acceptance length does not account for the computational cost of producing those accepted tokens. Each composition strategy has different overhead characteristics that the metric ignores entirely:

- **Confidence routing** requires generating *two* complete draft trees (one from each specialist) per verifier call, then scoring both by mean confidence before selecting one. The cost of generating the discarded tree is pure overhead that reduces throughput relative to a single-draft baseline with the same average acceptance length. The paper notes that compared to the strongest single checkpoint, confidence routing reduces speedup by 0.32× to 0.47× depending on backbone and temperature (Section "Discussion and Limitations"), but these overhead figures are computed from acceptance length ratios assuming ideal scaling—they do not include actual draft-generation latency, tree-construction time, or the cost of computing confidence scores.

- **Merged-tree verification** incurs an even larger cost: two complete draft trees must be generated, packed into a merged tree (Algorithm 1), and processed by the verifier in a single parallel pass where the verifier evaluates roughly twice as many nodes as a single-tree approach. The paper reports speedup reductions of 0.59× to 0.78× relative to the strongest single checkpoint (Section "Discussion and Limitations"). For a practitioner, this means that even though merged trees achieve the highest acceptance length (5.11 for HASS at temperature 0), the actual tokens-per-second throughput might be *lower* than confidence routing or even a well-tuned single-draft baseline, depending on the relative cost of the verifier's parallel pass versus draft generation.

- **The interaction with batching** is not explored. In a batched inference setting where multiple requests are processed simultaneously, the verifier's parallel pass cost is amortized, but draft generation is not. A strategy that generates two draft trees per request may become proportionally more expensive under batching than a strategy that generates one, further complicating the translation from acceptance length to throughput.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 1 provides only acceptance length numbers with no latency or throughput measurements. The overhead multipliers in Section "Discussion and Limitations" (0.32× to 0.78× speedup reduction) are derived analytically from acceptance length accounting—they assume draft generation cost is proportional to tree size and verifier cost is proportional to tree size, which may not hold in practice due to hardware factors like memory bandwidth, kernel launch overhead, and the different computational profiles of draft models (small, shallow) versus verifiers (large, deep). No wall-clock time measurements, GPU utilization data, or throughput benchmarks are reported. The hardware specification—"a single node with four NVIDIA A100 GPUs" (Section 1)—is provided for training, not for inference latency measurement.

**Mitigation status.** The paper is transparent about this limitation, explicitly scoping its contribution to acceptance length analysis and noting the need for future systems-level evaluation. The authors state: "we do not claim an end-to-end latency improvement without a separate systems analysis" (Section "Discussion and Limitations"). However, this transparency does not resolve the limitation for practitioners: the paper provides no guidance on when the acceptance length gains from merged trees or confidence routing would outweigh their overhead in a real deployment. A reader evaluating whether to adopt these methods has an algorithmic signal (acceptance length improves) without the systems signal (throughput may or may not improve, and the paper does not characterize the crossover point).

### Single Verifier Model and Scale

**The assumption or constraint.** All experiments use a single verifier: Meta-Llama-3-8B-Instruct, an 8-billion parameter instruction-tuned model. The paper proposes no variation in verifier architecture, parameter scale, pretraining corpus, or fine-tuning methodology. The draft model is a lightweight LLaMA-style decoder with approximately 0.8B parameters sharing the same tokenizer and vocabulary as this specific verifier. To its credit, the paper acknowledges this boundary:

> "the present evidence is narrower than the strongest version of the claim. We evaluate one target model, two source domains, two speculative backbones, and four benchmarks."

**The consequence.** The draft training distribution effects characterized in the paper may not generalize across verifier characteristics in several plausible ways:

- **Verifier scale.** A larger verifier (e.g., 70B parameters) produces a different next-token distribution `$q(\cdot)$` than an 8B verifier—it may be more confident, more calibrated, or qualitatively different in its token preferences. The draft model's job is to match `$q$`, and the difficulty of this matching task, the sensitivity of acceptance length to domain alignment, and the optimal training data composition could all shift with verifier scale. A 70B verifier might be harder to draft for (requiring more specialized draft training to achieve acceptable acceptance lengths) or easier to draft for (its distribution might be sharper and more predictable, making domain alignment less critical). The paper provides no evidence either way.

- **Verifier family and pretraining distribution.** Llama-3-8B-Instruct was pretrained on a specific data mixture and fine-tuned with a specific instruction-tuning recipe. A verifier from a different family (Mistral, Qwen, Gemma) or with a different pretraining corpus may exhibit different domain specializations in its own distribution. If, for example, a Mistral-based verifier is stronger at mathematical reasoning than conversational generation due to its pretraining data composition, the optimal draft training distribution might shift—a math-specialized drafter might match such a verifier better even on "conversational" benchmarks, or the conversational/math domain split might be less relevant. The paper's findings about MathInstruct vs. ShareGPT specialization are potentially specific to the Llama-3-8B-Instruct's particular domain balance.

- **Base model vs. instruction-tuned verifier.** The paper uses an instruction-tuned verifier. Base models (pretrained only, no instruction tuning) produce different next-token distributions—typically less calibrated, with more probability mass on formatting tokens, artifacts, and off-topic continuations. Whether draft training distribution effects are amplified or attenuated for base-model verifiers is unknown.

- **Tokenizer sharing.** The draft and verifier share the exact same tokenizer, which is a deliberate choice to avoid tokenization-mismatch confounds. In practice, this may not hold: a deployment might use a draft model from a different family with a slightly different tokenizer (e.g., a Llama-based draft assisting a Mistral-based verifier). The paper's findings are specific to the matched-tokenizer setting and do not address whether domain specialization effects persist or are overwhelmed by tokenization mismatches when tokenizers differ.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None—the verifier is held fixed across all experiments by design. The paper provides no ablation over verifier scale, family, or training paradigm. The findings' generalizability to other verifiers is an untested extrapolation.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges the single-verifier limitation in its discussion section but does not attempt to mitigate it through additional experiments or theoretical arguments. The strength of the paper's internal replication (two backbones, two temperatures, four benchmarks) establishes that the findings are robust within the tested configuration, but this does not address the question of whether the configuration itself is representative. A practitioner using a different verifier must treat the paper's findings as suggestive rather than directly applicable.

### Two-Domain Coverage with Unclear Domain Boundaries

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper studies exactly two training domains—MathInstruct (mathematical reasoning) and ShareGPT (conversational generation)—and evaluates on four benchmarks that map roughly to these two domain types: MT-Bench as conversational, GSM8K/MATH-500/SVAMP as mathematical reasoning. The claim that "task-specific training produces clear domain specialization" is demonstrated only for this specific domain pair.

**The consequence.** This two-domain coverage limits the paper's findings in two distinct ways:

- **Generality to other domain pairs is untested.** The paper's core insight—that draft training distribution matters for acceptance length—may or may not hold for domain pairs where the overlap in token distributions, reasoning patterns, or stylistic conventions is larger or smaller than the math/chat split. Code generation vs. conversational, scientific question answering vs. creative writing, multilingual vs. monolingual, retrieval-augmented vs. closed-book—each domain pair has a different degree of distributional separation, and the paper provides no evidence about where specialization effects are large versus negligible. A practitioner working in a domain not studied (e.g., legal document summarization vs. medical question answering) cannot infer from this paper alone whether domain-specific draft training would help.

- **Even within the tested domains, the boundary is blurry.** The SVAMP benchmark—classified as mathematical reasoning—is a partial counterexample to the specialization claim. Under HASS at temperature 0, ShareGPT (4.44) outperforms MathInstruct (3.13) on SVAMP, even though MathInstruct is the "math specialist." This suggests that SVAMP's word problems, which involve natural language variation and real-world scenarios, sit in a gray area where conversational training is actually more beneficial than math-specific training. The paper notes this SVAMP reversal (Section 5, RQ1 discussion) but does not characterize *which types* of math problems benefit from math-specific versus conversational training. The domain "matched/unmatched" binary the paper uses for its conclusions is a simplification—domain alignment is a spectrum, and the paper's two-domain setup does not explore where on that spectrum specialization effects emerge, peak, or reverse.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 1 includes SVAMP alongside GSM8K and MATH-500 as math benchmarks, and the SVAMP reversal is visible in the numbers but not deeply analyzed. The paper does not provide per-question difficulty analysis for math benchmarks—it does not distinguish, for example, between algebra word problems (more linguistic) and symbolic manipulation problems (more mathematical) within MATH-500, which could reveal whether the specialization effect is driven by a subset of problem types. The domain analysis is limited to benchmark-level aggregation.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges the limited domain coverage (two domains) in its discussion section but does not test additional domain pairs. The SVAMP reversal is noted as an observation but not investigated further. The paper's conclusions about "task-specific training" and "domain specialization" are qualified by the specific MathInstruct/ShareGPT split, but a reader could easily overgeneralize to "any domain pair," which the paper's evidence does not support.

### No Adaptive or Learned Routing; Binary Selection Only

**The assumption or constraint.** Confidence routing (Section "Inference-Time Composition") makes a hard binary decision: for each input, generate two complete draft trees, compute the mean confidence score for each, and pass only the higher-scoring tree to the verifier. The routing rule is deterministic (argmax), non-learned (no trained routing classifier), and based on a single scalar summary statistic (mean tree confidence). The paper explicitly characterizes this as intentionally simple:

> "The routing policy is intentionally simple and confidence-based rather than learned or cost-aware."

**The consequence.** This binary, threshold-free routing has several failure modes that the paper does not analyze or attempt to address:

- **Wrong specialist selection with no fallback.** If confidence routing selects the wrong specialist for a particular input—for example, routing a math problem to the ShareGPT specialist—the verifier only sees the mismatched specialist's proposals. There is no mechanism to detect low-quality proposals mid-verification and fall back to the alternative specialist. The acceptance length on that input degrades to whatever the mismatched specialist achieves, which on MATH-500 under HASS is 3.98 vs. the math specialist's 5.35—a ~26% reduction. The paper's Table 2 shows that confidence routing selects the "correct" specialist (by benchmark label) 81–97% of the time, meaning 3–19% of inputs get the wrong specialist with no recourse.

- **No ability to combine partial signals.** When both specialists produce reasonable but imperfect proposals—for example, on SVAMP where the math/chat boundary is blurry—confidence routing must pick one and discard the other, even though the verifier might accept some tokens from each. Merged-tree verification addresses this by submitting both trees, but at higher computational cost. A more sophisticated routing policy could combine the best elements of both trees (e.g., selecting the higher-confidence specialist for early tokens and the other for later tokens, or using per-token rather than per-tree confidence) without the full cost of merged-tree verification. The paper does not explore this middle ground.

- **No cost-awareness.** Confidence routing always generates two trees and always verifies one. It never decides that the input is so clearly in one domain that generating the second tree is unnecessary (which could save draft-generation cost), nor that the input is so ambiguous that both trees should be verified (which would fall back to merged-tree behavior). A cost-aware routing policy could adaptively allocate computation: generate the first tree, assess its quality, and decide whether to generate the second based on some confidence threshold. The paper's fixed two-trees-then-select approach represents only one point in a larger space of adaptive strategies.

- **No learned routing model.** A small classifier trained to predict which specialist will achieve higher acceptance length on a given input—perhaps using prompt features, draft model confidence distributions, or lightweight verifier probing—could potentially outperform the simple mean-confidence heuristic. The paper does not test whether such a learned router would close the gap between confidence routing and merged-tree verification, or whether the mean-confidence signal is already near the achievable ceiling.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 2 provides benchmark-level routing counts, showing that confidence routing makes correct-by-label decisions ~90%+ of the time on math benchmarks. However, the paper does not report per-input routing accuracy (whether the selected specialist actually achieved higher acceptance length on that specific input than the non-selected specialist would have). Benchmark-level counts assume that the domain-matched specialist is always optimal, which the SVAMP counterexample calls into question. The paper also does not report the distribution of confidence scores across inputs—it is possible that on the 3–19% of inputs where routing selects the "wrong" specialist, the confidence gap between the two trees is small, and a soft combination (rather than hard selection) would have performed better.

**Mitigation status.** The paper positions confidence routing as a baseline for inference-time composition, not as an optimal solution. The simplicity is intentional, and the paper acknowledges that "more sophisticated routing policies... could potentially outperform this baseline" (Section 3.4, Confidence-Based Routing discussion, paraphrased from the paper's description of its routing as "intentionally simple"). However, the paper does not characterize the performance ceiling: it is unclear how much of the gap between confidence routing (4.80 average HASS temperature 0) and merged-tree verification (5.11) is due to routing errors that could be corrected by a better router, versus being due to the inherent value of having both specialists' proposals available simultaneously. A learned or adaptive router could potentially close some or all of this gap, but the paper provides no evidence on this point.

### Difficulty Estimation Cost and Static Allocation for Composition

**The assumption or constraint.** Unlike many test-time compute optimization papers that require explicit difficulty estimation (see companion papers in this genre), TAPS does not estimate prompt difficulty explicitly—it routes based on draft confidence, which is computed automatically during tree generation. However, the composition strategies incur a different kind of unaccounted cost: **the overhead of generating and sometimes discarding entire draft trees is amortized over all inputs equally, with no mechanism to vary the composition strategy based on input characteristics.** Every input gets the same treatment: two trees generated, then either one selected (routing) or both verified (merged).

**The consequence.** This uniform allocation has two practical problems:

- **Wasted computation on easy-to-route inputs.** For inputs where the domain is unambiguous (e.g., a pure algebra problem or a straightforward conversational turn), generating the second specialist's tree is likely wasteful—the correct specialist is obvious, and the second tree would be discarded by routing or would contribute negligible benefit in merged-tree verification. A deployment serving a high volume of such inputs could reduce draft-generation cost by ~50% if it could identify these cases and skip the second tree. The paper provides no mechanism for doing so.

- **Insufficient computation on hard-to-route or ambiguous inputs.** For inputs where both specialists could contribute useful proposals (SVAMP-like problems, or inputs that combine conversational framing with mathematical content), the paper's strategies are fixed: routing picks one and discards the other (potentially losing useful proposals), while merged trees packs both but at fixed cost. There is no adaptive mechanism that decides, based on initial confidence signals or partial tree quality, to allocate more or fewer specialists' proposals per input. The paper's acceptance length numbers are averages over all inputs; the per-input variance in specialist utility is not characterized.

This limitation is structurally analogous to the difficulty estimation overhead discussed in other test-time compute papers (where the cost of estimating prompt difficulty before allocating compute is not amortized into the reported efficiency gains), but it manifests differently: here the overhead is in the form of uniform over-generation (always producing two trees) rather than explicit difficulty probing. Both represent unaccounted costs that reduce the practical efficiency gains relative to the headline metric.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper does not analyze per-input routing confidence distributions, does not report the standard deviation of acceptance length across inputs, and does not measure how often the "discarded" tree would have been better than the selected one. Table 2 provides benchmark-level routing fractions, but these are coarse aggregates—the paper does not characterize input-level ambiguity. The overhead multipliers (0.32× to 0.78× speedup reduction) are computed as averages, not as distributions over inputs.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation directly. The routing policy is fixed and uniform. The paper's acknowledgment that the routing policy is "intentionally simple" and that future work could explore learned or cost-aware policies implicitly recognizes this gap, but no adaptive allocation mechanism is proposed or evaluated. A practitioner deploying these methods in a cost-sensitive setting would need to develop their own heuristics for when to skip the second specialist, which the paper does not guide.

---

### Mixed-Data Training Evaluated at Only Two Mixture Scales and One Ratio

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's investigation of mixed-data training (RQ2) tests exactly two configurations: Mixed 35k+35k (70,000 total examples, 1:1 ratio) and Mixed 70k+70k (140,000 total examples, 1:1 ratio). The mixture ratio is fixed at 1:1 for both variants. The paper draws conclusions about mixture scaling—particularly the temperature-dependent reversal where Mixed 70k+70k outperforms Mixed 35k+35k at temperature 0 but underperforms at temperature 1—based on these two data points.

**The consequence.** The space of possible mixed-data configurations is multidimensional (domain ratio, total volume, domain count, data quality, curriculum ordering), and the paper's two-point exploration provides limited insight into where the Pareto-optimal configurations lie:

- **Optimal ratio is unexplored.** A 1:1 MathInstruct:ShareGPT ratio assumes equal importance of both domains. A deployment serving 80% conversational traffic and 20% math traffic might prefer a 4:1 ratio favoring ShareGPT. The paper provides no evidence about how acceptance length varies with mixture ratio—whether the relationship is monotonic, whether there is a "sweet spot" ratio, or whether the temperature-dependent fragility observed at 1:1 persists at other ratios.

- **Temperature-dependent mechanism is hypothesized but untested.** The paper suggests that the larger mixture's temperature-1 degradation occurs because broader training data "diffuses the draft model's probability mass" (Section 4, Innovation 5), hurting full-distribution matching at temperature 1 while helping mode-matching at temperature 0. This is a plausible hypothesis, but the paper does not test it—it does not measure the draft model's distributional calibration, token-level probability concentration, or per-token entropy as a function of mixture size. The mechanism could instead be related to optimization dynamics (the larger mixture requires more epochs to converge), data quality differences between MathInstruct and ShareGPT, or interactions with the draft model's limited capacity (0.8B parameters may not have enough representational capacity to model both distributions precisely at the larger data volume). The paper's evidence is an observation (the ranking reverses) without a demonstrated cause.

- **Curriculum vs. interleaved training is not compared.** All mixed-data checkpoints interleave MathInstruct and ShareGPT examples throughout training. A staged curriculum—for example, training on 70k ShareGPT examples first, then fine-tuning on 70k MathInstruct examples—might produce different specialization and robustness characteristics than joint interleaved training. The catastrophic forgetting and sequential learning literatures suggest that staged training often produces different outcomes than joint training, and the paper's mixed-data conclusions are specific to the interleaved approach.

- **Domain count is fixed at two.** The paper does not test mixtures involving three or more domains (e.g., MathInstruct + ShareGPT + a code generation dataset). The scalability of mixed-data training to many domains—whether acceptance length degrades gracefully or collapses once the number of domains exceeds some threshold relative to the draft model's capacity—is completely unexplored.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 1, RQ2 block, provides acceptance length for the two mixture configurations. Figure 8 (depth-wise acceptance) and the appendix tables break down these mixtures by depth, but do not provide additional insight into *why* the temperature-1 reversal occurs. The paper does not ablate the mixture ratio, the curriculum ordering, or the domain count.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not claim to have fully characterized the mixture design space—its conclusions are appropriately scoped to the specific configurations tested. However, the practical significance of the temperature-dependent reversal finding depends on whether it represents a general principle ("larger mixtures hurt temperature-1 performance") or an artifact of the specific 1:1 ratio and 140k data volume. A practitioner reading this paper cannot determine whether training on 100k+100k mixed data would be beneficial or harmful for their temperature-1 deployment, or whether a 3:1 ratio would avoid the problem entirely. The paper's findings establish that temperature interacts with mixture scale, which is valuable, but the degree of generality remains unknown.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper makes a **conceptual reframing contribution** rather than a paradigm shift: it takes a variable the speculative decoding literature has treated as a fixed background constant—the draft model's training distribution—and demonstrates that it is in fact a **first-class design variable with effects on acceptance length comparable in magnitude to architectural innovations**. This is not a new algorithm, a new loss function, or a new verification procedure; it is a controlled empirical demonstration that **what you train the drafter on matters at least as much as how you train it**.

The magnitude of this reframing should not be overstated. The paper does not upend the speculative decoding framework, introduce a new theoretical insight about lossless verification, or challenge the fundamental efficiency properties of draft-then-verify pipelines. What it does is change **where researchers and practitioners should focus their attention** when building speculative decoding systems. Before this work, the default assumption was: pick a good draft architecture (EAGLE-2, HASS, or the latest variant), train on whatever broad conversational corpus is available, and deploy. The paper shows that this assumption leaves substantial acceptance length on the table—a MathInstruct-trained HASS drafter on MATH-500 achieves 5.35 versus a ShareGPT-trained drafter's 3.98, a 34% relative improvement from changing only the training data. This is comparable to or larger than the gains reported from architecture-level innovations in prior work, yet it comes from a variable the field was not systematically manipulating.

The reframing has downstream consequences for how the speculative decoding community allocates research effort:

**Research directions that become more attractive:**

- **Draft training data curation and domain analysis.** The paper makes a strong empirical case that training data composition is a design choice that deserves the same level of methodological attention as architecture design. This opens a space for systematic studies of: which domain pairs produce the largest specialization effects; how to characterize domain boundaries in terms of token distribution overlap; whether domain-specific drafters can be automatically identified from downstream workload characteristics; and how to construct training mixtures that balance coverage against specialization at specific decoding temperatures. This is a data-centric research program (analogous to data-centric AI in other subfields) applied to speculative decoding.

- **Inference-time composition as a core capability.** The paper's strongest positive result—that keeping specialists separate and composing them at test time (confidence routing: 4.80, merged trees: 5.11 for HASS at temperature 0) dramatically outperforms weight-space merging (2.59)—establishes inference-time composition as the natural abstraction for multi-specialist drafting. This shifts attention away from trying to train a single universally-good drafter (which the mixed-data results show is fragile, particularly across temperatures) and toward developing more sophisticated composition mechanisms: learned routers, adaptive tree packing, cost-aware specialist selection, and hierarchical verification schemes that allocate verifier computation based on draft confidence signals.

- **Draft model as a systems choice rather than a fixed component.** The paper's depth-wise analysis (Figure 8) showing that specialization persists and often sharpens at deeper speculative depths suggests that the draft model is not a one-size-fits-all auxiliary module. It is a component whose selection or configuration should depend on the deployment workload, analogous to how retrieval corpora are selected per-domain in RAG systems or how expert subsets are routed in mixture-of-experts models. This reframing has practical implications for checkpoint management (maintaining multiple specialized draft checkpoints rather than one), inference serving infrastructure (supporting dynamic draft model selection), and training pipelines (domain-specific data curation for draft training).

**Research directions that become less attractive:**

- **Architecture-only draft improvements evaluated on generic benchmarks.** The paper's demonstration that training data alone can swing acceptance length by 1.0–1.5 tokens (roughly 20–35% relative change) on domain-specific workloads means that architecture comparisons evaluated only on generic data (typically ShareGPT) may be confounded. A new draft architecture that appears slightly better than EAGLE-2 on MT-Bench might owe its advantage to subtle interactions with the training data distribution rather than to architectural innovation. The paper implicitly argues that future architecture evaluations should control for training data composition, ideally reporting performance across multiple domain-specific training sets to separate architectural effects from data effects.

- **Default weight-space merging of fine-tuned drafters.** The decisive failure of checkpoint averaging (2.34–2.62 average acceptance length, consistently the weakest variant in Table 1) is an important negative result that should discourage practitioners from reaching for model averaging as a composition strategy for speculative decoding drafters, at least when the averaging is uniform and the specialists are trained on qualitatively different domains. While more sophisticated weight-space methods (Fisher merging, task arithmetic with sign consensus) might fare better, the paper's interpolation sweep (Figure 6) showing unstable acceptance length across the entire interpolation range suggests that the underlying parameter geometry is not amenable to simple linear combination. This negative result is practically valuable because weight-space averaging is an attractive approach—it requires no additional training, no inference-time overhead, and no routing logic—and the paper shows that this attractive approach simply does not work for this problem.

**Reconciling prior contradictions.** The paper resolves an implicit tension in how the speculative decoding literature has been developed versus evaluated. Prior work introduced architectural innovations (EAGLE-2's dynamic trees, HASS's harmonized objectives, EAGLE-3's training-time test) and reported improved acceptance lengths on generic benchmarks, creating an impression that architecture was the primary lever for quality. But these evaluations were conducted with drafters uniformly trained on broad conversational data like ShareGPT, which is well-aligned with the MT-Bench-style benchmarks on which they were tested. The paper shows that this evaluation setup masks a major source of performance variation: if those same architectures were deployed on a mathematical reasoning workload without corresponding training data alignment, their acceptance length would degrade substantially regardless of their architectural sophistication. This doesn't invalidate the architectural contributions—EAGLE-2 and HASS are genuinely effective backbones—but it contextualizes them: architecture determines the ceiling, but training data determines how close to that ceiling you get on a particular workload.

**Limitations of this reframing.** The paper's evidence comes from two domains (math and conversational), one verifier (Llama-3-8B-Instruct), and two backbones (HASS and EAGLE-2). The reframing is empirically grounded but not yet general: we do not know whether domain specialization effects hold for code-generation drafters, multilingual drafters, or retrieval-augmented drafters; for verifiers from different families or at different scales; or for domain pairs with more overlapping token distributions than math and chat. The reframing is also primarily empirical—the paper characterizes *that* training data matters but does not develop a theory of *why* certain domain pairs produce strong specialization while others (like the SVAMP math/chat boundary case) do not. Developing such a theory—perhaps grounded in distributional distance metrics, token vocabulary overlap, or draft model capacity relative to domain complexity—is a key open question that this paper enables but does not answer.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Characterizing the domain specialization landscape across diverse domain pairs and verifier scales.** The paper establishes that specialization exists for MathInstruct vs. ShareGPT with Llama-3-8B-Instruct. The natural next step is a systematic survey: train HASS and EAGLE-2 drafters on domain-specific corpora for code generation (e.g., The Stack, CodeAlpaca), scientific reasoning (e.g., SciQ, PubMedQA), multilingual generation (e.g., FLORES, Wikipedia-multi), and creative writing (e.g., WritingPrompts, Reddit stories), then evaluate on corresponding benchmarks with verifiers at multiple scales (8B, 70B) and from multiple families (Llama, Mistral, Qwen). The key measurement is the **specialization gap**—the difference in acceptance length between the domain-matched and domain-mismatched drafter on each benchmark, normalized by the absolute acceptance length. A large and consistent gap across domain pairs would establish training data as a universal lever for speculative decoding; a small or inconsistent gap would bound the reframing's generality and suggest that the MathInstruct/ShareGPT split is an outlier rather than a representative case. Within each domain pair, the study should characterize **boundary phenomena** like the SVAMP reversal—inputs where the "mismatched" drafter actually performs better—to understand what features of a benchmark (linguistic complexity, reasoning depth, stylistic conventions) determine whether domain-specific training helps or hurts. A particularly strong version of this study would measure the correlation between a domain pair's token vocabulary overlap (Jaccard similarity of top-K token sets, KL divergence of unigram distributions) and the observed specialization gap, testing whether simple distributional metrics predict when specialization matters.

**Learned cost-aware routing with per-input counterfactual evaluation.** The paper's confidence routing achieves 4.80 average acceptance length (HASS, temperature 0) but leaves a 0.31 gap to merged-tree verification (5.11). This gap represents the value of having both specialists' proposals available simultaneously—but how much of it could be closed by a better router that occasionally makes soft decisions or detects when both specialists are needed? A strong follow-up would train a lightweight routing classifier that takes as input the prompt text, the two draft trees' structural properties (tree size, depth distribution, per-node confidence quantiles, per-node entropy quantiles), and optionally a small number of verifier-side features (target model logits on the first few draft tokens, which can be obtained cheaply for both trees by running partial verification), and predicts which specialist—or combination strategy—will maximize acceptance length for that input. The router would be trained on a dataset where every input is evaluated with both specialists individually (to get counterfactual acceptance lengths) and with merged-tree verification, enabling it to learn not just "which specialist is better" but "how much better, and is a merged tree worth the cost." The evaluation would measure three quantities on a held-out test set: (1) **routing accuracy** (does the router select the specialist that actually achieves higher acceptance length on each input?); (2) **oracle gap** (what is the maximum possible routing performance if we always pick the better specialist per input?); (3) **cost-adjusted acceptance length** (acceptance length divided by total FLOPs including draft generation and verifier computation, with cost weights calibrated from wall-clock measurements on a specific hardware platform). A router that closes most of the oracle gap and outperforms both fixed routing and fixed merged-tree verification in cost-adjusted terms would be a practically deployable improvement over the paper's simple argmax baseline. A router that fails to close much of the oracle gap would indicate that the acceptance length benefit of merged trees comes from per-token combination (the verifier accepting different tokens from different specialists within a single tree) that no hard routing can replicate—an important negative result that would bound the routing approach.

**Depth-adaptive tree construction that shifts exploration-exploitation balance across speculative depth.** The paper's depth-wise analysis (Figure 8, Appendix Tables 5–8) reveals a pattern: mixed-data checkpoints often perform best at shallow depths (exploration benefit), while the task-matched specialist dominates at deeper depths (exploitation benefit). This suggests but does not test a design hypothesis: **the optimal draft tree construction strategy is depth-dependent**. A follow-up study would design and evaluate a depth-adaptive tree construction algorithm where early branching (depth 1–2) uses a broad proposal distribution—perhaps sampling from a mixed-data drafter or using higher temperature to increase diversity—while deeper branching (depth 3+) switches to a domain-specialized drafter with lower temperature to maintain alignment. The algorithm would need to decide at each node, based on the node's depth, the draft confidence so far, and perhaps entropy trends, whether to expand using the "exploration" or "exploitation" strategy. The evaluation would compare acceptance-by-depth curves between the depth-adaptive strategy and fixed strategies (single-domain, mixed-data, merged trees) to test whether the adaptive approach shifts the exploration-exploitation balance in the hypothesized direction—higher acceptance at shallow depths than a pure specialist, higher acceptance at deep depths than a mixed-data drafter. Crucially, this study would require per-node specialization analysis that goes beyond the paper's per-depth averages: it would measure, for each node in the draft tree, whether that specific node's acceptance was better predicted by the domain-matched specialist's confidence or by the mixed drafter's confidence, and use that signal to train the depth-adaptive switching policy. A finding that depth-adaptive construction outperforms both single-domain and mixed-data baselines would validate the paper's exploration-exploitation framing as an actionable design principle, not just a post-hoc characterization.

**Training-data-aware speculative decoding for verifier-agnostic draft deployment.** The paper's experiments all use a single verifier (Llama-3-8B-Instruct), but a practically important scenario is deploying the same draft model to assist multiple different verifiers—for example, a lightweight drafter serving as a drop-in accelerator for various open-weight models in a model-serving platform. The paper's core finding suggests that a drafter trained on a single domain will be well-aligned only with verifiers that share similar domain strengths. A follow-up study would train a single HASS or EAGLE-2 drafter on a deliberately multi-domain corpus spanning math, code, conversation, and science, then evaluate its acceptance length when paired with verifiers from different families (Llama-3-8B, Mistral-7B, Qwen-7B) on benchmarks that stress each domain. The key question is whether a drafter trained to cover many domains can serve as a reasonable generic accelerator (acceptable acceptance length across all verifiers and benchmarks), or whether the verifier-drafter domain mismatch compounds—for example, a MathInstruct-trained drafter might do well with a math-strong verifier on math benchmarks, but a multi-domain drafter might do poorly with all verifiers because it never develops the deep domain alignment that drives high acceptance length. The study would also test whether the domain composition of the drafter's training data can be tuned to match a specific verifier's known domain strengths (e.g., more code data for a code-strong verifier), introducing a **verifier-aware data curation** step into the draft training pipeline. This direction would establish whether the paper's findings about draft training distribution extend to the multi-verifier deployment scenario, or whether they are specific to the case where the verifier is fixed and known at training time.

**Verifier over-optimization in speculative decoding: when does better draft alignment hurt?** The paper focuses on improving acceptance length through better domain alignment, but it does not explore a potential dark side: what if a draft model that is too well-aligned with the verifier on a specific domain produces proposals that the verifier accepts too readily, leading to longer accepted sequences at the cost of output quality? Speculative decoding is lossless *in theory*—the acceptance rule (Equation 1) guarantees the verifier's output distribution is preserved regardless of draft quality. But in practice, the lossless guarantee only holds if the acceptance rule is implemented with perfect arithmetic and the verifier's per-token probabilities are correctly computed, which may not hold under quantization, batching, or sampling optimizations. A highly-aligned drafter that produces 10-token accepted sequences on average might interact with subtle implementation approximations in ways that a lower-alignment drafter producing 4-token sequences does not. A careful stress-test would measure the output distribution of speculative decoding with drafters at different acceptance lengths (e.g., MathInstruct on MATH-500: 5.35 acceptance length; ShareGPT on MATH-500: 3.98; Averaged: ~2.6) against the verifier's true autoregressive distribution on a set of prompts where the verifier's distribution can be computed exactly (short prompts, constrained vocabulary). The study would test whether measured divergence from the verifier's distribution correlates with acceptance length, controlling for quantization level (FP16, INT8, INT4), batch size, and sampling temperature. A null result (no measurable distribution shift regardless of acceptance length) would strengthen confidence in speculative decoding's lossless property under practical conditions. A positive result (longer accepted sequences show slightly larger distribution divergence) would suggest a previously unrecognized tradeoff between throughput and fidelity, and would motivate the development of acceptance-length-aware verification strategies that prioritize correctness over efficiency for high-alignment drafters.

**Entropy-guided early tree termination and adaptive draft budget allocation.** The paper's entropy analysis (Figure 7, Appendix Tables 3–4) establishes that rejected tokens consistently have higher draft entropy than accepted tokens, but this diagnostic signal is never used proactively. A natural extension is an **early termination policy**: during tree construction, if a node's draft entropy exceeds some threshold, stop expanding that node's subtree—the token is unlikely to be accepted, and any deeper tokens depending on it would be wasted computation. The threshold could be set based on the per-benchmark, per-depth entropy distributions of accepted and rejected tokens (which the appendix tables provide), allowing a calibrated tradeoff between premature termination (falsely pruning a token that would have been accepted) and wasted expansion (expanding a token that will be rejected). The experiment would measure: (1) the acceptance length and total FLOPs of entropy-guided tree construction compared to full-tree construction at a fixed node budget; (2) the accuracy of the termination decisions (do high-entropy nodes indeed get rejected at the predicted rate?); (3) whether the optimal entropy threshold varies with domain, draft variant, and speculative depth. A strong result would show that entropy-guided termination reduces draft tree FLOPs by, say, 15–20% with negligible acceptance length loss (less than 0.1 tokens), providing a "free" efficiency gain on top of the domain-alignment improvements the paper already demonstrates. A more ambitious version would make the termination threshold itself adaptive—tightening or loosening based on the verifier's recent acceptance patterns on similar inputs—to create a closed-loop system that continuously tunes the exploration-exploitation balance.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Multi-domain LLM serving platforms with specialized draft acceleration.** Consider a model-serving platform (similar to Together AI, Fireworks, or Anyscale) that offers Llama-3-8B-Instruct inference to customers with diverse workloads—some send conversational chat prompts, others send mathematical reasoning problems, others send code generation requests. The paper's findings directly motivate a deployment architecture where the platform maintains **multiple specialized draft checkpoints** (trained on math, chat, code, and mixed data) rather than a single generic drafter. For each incoming request, a lightweight confidence router (the paper's argmax mean-confidence heuristic, or a trained variant) selects the specialist most likely to achieve high acceptance length. The benefit is quantifiable from the paper's numbers: on MATH-500, using the MathInstruct specialist (5.35 acceptance length) instead of a generic ShareGPT-trained drafter (3.98) yields a 34% reduction in verifier calls—each verifier call with the math specialist produces 1.34 more tokens on average. For a customer sending 1 million math reasoning requests daily, this translates to roughly 250,000 fewer Llama-3-8B forward passes, directly reducing GPU-hours and latency. The paper's finding that confidence routing achieves 4.80 average acceptance length (HASS, temperature 0) and correctly selects the domain-appropriate specialist ~90%+ of the time (Table 2) provides evidence that such a system can operate automatically without per-customer configuration—the platform does not need to know what domain each customer is in; it just routes based on draft confidence signals. The key remaining engineering challenge (not addressed in the paper) is the overhead of storing and switching between multiple draft checkpoints in GPU memory, and the latency cost of generating two draft trees for routing decisions. A practical deployment would likely pre-load the most common specialist(s) and fall back to less-common ones only when confidence signals strongly indicate a domain mismatch, reducing average memory pressure.

**Cost-efficient training data generation for self-improvement and distillation pipelines.** Many LLM training pipelines use speculative decoding during data generation—for example, when using a large model (70B) to generate training data for a smaller model via distillation, or when running self-improvement loops where the model generates and then verifies its own outputs. In these settings, the verifier is fixed (the large model being distilled from, or the target model in the self-improvement loop), and the generation workload often has a known domain focus (e.g., generating math solutions for a math-specific fine-tuning dataset). The paper's findings suggest a concrete optimization: **train the draft model on data matching the generation workload's domain** rather than on a generic conversational corpus. The acceptance length improvement from domain-matched training (e.g., 5.35 vs. 3.98 on MATH-500 for HASS, a 34% relative increase) directly reduces the number of verifier forward passes needed to generate each output token, which is the dominant computational cost in these pipelines. For a distillation run that needs to generate 100 million tokens of math training data using Llama-3-8B-Instruct as the verifier, switching from a generic drafter (3.98 acceptance length) to a MathInstruct-specialized drafter (5.35) would reduce verifier calls from ~25.1 million to ~18.7 million—saving approximately 6.4 million 8B-parameter forward passes, which at typical cloud GPU pricing translates to hundreds or thousands of dollars depending on sequence lengths. The paper's mixed-data results further suggest that if the generation workload spans multiple domains, a mixed-data drafter (Mixed 70k+70k: 5.18 average HASS at temperature 0) or a confidence-routed specialist setup (4.80) can recover most of the specialization benefit without requiring per-domain manual configuration.

**On-device speculative decoding with domain-adaptive draft selection.** The paper's draft model is a lightweight 0.8B-parameter decoder with one transformer layer, small enough to run comfortably on edge devices (laptops, phones with ML accelerators, embedded systems). A common deployment pattern is using a small local draft model to accelerate a larger cloud-hosted verifier—reducing the number of network round-trips by accepting multiple tokens locally before sending the prefix to the cloud for verification. In this setting, the paper's findings suggest that the local draft model should be **selected or fine-tuned based on the user's typical workload**, not trained generically. For an educational math app where users primarily ask math questions, deploying a MathInstruct-trained local drafter yields 5.02–5.35 acceptance length (HASS/EAGLE-2 at temperature 0 on GSM8K/MATH-500), meaning each cloud verification call accepts ~5 tokens on average—a 5× reduction in network round-trips compared to standard autoregressive decoding. For a general-purpose chat assistant where user queries span multiple domains, the paper's results show that a Mixed 35k+35k drafter (4.47 HASS average at temperature 0) provides a stable baseline, while confidence routing between math and chat specialists (4.80 HASS average) could be implemented locally with minimal overhead—the local device generates two small draft trees, compares mean confidences, and ships the higher-confidence tree's tokens to the cloud verifier. The key practical insight from the paper is that the draft model is not a fixed binary shipped with the app; it is a configurable component whose training data should be part of the deployment's domain-specific optimization, and whose selection can be automated at inference time via the confidence-based routing the paper validates.

### When to Prefer This Method

**Prefer domain-specialized draft training when:**
- The deployment workload has a known, stable domain focus (e.g., a math tutoring application, a code completion service, a legal document analyzer). In these cases, training the draft model on domain-matched data (analogous to the paper's MathInstruct checkpoints) yields the largest acceptance length gains: 5.35 vs. 3.98 on MATH-500 under HASS at temperature 0, a ~34% relative improvement over generic conversational training.
- The verifier is fixed and known at draft training time, so the draft model can be trained on data that approximates the verifier's behavior on the target domain. The paper demonstrates this with Llama-3-8B-Instruct; the same principle should apply to any verifier where domain-matched training data can be curated.
- The deployment prioritizes throughput over marginal latency, since a single well-trained specialist avoids the overhead of generating and routing between multiple draft trees at inference time.

**Prefer mixed-data draft training when:**
- The deployment workload spans multiple domains (e.g., a general-purpose chatbot that handles math, coding, and conversation), and maintaining multiple specialized draft checkpoints is infeasible due to memory constraints, deployment complexity, or unpredictable workload mix.
- The decoding temperature is 0 (greedy), where the paper shows Mixed 70k+70k achieves the highest average acceptance length among trained checkpoints (5.18 HASS, 4.48 EAGLE-2). At temperature 1, prefer Mixed 35k+35k, which the paper shows is more stable (4.29 vs. 3.69 HASS average for 35k+35k vs. 70k+70k)—the larger mixture's temperature-dependent degradation is a concrete cautionary finding.
- The total training data budget is fixed (e.g., 70k examples), in which case a balanced 1:1 mixture across domains recovers most of the specialization benefit without requiring additional data volume: Mixed 35k+35k HASS achieves 4.47 average at temperature 0 vs. 4.10–4.12 for the single-domain specialists.

**Prefer inference-time composition (confidence routing or merged-tree verification) when:**
- Multiple specialized draft checkpoints are already available (e.g., from the open-weight ecosystem, from separate team efforts, or from previous domain-specific deployments). The paper shows that keeping them separate and composing at inference time (4.80–5.11 HASS average at temperature 0) dramatically outperforms merging them in weight space (2.59), so there is no benefit to weight-space unification.
- The workload mix is unknown, shifting, or includes ambiguous inputs where domain boundaries blur (like the paper's SVAMP case, where ShareGPT unexpectedly outperforms MathInstruct on a math benchmark). Confidence routing handles this by selecting per-input based on draft confidence, without requiring the system designer to pre-specify domain boundaries.
- The deployment can tolerate the overhead of generating two draft trees per input (for routing) or processing a larger merged tree (for merged verification). The paper's overhead estimates (0.32–0.78× speedup reduction relative to the strongest single checkpoint, depending on method and backbone) provide a rough calibration, but actual overhead depends on hardware, batching, and the relative cost of draft generation vs. verifier computation. For mixed-tree verification specifically, prefer when acceptance length improvement (5.11 vs. 4.80 for merged vs. routed under HASS at temperature 0) outweighs the additional verifier cost, which depends on the verifier's size, the draft tree size, and the hardware's parallel processing capacity—a systems-level tradeoff the paper explicitly does not resolve but provides the algorithmic numbers to inform.
