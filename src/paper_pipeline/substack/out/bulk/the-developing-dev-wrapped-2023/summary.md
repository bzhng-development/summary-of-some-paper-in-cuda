Here are the takeaways from this Ryan Peterman “Best of 2023” retrospective (the host reflects on a year of writing The Developing Dev newsletter and curates the top insights across eight growth categories):

**The origin and philosophy**

Ryan started the newsletter in 2023 with two explicit goals: improve his own writing and help as many software engineers as possible advance their careers. He set an initial target of 100 subscribers by year end, considering it ambitious since he had no social media following and thought “asking for emails was high friction.” By the time this retrospective was published, the subscriber count had reached over 42,000 — far beyond what he thought possible. Writing weekly forced clear improvement; looking back at older articles (he links to two early examples), he notes that new posts are “clearer and easier to digest.” He didn’t miss a single week across nine months.

**Inside the stats and the global reach**

The numbers told a story: **43 posts, ~947,000 total views** (averaging roughly 22,000 views per post). The single most shared and engaged piece hit 106 direct shares with an 18% engagement rate on its platform. What surprised him most wasn’t the volume but the geography — readers from all 50 U.S. states and 174 different countries. Writing online created a reach he described as personally “shocked to see,” a signal that career-growth content for software engineers transcends borders and company tiers.

**The eight pathways — curating by need rather than by chronology**

Instead of a chronological recap, Ryan organized an entire year’s posts into eight intentional “I want to…” pathways, each mapping to a specific career stage or need. This architecture itself embodies a lesson: **structure content around the reader’s goal, not the author’s timeline**. The pathways are (1) soft skills, (2) growing into a mid-level engineer, (3) growing into a senior engineer, (4) growing into a staff engineer, (5) growing to staff+ levels, (6) job hunting and interviewing, (7) learning from other engineers’ experiences, and (8) honing core engineering skills. Each pathway contains between two and eleven articles, with a handful flagged by a 🎖️ as “top 10” across the entire collection. The curation doubles as a self-assessment tool — readers can jump directly to the section matching their current growth edge.

**Soft skills as the densest category**

The soft-skills pathway is the largest, signaling Ryan’s emphasis on non-coding growth. Articles range from writing and communication (“Why Engineers Need To Write,” “Efficient Communication,” “How To Write”) to self-review mechanics (“How To Write an Effective Self-Review”) and time management (“Protect Your Time”). The inclusion of “Be Visible” alongside “How Promotions and Ratings Work” frames soft skills not as optional polish but as the operating system of career progression. Across all paths, roughly half the 🎖️ top-10 pieces fall into soft skills, underscoring a thesis that technical competence alone is insufficient. The article “6 Software Engineering Templates I Wish I Had Sooner” sits here too — templates as a soft-skill accelerator.

**The “speedrunning” framework through multiple levels**

A recurring resource type is the **speedrunning guide** — concise, tactical breakdowns mapping the delta between adjacent levels (Junior → Mid-level, Mid-level → Senior, Senior → Staff). Each guide is paired with a “FAANG Career Ladder” diagnostic that defines the differences in scope, autonomy, and expectations. Together they form a reusable meta-pattern: first understand what *actually* changes at the next level, then apply practical tactics to close that gap. This pairing reflects a core belief that promotion through deliberate, informed action beats “promotion by accident.”

**Growth-specific depth at each tier**

Beyond the speedrunning pairs, each growth pathway contains level-specific primers. For mid-level, it includes the generalist-vs-specialist decision and how to start reviewing code; for senior, it adds “Finding Next-Level Scope” and the leadership insight “How Some Engineers Always Lead”; for staff, “Setting Up Cross-Team Workstreams” and “Finding Staff-Level Scope”; for staff+, the comparative “Staff+ Career Growth: Product or Infra?” and “Optimizing for Opportunity.” The escalating progression — from individual capability to organizational leverage — mirrors the real expectations of the ladder, making the newsletter not just a collection but a curriculum.

**Learning from others: the experience-share pathway**

A distinct pathway collects interviews and case studies of engineers who moved fast or navigated inflection points. “Promotion via Job Hopping” provides an alternative to internal climbing; “From Microsoft Intern to Meta Staff Engineer” profiles Raviraj Achar’s trajectory; “New Grad to Senior Engineer in 2 years” dissects Jordan Cutler’s acceleration; and “Why I Stayed at Meta When All My Friends Left” explores the counter-cyclical decision to stay put. By placing these narratives alongside tactical guides, Ryan suggests that **calibrating against peers’ paths is a form of scope-finding itself** — it reveals what’s possible and what levers were pulled.

**Engineering skills as a standalone category**

The final pathway covers technical depth without the career-ladder framing: resilience through retrospectives (“Resilient Systems Through Retrospection”), the specific reliability design of Threads (“Threads’ Invisible Armor”), a “Tools of the Trade” reference, and a roundup of top tech company engineering blogs. The inclusion of “Move Fast Without Breaking Anything” signals that engineering velocity and safety are not opposing forces but jointly designable properties. This section serves engineers who are already confident in their career trajectory and primarily want to sharpen their craft. The job-hunting pathway, though smaller, is pointed: “Job Hunting in the Layoff Era” and “Cracking the Coding Interview” speak directly to a 2023 macro moment.

**The meta-lesson in a year-end retrospective**

Ryan’s closing poll — asking readers to vote on future topics — is itself a lesson embedded in the format. After a year of writing, the most valued next step is *listening*. The entire retrospective avoids self-congratulation and instead reroutes the spotlight to the reader’s needs. He thanks the audience, acknowledges the collective nature of growth, and uses the moment to tighten the feedback loop for year two. The final invitation — to share the post with a friend and subscribe — reflects a distribution philosophy rooted in organic word-of-mouth rather than algorithmic reach, consistent with the newsletter’s origin in zero-follower beginnings.

---

His **speedrunning guide** pattern and the **structure-content-around-the-reader’s-goal** architecture both emerge from the same underlying instinct: treat career growth as a system that can be reverse-engineered rather than a mysterious luck-driven process. The eight “I want to…” pathways operationalize that instinct into a self-assessment tool, while the meta-lesson of the retrospective — closing with a reader poll — shows that the system itself stays open to iteration. Across soft skills, leveling guides, and peer case studies, the collection quietly argues that the most leveraged thing an engineer can do is **study the map before walking the terrain**.
