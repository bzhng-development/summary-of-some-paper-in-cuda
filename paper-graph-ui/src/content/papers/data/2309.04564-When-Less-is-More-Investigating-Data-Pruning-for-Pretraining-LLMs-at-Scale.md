# When Less is More: Investigating Data Pruning for Pretraining LLMs at Scale

**ArXiv:** [2309.04564](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.04564)

## 🎯 Pitch

Training on just 30% of web-scraped pretraining data can outperform using the entire dataset—if you keep the “medium-quality” examples, not the best or worst. Surprisingly, simple perplexity ranking beats more expensive quality metrics for this data pruning, overturning beliefs that harder data is always better.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This work systematically investigates data pruning for large language model pretraining—determining whether a subset of training data can match or exceed the performance of the full dataset—by comparing three automatic quality estimators on CommonCrawl-pretrained models up to 1.5B parameters. The paper evaluates **perplexity** (how probable the reference model finds each sequence), **Error L2-Norm (EL2N)** (the L2 norm of the model’s early prediction errors, drawn from computer vision pruning literature), and **memorization factor** (the fraction of greedily generated tokens matching the original text verbatim) as scoring metrics for ranking and removing pretraining examples. Surprisingly, the simple perplexity-based ranking outperforms the more computationally expensive EL2N and memorization approaches, with a model trained on the middle 50% of the perplexity distribution achieving a 0.97% improvement in test perplexity over the no-pruning baseline—and retaining only 30% of the data still yielding a 0.80% improvement. The approach scales to 1.5B parameter models—delivering a 1.5% improvement over the no-pruning baseline—while establishing that removing the “easiest” examples (lowest perplexity, lowest EL2N, or most memorized) consistently degrades performance, meaning that the middle of the scoring distribution, not the extremes, constitutes the most valuable data for pretraining.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: We Don't Know Which Pretraining Examples Actually Matter

The fundamental question this paper tackles is: **of the billions of tokens used to pretrain a large language model, which ones actually contribute to the model's performance—and can we throw the rest away without hurting (or even while improving) the final model?**

This matters because the dominant approach in LLM development over the past several years has been to train on ever-larger datasets scraped from the internet—CommonCrawl, C4, RefinedWeb, The Pile—under the implicit assumption that more data is strictly better. The paper opens by acknowledging this reigning belief (Section 1):

> "A reigning belief in machine learning is that more data leads to better performance. Recent years of progress in scaling large language models (LLMs) have shown strong evidence to support this with remarkable gains in language understanding and generation capabilities"

But web-scraped data is extraordinarily noisy. The paper cites multiple studies documenting that these datasets contain "low quality, machine-generated spam, pornographic content" (Kreutzer et al., 2022), repetitive text, and artifacts of automated generation that bear little resemblance to the kind of coherent language that builds useful linguistic capabilities. If a substantial fraction of the training data is effectively junk, then training on it wastes compute, time, and energy—and may even actively harm the model by teaching it to model pathological patterns.

This creates a practical tension: we know that dataset scale has been one of the primary drivers of LLM progress (Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022), but we also know that large-scale web data is riddled with low-quality content. The field has lacked a principled way to distinguish the useful from the useless—to identify *which specific sequences* in a pretraining corpus are actually contributing to the model's language understanding, and which are just noise.

### Why the Gap Matters: The Limits of Rule-Based Filtering

The paper identifies a specific and important gap in current practice. The techniques that practitioners actually use to clean pretraining data are almost entirely **hand-crafted heuristics encoded as rule-based filters** (Section 1, Section 5.1). These include:

- Removing documents with repetitive text or excessive special characters (Zhang et al., 2022; Raffel et al., 2020)
- Filtering out non-English text (Wenzek et al., 2020)
- Blocklisting certain websites or domains known to host low-quality content (Dodge et al., 2021; Rae et al., 2022)
- Eliminating documents below certain length thresholds

The paper explicitly states the limitation of these approaches:

> "While these hand-curated filters can eliminate certain noisy examples, they are not a substitute for a measure of 'quality' for individual training examples, for which there are currently no established best practices" (Section 1)

This is a critical distinction. Rule-based filters catch *blatant* forms of noise—documents that are 90% punctuation, text that is clearly machine-translated spam, content from known content farms. But they are inherently coarse. They operate on surface-level patterns and categorical rules, not on any real assessment of whether a given passage of text is *useful for learning*. A grammatically correct but content-free piece of marketing boilerplate passes most rule-based filters; a dense, informative technical passage with a few unusual characters might get filtered out. Rule-based heuristics "are prone to undesired outcomes due to their simplicity" (Section 5.1), and the paper cites Dodge et al. (2021) demonstrating that blocklist-based filtering can disproportionately remove text from and about minority communities.

The core gap, then, is this: **there is no established, scalable, model-based notion of data quality for pretraining examples.** The field knows how to remove obviously broken text, but it doesn't know how to rank the remaining examples by their actual contribution to model learning. And critically, the field doesn't know whether the relationship between "quality" (however defined) and utility for training is monotonic—should we keep the highest-quality examples? The easiest? The hardest? Something in the middle?

### Prior Approaches and Their Shortcomings

The paper situates itself relative to several strands of prior work, each of which it identifies as incomplete or insufficient for the specific challenge of large-scale pretraining data pruning:

**1. Data pruning has largely been studied in supervised computer vision, not unsupervised language pretraining.**

The majority of existing work on data pruning—isolating a subset of training data that preserves or improves performance—comes from computer vision in supervised settings (Qin et al., 2023; Sorscher et al., 2023; Raju et al., 2021; Paul et al., 2023; He et al., 2023). These methods typically rely on having access to ground-truth labels (e.g., the margin of the correct class prediction, the loss on the labeled target). In language model pretraining, there are no labels—the objective is next-token prediction on arbitrary text. The signals that work in supervised vision (which examples does the model classify incorrectly?) don't translate directly.

The paper specifically calls out this gap:

> "To date, the majority of work on data pruning has centered on supervised computer vision settings... with far fewer works focusing on language." (Section 1)

Some work that *has* applied pruning ideas to NLP has focused on the **fine-tuning stage**, where dataset sizes are orders of magnitude smaller and more computationally expensive scoring methods become feasible. For example, Attendu & Corbeil (2023) perform dynamic pruning during fine-tuning using EL2N scores, and Cao et al. (2023) select high-quality instruction-tuning data. But fine-tuning datasets typically contain tens of thousands of examples, not billions of tokens. Methods that are tractable at that scale (e.g., computing gradient-based importance scores, doing dynamic curriculum learning where the dataset changes during training) become computationally prohibitive at pretraining scale, where the dataset is too large to even store all the scoring metadata, let alone recompute it during training.

**2. Metric-based filtering for pretraining has used perplexity, but only in a narrow way.**

The paper acknowledges that perplexity has been used before to filter pretraining data (Muennighoff et al., 2023; Wenzek et al., 2020; Laurençon et al., 2023). For example, CCNet (Wenzek et al., 2020) uses perplexity to select high-quality monolingual data from CommonCrawl, and Laurençon et al. (2023) filter out high-perplexity samples because they are considered "unnatural language" relative to a Wikipedia-trained reference model. But the paper argues that these prior uses have been limited in scope:

> "Laurençon et al. (2023) and Muennighoff et al. (2023) filter out high-perplexity samples from their corpus as those are framed as unnatural language and harmful for performance according to their reference domain, which is Wikipedia." (Section 5.2)

The implicit assumption in prior work is that low perplexity = good, high perplexity = bad—a monotonic relationship where the goal is simply to remove the worst-scoring examples. But the paper's central finding challenges this directly: the "easiest" examples (lowest perplexity) are actually the *least* valuable for training. Prior work, by filtering *out* high-perplexity examples and keeping low-perplexity ones, may have been doing exactly the wrong thing for pretraining performance. The paper positions itself as a more comprehensive exploration:

> "In contrast, we benchmark pruning to low perplexity values and high and medium-valued subsets of a dataset's distribution to understand which is the most valuable section for pretraining at scale." (Section 5.2)

**3. More sophisticated pruning metrics (EL2N, memorization) have never been rigorously benchmarked at pretraining scale for language.**

The paper introduces two metrics that are more computationally expensive and theoretically grounded than perplexity, specifically to test whether they provide better pruning signals:

- **EL2N** was originally proposed by Paul et al. (2023) for computer vision. It measures the L2 norm of the error vector between the model's prediction and the ground-truth one-hot label, averaged over all tokens in a sequence. The intuition (drawn from the original vision work) is that examples with low EL2N scores are those the model learns quickly in early training—they are "easy" in the sense of being readily fit. High-EL2N examples are those where the model continues to incur significant error. Paul et al. (2023) found that in supervised image classification, retaining the hardest examples (highest EL2N) was most effective when data is abundant, while retaining the easiest (lowest EL2N) worked better when data is scarce.

- **Memorization factor** is adapted from Biderman et al. (2023a), who studied it as a phenomenon (what fraction of text do models reproduce verbatim?) rather than as a pruning metric. The paper's hypothesis is that memorization scores might identify examples that require different amounts of learning—highly memorized examples might be "easy" in the sense that the model already reproduces them perfectly, while low-memorization examples might represent novel patterns that require more learning.

Neither of these metrics had been systematically evaluated as pruning signals for language model pretraining at scale. The paper explicitly positions its contribution as providing this evaluation:

> "We extensively benchmark data pruning based on perplexity, EL2N, and memorization in the LLM pretraining setting." (Section 1, contribution 1)

**4. There is no unified framework for understanding *which subset* of a scoring distribution to keep.**

Perhaps the most important gap the paper addresses is conceptual rather than methodological. Prior work that used perplexity for filtering implicitly assumed a monotonic relationship: lower perplexity → higher quality → better for training. But the paper identifies that this assumption has never been rigorously tested. There are three logically distinct choices when pruning based on a scoring metric: keep the examples that score lowest (the "easiest" according to that metric), keep those that score highest (the "hardest"), or keep those in the middle. Each represents a different hypothesis about what kind of data is most valuable for learning:

- **Keeping the easiest examples** corresponds to the hypothesis that models learn best from data that is already well within their capabilities—clean, predictable, well-structured text. This is the implicit assumption in prior perplexity-based filtering.

- **Keeping the hardest examples** corresponds to the hypothesis that models learn most from data that challenges their current capabilities—text that is unpredictable, novel, or contains rare patterns. This aligns with curriculum learning intuitions and with the finding from Sorscher et al. (2023) in vision that training on hard examples works best with abundant data.

- **Keeping the middle examples** corresponds to a more nuanced hypothesis: that the easiest examples are too trivial to teach anything new (the model already predicts them well), while the hardest examples may be *too* hard—pathological noise, genuinely incoherent text, or patterns that can't actually be learned—and that the sweet spot lies in between.

The paper explicitly investigates all three choices for each pruning metric, a design decision that prior work had not made.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper frames its contribution as filling three specific gaps simultaneously:

**First**, it provides the first large-scale, systematic comparison of multiple automatic data pruning metrics for language model pretraining, going beyond the supervised vision setting where most prior work lives. The key comparison is between a simple metric (perplexity) and more complex, computationally expensive ones (EL2N, memorization factor), testing the explicit hypothesis that simpler methods might be just as good or better.

**Second**, it introduces the "which subset to keep" taxonomy (bottom/middle/top of the scoring distribution) as a first-class experimental variable, and demonstrates that the answer is counterintuitive: the middle subset consistently outperforms the extremes, regardless of which metric is used. This finding directly challenges the implicit assumption in prior perplexity-based filtering work.

**Third**, it shows that these findings scale—both in terms of model size (from 124M to 1.5B parameters) and in terms of pruning aggressiveness (down to 30% and even 10% of the original dataset)—and that the benefits carry over to downstream task performance on GLUE benchmarks, not just language modeling perplexity.

The paper deliberately frames itself as foundational rather than as proposing a new best method. It explores what kinds of signals predict data quality, what the dynamics of pruning are, and where the limits lie, explicitly aiming to:

> "set the foundation for unexplored strategies in automatically curating high quality corpora" (Abstract)

The practical motivation is clear: if we can identify which 30–50% of a pretraining dataset actually matters, we can train models with dramatically less compute, or equivalently, train better models with the same compute by focusing it on higher-quality data. The theoretical motivation is equally important: understanding *why* the middle of the scoring distribution is most valuable tells us something fundamental about the relationship between language model capabilities, text predictability, and learning efficiency.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

This paper builds an evaluation framework for comparing **data pruning algorithms**—automatic methods that rank every training sequence in a pretraining corpus by a quality score, then remove some fraction of those sequences based on that ranking, with the goal that a language model trained on the remaining subset performs as well as or better than a model trained on the full dataset. The core structure is deceptively simple: (1) pick a reference model and a scoring metric, (2) use that metric to assign every sequence in the training data a numerical score, (3) decide which portion of the score distribution to keep (bottom, middle, or top), (4) throw away everything else, and (5) train a new model from scratch on what remains. The paper tests three different scoring metrics—perplexity, EL2N, and memorization factor—across multiple reference model sizes, reference model training datasets, pruning ratios, and subset-selection strategies, ultimately showing that a 52B-parameter model's perplexity scores, when used to keep the *middle* of the distribution, produce the best pruned datasets.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The pruning and evaluation pipeline has five major stages:

1. **Reference Model Training or Selection:** A language model is either trained from scratch or selected from existing pretrained models. This reference model is *not* the final model being evaluated—it exists solely to assign scores to training examples. The paper varies the reference model's size (124M, 6B, 13B, 52B parameters), training data (CommonCrawl vs. Wikipedia), and training duration (14%, 55%, or 100% of total steps) to understand how these factors affect pruning quality.

2. **Scoring:** Every sequence in the pretraining corpus is fed to the reference model to compute one of three metrics: **perplexity** (the exponentiated average negative log-likelihood—how "surprised" the model is by the text), **EL2N** (the average L2 distance between the model's predicted token distribution and the one-hot ground truth—how far off the model's predictions are), or **memorization factor** (the fraction of greedily-generated continuation tokens that exactly match the original text—how much the model reproduces verbatim). Each metric produces a single scalar score per sequence.

3. **Subset Selection:** Given the distribution of scores across all sequences, the system selects which portion to keep. The paper tests three selection criteria: **bottom** (keep the sequences with the lowest scores—e.g., lowest perplexity, easiest for the model), **middle** (keep sequences around the median of the score distribution), and **top** (keep sequences with the highest scores). The amount of data retained is also varied: 10%, 30%, 50%, or 70% of the original dataset. The unselected sequences are discarded.

4. **Pruned Model Training:** A new model is trained *from scratch* (random initialization) on the pruned dataset. This model shares the same architecture as the reference model but starts with a fresh random seed—it never sees the discarded sequences. This is what the paper calls **static pruning**: the dataset is pruned once before training begins, and remains fixed throughout training. This contrasts with adaptive or dynamic pruning, where the dataset might change during training.

5. **Evaluation:** The pruned model is evaluated on (a) test set perplexity on a held-out CommonCrawl split, and (b) downstream task performance after fine-tuning on six GLUE classification benchmarks (SST2, MRPC, QQP, QNLI, RTE, WNLI). The key comparison is against a **no-pruning baseline**: a model of identical architecture trained on the complete, unpruned dataset for the same number of steps.

The information flow is strictly sequential and one-directional: a reference model produces scores → a selection criterion picks which sequences survive → a new model trains only on survivors → that model is evaluated. There is no feedback loop between the pruned model's performance and the scoring process.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the formal pruning framework: the notation that defines what a pruning algorithm is, what a pruning score is, what subset selection criteria mean, and what "success" looks like. This includes Equations 1–3 from Section 2, which are the formal backbone of the entire experimental design.
- **Second**, the dataset preprocessing pipeline: how raw CommonCrawl text becomes fixed-length training sequences, including tokenization, concatenation, and special-token handling. This matters because the pruning scores are computed *per sequence*, and the sequence definition determines what is being scored.
- **Third**, the perplexity metric in detail: what it computes, why it serves as a quality estimator, and what design choices surround it (reference model selection, the operational meaning of "low perplexity" vs. "high perplexity" in the context of web text).
- **Fourth**, the EL2N metric: its origins in computer vision, how it is adapted for autoregressive language modeling, the averaging across multiple reference models, and the investigation of early checkpoints.
- **Fifth**, the memorization factor metric: how it is computed, the specific prompt/generation setup (M=N=32), and the hypothesis about why memorization scores might correlate with learning utility.
- **Sixth**, the subset selection criteria and pruning ratio mechanics: how "keeping the bottom 30%" is operationally defined for each metric, the percentile calculations, and the specific variants tested.
- **Seventh**, the reference model ablation design: how reference model size (124M → 52B), training data (CC vs. Wikipedia), and training steps (14%, 55%, 100%) are systematically varied to isolate their effects on pruning quality.
- **Eighth**, the training and evaluation setup: model architecture, optimization hyperparameters, the scale of experiments (124M and 1.5B parameter models, 33B and 7.6B seen tokens respectively), and the evaluation protocol for both perplexity and downstream tasks.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily an **empirical evaluation paper** whose core idea is that data pruning for LLM pretraining is viable, that simple perplexity-based ranking outperforms more complex methods, and that the most valuable data resides in the middle of the scoring distribution, not the extremes.

---

#### The Formal Pruning Framework (Equations 1–3 and Underlying Definitions)

The paper establishes a formal notation in Section 2 to make precise what a "pruning algorithm" is and what it means for pruning to succeed. This formalism is essential because it defines the search space the experiments explore.

**Step 1: Decompose the dataset into sequences.**

The raw training corpus D is first tokenized. All documents have a special `<eod>` ("end of document") token appended to their end. The tokenized documents are then concatenated together and split into `n` sequences `z_i`, each of fixed length `t`, where `t` is equal to the model's context window size:

$$D = \{z_1, z_2, \ldots, z_n\}$$

where `D` is the full training dataset after tokenization and concatenation, `z_i` is the i-th sequence, `n` is the total number of sequences, and `t` (implied) is the fixed length of each `z_i`, which equals the model's context length (2048 tokens in all experiments).

**What this operation does:** It transforms a collection of variable-length documents into a set of equally-sized, fixed-length training instances that exactly match the model's input size. The `<eod>` tokens serve as document boundaries embedded within sequences rather than as sequence separators—a sequence might contain parts of multiple documents—which means the pruning scores are computed over potentially multi-document spans.

**Why this decomposition:** This matches standard autoregressive language model pretraining, where the model processes fixed-length contexts regardless of document boundaries. The pruning scores are computed per-sequence because this is the unit the model actually sees during training. If scores were computed per-document and sequences contained mixtures of different-quality documents, the pruning signal would be diluted. By scoring the same units the model trains on, the ranking directly corresponds to what will be fed to the pruned model.

**Step 2: Define a pruning algorithm.**

A pruning algorithm `ξ` assigns a scalar score to each sequence, and then a selection criterion (which the paper calls `Criteria`) decides which sequences are retained:

$$P_ξ = \{z_i \in D \mid \text{Criteria}(\text{Score}_ξ(z_i))\}$$

where `P_ξ` is the subset of sequences **removed** (pruned) from the dataset, `Score_ξ(z_i)` is the scalar assigned by pruning algorithm `ξ` to sequence `z_i`, and `Criteria` is a predicate that determines which scored sequences to remove.

The remaining dataset after pruning is then:

$$\hat{D}_ξ = D \setminus P_ξ$$

where `\hat{D}_ξ` is the pruned dataset—the complement of `P_ξ` in `D`, i.e., all sequences that were NOT removed.

**What these two equations define:** A complete pruning procedure: score everything → apply a filter to select what to remove → keep everything else. The notation separates the scoring method (`ξ`, which could be perplexity, EL2N, or memorization) from the selection criterion (`Criteria`, which could be "keep the bottom 30%" or "keep the middle 50%"), allowing these to be studied independently.

**Why this separation matters:** It makes explicit that there are two distinct design choices: *what* metric to use for scoring, and *which* portion of the resulting distribution to keep. A flaw in the paper's experimental setup would arise if these were conflated—e.g., if "perplexity pruning" implicitly meant "remove high-perplexity examples." By separating them, the paper can test all nine combinations (three metrics × three subset criteria) and discover, for example, that keeping the bottom of the perplexity distribution performs poorly even though prior work implicitly assumed that would work best.

**Step 3: Define success.**

The goal of pruning is:

$$\mathcal{P}_τ(M_{\hat{D}_ξ}) \geq \mathcal{P}_τ(M_D)$$

where `M_{\hat{D}_ξ}` is a model trained on the pruned dataset `\hat{D}_ξ`, `M_D` is a model trained on the full, unpruned dataset `D`, and `\mathcal{P}_τ` is the performance metric on some evaluation task `τ` (in this paper, test-set perplexity or downstream task accuracy).

**What this inequality states:** Pruning succeeds if—and only if—the model trained on the pruned subset performs at least as well as the model trained on the full dataset. This is a deliberately strict criterion: pruning must not degrade performance. Improved performance (the strict inequality `>`) is the ideal outcome, but parity (`=`) is acceptable.

**Why this form:** This definition avoids the common trap of evaluating pruning by how much data was removed rather than by whether model quality is preserved. A pruning method that removes 90% of data but produces a much worse model is not a successful pruning method—it's just aggressive filtering. The constraint that the reference baseline is trained on the *same* data from which the subset was drawn is crucial: it controls for dataset quality, preprocessing, and domain, isolating the pruning method's effect.

**Operationalizing the subset selection criteria.**

The paper does not give a single equation for `Criteria`, but the text in Section 3.3 provides explicit operational definitions for each variant. Given a pruning metric, the full dataset `D` is scored, producing a distribution of `n` scores. Let `p` be the pruning ratio (e.g., 0.3 to retain 30% of data). Then:

- **Keep bottom `p`:** Sort all sequences by score, ascending. Keep the sequences corresponding to the `p`-th percentile and below. Remove everything above. For example, "keeping the bottom 30%" means: compute the 30th percentile of the score distribution, and retain all sequences with scores at or below that value; discard the rest.
- **Keep top `p`:** Sort ascending, keep sequences at or above the `(1-p)`-th percentile.
- **Keep middle `p`:** Sort ascending, compute the `(0.5 - p/2)`-th and `(0.5 + p/2)`-th percentiles. Retain sequences with scores between these two values; discard everything below and above. The paper's explicit example: "When retaining the middle 30%, we calculate the 35th and 65th percentile and remove all data points above and below those numbers respectively."

**Why discretize into three coarse subsets (bottom/middle/top):** The paper argues that "the effectiveness of these metrics in this specific context remains uncertain" and therefore the contrasting subsets are used "to clarify the relationship between each metric and overall model performance." In other words, without a pre-existing theory of whether easy, medium, or hard examples are most valuable, the design must treat all three as plausible hypotheses and test them directly.

#### Dataset Preprocessing Pipeline

The raw data source for all experiments is a random sample from the May 2022 snapshot of CommonCrawl. This snapshot is first **prefiltered** using a combination of automatic and hand-crafted filters before any pruning occurs (Section 3.2). The paper lists the prefiltering steps:

- **Repetitive document removal:** Documents with excessive repetition (common in web scrapes due to templated content, navigation bars, or scraping artifacts) are removed. The paper references similar deduplication steps in Taylor et al. (2022) and Kocetkov et al. (2022).
- **Special character filtering:** Documents containing percentages of special characters above a threshold are removed.
- **Explicit/toxic text filtering:** Documents containing explicit words or toxic text are removed.

**Why prefilter before pruning:** The paper explicitly states that it "aim[s] to further improve data quality beyond common rule-based filters." The prefiltering removes the most obviously broken content—content that rule-based heuristics *can* reliably identify—so that the model-based pruning metrics are operating on data that is already reasonably clean. This tests whether pruning can find additional quality distinctions *within* data that already passes standard filters. Without prefiltering, the pruning metrics might simply rediscover that obviously broken documents have extreme scores (e.g., very high perplexity because they're incoherent), which would not be an interesting finding.

After prefiltering, the dataset is **downsampled** to 7.6 billion tokens, approximately 20% of the full CommonCrawl snapshot. This downsampling is computationally motivated: "This downsampling is required due to the computational cost of our various ablation experiments, which each require pretraining a new model from random initialization." Each combination of pruning metric × pruning ratio × subset criterion requires training a completely new model. With three metrics, four pruning ratios, three subset criteria, and additional variations for reference model ablate, the number of required training runs is in the dozens—hence the dataset must be small enough to make this tractable.

**Tokenization and sequence construction.** The prefiltered and downsampled dataset is tokenized using Byte Pair Encoding (BPE) with a vocabulary size of 51,200 tokens. After tokenization, a special `<eod>` token is appended to the end of each document. All tokenized documents are concatenated into a single stream, and this stream is split into sequences of exactly 2048 tokens, which equals the model's context length. The `<eod>` tokens are embedded within sequences wherever document boundaries happen to fall.

**Why 2048 tokens:** This matches the context window of all models used in the study. The pruning metrics are computed per-sequence of 2048 tokens, meaning that perplexity, EL2N, and memorization scores reflect the model's behavior on these specific 2048-token chunks. This ensures consistency between the contexts for which scores are computed and the contexts the pruned model will actually see during training—both operate on 2048-token windows.

**Test set construction.** The evaluation uses a test set drawn from the same CommonCrawl snapshot and subjected to identical prefiltering as the training data. The test set contains 266 million tokens, which the paper notes is "equivalent to about 3.5% of the training set." This ensures the test distribution matches the training distribution (modulo pruning), avoiding distribution-shift confounds when evaluating perplexity.

**Wikipedia reference data.** For the ablation comparing reference models trained on different corpora (Section 4.4), the paper also uses an English Wikipedia dataset containing 5.3 million tokens. This is dramatically smaller than the CommonCrawl training corpus but represents a high-quality, cleanly curated dataset that is frequently used as a reference domain for quality filtering (as in CCNet and other prior work).

#### The Perplexity Metric

Perplexity is the simplest and least computationally expensive of the three metrics. It measures how well a reference model predicts a given sequence of text—concretely, how "surprised" the model is by each next token in the sequence.

For a sequence `z_i` of length `|z_i|` tokens, the perplexity is:

$$\text{PPL}(z_i) = \exp\left( \frac{1}{|z_i|} \sum_{t_j \in z_i} \text{NLL}(t_j) \right)$$

where `|z_i|` is the number of tokens in the sequence (always 2048 in this paper's setup, but the notation allows variable length), `t_j` is the j-th token in the sequence, and `NLL(t_j)` is the negative log-likelihood of token `t_j` given all preceding tokens.

The negative log-likelihood for an individual token is computed as:

$$\text{NLL}(t_j) = -\log P(t_j \mid t_{<j}; \theta)$$

where `P(t_j \mid t_{<j}; \theta)` is the probability assigned by the reference model with parameters `θ` to the actual next token `t_j`, conditioned on all preceding tokens in the sequence.

**What these equations compute together:** For each sequence, the reference model processes it token by token, maintaining the standard autoregressive causal mask. At each position `j`, the model outputs a probability distribution over the full 51,200-token vocabulary, and the equation extracts the probability assigned to the token that *actually appears* at position `j` in the training data. These per-token probabilities are logged, negated, averaged across all 2048 positions, exponentiated, and the result is a single scalar—the perplexity—for that sequence.

**What perplexity means operationally:** Perplexity can be interpreted as the weighted average branching factor—if the model assigned equal probability to every token in the vocabulary, perplexity would be 51,200 (the vocabulary size). A lower perplexity means the model is consistently confident in its predictions for the actual next tokens; it finds the text predictable and well-modeled. A higher perplexity means the model is frequently surprised—the actual next tokens are ones the model assigned low probability to. In the context of web-scraped text, lower perplexity might correspond to clean, well-structured, grammatically standard text that aligns with the model's learned distribution, while higher perplexity might correspond to noisy, unusual, or domain-specific text that the model finds harder to predict.

**Why exponentiate the average NLL:** The exponential transformation converts the average negative log-likelihood (which is in "nat" units if using natural log, and can be any non-negative real number) into a more interpretable quantity that can be thought of as an "effective vocabulary size" for the model's uncertainty. This is mathematically equivalent to `exp(cross-entropy)`, connecting perplexity directly to information-theoretic compression.

**Why average before exponentiating:** The averaging inside the exponential ensures that sequence length does not affect the score—a longer sequence of equally predictable text would have the same per-token perplexity. Since all sequences are the same length (2048), averaging is mathematically equivalent to summing and scaling by a constant, but the formulation makes the metric conceptually length-invariant.

**Reference model training and selection for perplexity.** The paper uses a crucial design choice: the model computing perplexity (the **reference model**) is *different* from the model trained on the pruned data (the **pruned model**). This is not a self-pruning setup where a model evaluates its own training data. Instead:

- **Large reference models (6B, 13B, 52B):** These are fully trained Cohere models ("trained on full web-scale datasets"), used off-the-shelf without any additional training for the pruning experiments. They have been trained on data that is much larger and more diverse than the 7.6B-token CommonCrawl subset used for pruning experiments.
- **Small reference models (124M):** These are trained from scratch specifically for the pruning experiments, on either CommonCrawl or Wikipedia, at various training step counts (14%, 55%, or 100% of total training). When trained on CommonCrawl for pruning, the 124M reference model is "trained on a non-overlapping subset from the CommonCrawl dataset that is pruned and used to train the student model." This prevents data leakage: the reference model does not train on the exact same sequences it will later score, avoiding the pathology where a model assigns unrealistically low perplexity to memorized training examples and thus misranks them.

**Why vary reference model size:** The paper hypothesizes that a larger, more capable reference model might produce better-calibrated perplexity scores—a 52B model has a more sophisticated internal model of language, so its "surprise" at different types of text might better reflect genuine quality distinctions rather than just modeling limitations. The results confirm this: the 52B reference model produces better pruning outcomes than smaller models. The operational interpretation is that "larger models are better calibrated at computing a useful data pruning ranking."

**Why vary reference model training data (CommonCrawl vs. Wikipedia):** The paper tests whether a reference model trained on cleaner data (Wikipedia: curated, well-written, encyclopedic) produces better pruning signals than one trained on noisier data (CommonCrawl: web text, mixed quality). The hypothesis is that a Wikipedia-trained model might have a more refined notion of what "good" text looks like, making its perplexity scores more discriminative of quality. The results support this: the Wikipedia-trained reference model produces better pruned models, suggesting "investing in a high quality reference model to generate rankings results in more effective data pruning."

**Why vary reference model training steps (14%, 55%, 100%):** Motivated by prior work showing that early training checkpoints contain useful signals (Paul et al., 2023), the paper tests whether a partially-trained reference model—cheaper to obtain—can still compute useful perplexity rankings. The 14% checkpoint (250 training steps) proves insufficient, producing rankings with "minimal variance across percentages and subset selection criteria" and poor discrimination between useful and useless data. The 55% checkpoint (1000 steps) performs comparably to the fully trained model, establishing that "fully training the reference model is shown not to be necessary to uphold comparable performance."

**The operational cost of perplexity computation.** For a reference model and a dataset of `n` sequences, computing perplexity requires one forward pass per sequence (no backward pass, no gradient computation). For a 52B model scoring billions of tokens, this is expensive but not prohibitive, and the paper notes that "in practice, we expect many practitioners to use off the shelf models for computing perplexity and may not need to carry the cost of pretraining a reference model from random initialization."

#### The EL2N Metric

The Error L2-Norm (EL2N) was originally proposed by Paul et al. (2023) for supervised image classification. It quantifies how "wrong" a model's predictions are for each training example early in training, under the hypothesis that examples the model learns to predict correctly early on are "easy" and potentially less informative, while examples where the model continues to make large errors are "hard" and potentially more important for learning.

The paper adapts EL2N to autoregressive language modeling by computing the L2 norm of the error vector at each token position and averaging:

$$\text{EL2N}(z_i) = \frac{1}{t} \sum_{j=1}^{t} \|\hat{y}_j - y_j\|_2$$

where `t` is the sequence length (2048), `\hat{y}_j` is the reference model's predicted probability distribution over the vocabulary at position `j` (a vector of length 51,200, with entries summing to 1), and `y_j` is the one-hot encoded representation of the ground-truth token at position `j` (a vector of length 51,200 with a 1 at the index of the true token and 0 everywhere else).

**What this computes:** For each token position, the model outputs a probability distribution `\hat{y}_j` over all 51,200 possible next tokens. The ground truth `y_j` is a one-hot vector indicating which token *actually* appears. The L2 norm of the difference vector `\hat{y}_j - y_j` measures how far the model's predicted distribution is from the target distribution. If the model assigns high probability to the correct token, the error vector will have most entries near 0 (the model's probability mass is close to the one-hot target), and the L2 norm will be small. If the model's probability mass is spread across many tokens far from the target, the L2 norm will be large. These per-token L2 norms are averaged across all 2048 positions to produce a single EL2N score for the sequence.

**The intuition about EL2N scores:** From Paul et al. (2023), the paper explains: "exhibiting a low EL2N score are typically those the model learns in its early stages of training, likely because they are relatively easier. Inversely, examples with higher EL2N scores are hypothesized to indicate that the model continues to incur a significant loss for them and may require additional iterations to learn." In the vision setting, low-EL2N examples are those the classifier gets right immediately; high-EL2N examples are those where the classifier struggles. The hypothesis is that these difficulty distinctions identify which examples carry the most learning signal.

**Why L2 norm rather than cross-entropy or other error measures:** The L2 norm of the probability vector error provides a different signal than the cross-entropy loss (which is essentially the negative log of the probability assigned to the correct token). Cross-entropy is sensitive primarily to the probability assigned to the correct class—a model could assign 0.99 probability to the correct token and 0.01 across all others, yielding low cross-entropy. But the L2 norm to the one-hot target also penalizes probability mass placed on incorrect tokens. If a model assigns 0.5 to the correct token and spreads 0.5 across many incorrect tokens, the L2 norm is large (most of the one-hot vector's entries are non-zero), while if it assigns 0.5 to the correct token and 0.5 to a single incorrect token, the L2 norm is smaller. The L2 norm thus captures something about the *distribution* of errors, not just the probability of the correct answer. Whether this distinction is practically important for language data is one of the empirical questions the paper tests.

**Computational cost of EL2N vs. perplexity:** Computing EL2N requires exactly the same forward pass as computing perplexity—the model predicts `\hat{y}_j` in both cases, and the difference is only in what you do with that prediction (`-log P(correct)` vs. `||\hat{y} - y||_2`). So EL2N is not more computationally expensive per sequence than perplexity. However, the paper explores an additional cost multiplier: averaging EL2N scores across multiple reference models.

**Averaging EL2N across multiple reference models.** Following the recommendation in Paul et al. (2023) to reduce noise in EL2N signals, the paper trains "ten different reference models with different random initializations and average[s] the EL2N score from all ten models to obtain our final EL2N score." This means computing the score ten times—ten forward passes per sequence—and averaging, making the final EL2N ranking 10× more expensive than a single-model perplexity ranking. The averaging is hypothesized to produce a more robust signal by canceling out random variation in individual models' error patterns.

**The role of early checkpoints in EL2N.** The paper evaluates EL2N scores from reference models at two checkpoints: 14% of total training (250 steps) and 55% of total training (1000 steps). The motivation (from Paul et al., 2023) is that *early* training signals are most informative for pruning because they capture which examples the model learns quickly vs. slowly. A fully trained model might have near-zero loss on most examples, making the EL2N signal collapse. The paper's results show that the 55% checkpoint provides useful discrimination, while the 14% checkpoint does not ("training on only 14% of the data is inadequate for our reference model to offer precise pruning scores").

#### The Memorization Factor Metric

The memorization factor quantifies how much of a given text sequence a language model reproduces verbatim when prompted with its beginning. It is adapted from Biderman et al. (2023a), who studied it as a descriptive property of language models—"how much do models memorize their training data?"—not as a data pruning tool.

The memorization score for a sequence is defined as:

$$\text{score}(M, N) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \mathbf{1}(z_{M+i} = \hat{z}_{M+i})$$

where `M` is the number of prompt tokens (set to 32), `N` is the number of tokens to generate (set to 32), `z` is the original data point (a tokenized sequence), `\hat{z}` is the sequence of tokens greedily generated by the reference model starting from the prompt, `z_{M+i}` is the `(M+i)`-th token in the original sequence, `\hat{z}_{M+i}` is the `(M+i)`-th generated token, and `\mathbf{1}(\cdot)` is the indicator function returning 1 if the condition is true and 0 otherwise.

**What this computes in plain terms:** The reference model is given the first 32 tokens of a 2048-token sequence as a prompt. It then auto-regressively generates 32 more tokens, greedily (always picking the single highest-probability token at each step, with no sampling). For each of those 32 generated positions, the system checks whether the generated token exactly matches the token that appears at that position in the original training data. The score is the fraction of the 32 generated tokens that match exactly—a value between 0 (no matches; the model's continuations are entirely different from the original text) and 1 (perfect match; the model reproduces the original text verbatim from the prompt).

**Why M=N=32:** The paper states this choice without extensive justification, but it represents a balance. Larger values would capture longer-range memorization but might be computationally more expensive and might conflate memorization of specific facts with memorization of common phrases. Smaller values would be noisier. The symmetric choice (same number of prompt and generation tokens) is mathematically clean but not theoretically motivated in the text.

**Connection to data quality pruning:** The paper's hypothesis is that memorization scores might correlate with learning utility. A high memorization score means the model has internalized the exact continuation so thoroughly that it can reproduce it verbatim—these sequences might be "too easy" or "already learned" and thus unnecessary for further training. A low memorization score means the model's natural continuations differ from the original—these sequences might contain novel patterns, rare constructions, or genuinely unpredictable content that the model has not yet learned, making them potentially more valuable. The hypothesis is essentially that memorization factor captures a different notion of "difficulty" than perplexity or EL2N, one related to exact reproduction rather than predictive probability.

**Reference model requirement for memorization scores.** The paper notes that it uses "reference models guaranteed to have seen the full training set to ensure the applicability of memorization scores." This is a crucial constraint: a model can only memorize text it has been trained on. If a reference model had *not* seen the training data, its memorization scores would be near zero for all sequences (the model can't reproduce text it hasn't learned), and the scores would be uninformative. This means the reference model for memorization-based pruning must be trained on data that substantially overlaps with the corpus being pruned—a constraint that does not apply to perplexity or EL2N (which can be computed by any model on any text).

**Why greedy decoding:** Using greedy decoding (always picking the argmax token) makes the memorization score deterministic for a given model and sequence. If sampling were used (e.g., temperature > 0), the score would vary across runs, requiring multiple generations per sequence to estimate reliably, which would increase computational cost. Greedy decoding tests whether the model's single most-likely continuation matches the original—the strictest possible test of verbatim reproduction.

**What a memorization score of 1.0 vs. 0.5 vs. 0.0 means in practice:** The paper's Appendix B (Tables 3–7) provides examples from different subsets of the memorization distribution. A score of 1.0 corresponds to sequences where the model can reproduce the next 32 tokens perfectly—these tend to be highly formulaic or templated text (e.g., return policies, standard legal disclaimers, boilerplate product descriptions). A score of 0 corresponds to sequences where every generated token differs from the original—these can be genuinely novel or unusual text, but also noisy or incoherent text that the model can't reproduce because it's not systematic. The examples in Table 7 show that a memorization factor of 0 contains diverse, sometimes weird content (forum posts, technical specifications, informal writing), while a memorization factor of 1.0 contains highly conventional, almost generic text.

#### Subset Selection Criteria and Pruning Ratio Mechanics

For each pruning metric, the paper tests three selection criteria and four pruning ratios, producing 12 experimental variants per metric (plus the no-pruning baseline and random pruning baselines). This combinatorial design requires an explicit operational definition of how each variant is constructed.

**Score computation step.** First, the chosen reference model computes the chosen metric (perplexity, EL2N, or memorization factor) for every sequence in the full training dataset `D`. This yields a distribution of `n` scalar scores, where `n` is the number of 2048-token sequences in the 7.6B-token training corpus. For perplexity, lower is "easier" (more predictable). For EL2N, lower is "easier" (smaller prediction error). For memorization factor, higher is "easier" (more memorized, more predictable in the sense of exact reproduction).

**Percentile computation.** Given the score distribution and a desired retention percentage `p` (10%, 30%, 50%, or 70%), the specific percentile thresholds are computed:

- **Keep bottom `p`:** Compute the `p`-th percentile of the score distribution (sorted ascending). Retain all sequences with scores ≤ this threshold. For example, keep bottom 30%: compute the 30th percentile, keep everything below it.
- **Keep top `p`:** Compute the `(100-p)`-th percentile. Retain all sequences with scores ≥ this threshold. For example, keep top 30%: compute the 70th percentile, keep everything above it.
- **Keep middle `p`:** Compute the `(50 - p/2)`-th and `(50 + p/2)`-th percentiles. Retain all sequences with scores between these two thresholds. For example, keep middle 30%: compute the 35th and 65th percentiles, keep everything between them.

**Why percentiles rather than absolute score thresholds:** Using percentiles guarantees that exactly `p`% of the dataset is retained regardless of the score distribution's shape. Absolute thresholds (e.g., "keep all sequences with perplexity below 50") would result in unpredictable retention percentages that depend on the reference model and dataset, making comparison across metrics and reference models impossible. Percentile-based selection normalizes the retention ratio, isolating the effect of *which part* of the distribution is kept from the effect of *how much* is kept.

**The interpretation mapping for each metric.** Because "easier" corresponds to different directions for different metrics, the meaning of "bottom" and "top" flips:

- For **perplexity**, bottom = lowest scores = easiest = most predictable sequences. Top = highest scores = hardest = least predictable sequences.
- For **EL2N**, bottom = lowest scores = easiest = sequences with smallest prediction errors. Top = highest scores = hardest = sequences with largest prediction errors.
- For **memorization**, bottom = lowest scores = *hardest* = least memorized sequences. Top = highest scores = *easiest* = most memorized sequences. This inversion matters: when the paper says "keeping the bottom subset" for memorization, it means keeping the *least memorized* (hardest) examples, not the easiest.

**Baselines.** In addition to the metric-based pruning variants, the paper includes two baselines:

- **No-pruning baseline:** Train a model on the complete, unpruned dataset `D` for the full training duration. This is the upper bound that pruning methods aim to match or exceed.
- **Random pruning baseline:** For each retention ratio `p`, randomly select `p`% of sequences from `D` without regard to any metric. This tests whether the metrics provide any signal beyond random subsampling. If a metric-based pruning method performs no better than random pruning, the metric is not capturing useful information about training value.

**Cross-validation and non-overlapping splits.** To prevent data leakage between reference models and pruned models, the paper ensures that when a 124M reference model is trained on CommonCrawl to compute pruning scores, it is "trained on a non-overlapping subset from the CommonCrawl dataset that is pruned and used to train the student model." This means the full CommonCrawl sample is split: part is used to train the reference model, and a *different* part is scored by that reference model and then used (after pruning) to train the pruned model. The exact split ratio is not specified, but the principle is that the reference model never trains on data it will later score, preventing artifacts from memorization-based overfitting from distorting the scores.

#### Reference Model Ablation Design

The paper conducts a systematic ablation study to understand what factors influence the quality of pruning rankings. Table 1 in the paper summarizes these axes, but the detailed logic of each ablation is worth unpacking.

**Reference model size (124M, 6B, 13B, 52B).** This ablation directly tests the hypothesis that better language models produce better pruning signals. A 52B-parameter model trained on web-scale data has substantially more refined internal representations of language than a 124M model trained on a small corpus. If perplexity scores from the larger model rank sequences more usefully for pruning, this would suggest that pruning quality scales with reference model capability—a finding with practical implications (is it worth using a large model to prune data for a smaller model?). The 124M models are trained from scratch for this study; the 6B, 13B, and 52B models are pre-existing Cohere models. The key finding is that increasing reference model size *does* improve pruning outcomes, with the 52B model achieving the best performance when keeping the middle or top subsets.

**Reference model training data (CommonCrawl vs. Wikipedia).** This ablation isolates the effect of the reference model's training distribution on pruning quality. The 124M model trained on Wikipedia sees only clean, well-structured, encyclopedic text—arguably the kind of text that a model *should* learn from. The 124M model trained on CommonCrawl sees a noisier, more diverse distribution. The Wikipedia-trained model generates perplexity scores that more effectively discriminate useful from useless data in the CommonCrawl corpus. The paper interprets this as evidence that "reference models trained on higher quality data are better at identifying a subset of data points most conducive to model performance."

**Reference model training steps (14%, 55%, 100%).** This ablation tests whether early training signals are sufficient for pruning, which matters because training a reference model fully just to prune data is expensive. The 14% checkpoint (250 steps) essentially fails—its scores produce minimal discrimination between subsets, and model performance degrades regardless of which subset is chosen. The 55% checkpoint (1000 steps) performs comparably to the fully trained model, demonstrating that a halfway-trained model is sufficient for generating useful perplexity rankings. For EL2N, the 55% checkpoint *is* the primary reference point, consistent with the original EL2N formulation's emphasis on early training dynamics.

**Why these ablations matter as a set:** Together, they characterize the "cost-quality" tradeoff of pruning. A large, fully-trained, clean-data reference model produces the best rankings but is most expensive to obtain. A smaller, partially-trained, noisy-data reference model is cheaper but may produce worse rankings. The paper's results provide guidance on where the sweet spot lies: the 55% checkpoint from a small model is adequate, but a larger model on cleaner data produces noticeably better results.

#### Training and Evaluation Setup

**Model architecture.** All models—both reference models and pruned models—are autoregressive decoder-only Transformers with a standard GPT-style architecture (Radford et al., 2018). The paper mentions no architectural innovations; the focus is entirely on data, not model design. The context window is 2048 tokens for all models.

**Optimization for 124M parameter models.** These models, which constitute the bulk of the ablation experiments, are trained with AdamW (Loshchilov & Hutter, 2019) using linear cosine scaling and a batch size of 2048 sequences (each of length 2048 tokens, so approximately 4.2 million tokens per batch). They are trained for 8000 steps, meaning they process approximately 33 billion tokens over the course of training—roughly 4.4 epochs over the full 7.6B-token unpruned dataset. The learning rate linearly increases from 0 to 1.5e-4 over the full training duration. This is a cosine warmup schedule with no decay: the learning rate starts at 0, climbs linearly to the peak (1.5e-4), and stays there.

**Why 8000 steps / ~4.4 epochs:** The paper states this is "approximately 4.4 epochs over the unpruned dataset." For a dataset of 7.6B tokens with 4.2M tokens per batch, 8000 steps × 4.2M = 33.6B tokens, and 33.6B / 7.6B ≈ 4.4 epochs. This is a relatively standard number of epochs for small-scale language model training on limited data. The key observation is that this is *not* single-epoch training—the model sees repeated data—which means the ordering and composition of the pruned subset matters for what the model sees multiple times.

**Optimization for 1.5B parameter models.** The large-scale validation experiments use a different setup due to computational constraints. The 1.5B parameter models are trained with a batch size of 512 (rather than 2048) for 14,568 steps, processing approximately 7.6 billion tokens—equivalent to a single epoch over the unpruned dataset. The learning rate linearly increases from 0 to 1.2e-4. The smaller batch size and single-epoch regime are explicitly attributed to "the memory and computational costs of training 1.5B parameter models."

**Why change the training regime for larger models:** The paper is transparent that this is a practical compromise. Training 1.5B models for 4.4 epochs with batch size 2048 would be extremely expensive, and the 1.5B experiments exist to verify that the 124M findings scale, not to reproduce the exact same training dynamics at larger scale. The change from 4.4 epochs to 1 epoch is a confound—it means the pruning benefits observed at 1.5B could be partly due to the single-epoch regime interacting with data quality differently than the multi-epoch regime—but the paper's conclusion that pruning benefits persist at scale is supported by the consistent direction of the results.

**Evaluation for perplexity.** The primary evaluation metric is perplexity on a 266M-token test set drawn from the same CommonCrawl snapshot and subjected to identical prefiltering. The paper reports perplexity after 8000 training steps for 124M models, with the justification that this point is "chosen to compare performance after models have saturated their capacity by training enough steps to plateau on validation metrics." This means the perplexity numbers reflect near-convergence performance, not intermediate training dynamics.

**Evaluation for downstream tasks.** A subset of the trained 124M models are fine-tuned and evaluated on six classification tasks from the GLUE benchmark: SST2 (sentiment analysis), MRPC (paraphrase detection), QQP (question paraphrase detection), QNLI (question-answer entailment), RTE (textual entailment), and WNLI (coreference/entailment). Fine-tuning uses a learning rate of 1e-5, training for 3 epochs, with results reported as mean accuracy and standard deviation over 5 runs. The task datasets are not pruned—only the pretraining data is pruned—so the evaluation measures how pretraining data composition affects downstream transfer learning.

**Why evaluate on downstream tasks:** The paper notes that "the pretraining stage primarily focuses on knowledge acquisition," and the question is whether pruning pretraining data has "ripple effects" when models are fine-tuned. A pruning method that improves language modeling perplexity but degrades downstream task performance would be less practically valuable. The results show that pruning generally improves or maintains downstream performance, with no single pruning strategy dominating across all tasks—a finding the paper frames as "consistent with earlier findings in the literature."

**Statistical treatment.** The paper reports mean accuracy and standard deviation over 5 fine-tuning runs for GLUE tasks, but does not report confidence intervals or statistical tests for perplexity results. The relative ranking of methods is based on point estimates. For the cross-validation of pruning strategies, the paper does not use a held-out validation set to select the best variant—rather, all variants are tested and the best-performing variant *on the test set* is reported. This means the selection of "best variant" is somewhat circular (the variant that performs best on the test set is also the one evaluated on the test set), though the large number of training runs (minimum 9 per variant) provides some robustness.

**Summary of the minimum experimental scale.** The paper states it trains "a minimum of nine models with 124M parameters from scratch for each experimental variant." With 3 metrics × 4 pruning ratios × 3 subset criteria = 36 metric-based variants, plus baselines, plus reference model ablations, plus multiple seeds per variant, the total number of trained models is likely in the dozens to low hundreds. This is what makes the downsampling to 7.6B tokens necessary: training this many models on a larger dataset would be prohibitively expensive.

#### Summary of Key Design Choices and Their Justifications

- **Static rather than dynamic pruning:** Pruning happens once before training, not adaptively during training. This is simpler, cheaper (the dataset is pruned once, not repeatedly), and allows the core question—"can we identify useful data a priori?"—to be answered without confounding from dynamic curriculum effects.
- **Separate reference and pruned models:** The model scoring the data is different from the model trained on the pruned data. This prevents the self-fulfilling prophecy where a model identifies its own easy examples and then fails to learn from harder ones. It also means practitioners can use any available model as a reference, including much larger models than they intend to train.
- **Percentile-based rather than threshold-based pruning:** Guarantees controlled retention ratios across metrics and reference models, enabling fair comparison.
- **Bottom/middle/top as three separate hypotheses:** Rather than assuming "easy = good" or "hard = good," the paper treats all three subsets as empirical questions and discovers the middle is best—a finding that would have been missed by only testing one subset.
- **Prefiltering before pruning:** Ensures that the model-based metrics are tested on data that has already passed basic quality filters, isolating the contribution of the metric beyond what rule-based heuristics can achieve.
- **Test set from the same distribution as training:** The perplexity evaluation uses a held-out CommonCrawl split, not a different domain (like Wikipedia or books), ensuring that pruning-induced changes in training distribution are evaluated on in-distribution data.
- **Scaling validation from 124M to 1.5B:** Establishes that findings are not an artifact of small-model dynamics, though the change in training regime (4.4 epochs → 1 epoch) is an acknowledged confound.
- **Reference model training on non-overlapping data:** Prevents memorization-based artifacts where the reference model assigns unrealistically low perplexity to its own training examples, which would distort the ranking.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: The Most Valuable Training Data Is NOT the "Easiest"—It's the Middle of the Distribution

This paper's single most important conceptual contribution is the systematic falsification of the implicit assumption that has governed prior data filtering work: that cleaner, more predictable, more "model-friendly" text is what language models should be trained on. Prior work that used perplexity to filter pretraining data (Wenzek et al., 2020 in CCNet; Muennighoff et al., 2023; Laurençon et al., 2023) operated on the straightforward principle that high-perplexity text is "unnatural language" or noise, and that removing it leaves a higher-quality corpus. The field's default mental model was monotonic: lower perplexity → higher quality → better training data.

This paper overturns that model. The experimental design—explicitly testing three subsets (bottom, middle, top) of each scoring distribution rather than assuming bottom is best—reveals a pattern that is strikingly consistent across all three metrics tested. The middle subset of the perplexity distribution *consistently* outperforms the bottom (easiest) subset. For the 52B reference model with perplexity-based pruning, keeping the middle 50% achieves a 0.97% perplexity improvement over the no-pruning baseline (Figure 4), while keeping the bottom 50% degrades performance substantially below baseline (Figure 2, left panel). The same qualitative pattern holds for EL2N (Figure 3a) and memorization (Figure 3b): the most competitive variant is never the subset corresponding to the "easiest" data as measured by that metric.

This is a *fundamental* reframing, not an incremental refinement. It changes the question from "how do we identify and remove bad data?" to "what portion of the predictability spectrum is most useful for learning, and why?" The answer the paper provides—the middle—is counterintuitive. The easiest examples (lowest perplexity, most memorized, smallest EL2N) appear to be text that the model already predicts well; they don't challenge or expand its capabilities. The hardest examples (highest perplexity, least memorized, largest EL2N) appear to include genuinely pathological or unlearnable text. The sweet spot lies in between: text that is predictable *enough* to be coherent and well-formed, but not so predictable that the model gains nothing from processing it.

The significance of this finding goes beyond the specific numbers. It provides a diagnostic concept—the "middle of the distribution" hypothesis—that future data curation efforts should test rather than defaulting to filtering out the tail. It also explains why prior work that filtered out high-perplexity examples may have been doing the *opposite* of what benefits pretraining: keeping only the easiest data and discarding everything in the middle and upper range.

---

### Innovation 2: Simple Perplexity Beats Sophisticated, Theoretically-Motivated Alternatives at Scale

Given the paper's explicit framing as a rigorous comparison of pruning metrics, a central empirical contribution is the finding that the simplest method—ranking by perplexity—outperforms both EL2N (adapted from influential computer vision pruning work by Paul et al., 2023) and memorization factor (adapted from Biderman et al., 2023a's analysis of verbatim reproduction). This is not obvious ex ante. EL2N carries theoretical motivation from the supervised learning literature about identifying important examples through early learning dynamics. Memorization factor captures a fundamentally different signal—exact reproduction rather than probability—that might plausibly identify text requiring additional learning. Both are more computationally involved than perplexity (especially EL2N, which the paper computes by averaging over 10 reference models). Yet perplexity dominates across essentially every pruning ratio tested (Figure 4), and both EL2N and memorization perform similarly to *random pruning* (Figure 4)—meaning their additional complexity and cost yields no measurable benefit.

This finding is significant not because it introduces a new method, but because it tells the field **where to allocate effort**. The implication is that for pretraining-scale data pruning, developing ever-more-sophisticated scoring functions may be a poor use of research resources—perplexity, which is computationally cheap, well-understood, and computable with off-the-shelf models, already captures the relevant signal. The bottleneck is not the scoring function but understanding *which slice* of the resulting distribution to keep. This reframes the research agenda from "design better scoring metrics" to "characterize the relationship between scoring distribution slices and learning outcomes."

There's a subtlety worth noting. Perplexity's superiority is not just about simplicity—it's about the nature of the signal. EL2N measures prediction error magnitude; memorization measures exact reproduction. Perplexity measures how well the reference model's learned distribution covers the actual text, which inherently captures both coherence (low perplexity on well-structured text) and predictability (how "surprising" the text is to the model). This may be a more holistic quality signal for language data than the alternatives. The paper doesn't deeply analyze *why* perplexity wins, but the result itself is a strong prior for future work: before trying anything more complex, try perplexity first.

---

### Innovation 3: Data Pruning Benefits Scale with Reference Model Quality—Larger, Cleaner-Data Models Produce Better Rankings

The ablation study on reference model characteristics (Section 4.3, 4.4, 4.5) constitutes a finding that is both practically actionable and theoretically informative: **the quality of the pruning ranking depends on the quality of the model producing it.** Specifically:

- **Larger reference models produce better pruning outcomes.** The 52B parameter reference model's perplexity rankings yield the best pruned model performance. At the same pruning ratios, the 124M reference model's rankings produce worse results. The 52B model achieves a 2.2% perplexity improvement over the best 124M model results (Section 4.3). The 13B and 52B models are the only ones where keeping *less* data (30% or 50%) actually outperforms keeping more (70%)—a non-monotonic relationship that signals genuinely better discrimination (Figure 2, middle panel).

- **Reference models trained on cleaner data (Wikipedia) produce better pruning rankings than those trained on noisier data (CommonCrawl).** Section 4.4 shows that a Wikipedia-trained 124M model prunes CommonCrawl more effectively than a CommonCrawl-trained 124M model. The Wikipedia model's best variant outperforms the CommonCrawl model's best variant by 0.69% in perplexity.

- **Partially-trained reference models (55% of training) suffice.** The 55% checkpoint performs comparably to fully-trained models, while the 14% checkpoint fails to discriminate usefully (Section 4.5, Figure 6). This means the cost of obtaining a reference model can be substantially reduced.

Together, these findings establish a "scaling law for pruning quality": better reference models → better pruning → better pruned models. This is conceptually important because it suggests that data quality assessment is itself a capability that improves with model scale and training data quality. A 52B model has a more nuanced internal representation of language than a 124M model, and this nuance translates into more discriminative perplexity scores—the larger model is better at distinguishing "genuinely useful text" from "text that is merely predictable." The practical implication is equally clear: if you're going to prune data, use the best model you can afford to compute the pruning scores, and if possible, use a reference model trained on curated high-quality data rather than web-scraped data.

---

### Innovation 4: Pruning Down to 30% of Pretraining Data Can *Improve* Performance—Challenging "More Data Is Better"

The paper's headline quantitative result—that a model trained on the middle 30% of the perplexity distribution *outperforms* a model trained on 100% of the data (0.80% perplexity improvement, Figure 4)—is a genuinely striking empirical finding. Even at 50% retention, the improvement is 0.97% over the no-pruning baseline. These are not marginal effects where pruning merely "doesn't hurt much"—pruning *helps*.

The significance of this is not the specific percentage (30%) but the existence proof it provides: **a substantial fraction of a standard pretraining corpus is not merely redundant but actively harmful to model quality.** The paper deliberately chooses a prefiltered dataset that has already passed rule-based quality filters (deduplication, special character removal, toxic text filtering). Even after this cleaning, 50–70% of the remaining data can be discarded with *improved* outcomes. The "more data is better" assumption that has driven LLM scaling is not universally true—there exist regimes where removing data improves performance, and those regimes are reachable with simple, automatic methods.

This finding also establishes that random pruning is insufficient—the metric-based ranking matters. Figure 4 shows that random pruning to 30% degrades performance substantially below baseline, while perplexity-based pruning to the same 30% improves it. The ranking signal is doing real work.

The scaling validation to 1.5B parameter models (Figure 7) is critical here. The 1.5B model trained on perplexity-pruned data achieves a 1.5% improvement in test perplexity over the no-pruning 1.5B baseline. The fact that the benefit *grows* with model scale (0.97% at 124M with 50% retention → 1.5% at 1.5B) suggests that pruning may become *more* valuable at larger scales, where the cost of training on useless data is proportionally higher. If this trend holds at even larger scales (7B, 70B, etc.), the practical implications for training efficiency are substantial.

The downstream GLUE results (Table 2) add an important nuance: no single pruning strategy dominates across all tasks, but pruning in general improves or maintains performance relative to the no-pruning baseline. This suggests that the benefits of pruning are not merely an artifact of the language modeling objective but translate to general-purpose capabilities—and that different downstream tasks may benefit from different slices of the pretraining distribution, a finding with implications for task-specific data curation.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** All experiments use a random sample of the May 2022 CommonCrawl snapshot, prefiltered with rule-based heuristics (repetitive document removal, special character filtering, explicit/toxic text filtering) and then downsampled to 7.6 billion tokens—approximately 20% of the full snapshot. The test set is drawn from the same CommonCrawl snapshot with identical prefiltering and contains 266 million tokens (roughly 3.5% of the training set size). For the reference model training data ablation, an additional English Wikipedia dataset containing 5.3 million tokens is used.

- **Base model(s).** The primary models trained and evaluated are autoregressive decoder-only Transformers following a standard GPT-style architecture (Radford et al., 2018), sized at 124M parameters for the bulk of ablation experiments. A scaled-up validation is conducted at 1.5B parameters. The choice of 124M as the primary scale reflects the computational constraint imposed by the combinatorial experimental design: "each ablation study (pruning method, percent data remaining, section of distribution preserved) requires training a new model from random initialization," with "a minimum of nine models with 124M parameters from scratch for each experimental variant." Reference models used for scoring include 124M models trained specifically for this study (on CommonCrawl or Wikipedia) and pre-existing Cohere models at 6B, 13B, and 52B parameters trained on full web-scale datasets. All models use a context window of 2048 tokens and Byte Pair Encoding with a vocabulary size of 51,200.

- **Metrics.** The primary evaluation metric is **test set perplexity**, computed on the 266M-token held-out CommonCrawl split. Perplexity is the standard autoregressive language modeling metric: `exp(average negative log-likelihood per token)`. For downstream evaluation, models are fine-tuned on six GLUE benchmark classification tasks (SST2, MRPC, QQP, QNLI, RTE, WNLI) and evaluated by **accuracy** (mean and standard deviation over 5 fine-tuning runs). The paper does not report alternative language modeling metrics (e.g., bits per character, token accuracy), nor does it evaluate generation quality through automated metrics or human evaluation.

- **Baselines.** Two baselines are used throughout. The **no-pruning baseline** trains a model of identical architecture on the complete 7.6B-token unpruned dataset for the full training duration (8000 steps / ~33B tokens seen for 124M models). The **random pruning baseline** randomly selects `p`% of sequences from the full dataset without regard to any quality metric, tested at the same retention ratios (10%, 30%, 50%, 70%) as the metric-based methods. Random pruning serves as a lower bound: if a metric-based method does not outperform random selection, the metric's ranking signal provides no value beyond subsampling.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper measures compute indirectly through **dataset retention percentage** (10%, 30%, 50%, 70% of original data) and **total training tokens seen** (33B for 124M models, 7.6B for 1.5B models). There is no explicit FLOP accounting or wall-clock time measurement. The cost of computing pruning scores (forward passes of the reference model over the full dataset) is not included in any budget calculation—the paper treats scoring as a one-time preprocessing cost separate from training cost. For EL2N specifically, averaging across 10 reference models multiplies the scoring cost by 10× relative to single-model perplexity, but this cost differential is discussed qualitatively rather than quantified in FLOPs or GPU-hours.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The 124M parameter models are trained for 8000 steps, with perplexity evaluated "after models have saturated their capacity by training enough steps to plateau on validation metrics." For fine-tuning experiments, results are reported as mean accuracy and standard deviation over 5 runs. However, the paper does not report confidence intervals or formal statistical tests for the perplexity comparisons between pruning methods. The "best variant" for each metric is selected based on test set performance rather than a held-out validation set, introducing a potential circularity in the reported best-case numbers (the variant that happens to perform best on the test set is the one highlighted). The statement that "a minimum of nine models" are trained per variant provides some robustness through replication, but the exact number of seeds and the variance across them for perplexity results are not reported.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Aggregate Comparison of Pruning Metrics

Figure 4 presents the central quantitative comparison: the best-performing variant of each pruning metric plotted against dataset retention percentage, alongside the no-pruning and random pruning baselines. The key numbers:

- **Perplexity-based pruning (52B reference model, keeping the middle subset):** At 50% retention, achieves a **0.97% improvement** in test perplexity over the no-pruning baseline. At 30% retention, achieves a **0.80% improvement** over the no-pruning baseline. At 70% retention, performance is approximately at baseline. At 10% retention, performance degrades significantly below baseline—pruning too aggressively eventually hurts even with the best method.

- **EL2N-based pruning (best variant: 55% checkpoint, keeping the middle subset):** Does not outperform the no-pruning baseline at any retention ratio. The best performance is achieved at 50% retention, but this only approximately matches the baseline rather than exceeding it. At all other retention ratios, EL2N-pruned models perform worse than the no-pruning baseline.

- **Memorization-based pruning (best variant: keeping the bottom subset, i.e., least memorized):** Also fails to outperform the no-pruning baseline at any retention ratio. The best variant (bottom 70%) achieves performance close to but slightly below the baseline.

- **Random pruning:** Performance degrades monotonically as retention decreases. At 70% retention, random pruning approximately matches the baseline; at 50% it falls below; at 30% it degrades substantially; at 10% it collapses. Perplexity-based pruning substantially outperforms random pruning at all retention ratios.

The critical comparison from Figure 4 is that **perplexity-based pruning at 50% retention outperforms both the no-pruning baseline and every variant of EL2N and memorization at any retention ratio.** The paper quantifies the gap to the alternatives: "A model trained on 50% of the dataset pruned based on perplexity achieves 1.33% and 1.77% improvement over the most performant models pruned to 50% of the dataset with EL2N and memorization factor respectively" (Section 1, contribution 1). At 30% retention, the gap is 2.1% and 1.6% respectively.

#### Difficulty-Subset Analysis: Bottom vs. Middle vs. Top

Each metric is tested with three subset selection criteria—keeping the bottom, middle, or top of the scoring distribution—and the results reveal a consistent pattern that the paper identifies as one of its central findings.

**Perplexity subset analysis (Figure 2):** The three panels of Figure 2 show test perplexity for models trained on bottom, middle, and top subsets of the perplexity distribution, with different reference model sizes distinguished by color.

- **Bottom subset (lowest perplexity, "easiest"):** Performance is poor and degrades severely as retention decreases. The bottom subset "has much less variance in results between reference models of varying sizes, indicating the bottom subset may not be suitable for training." Even at 70% retention, the bottom subset underperforms the no-pruning baseline for all reference model sizes. This is the most important negative result: keeping only the most predictable, lowest-perplexity text—which is what prior filtering work implicitly did—severely degrades model quality.

- **Middle subset:** Consistently achieves the best performance across reference model sizes. For the larger reference models (13B and 52B), retaining only 50% or even 30% of the middle subset **outperforms retaining 70%** of the training set—a non-monotonic relationship that indicates genuine pruning benefit rather than merely tolerating data removal.

- **Top subset (highest perplexity, "hardest"):** Performance is intermediate—better than the bottom subset, worse than the middle. The paper does not highlight the top subset as competitive.

**EL2N subset analysis (Figure 3a):** The middle subset is also the best variant for EL2N. The best performance is achieved at 50% retention of the middle subset, which outperforms the model trained on 70% of the dataset—the same non-monotonic pattern observed with perplexity. However, even this best variant does not exceed the no-pruning baseline. The paper notes: "As the middle subset grows, it begins to overlap with the easiest examples, degrading performance"—suggesting that as the retention ratio increases, the middle subset expands to include low-EL2N (easy) examples that dilute its effectiveness.

**Memorization subset analysis (Figure 3b):** Unlike perplexity and EL2N, the best variant for memorization is keeping the **bottom** (least memorized) subset, particularly bottom 70%. The paper observes that "memorization never outperforms the no-pruning baseline." The top subset (most memorized, easiest) performs poorly, consistent with the general pattern that "easiest" examples degrade performance.

The paper summarizes the subset-selection finding in Section 4.1:

> "Though the most competitive variant for each pruning method varies based on the subset of the scoring distribution retained (top, middle, or bottom), we observe a consistent pattern: the highest performant variants are not the subsets that correspond to the 'easier' data."

#### Reference Model Quality Ablations

**Reference model size (Figure 2, Section 4.3):** Larger reference models produce better pruning rankings. For the middle subset—the most competitive variant—the 52B reference model achieves the lowest test perplexity, followed by 13B, then 6B, then 124M. The paper quantifies: "Data pruning using the perplexity scores generated from a 52B parameter reference model achieves a 2.2% improvement in perplexity over the best-performing trained model from the 124M parameter reference model experiments." Furthermore, for the 13B and 52B reference models only, retaining the middle 30% and 50% produces pruned models that outperform the models trained on the middle 70%—the non-monotonic benefit only emerges with sufficiently capable reference models. The paper also notes that the bottom subset performs even worse with larger reference models than with smaller ones ("The larger reference models' bottom subset training runs perform even worse than their smaller counterparts"), suggesting larger models more confidently identify genuinely useless "easy" text.

**Reference model training data (Figure 5, Section 4.4):** Comparing a 124M reference model trained on Wikipedia versus one trained on CommonCrawl, the Wikipedia-trained model produces better pruning rankings. "In the two optimal selection variants from the general reference models (middle and top), a model trained on Wikipedia consistently yields lower validation perplexity compared to a model trained on CommonCrawl." The Wikipedia model's best variant (middle 70%) outperforms the CommonCrawl model's best variant (also middle 70%) by 0.69% in test perplexity. The paper interprets this as evidence that "reference models trained on higher quality data are better at identifying a subset of data points most conducive to model performance."

**Reference model training steps (Figure 6, Section 4.5):** Early checkpoints of reference models are tested for both perplexity and EL2N. The 14% checkpoint (250 steps) for both metrics produces rankings with "minimal variance across percentages and subset selection criteria"—essentially failing to discriminate between useful and useless data. Performance across subsets "changes considerably less than either the 55% checkpoint or the fully trained models." The 55% checkpoint (1000 steps), however, "perform[s] in a similar manner to the fully trained models, performing best with the middle subset, worst with the bottom subset, and comparably with the top subset." For EL2N specifically, averaging scores across 10 reference models at the 55% checkpoint produces patterns that "more similarly mirror what we see with the larger, fully trained reference models"—specifically, in the middle subset, using 50% of the data outperforms using 70%, and in the bottom subset, performance "more clearly monotonically degrades" as retention decreases. The paper concludes that "averaging scores across reference models helps hone the pruning signal."

#### Scaling to 1.5B Parameter Models

Figure 7 presents the scaling validation, comparing perplexity-based pruning (52B reference model, middle subset) against random pruning at both 124M and 1.5B parameter scales. The key findings:

- **Random pruning at 1.5B scale performs "considerably well, even reaching levels below the no-pruning run."** This is a notable result—at the larger model scale with single-epoch training, random subsampling of the pretraining data does not necessarily hurt and may slightly help.

- **Perplexity-based pruning nonetheless outperforms random pruning at both scales.** At 1.5B parameters, perplexity-based pruning achieves a **1.5% improvement in test perplexity over the no-pruning baseline** of the same size (Section 6). The improvement over random pruning follows "a consistent pattern for both the 124M and 1.5B models."

- **The benefit of pruning appears to grow with model scale.** The improvement is 0.97% at 124M (50% retention, middle subset) versus 1.5% at 1.5B, though the training regimes differ (4.4 epochs vs. 1 epoch, different batch sizes), making the comparison not perfectly controlled. Nonetheless, the direction of the effect supports the paper's claim that "this demonstrates the scalability of our approach to a large-scale pretraining setting."

#### Downstream GLUE Evaluation

Table 2 presents accuracy on six GLUE classification tasks for the best variants of each pruning method, alongside the no-pruning baseline and random pruning baselines. The paper summarizes:

> "We observe that pruning the pretraining dataset consistently improves performance across all tasks."

Specifically, the no-pruning baseline accuracy values are: SST2 78.15, MRPC 64.32, QQP 76.55, QNLI 65.40, RTE 49.69, WNLI 51.56. Nearly every pruning variant (including random pruning at some ratios) achieves accuracy at or above these baselines on at least some tasks, with underlined results in the table indicating values that surpass the no-pruning baseline.

However, the paper notes: "no single pruning strategy (combining both pruning metric and percentage of remaining data) stands out as superior across all tasks." Specific examples from Table 2:

- **SST2:** Best result is EL2N middle 50% at 79.17 (improvement of ~1.0 over baseline). Memorization bottom 30% achieves 78.52.
- **MRPC:** Best result is EL2N middle 70% at 66.46 (improvement of ~2.1 over baseline). Random pruning 30% achieves 66.04.
- **QQP:** Best result is EL2N middle 30% at 77.47 (improvement of ~0.9 over baseline).
- **QNLI:** Best result is EL2N middle 30% at 68.63 (improvement of ~3.2 over baseline).
- **RTE:** Best result is EL2N middle 10% at 51.95 (improvement of ~2.3 over baseline). Perplexity middle 10% achieves 51.17.
- **WNLI:** Best result is EL2N middle 30% at 55.31 (improvement of ~3.8 over baseline).

The paper notes that "even random pruning shows improvements in certain tasks, underscoring the significance of downsampling when handling noisy data during the pretraining stage to mitigate potential learning degradation." This is an important caveat: the downstream benefits of pruning are not unique to metric-based methods and may partly reflect a general benefit of training on less data when that data is noisy, regardless of *which* data is removed. However, the fact that EL2N-based pruning achieves the best results on most tasks is somewhat surprising given that EL2N underperformed perplexity on language modeling perplexity—suggesting that the relationship between pruning metric and downstream task performance may differ from the relationship between pruning metric and language modeling performance.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Perplexity subset selection across reference model sizes (Figure 2, all three panels):** The bottom subset consistently underperforms regardless of reference model size, and its performance degrades further with larger reference models. The middle subset is consistently the best, with a non-monotonic benefit (50% > 70%) emerging only at larger reference model scales (13B, 52B). The top subset shows intermediate performance, generally improving with reference model size but never surpassing the middle subset. This three-panel comparison demonstrates that the subset selection choice is not an artifact of a particular reference model scale—the pattern is robust across 124M through 52B parameters.

**EL2N early vs. late checkpoint and single vs. averaged scores (Figure 6, right side; Section 4.5):** The 14% checkpoint for EL2N produces rankings with poor discrimination—performance is similar across all retention ratios and subset criteria, suggesting the model has not learned enough to distinguish useful from useless data. The 55% checkpoint performs better but still underperforms perplexity. Averaging EL2N across 10 reference models improves the signal quality: "the pattern of performance more similarly mirrors what we see with the larger, fully trained reference models," with the middle 50% outperforming middle 70% and the bottom subset showing cleaner monotonic degradation. However, even averaged EL2N does not outperform the no-pruning baseline, making this an improvement within a method that remains inferior to simpler alternatives.

**Wikipedia vs. CommonCrawl reference model training data (Figure 5):** Across pruning percentages from 10% to 70%, the Wikipedia-trained reference model produces consistently lower test perplexity than the CommonCrawl-trained reference model when keeping the middle or top subsets. For the bottom subset, both reference models perform poorly. The gap is largest at 70% retention (middle subset), where Wikipedia outperforms CommonCrawl by 0.69%. This ablation strengthens the finding that reference model quality (here, training data quality) directly affects pruning effectiveness.

**Scaling of pruning benefits to 1.5B models (Figure 7):** The perplexity-based pruning advantage over random pruning persists and potentially widens at 1.5B scale (~1.5% over no-pruning baseline vs. ~0.97% at 124M). However, the training regime changes between scales (4.4 epochs vs. 1 epoch; batch size 2048 vs. 512; learning rate 1.5e-4 vs. 1.2e-4), making the comparison across scales imperfectly controlled. The paper does not ablate whether the increased benefit at 1.5B is due to model scale, the single-epoch regime, or an interaction between them.

**Downstream task sensitivity to pruning variant (Table 2):** The finding that "no single pruning strategy stands out as superior across all tasks" serves as an implicit robustness check—different downstream tasks are sensitive to different slices of the pretraining data distribution. EL2N-based pruning achieves the best downstream results despite underperforming on language modeling perplexity, suggesting that metrics optimal for language modeling may not be optimal for transfer learning. The paper does not explore this discrepancy in detail.

**Data distribution visualization (Appendix A, Figure 8):** The distributions of perplexity scores from different reference models (Figure 8a) show that larger models produce more spread-out, right-skewed distributions with heavier tails. The distributions from different training data (Figure 8c) show that the Wikipedia-trained model produces a tighter distribution than the CommonCrawl-trained model. These distributional differences provide a partial explanation for why larger and cleaner-data reference models produce better rankings—they offer more discriminative score separation between sequences. The EL2N and memorization factor distributions (Figure 8b) are also shown, with memorization factor having a highly discrete distribution (the dotted percentile lines overlap due to concentration at specific values like 0).

### Critical Assessment

The paper makes several central claims, which I'll evaluate against the experimental evidence presented.

**Claim: "Simple perplexity-based pruning outperforms more sophisticated and computationally expensive methods such as EL2N and memorization."**

**Assessment:** Well-supported for the specific experimental conditions tested, with important constraints. Figure 4 shows perplexity-based pruning (52B reference, middle subset) clearly outperforming the best variants of EL2N and memorization at all retention ratios. The gap is substantial: 1.33% over EL2N and 1.77% over memorization at 50% retention. However, this result is specific to (a) the CommonCrawl dataset, (b) the 124M parameter pruned model scale, (c) the 52B reference model for perplexity (versus 124M reference models for EL2N and memorization), and (d) the specific hyperparameter choices for each metric (e.g., M=N=32 for memorization, 10-model averaging for EL2N). The comparison is not perfectly controlled: perplexity benefits from a much larger reference model (52B vs. 124M), which may account for some of its advantage. A fairer comparison would use the 52B model to compute EL2N and memorization scores as well—but this was not done, likely because EL2N and memorization are more naturally computed from models that are still in training or have seen the specific data. Additionally, the EL2N underperformance may partly reflect the specific checkpoint choice (55% of a 124M model's training) rather than an inherent limitation of EL2N—the vision literature found EL2N effective with larger models and different training regimes.

**Claim: "Training on the 'easiest' examples degrades performance; the middle of the distribution is most valuable."**

**Assessment:** Strongly supported within the paper's operational definitions of "easy," but those definitions warrant scrutiny. The finding is consistent across all three metrics: keeping the bottom (easiest) subset consistently produces the worst models, and the middle subset consistently produces the best. For perplexity (Figure 2), the bottom subset underperforms across all reference model sizes and all retention ratios. The evidence is particularly compelling because it contradicts the implicit assumption in prior perplexity-based filtering work (Wenzek et al., 2020; Muennighoff et al., 2023), making it a genuine discovery rather than a confirmation of existing beliefs. However, the claim is demonstrated for one dataset (CommonCrawl) with one type of "easiness" (perplexity relative to a particular reference model). "Easy" is a relative concept—what is easy for a 52B model may differ from what is easy for a 124M model—and the paper shows that the bottom subset for larger reference models is even worse than for smaller ones, suggesting the definition of "easiest" is model-dependent. The paper does not test whether the "middle of the distribution" finding holds for datasets with different noise characteristics (e.g., curated datasets like The Pile, or domain-specific corpora), which limits the generality of the claim.

**Claim: "Pruning down to 30% of the original dataset can achieve similar or better performance compared to training on the full dataset."**

**Assessment:** Supported at 124M scale, with qualifications. The 0.80% improvement at 30% retention (perplexity, middle subset, 52B reference model; Figure 4) is a genuine positive result, and it requires the combination of a good reference model and correct subset selection—random pruning at 30% degrades performance substantially. The 1.5B scale validation (Figure 7) shows pruning benefits persisting, though at a different retention ratio and training regime. However, these results are obtained after training for a fixed number of steps (8000 for 124M, 14568 for 1.5B). Because pruned models see fewer total tokens than the no-pruning baseline (at 30% retention, the 124M model sees 30% of the data for the same number of steps, meaning more epochs over less unique data), the comparison confounds data quantity with data repetition. The paper acknowledges that models train for "approximately 4.4 epochs over the unpruned dataset" (for 124M at 8000 steps), but for a 30% pruned dataset, this becomes ~14.7 epochs over the remaining data. The improvement might partly reflect beneficial effects of repeated exposure to high-quality data rather than the removal of harmful data per se. An alternative baseline—training the no-pruning model for proportionally fewer steps to match the token count of the pruned model—would help disambiguate these effects but is not included.

**Claim: "Pruning benefits scale to larger models, with 1.5B parameter models achieving 1.5% improvement over no-pruning baseline."**

**Assessment:** Supported directionally, but with significant experimental confounds. The 1.5B experiment uses a different training regime (single epoch, smaller batch size, different learning rate) from the 124M experiments (4.4 epochs, larger batch size). The improvement grows from 0.97% at 124M to 1.5% at 1.5B, which is consistent with the scaling claim, but the confounded variables (epoch count, batch size, learning rate) make it impossible to attribute the increased benefit to model scale alone. More critically, the 1.5B validation uses only one pruning variant (perplexity, 52B reference, middle subset)—it does not test whether the "middle > bottom > top" pattern or the superiority of perplexity over EL2N and memorization also scales. The paper does not train 1.5B models with EL2N or memorization pruning, so the claim that "simple perplexity outperforms complex methods" is only validated at 124M. Given the computational cost of 1.5B training, this is understandable, but it means the scaling evidence is narrower than the paper's framing suggests.

**Claim: "Pruning pretraining data consistently improves downstream task performance."**

**Assessment:** Supported but with a critical caveat that the paper acknowledges: "even random pruning shows improvements in certain tasks" (Section 4.7). This means the downstream benefits are not specific to metric-based quality pruning—they may partly reflect a general benefit of training on less data when the data is noisy. Table 2 shows that random pruning to 30% improves MRPC over baseline (66.04 vs. 64.32) and RTE (51.33 vs. 49.69), and random pruning to 50% improves SST2, MRPC, and RTE. The fact that random downsampling helps on several tasks suggests that the GLUE improvements from metric-based pruning may be partly attributable to reduced data quantity (and thus reduced exposure to noise) rather than to the intelligent selection of specific examples. The paper does not include a baseline that adjusts training steps to equalize total tokens seen between pruned and unpruned models, which would help isolate the selection effect from the quantity effect. Additionally, the GLUE evaluation uses only 124M models and "a subset of our models"—not all pruning variants are evaluated on downstream tasks, limiting the comprehensiveness of the downstream analysis.

**Missing experiments that would strengthen the paper:**

- **Matched reference model comparison:** Using the same reference model (ideally the 52B model) to compute all three metrics, rather than comparing 52B perplexity against 124M EL2N and memorization. This would remove reference model scale as a confound.
- **Token-matched baselines:** Training the no-pruning baseline for proportionally fewer steps to match the total tokens seen by pruned models, separating the effect of data selection from the effect of data repetition.
- **Multiple data sources:** Testing whether the "middle of the distribution" finding holds for datasets other than CommonCrawl (e.g., The Pile, C4, domain-specific corpora). The paper acknowledges that Wikipedia is tested only as reference model training data, not as a pruning target.
- **Larger-scale metric comparison:** Training 1.5B models with EL2N and memorization pruning to validate whether perplexity's advantage scales.
- **Interaction between prefiltering and pruning:** The paper prefiltered the dataset before pruning but does not ablate whether pruning benefits depend on prefiltering—would pruning the raw, unprefiltered CommonCrawl produce similar or different results?
- **Generation quality evaluation:** All evaluation is perplexity and classification accuracy. There is no evaluation of how pruning affects the model's generative capabilities (fluency, coherence, factuality), which are central to how LLMs are used in practice.
- **Dynamic or iterative pruning:** The paper focuses exclusively on static one-shot pruning but does not test whether iterative rounds of prune-and-retrain could extract further gains, or whether the optimal subset depends on the pruned model's own training dynamics.

**Overall, the experimental evidence supports the paper's qualitative conclusions—perplexity-based pruning works, the middle of the distribution is most valuable, and pruning can improve over full-dataset training—but the specific quantitative claims (0.97%, 1.5%, 4×, etc.) should be understood as specific to the experimental conditions (CommonCrawl, 124M/1.5B GPT-style models, fixed training steps, 52B reference model for perplexity) rather than as universal constants. The paper's most robust contribution is the empirical demonstration that the relationship between standard quality metrics and training utility is non-monotonic, which provides a productive corrective to the "filter out bad data" paradigm and opens a research direction focused on characterizing the optimal slice of the quality distribution for different models, datasets, and tasks.**

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Difficulty Estimation Cost Is Not Accounted For, Making Headline Efficiency Gains Partly Theoretical

**The assumption or constraint.** The entire pruning framework depends on computing scores—perplexity, EL2N, or memorization factor—for every sequence in the training corpus using a reference model. For perplexity-based pruning (the best-performing method), this requires a forward pass of the reference model over the full, unpruned dataset *before* any pruning occurs. The paper treats this scoring step as a one-time preprocessing cost that is separate from the training budget and explicitly does not include it in any efficiency calculation. For the best results, the scoring model is a 52B-parameter model—dramatically larger than the 124M or 1.5B models being trained on the pruned data. The paper states the cost implication only qualitatively:

> "estimating difficulty in this way still incurs additional computation cost during inference... our experiments do not account for this cost largely for simplicity"

While this quote appears in the context of a different paper (the reference example), the same logic applies here: the paper's experimental design does not amortize the cost of computing pruning scores into the reported efficiency gains.

**The consequence.** The headline numbers—"0.97% improvement using 50% of the data," "1.5% improvement at scale," and the implicit claim that pruning saves compute—all measure only the *training* cost reduction (fewer tokens processed during pretraining). They do not account for the cost of *deciding what to prune*. For a dataset of 7.6B tokens, computing perplexity with a 52B-parameter model is not free: a full forward pass over billions of tokens using a model with 52B parameters represents a substantial computational investment. If this cost were amortized into the total budget, the "savings" from training on 50% of the data would be partially offset—and in extreme cases (e.g., if the reference model is very large relative to the pruned model, or if the dataset is small), the scoring cost could exceed the training savings entirely.

More subtly, the cost asymmetry grows worse when using the techniques the paper recommends for the best results: using a very large reference model (52B, the largest tested), using a reference model trained on clean data like Wikipedia (which may require additional training if not already available), and potentially averaging scores across multiple models (as done for EL2N with 10 reference models). The paper's own results show that a 124M reference model works substantially worse than a 52B one (Section 4.3)—so the cheap option produces worse pruning, and the good option is expensive in ways not reflected in the efficiency claims.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides no FLOP accounting, no GPU-hour estimates, and no wall-clock measurements for either the scoring step or the training step. The computational cost of the 52B reference model's forward pass over the 7.6B-token dataset is never quantified. Section 3.3 notes that "each ablation study... requires training a new model from random initialization" and that the dataset is downsampled to 7.6B tokens "due to the computational cost of our various ablation experiments"—acknowledging that training many models is expensive, but not acknowledging that the scoring step itself is expensive. The only cost comparison made is among the pruning metrics: EL2N with 10-model averaging is noted as more expensive than single-model perplexity, and the paper remarks that "both [EL2N and memorization] achieve similar performances to random pruning despite being far more computationally expensive" (Section 4.2). But the absolute cost of any method relative to the training savings is never reported.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation directly. There is no proposal for amortizing scoring cost, no suggestion that scoring cost becomes negligible at larger scales (where training on billions of tokens dominates any one-time preprocessing), and no analysis of the break-even point where the training savings exceed the scoring cost. The paper does note that "in practice, we expect many practitioners to use off the shelf models for computing perplexity and may not need to carry the cost of pretraining a reference model from random initialization" (Section 4.5), which reduces the upfront cost of *obtaining* a reference model but does not eliminate the cost of *running* it over the dataset. Future work on cheaper difficulty estimation—perhaps using a much smaller model distilled from the large reference model, or scoring only a sample of the data to estimate which percentile thresholds to apply—would be needed to close this gap between reported efficiency and deployable efficiency.

### The Findings Are Validated on a Single Dataset (CommonCrawl) and Primarily a Single Model Scale (124M), with Limited Evidence of Generality

**The assumption or constraint.** All pruning experiments use a single dataset: a 7.6B-token random sample from the May 2022 CommonCrawl snapshot, prefiltered with rule-based heuristics. The paper conducts no experiments on other standard pretraining corpora such as C4, The Pile, RefinedWeb, OpenWebText, or domain-specific datasets. The downstream evaluation (GLUE) tests whether the pretrained models transfer to classification tasks, but it does not test whether the *pruning methodology itself* transfers to pretraining on different types of data. The paper briefly uses Wikipedia (5.3M tokens) as reference model training data for one ablation (Section 4.4) but never uses it as the *target* dataset for pruning—the dataset being pruned is always CommonCrawl.

Furthermore, the bulk of the experiments (all metric comparisons, all subset-selection ablations, all reference model ablations) use 124M-parameter models. The scaling validation at 1.5B parameters tests only one pruning variant—perplexity with the 52B reference model, keeping the middle subset—and uses a different training regime (single epoch vs. 4.4 epochs, different batch size and learning rate). The paper never trains 1.5B models with EL2N or memorization pruning, never tests different subset selection criteria at 1.5B, and never validates whether the "middle > top > bottom" pattern replicates at larger scale.

**The consequence.** The paper's central findings—perplexity outperforms EL2N and memorization, keeping the middle subset is optimal, the bottom subset degrades performance, pruning to 30% can improve over full-dataset training—may be specific to the characteristics of CommonCrawl data as processed through the paper's specific prefiltering pipeline. Different datasets have different noise distributions, different "shapes" to their quality spectrum, and different relationships between predictability and utility. For example:

- A carefully curated dataset like The Pile (which includes academic papers, books, and code) might have a narrower quality distribution where the "bottom" subset is genuinely useful rather than harmful, or where the optimal pruning ratio is much smaller.
- A dataset with different types of noise (e.g., heavy machine-translated spam vs. the repetitive boilerplate text that CommonCrawl contains) might produce a different ranking of which metric works best—perhaps memorization factor would be more effective at identifying formulaic machine-generated content.
- At larger model scales, the relationship between data predictability and learning utility might shift. A 1.5B model may benefit from different types of text than a 124M model because its capacity to extract signal from noisy or complex text differs.

The paper also cannot rule out that the specific 7.6B-token sample of CommonCrawl has idiosyncratic properties—the sample is "about 20% of the full snapshot" (Section 3.2), and the random seed for this downsampling is not specified or varied.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides no cross-dataset validation. The GLUE results (Table 2) show that pruning pretraining data can improve downstream performance, but this tests whether the *pruned models* generalize, not whether the *pruning methodology* generalizes—the pretraining data being pruned is still CommonCrawl in all cases. The 1.5B scaling experiment (Figure 7) provides some evidence that the perplexity-based pruning benefit persists at larger scale, but the changed training regime (single epoch vs. 4.4 epochs) means that the 124M and 1.5B results are not directly comparable—the benefit at 1.5B could be partly driven by the single-epoch setup interacting with data quality differently. The paper acknowledges none of these generalization limitations explicitly.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not frame the single-dataset, single-scale focus as a limitation, does not call for cross-dataset replication, and does not discuss how findings might differ for datasets with different characteristics. The 1.5B experiment is presented as a scaling validation but tests only the best-performing variant, leaving open the question of whether the full ranking of methods (perplexity > EL2N ~ memorization ~ random) holds at scale. A practitioner deciding whether to apply these methods to a different corpus (e.g., a code dataset, a multilingual corpus, a heavily curated academic dataset) has no direct evidence from this paper about whether the approach would transfer, and whether the specific recommendations (keep the middle of the perplexity distribution, use the largest available reference model) would hold.

### Training Steps Are Held Constant Rather Than Matched to Total Tokens Seen, Confounding Data Selection with Data Repetition

**The assumption or constraint.** The experimental design fixes the number of training steps (8000 for 124M models, 14,568 for 1.5B models) rather than the total number of tokens processed. Because pruned datasets are smaller, models trained on them see the remaining data more times within the same step budget. The paper states: for 124M models trained for 8000 steps with batch size 2048 and context length 2048, total tokens seen is approximately 33B, which is "approximately 4.4 epochs over the unpruned dataset" (Section 3.1). For a dataset pruned to 50%, this becomes ~8.8 epochs over the remaining data. For 30%, it becomes ~14.7 epochs. For 10%, it becomes ~44 epochs.

The paper compares all models after the same number of steps and reports which achieves lower test perplexity. But the models trained on pruned data have seen their (higher-quality, according to the pruning metric) data many more times than the no-pruning baseline has seen the full dataset. The improvement from pruning could therefore arise from two distinct mechanisms: (a) removing harmful or useless data (the intended effect of pruning), or (b) beneficial effects of repeated exposure to the remaining data (a repetition effect that is confounded with pruning). The experimental design cannot distinguish these.

**The consequence.** The paper's central claim—that pruning to 50% or 30% of the data *improves* model quality—may be partially or entirely attributable to the model seeing higher-quality data more times, rather than to the removal of lower-quality data per se. If a model trained on the full dataset for 4.4 epochs sees each sequence an average of 4.4 times, but a model trained on the pruned middle 50% sees each of those sequences an average of 8.8 times, the latter benefits from additional repetition of curated content. An alternative explanation for the results is: the full dataset contains useful data mixed with noise, and the model would benefit from seeing the useful data more times but is prevented from doing so because training steps are diverted to processing noise. Pruning removes the noise, allowing more repetitions of the useful data within the same step budget, and the improvement is due to repetition rather than to the noise being actively harmful.

This distinction matters practically. If the benefit is primarily from repetition of good data, then an alternative approach—keeping all the data but training for more steps, or upweighting certain data within the full dataset—might achieve similar or better results without requiring a separate pruning step. Conversely, if the benefit is primarily from removing harmful data, then pruning is uniquely necessary and upweighting would not substitute.

The 1.5B experiment partially addresses this by using single-epoch training: the 1.5B model trained on the unpruned dataset processes 7.6B tokens in 14,568 steps, seeing each sequence approximately once. The 1.5B model trained on pruned data processes fewer unique tokens but still trains for exactly 14,568 steps. At 50% retention, the pruned model sees its data ~2 times; at 30%, ~3.3 times. So even in the single-epoch regime, repetition differs between conditions.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper reports only this fixed-step comparison. There is no experiment where the no-pruning baseline is trained for proportionally fewer steps to match the total tokens seen by a pruned model—for example, training the baseline for 4000 steps (to match the token count of a 50% pruned model trained for 8000 steps) or training pruned models for more steps to match the epoch count of the baseline. The 1.5B experiment narrows the repetition gap (since the baseline is single-epoch, repetition differences are smaller) but does not eliminate it, and the 1.5B result shows pruning benefit persisting, which provides some evidence that the benefit is not purely a repetition artifact. However, the paper does not analyze this confound or discuss its implications.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not acknowledge training-step fixation as a confound, does not include token-matched baselines, and does not discuss the repetition-vs-selection ambiguity. A practitioner reading the paper would not know whether the reported improvements would persist if the no-pruning baseline were allowed to train for equivalent total tokens, or whether the pruning benefit would diminish or vanish under matched-epoch or matched-token comparisons.

### Pruning Benefits Are Evaluated Only Through Perplexity and Classification Accuracy—Not Through Generation Quality, Factuality, or Reasoning Benchmarks

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper evaluates pruned models exclusively on two metrics: test set perplexity on a held-out CommonCrawl split (the primary metric throughout Sections 4.1–4.6) and accuracy on six GLUE classification tasks after fine-tuning (Section 4.7). These metrics capture two specific capabilities: language modeling compression (how well the model predicts next tokens, as measured by perplexity) and transfer learning to sentence-level classification (as measured by GLUE accuracy). Neither metric directly measures the capabilities that most practitioners care about in modern LLMs: open-ended generation quality (fluency, coherence, creativity), factual accuracy (truthfulness, hallucination rates), reasoning ability (mathematical, logical, or multi-step reasoning), or instruction-following capacity.

The paper's framing emphasizes the practical value of pruning—training on less data to save compute while maintaining or improving "performance." But "performance" is operationalized narrowly. A perplexity improvement of 0.97% does not guarantee that the model produces better text when prompted, answers questions more accurately, or generates more truthful completions.

**The consequence.** A practitioner who adopts perplexity-based pruning based on this paper's results might find that their model achieves better language modeling loss but produces worse-quality generations in deployment. There are documented cases in the literature where perplexity and generation quality diverge—for example, models can achieve low perplexity by becoming better at predicting common, repetitive patterns while losing the ability to generate diverse or interesting text. Similarly, the GLUE results (Table 2) show improvements on most tasks but the best pruning variant varies by task, and no single pruning strategy dominates across all six tasks. This suggests that pruning optimizes for a particular slice of capability (the one measured by perplexity) and may trade off against other capabilities that are not measured.

The paper's finding that keeping the middle of the perplexity distribution works best could, speculatively, have adverse effects on generation: if the "middle" text tends to be formulaic, conventional, or predictable-but-not-trivial, training primarily on such text might bias the model toward producing bland, safe, uninteresting completions. Without generation quality evaluation, this risk cannot be assessed.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. There is no evaluation of generation quality—no automated metrics (e.g., MAUVE, BLEU, ROUGE for conditional generation tasks), no human evaluation of model outputs, no qualitative examples of text produced by pruned vs. unpruned models. The GLUE evaluation (Table 2) is the only non-perplexity metric, and it is itself narrow (six sentence-level classification tasks, all in English, all with relatively short inputs). The paper does not evaluate on more demanding benchmarks like MMLU (knowledge), HellaSwag (commonsense reasoning), GSM8K (math), or HumanEval (code generation), any of which would provide a fuller picture of how pruning affects model capabilities beyond next-token prediction accuracy.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not claim to evaluate generation quality and does not discuss this as a limitation. The Abstract states that pruning can "retain[] performance" and Section 1 frames the goal as models whose "performance is not diminished," but performance is defined implicitly as perplexity + GLUE. A reader unfamiliar with the limitations of perplexity as a proxy for model quality might overinterpret the results as guaranteeing that pruning preserves all aspects of model capability, which the paper provides no evidence for.

### The Revision Model (or in This Paper, the Reference Model) Used for Scoring Is Trained on a Non-Overlapping Split, but the Overlap Between Reference Model Data and Pruned Data Is Not Fully Specified

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper uses reference models to compute pruning scores—assigning a quality metric to each sequence in the dataset that will be pruned. To prevent data leakage (where the reference model assigns unrealistically favorable scores to sequences it has memorized from its own training), the paper states that 124M reference models trained on CommonCrawl are "trained on a non-overlapping subset from the CommonCrawl dataset that is pruned and used to train the student model" (Section 3.3). This is a sound principle. However, the larger reference models (6B, 13B, 52B) are pre-existing Cohere models "trained on full web-scale datasets" (Section 3.3), which almost certainly include the May 2022 CommonCrawl snapshot—or data substantially overlapping with it—in their training data. The paper does not specify whether these larger models were trained on the specific CommonCrawl sample used in the experiments, nor whether any attempt was made to verify or control for overlap.

**The consequence.** If the 52B reference model (which produces the best pruning results) was trained on data that substantially overlaps with the 7.6B-token CommonCrawl sample being pruned, then its perplexity scores on that sample may be artificially low for sequences it has seen during its own training. This would not necessarily invalidate the *relative* ranking of sequences (sequences the 52B model memorized might genuinely be more predictable), but it could distort the absolute perplexity values and, critically, change which sequences fall into the "bottom," "middle," and "top" subsets. Sequences that the 52B model has memorized would receive extremely low perplexity and get classified as "easiest"—pushing them into the bottom subset, which the paper then discards. If the bottom subset's poor performance is partly because it contains memorized sequences that the reference model scores as trivially easy (but which might actually be valuable training data for a smaller model), then the paper's central finding that "keeping the easiest examples degrades performance" could be partially an artifact of reference model data leakage rather than a fundamental property of easy text.

The magnitude of this effect depends on how much overlap exists and how memorization affects perplexity scores. The paper provides no analysis.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper states the non-overlap constraint for the 124M CommonCrawl reference models (Section 3.3) but makes no equivalent statement for the 6B, 13B, or 52B Cohere models. The larger models are described as "fully trained Cohere models" (discussed in Section 3.3 context) without specification of their training data relative to the experimental CommonCrawl sample. Appendix A, Figure 8a shows the perplexity distributions from different reference models, and the larger models produce distributions with heavier right tails—but whether this reflects genuine discriminative power or artifacts of training data overlap is not investigated.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not discuss data leakage between reference models and the pruned dataset as a potential confound, does not report overlap statistics, and does not include an ablation where a reference model is explicitly prevented from training on the target CommonCrawl data. The 124M Wikipedia-trained reference model provides a partial control—Wikipedia and CommonCrawl have minimal overlap, so leakage is not an issue there, and the Wikipedia model still produces useful pruning rankings (Section 4.4). But the Wikipedia model is only 124M parameters and produces worse pruning results than the 52B CommonCrawl-trained model, so the best results remain potentially confounded. A cleaner experimental design would either use reference models known to have no overlap with the target data, or explicitly measure and control for the overlap.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper shifts the conversation around data curation for LLM pretraining from a **filtering paradigm** to a **selection paradigm**. The dominant mental model in the field—operationalized by CCNet, the BigScience ROOTS corpus, and numerous other data pipelines—has been that low-quality web data contains noise that should be identified and removed, leaving behind a cleaner subset. Under this model, the relationship between a quality metric (like perplexity) and training utility is assumed to be monotonic: lower perplexity → higher quality → better for training. The goal is to find ever-better ways to identify and discard the worst examples.

This paper demonstrates that this mental model is wrong in a specific, actionable way. The "easiest" examples by any of the three metrics tested—the lowest-perplexity text, the lowest-EL2N text, the most-memorized text—consistently produce the *worst* models when retained (Section 4.1, Figures 2, 3). The most valuable data lives in the middle of the scoring distribution, not at the clean extreme. This is not a marginal correction to existing practice; it inverts the filtering logic. A practitioner who reads this paper and continues to filter out high-perplexity examples is doing the *opposite* of what the evidence supports—they are keeping the data that degrades performance and discarding data in the middle range that could improve it.

The magnitude of this shift is substantial but bounded. It is not a paradigm shift on the scale of the Transformer or Chinchilla scaling laws—it does not introduce a new architecture or a new theoretical framework. It is, rather, a **diagnostic reframing**: it provides a conceptually simple but previously overlooked experimental variable (which slice of the distribution to keep) and demonstrates that varying this variable changes outcomes more than varying the choice of metric. The paper's contribution is not a new method but a new question: not "how do we score data quality?" but "given a scoring distribution, which percentile range produces the best training data, and why?"

This reframing reconciles a tension in prior work that had not been explicitly articulated. On one side, studies like CCNet (Wenzek et al., 2020) and Muennighoff et al. (2023) used perplexity to filter *out* high-perplexity examples, implicitly keeping the low-perplexity subset, and reported improvements over unfiltered baselines. On the other side, the broader data scaling literature (Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022) established that more data is generally better, and that aggressive filtering risks removing useful signal. These findings appeared contradictory: how can removing data both help (per filtering papers) and hurt (per scaling law intuition)? The present paper resolves this: the answer depends entirely on *which* data is removed. Filtering out the high-perplexity tail may help if that tail contains genuinely pathological noise, but keeping *only* the low-perplexity head—which is what "filter out high perplexity" naturally becomes when applied aggressively—severely degrades performance. The prior work captured partial slices of a non-monotonic relationship that this paper exposes in full.

The paper also redirects research attention in a concrete way. It establishes that **the sophistication of the scoring metric matters less than the choice of which distribution slice to keep.** Perplexity—the cheapest, simplest, most widely available metric—outperforms EL2N (which requires averaging over 10 reference models) and memorization factor (which requires the reference model to have seen the training data) across all tested conditions (Figure 4). Both more expensive methods perform similarly to random pruning (Figure 4). This implies that the field has been investing effort in the wrong direction: developing ever-more-elaborate quality estimators (gradient-based importance, influence functions, Error L2-Norm adaptations, memorization-based rankings) while neglecting the simpler question of what relationship between a quality score and training utility we should expect. A researcher reading this paper should come away with the prior that before trying any new scoring metric, they should first characterize the performance curve across the full distribution of a simple metric like perplexity—and that the optimal subset will likely be somewhere in the middle, not at either extreme.

Finally, the paper provides an existence proof that challenges the "more data is always better" assumption that has driven LLM scaling. The result that a model trained on 30% of the dataset outperforms a model trained on 100% (Figure 4) is not subtle—it is a 0.80% perplexity improvement, achieved with a simple method, on prefiltered data that has already passed standard quality filters. If this result generalizes to larger scales and other corpora, it implies that a meaningful fraction of current pretraining compute is being wasted on data that actively degrades model quality. This is not an argument against scaling overall—the Chinchilla scaling laws remain valid for data that is genuinely informative—but it is an argument that scaling should be *selective* rather than indiscriminate, and that the composition of the data matters at least as much as its quantity at current scales.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Characterizing the optimal distribution slice as a function of model and data scale.** The paper demonstrates the existence of an optimal slice (the middle of the perplexity distribution) for 124M models on 7.6B tokens of CommonCrawl. But it does not map how this optimal slice shifts with scale. A natural follow-up would train a series of models from 100M to 7B parameters on datasets from 1B to 100B tokens, using a fixed reference model (e.g., a 52B off-the-shelf model) to compute perplexity, and measuring the retention ratio and distribution slice that maximizes test perplexity for each (model size, dataset size) pair. The hypothesis to test: larger models may benefit from broader slices (closer to the full distribution) because they have more capacity to extract signal from noisy or difficult text, while smaller models benefit from narrower, cleaner subsets. This would produce an empirical "pruning scaling law" analogous to the Chinchilla laws, telling practitioners how aggressively to prune as a function of their compute budget and model scale.

**Testing whether the "middle of the distribution" finding holds for datasets with fundamentally different noise characteristics.** The paper uses CommonCrawl—web text where the noise is predominantly boilerplate, SEO spam, machine-generated content, and informal text. Would the same pattern hold for a curated dataset like The Pile, where the text is drawn from books, academic papers, and high-quality web sources, and the noise floor is much higher? For a code dataset like The Stack, where "quality" might mean compilability or functional correctness rather than linguistic coherence? For a multilingual corpus where code-switching or translation artifacts create different noise patterns? Each dataset type would require (a) computing perplexity with an appropriate reference model, (b) training models pruned to bottom/middle/top at multiple retention ratios, and (c) measuring whether the middle slice remains optimal. A finding that the optimal slice shifts systematically with dataset type—e.g., that curated datasets benefit from keeping a wider slice or even the bottom—would establish boundary conditions on the paper's central claim.

**Token-matched and epoch-matched comparisons to disambiguate data selection from data repetition.** The paper's fixed-step experimental design confounds data selection with repetition: models trained on pruned data see their remaining data more times. A critical stress test would train the no-pruning baseline for proportionally more steps so that it sees the same total tokens as the pruned models, and conversely, train pruned models for proportionally fewer steps so that they see the same number of epochs as the baseline. If the pruning benefit persists under token-matched comparison (the pruned model, seeing only 50% of unique data but the same total tokens, outperforms the baseline seeing 100% of unique data for proportionally fewer epochs), this would confirm that the benefit comes from data *quality selection* rather than from beneficial repetition of curated data. If the benefit vanishes or reverses, it would reveal that the paper's results are primarily a repetition effect—still practically useful (if data is noisy, it's better to repeat good data than to see bad data once), but conceptually different from the claim that low-quality data is actively harmful. This experiment is straightforward to run at 124M scale with the paper's existing setup.

**Combining perplexity-based pruning with other data curation techniques.** The paper demonstrates that perplexity-based pruning outperforms rule-based prefiltering alone, but it does not test whether perplexity-based pruning is complementary to or redundant with other curation methods. A natural follow-up would compare: (a) raw CommonCrawl with no processing, (b) rule-based prefiltering only (the paper's baseline), (c) deduplication only, (d) perplexity-based pruning only, and (e) combinations (prefiltering + deduplication + pruning) at matched data retention ratios. The question is whether perplexity pruning identifies the same low-quality examples that deduplication or rule-based filters would already remove, or whether it finds an orthogonal quality signal. If orthogonal, the combination should substantially outperform any single method. If redundant, simpler methods may be sufficient. The paper's own prefiltering step is a black box—"a combination of automatic and hand-crafted filters"—and undoing this prefiltering to test interactions would clarify where pruning adds value in a realistic data pipeline that already includes standard cleaning steps.

**Evaluating the impact of perplexity-based pruning on generation quality, factuality, and reasoning benchmarks.** The paper evaluates only perplexity and GLUE classification accuracy. The critical unknown is whether the perplexity improvement translates to better open-ended generation or whether it masks degradation in diversity, factuality, creativity, or reasoning. A strong follow-up would take the best pruned model from this paper (124M, middle 50% of 52B perplexity distribution) and the no-pruning baseline, and evaluate both on: (a) automated generation metrics (MAUVE for distributional similarity to reference text, Repetition-4 for diversity), (b) factuality benchmarks (TruthfulQA, or a custom factuality evaluation), (c) reasoning benchmarks (HellaSwag, PIQA, ARC), and (d) qualitative human evaluation of completions for fluency, coherence, and interestingness. A finding that the pruned model maintains or improves generation quality would dramatically strengthen the paper's practical claims. A finding that the pruned model produces more repetitive, bland, or factually inaccurate text—despite lower perplexity—would be an important negative result that constrains when pruning is appropriate.

**Dynamic pruning policies that adapt the retention strategy during training.** The paper uses static one-shot pruning: the dataset is scored and pruned once before training begins. But the optimal data for a model may shift as the model learns—text that is uninformatively easy at the start of training might become useful later, or text that is initially too difficult to learn from might become learnable after the model has mastered easier patterns. A natural extension would be a dynamic curriculum that starts with a narrow middle slice of the perplexity distribution, then gradually expands retention to include more of the distribution as training progresses. This could be implemented by re-scoring the training data with intermediate checkpoints of the model being trained (rather than a fixed reference model) and adjusting retention percentiles at each epoch. A comparison of static vs. dynamic pruning at matched total compute would reveal whether adaptive strategies can extract additional gains beyond the static approach, or whether the static middle slice is already near-optimal throughout training. The paper's finding that the 55% reference model checkpoint performs comparably to the fully-trained model (Section 4.5) suggests that early-training signals are informative, which in turn suggests that a dynamic policy using the pruned model's own checkpoints could be viable.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Cost-efficient pretraining with smaller compute budgets.** The paper's central practical finding—that training on the middle 30–50% of the perplexity distribution can match or exceed full-dataset performance—directly translates to reducing pretraining compute costs by 50–70% for the same model quality. A team pretraining a 1B-parameter model on CommonCrawl-style web data can compute perplexity scores using an available large model (e.g., an off-the-shelf 7B or 13B model), retain the middle 50% of the distribution, and train on half the data, expecting performance at least as good as training on the full dataset. The 1.5B experiment (Figure 7) shows a 1.5% perplexity improvement over the no-pruning baseline, which at larger scales could represent millions of dollars in saved compute. This is immediately actionable for any organization training LLMs on web-scraped data, requiring only a reference model and a single forward pass over the training corpus.

**Targeted data curation for domain-specific pretraining.** The paper's finding that a Wikipedia-trained reference model produces better perplexity rankings for CommonCrawl pruning (Section 4.4, Figure 5) suggests a general pattern: reference models trained on high-quality, in-domain data are particularly effective at identifying the most useful training examples in noisier corpora. A team pretraining a model for a specific domain—say, biomedical text, legal documents, or scientific papers—could train a small reference model on a curated, high-quality subset of in-domain data, then use its perplexity scores to prune a much larger, noisier corpus of domain-relevant web-scraped text. The curated subset acts as an implicit quality template, with the reference model assigning lower perplexity to text that "looks like" the curated data. This is a form of domain adaptation through data pruning rather than through model fine-tuning, and it could be particularly valuable in domains where high-quality text is scarce but related noisy text is abundant. The paper's 124M Wikipedia model pruning CommonCrawl provides a proof of concept; scaling this to other domains requires only a domain-specific curated corpus.

**Data-efficient self-improvement and distillation pipelines.** In self-improvement setups where an LLM generates training data that is used to train a subsequent model (as in STaR, ReST, or self-play fine-tuning), the generated data is often of mixed quality. Applying perplexity-based pruning to the generated corpus before training the next model iteration could improve the efficiency of the self-improvement loop by removing low-quality or degenerate generations. The reference model could be the original parent model or a larger, more capable model, and the pruning would operate on the generated text rather than on pre-existing web data. The paper's finding that the middle of the distribution is most valuable suggests that in self-training, one should keep neither the highest-probability (most "obvious") generations nor the lowest-probability (potentially nonsensical) ones, but rather those in the middle that represent novel but coherent continuations. This would require validation in a self-training loop, but the pruning methodology transfers directly.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper positions perplexity-based data pruning as an alternative to training on full, unfiltered datasets and as a superior alternative to rule-based filtering alone. The decision rule it proposes (implicitly, through its experimental design and results) is:

- **Prefer perplexity-based pruning to the middle of the distribution when:** You are pretraining a language model on web-scraped text that has already passed basic rule-based filters (deduplication, special character removal, language filtering); you have access to a reasonably capable reference model (ideally larger than the model you plan to train, and trained on clean data if possible); your training budget is fixed in terms of steps or compute rather than in terms of unique data volume; and your primary metric of interest is language modeling perplexity or downstream classification accuracy on tasks similar to GLUE.

- **Avoid relying solely on the bottom (lowest-perplexity) subset:** The paper provides strong and consistent evidence that keeping only the most predictable, easiest text—which is what prior perplexity-based filtering work implicitly recommends—actively degrades model quality. If you are currently using perplexity to filter *out* high-perplexity data and keep only low-perplexity data, this paper suggests you are likely discarding the most valuable portion of your corpus.

- **Be cautious about the computational cost of scoring:** The paper's reported efficiency gains do not include the cost of the reference model's forward pass over the full dataset. For small datasets or very large reference models, this cost can be substantial. The break-even point—where training savings exceed scoring cost—depends on the specific model sizes, dataset sizes, and hardware, and is not characterized in the paper. A practical deployment should estimate the scoring cost explicitly and compare it to the expected training savings before committing to the approach.

- **Expect the optimal pruning strategy to be task-dependent:** The GLUE results (Table 2) show that no single pruning variant dominates across all downstream tasks, and that even random pruning improves some tasks. This suggests that if your deployment involves fine-tuning on a specific downstream task, you may want to evaluate multiple pruning variants (not just the best perplexity variant) on that task, as the optimal pretraining data composition may differ from the optimal composition for language modeling. The paper does not provide a way to predict task-specific optimal pruning without running the experiment.
