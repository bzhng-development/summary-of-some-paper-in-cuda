Here are the takeaways from this **Ryan Peterman** newsletter on why you should interview even when you're happy:

**The IC8 Who Interviews Annually**

Ryan opens with a striking anecdote: an engineer with one of the fastest-growing careers he's ever encountered—**IC3 to IC8 in 5 years at Meta**—shared a counterintuitive practice. Despite having worked at Meta for over a decade, this engineer interviews at other companies once a year. "Interviewing and choosing to stay gave him confidence that he was always in the best spot." Ryan later heard similar advice from a Staff (IC6) mentor of his, cementing the insight as a recurring theme among high-performers. The practice isn't about jumping ship; it's about staying sharp and maintaining clarity about your own position.

**Abundance & Opportunity (The Core Logic)**

The core logic revolves around **abundance and leverage**. "The worst time to look for a new job is when you need one." Needing to succeed breeds anxiety, which harms interview performance—desperation is perceptible and makes you less compelling. But the downstream effect is equally important: even if you get an offer, negotiations will be weaker. If your best alternative (your current job) is bad, you have no credible fallback and less leverage to push for better terms. Looking when you aren't desperate makes you simultaneously more likely to land the offer *and* negotiate better terms. Yet the deepest reason is strategic: "Access to the right opportunity becomes the limiting factor for career growth eventually." Regularly scanning the landscape is simply the most effective way to surface those opportunities *before* you're forced to hunt—it's a passive radar system for your career.

**The Case Against (and Why It's Wrong)**

Ryan tackles the obvious objection head-on: most engineers don't interview when they're happy because it feels like a waste of time—"a contrived process that doesn't make you a better engineer." He flips this by arguing that interviewing *from a position of happiness* is actually the most time-efficient approach, for two reinforcing reasons. First, **you apply for fewer roles**. Since you genuinely like your current job, your bar for what constitutes an improvement is high, and the list of potential roles shrinks dramatically—you're not spraying applications, you're selectively exploring. Second, **you don't need to overprepare**. Because there's no desperation attached to the outcome, "you can just spend the 20% of the time that gets you 80% of the benefit"—a light touch on preparation that avoids the grinding, anxiety-fueled cramming of someone who *must* land the job. The asymmetry is clean: "Worst case, you remain happy in your current role and you're sharper in case you need to interview later. Best case, you find an opportunity that is better than what you have."

---

His **abundance and leverage** framing ties directly to the IC8's annual interviewing ritual: staying sharp isn't just about keeping skills warm, it's about permanently removing desperation from the equation. When your default state is contentment and your exploration is systematic rather than reactive, every interview becomes a low-stakes scan rather than a high-stakes gamble—which paradoxically produces better outcomes on both sides of the decision.
