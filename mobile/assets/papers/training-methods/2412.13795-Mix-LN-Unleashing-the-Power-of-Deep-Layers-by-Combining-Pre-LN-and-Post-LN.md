# Mix-LN: Unleashing the Power of Deep Layers by Combining Pre-LN and Post-LN

**ArXiv:** [2412.13795](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.13795)

## 🎯 Pitch

Deeper layers in LLMs are largely dead weight, contributing almost nothing to performance—and the culprit is Pre-LN normalization, which starves them of gradient. Mix-LN fixes this by applying Post-LN to early layers and Pre-LN to later ones, yielding uniform gradients that cut perplexity by up to 1.65 points and boost downstream task accuracy by over 4% without adding a single parameter.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **Mix-LN**, a hybrid normalization technique that combines Post-Layer Normalization in the earlier layers with Pre-Layer Normalization in the deeper layers to address the training shortfall that causes deeper layers in LLMs to contribute minimally to performance. The authors first diagnose this problem across both open-weight models—LLaMa2-7B as a representative Pre-LN model and BERT-large as a Post-LN model—and in-house trained LLaMA-130M models, demonstrating that Pre-LN, the dominant normalization strategy in modern LLMs, produces diminishing gradient norms in deeper layers (leading to near-zero performance drop when those layers are pruned), while Post-LN preserves deeper gradients but suffers from vanishing gradients in earlier layers. By applying Post-LN to the first fraction of layers and Pre-LN to the remainder—operationalized as a hyperparameter α controlling the Post-LN ratio—Mix-LN achieves more uniform gradient norms across the network and consistently outperforms both Pre-LN and Post-LN across model sizes from 70M to 7B parameters, reducing perplexity by up to 1.65 points over Pre-LN in pre-training, yielding average gains of 4.26% across downstream tasks during supervised fine-tuning, and achieving a higher reward (1.32 vs. 0.75) in RLHF, establishing that deeper layers can be trained effectively when gradient flow is balanced across the network but only when the normalization strategy is adapted to the depth-dependent gradient dynamics.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Deep Layers in LLMs Are Wasted Capacity

The fundamental problem this paper tackles is both simple and economically significant: **the deeper layers of modern Large Language Models contribute shockingly little to performance, and this represents vast wasted computational investment**. Recent empirical work has documented a pattern that should alarm anyone paying for LLM training: layers beyond the middle of the network can be pruned substantially (Yin et al., 2023), removed entirely (Gromov et al., 2024; Men et al., 2024), or even swapped with one another (Lad et al., 2024) without notably affecting the model's overall capabilities. Li et al. (2024) extended this finding to fine-tuning, showing that deeper layers contribute minimally during supervised fine-tuning as well.

The paper frames this not as an interesting curiosity or a convenient compression opportunity—which is how much of the community has treated it—but as **a training pathology that wastes enormous resources**. As the authors point out in Section 1:

> "The training of LLMs is extraordinarily resource-intensive, often requiring thousands of GPUs or TPUs and several months of computation on vast datasets. For example, the training of GPT-3 reportedly incurred millions of dollars in computational costs. The underutilization of deeper layers leads to inefficiencies, squandering resources that could otherwise be leveraged to enhance model performance."

This perspective shift is important. The prevailing reaction to the "unreasonable ineffectiveness of the deeper layers" (to borrow Gromov et al.'s phrase) has been to exploit it: if deep layers don't do much, we can prune them to reduce inference cost. But the authors argue this misses the real opportunity. If we could fix the underlying cause and make those layers genuinely useful, we would get the parameter efficiency we paid for—better performance without increasing model size. The paper frames this as a training shortfall rather than an architectural inevitability.

### Why This Matters: The Economics of Deep Layers

To understand the stakes, consider what deep layers represent in a modern LLM. In a 7B-parameter model with 32 layers, each layer accounts for roughly 3% of the total parameters. If the last 8–10 layers are contributing negligibly—as the pruning literature suggests—then 25–30% of the training compute budget is being spent on parameters that could be removed without consequence. At the scale of frontier model training (hundreds of millions of dollars), this is an enormous inefficiency.

But the paper's motivation goes deeper than cost. The authors articulate a normative principle about what good training should achieve:

> "Ideally, all layers in a model should be well-trained, with sufficient diversity in features from layer to layer, to maximize the utility of the network's parameters."

This principle—that representation should change substantially from layer to layer to justify each layer's existence—is grounded in the observation that identical or near-identical representations across consecutive layers mean those layers are essentially computing an identity function (perhaps with minor perturbations), which is a waste of parameters. The angular distance metric the paper uses (borrowed from Gromov et al., 2024) operationalizes this: when the input to layer ℓ and the input to layer ℓ+n are nearly identical vectors, those n layers are doing very little useful transformation.

### The Normalization Landscape: Pre-LN's Dominance and Its Hidden Cost

To understand why the paper targets layer normalization specifically, we need to understand the two dominant paradigms and their history.

**Post-Layer Normalization (Post-LN)** was the original design from the Transformer paper (Vaswani et al., 2017). It places layer normalization *after* the residual addition:

$$\text{Post-LN}(x) = \text{LN}(x + F(x))$$

where $F(x)$ is either the attention or feed-forward sublayer. Post-LN was the natural extension of batch normalization practices from computer vision: normalize the output of each sub-block before passing it to the next.

**Pre-Layer Normalization (Pre-LN)** emerged as a fix when researchers tried to scale Transformers to more layers. Under Post-LN, training deep Transformers proved unstable (Nguyen & Salazar, 2019; Xiong et al., 2020). Pre-LN moves the normalization *before* the residual addition:

$$\text{Pre-LN}(x) = x + F(\text{LN}(x))$$

This seemingly small change has profound consequences. Pre-LN stabilizes training at any depth, which is why it became the de facto standard for essentially all modern LLMs: GPT (Brown, 2020), LLaMA (Touvron et al., 2023), Mistral (Jiang et al., 2023), and the LLaMA 3 herd (Dubey et al., 2024).

**But stability came at an unexamined cost.** The paper's key insight—building on the gradient analysis of Xiong et al. (2020) and Takase et al. (2023)—is that Pre-LN's stability mechanism *necessarily* entails diminishing the gradient signal in deeper layers. The mathematical argument, developed in Section 2.1, goes as follows.

For Post-LN, the derivative of the layer operation with respect to its input is:

$$\frac{\partial \text{Post-LN}(x)}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial \text{LN}(x + F(x))}{\partial (x + F(x))} \left(I + \frac{\partial F(x)}{\partial x}\right)$$

The critical term is the Jacobian of layer normalization, $\frac{\partial \text{LN}(x')}{\partial x'}$, where $x'$ is the LN input. Under the assumption that $x'$ follows a normal distribution with mean zero (which layer normalization enforces for the normalized features), this Jacobian simplifies to approximately:

$$\frac{\partial \text{LN}(x')}{\partial x'} \approx \frac{1}{\sigma_{x'}} I$$

where $\sigma_{x'}$ is the standard deviation of the input to LN. Since $\sigma_{x'}$ grows larger than 1 during training, the factor $1/\sigma_{x'}$ is less than 1. This means **each Post-LN layer multiplies the gradient by a factor smaller than 1**, and over $L$ layers, the gradient in early layers gets attenuated by $\prod_{l=1}^{L} 1/\sigma_{x'_l}$—a vanishing gradient problem. This explains why Post-LN produces large gradients near the output (where few attenuation factors have accumulated) but vanishing gradients near the input.

For Pre-LN, the derivative decouples the residual path from the LN Jacobian:

$$\frac{\partial \text{Pre-LN}(x)}{\partial x} = I + \frac{\partial F(\text{LN}(x))}{\partial \text{LN}(x)} \frac{\partial \text{LN}(x)}{\partial x}$$

The identity term $I$ from the residual connection ensures that gradients can always flow backward through the residual path, preventing vanishing gradients in early layers. **However**, as depth increases, the model's output accumulates the contributions of many residual blocks. This accumulation means that for very deep layers, the identity term dominates the derivative, causing the entire Pre-LN operation to approach an identity mapping—the layer ceases to contribute to learning. As the paper states:

> "Since Pre-LN does not constrain the residual connection, the outputs of successive transformer blocks accumulate as the layer depth grows. Consequently, the derivative of Pre-LN in Equation (4) approaches an identity matrix, indicating that the entire Pre-LN operation ceases to contribute effectively to learning."

This is the gradient-based mechanism behind the empirical observation that deep layers in Pre-LN models are redundant: they're not learning because the gradient signal tells them they don't need to change. The network's final output is already well-approximated by the residual accumulation from earlier layers; the deep layers receive gradients that are effectively zero, so they learn nothing useful.

### Conflicting Evidence and Gaps in Prior Work

The existing literature contained several relevant threads, but none that fully diagnosed and resolved the problem as the paper does.

**Post-LN stabilizers.** Researchers recognized Post-LN's instability and proposed fixes: Admin (Liu et al., 2020) adds additional parameters to control residual dependencies; DeepNorm (Wang et al., 2024) upscales the residual connection before applying LN, reducing update magnitudes; Sandwich-LN (Ding et al., 2021) normalizes both the input and output of each sub-layer; B2T (Takase et al., 2022) bypasses all LN except the final one in each layer with a special residual connection; Scaled Initialization (Nguyen & Salazar, 2019; Scao et al., 2022) initializes weight matrices with smaller variances to stabilize training dynamics. Each of these addresses Post-LN's instability but does not compare the *layer effectiveness profile* against Pre-LN. They make Post-LN trainable, but they don't ask whether Post-LN produces better deep layers. The paper's comparison in Section 5 shows that DeepNorm still diverges at 1B parameter scale (Table 1), and the Admin/Group-LN/Sandwich-LN comparisons in Table 8 show only marginal improvements over Pre-LN—none reaching Mix-LN's performance.

**Pruning and redundancy papers.** The line of work documenting deep-layer redundancy (Yin et al., 2023; Gromov et al., 2024; Men et al., 2024; Siddiqui et al., 2024; Lad et al., 2024) established the phenomenon but did not explain its cause. Gromov et al. used angular distance—the same metric this paper adopts—to show that deep layers' representations are highly similar to their neighbors, but they stopped at observation. They didn't connect it to gradient dynamics or normalization choice. The current paper explicitly builds on Gromov et al.'s measurement methodology (Section 2.2) but provides the causal mechanism those papers lacked.

**Gradient analyses of normalization.** Xiong et al. (2020) provided the foundational gradient analysis for Pre-LN vs. Post-LN, showing that Pre-LN scales down gradients with depth while Post-LN produces larger gradients near the output. However, Xiong et al. focused on training stability—making the case for why Pre-LN avoids the need for careful warm-up scheduling—not on layer effectiveness or model quality. The current paper extends their gradient analysis to draw the connection to deep-layer ineffectiveness, which is a new implication of their mathematics.

**Takase et al. (2022, 2023)** came closest to anticipating this paper's findings. They noted that Post-LN preserves larger gradient norms in deeper layers, potentially leading to more effective training of those layers, and developed B2T to address early-layer gradient vanishing. However, B2T is a specialized architecture (bypassing most LN layers), not a general-purpose normalization strategy. And Takase et al. did not systematically evaluate the layer-by-layer effectiveness of their approach using pruning-based metrics or angular distance. The current paper's contribution is showing that the gradient patterns documented by Xiong et al. and Takase et al. directly cause measurable differences in layer quality (via angular distance and performance drop), and that a simple hybrid architecture can capture the benefits of both without complex modifications.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper positions itself at the intersection of two previously disconnected conversations: the normalization design space (Pre-LN vs. Post-LN and their variants) and the empirical observation of deep-layer ineffectiveness. Its central claim is that **these are causally connected**: the choice of normalization determines the gradient flow, which in turn determines which layers learn effectively, which in turn determines model quality.

The key positioning moves are:

1. **Reframing pruning results as training failures, not efficiency opportunities.** The paper explicitly rejects the framing that deep layers are "naturally" less important. Instead, it argues they are under-trained due to a systematic gradient imbalance caused by Pre-LN. If you fix the gradient flow, deep layers become useful.

2. **Providing a mechanistic explanation, not just empirical correlation.** The paper doesn't just show that Pre-LN models have ineffective deep layers (which prior work established) and Post-LN models have ineffective early layers (which is less well-documented). It provides the gradient-based mechanism in Section 2.1 that explains *why* each normalization strategy leads to its characteristic layer effectiveness profile.

3. **Proposing a principled hybrid rather than a new normalization mechanism.** Mix-LN is not a novel normalization operation (like RMSNorm was). It's a deployment strategy: apply existing techniques (Post-LN, Pre-LN) to different parts of the network based on their depth-dependent properties. The innovation is in the allocation strategy and the empirical demonstration that the hybrid outperforms either pure form.

4. **Connecting layer effectiveness to downstream task performance.** Prior work on layer redundancy focused on pre-training perplexity or pruning tolerance. The paper extends this to supervised fine-tuning (Table 4) and RLHF (Table 5), showing that models with better-trained deep layers learn more effectively during alignment—a finding with direct practical implications for the LLM deployment pipeline.

The paper's empirical strategy in Section 2 is designed to isolate the effect of normalization from other architectural choices. By first comparing open-weight models (LLaMa2-7B vs. BERT-large, Figure 2) and then replicating the finding in controlled in-house models where *only* the normalization differs (Figure 3), the paper builds a compelling case that normalization choice is the causal factor, not an artifact of other design differences between BERT and LLaMA (training data, activation functions, context length, objective, etc.).

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

Mix-LN is a **deployment strategy for existing normalization techniques** — not a new normalization operation — that assigns Post-Layer Normalization to the earliest layers and Pre-Layer Normalization to the remaining layers within a single transformer model. It solves the problem that Pre-LN (the dominant normalization in modern LLMs) causes deep layers to learn almost nothing by starving them of gradient signal, while Post-LN causes early layers to suffer from vanishing gradients, by spatially partitioning the network so that each region uses the normalization best suited to its depth.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The Mix-LN system has two major components, both operating on a standard transformer backbone:

1. **Post-LN Early Block** — the first `⌊αL⌋` layers of the transformer, where each sublayer (attention and FFN) applies layer normalization *after* the residual addition. This region preserves large gradient norms in middle and deeper portions of the network because the Post-LN gradient attenuation chain is truncated to only `αL` layers rather than all `L`.

2. **Pre-LN Late Block** — the remaining `⌈(1-α)L⌉` layers, where each sublayer applies layer normalization *before* the residual addition. This region benefits from Pre-LN's inherent training stability and avoids the divergence that would occur if Post-LN were extended all the way to the output. The identity path through the residual connections remains intact, but with fewer Pre-LN layers remaining (only `(1-α)L` rather than all `L`), the gradient-diminishing effect that makes deep layers ineffective is substantially reduced.

Information flows sequentially: an input embedding enters the Post-LN block → passes through `⌊αL⌋` transformer layers (each computing `LN(x + F(x))`) → enters the Pre-LN block → passes through `⌈(1-α)L⌉` transformer layers (each computing `x + F(LN(x))`) → produces the final hidden state.

The single hyperparameter `α ∈ [0,1]` controls the transition point. `α = 0` recovers pure Pre-LN; `α = 1` recovers pure Post-LN; intermediate values produce the hybrid. The paper empirically determines `α = 0.25` works best across model sizes from 71M to 1B parameters (Table 7), though scaling to 7B required a smaller `α = 0.0625` for stability.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the formal gradient analysis (Section 2.1 of the paper): the derivatives of Post-LN and Pre-LN as functions of the layer normalization Jacobian, and how depth accumulates attenuation or identity-mapping effects. This is the mechanistic explanation for *why* deep layers fail under Pre-LN and early layers fail under Post-LN.

- **Second**, the empirical validation methodology (Section 2.2): how the paper measures layer effectiveness using angular distance and performance drop, and how it isolates normalization choice from other architectural variables using both open-weight and in-house models.

- **Third**, the Mix-LN construction (Section 3): the spatial partitioning logic, the role of the hyperparameter `α`, and why applying Post-LN first (not last) is the correct ordering.

- **Fourth**, the gradient norm implications: why truncating the Post-LN chain improves deep-layer gradients without reintroducing early-layer vanishing, using the gradient norm measurements from Figure 6a.

- **Fifth**, the training stability trade-off: why Mix-LN avoids Post-LN's divergence (Table 1) by limiting the depth of the Post-LN region, and the sensitivity of `α` at larger scales (7B).

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is a **diagnosis-and-fix paper** whose core idea is that the spatial distribution of gradient norms across layers — and therefore which layers learn effectively — is determined by where layer normalization is placed relative to the residual connection, and that a depth-dependent hybrid (Post-LN for early layers, Pre-LN for late layers) produces uniformly healthier gradients than either pure form alone.

#### The Gradient Mechanism: Why Normalization Placement Determines Which Layers Learn

The paper develops its mechanistic argument by analyzing the Jacobian (matrix of partial derivatives) of a single transformer layer with respect to its input, under both Post-LN and Pre-LN. This analysis builds directly on Xiong et al. (2020) and Takase et al. (2023) but is applied to the new question of layer effectiveness rather than training stability.

**Post-LN derivative.** For a Post-LN layer, the forward computation is:

$$ \text{Post-LN}(x) = \text{LN}(x + F(x)) $$

where `$x$` is the input to the layer (the hidden state from the previous layer), `$F(x)$` is either the multi-head attention or feed-forward sublayer, and `$\text{LN}(\cdot)$` is the layer normalization operation. Taking the derivative with respect to the input `$x$`:

$$ \frac{\partial \text{Post-LN}(x)}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial \text{LN}(x + F(x))}{\partial (x + F(x))} \left(I + \frac{\partial F(x)}{\partial x}\right) $$

where `$I$` is the identity matrix (the derivative of the residual connection `$x$` with respect to itself) and `$\frac{\partial F(x)}{\partial x}$` is the Jacobian of the sublayer.

The critical factor is `$\frac{\partial \text{LN}(x')}{\partial x'}$`, the Jacobian of layer normalization evaluated at `$x' = x + F(x)$`. The paper follows the derivation of Xiong et al. (2020), which assumes that the input to layer normalization follows a normal distribution with mean zero (an assumption enforced by the normalization operation itself). Under this assumption:

$$ \frac{\partial \text{LN}(x')}{\partial x'} = \frac{\sqrt{d}}{\|x'\|_2} \left(I - \frac{x' x'^\top}{\|x'\|_2^2}\right) $$

where `$d$` is the hidden dimension and `$\|x'\|_2$` is the L2-norm of the input vector. This can be re-expressed in terms of the standard deviation `$\sigma_{x'}$` of `$x'$`:

$$ \frac{\partial \text{LN}(x')}{\partial x'} = \frac{1}{\sigma_{x'}} \left(I - \frac{zz^\top}{d}\right) $$

where `$z = (x' - \mu_{x'}) / \sigma_{x'}$` is the standardized (zero-mean, unit-variance) version of `$x'$`. The term `zz^\top/d` is an outer product normalized by dimension.

The key simplification — and the one that drives the entire analysis — is that in large language models, the hidden dimension `$d$` is very large (512 to 4096 in the paper's experiments), which makes the outer product term `$zz^\top / d$` dominated by the identity matrix `$I$`. Therefore:

$$ \frac{\partial \text{LN}(x')}{\partial x'} \approx \frac{1}{\sigma_{x'}} I $$

**What this means operationally:** the derivative of a Post-LN layer with respect to its input is approximately proportional to `$1/\sigma_{x'}$` times the identity matrix, multiplied by the derivative of the residual branch. The factor `$1/\sigma_{x'}$` is a scalar that multiplies every element of the gradient. Since the paper observes that `$\sigma_{x'}$` grows larger than 1 during training (the inputs to layer normalization become more variable as training progresses), this scalar is strictly less than 1.

**Why this causes vanishing gradients in early layers:** each Post-LN layer applies its own `$1/\sigma_{x'}$` factor to the gradient flowing backward. Over `$L$` layers, the gradient reaching the earliest layer gets multiplied by the product:

$$ \prod_{l=1}^{L} \frac{1}{\sigma_{x'_l}} $$

which is the product of `$L$` numbers each less than 1. This product decays toward zero as `$L$` increases — this is the classic vanishing gradient problem, but with a specific mechanism tied to layer normalization rather than activation function saturation. The consequence is that **Post-LN produces large gradient norms near the output layer** (where few factors have been applied) and **exponentially smaller gradient norms near the input layer**. The deep layers receive strong learning signals; the early layers receive almost none.

**Pre-LN derivative.** For a Pre-LN layer, the forward computation is:

$$ \text{Pre-LN}(x) = x + F(\text{LN}(x)) $$

The derivative is:

$$ \frac{\partial \text{Pre-LN}(x)}{\partial x} = I + \frac{\partial F(\text{LN}(x))}{\partial \text{LN}(x)} \frac{\partial \text{LN}(x)}{\partial x} $$

The crucial structural difference from Post-LN is that the identity matrix `$I$` is **additively separated** from the term involving `$\frac{\partial \text{LN}}{\partial x}$`. In Post-LN, the LN Jacobian multiplies the entire residual branch including `$I$`. In Pre-LN, the `$I$` term is naked — it passes through the derivative without being scaled by the LN Jacobian.

Substituting the LN Jacobian approximation `$\frac{\partial \text{LN}(x)}{\partial x} \approx \frac{1}{\sigma_x} I$`:

$$ \frac{\partial \text{Pre-LN}(x)}{\partial x} \approx I + \frac{1}{\sigma_x} \frac{\partial F(\text{LN}(x))}{\partial \text{LN}(x)} $$

**What this means operationally:** the gradient flowing backward through a Pre-LN layer consists of two additive components: (1) the raw gradient from the output, passed through the identity term, and (2) the gradient modified by the sublayer transformation, scaled down by `$1/\sigma_x$`. The identity term ensures that a copy of the gradient always reaches the previous layer regardless of what the sublayer does or how small `$1/\sigma_x$` becomes. This is why Pre-LN does not suffer from vanishing gradients in early layers — the identity pathway is a gradient highway.

**Why this causes ineffective deep layers:** the identity term also means that as depth increases, the model's output at layer `$L$` can be well-approximated by the sum of the input embedding plus the contributions from early layers. The later layers receive a gradient signal, but the signal that propagates through the identity path tells those layers: "don't change, the current hidden state is already good enough." The authors formalize this by noting that as the residual accumulation grows, the derivative approaches an identity matrix, meaning the Pre-LN operation effectively computes `$\text{Pre-LN}(x) \approx x$` — an identity mapping. A layer that computes the identity contributes nothing to learning, and the gradient it receives confirms that it should remain an identity.

**Contrast summary:** Post-LN produces **gradient imbalance skewed toward deep layers** (early layers vanish). Pre-LN produces **gradient imbalance skewed toward early layers** (deep layers become identity). Neither produces uniform gradient magnitudes across depth. The natural solution — and the one Mix-LN implements — is to use Post-LN where you need strong deep-layer gradients (early in the network, so the attenuation chain is short) and Pre-LN where you need stability and early-layer gradients (late in the network).

#### Empirical Validation: Measuring Layer Effectiveness

The paper's diagnosis in Section 2 uses two complementary metrics to quantify how much each layer contributes to the model's output. Both are needed because they capture different aspects of layer effectiveness.

**Angular Distance (Equation 8).** This metric, borrowed from Gromov et al. (2024), measures how much the hidden representation changes across a block of consecutive layers:

$$ d(x^\ell, x^{\ell+n}) = \frac{1}{\pi} \arccos\left( \frac{x^\ell_T \cdot x^{\ell+n}_T}{\|x^\ell_T\| \|x^{\ell+n}_T\|} \right) $$

where `$x^\ell_T$` is the input hidden state to layer `$\ell$` for a single token `$T$`, `$x^{\ell+n}_T$` is the input to layer `$\ell+n$` for the same token, `$\cdot$` denotes the dot product, and `$\|\cdot\|$` denotes the L2 norm. The factor `$1/\pi$` scales the arccosine output (which is in radians, range `$[0, \pi]$`) to the range `$[0, 1]$`.

**What it computes:** the normalized angle between two vectors in high-dimensional space. When `$d(x^\ell, x^{\ell+n})$` is close to 0, the two vectors point in nearly the same direction — the representations at layer `$\ell$` and layer `$\ell+n$` are almost identical. When the distance is larger, the representation has changed substantially across those `$n$` layers.

**Why this form:** angular distance captures representation similarity independent of vector magnitude — two vectors can have very different L2 norms but still encode essentially the same information if they point in the same direction. The arccos of the cosine similarity is a proper metric (satisfying triangle inequality, symmetry, identity of indiscernibles), unlike raw cosine similarity. The paper averages this over 256K tokens from the C4 dataset to eliminate token-level randomness.

**Performance Drop (Equation 9).** This metric quantifies how much model performance degrades when a single layer is surgically removed (not pruned — actually excised, with the input to that layer fed directly to the next):

$$ \Delta P(\ell) = P_{\text{pruned}}^{(\ell)} - P_{\text{original}} $$

where `$P_{\text{original}}$` is the model's performance without any modification and `$P_{\text{pruned}}^{(\ell)}$` is performance after removing layer `$\ell$`. A value close to zero (or negative — pruning sometimes slightly *improves* performance) means the layer contributes little. A large negative value means the layer is essential.

**What it computes:** the causal contribution of a specific layer to the model's output quality. Unlike angular distance (which is correlational — showing similarity), performance drop is interventional — it shows what happens when the layer is actually removed.

**Why this form:** if a layer is truly redundant, removing it should cause minimal performance change. The specific choice of evaluation task depends on model scale: MMLU for LLaMA2-7B (a knowledge-intensive benchmark suitable for large models), SQuAD v1.1 for BERT-large (a reading comprehension task it was designed for), and ARC-e for the small in-house models after fine-tuning (a grade-school science reasoning task within their capability range).

**Gradient Norm (Figure 3e, Figure 6a).** The paper also directly measures the L2 norm of the gradient of the loss with respect to each layer's parameters at initialization. This is the most direct operationalization of the gradient flow argument: Post-LN should show small norms in early layers and large norms in late layers; Pre-LN should show the opposite; Mix-LN should show a more uniform profile.

**Open-weight model results (Figure 2).** For LLaMA2-7B (Pre-LN), Figure 2b shows the angular distance matrix: rows near the bottom (layers 20-30) and columns close to the diagonal are bright yellow (distance near 0), indicating deep layers produce nearly identical representations to their immediate neighbors. Figure 2d shows that removing layers 25-30 causes negligible MMLU accuracy drop, while removing layers 0-5 causes catastrophic degradation. For BERT-large (Post-LN), Figure 2a shows high similarity (yellow) concentrated in the first half of layers (layers 3-4 and 9-11). Figure 2c shows that removing layers 2-3 *improves* SQuAD performance slightly (positive `$\Delta P$`), confirming that early Post-LN layers can be net detrimental.

**In-house model results (Figure 3).** These are the cleanest evidence because the only difference between the two LLaMA-130M models is the normalization placement. Figure 3e directly confirms the predicted gradient norm pattern: Post-LN shows a monotonically increasing gradient norm with depth (smallest at layer 0, largest at layer 11), while Pre-LN shows the reverse (largest at layer 0, decreasing through layer 11). Figure 3a-b show the angular distance patterns replicate: Post-LN concentrates similarity in early layers (0-3), Pre-LN in deep layers (8-11). Figure 3c-d confirm via performance drop: removing early Post-LN layers causes minimal ARC-e degradation; removing early Pre-LN layers causes large degradation.

#### Mix-LN Construction: Spatial Partitioning by Depth

The core design decision of Mix-LN is simple: apply Post-LN to a contiguous prefix of layers and Pre-LN to the suffix. The formal definition (Section 3):

For an LLM with `$L$` total layers, Mix-LN applies Post-LN to the first `$\lfloor \alpha L \rfloor$` layers and Pre-LN to the remaining `$\lceil (1 - \alpha) L \rceil$` layers, where `$\alpha \in [0, 1]$` is a hyperparameter controlling the fraction of layers using Post-LN. The operators `$\lfloor \cdot \rfloor$` and `$\lceil \cdot \rceil$` denote floor and ceiling respectively, ensuring integer layer counts.

Concretely, for LLaMA-250M with `$L = 24$` layers and `$\alpha = 0.25$`: the first `$\lfloor 0.25 \times 24 \rfloor = 6$` layers use Post-LN, and the remaining `$\lceil 0.75 \times 24 \rceil = 18$` layers use Pre-LN. For LLaMA-1B with `$L = 24$` layers and the same `$\alpha$`: also 6 Post-LN layers followed by 18 Pre-LN layers.

**Why Post-LN first, not last?** This ordering is critical and non-arbitrary. If Post-LN were applied to the *last* layers (near the output), the gradient attenuation chain `$\prod 1/\sigma_{x'}$` would span nearly all `$L$` layers before reaching the early-layers gradient, reproducing Post-LN's vanishing gradient problem in early layers and defeating the purpose. By placing Post-LN at the *beginning* of the network, the attenuated gradient only has to traverse `$\alpha L$` layers before entering the Pre-LN region. The Pre-LN region then provides an identity highway for gradients to reach the earliest layers.

Conversely, placing Pre-LN in the later portion ensures that the identity-mapping problem (where deep layers stop learning) affects only the last `$(1-\alpha)L$` layers rather than all `$L$` layers. Since `$(1-\alpha)L < L$` whenever `$\alpha > 0$`, fewer layers are vulnerable to becoming identity mappings, and those that are (the very last few) can still benefit from the stronger gradient signal propagated through the Post-LN region.

**The gradient norm profile of Mix-LN (Figure 6a).** The paper measures gradient norms at initialization for LLaMA-250M models with Pre-LN, Post-LN, and Mix-LN (`$\alpha = 0.25$`). Post-LN shows a clear upward slope from layer 0 (near zero) to layer 23 (highest). Pre-LN shows a downward slope from a high value at layer 0 to a low value at layer 23. Mix-LN produces a profile that is more uniform than either: it starts at an intermediate value (lower than Pre-LN's early peaks, higher than Post-LN's near-zero start), rises through the Post-LN region (layers 0-5), peaks around the transition (layer 6), and maintains elevated values through the Pre-LN region (layers 7-23), declining only slightly toward the very end. The absolute magnitudes are not perfectly flat — there is still some variation — but the range of gradient norms is substantially narrower than either pure form.

This profile directly embodies the design logic: the Post-LN prefix imparts momentum to the gradient that carries through the Pre-LN suffix, preventing the deep layers from receiving near-zero gradients. The Pre-LN suffix stabilizes the gradient in the early layers via its identity pathway, preventing the vanishing that would occur under pure Post-LN.

---

#### Hyperparameter Selection: The Role of α

The single hyperparameter `$\alpha$` controls the boundary between the two normalization regimes. The paper sweeps `$\alpha$` over the set `$\{0, 0.167, 0.25, 0.33, 0.417, 0.5, 1.0\}$` using LLaMA-1B trained on the C4 dataset (Table 7).

The resulting perplexities follow a U-shaped curve:
- `$\alpha = 0$` (pure Pre-LN): **18.65** perplexity.
- `$\alpha = 0.167$` (4 Post-LN layers out of 24): **18.34** — a modest improvement.
- `$\alpha = 0.25$` (6 Post-LN layers): **18.18** — the minimum, a 0.47-point improvement over Pre-LN.
- `$\alpha = 0.33$` (8 Post-LN layers): **18.41** — better than Pre-LN but worsening.
- `$\alpha = 0.417$` (10 Post-LN layers): **18.55** — approaching Pre-LN performance.
- `$\alpha = 0.5$` (12 Post-LN layers): **18.86** — slightly worse than pure Pre-LN.
- `$\alpha = 1.0$` (pure Post-LN): **1434** — catastrophic divergence, confirming Post-LN's instability at this scale.

The U-shape reveals a clear trade-off: too little Post-LN (`$\alpha = 0.167$`) provides only marginal benefit because the gradient boost to deep layers is insufficient. Too much Post-LN (`$\alpha \geq 0.33$` or so) reintroduces the vanishing gradient problem in early layers by extending the attenuation chain too far. The optimum at `$\alpha = 0.25$` represents the Goldilocks point: enough Post-LN layers to boost deep-layer gradients meaningfully, but not enough to destabilize training or starve early layers.

**Why `$\alpha = 0.25$` generalizes across model sizes.** The paper applies the same `$\alpha = 0.25$` to all model sizes from 71M to 1B (Section 4.1), achieving consistent improvements (Table 1). This suggests that the optimal fraction of Post-LN layers is relatively insensitive to total depth — it's a property of the gradient propagation dynamics, not the absolute number of layers. At `$\alpha = 0.25$`, the Post-LN prefix contains 25% of the layers regardless of `$L$`, meaning the gradient attenuation chain length scales proportionally with total depth rather than being fixed.

However, the paper does acknowledge that this breaks at the 7B scale (Section 4.2), where a smaller `$\alpha = 0.0625$` (6.25%, or 2 layers out of 32) was required to maintain stable training. The authors conjecture that "Mix-LN becomes more sensitive to `$\alpha$` when scaling up" and attribute the instability to the Post-LN component, noting that "using Post-LN alone also leads to the same issue." They suggest that "smaller values of `$\alpha$` and a longer warm-up period are required to stabilize the training of Mix-LN at a larger scale." This sensitivity is an acknowledged limitation (Section 6) and an open research direction.

---

#### Training Stability: Why Mix-LN Avoids Post-LN's Divergence While Keeping Its Benefits

Post-LN's training instability at scale is well-documented: Table 1 shows that pure Post-LN diverges to perplexity >1400 for both LLaMA-250M and LLaMA-1B (the worst possible perplexity for C4, where uniform guessing yields very high perplexity). DeepNorm, which modifies Post-LN by upscaling the residual connection before applying LN, partially mitigates this — it achieves 22.77 on LLaMA-250M (close to Pre-LN's 21.92) — but still diverges at 1B (1410.94 perplexity).

The paper's explanation for why Mix-LN avoids this instability is in the gradient attenuation analysis. Under pure Post-LN, the early-layer gradient is multiplied by `$\prod_{l=1}^{L} 1/\sigma_{x'}$`, which for `$L = 24$` involves 24 factors each less than 1. This product is extremely small, effectively zero, meaning early layers receive no gradient and cannot learn. They remain at their random initialization, producing input to subsequent layers that is essentially noise. This noise propagates forward, and the deeper layers, which do receive gradients, attempt to fit the loss function using corrupted inputs — a recipe for instability.

Under Mix-LN with `$\alpha = 0.25$`, the attenuation chain involves only `$\alpha L = 6$` factors of `$1/\sigma_{x'}$` before the gradient enters the Pre-LN region. With 6 factors rather than 24, the accumulated attenuation is `$(\text{something less than 1})^6$` rather than `$(\text{something less than 1})^{24}$`. Even if each factor is 0.5 (a very aggressive estimate), the total attenuation would be `$0.5^6 \approx 0.016$` for Mix-LN versus `$0.5^{24} \approx 6 \times 10^{-8}$` for pure Post-LN — more than a million times larger. In practice, the factors are closer to 1 early in training, but the scaling argument holds: reducing the number of multiplicative attenuation factors by 75% dramatically reduces the severity of the vanishing gradient problem.

The remaining Pre-LN layers (the last 18) provide the stability guarantee: their identity residual path prevents any further gradient attenuation, ensuring the earliest layers (layers 0-5 in the Post-LN block) receive gradients that have only been attenuated by 6 LN Jacobians, not 24. The gradient that reaches layer 0 has traversed 6 Post-LN layers and 18 Pre-LN layers, but the Pre-LN layers preserve gradient magnitude via their identity terms, so the effective attenuation is equivalent to only 6 Post-LN layers.

**The warm-up requirement.** The paper notes that longer warm-up is needed at the 7B scale, which is consistent with known Post-LN behavior. Xiong et al. (2020) showed that Post-LN requires careful learning rate warm-up to avoid gradient explosion early in training (when `$\sigma_{x'}$` is small and `$1/\sigma_{x'}$` is large, temporarily producing large gradients before the network stabilizes). By including *some* Post-LN layers, Mix-LN inherits a milder version of this warm-up dependency, which becomes more pronounced at larger scales. The paper frames this as a stability-sensitivity trade-off that warrants further investigation.

---

#### Measurement Protocol: How Layer Effectiveness Is Quantified

The paper's empirical validation methodology (Section 2.2) is designed to provide converging evidence from multiple measurement modalities. Each metric captures a different aspect of layer quality:

**Angular distance measurement procedure.** For a given model, the paper processes 256K tokens from the C4 dataset (the same dataset used for pre-training, ensuring the representations reflect what the model actually learns). For each token, it records the hidden state at the input to each layer. For each layer index `$\ell$` and each block size `$n$`, it computes the angular distance between the hidden state at layer `$\ell$` and the hidden state at layer `$\ell + n$`. These are averaged over all 256K tokens to produce the distance matrices shown in Figures 2a-b and 3a-c. Each cell `$(\ell, n)$` in these matrices represents the average angular distance between layers `$\ell$` and `$\ell+n$`. The row `$n=1$` (the diagonal just above the main diagonal) shows similarity between adjacent layers — this is the most informative strip for identifying redundant layers.

**Performance drop measurement procedure.** For each layer `$\ell$` in the model, the paper creates a modified version of the model where layer `$\ell$` is bypassed: the input to layer `$\ell$` is fed directly as input to layer `$\ell+1$`. This is not weight pruning — it's structural removal. The modified model is then evaluated on the appropriate benchmark (MMLU for LLaMA2-7B, SQuAD v1.1 for BERT-large, ARC-e for the in-house 130M models). The performance drop `$\Delta P(\ell)$` is the difference between the modified model's score and the original model's score. Negative values indicate the layer is important (removing it hurts); values near zero or positive indicate the layer is redundant or slightly harmful.

**Why both metrics together are needed.** Angular distance is a correlational metric — it shows that layers produce similar representations, but it doesn't prove those layers are causally unimportant. Two layers could produce similar representations but both be essential (removing either would break the representation). Performance drop is causal but coarser — it shows that removing a layer causes minimal performance change, but it doesn't explain *why* the layer was redundant (were its outputs identical to the previous layer's? were they different but easily compensated for by later layers?). Together, they triangulate: high angular similarity + near-zero performance drop = strong evidence of genuine redundancy.

**Cross-validation note.** The in-house 130M models in Figure 3 are trained with *identical* configurations except for normalization placement (RMSNorm in Pre-LN or Post-LN position), SwiGLU activations, and the LLaMA architecture. Training on C4 with the same batch size, sequence length, learning rate, and optimizer eliminates confounding variables present in the open-weight comparison (where BERT and LLaMA differ in training data, architecture, activation functions, context length, and training objective). The consistent pattern across both comparison types — open-weight and controlled — strengthens the causal attribution to normalization.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Reframing Deep-Layer Redundancy as a Training Pathology, Not an Architectural Inevitability

The paper's most fundamental conceptual move is to reinterpret the well-documented phenomenon of deep-layer redundancy in LLMs — previously treated as either a curiosity (Gromov et al., 2024; Lad et al., 2024) or a compression opportunity (Yin et al., 2023; Men et al., 2024; Siddiqui et al., 2024) — as **a diagnosable and fixable training failure rooted in the normalization scheme**. This reframing matters because it changes the research objective from "how do we prune these useless parameters?" to "how do we make these parameters useful?" — a shift from damage control to capability enhancement.

Prior work had thoroughly documented that deeper layers in Pre-LN models (which includes GPT, LLaMA, Mistral, and essentially all modern autoregressive LLMs) contribute negligibly to output quality. Gromov et al. (2024) showed via angular distance that deep-layer representations are near-identical neighbors; Men et al. (2024) demonstrated entire blocks of deep layers can be removed with minimal perplexity impact; Lad et al. (2024) found that deep layers can be randomly swapped without degrading performance. The dominant interpretation was that deep layers are simply less important — perhaps because later stages of computation require less transformation, or because residual networks naturally concentrate useful computation in earlier layers.

The paper's counterargument is sharp: if deep layers are underperforming, it's because **the training signal never reached them**, not because the architecture inherently limits their potential. The gradient analysis in Section 2.1 — showing that Pre-LN necessarily produces diminishing gradient norms with depth — provides the mechanistic explanation that the prior empirical literature lacked. By connecting the observation (deep layers don't learn) to the cause (Pre-LN's gradient attenuation), the paper transforms what looked like an architectural constraint into a training design flaw. The practical implication is enormous: if you can fix gradient flow to deep layers, you recover the capacity those parameters always had but never developed.

The evidence that this reframing is correct comes from the controlled experiment in Section 2.3.2 (Figure 3). When the *same architecture* is trained with Post-LN instead of Pre-LN, the layer effectiveness profile inverts: deep layers become essential (large performance drop when removed) and early layers become redundant. This demonstrates that which layers are effective is determined by normalization placement, not by some intrinsic property of depth. The Post-LN model's deep layers *do* learn useful transformations — they're just never given the chance under Pre-LN.

What makes this intellectually distinctive is that it converts a descriptive finding ("deep layers are redundant") into a normative claim ("deep layers *shouldn't be* redundant — their redundancy is a bug, not a feature") and provides both the causal mechanism and the fix. This is akin to the shift from "vision transformers need lots of data" (descriptive) to "vision transformers lack appropriate inductive biases for low-data regimes — here's how to add them" (diagnostic + prescriptive). The paper doesn't just say "Pre-LN is bad for deep layers"; it says "the fact that Pre-LN is bad for deep layers *explains* the entire literature on deep-layer pruning, and we can fix it."

---

### Innovation 2: The Gradient Attenuation Chain as a Unifying Framework for Normalization-Depth Interactions

The paper derives — and then empirically validates — a clean, quantitative framework for understanding how normalization placement interacts with network depth to determine which layers receive effective learning signals. While the individual pieces of this analysis existed in prior work (Xiong et al., 2020 derived the LN Jacobian approximation; Takase et al., 2023 connected it to gradient norms), **the paper constructs from them a predictive model of layer effectiveness that unifies previously disconnected empirical observations about both Pre-LN and Post-LN architectures**.

The core insight is that the layer normalization Jacobian acts as a **per-layer gradient scaler**: each LN operation multiplies the backward gradient by approximately `1/σ`, where `σ > 1` during training. Under Post-LN, where LN is applied after the residual addition, this scalar multiplies the *entire* gradient including the identity path, so deep layers accumulate an attenuation chain of length `L` (the full depth) when propagating gradients to early layers. Under Pre-LN, the identity path is decoupled from the LN Jacobian, so gradients flow freely to early layers — but the trade-off is that deep layers receive only the (now very small) LN-scaled component, making them asymptotically approach identity mappings.

This framework predicts — without needing to train a model — three patterns that the paper then verifies:

1. **Pre-LN models should show decreasing gradient norms with depth, and consequently decreasing layer contribution with depth.** Confirmed in Figures 3e and 3d for in-house 130M models, and Figure 2d for LLaMA2-7B.

2. **Post-LN models should show increasing gradient norms with depth, and consequently the *early* layers should be least effective.** Confirmed in Figures 3e and 3c for in-house models, and Figure 2c for BERT-large. The paper's observation that removing BERT-large's layer 2 *improves* SQuAD performance (Figure 2c) is a particularly striking confirmation — the gradient-starved early layer is actively harmful.

3. **A hybrid with Post-LN on early layers should truncate the attenuation chain to `αL` factors while still providing the identity gradient highway for the earliest layers, producing more uniform gradient norms.** Confirmed in Figure 6a, where Mix-LN's gradient norm profile is visibly flatter than either pure form.

What distinguishes this from prior gradient analyses is the **explanatory scope**. Xiong et al. (2020) used similar mathematics to explain why Pre-LN doesn't need warm-up (stability) and why Post-LN does (instability). Takase et al. (2023) extended it to explain gradient spike phenomena. But neither connected the gradient patterns to the *spatial distribution of layer quality* across depth — the key phenomenon that the pruning literature had been documenting without explanation. The paper's framework explains not just *that* deep Pre-LN layers are ineffective, but *why* they specifically become identity mappings (the identity term dominates as residual accumulation grows), and *why* the effect is concentrated in the deepest layers (the attenuation of the LN-scaled component compounds with depth).

This is a moderate but genuine theoretical advance. It doesn't invent new mathematics — the derivative derivations follow Xiong et al. directly — but it applies existing mathematics to a new explanatory target (layer effectiveness rather than training stability) and generates falsifiable predictions that the paper systematically tests. The framework's predictive power is what elevates it beyond observation: it tells you what will happen if you change `α` (Table 7 shows the U-shaped perplexity curve exactly as predicted), what will happen at different model scales (Section 4.2 confirms the framework with the 7B caveat about `α` sensitivity), and what alternative fixes should or shouldn't work (Table 8 shows that other normalization variants, which don't structurally address the attenuation chain, underperform Mix-LN).

---

### Innovation 3: The Spatial Partitioning Principle — Depth-Conditioned Normalization Assignment

The paper introduces a **spatial partitioning approach to normalization design** — the idea that different depth regions of a network have fundamentally different gradient dynamics, and therefore should use different normalization strategies, even though the transformer layers themselves are architecturally identical. This is conceptually distinct from prior work on normalization variants, which typically propose a single new normalization operation applied uniformly across all layers.

To appreciate the novelty, consider the design space of prior normalization research:

- **Uniform variants:** RMSNorm (Zhang & Sennrich, 2019), Group-LN (Wu & He, 2018), and Admin (Liu et al., 2020) all apply the same normalization operation to every layer. They modify the *form* of normalization (removing mean centering, grouping channels, adding learnable scaling) but not its *spatial deployment*.

- **Per-sublayer variants:** Sandwich-LN (Ding et al., 2021) normalizes both input and output of each sublayer, but does so uniformly at every depth. B2T (Takase et al., 2022) connects the input and output of each layer with a special residual path, but again applies the same design everywhere.

- **Residual scaling:** DeepNorm (Wang et al., 2024) scales the residual connection before normalization, which changes gradient magnitudes, but does so with the same scaling factor at every depth.

All of these treat depth as a dimension to *scale across* (deeper networks need more stabilization) but not as a dimension with *qualitatively different requirements at different positions* (early layers need one kind of gradient profile; late layers need a different one). Mix-LN's innovation is to recognize that the gradient dynamics are depth-dependent in a way that a uniform solution cannot optimally address — early layers benefit from Post-LN's gradient-preserving properties for their downstream neighbors, while late layers benefit from Pre-LN's stability and identity pathway for gradient propagation to the input. A single normalization placement cannot simultaneously provide Post-LN's deep-gradient boost and Pre-LN's early-gradient stability; spatial partitioning lets you have both.

The paper provides a principled justification for the Post-LN-first ordering (Section 3 in the paper, Section 3.4 in the prior analysis): placing Post-LN at the beginning of the network truncates the gradient attenuation chain to `αL` layers, minimizing the vanishing gradient problem for the earliest layers. Placing Post-LN at the end would extend the attenuation chain to nearly `L` layers, reproducing Post-LN's instability. The ordering is therefore not an arbitrary design choice — it follows directly from the gradient attenuation analysis.

The `α` hyperparameter operationalizes this principle as a continuous trade-off between Post-LN's benefits (stronger deep-layer gradients) and its costs (vanishing early-layer gradients as more layers are included). Table 7's U-shaped perplexity curve demonstrates that the trade-off is real and optimizable: there exists a sweet spot where the benefits outweigh the costs, and it's not at either extreme. The fact that `α = 0.25` works well across model sizes from 71M to 1B (Table 1) suggests the optimal partition fraction is relatively stable — the Post-LN prefix needs to be long enough to meaningfully boost deep-layer gradients (hence `α > 0.16` is necessary) but short enough to avoid destabilizing early-layer training (hence `α < 0.33`).

This spatial partitioning principle has implications beyond the specific Mix-LN design. It suggests a broader design philosophy for deep networks: **whenever a component has depth-dependent effects (gradient attenuation, feature specialization, representational capacity), the architecture should adapt to depth rather than being depth-uniform**. The paper hints at this with the 7B scaling observation (Section 4.2) — larger models need smaller `α`, suggesting that the optimal partition may not just be scale-invariant but may require scale-dependent tuning. This opens a research direction in depth-conditioned architectural design that the paper initiates but does not exhaust.

---

### Innovation 4: Demonstrating That Well-Trained Deep Layers Improve Alignment, Not Just Pre-Training

A key empirical finding that distinguishes this paper from prior work on normalization is the demonstration that **the benefits of balanced gradient flow extend from pre-training into supervised fine-tuning and RLHF**, with effects that are disproportionate to the pre-training perplexity improvements. This is not an obvious consequence of normalization design — one might reasonably expect that a 0.47 perplexity improvement at pre-training would produce proportional gains downstream. The paper shows it produces much larger gains, suggesting that well-trained deep layers provide a qualitatively different kind of representational capacity that downstream tasks exploit.

Consider the numbers: for LLaMA-250M, Mix-LN improves pre-training perplexity over Pre-LN by 0.53 points (21.92 → 21.39, Table 1), a modest ~2.4% reduction. But in supervised fine-tuning (Table 4), the average accuracy across 7 downstream tasks improves by 4.41 percentage points (36.93 → 41.34), an 11.9% relative improvement. The largest single-task gain is BoolQ: 17.77 percentage points (38.35 → 56.12), a 46.3% relative improvement. For LLaMA-1B, the pre-training perplexity improvement is smaller (18.65 → 18.18, a 2.5% reduction), but the downstream gain is still 1.65 percentage points on average (43.01 → 44.66), with Winogrande showing a 5.42-point gain (50.51 → 55.93).

These downstream gains are disproportionately large relative to the pre-training gains. A standard scaling law would predict that the downstream improvement should be roughly proportional to the log-perplexity improvement — not 5× larger for BoolQ. The paper's explanation (Section 4.3) is that "the superior middle and deeper layers produced by Mix-LN are better equipped to learn during supervised fine-tuning" because they "capture more diverse and rich features compared to those trained with Pre-LN." In other words, Pre-LN's deep layers don't just contribute less to pre-training loss — they contain less *information* that fine-tuning can later extract. They've learned near-identity mappings that provide no useful features for downstream task adaptation. Mix-LN's deep layers, having received meaningful gradient signals during pre-training, have developed non-trivial transformations that fine-tuning can leverage.

The RLHF result (Table 5) reinforces this interpretation. RLHF trains a reward model and then optimizes the language model policy against it — a process that depends on the policy model having a rich enough feature space to represent nuanced preference distinctions. Mix-LN achieves a final reward of 1.32 versus Pre-LN's 0.75 (a 76% improvement), suggesting that Pre-LN's deep layers constrain the policy's representational capacity during RL optimization. The paper doesn't provide a mechanism for why RLHF specifically benefits, but the implication is clear: alignment procedures that require the model to develop new capabilities (not just surface new knowledge from pre-training) are limited by the quality of features in deep layers.

This finding changes the cost-benefit calculus for normalization design. If Mix-LN's benefits were confined to pre-training perplexity, the case for adoption would rest on training efficiency (better perplexity per FLOP). But the outsized downstream gains mean that **a model pre-trained with Mix-LN is not just a more efficient version of a Pre-LN model — it's a qualitatively better foundation for alignment**. Organizations investing heavily in SFT and RLHF should care more about the deep-layer representational quality of their base models than about pre-training perplexity alone, and this paper provides a concrete mechanism for improving it.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The primary pre-training dataset is the C4 corpus (Raffel et al., 2020), a cleaned version of Common Crawl web text. All in-house models from 71M to 7B parameters are trained on C4 following the configurations established by Lialin et al. (2023a) and Zhao et al. (2024). For the open-weight large-scale LLM evaluation in Section 2, the paper uses the C4 dataset to compute angular distance metrics (averaging over 256K tokens to eliminate token-level randomness). For downstream evaluation, the paper uses seven commonsense reasoning benchmarks from the Commonsense170K collection (Hu et al., 2023): MMLU (Hendrycks et al., 2020), BoolQ, ARC-e (Clark et al., 2018), PIQA, Hellaswag, OpenBookQA, and Winogrande. Additionally, the performance drop analysis in Section 2 uses MMLU (for LLaMA2-7B), SQuAD v1.1 (Rajpurkar, 2016; for BERT-large), and ARC-e (for the in-house small-scale LLMs after supervised fine-tuning).

- **Base model(s).** The paper uses two categories of models. For **open-weight large-scale LLMs**, it selects LLaMA2-7B (Touvron et al., 2023) as the representative Pre-LN model and BERT-large (Devlin, 2018) as the representative Post-LN model. These serve as uncontrolled comparisons that reflect real-world Pre-LN and Post-LN deployments, though they differ in training data, activation functions, context length, and architecture beyond normalization. For **in-house small-scale LLMs**, the paper trains LLaMA-based architectures from scratch using configurations following Lialin et al. (2023b) and Zhao et al. (2024), with RMSNorm (Zhang & Sennrich, 2019) and SwiGLU activations (Shazeer, 2020). Model sizes span 71M, 130M, 250M, 1B, and 7B parameters. The controlled in-house experiments ensure that the *only* difference between Pre-LN, Post-LN, and Mix-LN variants is the normalization placement — eliminating the confounding variables present in the open-weight comparison. The LLaMA architecture is chosen because it is "representative of contemporary LLMs" and uses Pre-LN by default, making it the natural testbed for the paper's hypothesis.

- **Metrics.** The paper uses three classes of metrics:
  - **Pre-training quality:** Perplexity on the C4 validation set, measured after the specified number of training tokens for each model size (Table 1), with lower values indicating better language modeling.
  - **Layer effectiveness:**
    - *Angular distance* `d(x^ℓ, x^{ℓ+n})` (Equation 8): scaled arccosine of the cosine similarity between hidden states at layer `ℓ` and layer `ℓ+n`, averaged over 256K C4 tokens. Range [0,1], where 0 indicates identical representations and 1 indicates orthogonal representations. A smaller value means the representations are more similar, suggesting the intermediate layers contribute little.
    - *Performance drop* `ΔP(ℓ)` (Equation 9): the difference in task performance between the original model and the model with layer `ℓ` surgically removed (bypassed), where negative values indicate the layer is important (removing it hurts performance) and values near zero or positive indicate the layer is redundant or slightly detrimental.
  - **Downstream performance:**
    - *Supervised fine-tuning:* Accuracy on each of the seven commonsense reasoning tasks from Commonsense170K, plus an average across all tasks (Table 4).
    - *RLHF:* Final reward score after reinforcement learning from human feedback on the UltraFeedback dataset (Table 5), where higher is better.
    - *Vision:* Top-1 accuracy on ImageNet-1K for ViT models (Table 6).

- **Baselines.** The paper compares Mix-LN against four primary normalization strategies:
  - **Post-LN** (Ba, 2016; Vaswani et al., 2017): Layer normalization applied *after* the residual addition in every layer. Controls for the deep-layer gradient benefit but serves primarily as a negative control due to known training instability at scale (Nguyen & Salazar, 2019; Xiong et al., 2020).
  - **Pre-LN** (Dai, 2019; Baevski & Auli, 2018): Layer normalization applied *before* the residual addition in every layer. This is the dominant normalization in modern LLMs (GPT, LLaMA, Mistral) and serves as the primary baseline — Mix-LN must outperform Pre-LN to be practically relevant.
  - **DeepNorm** (Wang et al., 2024): A modified Post-LN that upscales the residual connection before applying layer normalization, designed to stabilize training in very deep transformers. Included to test whether existing Post-LN stabilization techniques achieve the same deep-layer benefits as Mix-LN without the hybrid design.
  - **Other normalization methods** (Table 8): Admin (Liu et al., 2020), Group-LN (Wu & He, 2018; Ma et al., 2024), and Sandwich-LN (Ding et al., 2021), tested on LLaMA-250M to compare against recent normalization innovations beyond the Pre-LN/Post-LN dichotomy.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper measures compute in terms of **training tokens** processed, not inference generations. Each model size is trained on a fixed token budget: 1.1B tokens for 71M, 2.2B for 130M, 3.9B for 250M, 5B for 1B, and 1.7B for the 7B experiments (Table 9 in Appendix A). All models of the same size are trained with identical configurations except for the normalization method, ensuring fair comparison. Training hyperparameters (batch size 512, sequence length 256, Adam optimizer with cosine annealing) are held constant across normalization variants for each model size. The angular distance measurements in Section 2 use a fixed 256K-token sample from C4 for all models, eliminating token-sampling variance as a confound. For the `α` sweep in Table 7, the same training budget and configuration are used with only `α` varied.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not employ formal cross-validation for the main pre-training results — each model size and normalization combination is trained once, and perplexity is reported on the C4 validation set. For the supervised fine-tuning results (Table 4), all models fine-tuned from the same pre-training runs are evaluated on the full test sets of the seven downstream benchmarks; the average across tasks is a simple arithmetic mean. The angular distance measurements use 256K tokens to reduce sampling variance, and the paper notes this averaging explicitly ("the angular distance reported in this paper is averaged over 256K tokens from the C4 dataset" — Section 2.2). For the `α` sweep (Table 7), a single run is reported per `α` value. The paper does not report confidence intervals, standard deviations, or results from multiple training seeds, which means the reported perplexity differences (e.g., 21.39 vs. 21.92 for Mix-LN vs. Pre-LN on LLaMA-250M, a gap of 0.53) cannot be assessed for statistical significance. This is a limitation given that training runs at these scales can exhibit non-trivial variance, though the consistent pattern across model sizes provides some robustness against seed noise.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Pre-Training Perplexity Across Model Scales (Section 4.1)

The central claim of Section 4.1 is that Mix-LN consistently achieves lower perplexity than Pre-LN, Post-LN, and DeepNorm across model sizes from 71M to 1B parameters, and that Post-LN and its stabilized variant DeepNorm diverge at larger scales while Mix-LN remains stable.

**Table 1** reports the validation perplexity for each normalization method at each model size after the full training budget. The headline numbers:

- **LLaMA-71M** (1.1B training tokens): Mix-LN achieves **33.12** perplexity, improving over Pre-LN's 34.77 by **1.65 points** (a 4.7% relative reduction). Post-LN produces 35.18 (worse than Pre-LN), and DeepNorm produces 34.87 (marginally better than Post-LN but still worse than Pre-LN).
- **LLaMA-130M** (2.2B tokens): Mix-LN achieves **26.07**, improving over Pre-LN's 26.78 by **0.71 points** (2.7% relative). Post-LN is at 26.95 and DeepNorm at 27.17 — here DeepNorm is *worse* than pure Post-LN and both trail Pre-LN.
- **LLaMA-250M** (3.9B tokens): Mix-LN achieves **21.39**, improving over Pre-LN's 21.92 by **0.53 points** (2.4% relative). Post-LN diverges to **1409.09** perplexity — a catastrophic failure indicating the model has completely broken. DeepNorm achieves 22.77, which is better than diverging Post-LN but **worse than Pre-LN by 0.85 points**, meaning DeepNorm fails to match the standard baseline at this scale.
- **LLaMA-1B** (5B tokens): Mix-LN achieves **18.18**, improving over Pre-LN's 18.65 by **0.47 points** (2.5% relative). Post-LN diverges to **1411.54**. DeepNorm also diverges to **1410.94** — establishing that even the stabilized Post-LN variant cannot train at 1B parameter scale, while Mix-LN (which contains 6 out of 24 layers as Post-LN) trains successfully and outperforms Pre-LN.

Several patterns in these numbers merit attention. First, the **absolute perplexity improvement from Mix-LN diminishes with model size**: 1.65 points at 71M, 0.71 at 130M, 0.53 at 250M, and 0.47 at 1B. This could reflect either diminishing returns from better deep layers as model capacity increases, or simply that larger models start from a lower baseline perplexity where improvements are harder to achieve. The paper does not comment on this trend. Second, the **Post-LN divergence threshold appears between 130M and 250M parameters** for this architecture and training configuration. At 71M and 130M, Post-LN trains successfully (albeit worse than Pre-LN); at 250M and above, it diverges catastrophically. This is consistent with prior literature on Post-LN instability scaling with depth and width (Xiong et al., 2020) but provides a specific threshold for the LLaMA architecture. Third, **DeepNorm's failure at 1B** (1410.94 perplexity, indistinguishable from Post-LN's divergence) is notable because DeepNorm was explicitly designed for very deep transformers (Wang et al., 2024) and was reported to scale to 1000 layers. Its failure here at only 24 layers but 1B parameters suggests that the divergence is driven by model width (hidden dimension 2048 at 1B) rather than depth alone — a finding the paper does not explore but that has implications for the generality of Post-LN stabilization techniques.

The paper does not report the training loss curves for these models (only evaluation perplexity at the final checkpoint), so it is impossible to determine whether Mix-LN converges faster, reaches the same perplexity with fewer tokens, or has different overfitting dynamics. The 7B experiment (Figure 4) is the only one shown as a training curve, but it was stopped at 13,000 steps (approximately 1.7B tokens) before convergence, limiting what can be concluded about Mix-LN's scaling behavior at that size.

#### Scaling to 7B Parameters (Section 4.2)

The 7B experiment uses a LLaMA-7B architecture (embedding size 4096, 32 layers, 32 heads) trained for 13,000 steps (~1.7B tokens), with all configurations identical except normalization. Due to computational constraints, this is a partial training run, not a full convergence experiment.

**Figure 4** shows the evaluation perplexity curves for Mix-LN (with `α = 0.0625`, or 2 out of 32 layers as Post-LN) and Pre-LN over the 13,000 training steps. At the final step, Mix-LN achieves lower evaluation perplexity than Pre-LN, though the paper does not quote the exact final perplexity values in the main text. The curves show that Mix-LN maintains a consistent advantage over Pre-LN throughout the latter half of training (from roughly step 7,000 onward), with no evidence of instability or divergence. The gap appears to widen slightly toward the end of training, suggesting the benefit may increase with more training, though the limited training horizon prevents confirmation.

The paper reports a critical caveat for the 7B scale: **Mix-LN becomes more sensitive to the `α` hyperparameter**. While `α = 0.25` worked optimally for all models from 71M to 1B, the 7B experiment required a much smaller `α = 0.0625` (2 layers out of 32, rather than the 8 layers that `α = 0.25` would dictate). The authors state:

> "In general, smaller values of α and a longer warm-up period are required to stabilize the training of Mix-LN at a larger scale."

They conjecture "this instability is relevant to Post-LN, as using Post-LN alone also leads to the same issue," and flag addressing this challenge as "an interesting research direction" (Section 4.2).

This sensitivity is significant for two reasons. First, it means the `α` value determined by sweeps at smaller scales (Table 7, on LLaMA-1B) does **not** directly transfer to larger scales — the optimal partition fraction depends on model size in a way the paper has not fully characterized. Second, it raises the question of whether `α` must be tuned per-model-size, which would increase the cost of adopting Mix-LN in practice (requiring multiple `α` sweeps at the target scale). The paper provides only two data points for scale-dependent `α` (0.25 for 1B, 0.0625 for 7B), which is insufficient to establish a scaling trend or to guide practitioners on how to choose `α` for intermediate or larger models.

Additionally, the paper does not report whether larger `α` values were attempted and failed at 7B, or whether 0.0625 was chosen based on a sweep or through trial and error. If only `α = 0.0625` was tried, the reported performance is a lower bound — a more carefully optimized `α` might yield larger gains. If larger `α` values were tried and diverged, the instability threshold between 1B and 7B would be informative but is not characterized.

#### Supervised Fine-Tuning Results (Section 4.3)

The fine-tuning experiments take the pre-trained models from Section 4.1 (LLaMA-250M and LLaMA-1B, each with all four normalization variants) and fine-tune them on Commonsense170K, evaluating on seven downstream tasks. **Table 4** reports accuracy on each task and the average across all seven.

**LLaMA-250M results:**

- Mix-LN achieves an average accuracy of **41.34%**, compared to Pre-LN's 36.93% — a gain of **4.41 percentage points** (11.9% relative improvement). This is the largest relative gain in the paper and substantially exceeds what the pre-training perplexity improvement (2.4%) would predict.
- The largest single-task gain is on **BoolQ**: Mix-LN achieves 56.12% vs. Pre-LN's 38.35%, a difference of **17.77 percentage points** (46.3% relative). This is an extraordinary improvement for a normalization change and suggests that BoolQ requires features that Pre-LN's deep layers fail to learn.
- Mix-LN outperforms Pre-LN on **every single task**: MMLU (26.53 vs. 24.93, +1.60), ARC-e (41.68 vs. 40.15, +1.53), PIQA (66.34 vs. 63.55, +2.79), Hellaswag (30.16 vs. 26.34, +3.82), OBQA (18.00 vs. 16.20, +1.80), and Winogrande (50.56 vs. 49.01, +1.55).
- Post-LN and DeepNorm both underperform Pre-LN on average, with Post-LN achieving only 32.54% and DeepNorm 35.63% (the latter is 1.30 points below Pre-LN). This is notable because DeepNorm was competitive with Pre-LN on pre-training perplexity at this scale (22.77 vs. 21.92, Table 1) but the gap widens substantially in fine-tuning, suggesting that DeepNorm's gradient dynamics produce representations that are less adaptable to downstream tasks even when pre-training loss is similar.

**LLaMA-1B results:**

- Mix-LN achieves an average accuracy of **44.66%**, compared to Pre-LN's 43.01% — a gain of **1.65 percentage points** (3.8% relative). This is modest but consistent, and again exceeds what the pre-training perplexity improvement (2.5%) would predict proportionally.
- The largest gain is on **Winogrande**: Mix-LN achieves 55.93% vs. Pre-LN's 50.51%, a gain of **5.42 percentage points** (10.7% relative). BoolQ, which showed the largest gain at 250M, shows a slight *decrease* at 1B (61.93% vs. 62.20%, -0.27 points) — the only task where Mix-LN underperforms Pre-LN.
- On other tasks, Mix-LN outperforms Pre-LN: MMLU (27.99 vs. 26.54, +1.45), ARC-e (48.11 vs. 45.70, +2.41), PIQA (68.50 vs. 67.79, +0.71), Hellaswag (31.35 vs. 30.96, +0.39), and OBQA (18.80 vs. 17.40, +1.40).
- Post-LN and DeepNorm again substantially underperform, with Post-LN at 31.96% average and DeepNorm at 32.67% — DeepNorm is slightly better than Post-LN but both are far below Pre-LN and Mix-LN.

The pattern across model sizes reveals an interesting dynamic: **the downstream gains from Mix-LN are larger at 250M (+4.41 points) than at 1B (+1.65 points)**. This could indicate that larger models are partially able to compensate for poor deep-layer representations through other mechanisms (more capacity in early layers, or the ability to route around gradient-starved layers), reducing the relative benefit of fixing the deep layers. Alternatively, it could mean that the optimal `α` shifts with scale for fine-tuning as well as for pre-training, and the 1B model might benefit from a different `α` than 0.25. The paper does not explore `α` sensitivity for fine-tuning performance.

#### Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (Section 4.4)

The RLHF experiment takes the LLaMA-1B models pre-trained with Pre-LN and Mix-LN, implements the RLHF workflow from InstructGPT (Ouyang et al., 2022), and trains them on the UltraFeedback dataset to optimize a preference reward. **Table 5** reports the final reward scores.

The result is stark: Mix-LN achieves a final reward of **1.32**, compared to Pre-LN's **0.75** — a difference of 0.57, or a 76% relative improvement. The paper provides minimal detail on the RLHF setup beyond citing Adam-mini (Zhang et al., 2024) and the InstructGPT workflow, and does not report intermediate metrics (reward curves, KL divergence from the reference policy, win rates against baselines). The metric "final reward" is not precisely defined — it is presumably the reward model's score on held-out preference data, but the paper does not specify.

This result is important because it extends the Mix-LN benefit to a third training phase (pre-training → SFT → RLHF) and shows it persists through the entire LLM development pipeline. However, the reporting is thin. The lack of reward curves prevents understanding whether Mix-LN converges to a higher reward more quickly, achieves a higher asymptote, or both. The lack of KL divergence metrics means we cannot assess whether the higher reward comes at the cost of greater deviation from the reference policy (which would make the model less coherent or more prone to reward hacking). And with only a single run per normalization variant, there is no variance estimate.

Despite these limitations, the result is directionally consistent with the SFT findings: better pre-trained representations in deep layers translate to better alignment outcomes. The magnitude of the improvement (76% relative) is large enough to be practically significant even if some of the gain is due to training noise.

#### Vision Transformer Results (Section 4.5)

The paper extends Mix-LN beyond language models by replacing Pre-LN with Mix-LN (`α = 0.25`) in ViT-Tiny and ViT-Small models, trained for 120 epochs on ImageNet-1K following the ConvNeXt (Liu et al., 2022c) configurations. **Table 6** reports top-1 accuracy.

ViT-Tiny shows a negligible improvement: Mix-LN achieves **67.34%** vs. Pre-LN's 67.30%, a difference of only 0.04 percentage points — well within the range of training noise. ViT-Small shows a more meaningful gain: Mix-LN achieves **76.40%** vs. Pre-LN's 75.99%, a difference of 0.41 percentage points. The paper notes that "the benefits of Mix-LN extend to non-language tasks, with performance gains that are more pronounced in larger models (ViT-Small) compared to smaller ones (ViT-Tiny)."

This result is important for establishing generality beyond autoregressive language modeling, but the evidence is thin. Two data points (Tiny and Small) with small absolute gains do not strongly establish a trend, and the paper does not report whether `α = 0.25` was optimal for ViT (it was chosen based on language model sweeps). ViT architectures differ from LLaMA in several ways that could affect normalization dynamics (no causal masking, different depth-to-width ratios, different activation patterns from image rather than text inputs), and the paper provides no analysis of whether the same gradient attenuation mechanism operates in ViTs.

The larger gain for ViT-Small compared to ViT-Tiny is consistent with the language model pattern (larger models benefit more from well-trained deep layers), but with only two sizes, this is a hint rather than a robust finding.

#### Angular Distance and Performance Drop Analysis (Section 2.3)

The empirical diagnosis in Section 2 uses angular distance and performance drop to validate the claim that normalization placement determines the layer effectiveness profile.

**Open-weight models (Figure 2):** For LLaMA2-7B (Pre-LN), Figure 2-b shows that angular distances between adjacent layers decrease with depth: layers 20-30 show bright yellow (distance near 0) for small block sizes, indicating near-identical representations. Figure 2-d confirms via MMLU performance drop that removing layers 25-30 causes negligible accuracy loss (flat line near zero), while removing layers 0-5 causes catastrophic degradation (sharp drops). For BERT-large (Post-LN), Figure 2-a shows the opposite: the first half of layers (especially layers 3-4 and 9-11) exhibit small angular distances, while deeper layers show larger distances. Figure 2-c shows that removing early layers (0-5) causes minimal SQuAD performance change, with layers 2-3 removal producing a slightly *positive* `ΔP` (performance improvement), while removing deep layers causes substantial degradation.

**In-house controlled models (Figure 3):** For LLaMA-130M trained with Pre-LN only vs. Post-LN only, with all other variables controlled:

- **Angular distance (Figure 3a-b):** Post-LN shows concentrated low-distance regions (yellow) in early layers (0-3), with layers becoming more distinctive (purple) at depth. Pre-LN shows gradually decreasing distances with depth, with the deepest layers (8-11) showing the highest similarity — the exact inverse pattern.
- **Performance drop (Figure 3c-d):** Post-LN: removing layers 0-7 causes near-zero ARC-e performance change (flat line near 0); removing layers 9-11 causes large drops (sharp negative slopes). Pre-LN: removing layers 0-1 causes large drops; removing layers 2-11 causes minimal change (near-zero `ΔP`). This directly mirrors the angular distance patterns.
- **Gradient norm at initialization (Figure 3e):** Post-LN shows an increasing gradient norm with depth (near zero at layer 0, highest at layer 11); Pre-LN shows a decreasing profile (highest at layer 0, lowest at layer 11). This is the most direct causal evidence: the gradient pattern at initialization predicts which layers will be effective after training, consistent with the paper's mechanistic argument.

The strength of this evidence is that it **triangulates** across model families (BERT vs. LLaMA), scale (7B vs. 130M), and measurement modalities (correlational angular distance, causal performance drop, mechanistic gradient norm). The consistent pattern — Post-LN concentrates effectiveness in deep layers, Pre-LN in early layers — across both open-weight and controlled comparisons makes a compelling case that normalization placement is the causal factor.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

- **Post-LN ratio α sweep (Table 7):** Sweeping `α` over `{0, 0.167, 0.25, 0.33, 0.417, 0.5, 1.0}` on LLaMA-1B trained on C4 reveals a U-shaped perplexity curve, with the minimum at `α = 0.25` (perplexity 18.18). Pure Pre-LN (`α = 0`) achieves 18.65, pure Post-LN (`α = 1.0`) diverges to 1434, and all intermediate values between 0.167 and 0.5 outperform Pre-LN (range 18.34 to 18.86), demonstrating that the hybrid approach is robust to an interval of `α` values and not a brittle optimum. The U-shape confirms the predicted trade-off: too little Post-LN (0.167) provides only marginal benefit (18.34 vs. 18.65), while too much (≥0.5) introduces sufficient vanishing-gradient pressure to degrade performance below Pre-LN. This ablation is the central evidence that the spatial partitioning principle is sound and that the benefit is not simply from adding any amount of Post-LN.

- **Comparison against other normalization methods (Table 8):** On LLaMA-250M, Mix-LN (22.33 perplexity) is compared against Admin (24.82), Group-LN (23.10), Sandwich-LN (23.26), and Pre-LN (23.39). All alternative methods underperform Mix-LN by at least 0.77 perplexity points, and Admin performs worse than Pre-LN. This demonstrates that recently proposed normalization variants — each applying a uniform strategy across all layers — cannot match Mix-LN's depth-conditioned approach, even though some (Group-LN, Sandwich-LN) slightly improve over Pre-LN. Note: the Pre-LN perplexity here (23.39) differs from Table 1 (21.92) for LLaMA-250M, which the paper does not explain. This may reflect different training configurations or a different training duration, but the discrepancy is not discussed.

- **Mix-LN angular distance compared to Pre-LN and Post-LN (Figure 5):** Evaluating LLaMA-130M models, Figure 5 shows that Mix-LN produces angular distances that are more uniform across layers — darker colors (larger distances) throughout most of the matrix, with the exception of the final two layers which show slightly higher similarity. This confirms the gradient norm result quantitatively: Mix-LN's layers produce more diverse representations than Pre-LN (which shows concentrated low-distance regions in deep layers) and Post-LN (which shows concentrated low-distance regions in early layers). The exception in the final two layers is consistent with the expectation that even under Mix-LN, the very last Pre-LN layers may still experience some gradient diminishment, though substantially less than under pure Pre-LN.

- **Mix-LN layer gradient norm (Figure 6a):** Measured at initialization for LLaMA-250M, Mix-LN's gradient norm profile is visibly more balanced than either Pre-LN (decreasing with depth) or Post-LN (increasing with depth). The profile shows intermediate values in early layers (higher than Post-LN's near-zero, lower than Pre-LN's peak), rising through the Post-LN region, peaking around the transition (layer index ~5-6 for `α = 0.25` with 24 layers), and declining only modestly through the Pre-LN region. This is the most direct mechanistic validation: Mix-LN achieves its goal of producing more uniform gradient norms.

- **Mix-LN performance drop across layers (Figure 6b):** Comparing `ΔP` for layer pruning on LLaMA-130M across Pre-LN, Post-LN, and Mix-LN, Figure 6b shows that Mix-LN's deeper layers exhibit larger performance drops than Pre-LN's (indicating they have become more important), while its early layers maintain substantial importance (unlike Post-LN, where early layers show near-zero `ΔP`). The profile is intermediate between Pre-LN and Post-LN, which is exactly what the design intends: neither extreme (only early layers matter or only deep layers matter) but a more balanced distribution of layer importance. However, the `ΔP` values for Mix-LN are not as high in deep layers as Post-LN's — the paper achieves "more balanced" rather than "maximally deep-effective," reflecting the trade-off inherent in the hybrid.

- **Compatibility with stabilization techniques (Table 10, Appendix B):** The paper evaluates Mix-LN's interaction with Scaled Initialization (Nguyen & Salazar, 2019) and Scaled Embed (Takase et al., 2023), two techniques designed to stabilize Post-LN training. On LLaMA-130M: Mix-LN without either technique achieves 29.95 perplexity vs. Pre-LN's 32.18 (a 2.23-point gap). Adding Scaled Initialization improves both, with Mix-LN achieving 29.77 vs. Pre-LN's 30.63 (a 0.86-point gap). Adding both Scaled Initialization and Scaled Embed degrades both: Mix-LN achieves 31.19 vs. Pre-LN's 31.28 (a 0.09-point gap). The key finding is that **Scaled Embed degrades performance** when combined with either normalization, and the Mix-LN advantage narrows substantially (from 2.23 to 0.09 points) when both techniques are applied. This suggests Mix-LN's benefits are partially redundant with Scaled Initialization (both address gradient flow in deep layers) and that the combination of stabilization techniques with Mix-LN is non-additive and potentially antagonistic. The result is a practical caveat: practitioners using Mix-LN may not need additional stabilization techniques, and adding them indiscriminately can erode Mix-LN's benefits.

- **Scaling behavior of competing methods (Table 1):** Post-LN diverges at 250M (perplexity 1409.09) and 1B (1411.54). DeepNorm trains successfully at 71M (34.87), 130M (27.17), and 250M (22.77), but diverges at 1B (1410.94). This establishes that Mix-LN (stable up to at least 7B with appropriate `α`) is more robust than DeepNorm at scale, which is notable because DeepNorm was specifically designed for deep transformer training. The failure at 1B with only 24 layers but larger hidden dimension (2048) suggests width, not just depth, drives Post-LN instability — a finding that challenges the depth-centric framing of prior Post-LN stabilization work.

- **7B sensitivity to α (Section 4.2, Figure 4):** The requirement of `α = 0.0625` (rather than the 0.25 used at smaller scales) for stable 7B training is both an ablation (showing `α` sensitivity) and a limitation. The paper provides no sweep over `α` at 7B, so we cannot determine whether 0.0625 is optimal, whether larger values fail entirely, and what the performance gradient looks like. The paper also notes that "a longer warm-up period" is required but does not specify the warm-up duration used or compare it to the smaller-scale warm-up.

### Critical Assessment

The experimental evidence supports the paper's claims with several important qualifications, and certain claims are supported more strongly than others.

#### Claim 1: Pre-LN is the root cause of deep-layer ineffectiveness in LLMs.

This claim is **well-supported** by the converging evidence in Section 2. The open-weight comparison (Figure 2) shows that the Pre-LN model (LLaMA2-7B) concentrates ineffectiveness in deep layers while the Post-LN model (BERT-large) concentrates it in early layers. The controlled in-house comparison (Figure 3) isolates normalization as the causal factor and confirms the pattern. The gradient norm measurements (Figures 3e, 6a) provide the mechanistic link — showing that the gradient patterns at initialization predict which layers will be ineffective after training. The angular distance and performance drop metrics are complementary (one correlational, one causal) and yield consistent results.

**However**, the open-weight comparison is not perfectly controlled. BERT-large and LLaMA2-7B differ in architecture (encoder-only vs. decoder-only), training objective (masked language modeling vs. autoregressive next-token prediction), training data, activation functions, and context length. The fact that they show opposite layer effectiveness profiles is consistent with the normalization explanation, but it does not rule out contributions from these other factors. The controlled in-house experiment addresses this concern and provides the cleaner evidence.

A more subtle qualification: the paper demonstrates that Post-LN produces effective deep layers *in small models* (130M) and that Pre-LN produces ineffective deep layers *across scales*. It does not demonstrate that Post-LN would produce effective deep layers at 7B or larger — Post-LN diverges at 250M and above under the paper's training configuration. This leaves open the possibility that making deep layers effective at scale requires something beyond what Post-LN provides, and that Mix-LN's mechanism at 7B (with its reduced `α = 0.0625`) is less about Post-LN's gradient boost and more about some other effect of the hybrid that has not been fully characterized.

#### Claim 2: Mix-LN consistently outperforms Pre-LN and Post-LN in pre-training.

This claim is **supported with qualifications**. Table 1 shows consistent perplexity improvements over Pre-LN across 71M, 130M, 250M, and 1B models, with gains ranging from 0.47 to 1.65 points. Figure 4 extends this to 7B with partial training. The `α` sweep (Table 7) shows the improvement is robust to a range of `α` values, not a brittle optimum.

**Qualifications:**

1. **No variance estimates.** Every perplexity in Table 1 is a single number from a single training run. LLM pre-training at these scales exhibits non-trivial run-to-run variance (from random initialization, data ordering, and hardware non-determinism). Without confidence intervals, we cannot assess whether the 0.47-point improvement at 1B (18.18 vs. 18.65) is statistically significant or within the noise floor. The consistent direction across four model sizes is reassuring, but the paper's main claim would be stronger with even two seeds per configuration.

2. **The 7B experiment is incomplete.** Training was stopped at 13,000 steps (~1.7B tokens) due to computational constraints, which is far from convergence for a 7B model. The standard for full 7B training runs is hundreds of billions of tokens (LLaMA 7B was trained on 1T tokens). We cannot conclude from Figure 4 that Mix-LN's benefit persists at convergence or that the optimal `α` at full training scale is 0.0625. The paper acknowledges this constraint but the partial result is presented as supporting evidence for scaling, which is reasonable only if qualified.

3. **The `α` transfer problem.** The paper uses `α = 0.25` for all models 71M-1B based on a sweep at 1B, but then uses `α = 0.0625` at 7B. This demonstrates that `α` is scale-dependent in a way the paper has not characterized. A practitioner wanting to use Mix-LN at 3B or 13B parameters would have no guidance on choosing `α` except to run their own sweep at the target scale. The paper's claim that Mix-LN "consistently outperforms" would be more actionable if it included a scaling trend for `α`.

4. **Perplexity gains diminish with scale.** The improvement shrinks from 1.65 at 71M to 0.47 at 1B. If this trend continues, the benefit at 7B might be negligible after full training. The paper does not discuss this trend or what might cause it.

#### Claim 3: Mix-LN models learn better during supervised fine-tuning and RLHF.

This claim is **supported with thin but suggestive evidence**. Table 4 shows downstream accuracy gains for Mix-LN across seven tasks at two model sizes, with average improvements of 4.41 points (250M) and 1.65 points (1B). Table 5 shows a large RLHF reward improvement (1.32 vs. 0.75) for the 1B model.

**Qualifications:**

1. **Single fine-tuning run per configuration.** As with pre-training, the SFT results are from single runs. Fine-tuning on Commonsense170K with these small models is more stable than pre-training, but there is still variance from data ordering and optimization stochasticity. The paper does not report standard deviations or confidence intervals.

2. **The RLHF results are minimally reported.** Table 5 provides a single number ("Final Reward") with no experimental detail — no reward model architecture, no training hyperparameters, no KL penalty coefficient, no win rates, no reward curves over training. A 76% improvement in reward is extraordinary, and the paper provides essentially no information that would allow a reader to assess whether this is a robust finding or an artifact of a particular RLHF configuration. The claim that Mix-LN benefits RLHF is therefore **suggestive but not yet convincing** — it needs replication with more detail and ideally across multiple RLHF recipes.

3. **No SFT data scaling experiments.** The paper fine-tunes on a fixed dataset (Commonsense170K) and does not vary the amount of fine-tuning data. It is possible that Pre-LN models would catch up to Mix-LN given more fine-tuning data, or that the Mix-LN advantage is largest in low-data regimes. Without data scaling curves, we cannot determine whether Mix-LN provides a fundamental representational advantage or simply converges faster during fine-tuning.

4. **Task selection is narrow.** The seven benchmarks are all multiple-choice commonsense reasoning tasks. The paper does not evaluate on generation tasks (summarization, translation), code tasks, or open-ended reasoning, which limits the generality of "learns better during fine-tuning." BoolQ, where the largest gain is observed, is a binary question-answering task that may be particularly sensitive to specific features that Pre-LN's deep layers fail to encode — results on a broader task distribution would clarify whether the benefit generalizes.

#### Claim 4: Mix-LN promotes more balanced, healthier gradient norms and more diverse representations.

This claim is **well-supported** by the measurements in Figures 5 and 6. Figure 6a directly shows Mix-LN's gradient norm profile is more uniform than either pure form. Figure 5 shows that Mix-LN's angular distances are larger (more diverse) across most of the layer-depth matrix compared to Pre-LN and Post-LN. Figure 6b shows a more balanced performance-drop profile. The evidence is consistent and directly measured.

**Qualification:** These measurements are at initialization (Figure 6a) or after training on small models (Figures 5, 6b). The gradient norm balance at initialization is a necessary condition for the paper's mechanism but does not guarantee that the balance persists throughout training. Gradient norms evolve during training as the network adapts, and the paper does not show gradient norm trajectories over the course of training. It is possible that Mix-LN's initial balance degrades during training, or that Pre-LN's imbalance partially corrects itself — the paper provides only the initialization snapshot.

#### Missing experiments that would strengthen the paper:

1. **Multiple training seeds** for at least one model size (e.g., LLaMA-250M) to quantify run-to-run variance and establish statistical significance of the perplexity differences.

2. **Full-training 7B results.** The partial 7B run demonstrates scalability potential but does not establish it. A fully-trained 7B comparison (or even a 3B comparison with more training tokens) would substantially strengthen the scaling claim.

3. **An `α` sweep at 7B** (or at least a few values around 0.0625) to characterize the sensitivity and establish whether the 1B-optimal `α = 0.25` genuinely fails at 7B or was simply not tried.

4. **Training curves for Table 1 models.** The paper reports only final perplexities, not learning curves. This prevents assessing whether Mix-LN converges faster (same perplexity with fewer tokens), achieves a lower asymptote, or both. Training curves are standard in LLM scaling papers and their absence limits analysis.

5. **A direct combination of Mix-LN with deeper architectures.** The paper uses standard LLaMA depths (12, 24, 32 layers). Since the gradient attenuation problem worsens with depth, Mix-LN's benefit should theoretically increase for deeper networks. Testing on a 48-layer or 64-layer model would test this prediction and potentially show larger gains.

6. **Ablation on the Post-LN-first vs. Post-LN-last ordering.** The paper argues Post-LN must be placed in early layers based on the gradient analysis, but does not empirically compare this against placing Post-LN in late layers (or interleaving Post-LN and Pre-LN). This would directly test the mechanistic claim and potentially reveal whether the ordering matters as much as the analysis predicts.

7. **Evaluation on non-multiple-choice downstream tasks.** Fine-tuning results on generation tasks (e.g., summarization on CNN/DailyMail, translation) would establish whether the downstream benefit generalizes beyond classification-style benchmarks.

#### Bottom line on experimental strength:

The paper's core diagnosis — that Pre-LN causes deep-layer ineffectiveness via gradient attenuation — is convincingly established through converging evidence across model scales, families, and measurement modalities. The proposed fix, Mix-LN, demonstrates consistent pre-training improvements at scales where it is fully tested (71M to 1B), with promising but incomplete evidence at 7B. The downstream benefits (SFT, RLHF) are directionally consistent but reported with insufficient detail for the RLHF results in particular. The main experimental weaknesses are the absence of variance estimates, the incomplete 7B experiment, and the thin reporting on RLHF. None of these undermine the central claims, but they do mean that the paper's results should be interpreted as establishing *existence* and *direction* of benefits rather than providing precise effect-size estimates that practitioners can rely on for cost-benefit calculations at production scale.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The α Hyperparameter Does Not Transfer Across Scales and Must Be Re-Tuned

**The assumption or constraint.** Mix-LN introduces a single hyperparameter `α` that controls the fraction of layers using Post-LN. The paper's core empirical finding is that `α = 0.25` works optimally across model sizes from 71M to 1B, as established by a sweep on LLaMA-1B (Table 7) and applied uniformly to all smaller models (Section 4.1). However, this transfer fails at the 7B scale. The paper explicitly acknowledges in Section 4.2:

> "Mix-LN becomes more sensitive to α when scaling up to 7B training. In general, smaller values of α and a longer warm-up period are required to stabilize the training of Mix-LN at a larger scale."

The 7B experiment required `α = 0.0625` (2 out of 32 layers, rather than the 8 layers that `α = 0.25` would prescribe), and the authors conjecture that "this instability is relevant to Post-LN, as using Post-LN alone also leads to the same issue."

**The consequence.** A practitioner wanting to deploy Mix-LN at a new scale (say, 3B, 13B, or 70B parameters) cannot use the `α = 0.25` value established in the paper — it may cause training instability at larger scales. Instead, they must conduct their own `α` sweep at the target scale, which requires training multiple large models (or at least running partial training runs to detect divergence). The paper provides only two data points for scale-dependent `α` (0.25 for ≤1B, 0.0625 for 7B), which is insufficient to establish a scaling law or even a trend. A practitioner does not know whether the optimal `α` continues to decrease with scale (e.g., to 0.03 at 13B, 0.01 at 70B), plateaus, or follows some other pattern. Given that an `α` sweep at 7B scale could cost tens of thousands of dollars in compute even for partial training runs, this transfer problem represents a non-trivial adoption tax.

The warm-up sensitivity compounds this issue. Mix-LN inherits Post-LN's dependence on learning rate warm-up (Xiong et al., 2020), and the paper notes that "a longer warm-up period" is required at 7B (Section 4.2) but does not specify the warm-up duration used, compare it to the smaller-scale warm-up (10% of training steps, per Appendix A), or provide guidance on how warm-up should scale with model size and `α`. A practitioner must tune both `α` and warm-up jointly, expanding the hyperparameter search space.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 7 establishes the U-shaped `α`-perplexity relationship at 1B, with optimal `α = 0.25`. Section 4.2 reports that 7B training required `α = 0.0625` for stability, but provides no sweep — we do not know whether larger `α` values were attempted and failed, or whether `α = 0.0625` is optimal at 7B. The paper does not include a figure showing `α` sensitivity at 7B comparable to Table 7 at 1B.

**Mitigation status.** The paper explicitly acknowledges this as a limitation in Section 6:

> "One limitation of Mix-LN is the introduction of Post-LN leads to instability issues when scaling up training, i.e., >7B. We conjecture that this instability is relevant to Post-LN, as using Post-LN alone also leads to the same issue. An interesting research direction is to explore how to mitigate the ineffectiveness of later layers without relying on Post-LN or to address the instability associated with Post-LN."

The paper frames this as an open research problem, not a solved issue. No practical guidance is offered for choosing `α` at new scales, though the direction of the effect (smaller `α` at larger scales) is indicated.

### Difficulty Estimation Cost Is Not Accounted For and May Rival the Training Budget for Practice-Relevant Model Sizes

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's central diagnostic methodology in Section 2—and, by extension, the entire justification for Mix-LN's spatial partitioning design—rests on measuring layer effectiveness through angular distance (requiring processing 256K tokens through the full model) and performance drop (requiring training and evaluating multiple pruned model variants). Section 2.2 states:

> "To eliminate the effect of randomness, the angular distance reported in this paper is averaged over 256K tokens from the C4 dataset."

More critically, determining the optimal `α` for a new model scale requires a sweep like Table 7: training multiple full models (or at least running partial training to detect instability) at different `α` values to find the minimum perplexity. At the 1B scale, the sweep involved 7 training runs (α values from 0 to 1.0). At the 7B scale, the paper reports only a single successful run with α = 0.0625; if larger α values were attempted and diverged, each such attempt would have consumed compute before the divergence was detected.

**The consequence.** The cost of *finding* the right α for a given model scale is not included in the paper's efficiency accounting. Table 1 compares Mix-LN against Pre-LN given that α has already been optimized, but the optimization process itself consumes training compute that is not amortized in the reported perplexity numbers. At the 1B scale, the Table 7 sweep represents approximately 7 × 5B = 35B training tokens just to determine α, compared to the 5B tokens used to train the final model. The α-selection cost is 7× the cost of the model whose improvement is being reported. At the 7B scale, if a similar fraction of effort is needed for α selection (even with cheaper partial training runs to detect divergence), the overhead could be substantial.

This is analogous to the unaccounted difficulty-estimation cost in the original example paper's analysis, but with a different mechanism: here, the "difficulty" is the model's sensitivity to the Post-LN/Pre-LN boundary, and "estimating" it requires training models. The paper's headline claim—that Mix-LN improves perplexity by 0.47 points over Pre-LN at 1B (Table 1)—represents the benefit *conditional on already knowing the optimal α*, not the net benefit after paying the search cost. For a practitioner training a single 1B model, running a 7-point α sweep first is prohibitively expensive relative to simply training with Pre-LN and accepting the slightly higher perplexity.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 7 provides the α sweep at 1B. The paper does not report the computational cost of this sweep or discuss how α selection should be amortized across multiple training runs (e.g., if an organization trains many models at a given scale, the sweep cost is paid once and amortized). For the 7B experiment, no sweep is reported and no cost estimate is given. The paper does not frame the α selection process as a limitation or discuss its practical economics.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not propose a cheaper method for selecting α (e.g., a predictive model based on smaller-scale sweeps, a theoretical scaling law for α, or a dynamic α selection strategy during training). The suggestion in Section 6 to "explore how to mitigate the ineffectiveness of later layers without relying on Post-LN" could sidestep the issue by removing the Post-LN component entirely, but no concrete approach is offered.

### All Results Are on a Single Model Family and Predominantly a Single Training Paradigm

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's main experimental results (Tables 1, 4, 5 and Figures 4, 5, 6) use LLaMA-based architectures exclusively, trained on the C4 dataset with RMSNorm and SwiGLU activations. The vision experiments (Table 6) extend to ViT, but with only two model sizes (Tiny and Small) and small absolute gains (0.04 and 0.41 points). The BERT-large comparison in Figure 2 is a diagnostic, not a training result—BERT is evaluated off-the-shelf, not re-trained with Mix-LN. The paper does not address how Mix-LN's dynamics interact with architectural choices that differ from the LLaMA family.

Several architectural features of LLaMA could modulate the gradient attenuation mechanism that Mix-LN exploits:

- **RMSNorm vs. standard LayerNorm.** The paper uses RMSNorm (Zhang & Sennrich, 2019), which omits mean centering and only scales by the root-mean-square of activations. Standard LayerNorm, used in BERT and GPT-2, includes both centering and scaling. The Jacobian analysis in Section 2.1 assumes standard LN with zero-mean inputs (Equation 5 explicitly references the mean-centered form). It is unclear whether the approximation `∂LN(x')/∂x' ≈ (1/σ_x') I` holds identically for RMSNorm, since RMSNorm does not enforce zero mean.
- **SwiGLU vs. standard ReLU/GELU FFNs.** SwiGLU (Shazeer, 2020) introduces a gating mechanism in the feed-forward sublayer that changes its Jacobian structure. The paper's gradient analysis models `F(x)` generically (Equation 3-4) without specifying the sublayer type, but the actual `∂F(x)/∂x` term differs between a simple ReLU MLP and a gated SwiGLU. It is theoretically possible that SwiGLU's gating amplifies or dampens the gradient attenuation effect.
- **Parallel vs. sequential attention/FFN.** The LLaMA architecture applies attention and FFN sequentially within each layer. Architectures that apply them in parallel (Wang et al., 2019; some efficient transformer variants) would have a different residual structure, potentially changing how gradients accumulate.
- **Encoder-decoder vs. decoder-only.** BERT-large is encoder-only; LLaMA is decoder-only. The gradient dynamics in encoder-decoder architectures (T5, BART) involve both self-attention and cross-attention gradients, which may interact differently with normalization placement.

**The consequence.** A practitioner using a non-LLaMA architecture—say, a GPT-3-style model with standard LayerNorm and GELU activations, or a T5-style encoder-decoder—cannot assume that `α = 0.25` is optimal or even that Mix-LN will provide any benefit. The ViT results in Table 6 provide only weak evidence of cross-architecture generalization: the gain at ViT-Small (0.41 points) is small enough to be within training noise for a single run, and ViT-Tiny shows essentially no improvement (0.04 points). Two data points with small effects do not establish that the mechanism generalizes meaningfully to vision architectures.

Additionally, all training uses the C4 dataset. C4 is a cleaned web-text corpus. It is possible that the gradient dynamics—and therefore the optimal `α`—depend on data distribution. A model trained on code (which has different statistical properties, more repetitive structure, and longer dependency chains) or on multilingual data (with different token frequency distributions) might exhibit different gradient norm profiles across layers, shifting the optimal Post-LN/Pre-LN boundary.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The LLaMA architecture and C4 dataset are used in all training experiments (Tables 1, 4, 5, 7, 8, 10). BERT-large is evaluated only for layer effectiveness diagnostics (Figure 2a,c), not re-trained. ViT results (Table 6) provide the only cross-architecture evidence, and the gains are modest. The paper does not discuss how architectural choices (RMSNorm, SwiGLU, decoder-only design) might interact with its gradient mechanism.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not discuss architecture dependence, does not test on non-LLaMA language models, and does not train on datasets other than C4. Section 8 ("future work") does not mention cross-architecture or cross-data generalization. The paper's strong claims about Mix-LN's generality (e.g., "making it an ideal choice for large-scale LLMs" in Section 4.1) are not supported by evidence beyond the LLaMA family.

### The RLHF Evidence Is Too Sparse to Support Its Weight in the Paper's Claims

**The assumption or constraint.** Section 4.4 presents Mix-LN's RLHF results in a single-sentence paragraph and Table 5, which contains two numbers: Pre-LN achieves final reward 0.75, Mix-LN achieves 1.32. The paper states:

> "Table 5 illustrates that Mix-LN achieves a notable reward gain (higher is better) over Pre-LN, i.e., 1.32 vs. 0.75."

That is the entirety of the RLHF experimental description. The paper does not report:
- The reward model architecture or training procedure.
- The KL penalty coefficient governing deviation from the reference policy.
- The number of RLHF training steps or total tokens processed.
- Win rates against a baseline or human preference judgments.
- Reward curves over the course of RL training (is Mix-LN improving faster, reaching a higher asymptote, or both?).
- The policy's downstream task performance after RLHF (does the higher reward translate to better outputs, or is the policy exploiting the reward model?).
- Multiple runs or any measure of variance.

The RLHF result appears to be a single training run per normalization variant, following the InstructGPT workflow (Ouyang et al., 2022) using the Adam-mini optimizer (Zhang et al., 2024) on the UltraFeedback dataset—but even these details are specified only by citation, not by description.

**The consequence.** A 76% improvement in final reward (from 0.75 to 1.32) is an extraordinarily large effect for a normalization change, especially given that the pre-training perplexity improvement at this scale is only 2.5% (18.18 vs. 18.65, Table 1). Large-magnitude results with minimal reporting are a red flag in the RLHF literature, where reward model exploitation, KL divergence from the reference policy, and training instability are well-documented failure modes (Gao et al., 2023; Casper et al., 2023). Without KL divergence metrics, we cannot determine whether Mix-LN's higher reward comes from learning a genuinely better policy or from drifting further from the reference model in ways that produce high-reward but low-quality outputs (reward hacking). Without downstream task evaluation after RLHF, we cannot verify that the higher reward translates to a practically better model.

The paper's claim that "the benefits of Mix-LN can be seamlessly transferred to RLHF" (Section 4.4) and the conclusion's statement that Mix-LN "demonstrate[s] superior learning during... RLHF" (Section 7) rest entirely on this single, minimally-reported result. Given the practical importance of RLHF in the LLM deployment pipeline—and the paper's argument that Mix-LN matters precisely because it improves downstream alignment, not just pre-training—this evidentiary gap is significant.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 5 reports two numbers (0.75 and 1.32) with no supporting detail. The paper's Appendices provide no additional RLHF information. The training configuration tables in Appendix A (Table 9) cover pre-training only.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper treats the RLHF result as confirmatory evidence without acknowledging its sparseness. The limitation section (Section 6) discusses training instability at scale but not the RLHF experimental validity. A reader evaluating Mix-LN for adoption in an RLHF pipeline would need to independently replicate this result with proper metrics before trusting it.

### Hard Problems in the Gradient Landscape: Post-LN Instability Is Not Solved, Only Postponed

**The assumption or constraint.** Mix-LN's central design trade-off is that it reduces, but does not eliminate, the instability associated with Post-LN. The paper's analysis (Section 2.1) shows that Post-LN's gradient attenuation chain of length `L` causes early-layer vanishing gradients. Mix-LN truncates this chain to length `αL`, which dramatically reduces the attenuation for `α = 0.25` (from 24 factors to 6 at 1B scale). However, the attenuation is still present—the Post-LN prefix layers still experience cumulative gradient down-scaling, and the earliest of those layers still receive gradients attenuated by `αL` LN Jacobians.

The empirical evidence reveals that this residual attenuation becomes problematic as model scale increases. At 1B with `α = 0.25` (Table 7), `α = 0.5` (12 Post-LN layers) already underperforms Pre-LN (18.86 vs. 18.65). At 7B, `α = 0.25` (8 Post-LN layers) was apparently unstable—the paper had to reduce to `α = 0.0625` (2 layers) for stable training. The trend suggests that the maximum tolerable Post-LN prefix length does not scale proportionally with total depth; it may actually shrink as a fraction of total layers as the model grows larger.

**The consequence.** There may exist a scale at which even `α` approaching zero—a single Post-LN layer—causes instability, meaning Mix-LN would offer no benefit over Pre-LN at that scale because the Post-LN component would have to be eliminated entirely. The paper does not characterize whether such a scale exists within the range of current frontier models (70B to 400B+ parameters). If the tolerable `α` continues to shrink with scale, Mix-LN's benefit becomes progressively smaller: at `α = 0.0625` (7B), only 6.25% of layers use Post-LN, providing only a modest gradient boost to deep layers compared to pure Pre-LN. At `α = 0.01` (hypothetical for a 100B model), the benefit would be negligible—essentially Pre-LN with a rounding error.

This is a **fundamental, not incidental, limitation** of the Mix-LN approach: it does not solve the underlying Post-LN instability problem; it only mitigates it by reducing the fraction of layers affected. The Post-LN instability mechanism (gradient attenuation accumulating over depth) remains active in the prefix region, and its severity appears to increase with model scale. The paper's framing—that Mix-LN "resolves" or "addresses" the gradient imbalance—is accurate at tested scales but the resolution may not extend to arbitrarily large models.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The `α` sweep (Table 7) shows performance degradation beginning at `α = 0.33` at 1B scale (`αL = 8` Post-LN layers). The 7B experiment (Section 4.2) required `α = 0.0625` (`αL = 2` layers) for stability, implying that even moderate Post-LN prefixes are unstable at 7B. The paper does not test whether `α = 0.125` (4 layers) or `α = 0.25` (8 layers) were attempted and failed at 7B, nor does it characterize the divergence boundary. DeepNorm's failure at 1B (Table 1, perplexity 1410.94) further demonstrates that Post-LN stabilization techniques do not scale trivially to wider models.

**Mitigation status.** Partially acknowledged. The paper's limitation section (Section 6) identifies "instability issues when scaling up training, i.e., >7B" and suggests "explor[ing] how to mitigate the ineffectiveness of later layers without relying on Post-LN or to address the instability associated with Post-LN" as future work. This correctly identifies the problem but frames it as a scaling challenge rather than a fundamental bound. The paper does not discuss whether the α → 0 trend implies a hard ceiling on Mix-LN's applicability, nor does it propose concrete alternatives (e.g., a Post-LN replacement that provides deep-layer gradient boost without the attenuation mechanism, or a dynamic `α` that adapts during training).

### Training Curves and Variance Estimates Are Absent, Limiting Causal Interpretation

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper reports final perplexity values for pre-training experiments (Table 1) as single numbers from single training runs, without training curves, confidence intervals, standard deviations, or multiple seeds. The only training curve shown is the partial 7B experiment (Figure 4). For the downstream experiments (SFT in Table 4, RLHF in Table 5, vision in Table 6), each number is also from a single run with no variance estimate.

Single-run reporting is standard in LLM pre-training papers at large scales due to computational cost, but the models studied here are small by modern standards: the largest fully-trained model is 1B parameters trained on 5B tokens, which can be trained on a single GPU in hours to days. At this scale, running even 2–3 seeds for at least one model size (e.g., LLaMA-250M or LLaMA-1B) would be feasible and would provide critical information about signal-to-noise ratio. The paper's `α = 0.25` sweep (Table 7) shows perplexity differences as small as 0.07 between adjacent `α` values (18.41 at `α = 0.33` vs. 18.34 at `α = 0.167`). Without variance estimates, a reader cannot determine whether these differences are statistically meaningful or within the noise floor of LLM pre-training at this scale.

The absence of training curves is equally important. The paper claims that Mix-LN produces "healthier gradient norms" (Figure 6a) and "more balanced training" (Section 5), but without training curves showing that Mix-LN models converge faster or to a lower asymptote, the connection between initial gradient balance (measured at initialization) and training dynamics (which evolve over thousands of steps) is asserted rather than demonstrated. It is possible that Pre-LN models catch up to Mix-LN given more training tokens, or that the gradient balance degrades during training such that the final models are not as different as the initialization measurements suggest.

**The consequence.** The reported perplexity gaps—1.65 at 71M, 0.71 at 130M, 0.53 at 250M, 0.47 at 1B (Table 1)—should be interpreted as point estimates with unknown confidence intervals. A practitioner basing a decision on a 0.47 perplexity improvement at 1B (a ~2.5% relative reduction) needs to know whether that gap is reliably achievable or within the range of what different random seeds would produce. The diminishing gap with scale (1.65 → 0.47 across model sizes) could reflect a genuine trend or could be partially attributable to noise at smaller scales inflating the apparent benefit.

For the SFT results (Table 4), the lack of variance is particularly relevant for the BoolQ result at 250M: a 17.77 percentage point improvement (38.35 → 56.12) is an outlier-sized effect, and without knowing whether this is a stable finding or a single-run artifact, a reader cannot assess whether BoolQ's sensitivity to deep-layer quality is a robust phenomenon or a chance result.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 1 reports single perplexity numbers for each model size and normalization variant. Figure 4 shows a single training curve for 7B. No standard deviations, confidence intervals, or multi-seed results appear anywhere in the paper or appendices. Training configurations in Appendix A (Table 9) specify hyperparameters but do not mention seeds or reproducibility measures.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not discuss variance, run-to-run noise, or the statistical significance of its results. The limitation section (Section 6) focuses exclusively on scaling instability and does not mention experimental methodology. This is a gap in the paper's rigor rather than a limitation of the method itself, but it directly affects how strongly a practitioner can rely on the reported effect sizes for cost-benefit decisions.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

Mix-LN reorients the conversation around deep-layer redundancy in LLMs from a compression opportunity to a **diagnosable and fixable training pathology**. This is less a paradigm shift than a **reframing with a concrete prescription** — the paper does not introduce fundamentally new normalization mathematics, but it establishes a causal mechanism (gradient attenuation via LN Jacobian accumulation) that explains a widely observed phenomenon and provides a deployable fix whose cost is negligible relative to the training budget it optimizes.

The conceptual shift is this: prior work asked "how much can we remove without hurting performance?", implicitly accepting that deep layers are less valuable. This paper asks "why are deep layers underperforming, and can we make them useful?" — a question that leads not to pruning but to a training design change that recovers the capacity those parameters always represented. The evidence that this reframing is correct comes from the controlled experiment in Figure 3: the *same architecture* trained with Post-LN instead of Pre-LN inverts the layer-effectiveness profile entirely, proving that which layers matter is a consequence of normalization placement, not an architectural constant.

The paper also resolves a latent tension in the literature that the authors do not explicitly highlight. Two separate empirical literatures had developed — one showing that Pre-LN models (GPT, LLaMA) have redundant deep layers (Gromov et al., 2024; Men et al., 2024; Yin et al., 2023), and another showing that Post-LN models (original Transformer, BERT) have shallow-layer instability (Xiong et al., 2020; Nguyen & Salazar, 2019; Takase et al., 2022). These were treated as unrelated facts about different architectures. Mix-LN's gradient analysis unifies them: both are manifestations of the same LN Jacobian attenuation mechanism, operating in opposite spatial directions. Pre-LN pushes the attenuation toward the output; Post-LN pushes it toward the input. The "ineffectiveness" is always present; only its depth-location changes.

Several research directions become more attractive as a result:

- **Depth-conditioned architectural design** is now empirically validated as a design principle. Rather than treating all layers identically, the finding that different depth regions have fundamentally different gradient dynamics suggests that other architectural hyperparameters (learning rates, initialization scales, dropout rates) might also benefit from depth-dependent scheduling. Mix-LN's `α` hyperparameter is the first example of a depth-partitioning design choice with demonstrated benefits; the principle generalizes.
- **Gradient-flow-aware training recipes** become a priority. The paper shows that initial gradient norm profiles (Figure 6a) predict trained-layer effectiveness, suggesting that monitoring gradient norms by depth during early training could serve as a diagnostic for whether deep layers will ultimately contribute. A training run where deep-layer gradient norms collapse early might be restarted with different normalization before wasting a full training budget.
- **Post-LN stabilization techniques** remain relevant but are now reframed as ways to *extend* Mix-LN's Post-LN prefix depth at larger scales, rather than as standalone alternatives to Pre-LN. The paper's finding that DeepNorm diverges at 1B (Table 1) while Mix-LN trains successfully suggests that truncating Post-LN depth is more robust than modifying Post-LN's internal mechanics — but combining the approaches (DeepNorm-style residual scaling *within* the Mix-LN Post-LN prefix) might enable larger `α` at scale.
- **Large-scale architecture ablation studies** that previously treated normalization as a binary choice (Pre-LN vs. Post-LN) now need to consider the continuous `α` dimension. Scaling law analyses (Hoffmann et al., 2022; Kaplan et al., 2020) that fit power laws to model size, data, and compute should include normalization strategy as a variable — Mix-LN may shift the compute-optimal frontier, and `α` may itself follow a scaling relationship with model size.

Directions that become *less* attractive include: pure layer-pruning as a primary response to deep-layer redundancy (the paper argues we should fix the training, not prune the parameters); and new uniform normalization variants (Table 8 shows Admin, Group-LN, and Sandwich-LN all underperform Mix-LN, suggesting that spatial partitioning is more impactful than the specific form of the normalization operation).

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**1. An `α` scaling law across model size.** The paper provides two data points for scale-dependent `α`: 0.25 works for 1B and below, 0.0625 is required for stable 7B training. A critical practical question is whether the optimal (or maximum stable) `α` follows a predictable scaling trend — e.g., α_opt ∝ N^(-β) for some exponent β, where N is parameter count — that would let practitioners choose `α` without expensive sweeps at the target scale. A strong follow-up would train LLaMA models at 250M, 500M, 1B, 3B, and 7B (or use Chinchilla-optimal token budgets at each scale for efficiency), sweeping `α` at each scale to map the stability boundary and the optimal-perplexity `α` as functions of model width, depth, and total parameters. The key measurement is whether the divergence threshold (maximum `α` before perplexity explodes) shrinks to zero at some finite scale, which would define a hard ceiling on Mix-LN's applicability. This experiment would also characterize whether Mix-LN's benefit persists to convergence at 7B, closing the paper's open question from Section 4.2.

**2. Post-LN-first vs. Post-LN-last ablation.** The paper's gradient analysis (Section 2.1) argues that Post-LN must be placed in the *early* layers to truncate the attenuation chain, but this claim is never empirically tested against alternative orderings. A direct ablation would compare three configurations at the 1B scale: (a) Mix-LN with Post-LN in the first `αL` layers (the paper's configuration), (b) Post-LN in the *last* `αL` layers (reversed ordering), and (c) Post-LN and Pre-LN interleaved (alternating every 2 layers). If the gradient analysis is correct, configuration (b) should diverge or perform substantially worse (because the attenuation chain spans nearly `L` layers before reaching early-layer gradients), and (c) should underperform uniform-block Mix-LN (because frequent transitions prevent either regime from establishing its gradient profile). A null result — all orderings perform similarly — would challenge the mechanistic claim and suggest Mix-LN's benefit comes from some other property of the hybrid, such as simply having *some* layers with different normalization dynamics to break symmetry.

**3. Mix-LN combined with Post-LN stabilization techniques.** The paper shows that DeepNorm diverges at 1B while Mix-LN trains successfully (Table 1), and that Mix-LN's maximum stable `α` shrinks at 7B (Section 4.2). This suggests a natural combination: apply DeepNorm's residual scaling (or Admin's adaptive initialization, or B2T's bypass connections) *within the Post-LN prefix region* of Mix-LN, potentially allowing larger `α` at scale without instability. A concrete experiment would train LLaMA-7B with Mix-LN at `α = 0.25` (8 Post-LN layers) combined with DeepNorm-style residual scaling in those 8 layers only, comparing against the paper's `α = 0.0625` (2 layers, no stabilization). If the stabilized 8-layer prefix trains successfully, the larger Post-LN fraction should provide stronger deep-layer gradient boost, and the perplexity gain over Pre-LN should exceed the paper's Figure 4 result. The broader question is whether Post-LN stabilization techniques that failed at scale when applied uniformly (DeepNorm at 1B) become viable when applied to a truncated depth — the attenuation chain is shorter, so stabilization may be easier.

**4. Layer contribution dynamics during fine-tuning and RLHF.** The paper demonstrates that Mix-LN pre-trained models perform better after SFT and RLHF (Tables 4, 5), but provides no analysis of *which layers* are responsible for the downstream gains. Do Mix-LN's deep layers contribute more during fine-tuning than Pre-LN's? Does RLHF particularly depend on deep-layer representations? A strong follow-up would replicate the Figure 6b performance-drop analysis *after* SFT and RLHF — pruning one layer at a time from the fine-tuned or RLHF-optimized model and measuring downstream task degradation. If Mix-LN's deep layers show larger performance drops post-SFT than Pre-LN's, this directly validates the paper's claim that "the superior middle and deeper layers produced by Mix-LN are better equipped to learn during supervised fine-tuning" (Section 4.3). The RLHF analysis would be particularly informative given the sparse reporting in Table 5: if Mix-LN's RLHF reward advantage disappears when deep layers are pruned, that would establish deep-layer representations as the causal mechanism for the reward improvement.

**5. Cross-architecture and cross-data validation.** The paper's results are confined to LLaMA architectures with RMSNorm and SwiGLU, trained on C4. The ViT results (Table 6) provide only weak cross-domain evidence (0.41-point gain at ViT-Small). A comprehensive cross-validation would test Mix-LN on: (a) a GPT-2 or GPT-3 architecture with standard LayerNorm and GELU activations, to verify that the gradient mechanism does not depend on RMSNorm's specific properties (the Jacobian analysis in Section 2.1 assumes standard LN with mean centering, which RMSNorm lacks); (b) an encoder-decoder architecture (T5), where cross-attention gradients may interact differently with normalization placement; (c) a non-English dataset or a code dataset, to test whether data distribution affects the optimal `α`. The critical measurement is whether the U-shaped `α`-perplexity curve (Table 7) replicates with different architectures and data — if the minimum consistently occurs at `α ≈ 0.25` for small models regardless of architecture, Mix-LN is a broadly applicable training recipe; if the optimum shifts substantially, practitioners need architecture-specific guidance.

**6. Dynamic `α` scheduling during training.** Mix-LN uses a fixed `α` throughout training, but the gradient attenuation mechanism depends on `σ_x'`, the standard deviation of LN inputs, which evolves during training (the paper notes that `σ_x'` "gradually grows larger than one during training" — Section 2.1). This suggests that the optimal Post-LN/Pre-LN boundary may shift during training: early in training when `σ_x'` is small and `1/σ_x'` is large, more Post-LN layers might be tolerable; later, as `σ_x'` grows, the attenuation per layer becomes more severe, and the Post-LN region might need to shrink. A dynamic `α` schedule — starting with a larger Post-LN fraction early in training to boost deep-layer gradient initialization, then gradually decreasing `α` to maintain stability as `σ_x'` grows — could capture the benefits of a larger Post-LN region without the late-training instability that forces small `α` at 7B. A concrete experiment would train LLaMA-7B with `α` linearly decaying from 0.25 to 0.0625 over the first 50% of training, comparing against the fixed `α = 0.0625` baseline. The risk is that changing normalization mid-training might disrupt the learned representations, but the potential upside (stronger deep-layer gradients during the critical early-training phase) could outweigh the disruption.

**7. Post-LN replacement with a gradient-preserving alternative.** The paper's limitation section identifies the core challenge: "explore how to mitigate the ineffectiveness of later layers without relying on Post-LN or to address the instability associated with Post-LN" (Section 6). This suggests a direct engineering target: design a normalization placement that provides Post-LN's deep-layer gradient boost (by scaling the residual branch's gradient) without Post-LN's multiplicative attenuation chain. One candidate approach is a **scaled residual connection** that amplifies the gradient contribution from the LN-scaled term without placing LN after the residual addition — effectively, `x + γ * F(LN(x))` with `γ > 1` for layers where you want stronger gradients. This is Pre-LN with an amplified sublayer contribution, preserving the identity gradient highway while increasing the sublayer's learning signal. A sweep over `γ` as a function of depth (larger `γ` for deeper layers) could approximate Mix-LN's gradient profile without any Post-LN layers, potentially eliminating the `α`-sensitivity problem at scale entirely. The experiment would compare this "scaled Pre-LN" against Mix-LN at 7B, measuring whether it achieves similar deep-layer effectiveness (via angular distance and performance drop) with better training stability.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**1. Cost-efficient pre-training of small-to-medium LLMs (up to ~1B parameters).** For organizations training custom LLMs at the 100M to 1B scale — a common range for domain-specific models, on-device deployment, or academic research — Mix-LN with `α = 0.25` provides a **drop-in perplexity improvement of 0.47 to 1.65 points over Pre-LN** (Table 1) with no additional training cost and a trivial implementation change (modifying which layers apply LN before vs. after the residual connection). The `α` sweep cost (Table 7) is amortized if multiple models are trained at the same scale, but even for a single 1B model, the 0.47 perplexity gain may justify running a few partial-training `α` probes if the model will be deployed in a performance-sensitive application. The supervised fine-tuning gains (Table 4) provide additional downstream value: the 4.41-point average accuracy improvement at 250M and 1.65-point improvement at 1B mean that a Mix-LN pre-trained model is a stronger foundation for task-specific adaptation, particularly on tasks like BoolQ where the gain is disproportionate (17.77 points at 250M). For teams already using the LLaMA architecture (or similar), adopting Mix-LN is a low-risk, low-effort change with measurable upside.

**2. Improving alignment outcomes in instruction-tuned and RLHF-optimized models.** The paper's downstream results (Tables 4, 5) suggest that Mix-LN's benefits compound through the alignment pipeline: pre-training gains are small (~2.5% perplexity reduction at 1B), but SFT gains are larger (~3.8% average accuracy improvement at 1B), and the single RLHF result shows a 76% reward improvement (0.75 → 1.32). Organizations investing heavily in RLHF — which is expensive, requires human preference data, and is sensitive to reward model quality — should care about the representational quality of their base model's deep layers. If Mix-LN's deep layers provide richer features for the policy to exploit during RL optimization, the same RLHF budget (preference data, training compute) may yield a substantially better final model. The concrete decision rule: for any project planning to apply RLHF at scale, pre-training the base model with Mix-LN is a relatively cheap insurance policy against representational bottlenecks during alignment. The key caveat is that the RLHF result needs replication with proper metrics (KL divergence, win rates, downstream evaluation) before it can inform production decisions — the paper's Table 5 is too sparse to act on directly.

**3. On-device or edge-deployed models where parameter efficiency is critical.** In settings where model size is constrained by hardware (mobile devices, embedded systems, browser-based inference), every parameter must justify its memory and compute cost. The paper's central finding — that Pre-LN's deep layers are effectively wasted parameters — is particularly damaging in these regimes. If 25-30% of a deployed model's parameters contribute near-zero to output quality (as Figures 2d and 3d suggest for Pre-LN), those parameters are consuming memory bandwidth, cache space, and inference FLOPs for negligible benefit. Mix-LN addresses this not by removing parameters (pruning) but by making them useful — the same parameter budget produces higher-quality outputs. For a 1B-parameter on-device model, the 0.47 perplexity improvement and 1.65-point downstream accuracy gain come at **zero additional inference cost** (Mix-LN's normalization placement has identical inference FLOPs to Pre-LN — both apply one LN per sublayer). This is a pure Pareto improvement: same latency, same memory, better quality. For edge deployment teams choosing between Pre-LN and Mix-LN, there is no trade-off to weigh — Mix-LN dominates on the quality axis with no cost penalty.

**4. Re-evaluating deep-layer pruning as a diagnostic rather than a deployment strategy.** The paper's reframing has a practical implication for the model compression community: **layer pruning on Pre-LN models is measuring a training artifact, not an architectural constant**. A team developing a pruning algorithm on LLaMA-7B and finding that the last 25% of layers can be removed with minimal perplexity impact (as Gromov et al., 2024 and Men et al., 2024 report) should not conclude that "7B models only need 24 layers." The correct conclusion is "Pre-LN 7B models only effectively use 24 layers, and the remaining layers *could* be useful if trained differently." For pruning practitioners, this suggests a workflow: (1) train the base model with Mix-LN, (2) measure per-layer importance (angular distance, performance drop) on the Mix-LN model, (3) prune layers that remain redundant even under Mix-LN. The Mix-LN model's deeper layers are more important (Figure 6b), so the pruning algorithm should find fewer removable layers — but the ones it does remove will be genuinely redundant rather than artifacts of Pre-LN's gradient starvation. This workflow produces a smaller, higher-quality model than pruning a Pre-LN model to the same sparsity, because the remaining layers are all well-trained.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper positions Mix-LN as a **drop-in replacement for Pre-LN** in the standard LLM training pipeline, with no architectural changes required beyond normalization placement. The decision rule is straightforward and grounded in the paper's empirical coverage:

- **Prefer Mix-LN over Pre-LN when training LLaMA-based (or similar) decoder-only LLMs up to at least 1B parameters**, where `α = 0.25` is validated across model sizes (Table 1), the perplexity gain is consistent (0.47–1.65 points), and the downstream improvements are substantial (Tables 4, 5). There is no trade-off: implementation effort is minimal, training stability is maintained, and inference cost is identical. The `α` sweep cost (Table 7) is the only overhead, and it is amortized across training runs at the same scale.

- **Prefer Pre-LN over Mix-LN when training at 7B scale or above without the budget for `α` tuning**, since Mix-LN's `α` sensitivity requires scale-specific tuning (Section 4.2) and the paper provides no `α` scaling law to guide choice. If a project can afford only one training run at 7B+ without `α` exploration, Pre-LN is the safer choice — Mix-LN with a poorly chosen `α` could diverge (as pure Post-LN does at 250M+), while Pre-LN is guaranteed stable.

- **Prefer Mix-LN with `α ≈ 0.06` as a starting point at 7B**, based on the single data point in Section 4.2, if the project can afford at least partial `α` probing. The paper's Figure 4 shows Mix-LN outperforming Pre-LN throughout training even with this conservative `α`, and the gains might increase with more training tokens. A project with moderate risk tolerance and the ability to monitor for training instability can adopt Mix-LN at 7B and fall back to Pre-LN if divergence is detected early.

- **Prefer Mix-LN over Post-LN or DeepNorm unconditionally** — Post-LN diverges at 250M+ (Table 1), and DeepNorm diverges at 1B. Neither is a viable training strategy at the scales where Mix-LN is tested. The paper positions Mix-LN as a way to capture Post-LN's deep-layer gradient benefits without its instability, and the evidence supports this: Mix-LN trains successfully at all tested scales (71M–7B) while pure Post-LN and DeepNorm fail at moderate scales.

- **Prefer Pre-LN when using non-LLaMA architectures without validation.** The paper's evidence is confined to LLaMA (decoder-only, RMSNorm, SwiGLU) and ViT (encoder-only vision). Organizations using GPT-style architectures with standard LayerNorm, encoder-decoder architectures (T5, BART), or non-transformer LLMs should not assume Mix-LN transfers without in-house testing. The ViT results (Table 6) are too weak (0.04–0.41 point gains) to justify adoption on vision tasks without further validation.
