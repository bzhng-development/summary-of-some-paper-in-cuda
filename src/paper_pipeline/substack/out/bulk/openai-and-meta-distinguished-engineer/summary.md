Here are the takeaways from this Philip Su interview (went from IC7 to Distinguished Engineer / IC9 at Meta by founding and scaling the London office, later requested a demotion back to IC7 to return to hands-on technical work):

**Growing fast at Microsoft and the early career velocity**

Philip Su grew extremely quickly at Microsoft, reaching something equivalent to Senior Staff Engineer (IC7) very early in his career. He describes the environment as one where he was deeply technically focused, and the rapid progression came from a combination of raw output and being in a domain where impact was legible. The early velocity established a pattern: he learned to operate with high agency and to seek out problems where his work could have a broad effect, even early on.

**Joining Facebook as an IC7**

When he joined Facebook, he came in at the IC7 level. The transition from Microsoft to Facebook meant adapting to a different engineering culture—one that was much faster-moving, more bottom-up, and where the expectations for senior individual contributors were less about deep specialization in one component and more about moving quickly across the stack to solve whatever was most pressing. He notes that his generalist bent was a natural fit for this environment. At Facebook, being a generalist and being able to jump into any problem was not just tolerated but rewarded, because the company’s priorities shifted so quickly that deep specialists could sometimes find themselves without the most urgent work.

**Working with Mark Zuckerberg: constant devotion to growth**

Philip worked directly with Mark Zuckerberg and shared a personal story that crystallized what made Zuck outstanding. The defining trait was Zuck’s constant, almost relentless devotion to personal and professional growth. Philip describes Zuck as someone who never stopped trying to learn, improve, and question his own assumptions—even when he was already running one of the most successful companies in the world. This wasn’t an abstract observation; Philip witnessed it firsthand in interactions where Zuck would dig into technical details not to micromanage but to understand, and would visibly incorporate new information into his thinking in real time. The lesson Philip took away is that the most effective leaders maintain a **beginner’s mindset at scale**, treating their own expertise as perpetually incomplete.

**Working with John Carmack: prolific engineering ability across domains**

John Carmack was one of the most impressive engineers Philip ever worked with, but not for the reasons people might expect. Philip says what set Carmack apart was his “prolific engineering ability”—specifically his capacity to **drop into a codebase he knew nothing about and give excellent, actionable feedback**. Carmack could walk up to a system he’d never seen, read the code, understand its structure and weaknesses with astonishing speed, and then offer insights that the team who had worked on it for months hadn’t articulated. This wasn’t magic; Philip observed that Carmack had an extraordinary mental model of how software systems should be built, and he applied that model like a diagnostic tool. The takeaway for Philip was that deep, transferable engineering judgment—built through years of building real systems end-to-end—scales across domains in a way that narrow specialization does not.

**Scaling the London office and earning IC9 (Distinguished Engineer)**

Philip was promoted to Distinguished Engineer (IC9) at Meta by founding and scaling the company’s London office. When he took it on, the office had roughly a dozen engineers; over time he grew it to more than five hundred. The **blast radius** of this work—the number of engineers, teams, and product outcomes whose success he could directly guarantee—was what met the IC9 bar. He explains that at the highest IC levels, expectations are not a checklist of quantitative metrics but are fundamentally about blast radius: the scope of the organization or system for which you are the guarantor of success. In his case, being responsible for the success of a 500+ engineer site meant that everything from hiring and culture to technical direction and execution quality fell within his orbit. The role required deep technical judgment, but also the ability to build an organization that could sustain itself and produce excellent work without him in every decision.

**Qualitative feedback at the highest promotion levels**

Philip is emphatic that promotions at the IC9 level—and really at the highest levels generally—are driven by **qualitative feedback, not quantitative checklists**. He says that at some point, no spreadsheet of commits, launches, or headcount numbers can capture what makes someone’s work special. What matters is the judgment of someone already at that level or higher who can articulate, in narrative form, why the person’s contributions were exceptional and irreplaceable. This means the promotion packet is less a list of accomplishments and more a story, told by credible voices, about the unique value the engineer created. The practical implication: senior ICs aiming for the top levels must not only do great work but must also ensure that the people who will evaluate them have direct, vivid exposure to that work and can speak to its significance in their own words.

**The IC/EM pendulum: switching six times**

Philip switched between IC and Engineering Manager roles six times over his career. He says that for generalists, there is “a lot of commonality between management and tech leadership,” and treating them as entirely separate tracks is a mistake. The skills overlap more than people think: both require diagnosing system-wide problems, aligning people toward a goal, and making hard tradeoffs under uncertainty. By keeping the door open and not identifying too strongly with either label, he was able to switch based on business need and personal enjoyment. This flexibility also meant he never felt trapped—when management felt draining, he could move back to deep technical work, and when technical work felt isolating, he could return to leading teams. The **pendulum** was a feature, not a bug, of his career.

**Requesting a demotion from IC9 back to IC7**

Philip eventually did something that surprises many people: he requested a demotion from IC9 back to IC7. The reason was simple—he wanted to do more hands-on technical work, and the expectations and responsibilities of the IC9 role had pulled him so far from the code that he missed it. The demotion was not a punishment or a failure; it was a deliberate act of **career design**. By stepping down, he relieved himself of the immense pressure of being the guarantor for a 500-person organization and reclaimed the ability to focus on the technical problems he found most energizing. He frames this without regret—the IC9 experience was valuable, but so was the decision to leave it behind. The lesson is that levels are tools to align work with what you actually want to do, not fixed statuses you must defend at all costs.

**Where generalists beat specialists (and vice versa)**

Philip has a clear model for the **generalist vs. specialist tradeoff across company stages**. At smaller companies, generalists are much more effective because the surface area of problems is huge and the depth required on any single one is relatively shallow; a generalist can cover vast territory and have outsized impact. As companies grow larger and more mature, specialists gain the advantage because they can go arbitrarily deep on a single area, and that depth becomes the bottleneck—there are enough generalists to cover breadth, so the scarce resource is the person who truly understands a specific system at its foundations. He sees this as a natural structural difference, not a commentary on which type of engineer is better in the abstract. His own career bears this out: he thrived as a generalist at fast-scaling Facebook but recognizes that the specialists he worked alongside became increasingly essential as the company matured.

**How to write well: read great writing**

Philip is known as an excellent writer, and when Ryan asks how he improved, his number one piece of advice is straightforward: to write well, you need to **read examples of good writing and steal from them**. He says that most engineers trying to improve their writing focus on rules or frameworks, but the fastest path to improvement is exposure to great prose—reading it, studying how it works, and then taking inspiration from its structure, rhythm, and clarity to improve your own. This isn’t about copying content but about absorbing patterns. He treats writing as a craft skill that is learned osmotically through deep engagement with excellent examples, much the way a musician learns by listening to and transcribing great performances. The implicit message is that writing quality is not a talent you have or lack; it’s a skill built through deliberate exposure and imitation.

**Leaving for OpenAI before it was big**

After Meta, Philip joined OpenAI before it became the household name it is today—a move that fit his pattern of seeking high-impact, technically rich environments. The decision reflected his generalist instinct to go where the surface area of unsolved problems was vast and the depth required was still manageable, exactly the conditions where he believes generalists thrive. Though the transcript does not detail his specific projects there, the move itself underscores his willingness to bet on early-stage technical intensity over the safety of an established role, a through-line in a career defined by agency and deliberate re-invention.

---

Philip’s career is a long-running argument that levels are temporary instruments, not permanent identities. His **pendulum** swings between IC and EM, his deliberate use of **blast radius** to calibrate scope, and his ultimate act of **career design** in requesting a demotion all point to the same conviction: the work itself—its texture, its depth, its daily satisfactions—must dictate the role, not the other way around. Even his frameworks for what makes others exceptional (Zuck’s **beginner’s mindset at scale**, Carmack’s **drop-in diagnostic ability**) double as a map for the kind of engineer he wants to be: one who treats every level as provisional, every domain as learnable, and every piece of great writing as raw material for the next draft.
