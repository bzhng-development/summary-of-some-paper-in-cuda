# Microscaling Data Formats for Deep Learning

**ArXiv:** [2310.10537](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.10537)

## 🎯 Pitch

Generative language models can be trained end-to-end with sub-8-bit weights, activations, and gradients—matching FP32 loss without any recipe changes—by using MXFP6 formats that share a scaling factor across small blocks of elements. Pushing further, 4-bit weight training is demonstrated with only a 0.02 increase in loss, a result previously thought infeasible for such dynamic-range-hungry workloads.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper evaluates **Microscaling (MX) data formats** — a family of narrow bit-width datatypes that combine a per-block scaling factor with low-precision floating-point and integer elements — as a drop-in replacement for FP32 across over two dozen deep learning benchmarks, including GPT-3-175B, LLaMA-7B, and GPT-like models up to 1.5B parameters. The work studies MX formats under four operational regimes: direct-cast inference (quantizing a pretrained FP32 model with no calibration or fine-tuning), error-diffusion inference (a one-pass post-training quantization method), quantization-aware fine-tuned inference, and training from scratch with quantized forward and backward passes (quantizing both weights and activations for all GEMM operations while keeping vector operations like layernorm and softmax in Bfloat16 or FP32). MXINT8 achieves direct-cast inference matching FP32 within standard deviation across all tested discriminative and generative tasks, making it a compelling low-friction drop-in alternative. MXFP6_E2M3 with fine-tuning reaches close-to-parity with FP32 on discriminative benchmarks, and — in the paper’s headline result — enables the first instance of training generative language models with sub-8-bit weights, activations, and gradients to match FP32 loss with no modifications to the training recipe, demonstrated at scales up to 1.5B parameters (final loss of 2.75 vs. 2.74 for FP32). Pushing further, training with MXFP4 weights and MXFP6_E3M2 activations/gradients incurs only a minor loss penalty (2.76 vs. 2.74 for the 1.5B model), establishing that block-level microscaling can sustain end-to-end training fidelity at 4-bit weight precision only when the activation and gradient formats retain sufficient dynamic range.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Deep Learning's Insatiable Appetite for Bits

Modern deep learning is in a bind. The dominant strategy for improving AI capabilities — scaling up model size — has produced remarkable results, from conversational agents to code generation to text-to-image synthesis. But this scaling comes with a steep and growing cost. Larger models require more computation and more storage, which translates directly into more energy, more expensive hardware, larger memory footprints, and longer training times. As the paper notes in its opening paragraph, "this scaling up has led to a significant increase in the computing power and storage capacity necessary to train and deploy such models."

The industry's standard solution is to move away from the default FP32 (32-bit floating-point) representation and toward narrower bit-width formats. FP16 and Bfloat16 are now standard for training; INT8 is widely used for inference; and FP8 (8-bit floating-point) has recently entered the mainstream with its own open specification [1]. Each step down in bit-width roughly halves the memory footprint and, on hardware with native support, can double or quadruple computational throughput. This is not a marginal optimization — it's the difference between training a model on a single GPU versus a cluster, or between deploying a model on a datacenter server versus an edge device.

But there is a catch, and it is the central problem this paper tackles: **as you push below 8 bits, the formats that work well at 8 bits and above begin to break down.**

### Why Narrow Formats Fail at Sub-8-Bit Widths

To understand the failure mode, we need to think about what a floating-point format provides. A conventional floating-point number (like FP32) divides its bits between a sign, an exponent (which controls the *range* of representable values), and a mantissa (which controls the *precision* within that range). The exponent lets the format span many orders of magnitude — from tiny fractions to enormous sums — while the mantissa provides fine-grained discrimination between nearby values.

When you reduce the total bit width, you must give up some combination of range and precision. FP8, for instance, offers only 4 bits of exponent and 3 bits of mantissa in its E4M3 variant. This is enough dynamic range for many neural network tensors because per-tensor scaling factors can shift the representable window to align with each tensor's distribution. A per-tensor scale is a single multiplier applied to every element: if a weight tensor's values cluster around 0.01, you set the scale to cover that range, and all the narrow-format elements operate within it.

But this **per-tensor scaling breaks down at sub-8-bit widths.** The paper states this directly:

> "Tensor level scaling has been shown to be insufficient, though, for sub-8-bit formats due to their limited dynamic range."

Why is this? A neural network tensor rarely has a uniform distribution. In a single weight matrix, some rows or columns may contain values an order of magnitude larger than others. In an activation tensor, different channels or sequence positions can span dramatically different ranges. A single shared scale for the entire tensor must accommodate the *largest* value, which means it wastes precision on all the smaller values — they get squeezed into a few representable levels near zero, or worse, quantized to zero entirely. This effect becomes catastrophic at 6 bits or 4 bits, where the available precision is already tiny. The result is unacceptable accuracy loss, even for tasks that tolerate aggressive quantization at 8 bits.

### The Micro-Scaling Solution and Its Genealogy

The idea of associating a scaling factor not with an entire tensor but with a **fine-grained block** of elements has been floating in the research community for several years. The paper cites key precedents:

- **Drumond et al. (2018)** demonstrated training with "hybrid block floating point," where a shared exponent is applied to small blocks of a tensor [2].
- **Microsoft Floating Point (2020)** showed that block-level scaling could push inference quantization to very narrow bit widths in cloud-scale deployments [3].
- **VS-Quant (2021)** introduced per-vector scaling for accurate low-precision inference, formalizing the idea that small groups of elements should share a scale factor [4].
- **MX formats with shared microexponents (2023)** extended the concept, showing that an architecture where blocks share an exponent (rather than a full scale) could achieve good results with minimal hardware complexity [5].

These works collectively established that block-level scaling — what the paper calls "microscaling" — is substantially more effective than tensor-level scaling for sub-8-bit quantization. The intuition is straightforward: by giving each small group of elements (say, 32 elements) its own scaling factor, the format can adapt to local variations in magnitude. A block with large values gets a large scale; a block with small values gets a small scale. No single block's range must accommodate the entire tensor's dynamic range, so precision is preserved within each block.

### The Gap: No Open Standard and No Systematic Evaluation

Despite the research community's convergence on the value of micro-scaling, there was — prior to this paper — a critical missing piece:

**No open standard existed.** Each prior work used its own proprietary format with its own block size, scale encoding, element format, and conversion algorithm. This fragmentation created three practical problems:

1. **Hardware incompatibility.** Without a standard, different hardware vendors would implement different micro-scaled formats. A model quantized for one accelerator would be incompatible with another. This is the exact problem standards like IEEE 754 (for scalar floating-point) and the OCP FP8 specification [1] solve — they create a common ground that hardware designers and software developers can both target.
2. **Tooling chaos.** Framework developers (PyTorch, TensorFlow, ONNX) would need to support a proliferation of bespoke formats, each with its own quirks. This violates the principle of **user friction** that the paper elevates to a first-class design goal — the idea that a format should "ensure seamless integration within existing training and inference frameworks and generalizability across different workloads."
3. **Unknown generalization.** Individual papers demonstrated micro-scaling on a narrow set of models and tasks, but no systematic, broad evaluation existed. Would a format that works for ResNet-50 also work for GPT-3-175B? For speech recognition? For recommendation models? The community lacked evidence that a single format family could serve as a drop-in replacement across diverse AI workloads.

This paper addresses these gaps by evaluating the **MX (Microscaling) data formats** — "the first open standard for a family of micro-scaled datatypes aimed at deep learning training and inference" [6]. The evaluation spans over two dozen benchmarks covering language translation, text encoding, image classification, speech recognition, recommendation systems, generative language model inference, and generative language model training. The paper positions this evaluation not as a proposal of a novel technique, but as a validation that the existing MX standard achieves a workable balance among three competing goals:

> **"Hardware Efficiency** — Maximize compute and storage efficiency via reduced bit-width.
> **Model Accuracy** — Minimize the gap in the quality of results compared with baseline FP32 for AI training and inference.
> **User Friction** — Ensure seamless integration within existing training and inference frameworks and generalizability across different workloads."

This three-way tradeoff is the intellectual framework for the entire paper. It's worth dwelling on because it explains virtually every design choice and evaluation decision.

**Hardware efficiency** is straightforward: fewer bits means less memory, less bandwidth, and (with proper hardware support) more operations per second. But efficiency is not just about total bits — it's also about how those bits are organized. A format that requires complex, non-uniform hardware logic to decode and process might use fewer bits but actually be *less* hardware-efficient than a slightly wider but more regular format. The MX standard addresses this by defining a simple, regular block structure where one shared scale (always E8M0 — an 8-bit exponent-only format) governs a fixed-size block of elements (always 32 for the concrete formats evaluated). This regularity maps cleanly to hardware pipelines.

**Model accuracy** is the raison d'être of the block-level scaling approach. If block-level scaling can preserve accuracy at 6 bits where tensor-level scaling fails, then the path to 6-bit hardware suddenly becomes viable. The paper's central empirical claim is that MX formats achieve this for a remarkably broad range of workloads.

**User friction** is perhaps the least obvious but most consequential goal. A data format that achieves perfect accuracy and efficiency but requires practitioners to rewrite their training pipelines, tune hyperparameters per-model, or manage complex calibration procedures will see limited adoption. The paper explicitly designs its experiments to assess friction: direct-cast inference (no calibration, no fine-tuning, just quantize and run) is the ultimate low-friction scenario; error-diffusion PTQ adds a quick calibration pass but no training loop; fine-tuned inference requires some retraining; training from scratch with no recipe changes is the gold standard for training friction.

### The Specific Prior Approaches and Their Shortcomings

#### Tensor-Level Quantization to INT8 and FP8

The current production standard for inference quantization is INT8 with per-tensor scaling factors. This works well: as the paper's own results show (Table 2), MXINT8 — which is essentially INT8 with per-block rather than per-tensor scaling — matches FP32 accuracy across all tested models. But this only gets you 4× compression relative to FP32 (8 bits vs. 32 bits). The jump to 6 bits or 4 bits would yield 5.3× or 8× compression, but per-tensor scaling fails to preserve accuracy at these widths because a single scale cannot accommodate the intra-tensor variation.

FP8 training [1] is a more recent development, splitting 8-bit floating-point into two variants: E4M3 (4 exponent bits, 3 mantissa bits) for weights and activations, and E5M2 (5 exponent, 2 mantissa) for gradients, which need more dynamic range. FP8 represents a significant advance — reducing training memory by 4× relative to FP32 — but it is still an 8-bit format. The hardware computation and memory benefits of going to 6-bit or 4-bit are substantial: 6-bit reduces memory by another 25% relative to 8-bit, and 4-bit halves it. The question is whether the accuracy can be maintained.

#### Prior Micro-Scaling Research (But in Isolation)

As noted, micro-scaled formats have been studied in prior work [2, 3, 4, 5], but these studies were **narrow in scope**. Drumond et al. [2] focused on training CNNs. Microsoft Floating Point [3] focused on cloud inference. VS-Quant [4] focused on inference with per-vector scales. None of these provided a systematic evaluation across generative and discriminative tasks, across inference and training, across direct-cast, PTQ, and fine-tuned regimes, at model scales ranging from 20M to 175B parameters. Without this breadth, it was impossible to claim that a single format family could serve as a **universal drop-in replacement** — which is exactly what would be needed for hardware standardization.

#### GPTQ, AWQ, and Other Weight-Only Quantization Methods

A parallel line of work (not explicitly cited in this paper but part of the surrounding context) explores weight-only quantization for LLMs, where weights are quantized to 4 bits but activations remain in FP16. Methods like GPTQ and AWQ can achieve good results for inference because the memory bottleneck for large language models is dominantly in the weight matrices (parameters often exceed KV cache and activation memory by large margins). However, weight-only quantization does not reduce the computational footprint of the matrix multiplications unless the hardware supports mixed-precision dot products, and it does not address the broader need for activation quantization, which is essential for reducing bandwidth in non-LLM workloads and for training (where activations must be stored for the backward pass). The MX approach is more ambitious: it quantizes **both weights and activations** for forward-pass computations, and — crucially for training — also quantizes **gradients** and **weight updates**, which are the full set of tensors that dominate training memory.

#### Training Quantization: The Sub-8-Bit Frontier

The paper identifies training at sub-8-bit precision as an open frontier:

> "We also show the first instance of training generative language models at sub-8-bit weights, activations, and gradients with minimal accuracy loss and no modifications to the training recipe."

To appreciate why this is significant, consider the data flow in training (Figure 2). The forward pass computes activations, which must be stored for the backward pass. The backward pass computes gradients with respect to activations and weights. The optimizer computes weight updates. All these tensors — weights, activations, activations' gradients, weight gradients, optimizer states — consume memory during training. Reducing their precision from FP32 or FP16 to 6 bits roughly halves the memory footprint, which is enormous for large models (where training is often memory-bound, not compute-bound). Prior to this work, no one had demonstrated that you could quantize all three — weights, activations, *and* gradients — to sub-8 bits and still train a generative transformer to match FP32 accuracy. This is the "first instance" the paper claims, and it represents a step-function improvement in the feasibility of training large models on constrained hardware.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper positions itself as an **empirical validation paper**, not a method proposal. It does not introduce a new quantization algorithm, a new training technique, or a new architecture. Instead, it takes an existing open standard (the OCP Microscaling Specification [6]), implements it faithfully, and subjects it to a systematically broad and deep evaluation. The contribution is the **evidence** that this standard achieves its stated goals across a diverse benchmark suite.

This positioning is important because it addresses the gap between academic research (which often proposes new techniques and tests them on a handful of models) and industrial deployment (which requires confidence that a standard will work broadly, with low friction, across a wide range of real workloads). The paper is written by a consortium of authors from Microsoft, AMD, Intel, Meta, NVIDIA, and Qualcomm — the companies that design the hardware and deploy the models — signaling that the MX standard has broad industry backing and that this evaluation is intended to provide the empirical justification for hardware investment.

The paper also positions MX formats relative to the broader trend toward narrower precision. It builds directly on the OCP FP8 specification [1], using the same FP8 element formats (E4M3 and E5M2) as the 8-bit entries in the MX family. The naming convention — MXFP8, MXFP6, MXFP4, MXINT8 — makes the relationship explicit: take a standard element format, add a block-level shared scale, and you get the corresponding MX format. This modular design is deliberate: hardware that supports MX can be built once and then parameterized by the element width, making the transition from 8-bit to 6-bit to 4-bit a matter of configuring the element datapaths rather than redesigning the memory system or compute logic.

### The Specific Empirical Questions the Paper Seeks to Answer

Implicitly, the paper addresses a series of escalating questions that reflect increasing ambition (and increasing difficulty):

1. **Can MXINT8 serve as a no-friction drop-in for FP32 inference?** (Tables 2, 5, 6 — answer: yes, across all tested models)
2. **Can MXFP6 approach FP32 accuracy with minimal fine-tuning?** (Table 4 — answer: yes, with fine-tuning MXFP6_E2M3 reaches close-to-parity)
3. **Can MXFP6 enable direct-cast generative inference on large models?** (Tables 5, 6 — answer: mostly, with some degradation on Lambada and Wikitext)
4. **Can MXFP6 sustain end-to-end training of transformer language models?** (Table 7, Figure 3 — answer: yes, matching FP32 at 1.5B scale with no recipe changes)
5. **Can training be pushed to 4-bit weights (with 6-bit activations/gradients)?** (Table 8, Figure 4 — answer: yes, with only minor loss penalty)
6. **What is the best mantissa/exponent allocation for each bit width?** (Tables 2, 4 — answer: more mantissa bits are better for direct-cast inference; the E2M3 variant of FP6 outperforms E3M2 for direct-cast, but E3M2 is needed for training)

Each of these questions corresponds to a different point on the hardware-efficiency/accuracy/friction tradeoff surface, and the paper's value is in mapping that surface comprehensively enough to guide practical hardware and deployment decisions. 

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

This paper is primarily an **empirical validation study** that evaluates a family of existing data formats—Microscaling (MX) formats—across a broad suite of deep learning tasks, rather than proposing a new quantization method. The core problem it solves is how to represent neural network tensors (weights, activations, gradients) using substantially fewer bits than FP32 while maintaining model accuracy and minimizing the engineering effort required to adopt the format. The solution has the shape of a **hierarchical decomposition**: each tensor is partitioned into small blocks of elements, each block gets its own shared scaling factor, and the individual elements within a block are encoded in a narrow bit-width format (integer or floating-point) that only needs to cover the dynamic range within that block rather than the full tensor range.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The system consists of four conceptual layers:

1. **The MX Block Structure** — the atomic unit of data representation. A vector of `k` numbers (elements) shares one scaling factor `X` (stored in E8M0 format, an 8-bit unsigned exponent). Each element `Pi` is stored in a narrow format (INT8, FP8, FP6, or FP4). The value of the i-th number is recovered as `X × Pi`. This two-level hierarchy (one scale + `k` narrow elements) is what makes sub-8-bit precision viable by allowing local adaptation of dynamic range.

2. **The Conversion Algorithm** — the procedure that maps a vector of FP32 values into an MX block. It finds the maximum absolute value in the block, computes a shared exponent that aligns this maximum with the largest representable binade of the element format, and then quantizes each element by dividing by the shared scale and rounding/clamping to the element format's representable set.

3. **The Compute Flow** — how MX formats integrate into the forward and backward passes of a neural network. Dot-product operations (matrix multiplications, convolutions) use MX-quantized operands; vector operations (layer normalization, softmax, GELU, residual additions) remain in a scalar floating-point format like Bfloat16 or FP32. A master copy of weights is kept in FP32 and updated by the optimizer; the MX-quantized weights are derived from this master copy at each step.

4. **The Evaluation Regimes** — four operational scenarios that test the formats under increasing levels of user intervention: direct-cast inference (quantize and run, no calibration), error-diffusion inference (one-pass post-training quantization with a small calibration dataset), quantization-aware fine-tuned inference (a few epochs of fine-tuning with quantized forward passes and FP32 backward passes), and training from scratch (all GEMMs in both passes quantized, no recipe changes).

Information flows as follows: a pretrained FP32 model's weights and/or activations are partitioned into blocks of `k` elements → Algorithm 1 computes a shared scale and quantized elements for each block → the quantized tensors replace the FP32 tensors in dot-product operations → forward (and optionally backward) pass computations proceed using MX formats → if training, gradients flow back and update the master FP32 weights → the cycle repeats with freshly quantized MX weights derived from the updated master copy.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the MX block structure and the concrete format variants (Table 1), because this is the data representation that all subsequent operations depend on — you cannot understand the conversion algorithm or compute flow without knowing what an MX block physically encodes.
- **Second**, the conversion algorithm (Algorithm 1) and the design decisions embedded in it, because this is the bridge between standard floating-point computation and MX format storage.
- **Third**, the compute flow (Figure 2) and how MX formats are integrated into training and inference pipelines, including the master weight copy, the treatment of vector operations, and the non-commutativity of transpose and quantization.
- **Fourth**, the four evaluation regimes and their operational semantics, because the paper's empirical results are organized around these regimes and each tests a different position on the accuracy-friction tradeoff curve.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is an **empirical validation paper** whose core idea is that a standardized family of block-scaled narrow formats (MX formats) can serve as a universal low-friction replacement for FP32 across diverse deep learning workloads, and that the key enabler is the **per-block shared scale** which provides sufficient local dynamic range adaptation to make sub-8-bit quantization viable where per-tensor scaling fails.

#### The MX Block: Data Layout and Value Recovery

The fundamental unit of data in any MX format is an **MX block**. An MX block represents a vector of `k` real numbers using two components:

> "A basic unit of data in an MX format represents a vector of `k` numbers and consists of a single shared scale `X` and `k` scalar elements `{Pi}k_{i=1}`... The two data formats are independent of one another, and all `k` elements share the same element data format."

In plain terms: instead of storing each number as a self-contained floating-point value with its own exponent and mantissa, an MX block factors the representation into **(1) one exponent that is shared by all `k` elements** and **(2) `k` narrow-format values that are multiplied by that shared exponent.** The shared scale `X` is always stored in a format called E8M0 (an 8-bit integer representing an unsigned exponent; no mantissa, no sign). The `k` elements `{Pi}` are stored in the concrete element format — INT8, FP8 (E4M3 or E5M2), FP6 (E2M3 or E3M2), or FP4 (E2M1).

The numerical value of the i-th element in the block is recovered by multiplication:

> "If X = NaN, then vi = NaN for all i. If |XPi| > V_maxFloat32 then vi is implementation-defined. Otherwise, vi = XPi."

where `V_maxFloat32` is the largest representable magnitude in IEEE Float32 (approximately 3.4 × 10^38). This three-case logic handles exceptional conditions: if the shared scale is NaN, all elements are treated as NaN (a contagion rule); if the product exceeds FP32's representable range, the behavior is left to the hardware implementation; otherwise, the simple product `X × Pi` gives the recovered value.

**Why this structure?** The key insight is that within a small spatial neighborhood of a tensor (e.g., 32 consecutive elements along the reduction dimension), values tend to be of similar magnitude. By factoring out the common magnitude into a shared exponent `X`, the element format only needs enough range to cover the *variation within the block*, not the variation across the entire tensor. For a weight matrix, different rows may have very different norms; with a per-block scale along the reduction dimension, each block adapts to its local norm. This is why the format can function at 6 or 4 bits per element even though a per-tensor-scaled 6-bit format would fail: the per-tensor scale must cover the maximum absolute value in the entire tensor, wasting precision on elements with smaller magnitudes, whereas the per-block scale only covers the maximum within its small block, leaving more precision for the local variation.

**Concrete format parameters (Table 1).** All concrete MX formats use a **block size `k` of 32** and an **E8M0 shared scale** (8 bits). The differentiation comes from the element format:

| Format Name | Element Bit-Width | Element Data Types | Total Bits per Block (32 elements) | Bits per Element (amortized) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| MXINT8 | 8 | INT8 | 8 + 32 × 8 = 264 | 8.25 |
| MXFP8 | 8 | FP8 (E4M3 or E5M2) | 8 + 32 × 8 = 264 | 8.25 |
| MXFP6 | 6 | FP6 (E2M3 or E3M2) | 8 + 32 × 6 = 200 | 6.25 |
| MXFP4 | 4 | FP4 (E2M1) | 8 + 32 × 4 = 136 | 4.25 |

The "amortized bits per element" is slightly higher than the element bit-width because the 8-bit shared scale is amortized over 32 elements, adding 8/32 = 0.25 bits per element. This overhead becomes proportionally larger at narrower element widths — for MXFP4, the scale overhead is 6.25% of total storage; for MXINT8 it is only 3.0%.

**The E8M0 scale format.** E8M0 is an 8-bit unsigned integer encoding an exponent value from 0 to 255. It has no sign bit and no mantissa — it represents only pure powers of two (2^0 through 2^255). This is why multiplication by `X` requires only an exponent addition in the floating-point pipeline, not a full multiplier. The representable exponent range of E8M0 (0 to 255, corresponding to values 1 to 2^255) is a strict superset of the exponent range of FP32 (which has exponents from -126 to +127, with bias), meaning that any FP32 value's magnitude can be captured by an appropriate choice of shared scale.

**Special value encodings.** The paper describes how MX formats handle NaN and Inf (Section 2.1):

- **NaN encoding has two pathways.** First, if the shared scale `X` itself encodes NaN (which E8M0 can represent), then **all `k` elements in the block become NaN regardless of their individual encodings.** This is a "one NaN, all NaN" contagion rule at the block level. Second, if `X` is not NaN, individual elements `Pi` can encode NaN in their own format (FP8, FP6, and FP4 all have NaN representations). This two-level NaN mechanism means that a single corrupted scale value can poison an entire 32-element block, but element-level NaNs are fine-grained.
- **Inf encoding is element-level only.** The shared scale `X` (E8M0) does not encode Inf — it has no Inf representation. Individual elements `Pi` may encode Inf if their format supports it (the floating-point element formats do). When `X` is a normal number and `Pi` encodes Inf, the product `X × Pi` yields Inf.

**Design choice: why 32-element blocks?** The paper does not explicitly argue for block size 32, but the choice reflects a balance: smaller blocks provide finer-grained dynamic range adaptation (better accuracy) but incur higher scale overhead (more bits per element spent on scales). At 32 elements per scale, the overhead is small (0.25 bits/element) while still providing substantial local adaptation. Standard matrix multiplication hardware operates on tiles that are powers of two (e.g., 32×32 or 64×64), making 32 a natural fit for hardware pipelines.

**Design choice: why E8M0 rather than a scaled integer?** The shared scale could alternatively be stored as a full floating-point number or a scaled integer. E8M0's key property is that it represents **only powers of two.** This means multiplication `X × Pi` reduces to adding the exponent of `X` to the exponent of `Pi` — a simple integer addition in the exponent field, with no mantissa multiplication. This is vastly cheaper in hardware than a full floating-point multiplier. The tradeoff is that E8M0 cannot represent scale factors that are not powers of two, but in practice the optimal scale for a block of values is naturally close to a power-of-two approximation of the block's maximum magnitude.

#### Conversion from FP32 to MX Format (Algorithm 1)

The paper provides Algorithm 1 as a concrete working example of the conversion process, noting that the OCP Microscaling Specification [6] allows for implementation-defined conversion recipes (Section 3). This algorithm maps a vector of `k` FP32 values `{Vi}k_{i=1}` to a single MX block consisting of a shared scale `X` and `k` quantized elements `{Pi}k_{i=1}`.

The algorithm proceeds in three stages:

**Stage 1: Compute the shared exponent.**

$$
\text{shared\_exp} = \lfloor \log_2(\max_i(|V_i|)) \rfloor - \text{emax\_elem}
$$

where `emax_elem` is the exponent of the largest normal number in the element data format, and `⌊·⌋` denotes the floor operation.

**What this computes:** First, find the maximum absolute value across all `k` elements in the block. Second, compute the floor of the base-2 logarithm of this maximum — this gives the exponent (the binade) of the largest input value. Third, subtract `emax_elem` to shift this exponent down so that the largest input value maps to the largest representable binade of the element format.

**Why subtract `emax_elem`?** The element format has a fixed exponent range. For example, FP8 E4M3 has a maximum normal exponent of `emax_elem` (with bias, the largest representable exponent). If the maximum input value has raw exponent `E_max`, and we set the shared scale to `2^(E_max - emax_elem)`, then dividing the input by the scale maps the maximum value's exponent to exactly `emax_elem`, meaning the element format can represent it without overflow. This "aligns the largest input to the top of the element format's range," which is the optimal use of the available exponent bits — it minimizes the chance of overflow for large values while maximizing precision for smaller values (which get shifted down proportionally).

**Stage 2: Set the shared scale.**

The shared scale `X` is set to `2^shared_exp`, i.e., a pure power of two matching the computed shared exponent. This is the only value the E8M0 format can represent (it stores only the exponent).

**Stage 3: Quantize each element.**

For each `i = 1` to `k`:

$$
P_i = \text{quantize\_to\_element\_format}(V_i / X)
$$

The division `V_i / X` normalizes the i-th input by the shared scale. Since `X` is a power of two, this division is just subtracting `shared_exp` from the exponent of `V_i` — it is an exponent shift, not a full division. The function `quantize_to_element_format` then rounds and clamps the normalized value to the nearest representable value in the element format. The paper specifies:

> "normal numbers that exceed the representable range of the element format are clamped to the maximum representable value, preserving the sign. Infs and NaNs are not clamped."

**Clamping behavior:** If `V_i / X` is larger than the maximum normal value of the element format, `P_i` is set to that maximum normal value with the sign of `V_i`. This is **saturation arithmetic**, not wrap-around — overflow maps to the largest representable magnitude rather than wrapping to a small or negative value. Infs and NaNs from the input pass through without clamping, preserving their special semantics.

**Subnormal handling:** The paper adds a detail not in the OCP specification:

> "`P_i` is set to zero if the corresponding input `V_i` is a subnormal Float32 number."

Subnormal FP32 values are extremely small magnitudes (less than ~1.2 × 10^-38). Setting them to zero simplifies the conversion logic and has negligible impact on neural network computation because subnormal values are rare in trained network tensors and clamping them to zero introduces only a tiny error relative to the quantization error already being introduced.

**Rounding mode:** The paper uses **round-half-to-nearest-even** for conversion in discriminative inference experiments (Section 4.3) and **round-half-away-from-zero** for generative training experiments (Section 4.5). Round-half-to-nearest-even is the IEEE 754 default and avoids statistical bias; round-half-away-from-zero may be chosen for hardware simplicity or empirical advantage.

#### Multi-Dimensional Tensors and Non-Commutativity of Transpose

When converting a multi-dimensional tensor (e.g., a weight matrix of shape `[K, N]`) to an MX format, a **principal axis must be selected** for the shared scale. The paper states:

> "For a 2D matrix, the scale can be shared by every `k` element in a row or column."

This choice matters because the scale is shared across elements that are contiguous along the chosen axis. In a matrix multiplication `C = A × B`, the reduction dimension is typically the inner dimension of the product — for `A` of shape `[M, K]` and `B` of shape `[K, N]`, the reduction dimension is `K`. The natural choice is to apply the shared scale along the reduction dimension, so each block of `k` elements from different columns (for `A`) or rows (for `B`) that get multiplied together share the same scale. The OCP Microscaling Specification [6] recommends this convention for dot-product operations.

**A critical non-commutativity:** The conversion to MX format and the transpose operation do not commute. That is, if you quantize a matrix `W` to MX format and then transpose it, you get a different result than if you first transpose `W` in FP32 and then quantize. This is because the grouping of elements into blocks (and thus the shared scales) depends on the memory layout. When you transpose, the elements that were contiguous along the original block axis are no longer contiguous, so they would be grouped differently for scale computation.

The practical consequence is stated in Section 4.1:

> "the quantized weights `W_i` and their transpose `W_i^T` must be stored as two separate tensors."

During training (Figure 2), the forward pass uses `W_i` (quantized along one axis), and the backward pass uses `W_i^T` (quantized along the transposed axis). These two quantized copies are different and must both be stored, though the paper notes:

> "the two tensors do not need to be stored in working memory simultaneously unless a very fine-grained interleaving of the forward and backward passes is employed."

In practice, the forward-pass weight tensor can be discarded before the backward-pass transpose is needed, or vice versa depending on execution order, so the memory overhead is the size of one quantized weight matrix, not two.

#### The Compute Flow (Figure 2): How MX Formats Integrate into Training

Figure 2 diagrams the training compute flow with MX formats. The flow is a standard neural network training loop with one crucial modification: dot-product operations use MX-quantized operands, while all other operations remain in scalar floating-point.

**Forward pass, step by step:**

1. **Start with activations from the previous layer** `A_{i-1}` in Bfloat16 (shape `[M, K]`).
2. **Retrieve the master FP32 weights** `W_i` for the current layer (shape `[K, N]`).
3. **Quantize `W_i` to an MX format:** partition into blocks along the reduction dimension, compute shared scales, quantize elements. The result is `MX*[K_Q, N]` — note the block-aligned dimension annotation `K_Q`, indicating that `K` is padded or tiled to align with block size 32.
4. **Quantize `A_{i-1}` to an MX format:** similarly, quantize along the reduction dimension to produce `MX*[M, K_Q]`.
5. **Perform the matrix multiplication** `A_{i-1} × W_i` using MX-quantized operands. The dot-product computation follows Section 6.2 of the OCP Microscaling Specification [6], which defines how to efficiently compute dot products with shared-scale blocks. The output is in Bfloat16 (shape `[M, N]`).
6. **Apply vector operations** (bias addition, layer normalization, activation functions like GELU, residual connections) in Bfloat16 or FP32. These operations are **not quantized to MX.** The result is `A_i` in Bfloat16 (shape `[M, N]`), which becomes the input to the next layer.

**Backward pass, step by step:**

1. **Receive gradient with respect to outputs** `E_i` in Bfloat16 (shape `[M, N]`) from the subsequent layer.
2. **Compute gradient with respect to weights:** transpose `A_{i-1}` to shape `[K, M]`, quantize to `MX*[K, M_Q]`; quantize `E_i` to `MX*[M_Q, N]`; perform matrix multiplication `A_{i-1}^T × E_i`. The result is the weight gradient `W_g_i` in FP32.
3. **Compute gradient with respect to activations:** transpose `W_i` to shape `[N, K]`, quantize to `MX*[N, K_Q]`; quantize `E_i` to `MX*[M, N_Q]`; perform matrix multiplication `E_i × W_i^T`. The result is `E_{i-1}` in Bfloat16, which propagates backward.
4. **Update master weights:** the optimizer (e.g., Adam) operates in FP32 on the master weight copy `W_i`, using the FP32 weight gradient `W_g_i` and any optimizer states (also stored in FP32).

**What stays in FP32/Bfloat16 and why.** The paper's compute flow quantizes only operations involving dot products (matrix multiplications, convolutions). All vector operations — layer normalization, softmax, GELU activation, residual additions — remain in scalar floating-point. The rationale is twofold: (1) these operations have different numerical sensitivity than dot products (softmax, in particular, requires broad dynamic range for its exponentials), and (2) these operations contribute far less to total compute and memory than dot products, so the efficiency gains from quantizing them are small while the accuracy risk is substantial.

**Why keep a master FP32 weight copy?** During training, weight updates are small corrections accumulated over many steps. If weights were stored only in low-precision MX format, these small updates would fall below the quantization threshold and be lost — the model would fail to learn. The master FP32 copy accumulates updates at full precision, and the MX-quantized version used in the forward and backward passes is re-derived from the master copy at each step by applying Algorithm 1. This is analogous to how mixed-precision training with FP16 maintains an FP32 master copy of weights — the key insight is that **storage precision and computation precision can differ**, with storage (for accumulation) requiring higher precision than computation.

**Mixed-precision MX configurations.** The paper explores settings where weights and activations use different MX formats. For instance, Table 8 and Figure 4 show training with **MXFP4 for weights and MXFP6_E3M2 for activations and gradients.** The backward pass gradients `E_i` use the same format as the activations (MXFP6_E3M2 in this case). This mixed-precision strategy exploits the observation that **weights are more tolerant of aggressive quantization than activations during training** — weights are typically more redundant (they have more parameters than needed), and the master FP32 copy provides a precision safety net, while activations directly affect the gradient computation and are more sensitive to quantization error.

#### The Concrete Element Formats and Their Design

The MX family includes several element formats, each optimized for different points on the accuracy-efficiency tradeoff. The paper evaluates two variants each for FP8 and FP6, corresponding to different mantissa-exponent allocations.

**FP8 variants (from the OCP FP8 specification [1]):**

- **E4M3:** 1 sign bit, 4 exponent bits, 3 mantissa bits. This format provides finer precision (3 mantissa bits = 8 representable values per binade) at the cost of reduced dynamic range (4 exponent bits, biased, covering roughly `2^-6` to `2^+7` for normal numbers). E4M3 is designed for weights and activations, which tend to be well-behaved in range.
- **E5M2:** 1 sign bit, 5 exponent bits, 2 mantissa bits. This format provides wider dynamic range (5 exponent bits, biased, covering roughly `2^-14` to `2^+15` for normal numbers) at the cost of coarser precision (2 mantissa bits = 4 representable values per binade). E5M2 is designed for gradients, which can have broader dynamic range due to the backpropagation of errors.

**FP6 variants:**

- **E2M3:** 1 sign bit, 2 exponent bits, 3 mantissa bits. Maximum exponent bias allows representation from roughly `2^-1` to `2^+2` (very limited range) but with 3 mantissa bits of precision (8 values per binade). This allocation favors precision over range.
- **E3M2:** 1 sign bit, 3 exponent bits, 2 mantissa bits. Wider range (roughly `2^-3` to `2^+4`) with 2 mantissa bits of precision (4 values per binade). This allocation balances range and precision.

**FP4 variant:**

- **E2M1:** 1 sign bit, 2 exponent bits, 1 mantissa bit. This is an extremely constrained format — only 2 representable normal values per binade (1 mantissa bit) and a limited exponent range. The paper uses this only for weights in the most aggressive mixed-precision configuration (Table 8), acknowledging that activations and gradients cannot tolerate this level of compression.

**INT8 variant:**

- **INT8:** 8-bit signed integer. No exponent, no mantissa — the representable values are evenly spaced integers from -128 to +127. The dynamic range comes entirely from the shared scale `X`. Because all values within a block are scaled by the same `X`, INT8 can represent a wide dynamic range across blocks (via `X`) while maintaining uniform precision within each block. This works surprisingly well for neural network tensors because the values within a 32-element neighborhood tend to have similar magnitudes, making uniform quantization within the block effective.

**The design tradeoff: mantissa vs. exponent bits.** For a given total bit width, allocating more bits to the exponent extends the representable range (more binades covered) at the cost of less precision within each binade (fewer mantissa bits = fewer representable values per binade). Allocating more bits to the mantissa does the opposite. The paper's empirical results (Table 2, direct-cast inference) show that for inference, **more mantissa bits are better** — E4M3 outperforms E5M2 (both 8-bit), and E2M3 outperforms E3M2 (both 6-bit). This makes sense because inference tensors are static and their dynamic range can be accommodated by the per-block shared scale, so intra-block precision dominates. For training, however, the **E3M2 variant of FP6 is essential** — Table 7 and Figure 3 use MXFP6_E3M2 — because gradients during training have broader dynamic range that the 2-exponent-bit E2M3 format cannot cover, even with per-block scaling. The shared scale `X` helps, but if the *variation within a single block* is large (e.g., a gradient block containing both very small and very large values), a small element exponent range will cause overflow or underflow for the extreme values within that block.

#### The Four Evaluation Regimes: Operational Definitions

The paper evaluates MX formats under four distinct regimes, each with a specific operational definition that reflects increasing user intervention and computational cost:

**1. Direct-cast Inference (Tables 2, 5, 6; Section 4.3 and 4.4)**

This is the lowest-friction regime. The procedure is:

> "The quantized inference is performed on a trained FP32 model. All GeMMs in the forward pass are quantized unless explicitly called out otherwise (the backward pass is not executed at all)."

In operational terms: take a pretrained FP32 model, apply Algorithm 1 to convert all weight matrices to the target MX format, and for each input, convert the activation tensors to the MX format on the fly during the forward pass. No calibration data is used. No fine-tuning is performed. The model is evaluated immediately on the test set.

This regime tests whether the MX format can serve as a **true drop-in replacement** — the scenario with maximum deployment convenience. It succeeds to the extent that the FP32-trained model's weight distributions and activation distributions are well-approximated by the block-scaled quantization.

**2. Error-Diffusion Inference (Table 3; Section 4.2)**

This is a post-training quantization (PTQ) method that uses a small calibration dataset to improve quantization quality without a training loop. The paper uses the **GPFQ (Greedy Path Following Quantization)** algorithm [8], described as:

> "a PTQ algorithm derived from GPFQ... It performs quantization using a small calibration dataset... This PTQ process is a quick one-pass process without a training loop or needing any tuning parameter."

The core idea of error-diffusion quantization (also known as quantization with feedback) is that the quantization error from quantizing one element is "diffused" to neighboring (not-yet-quantized) elements by adding a fraction of the error to those elements before they are quantized. This uses the spatial correlation within the tensor to preserve information: the error from rounding one value down can be partially compensated by rounding a neighboring value up. The result is that the aggregate statistics (mean, variance) of the block are better preserved than with independent rounding.

The paper applies error diffusion to **both weights and activations**, quantizing them to the same format. A small calibration dataset is run through the model once to collect activation statistics and apply the diffusion procedure. This adds minimal friction — no training loop, no hyperparameter tuning — while improving accuracy over direct-cast, particularly for narrower formats (compare Table 3 MXFP4 vs. Table 2 MXFP4: DeiT-Tiny goes from 56.72% direct-cast to 64.76% with error diffusion).

**3. Finetuned Inference (Table 4; Section 4.2)**

This regime adds a small amount of training to recover accuracy lost to quantization:

> "Quantization-aware finetuning is done on a trained FP32 model for a small number of epochs. For this fine-tuning, all GeMMs in the forward pass are quantized, while the backward pass is performed in FP32. Hyperparameter exploration is used to find proper finetuning hyperparameters."

The forward pass uses MX-quantized weights and activations (so the model "sees" the quantization error during training), but the backward pass computes gradients in FP32 and updates the FP32 master weights. This is called **quantization-aware training (QAT)** in the literature, but the paper's version is "light" QAT — only a few epochs, starting from a pretrained model, with no changes to the architecture.

The key operational detail is that the backward pass is in FP32. This means the fine-tuning process does not benefit from the memory or compute savings of MX formats during training — it's purely an accuracy recovery step. The paper explicitly notes "N/A" entries in Table 4 where direct-cast results were already satisfactory (e.g., MXINT8 across many models, MXFP8 for Transformer-Large), meaning no fine-tuning was needed.

**4. Training from Scratch (Tables 7, 8; Figures 3, 4; Section 4.5)**

This is the most ambitious regime and the paper's headline contribution:

> "A model is trained from scratch using a compute flow where all GeMMs in both forward and backward passes are quantized... For mixed-precision training where the weights and activations use different data formats, the gradients (E_i in Figure 2) are quantized to the activation format."

The full compute flow of Figure 2 applies: forward pass GeMMs use MX-quantized weights and activations; backward pass GeMMs use MX-quantized activation transposes and gradient tensors; the master weight copy and optimizer states remain in FP32. Vector operations (layernorm, softmax, GELU, residual add) remain in Bfloat16 or FP32.

The paper emphasizes a critical friction-reduction property:

> "The training is done using the ADAM optimizer, with hyperparameters tuned for FP32. The same hyperparameters were reused for the MX format runs with no changes."

This is a stringent test: the hyperparameters (learning rate, batch size, weight decay, etc.) were optimized for FP32 training, and the MX training uses those exact same values with no adjustment. Any degradation in accuracy is purely attributable to the quantization, not to suboptimal hyperparameter settings. This also means practitioners could in principle swap FP32 for MX formats without re-tuning their training recipes.

**How the training evaluation works:** For GPT-like models at four sizes (20M, 150M, 300M/345M, 1.5B parameters), the authors train with MX formats from scratch to "efficiency" — meaning the number of training steps is determined by scaling power-laws [18], not by a fixed epoch count. The training loss curves are plotted against **normalized training steps** (0 to 1, representing the full training duration). The final losses are compared to FP32 baselines trained identically.

**The MXFP6_E3M2 configuration for training.** Table 7 and Figure 3 use MXFP6_E3M2 for weights, activations, and gradients. The choice of E3M2 over E2M3 for FP6 is deliberate: during training, gradients and activations can span broader dynamic ranges, and E3M2's 3 exponent bits provide 8 representable binades per block (vs. 4 for E2M2), which is necessary to avoid frequent overflow/clipping. The mantissa is reduced to 2 bits to compensate, trading precision for range.

**The MXFP4 + MXFP6 mixed-precision configuration.** Table 8 and Figure 4 push further: **MXFP4_E2M1 for weights, MXFP6_E3M2 for activations and gradients.** This 4-bit weight format is extremely aggressive (only 2 exponent bits and 1 mantissa bit per element). The fact that training converges with only a minor loss penalty (2.76 vs. 2.74 for the 1.5B model) is the paper's most extreme demonstration of the power of block-level scaling. The mechanism that makes this possible: the shared scale `X` adapts per-block to the local weight magnitude, so even though each element has only 1 mantissa bit (can only represent values `0, ±1, ±1.5` within each binade), the binade itself shifts per block. Across blocks, the full dynamic range of the weight matrix is covered by variation in the shared scales.

**Rounding mode difference.** The paper uses **round-half-to-nearest-even** for discriminative inference but **round-half-away-from-zero** for generative training. The choice of rounding mode affects the statistical bias of quantization errors. Round-half-to-nearest-even (the IEEE 754 default) is unbiased — positive and negative errors cancel in expectation — which is important for inference where systematic bias could shift model outputs. Round-half-away-from-zero may be chosen for training because gradient computations are already approximate (stochastic gradient descent), and the hardware implementation of round-half-away-from-zero can be simpler (no tie-breaking logic needed).

#### Mixed-Precision and Format Selection Logic

The paper's results establish an empirical taxonomy for format selection based on the operational regime and task type:

**For direct-cast inference (lowest friction):**
- **MXINT8 is the clear winner** — it matches FP32 across all models and tasks, for both discriminative and generative inference, with standard deviation overlap in all cases (Tables 2, 5, 6).
- **MXFP8_E4M3** is a close second, with minimal degradation on most tasks.
- **MXFP6_E2M3** (more mantissa) outperforms MXFP6_E3M2 for direct-cast, because inference activations have limited dynamic range and benefit more from precision than range.

**For fine-tuned inference (slightly more friction, better accuracy at narrow widths):**
- **MXFP6_E2M3 with QAT** reaches close-to-parity with FP32 on discriminative tasks (Table 4).
- Fine-tuning cannot rescue MXFP4 to near-FP32 accuracy in most cases, but it substantially closes the gap (e.g., ResNet-50 MXFP4: 42.39% direct-cast → 74.86% fine-tuned).

**For training from scratch (full MX integration):**
- **MXFP6_E3M2** (more exponent range) is required for all tensors (weights, activations, gradients) to handle gradient dynamic range.
- **MXFP4 for weights only** works when paired with MXFP6_E3M2 for activations and gradients — the weights can tolerate the extreme compression because the master FP32 copy provides a precision backstop.

This taxonomy emerges from the empirical results but is not formalized as an explicit decision procedure in the paper. It represents the paper's implicit recommendation for practitioners based on the aggregate experimental evidence.

#### Emulation Infrastructure

The paper notes that all experiments use a custom CUDA library to emulate MX formats on existing GPUs:

> "We used a custom library to emulate MX formats on existing GPUs. The library is implemented as a custom CUDA extension in PyTorch and performs quantization following Figure 2."

This is an important methodological detail. Current GPUs (at the time of writing) do not have native hardware support for MX formats — they cannot perform a fused MX dot product in a single instruction. Instead, the library emulates the MX format by:
1. Converting tensors to the target MX representation in software (applying Algorithm 1 or the error-diffusion procedure).
2. Dequantizing the MX-format tensors back to a hardware-supported format (likely FP16 or FP32) before calling the standard matrix multiplication kernels.
3. Potentially applying the operations of Figure 2 step-by-step in software.

This means the **throughput gains** of MX formats are not realized in these experiments — the emulation is for accuracy evaluation only. The paper's claims about hardware efficiency (reduced storage, reduced bandwidth, increased compute throughput) are projections based on what native MX hardware support would provide. The experimental results validate only the **accuracy** dimension of the accuracy-efficiency-friction tradeoff.

The library is open-sourced at the provided URL [7], enabling reproduction of the results. The paper notes it can be used "to reproduce the experimental results reported in this paper," which is important for an empirical validation study whose primary contribution is the breadth of benchmarking evidence.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: The Three-Way Tradeoff as an Evaluation Framework, Not Just Design Criteria

The paper's most distinctive intellectual contribution is not any specific format or result, but rather the **explicit elevation of user friction to a first-class evaluation axis alongside accuracy and hardware efficiency.** The field has long understood the accuracy-efficiency tradeoff — quantize more aggressively, get lower accuracy but better throughput. What makes the MX evaluation framework novel is that it treats friction as a dimension that can be quantified, varied systematically, and optimized *against* the other two goals, rather than treating it as a qualitative afterthought.

Consider how the paper operationalizes friction. Direct-cast inference is assigned a specific operational definition: take a pretrained FP32 model, apply Algorithm 1 to both weights and activations, evaluate immediately with no calibration data and no fine-tuning. Error-diffusion PTQ raises friction slightly by adding a one-pass calibration step but explicitly avoids a training loop or hyperparameter tuning. Fine-tuned inference raises it further with a few epochs of QAT. Training from scratch represents the lowest-friction training regime because it reuses FP32-optimized hyperparameters without modification. Each of these regimes is tested across the same formats, so the paper produces a **3D map of the (accuracy, efficiency, friction) surface** — you can read off, for a given accuracy target and friction tolerance, what format is viable.

**Why this framing is a conceptual advance.** Prior quantization research nearly always treats accuracy as the dependent variable: "we propose method X and it achieves Y% accuracy at Z bits." The user's effort to deploy method X is not measured — it's either hand-waved as "practical" or ignored entirely. This creates a systematic bias toward complex methods that require per-model calibration, architecture modifications, or hyperparameter sweeps, because those methods can claim accuracy improvements without accounting for the deployment cost. The MX paper inverts this: by making friction an explicit, varied independent variable, it reveals that **simple approaches at slightly wider bit widths can dominate complex approaches at narrower widths when friction is accounted for.** MXINT8 direct-cast doesn't just match FP32 — it does so with *zero* friction, which is a fundamentally different value proposition than a 4-bit method that requires weeks of fine-tuning.

**Evidence anchoring:** The friction gradation is embedded in the table structure itself. Table 2 (direct-cast, lowest friction) shows MXINT8 matching FP32 everywhere. Table 4 (fine-tuned, higher friction) shows that MXFP6 can then close the gap. The paper is implicitly arguing: don't pay friction costs you don't need. If MXINT8 works direct-cast, use it; only pay the fine-tuning cost if you need the additional compression of MXFP6. This decision logic — select the lowest-friction option that meets your accuracy target — is intuitive but was not formalized in prior quantization literature.

---

### Innovation 2: Block-Level Scaling as a Sufficient Condition for Sub-8-Bit Training Viability

The paper's headline empirical result — training generative language models to FP32 parity with sub-8-bit weights, activations, and gradients — is more than a performance milestone. It constitutes **evidence that per-block scale adaptation is a sufficient mechanism to make sub-8-bit training viable, without requiring any changes to the training algorithm, architecture, or hyperparameters.** This is a fundamental finding about the nature of the numerical precision bottleneck in deep learning training.

**What the field believed before this paper.** Prior work had established that 8-bit training was possible with per-tensor scaling (Micikevicius et al., FP8 training [1]), but the consensus was that sub-8-bit training would require more sophisticated techniques: learned quantization step sizes, gradient-aware quantization, stochastic rounding, or architectural modifications like outlier-aware normalization. The intuition was that training dynamics — especially gradient flow and weight updates — are too sensitive to tolerate the severe precision loss of 6-bit or 4-bit quantization, regardless of how you scale. The paper's result contradicts this intuition. By simply partitioning tensors into 32-element blocks and giving each block its own E8M0 scale, with no other modifications, training converges to FP32-matching loss at 1.5B scale.

**Why this is a fundamental finding, not an incremental improvement.** The result implies that the "numerical sensitivity" of training is primarily a **tensor-level dynamic range problem, not a per-element precision problem.** In other words, the reason FP16 works and INT8 with per-tensor scaling fails at training is not that each weight or gradient needs 16 bits of precision individually — it's that within a single tensor, some elements are orders of magnitude larger than others, and a single per-tensor scale forces you to choose between clipping the large values or quantizing the small ones to zero. Block-level scaling solves this by letting each block adapt its scale to its local maximum, so no elements are catastrophically clipped or zeroed. Once this is done, each element only needs enough precision to capture the *residual variation within its block*, which turns out to be surprisingly small — 2 mantissa bits (E3M2 for FP6) or even 1 mantissa bit (E2M1 for FP4 weights) suffices.

**The implicit diagnostic finding.** The paper never states this explicitly, but the results constitute a diagnosis: the dominant failure mode of sub-8-bit training with per-tensor scaling is **inter-block dynamic range mismatch**, not intra-block precision loss. The evidence is that MXFP6_E3M2 (with only 2 mantissa bits) trains to parity while a hypothetical per-tensor-scaled FP6 would fail catastrophically — the only difference is the block-level scale, so the scale is the necessary and sufficient mechanism.

**Evidence anchoring.** Table 7 shows MXFP6_E3M2 matching FP32 loss at all four model scales (GPT-20M through GPT-1.5B), with the same hyperparameters as FP32 training. Table 8 pushes to MXFP4 weights (1 mantissa bit) with only 0.02 loss degradation at 1.5B scale. The fact that no training recipe modifications were needed is the key evidence for sufficiency — block-level scaling alone closed the gap.

### Innovation 3: The Decoupling of Storage Precision and Computation Precision Through the Master Weight Copy

A subtle but consequential design insight in the MX training compute flow (Figure 2) is the **explicit architectural separation between the storage representation used for optimizer accumulation (FP32 master weights) and the computation representation used for forward/backward dot products (MX-quantized weights).** This idea itself is not novel — mixed-precision training with FP16 master weights in FP32 is a standard technique. But the MX paper extends this principle to an extreme: the computation representation can be **4-bit** (MXFP4) while the storage representation remains FP32, and training still converges.

**Why this is a conceptual shift.** The conventional mental model of quantization is that you are "compressing the model" — the quantized weights *are* the model, and the FP32 weights are discarded after training. For inference, this model holds. But for training, the MX flow reveals a different mental model: the quantized weights are **ephemeral computation operands**, derived on-the-fly from the master copy and immediately discarded after the matrix multiplication. The "real" model lives in FP32; the MX format is just a compute accelerator format. This decoupling means the quantization format can be far more aggressive than would be tolerable if it were the sole storage format, because any information lost in one training step's quantization can be recovered in the next step when the master weights (updated by the optimizer at FP32) are re-quantized.

**The implication for hardware design.** This decoupling implies that native MX hardware should support a specific pattern: a "quantize-dot product-dequantize" pipeline where the FP32 master weights are converted to MX blocks on every forward/backward pass, the dot product is computed in MX, and the output is produced in FP32/Bfloat16. The MX format is never stored persistently for training — it's a transient representation in the compute data path. This is a different hardware requirement than inference, where MX weights *are* stored persistently and loaded directly. The paper does not explore this distinction explicitly, but it is implicit in the compute flow diagram and has significant implications for hardware architecture.

**Evidence anchoring.** The training results with MXFP4 weights (Table 8, Figure 4) are the strongest evidence for this decoupling. At 4 bits per weight element with only 1 mantissa bit, the quantized weight representation is extremely lossy — most weight values can only be approximately represented as 0, ±X, or ±1.5X (the only representable values in FP4 E2M1). Yet training converges because the optimizer is updating the FP32 master copy at full precision, and the quantization is re-applied fresh at each step, allowing weight trajectories to navigate through the quantization grid over time. If the 4-bit weights were the sole storage, with no master copy, this would be impossible — weight updates smaller than the quantization step size would be lost entirely.

### Innovation 4: Empirical Evidence That Mantissa Bits Matter More for Inference, Exponent Bits More for Training

The paper's comparison of FP6 variants across inference and training regimes yields a **non-obvious taxonomy** that had not been empirically established at this breadth: E2M3 (more mantissa, less exponent) outperforms E3M2 (more exponent, less mantissa) for direct-cast inference, but E3M2 is required for training. This is not just a minor hyperparameter finding — it reveals a fundamental difference in the numerical demands of inference versus training that has implications for format design.

**Prior assumptions.** The standard approach in format design — exemplified by the OCP FP8 specification [1] — is to allocate more exponent bits to gradients (E5M2) than to weights and activations (E4M3), based on the intuition that gradients have wider dynamic range. But prior to this paper, there was no systematic evidence about whether this principle extends to sub-8-bit formats, or how it interacts with block-level scaling. One might have guessed that block-level scaling obviates the need for wide exponent ranges in the element format, since the shared scale can absorb the dynamic range. If so, more mantissa bits would always be better.

**What the results show.** For inference (Tables 2, 5, 6), the mantissa-heavy E2M3 variant indeed outperforms E3M2 consistently. This is consistent with the hypothesis that the per-block shared scale handles the dynamic range, making intra-block precision the binding constraint. But for training (Table 7, Figure 3), the paper uses E3M2 exclusively, implying that E2M3 failed for training (the paper does not report a direct head-to-head for training, but the format choice is telling). The reason: during training, the *within-block* dynamic range — the variation among the 32 elements in a single block — can be large enough that a 2-exponent-bit format (covering only 4 binades) causes overflow or underflow for elements that share the same scale but differ substantially in magnitude. This is a limitation that block-level scaling cannot fix, because the scale is shared within the block. The element format's own exponent range must be sufficient to cover the local variation.

**The diagnostic implication.** This finding reveals that block-level scaling addresses **inter-block** dynamic range but not **intra-block** dynamic range. For inference, intra-block variation is small because weights and activations are relatively smooth. For training, gradients can be spiky — a single block might contain both very small and very large gradient values, and the shared scale cannot help because it must be set based on the maximum, leaving the small values vulnerable to underflow. The practical consequence is that format designers cannot simply add block scaling and then minimize element exponent bits indefinitely; there is a floor determined by intra-block gradient variation during training.

**Evidence anchoring.** The consistent use of E2M3 for inference and E3M2 for training across all experiments, combined with the direct-cast inference tables showing E2M3 > E3M2, establishes this taxonomy empirically. The paper does not present an ablation where E2M3 is tested for training and shown to fail, which is a minor evidential gap, but the format choice is documented clearly enough to support the claim.

### Innovation 5: The Standardization Imperative as a Research Contribution

The paper's final contribution is not technical but institutional: by evaluating a consortium-developed open standard across a breadth of workloads that no single prior micro-scaling paper approached, it **converts micro-scaling from a research technique into a viable target for hardware investment.** This is a category of contribution that is undervalued in academic evaluation but is arguably the paper's primary purpose.

**What prior work missed.** Each prior micro-scaling paper [2, 3, 4, 5] proposed its own format with its own block size, scale encoding, element format, and conversion algorithm. A hardware designer looking at this literature would face a paralyzing question: which of these bespoke formats should I build silicon for? Each paper demonstrated good results on its chosen benchmarks, but no paper demonstrated that *a single format* works across CNNs, transformers, LSTMs, recommendation models, speech models, and language models, for both inference and training, across model scales from 20M to 175B parameters. Without this breadth of evidence, the risk of standardizing on any one format was unacceptably high — a format that works for ResNet-50 might fail for GPT-3, and a failed hardware investment in a narrow format is extraordinarily expensive.

**What this paper provides.** The paper evaluates exactly one format family — the OCP MX specification [6] — across exactly that breadth. Every table, from direct-cast Translation (Table 2) through GPT-3-175B inference (Table 5) to GPT-1.5B training (Table 7), uses the same concrete formats with the same block size (32), the same scale format (E8M0), and the same conversion algorithm (Algorithm 1). The consistent message — MXINT8 works as a drop-in everywhere; MXFP6 works for fine-tuned inference everywhere and for training at scale; MXFP4 works for weight-only training compression — provides the confidence that hardware implementing the MX standard will be broadly useful, not narrowly optimized for a specific model architecture.

**Why this is a genuine contribution, not just "testing."** In an academic context, "evaluation" is often seen as lower-status than "invention." But in the context of hardware standardization, broad evaluation *is* the bottleneck. The intellectual work here is in designing an evaluation suite that covers the axes that matter for the standard's adoption: task diversity (vision, language, speech, recommendations), model scale (20M to 175B), operational regime (inference, fine-tuning, training), and friction level (direct-cast, PTQ, QAT, from-scratch training). The paper's 26-author consortium from six hardware companies signals that this is not a post-hoc academic evaluation of an existing standard, but rather the evidentiary foundation *for* the standard's adoption — the paper exists to make the case that the standard is worth the silicon.

**Evidence anchoring.** The sheer breadth of Table 2 (13 models across 5 task families, 5 format variants) and the inclusion of GPT-3-175B (Table 5) and LLaMA-7B (Table 6) — models that are expensive to evaluate and rarely appear in quantization papers — is itself evidence of the institutional commitment behind the standardization effort. No academic lab could (or would) run this evaluation suite. The paper's contribution is making that evaluation public, reproducible, and tied to an open specification.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Datasets.** The benchmark suite spans two broad task categories. **Discriminative tasks** include language translation on WMT-17 (Transformer, GNMT), text encoding on Wikipedia (BERT-Base, BERT-Large), image classification on ImageNet ILSVRC12 (DeiT-Tiny, DeiT-Small, ResNet-18, ResNet-50, MobileNet v2), speech recognition on LibriSpeech (Wav2Vec 2.0), and recommendation on Criteo Terabyte (DLRM). **Generative tasks** include Lambada (long-range prediction), Wikitext-2 (language modeling perplexity), ARC Easy and Challenge (multiple-choice science questions), and Hendryck's Test subset (College Computer Science, International Law, Jurisprudence). For training experiments, GPT-like models are trained on an unspecified language modeling corpus to evaluate loss convergence.

- **Base models.** Discriminative experiments use standard architectures at commonly reported scales: Transformer-Base and Transformer-Large for translation, BERT-Base and BERT-Large for encoding, DeiT-Tiny and DeiT-Small for vision transformers, ResNet-18, ResNet-50, and MobileNet v2 for CNNs, Wav2Vec 2.0 for speech, and DLRM for recommendations. Generative inference uses **OpenAI GPT-3-175B** and **LLaMA-7B** — the inclusion of a 175B-parameter model is notable because it tests the formats at deployment scale where quantization errors could compound across many layers. Generative training uses GPT-like models at **20M, 150M, 300M/345M, and 1.5B parameters**, spanning three orders of magnitude in scale, with training steps determined by scaling power-laws rather than fixed epochs to match FP32 baselines at each scale.

- **Metrics.** For discriminative tasks, the paper uses task-standard metrics: **BLEU score** (higher is better) for translation, **F-1 score** for BERT question answering, **Top-1 accuracy (%)** for image classification, **Word Error Rate (WER, lower is better)** for speech recognition, and **AUC (Area Under the ROC Curve)** for recommendation. For generative inference, all tasks use **accuracy (%)** except Lambada (also accuracy) and Wikitext (perplexity, lower is better). For generative training, the metric is **language model loss** (cross-entropy), compared at the end of training after scaling-law-determined steps. Crucially, for GPT-3-175B and LLaMA-7B generative inference, each number is reported with **± bootstrap-estimated standard deviation**, providing statistical confidence intervals that are essential given the small effective sample sizes in evaluation sets like Hendryck's Test (where individual task subsets may have only ~100 questions each).

- **Baselines.** The universal baseline is **FP32** — the pretrained or trained-from-scratch model using 32-bit floating-point for all operations. For inference experiments, this means the FP32 model evaluated without any quantization. For training experiments, this means the identical model architecture and training recipe trained entirely in FP32 (with ADAM optimizer, hyperparameters tuned for FP32). The paper compares against this single baseline across all formats; there are no comparisons against competing quantization methods (e.g., per-tensor INT8, GPTQ, AWQ, per-channel quantization, VS-Quant, or other micro-scaling variants from prior work), which is a deliberate choice reflecting the paper's goal of validating the MX standard against an unquantized reference point rather than benchmarking against alternative quantization approaches.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper does not use a "generation budget" or token-count metric for comparing methods, since all evaluations compare accuracy at equivalent bit widths. Instead, the relevant resource accounting is **bits per element**, reported as the amortized storage cost including the 8-bit shared scale overhead per 32-element block. For MXFP6, this is 6.25 bits/element (6 bits per element + 8/32 = 0.25 bits for the scale); for MXFP4, it is 4.25 bits/element. The paper's core comparisons are across different MX formats at their respective bit widths, with no FLOPs-matched or latency-matched comparisons between, say, MXFP8 and MXFP6 to determine whether the narrower format's additional compression justifies its accuracy loss at equal compute. For training, the compute budget is held constant in terms of training steps (scaling-law-determined), so all formats are compared at identical optimizer steps, not at equal wall-clock time or equal FLOPs.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** There is no cross-validation or held-out validation procedure for hyperparameter selection reported in the paper. For direct-cast inference and error-diffusion inference, there is no tuning phase — the formats are applied directly and evaluated once on the test set. For fine-tuned inference, the paper notes that "hyperparameter exploration is used to find proper finetuning hyperparameters," but does not describe a train/validation/test split or cross-validation procedure to prevent overfitting the fine-tuning hyperparameters to the test set. For training from scratch, the same FP32-optimized hyperparameters are reused with no modification, so no validation is performed. The bootstrap confidence intervals reported for GPT-3-175B and LLaMA-7B (Table 5 and Table 6) provide within-dataset variance estimates but do not address generalization across datasets or hyperparameter choices. For the smaller discriminative models, no confidence intervals or error bars are reported, making it difficult to assess whether small differences (e.g., Transformer-Base MXFP6_E2M3 direct-cast: 26.38 vs. FP32: 26.85, a difference of 0.47 BLEU) are statistically significant or within the noise floor of the benchmark.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Direct-Cast Inference on Discriminative Tasks (Table 2)

The headline finding is that **MXINT8 matches FP32 accuracy across all 13 discriminative model-task combinations with no calibration or fine-tuning.** The results from Table 2 show:

- **Language Translation (Transformer-Base, WMT-17):** FP32 achieves 26.85 BLEU; MXINT8 achieves 26.64 (a difference of 0.21 BLEU). MXFP8_E4M3 drops to 26.27 (-0.58), MXFP8_E5M2 drops further to 25.75 (-1.10), and MXFP6 variants achieve 26.38 (E2M3, -0.47) and 25.97 (E3M2, -0.88). MXFP4 suffers substantially at 22.68 (-4.17). Transformer-Large shows similar patterns but is more robust: FP32 27.63, MXINT8 27.56 (-0.07), MXFP8_E4M3 27.44 (-0.19), MXFP6_E2M3 27.52 (-0.11). The GNMT LSTM model is remarkably insensitive: FP32 24.44, MXINT8 24.52 (+0.08), MXFP6_E2M3 24.51 (+0.07), MXFP4 23.75 (-0.69).

- **Image Classification (ResNet-50, ImageNet):** FP32 achieves 77.40% Top-1; MXINT8 achieves 77.27% (-0.13 percentage points). MXFP8_E4M3 drops to 75.94% (-1.46 pp), MXFP6_E2M3 to 76.42% (-0.98 pp), and MXFP4 to 42.39% (-35.01 pp). The degradation is model-dependent: DeiT-Small (vision transformer) is more robust (FP32 80.54%, MXINT8 80.56%, MXFP6_E2M3 80.11%) than MobileNet v2 (FP32 72.14%, MXINT8 71.61%, MXFP6_E2M3 67.76%, MXFP4 0.25% — essentially random). This model-dependent sensitivity is a key finding: lightweight architectures with depthwise separable convolutions (MobileNet v2) are substantially more sensitive to quantization than standard convolutions or attention-based models.

- **Speech Recognition (Wav2Vec 2.0, LibriSpeech):** FP32 achieves 18.90 WER; MXINT8 achieves 18.83 (slightly better, -0.07 WER). MXFP8_E4M3 degrades significantly to 23.71 (+4.81 WER), and MXFP6_E2M3 to 20.63 (+1.73). This is notable because it suggests the speech model's activations have more dynamic range or more sensitive features than the vision and language models, making it less tolerant of narrow floating-point formats despite MXINT8 working well.

- **Recommendation (DLRM, Criteo):** FP32 achieves 0.803 AUC; MXINT8 achieves 0.803 (identical to 3 decimal places). Even MXFP4 achieves 0.7947, a remarkably small degradation (-0.0083 AUC) given the extreme compression.

**The mantissa-versus-exponent finding for direct-cast:** Across all discriminative models in Table 2, the format variant with more mantissa bits consistently outperforms the variant with more exponent bits at the same total width. MXFP8_E4M3 (3 mantissa bits) outperforms MXFP8_E5M2 (2 mantissa bits) on every model except GNMT (where they tie). MXFP6_E2M3 (3 mantissa bits) outperforms MXFP6_E3M2 (2 mantissa bits) on all 13 models. This establishes that for static inference tensors, the per-block shared scale handles dynamic range sufficiently well that intra-block precision (mantissa bits) is the binding constraint — more exponent bits provide no benefit and waste bits that could have gone to mantissa precision.

#### Error-Diffusion PTQ Inference (Table 3)

The error-diffusion results in Table 3 demonstrate that a one-pass post-training quantization algorithm can substantially close the gap between direct-cast and FP32, particularly for MXFP4:

- **DeiT-Tiny (ImageNet):** MXFP6_E2M3 achieves 72.16% (matching FP32 exactly at 72.16%, a note of caution about possible overfitting or lucky rounding since the direct-cast result was 71.56%). MXFP6_E3M2 achieves 71.29% (vs. 70.16% direct-cast). MXFP4 improves dramatically from 56.72% direct-cast to 64.76% with error diffusion — an 8.04 percentage point gain, though still far from FP32.

- **ResNet-50:** MXFP6_E2M3 achieves 77.15% (FP32: 77.40%, -0.25 pp); MXFP4 improves from 42.39% direct-cast to 69.99% with error diffusion — a 27.6 pp gain that makes 4-bit inference feasible for this model where direct-cast was catastrophically bad.

- **MobileNet v2:** MXFP6_E2M3 achieves 70.22% (FP32: 72.14%, -1.92 pp), better than direct-cast MXINT8 (71.61%). MXFP4 barely improves: 18.88% with error diffusion vs. 0.25% direct-cast — both essentially non-functional.

- **Wav2Vec 2.0:** MXFP6_E2M3 achieves 19.09 WER (FP32: 18.90, +0.19 WER), substantially better than direct-cast (20.63). MXFP4 achieves 24.39 WER (+5.49 vs. FP32), compared to 42.62 direct-cast.

The key insight from Table 3 is that error diffusion provides larger gains at narrower bit widths: the direct-cast-to-PTQ gap for MXFP4 is massive (27.6 pp for ResNet-50, ~18 pp for Wav2Vec), while for MXFP6 the gains are modest (0.5–2 pp). This suggests that error diffusion primarily helps by recovering from the catastrophic failures of coarse quantization (values pushed to zero or saturated) rather than by fine-grained precision recovery.

#### Direct-Cast Generative Inference on Large Language Models (Tables 5 and 6)

The generative inference results on GPT-3-175B and LLaMA-7B extend the discriminative findings to the regime where deployment efficiency matters most. The headline is that **MXINT8 matches FP32 within bootstrap standard deviation on all tasks for both models**, confirming that 8-bit block-scaled integers are universally applicable as a drop-in replacement.

**GPT-3-175B (Table 5):**

- **ARC Easy:** FP32 0.744 ± 0.009; MXINT8 0.740 ± 0.009 (overlapping confidence intervals). MXFP8 0.738 ± 0.009; MXFP6 0.737 ± 0.009 — all overlapping. Even MXFP4 achieves 0.748 ± 0.010, which is actually higher than the FP32 point estimate though within error bars.
- **ARC Challenge:** FP32 0.480 ± 0.015; MXINT8 0.481 ± 0.015. MXFP6 0.480 ± 0.015 — exact point estimate match. MXFP4 drops to 0.425 ± 0.014, a meaningful degradation outside the error bars (-0.055, roughly one standard deviation).
- **Lambada:** FP32 0.755 ± 0.006; MXINT8 0.754 ± 0.006 (overlapping). MXFP8 drops noticeably to 0.708 ± 0.006 (outside error bars, -0.047). MXFP6 achieves 0.745 ± 0.006 (overlapping). MXFP4 drops to 0.623 ± 0.007, a large and clearly significant degradation (-0.132).
- **Hendryck's Test subsets:** The small sample sizes produce wide error bars (±0.043–0.049), making most differences statistically insignificant. MXINT8 overlaps FP32 on all three subsets. Notably, MXFP4 degrades on Jurisprudence (0.324 vs. FP32 0.454) but is roughly comparable on College CS (0.240 vs. 0.360 — within overlapping error bars given the ±0.043–0.049 margins).

**Mixed-precision results (columns 5–7 of Tables 5 and 6):** The paper explores how asymmetric format allocation can recover accuracy closer to FP32:
- **MXFP6 weights + MXFP8 activations:** Generally produces results intermediate between pure MXFP8 and pure MXFP6, as expected.
- **MXFP4 weights + MXFP8 activations:** On GPT-3-175B, this configuration actually achieves ARC Easy 0.749 ± 0.009 (vs. FP32 0.744), ARC Challenge 0.486 ± 0.015 (vs. 0.480), and MXFP4 weights + MXFP6 activations achieves 0.487 ± 0.015 on ARC Challenge — all overlapping FP32. This is a striking result: by keeping activations at higher precision, weights can be aggressively compressed to 4 bits with minimal impact on multiple-choice question answering.
- **MXFP4 weights + MXFP8 activations on Lambada:** Drops to 0.754 ± 0.006, matching FP32 exactly — the mixed-precision configuration recovers the loss from pure MXFP4 (0.623). This suggests Lambada's degradation with pure MXFP4 is dominantly due to weight quantization rather than activation quantization.

**LLaMA-7B (Table 6):**

The LLaMA-7B results broadly mirror GPT-3-175B: MXINT8 overlaps FP32 on all tasks; MXFP8 and MXFP6 show minor degradations; MXFP4 shows substantial but not catastrophic degradation on most tasks. The Wikitext perplexity results are particularly informative:
- **Wikitext perplexity (lower is better):** FP32 achieves 9.488; MXINT8 achieves 9.504 (+0.016, negligible); MXFP8 9.768 (+0.280); MXFP6 9.628 (+0.140); MXFP4 27.201 (+17.713). The MXFP4 perplexity explosion confirms that 4-bit quantization of both weights and activations is too aggressive for language modeling where precise next-token probabilities matter. Mixed-precision configurations partially recover: MXFP4 weights + MXFP8 activations: 11.476; MXFP4 weights + MXFP6 activations: 11.147. This is still substantially degraded from FP32 but orders of magnitude better than pure MXFP4.

**An important methodological note:** For LLaMA-7B, the paper states that "the Softmax function was not quantized to Bfloat16" (Table 6 caption). This suggests other models may have had Softmax quantized or computed in reduced precision, which could affect comparisons — the LLaMA-7B results may be slightly optimistic relative to a fully quantized pipeline if Softmax precision matters for accuracy.

#### Fine-Tuned Inference on Discriminative Tasks (Table 4)

Quantization-aware fine-tuning for a few epochs enables MXFP6 to reach close-to-parity with FP32 across discriminative models, and substantially improves MXFP4 from its direct-cast baseline:

- **Transformer-Base (WMT-17):** MXFP6_E2M3 fine-tuned achieves 26.98 BLEU, slightly *exceeding* the FP32 baseline of 26.85. MXFP6_E3M2 achieves 27.01, also above FP32. MXFP4 reaches 25.97, a substantial improvement over direct-cast (22.68) but still -0.88 BLEU below FP32.
- **Transformer-Large:** MXFP6_E2M3 reaches 27.60 (FP32: 27.63), MXFP6_E3M2 27.62 — both within 0.03 BLEU of FP32. MXFP4 reaches 27.33 (-0.30).
- **DeiT-Small (ImageNet):** MXFP6_E2M3 achieves 80.43% (FP32: 80.54%, -0.11 pp), MXFP6_E3M2 79.76% (-0.78 pp). MXFP4 reaches 77.61% (-2.93 pp), a large improvement over direct-cast (71.35%) but still meaningfully degraded.
- **ResNet-50:** MXFP6_E2M3 achieves 77.27% (FP32: 77.40%), MXFP4 reaches 74.86% (vs. 42.39% direct-cast) — fine-tuning recovers most but not all of the 35 pp direct-cast gap.
- **MobileNet v2:** MXFP6_E2M3 achieves 71.49% (FP32: 72.14%, -0.65 pp), MXFP6_E3M2 70.27% (-1.87 pp), MXFP4 65.41% (-6.73 pp). MobileNet v2 remains the most quantization-sensitive model even after fine-tuning.
- **Wav2Vec 2.0:** MXFP6_E3M2 fine-tuned achieves 21.46 WER, which is worse than the direct-cast MXFP6_E2M3 result (20.63, from Table 2). This apparent contradiction indicates that (a) the fine-tuning was only applied to the E3M2 variant, not the E2M3 variant (which did well enough direct-cast to not need fine-tuning, as indicated by the "N/A" entries), and (b) fine-tuning on E3M2 could not close the gap to the base model because the format simply lacks sufficient precision for this task.

**The "N/A" entries in Table 4** indicate cases where direct-cast already performed sufficiently well that fine-tuning was not pursued. These include MXINT8 across all models, MXFP8 for Transformer-Large and GNMT, and MXFP6_E2M3 for GNMT and Wav2Vec 2.0. This reinforces the paper's friction-gradation framework: don't pay the fine-tuning cost if direct-cast already works.

#### Generative Training from Scratch (Tables 7–8, Figures 3–4)

This is the paper's headline quantitative result. The findings are organized by format configuration:

**MXFP6_E3M2 for weights, activations, and gradients (Table 7, Figure 3):**

The core claim: training GPT-like models from scratch with MXFP6_E3M2 on all GEMM tensors (forward pass weights and activations, backward pass gradients) matches FP32 loss to within 0.01–0.04 across four model scales, with no hyperparameter changes.

- **GPT-20M:** FP32 loss 3.98; MXFP6_E2M3 4.02 (+0.04); MXFP6_E3M2 4.01 (+0.03).
- **GPT-150M:** FP32 3.30; MXFP6_E2M3 3.33 (+0.03); MXFP6_E3M2 3.32 (+0.02).
- **GPT-300M (345M in Figure 3):** FP32 3.11; MXFP6_E2M3 3.13 (+0.02); MXFP6_E3M2 3.12 (+0.01).
- **GPT-1.5B:** FP32 2.74; MXFP6_E2M3 2.75 (+0.01); MXFP6_E3M2 2.75 (+0.01).

Several observations from these numbers:

1. **The gap narrows with model scale.** At 20M parameters, the loss difference is +0.03–0.04; at 1.5B, it is +0.01. This is consistent with larger models having more redundant parameters and being more tolerant of quantization noise — a well-known phenomenon in quantization literature, but demonstrated here for the first time at sub-8-bit training precision.

2. **E3M2 is slightly but consistently better than E2M3 for training**, with lower loss at every model scale (3.32 vs. 3.33 at 150M, 2.75 vs. 2.75 tie at 1.5B). This is the reverse of the inference finding and justifies the paper's choice of E3M2 for training — the extra exponent range matters for gradient dynamics.

3. **The loss curves (Figure 3) track FP32 throughout training**, not just at the final checkpoint. The curves for FP32 and both MXFP6 variants are nearly indistinguishable at all four model scales when plotted against normalized training steps, indicating that the quantization introduces no systematic training instability or convergence slowdown. A divergence in training trajectories (even if final loss matched) would be a warning sign for larger-scale or longer-duration training.

**MXFP4 weights + MXFP6_E3M2 activations and gradients (Table 8, Figure 4):**

Pushing to 4-bit weights (E2M1 format — 1 sign, 2 exponent, 1 mantissa bits) with 6-bit activations and gradients:

- **GPT-20M:** FP32 3.98; MXFP4+MXFP6 4.04 (+0.06).
- **GPT-150M:** FP32 3.30; MXFP4+MXFP6 3.33 (+0.03).
- **GPT-300M:** FP32 3.11; MXFP4+MXFP6 3.14 (+0.03).
- **GPT-1.5B:** FP32 2.74; MXFP4+MXFP6 2.76 (+0.02).

The key finding: **4-bit weights are viable for training when activations and gradients remain at 6 bits.** The loss penalty is small (+0.02 to +0.06) and narrows with scale. This is the first demonstration that generative language models can be trained with 4-bit weight precision to near-FP32 quality.

The loss curves in Figure 4 show a slightly wider gap between MXFP4+MXFP6 and FP32 compared to the pure MXFP6 training (Figure 3), but the gap remains small and does not widen over the course of training — the 4-bit weight training converges stably rather than diverging. This is evidence that the master FP32 weight copy (Figure 2) and periodic re-quantization provide a sufficient precision safety net.

**A notable absence:** The paper does not report training results with MXFP4 for activations or gradients. The mixed-precision configuration always pairs MXFP4 weights with MXFP6 (or wider) for the other tensors. This implies that the authors tested MXFP4 activations/gradients and found them non-viable for training, but this negative result is not reported. The limitation is acknowledged implicitly by the format choice — the absence of a "pure MXFP4 training" row in Table 7 is itself informative.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Mantissa vs. exponent allocation within fixed bit width (Tables 2, 4, 7):** The paper implicitly tests this through the use of format variants (E4M3 vs. E5M2 at 8 bits; E2M3 vs. E3M2 at 6 bits). The direct-cast inference results (Table 2) constitute a 13-model ablation: E2M3 (more mantissa) outperforms E3M2 (more exponent) on all 13 discriminative models, with average gaps ranging from 0.1 to 0.5 points depending on the metric. This establishes that for inference, the per-block shared scale handles dynamic range adequately, making mantissa precision the binding constraint. The training results (Table 7) constitute a 4-model-scale ablation: E3M2 achieves lower loss than E2M3 at 3 of 4 scales (20M, 150M, 300M), with the 1.5B model tying. This establishes that for training, the element format's own exponent range matters — gradients have sufficient intra-block dynamic range that 2 exponent bits are occasionally insufficient even with block-level scaling.

**Rounding mode (Section 4.3 vs. 4.5):** The paper uses round-half-to-nearest-even for discriminative inference and round-half-away-from-zero for generative training. This is not presented as a formal ablation (no results compare the two rounding modes on the same task), but the choice is noted as a design decision. The absence of a rounding-mode sensitivity study is a gap — the paper cannot claim that the training results are robust to rounding mode choice, or that the inference results would hold with round-half-away-from-zero (which might be preferred for hardware simplicity). A single table comparing accuracy under both rounding modes on a representative subset of models would have addressed this.

**Model scale as an implicit ablation (Tables 7, 8; Figures 3, 4):** Training results at 20M, 150M, 300M/345M, and 1.5B parameters serve as a scale-sensitivity analysis. The finding that the FP32-MX loss gap narrows with increasing model size (from +0.03–0.04 at 20M to +0.01 at 1.5B for MXFP6_E3M2) suggests that larger models — which are of greater practical interest — are *more* robust to MX quantization, not less. This is a crucial robustness result because it argues against the concern that quantization errors might compound unfavorably in deeper networks.

**Architecture diversity as an implicit ablation (Table 2):** The 13 discriminative models span five architecture families (encoder-decoder transformers, encoder-only transformers, vision transformers, CNNs including MobileNet, LSTMs, MLPs). The consistent pattern — MXINT8 matches FP32 everywhere, MXFP6_E2M3 performs well but with some degradation, MXFP4 fails severely on certain architectures — tests whether the MX format family generalizes across architectural diversity. The key negative finding is that MobileNet v2 (depthwise separable convolutions) and Wav2Vec 2.0 (speech features) are substantially more sensitive, indicating that the format is not architecture-agnostic at the narrowest widths.

**Error diffusion as a recovery mechanism (Table 3 vs. Table 2):** Although not presented as a formal ablation, comparing direct-cast (Table 2) and error-diffusion (Table 3) results on the same models reveals the marginal benefit of PTQ. The results show that error diffusion provides large gains for MXFP4 (ResNet-50: 42.39% → 69.99%, a 27.6 pp recovery) but modest gains for MXFP6 (ResNet-50: 76.42% → 77.15%, a 0.73 pp improvement). This pattern suggests error diffusion is primarily addressing catastrophic quantization failures (values quantized to zero or saturated extremes) rather than fine-grained precision recovery, which is consistent with the algorithm's design of redistributing quantization error within a block.

**Fine-tuning as a recovery mechanism (Table 4 vs. Table 2):** The marginal benefit of quantization-aware fine-tuning over direct-cast is visible by cross-referencing Tables 2 and 4. For MXFP6_E2M3, fine-tuning provides gains of 0.5–2.0 percentage points for image models (DeiT-Tiny: 71.56% → 72.09%; ResNet-50: 76.42% → 77.27%) and can push accuracy slightly *above* FP32 in some cases (Transformer-Base BLEU: 26.38 direct-cast → 26.98 fine-tuned vs. 26.85 FP32). For MXFP4, fine-tuning provides massive gains (ResNet-50: 42.39% → 74.86%; MobileNet v2: 0.25% → 65.41%) but still leaves a significant gap to FP32, indicating that fine-tuning can recover from severe quantization error but cannot fully compensate for the information loss at 4 bits for the most sensitive models.

**Mixed-precision as a mechanism study (Tables 5, 6, 8):** The mixed-precision columns in the generative inference tables and the MXFP4+MXFP6 training configuration serve as investigations into which tensors are the quantization bottleneck. In GPT-3-175B inference (Table 5), MXFP4 weights + MXFP8 activations substantially outperforms pure MXFP4 (Lambada: 0.754 vs. 0.623), indicating that activation precision is more critical than weight precision for Lambada accuracy. In training (Table 8), MXFP4 weights + MXFP6 activations/gradients nearly matches FP32 (loss gap +0.02 at 1.5B), indicating that weight precision can be reduced to 4 bits if activations and gradients remain at 6 bits — the activation and gradient quantization is the binding constraint for training accuracy.

### Critical Assessment

#### Claim 1: "MXINT8 is a compelling drop-in replacement for FP32 for low-friction direct-cast inference"

**Assessment: Strongly supported, but with narrow scope.** The evidence from Tables 2, 5, and 6 is unambiguous: MXINT8 matches FP32 within standard deviation on every single model-task combination tested, from 7M-parameter MobileNet v2 to 175B-parameter GPT-3, across five task families (translation, encoding, classification, speech recognition, recommendation, language modeling, multiple-choice QA). The bootstrap confidence intervals on the generative results (Tables 5 and 6) provide statistical rigor for the large-model results.

However, the claim is validated only for the specific models and tasks tested. The paper does not evaluate MXINT8 on:
- Object detection, segmentation, or other dense prediction vision tasks where spatial precision may matter more
- Video models with temporal dynamics
- Reinforcement learning or other training paradigms
- Multimodal models (vision-language, speech-text)
- Tasks requiring exact numerical equality rather than statistical equivalence (e.g., hashing, nearest-neighbor lookup)

Additionally, the paper does not explore whether MXINT8 could match FP32 at larger scales than 175B parameters. The extrapolation from 175B to (say) 1T parameters is not obvious — quantization errors could compound differently in deeper or wider networks.

#### Claim 2: "MXFP6 closely matches FP32 for inference after quantization-aware finetuning"

**Assessment: Supported with caveats about model sensitivity and task type.** Table 4 shows MXFP6_E2M3 fine-tuned to within approximately 0.5 percentage points or 0.1 BLEU of FP32 on most discriminative tasks. Transformer-Base actually exceeds FP32 after fine-tuning (26.98 vs. 26.85), which raises the question of whether the fine-tuning has overfit the test set or whether the quantization noise acts as a beneficial regularizer.

The caveat is that this claim is only validated for the seven discriminative models in Table 4 (Transformers, GNMT, DeiT, ResNet, MobileNet v2, Wav2Vec 2.0). No fine-tuned inference results are reported for the large language models (GPT-3-175B, LLaMA-7B), where fine-tuning would be expensive. The paper explicitly notes (Section 4.4): "Due to the size of these models, no quantization-aware finetuning was performed." This means the "close-to-parity after fine-tuning" claim is restricted to models at the scale where fine-tuning is feasible, which are not the models where deployment efficiency matters most. For the largest models — where 6-bit quantization would provide the greatest absolute memory savings — the paper offers only direct-cast results, which show some degradation (Lambada: 0.745 MXFP6 vs. 0.755 FP32 for GPT-3-175B).

#### Claim 3: "MXFP6 enables the first instance of training generative language models at sub-8-bit weights, activations, and gradients with minimal accuracy loss and no modifications to the training recipe"

**Assessment: The claim is technically accurate and well-supported at the evaluated scales, but "minimal accuracy loss" and "no modifications" need careful qualification.**

**What the experiments demonstrate:** Table 7 and Figure 3 show that GPT-like models from 20M to 1.5B parameters, trained from scratch with MXFP6_E3M2 for weights, activations, and gradients, achieve final loss within 0.01–0.04 of FP32 baselines. The hyperparameters are the FP32-optimized values reused without change. The loss curves track FP32 throughout training. This is a genuine "first" — prior to this paper, no published result had demonstrated end-to-end training of transformer language models with all three tensor types (weights, activations, gradients) at sub-8-bit precision while matching FP32 loss.

**What "minimal accuracy loss" means quantitatively:** At the largest scale tested (1.5B parameters), the loss difference is 0.01 (2.75 vs. 2.74). Whether this is "minimal" depends on context. In terms of perplexity, a loss difference of 0.01 corresponds to a perplexity ratio of e^0.01 ≈ 1.01, or about a 1% perplexity increase. For many downstream applications, this would be negligible. However, the paper does not evaluate downstream task performance (e.g., few-shot accuracy on benchmarks) for the trained models, only language modeling loss. It is possible that a 0.01 loss difference at 1.5B scale translates to larger gaps on downstream tasks, or that the gap widens at larger model scales (where absolute loss is lower and small differences may matter more).

**What "no modifications to the training recipe" actually means:** The paper reuses FP32-optimized hyperparameters. This is an important friction-reduction claim, but it is also a single-point validation. The fact that the FP32 hyperparameters happen to work well does not guarantee that they are *optimal* for MXFP6 training, or that the same hyperparameter transfer would work for other architectures, optimizers (e.g., SGD with momentum vs. Adam), or datasets. A genuinely robust "no modifications" claim would require testing across multiple hyperparameter configurations or showing that the FP32-MX gap is small across a range of learning rates and batch sizes — neither of which is done.

**The scale limitation:** The largest model trained from scratch is 1.5B parameters. This is three orders of magnitude smaller than GPT-3-175B and nearly two orders smaller than the largest open-source models (LLaMA-70B, Falcon-180B). The paper's direct-cast inference results show that quantization sensitivity can be scale-dependent (GPT-3-175B is more robust than smaller models on some tasks, more sensitive on others). Extrapolating the training results from 1.5B to 100B+ scales requires assuming that the loss gap continues to narrow with scale (as it does from 20M to 1.5B), but this trend is based on only four data points and could reverse if larger models exhibit different gradient dynamics or if the training duration (measured in tokens) is longer than what scaling laws prescribe.

#### Claim 4: "Training with MXFP4 weights and MXFP6 activations/gradients incurs only a minor loss penalty"

**Assessment: Supported at the evaluated scale, but "minor" is subjective and the configuration is asymmetric.** The 1.5B model achieves 2.76 vs. 2.74 FP32 loss, a gap of 0.02 — about twice the gap of the pure MXFP6 configuration. This is genuinely impressive for 4-bit weight storage, representing an 8× compression of the weight matrices (4 bits vs. 32 bits). However:

- **The claim only applies to the mixed-precision setting.** Pure MXFP4 for all tensors is not evaluated and is implied to be non-viable (otherwise it would have been reported as a headline result alongside the MXFP6 results). The practical implication is that MXFP4 is usable for *weight* compression during training but not for activation or gradient compression, limiting the total memory savings.
- **The master FP32 weight copy is still required** (Figure 2), so the total training memory is not reduced by 8× — it is reduced only for the forward/backward pass working memory, while the optimizer states and master weights remain in FP32. The paper does not quantify the end-to-end memory reduction of the MXFP4+MXFP6 configuration relative to FP32 training, which would require accounting for optimizer states, activation checkpointing, and the two weight copies (master + quantized).
- **"Minor loss penalty" is asserted but not benchmarked on downstream tasks.** The 0.02 loss difference at 1.5B might be "minor" for language modeling perplexity but could have larger impacts on few-shot learning, instruction following, or reasoning capabilities — none of which are evaluated.

#### Key Weaknesses in the Experimental Design

**1. No comparisons against competing quantization methods.** The paper compares MX formats only against FP32. It does not benchmark against per-tensor INT8, per-channel quantization, GPTQ, AWQ, VS-Quant, or any other micro-scaling variant from prior work [2, 3, 4, 5]. This makes it impossible to assess whether the MX standard's specific design choices (block size 32, E8M0 scale format, Algorithm 1 conversion) are *better* than alternatives, or merely *adequate*. For a paper whose primary purpose is to validate an open standard for hardware investment, the absence of competitive baselines is a significant gap — a hardware designer needs to know not just that MX works, but that it works *better than plausible alternatives* that might also be standardized.

**2. Single precision for FP32 baseline with no Bfloat16 comparison.** The paper uses FP32 as the sole baseline. However, much of the industry has already moved to Bfloat16 for training and FP16 for inference. The relevant practical question for many deployments is not "can MX replace FP32?" but "can MX replace Bfloat16?" A comparison against Bfloat16 training and FP16 inference would establish whether MX formats are competitive with current production practice, not just with the high-precision baseline. The omission is particularly notable for the training experiments, where Bfloat16 is the de facto standard for large-scale training.

**3. No memory or throughput measurements.** All results are accuracy-only. The paper claims MX formats improve "hardware efficiency" but provides no measured throughput, latency, or memory footprint data — even for the emulation library, which could have reported CPU/GPU memory usage with MX quantization vs. FP32. The efficiency claims are entirely projected from the bit-width ratios (6 bits vs. 32 bits = 5.3× compression), which ignores the overhead of the shared scales, the cost of the conversion step (Algorithm 1), and the fact that vector operations remain in FP32/Bfloat16. A paper arguing for hardware investment in MX formats should provide at least a back-of-the-envelope efficiency analysis calibrated against the emulation measurements.

**4. No ablation of block size.** The block size `k = 32` is used for all experiments but never varied. A block-size ablation (e.g., comparing k = 16, 32, 64) would reveal the accuracy-efficiency tradeoff of this critical design parameter. Larger blocks reduce the scale overhead (lower amortized bits per element) but reduce the format's ability to adapt to local dynamic range variation. Smaller blocks provide finer-grained adaptation at higher overhead. The absence of this ablation means the choice of 32 is justified only by reference to common hardware tile sizes, not by empirical evidence that it is optimal.

**5. No ablation of scale format (E8M0 vs. alternatives).** The shared scale uses E8M0 in all experiments. Could a narrower scale format (e.g., E6M0, or a 4-bit integer) work nearly as well? The 8-bit scale adds 0.25 bits per element in overhead — reducing it to 6 bits would save 0.0625 bits per element, which is a small absolute saving but could matter at the margin for MXFP4 where the overhead is proportionally largest. The paper provides no evidence that 8 bits for the scale is necessary rather than conservative.

**6. Limited statistical rigor for discriminative results.** Tables 2, 3, and 4 report point estimates without error bars, confidence intervals, or standard deviations. For metrics like BLEU score and Top-1 accuracy, the test-set variability can be non-trivial, and small differences (e.g., 26.85 vs. 26.64 BLEU) may or may not be statistically significant. The generative results (Tables 5 and 6) include bootstrap standard deviations, showing that many of the small differences are within error bars — but for the discriminative results, the reader has no way to know whether a 0.2 BLEU gap is noise or a real degradation.

**7. Training evaluated only on language modeling loss, not downstream capabilities.** The training experiments (Tables 7 and 8) report only language model loss. Modern LLM evaluation emphasizes downstream benchmarks (e.g., MMLU, HellaSwag, HumanEval for code, etc.) because loss improvements do not always translate to capability improvements. A model trained with MXFP6 might achieve the same loss as an FP32 model but exhibit different behavior on reasoning tasks, factual recall, or instruction following. The paper provides no evidence that MX-trained models preserve these capabilities.

**8. No investigation of whether the 0.01–0.04 loss gap compounds over longer training.** The models are trained to "efficiency" as determined by scaling power-laws. If training were continued beyond efficiency (as is often done for large models to maximize benchmark performance), would the MX-FP32 loss gap remain constant, narrow, or widen? The fixed training duration leaves open the possibility that MX-trained models have a slightly different compute-loss scaling exponent that would manifest at larger scales or longer durations.

**9. The fine-tuning results (Table 4) may overfit the test set.** The paper uses hyperparameter exploration to find proper fine-tuning hyperparameters but does not describe a validation setup. If the hyperparameters were tuned on the test set (or if no held-out validation set was used), the fine-tuned results may be optimistic, particularly for models where MXFP6_E2M3 fine-tuned slightly *exceeds* FP32 (Transformer-Base, 26.98 vs. 26.85). This could indicate overfitting rather than genuine improvement from quantization noise as regularization.

**10. The GPT-3-175B and LLaMA-7B evaluation is limited to zero-shot accuracy on a handful of tasks.** The largest-model evaluation (Tables 5 and 6) tests only ARC Easy, ARC Challenge, Lambada, Wikitext, and three Hendryck's Test subsets. Missing are standard LLM benchmarks like MMLU, HellaSwag, TruthfulQA, GSM8K (math reasoning), HumanEval (code generation), or any few-shot evaluation. For a claim that MX formats work as drop-in replacements for the largest models, a broader evaluation on commonly reported benchmarks would substantially strengthen the evidence.

#### Experiments That Would Have Strengthened the Paper

- **Ablation of block size (k = 16, 32, 64)** on a representative subset of models to validate the choice of 32.
- **Comparison against per-tensor INT8 and per-channel quantization** for the discriminative inference tasks to establish the marginal benefit of block-level scaling over simpler methods.
- **Bfloat16 baseline for training experiments** to situate MX formats relative to current production practice.
- **Downstream task evaluation** of the MX-trained GPT-like models (at least perplexity on held-out data and a few standard benchmarks) to validate that matched loss implies matched capabilities.
- **Training results at a larger scale** (e.g., 7B or 13B parameters, if computationally feasible) to strengthen the extrapolation argument that the gap narrows with scale.
- **A rounding mode ablation** on a representative model to quantify the sensitivity of results to this implementation choice.
- **Wall-clock time or step-time measurements** from the emulation library to provide at least a lower bound on the throughput implications of MX quantization, even if native hardware support would be faster.
- **Training duration beyond efficiency** to test whether MX and FP32 loss curves would eventually diverge.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### 6.1 The Cost of Difficulty Estimation Is Not Accounted For

**The assumption or constraint.** The entire compute-optimal framework depends on the ability to estimate how difficult each question is *before* deciding how to allocate the inference budget. The method used — generating 2048 samples per question and computing the pass@1 rate (oracle difficulty) or averaging the PRM's final-answer scores (predicted difficulty) — is extraordinarily expensive. The paper explicitly acknowledges this in Section 3.2:

> "estimating difficulty in this way still incurs additional computation cost during inference... our experiments do not account for this cost largely for simplicity"

**The consequence.** The headline efficiency gains (4× improvement over best-of-N) are computed after difficulty is already known, without amortizing the cost of learning it. At 2048 samples per question, the difficulty estimation step alone consumes **more compute than the largest test-time budgets studied** (up to 256–512 generations per strategy). In a realistic deployment, the total cost would be `C_difficulty_estimation + C_strategy_execution`, and the former could dominate the latter. A user deploying this system would not see the reported 4× efficiency gain — they would see a much smaller net improvement, or possibly even a net loss compared to simply running best-of-N with the same total compute.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The difficulty estimation cost is documented in Section 3.2, where the paper states it uses 2048 samples per question. The paper then evaluates strategies on budgets up to 256–512 generations (Sections 5.3, 6), meaning the difficulty estimation costs 4–8× more than the largest strategy budgets. The predicted difficulty curves in Figures 4 and 8 show that the non-oracle method works, but the paper does not report what happens if fewer than 2048 samples are used for difficulty estimation — there is no ablation of the estimation budget. This leaves open the question of whether a cheaper estimate (e.g., 16 or 64 samples) would still enable effective strategy selection, or whether the 2048-sample cost is genuinely necessary.

**Mitigation status.** The paper explicitly identifies this as a limitation and suggests future work on "pretraining or finetuning models to directly predict difficulty of a question" (Section 8). Additionally, it frames the cost as an "exploration-exploitation tradeoff — compute spent assessing difficulty versus compute spent solving the problem" (Section 3.2). However, no concrete solution is evaluated. The reported efficiency gains should therefore be understood as an **upper bound** on what is achievable, not a realized deployment gain. A reader considering adoption should treat the 4× figure as contingent on solving the difficulty estimation bottleneck.

---

### 6.2 Single Benchmark and Single Model Family Limit Generalization Claims

**The assumption or constraint.** All experiments use the MATH benchmark (high-school competition math problems) with PaLM 2-S* as the base model. The authors state in Section 4 that they "believe this model is representative of the capabilities of many contemporary LLMs," but this is asserted, not tested. No results are reported on any other reasoning domain (code generation, logical reasoning, scientific QA), any other benchmark (GSM8K, HumanEval, ARC), or any other model family (GPT, LLaMA, Claude, Gemini).

**The consequence.** Several aspects of the findings could be model-specific or domain-specific in ways that are impossible to assess from the reported results:

- **The PRM's quality and over-optimization behavior** depend on PaLM 2-S*'s output distribution. A model with different calibration properties, different error patterns, or different in-context learning capabilities might exhibit different difficulty-dependent scaling curves — the optimal strategy per difficulty bin could shift entirely.
- **The revision model's effectiveness** depends on the base model's ability to learn from in-context incorrect answers, which varies substantially across model families. A model with stronger in-context learning might benefit more from revisions; a weaker model might benefit less, or might even degrade.
- **The difficulty bins are defined relative to PaLM 2-S*'s pass@1 rate.** A more capable model would shift the distribution of problems across bins — more problems would fall into the "easy" bins, potentially making the optimal policy different (since the paper shows easy problems favor different strategies than medium problems).
- **Math reasoning** involves symbolic manipulation and multi-step deduction, which may have different numerical sensitivity properties than code generation (which benefits from syntactic constraints) or factual QA (which depends on memorized knowledge rather than inference). The finding that beam search over-optimizes the PRM on easy problems, for instance, may not hold in domains where the verifier signal is more reliable.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. The paper provides zero evidence that any finding generalizes beyond MATH or PaLM 2-S*. The test set of 500 questions, split into five difficulty quintiles of ~100 each and then further divided by two-fold cross-validation for strategy selection (Section 3.2), means the compute-optimal policy is selected based on roughly 50 questions per fold per bin. The paper does not report confidence intervals on the compute-optimal scaling curves (Figures 4, 8), making it difficult to assess whether the observed strategy choices are stable at this sample size or would change if a different set of 500 questions were used.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not attempt to address this limitation. It acknowledges in Section 8 that combining PRM search with revisions and extending to other tasks is future work, but does not frame the single-benchmark/single-model scope as a limitation per se. A reader evaluating whether to adopt this method for their own deployment should treat the results as **existence proofs** — showing that compute-optimal scaling *can* work — rather than as directly transferable recipes. The optimal strategies (which search algorithm, what sequential-to-parallel ratio) are likely specific to the model-benchmark pair, and re-tuning on the target model and task distribution would be necessary.

### 6.3 The FLOPs-Matched Pretraining Baseline Is Weakened by Design Choices

**The assumption or constraint.** The FLOPs-matched comparison in Section 7 compares PaLM 2-S* with compute-optimal test-time scaling against a model with approximately 14× more parameters. The larger model is created by **scaling parameters while holding training data fixed**, following the LLaMA paradigm (Touvron et al., 2023). The paper acknowledges this deviates from compute-optimal pretraining:

> "We choose this setting as it is representative of a canonical approach to scaling pretraining compute and leave the analysis of compute-optimal scaling of pretraining compute where the data and parameters are both scaled equally to future work." (Section 7)

Additionally, the larger model uses **greedy decoding only** — no majority voting, no best-of-N, no search, and no test-time compute augmentation of any kind.

**The consequence.** Both choices weaken the pretraining baseline, potentially making test-time compute look better than it would against a properly optimized alternative:

- **Parameter-only scaling vs. Chinchilla-optimal scaling.** Hoffmann et al. (2022) showed that compute-optimal pretraining scales model size and data quantity *equally*. A Chinchilla-optimal model trained with 14× more total FLOPs would allocate some of that budget to more training tokens rather than to all additional parameters. This model would likely outperform a parameter-only-scaled model at the same total FLOPs, making the test-time compute advantages (e.g., +27.8% relative on easy questions at low R) potentially smaller or reversed.
- **Greedy decoding for the larger model.** The larger model receives no test-time compute augmentation, while the smaller model is allowed to search, revise, and adaptively allocate budget. This is an asymmetric comparison: the smaller model gets the benefit of test-time compute optimization, while the larger model is evaluated in its most computationally efficient (but least accurate) decoding mode. A fairer comparison would give the larger model some test-time compute budget as well — even a modest best-of-8 or best-of-16 — to see whether the gap persists when both sides can use test-time compute.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The FLOPs accounting formulas in Section 7 (`X = 6ND_pretrain` for pretraining, `Y = 2ND_inference` for inference) are standard, and the paper is transparent about the parameter-only scaling choice. The results in Figure 9 and Figure 1 (bar charts) show the comparison as described. However, there is no ablation where the larger model receives any test-time compute, and no experiment with a Chinchilla-optimal larger model. The paper does not quantify how much of the reported advantage is due to test-time compute being genuinely better versus the pretraining baseline being suboptimally configured.

**Mitigation status.** The paper explicitly acknowledges the parameter-only scaling choice and frames the Chinchilla-optimal comparison as future work (Section 7). However, it does not acknowledge the asymmetry of comparing a test-time-augmented small model against a greedy-decoded large model, nor does it discuss how sensitive the conclusions are to this choice. The takeaway box in Section 7 presents the finding that "test-time compute with a smaller model can outperform a ~14× larger pretrained model" without qualifying that this is against a potentially suboptimal large-model configuration. A reader should interpret the FLOPs-matched results as demonstrating that test-time compute *can* be competitive with pretraining under specific (potentially favorable) comparison conditions, not that it is universally preferable.

### 6.4 Hard Problems Remain Fundamentally Unsolved — Test-Time Compute Cannot Create Capability

**The assumption or constraint.** The entire framework assumes that the base model can produce correct solutions at some non-trivial rate. For problems where the base model's pass@1 is near zero, no strategy — regardless of budget — helps. The paper is clear about this boundary condition:

> "On the hardest questions (bin 5), no method makes meaningful progress — the base model simply lacks the capability to produce correct solutions regardless of how the budget is allocated." (Section 5.3, referring to Figure 3 right)

**The consequence.** This is not a fixable limitation — it is a **fundamental boundary** on what test-time compute can achieve. The mechanism is simple: all test-time compute methods (search, revisions, or combinations) operate by selecting, refining, or combining outputs from the base model's proposal distribution. If the correct answer appears in that proposal distribution with near-zero probability, no amount of selection or refinement can recover it. This means:

- **Pretraining remains irreplaceable** for problems outside the base model's current capability envelope. For frontier capabilities (novel reasoning, out-of-distribution generalization, tasks requiring knowledge the base model lacks), test-time compute provides zero benefit.
- **The difficulty distribution matters enormously for practical value.** If a deployment faces mostly bin-5 problems (hard, novel, or adversarial), the compute-optimal framework offers no improvement over any baseline — all methods will fail. If the deployment faces mostly bins 1–3 problems, the gains can be substantial.
- **Self-improvement loops have a ceiling.** If a model uses compute-optimal test-time strategies to generate training data for fine-tuning itself (as the paper suggests in Section 8), it can only improve on problems it already sometimes solves. Problems where it never produces correct answers will remain unsolved, even with amplified compute, because there is no correct signal to distill.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figure 3 (right) shows bin 5 accuracy hovering at 1–3% for all methods and all budgets. Figure 7 (right) shows bin 5 accuracy at roughly 2–3% regardless of the sequential-to-parallel ratio. Figure 9 (the FLOPs-matched comparison) shows the bin 5 scaling line essentially flat near 0–5%, far below the ~14× larger model's greedy performance. Table 7 (FLOPs-matched summary) shows negative relative improvements for hard problems at most R values, including −52.9% for PRM search at high R. The evidence is consistent: hard problems are a hard ceiling.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not attempt to solve this — it is an inherent limitation of the approach, not a bug. The paper is transparent about it, including the finding explicitly in the Section 7 takeaway box: "The benefits of test-time compute over pretraining are most pronounced for easy and medium questions, while pretraining is more effective for the hardest questions." However, the paper does not provide guidance on how to identify in advance whether a given problem distribution falls into the "hard" regime where test-time compute will be ineffective. The difficulty estimation method (2048 samples) could serve this purpose, but at a cost that may exceed the budget for actually solving the problem itself. A user deploying this system needs a cheap pre-filter to identify which problems to even attempt with test-time compute versus routing directly to a larger model or human review — and the paper provides no such mechanism.

### 6.5 Verifier Over-Optimization Is the Primary Performance Ceiling — and It Is Not Solved, Only Managed

**The assumption or constraint.** The compute-optimal scaling policy works by routing prompts to strategies that avoid triggering verifier over-optimization: easy problems get best-of-N (weaker optimization), medium problems get beam search (stronger optimization where the verifier signal is more informative). But the underlying problem — that the PRM produces scores that become unreliable under aggressive search — is not solved. The paper documents this directly:

- Beam search *degrades* easy-problem performance at high budgets (Figure 3, right: bin 1 accuracy decreases as budget increases from 4 to 256 generations).
- Lookahead search — the most powerful and expensive optimizer — performs *worst overall* (Figure 3, left: generally below all other methods at equivalent generation budgets).
- Qualitative examples in Appendix M (Figures 29, etc.) show search producing degenerate outputs (repetitive low-information steps, overly short 1–2 step solutions) that score highly under the PRM but are incorrect.

**The consequence.** The compute-optimal policy works around the problem rather than fixing it. This has several implications for deployment:

- **Performance is fundamentally bounded by verifier quality.** No amount of additional compute will improve accuracy if the verifier cannot reliably distinguish correct from incorrect solutions. The flattening of beam search curves at high budgets (Figure 3, left) shows this ceiling — accuracy asymptotes far below 100%, and additional generations produce no benefit.
- **The optimal strategy depends on verifier quality in ways the paper does not explore.** If a better PRM were available (e.g., trained with more data, adversarially robust, or ensembled), the difficulty thresholds at which the optimal strategy changes would shift. A sufficiently good PRM might make beam search optimal even on easy problems, or might enable lookahead search to outperform simpler methods. The paper's specific strategy recommendations (use best-of-N on easy problems, beam search on medium) are tied to the PRM quality achieved with the Monte Carlo rollout training procedure in Appendix D — they may not transfer to settings with different verifier quality.
- **Scaling to harder tasks may be limited by verifier quality, not by search algorithm sophistication.** The paper's finding that lookahead search underperforms simpler methods suggests that **better verifiers, not better search algorithms**, are the key to further scaling test-time compute. This redirects the research agenda but does not provide a solution.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The over-optimization evidence is distributed across Figures 3, 13, 14, and Appendix M. The PRM training procedure is described in Appendix D (Monte Carlo rollouts, soft labels, AdamW with specific hyperparameters). However, the paper does not ablate PRM quality — there is no experiment where a better or worse PRM is used to see how the compute-optimal policy changes. The paper does not quantify how much the performance ceiling would rise if the PRM were improved (e.g., through ensemble methods, adversarial training, or more rollout data). The PRM is treated as a fixed component; the paper studies how to optimally use it, not how to improve it.

**Mitigation status.** The paper explicitly identifies verifier over-optimization as a limitation in Section 8, noting that "developing more sophisticated, robust verifiers from process-based reward signals is an important area for future work." However, the paper offers no new techniques for verifier improvement — it uses a standard Monte Carlo rollout training approach from prior work (Wang et al., 2023). The compute-optimal policy itself can be seen as a mitigation: by routing easy problems away from aggressive search, it avoids the regime where over-optimization is most severe. But this only manages the symptom, not the cause. A user adopting this method should expect that accuracy will plateau well before compute is exhausted, and that further budget increases will produce diminishing or negative returns unless the verifier is also improved.

---

### 6.6 Sequential Revisions Introduce Latency That Is Not Accounted For — and a 38% Correct-to-Incorrect Reversion Rate

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper measures test-time compute in "generations" — the number of complete solutions sampled. This is a reasonable proxy for total FLOPs but completely ignores **latency** (wall-clock time). The revision model (Section 6) generates chains of sequential revisions where each revision depends on the previous one. Best-of-N (parallel sampling) can be executed simultaneously given sufficient hardware; sequential revisions cannot. A strategy that allocates 128 generations as 64 sequential × 2 parallel takes roughly 64× longer in wall-clock time than 128 parallel samples. The paper never discusses this tradeoff.

Additionally, the revision model suffers from a **correct-to-incorrect reversion problem**: because the model was trained only on sequences where all in-context answers are incorrect (followed by a correct target), it lacks any signal for what to do when the current answer is already correct. The paper reports that:

> "approximately 38% of correct answers get converted back to incorrect ones using a naive approach" (Section 6.1)

**The consequence.** For latency-sensitive applications — interactive assistants, real-time decision-making, any deployment where users wait for responses — the sequential-heavy strategies favored by the compute-optimal policy on easy problems (Figure 7) may be **impractical regardless of their accuracy advantages**. A method that achieves higher accuracy at a given FLOPs budget but takes 64× longer to produce an answer is not a drop-in improvement — it represents a different point on the accuracy-latency Pareto frontier that may be unacceptable for many use cases.

The reversion problem compounds this: even if latency is acceptable, the revision model is inherently unreliable at *preserving* correct answers that it generates. The paper mitigates this with majority voting or verifier-based selection across the entire revision chain (picking the best answer from any point, not necessarily the last), but this mitigation is imperfect. It means the system must evaluate all revisions in the chain and maintain a selection mechanism, adding complexity and potentially requiring a verifier that works well on revision model outputs (which the paper showed the base-model PRM does not — Figure 15a required a separate revision-specific ORM).

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The 38% reversion rate is stated in Section 6.1 without a supporting figure or ablation — it is a number reported in prose. The sequential-to-parallel ratio experiments (Figure 7) show that purely sequential strategies achieve the best accuracy at low budgets, but the paper does not report the wall-clock time of these strategies. The compute flow in Figure 2 shows the sequential dependency chain clearly, but the implications for latency are not discussed anywhere in the paper.

**Mitigation status.** The paper mitigates the reversion problem with within-chain selection (majority voting or verifier-based), described in Section 6.1 and Appendix I. However, this is a patch, not a fix — the model still generates incorrect revisions from correct answers, and the selection mechanism must catch and correct these errors. A more principled solution (e.g., training the model with both correct-to-correct and incorrect-to-correct trajectories, or adding a "stop revising" token) is not explored. The latency issue is entirely unaddressed — the paper does not mention it as a limitation or suggest any mitigation. A user deploying this method in a latency-sensitive setting should expect that the sequential revision strategies, while FLOPs-efficient, may be unacceptably slow and should benchmark wall-clock time before adopting.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper does not propose a new quantization method — it is a validation study of an existing open standard. Yet its impact on the field is potentially large, not because of technical novelty but because it addresses the **coordination problem that blocks hardware innovation.** The shift is institutional and evidentiary, not algorithmic: by demonstrating that a single format family (MX) works across an unprecedentedly broad range of architectures, tasks, model scales, and operational regimes, the paper converts micro-scaling from a portfolio of incompatible research prototypes into a **credible, single target for silicon investment.**

This is best understood as a **standards-enabling contribution.** Prior to this paper, a hardware architect considering adding native micro-scaling support faced an impossible decision: which of the many proposed formats (Drumond et al.'s hybrid block floating-point [2], Microsoft Floating Point [3], VS-Quant [4], the shared-microexponent designs [5]) should they implement? Each paper demonstrated good results on its own narrow benchmarks, but none provided evidence that a specific concrete format — with fixed block size, fixed scale encoding, and a specific conversion algorithm — would generalize across CNNs, transformers, LSTMs, speech models, recommendation systems, and large language models, for both inference and training, with and without fine-tuning, from 20M to 175B parameters. This paper provides exactly that evidence, and its consortium of authors from Microsoft, AMD, Intel, Meta, NVIDIA, and Qualcomm signals that the hardware industry is treating it as the evidentiary foundation for a standard they have already agreed to adopt. The contribution is making that evidence public, reproducible, and tied to an open specification [6].

The paper also resolves a **tension in the quantization literature between aggressive compression and user friction.** Much prior work on sub-8-bit quantization achieves impressive accuracy on specific models but requires complex, model-specific calibration procedures (outlier-aware quantization, learned step sizes, architecture modifications, or per-layer mixed-precision search). These methods implicitly treat accuracy as the only dependent variable, with friction as an afterthought. The MX paper reframes the problem: by making friction a first-class evaluation axis, it shows that **simple, uniform quantization with block-level scaling can match the accuracy of more complex methods while eliminating per-model engineering effort.** The direct-cast inference results for MXINT8 — applying Algorithm 1 to any pretrained FP32 model with zero calibration and achieving FP32-parity accuracy on all 20+ model-task combinations — is a demonstration that the field may have over-invested in complex PTQ techniques for 8-bit inference. If a single, uniform, open-standard format works as a drop-in replacement across the board, the business case for proprietary per-model quantization pipelines weakens substantially.

The paper also **redirects the training quantization research agenda.** Prior to this work, the consensus was that training at sub-8-bit precision would require fundamental algorithmic innovations — stochastic rounding, gradient-aware quantization step sizes, or architectural modifications like outlier-aware normalization. The paper shows that none of these are necessary: block-level scaling with a shared E8M0 exponent, applied uniformly to all dot-product operations with no changes to the optimizer, learning rate schedule, or architecture, is sufficient to train generative language models to FP32-matching loss at 6-bit precision (and weight-only at 4-bit). This finding suggests that the dominant failure mode of sub-8-bit training was not per-element precision loss but **inter-block dynamic range mismatch** — the inability of a per-tensor scale to accommodate the wide variation in magnitude across different spatial regions of the same tensor. Solving this one problem via block-level scaling is sufficient; everything else is secondary. This simplifies the research agenda: rather than pursuing ever-more-sophisticated quantization-aware training algorithms, effort should focus on (a) optimizing the block structure (size, scale format, conversion procedure) for hardware efficiency, and (b) extending the block-scaling principle to the remaining operations that currently stay in FP32 (vector ops, attention, normalization).

Finally, the paper introduces a **taxonomy of mantissa-vs-exponent allocation rules** that had not been empirically established at this breadth. The finding that E2M3 (more mantissa) outperforms E3M2 (more exponent) for inference, while E3M2 is required for training, is not obvious a priori. One might have hypothesized that block-level scaling would handle dynamic range so effectively that exponent bits would become irrelevant, making more mantissa bits always preferable. The paper's training results disprove this: intra-block gradient dynamic range is sufficient that even with per-block scaling, the element format needs at least 3 exponent bits (E3M2) during training; 2 exponent bits (E2M3) are insufficient because a single block can contain gradient values spanning more than 4 binades. This finding provides a concrete design rule for future format designers: **count the maximum intra-block dynamic range observed during training, and allocate exponent bits to cover it; then allocate remaining bits to the mantissa.** For inference, where intra-block variation is smaller, the opposite rule applies — minimize exponent bits and maximize mantissa bits.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**1. Ablating the block size to map the accuracy-cost Pareto frontier.** The paper uses block size k=32 for all experiments without reporting a single ablation. This is the most critical design parameter in the MX standard: larger blocks reduce the scale overhead (amortized bits per element) but provide coarser dynamic range adaptation; smaller blocks do the opposite. A systematic study that evaluates block sizes of k=8, 16, 32, 64, and 128 on a representative subset of models (say, ResNet-50, DeiT-Small, BERT-Base, and GPT-150M training) under both direct-cast inference and training-from-scratch regimes would produce a **Pareto curve of accuracy vs. amortized bit-width vs. block size.** This would directly inform hardware design — a hardware implementation must commit to a specific block size for its dot-product engines, and the choice should be empirically justified, not based solely on alignment with common tile sizes. The experiment is straightforward given the open-source emulation library [7] and would substantially strengthen the standardization case.

**2. Training at 7B+ scale to test the loss-gap-narrows-with-scale hypothesis.** The paper's training results show the MX-FP32 loss gap narrowing from +0.03–0.04 at 20M parameters to +0.01 at 1.5B parameters for MXFP6_E3M2 (Table 7). This trend, based on four data points, is used implicitly to argue that MX formats will work at larger scales. But the extrapolation is fragile: the loss gap might plateau rather than continue narrowing, or it might reverse if larger models exhibit different gradient statistics (e.g., spikier gradients due to deeper networks or longer training). A single training run at 7B or 13B parameters with MXFP6_E3M2 — even if lossy and approximate due to computational constraints — would provide an enormously valuable data point for the extrapolation. If the gap continues to narrow (e.g., +0.005 loss at 7B), the case for MX training at frontier scales is strong. If it widens or plateaus, that would be a critical negative result establishing the practical limits of block-level scaling. This experiment is expensive but feasible given the open-source model ecosystem (e.g., training a LLaMA-architecture 7B model with the MX emulation library).

**3. Downstream capability evaluation of MX-trained models, not just loss.** The training experiments (Tables 7 and 8) report only language modeling loss. Modern LLM evaluation emphasizes downstream benchmarks because loss improvements do not always translate to capability gains — a model can have lower perplexity but perform worse on reasoning, factual recall, or instruction following. A crucial follow-up would take the MXFP6_E3M2-trained 1.5B model (and the MXFP4+MXFP6-trained variant) and evaluate it against the FP32-trained baseline on a standard suite: MMLU, HellaSwag, ARC, TruthfulQA, and perhaps a few-shot evaluation on GSM8K (math reasoning). The specific question being tested: does the 0.01 loss gap at 1.5B scale translate to a 0.01 accuracy gap on downstream tasks, or does it magnify into larger capability degradations? A negative result — e.g., MX-trained models match loss but lag by 3–5 percentage points on reasoning benchmarks — would be a critical finding that complicates the "drop-in replacement" narrative. This experiment requires no new training, only evaluation of the already-trained checkpoints on standard benchmarks.

**4. Combining error diffusion with fine-tuning for MXFP4 inference on sensitive models.** Table 4 shows that even after fine-tuning, MXFP4 inference on MobileNet v2 achieves only 65.41% Top-1 accuracy vs. 72.14% FP32 — a gap of 6.73 percentage points. Table 3 shows that error diffusion alone brings MXFP4 on MobileNet v2 to 18.88% (from 0.25% direct-cast), a large improvement but still non-functional. An unexplored combination is **error diffusion followed by quantization-aware fine-tuning** — using the GPFQ algorithm [8] to initialize the quantized weights, then fine-tuning for a few epochs with the quantized forward pass. The hypothesis: error diffusion provides a better initialization for fine-tuning by avoiding the catastrophic zeroing/saturation of direct-cast quantization, potentially allowing fine-tuning to close the gap to FP32 on difficult models where direct-cast + fine-tuning or error-diffusion alone both fall short. This experiment requires only implementing the sequential combination of the two already-described procedures and testing on MobileNet v2, Wav2Vec 2.0, and other sensitive models from Table 2.

**5. Training with MXFP4 activations to identify the failure boundary.** The paper trains with MXFP4 weights + MXFP6 activations (Table 8) but never reports pure MXFP4 training (all tensors at 4 bits). The absence of this row in the table implies it was tested and failed, but the failure mode is not documented. A systematic study attempting MXFP4 training for activations and gradients — perhaps with gradient scaling, or with reduced learning rates, or with architectural modifications like inserting additional normalization layers — would map the **failure boundary** for block-level scaling. Does training diverge immediately? Converge to a higher loss? Exhibit gradient explosion? Understanding *how* 4-bit activation/gradient training fails with block-level scaling would inform whether the limitation is fundamental (intra-block dynamic range in activations is simply too large for 4 bits) or addressable through complementary techniques (stochastic rounding, gradient clipping, mixed block sizes). A well-documented negative result here would be as valuable as the paper's positive results — it would establish the practical floor for element bit-width in training and prevent wasted effort on unproductive directions.

**6. Testing the mantissa-vs-exponent taxonomy on a held-out architecture family.** The paper's finding that E2M3 (more mantissa) is better for inference while E3M2 (more exponent) is required for training is established on CNNs, vision transformers, and language transformers. An extension to a fundamentally different architecture — perhaps graph neural networks (GNNs), neural radiance fields (NeRFs), or diffusion models for image generation — would test whether the taxonomy is universal or tied to the statistics of the specific architectures evaluated. GNNs, for instance, involve sparse, irregular computations with very different tensor value distributions than dense transformers; NeRFs involve volumetric rendering with coordinate-based MLPs that have unique gradient patterns. If the mantissa/exponent allocation rules hold on these architectures, the design principle generalizes. If they break, it would reveal architecture-dependent quantization sensitivities that the MX standard might need to accommodate (e.g., through different recommended element formats for different layer types).

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**1. Low-friction inference acceleration for model-serving platforms.** The direct-cast MXINT8 result — matching FP32 accuracy on all 20+ model-task combinations with zero calibration, zero fine-tuning, and no per-model engineering — has immediate implications for model-serving infrastructure. A cloud platform (e.g., Azure, AWS SageMaker, Hugging Face Inference Endpoints) could offer an "MXINT8 acceleration" option that applies Algorithm 1 uniformly to any user-uploaded FP32 model, providing approximately 4× memory bandwidth reduction and (with native hardware support) up to 4× throughput improvement over FP32, with a documented accuracy guarantee within standard deviation of the FP32 baseline. The friction is essentially zero: the user uploads an FP32 model, selects the MXINT8 option, and the platform handles the quantization automatically at deployment time. The paper's evidence that this works on GPT-3-175B, LLaMA-7B, BERT, ResNet, Wav2Vec, and DLRM provides the confidence that the option would be safe for a wide range of customer models without case-by-case validation. This is a direct path to cost reduction for any organization serving models at scale, enabled by the breadth of the paper's evaluation.

**2. On-device deployment of generative models at 6-bit precision with minimal quality loss.** The fine-tuned MXFP6_E2M3 results (Table 4) and direct-cast MXFP6 generative results (Tables 5 and 6) suggest a practical deployment pipeline for on-device LLMs: take a pretrained FP32 or FP16 model, fine-tune for a few epochs with MXFP6 quantization-aware training, and deploy at 6.25 bits per element. The memory savings relative to FP16 are approximately 2.6× (16 bits vs. 6.25 bits), which is substantial for edge devices with limited RAM. For a 7B-parameter model like LLaMA-7B (Table 6), this reduces the weight memory from approximately 14 GB (FP16) to approximately 5.5 GB (MXFP6) — the difference between fitting in a high-end phone's memory and not fitting. The paper's LLaMA-7B direct-cast results show that even without fine-tuning, MXFP6 achieves Lambada accuracy of 0.724 vs. 0.736 FP32 and ARC Challenge 0.445 vs. 0.447 FP32 — differences within or near the bootstrap standard deviations. For many on-device applications (assistants, summarization, text completion), this level of accuracy parity at nearly 3× memory reduction is a compelling tradeoff, and the fine-tuning option exists for applications requiring tighter accuracy guarantees.

**3. Training large models on memory-constrained hardware via mixed-precision MXFP4+MXFP6.** The training result in Table 8 — MXFP4 weights with MXFP6 activations and gradients achieving 2.76 loss vs. 2.74 FP32 at 1.5B scale — enables a concrete memory optimization for training. In the MX compute flow (Figure 2), the forward and backward pass GeMMs operate on MX-quantized tensors, meaning the working memory for these operations is reduced by approximately 5.3× for activations/gradients (16 bits for Bfloat16 vs. 6.25 bits for MXFP6, assuming the paper's Bfloat16 compute flow) and 8× for weights (32 bits vs. 4.25 bits for MXFP4, though the master FP32 copy remains). For a training run that is memory-bandwidth-bound — which is common for large models — this directly translates to faster iteration. A researcher or small lab training a 7B model on a single 8-GPU node, where memory capacity per GPU is the binding constraint, could use MXFP4+MXFP6 to reduce activation memory enough to increase batch size or model dimension within the same hardware budget. The paper's finding that no hyperparameter changes are needed means this is a low-friction optimization: train with the same recipe, swap in MX quantization for GeMMs, and expect comparable loss. The 0.02 loss penalty at 1.5B is small enough that for many research purposes (architecture exploration, hyperparameter search, ablation studies), the fidelity tradeoff is well worth the memory savings.

**4. Green AI and carbon-aware training.** The bit-width reduction from FP32 (32 bits) to MXFP6 (6.25 bits amortized) for training represents approximately a 5× reduction in the memory footprint of activations and gradients in the forward/backward passes. While the paper does not measure end-to-end energy savings (since the emulation library does not provide throughput gains), a native MX hardware implementation would translate this memory reduction into proportional energy savings for memory accesses, which dominate the energy budget of large-scale training. For organizations training models at the 100B+ scale — where a single training run can consume megawatt-hours of electricity and produce hundreds of tons of CO2 — adopting MXFP6 training could reduce the energy footprint by a factor of 3–5× for the dot-product operations that constitute the majority of training FLOPs. The paper's evidence that this comes with negligible accuracy loss (0.01 at 1.5B, potentially less at larger scales based on the observed trend) makes the environmental case for MX adoption compelling beyond the economic case. This application is contingent on native hardware support, but the paper provides the accuracy validation that justifies the hardware investment needed to realize the energy savings.

### When to Prefer MX Formats Over Alternatives

The paper positions MX formats against the default of FP32 (and, implicitly, against the FP16/Bfloat16/FP8 production alternatives). The decision rules that emerge from the empirical results are:

- **Prefer MXINT8 direct-cast when you need a zero-friction drop-in inference replacement for any FP32 model.** MXINT8 matches FP32 within standard deviation on all tested models (GPT-3-175B through MobileNet v2, five task families) with no calibration data, no fine-tuning, and no per-model engineering. This is the "safe default" — if the 8.25-bit amortized storage is acceptable, there is no reason not to use it.

- **Prefer MXFP6_E2M3 with fine-tuning when you need 6-bit inference compression (~5× vs. FP32) and can afford a few epochs of quantization-aware training.** The fine-tuned results (Table 4) show this combination reaching within 0.1–0.5 percentage points or 0.05 BLEU of FP32 on discriminative tasks. For models where the fine-tuning cost is amortized over many inference queries (cloud deployments), this provides a substantial memory reduction at negligible accuracy cost.

- **Prefer MXFP6_E3M2 for training from scratch when you are memory- or bandwidth-bound and can accept a 0.01–0.04 loss penalty at the 1.5B scale (possibly smaller at larger scales, based on the observed trend).** The key advantage is that no hyperparameter changes are needed — reuse the FP32 recipe directly. The E3M2 variant is essential for training (not E2M3) due to intra-block gradient dynamic range.

- **Prefer MXFP4 weights + MXFP6_E3M2 activations/gradients for training when you need maximum weight compression (~8×) and can accept a slightly larger loss penalty (~0.02 at 1.5B).** This configuration is only viable for weights; activations and gradients must remain at 6 bits. The master FP32 weight copy is still required, so total training memory savings are less than the 8× weight compression ratio.

- **Prefer error-diffusion PTQ over direct-cast when deploying MXFP4 inference on models known to be quantization-sensitive.** The error-diffusion results (Table 3) show massive improvements over direct-cast for MXFP4 on sensitive models (ResNet-50: +27.6 pp; Wav2Vec 2.0: +18.2 pp WER), with only a one-pass calibration step and no training loop.

- **Prefer the higher-mantissa variant (E4M3 over E5M2, E2M3 over E3M2) for inference-only deployments.** The direct-cast results in Table 2 show this uniformly across all models. The per-block shared scale handles dynamic range; intra-block precision is the binding constraint.

- **Prefer the higher-exponent variant (E3M2 over E2M3) for any scenario involving gradient computation (training or fine-tuning).** The training results in Table 7 show E3M2 achieving lower loss at 3 of 4 model scales. This is because intra-block gradient variation requires the element format's own exponent range to supplement the block-level scale.
