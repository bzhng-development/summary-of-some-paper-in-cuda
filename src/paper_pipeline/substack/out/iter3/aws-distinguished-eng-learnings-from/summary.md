Here are the takeaways from this Marc Brooker interview (Distinguished Engineer at AWS who started as a new grad, learned distributed systems from 3,000+ postmortems, and recently built Aurora DSQL):

**Finding problems that matter**

Marc Brooker believes the best ideas come from a "collision" of two signals: what customers are struggling with, and what the underlying technology trends now make possible. He spends significant time meeting with AWS customers, listening for where they'd prefer not to be spending their time—the unsolved problems that pull them away from their core business. Simultaneously, he tracks the "speeds and feeds": faster networks, faster storage, the explosion in multicore and now GPUs. "It is those kind of moments of change that have… the opportunity to build things."

He offers a concrete example from 2020 on the Lambda team. Customers were excited about serverless and containers, but relational databases remained stubbornly "serverful"—operationally mismatched with the new paradigm. "These customers have brought to me a gift of understanding something that's really important," he says. He joined the Aurora team, built Aurora Serverless, then DSQL. The latter came from another collision: the technical trend of block storage becoming the default durability layer for databases, married to S3 as the underlying durability layer for a new database—but S3 doesn't have the latency properties or rich interface an online database needs. The result was an architecture where "we're going to build… on top of that that deals with all of these other things in a much better way, but doesn't have to worry about durability." He stresses that building something market-leading "would be hard to imagine without either of those input signals."

**Learning distributed systems through 15 years of on-call**

Brooker stayed on-call for fifteen years—a choice many senior engineers negotiate out of—because "the majority of my in-practice knowledge about how to build distributed systems has come from being on call and analyzing and deeply understanding these postmortems." Engineers leave college with strong fundamentals but lack "the grounded knowledge of what it actually means to run and understand systems." On-call reveals how systems really behave, how customers really use them, and what happens when systems encounter unexpected usage patterns. He distinguishes rote ticket-closing—which should be automated—from the high-value work of deep investigation, and describes a core AWS mechanism: a weekly Wednesday-morning meeting where engineers and senior leaders across the company gather to discuss postmortems (COEs) and extract cross-cutting lessons. He calls this meeting "one of the things that has been a core, almost causal factor behind AWS's success" because it forces leadership bandwidth and the time of the best engineers onto deeply understanding how systems actually operate. Being "extremely grounded in reality helps you design better products, helps you architect better systems, and it helps you think more clearly about the next round of things."

Ryan notes that most engineers avoid on-call, but Brooker frames it as optimizing for finding the most important problems: "If you aren't close to operating your actual system and you don't know how it's actually working, how are you supposed to identify what to fix? You can come up with some theories… but they're probably not going to be right." He estimates he's read between 3,000 and 4,000 industry postmortems and internal COEs over his career—"and so even a little bit of lesson from each one, and it tends to stick."

**What makes a great postmortem**

A great postmortem begins with deeply understanding what actually happened rather than assuming based on prior biases. If you can't understand what happened, "that teaches you something about your logging and metrics and observability and simulations." From there, it steps through multiple levels of "why": a code bug, yes—but why was it missed in testing? Why were testing and validation where they were? Why did the team assume a certain behavior about the system? A great postmortem produces concrete action items at every level: fix the specific line of software, fix the testing processes that didn't catch it, and if you see patterns across multiple postmortems, "level those up" and ask whether a service, library, or community of practice could eliminate whole classes of problems. He warns against two failure modes: insufficient focus on outcomes, and the far more insidious **normalization of operational heroics**—the team culture that feels virtuous ("these people are super strong owners, they really care") but is actually "taking all of these people and their strong ownership and their expertise and spending them just on this break-fix cycle." The fix is to take that same energy and redirect it from tactical firefighting to strategic improvement, so teams exit the cycle and "improve your product in different ways."

Brooker describes an **outer loop** over the postmortem process where patterns across incidents inform new product design. When building DSQL, the team studied relational-database postmortems—both their own and customers'—and noticed a recurring outage pattern: a client starts a transaction, then goes out to lunch (GC pause, lossy network, lost connectivity), holding locks and degrading the database. Relational databases "don't tend to be resilient to clients misbehaving in that way." DSQL's answer: eliminate pessimistic locking entirely. Within a transaction, reads use multi-version concurrency control (storing a history of row versions so old versions can be read without blocking writers), writes are spooled locally, and commit-time optimistic checks determine whether the transaction can proceed. "There is no way that a reader of a piece of data can block other writers… writers can't block writers, but they can prevent other writers' transactions from eventually committing." The storage overhead for keeping old row versions is surprisingly small—typically under 10% for online workloads, because write traffic tends to be concentrated on a small subset of rows while "the vast majority of data isn't changing."

**Why caches are bad**

Caching exploits temporal and spatial locality to make systems faster, but Brooker warns against the **metastable failure** mode caches introduce. In the "full and correct" mode, the system is fast and healthy. In the "empty or wrong" mode, the backend—never scaled for uncached traffic—becomes overloaded, the system slows or goes down, and often can't recover on its own because contention or saturation prevents the cache from refilling. "You might go years without seeing something like that," he says, "but if you look across the biggest, most impactful system postmortems across the industry… these kinds of metastable failures have been an underlying cause in probably a majority of them." They produce larger-scale issues, longer recovery times, and situations where "you have to often turn it off and turn it back on again"—a painful last resort.

His preferred alternatives: first, a **complete materialized view**—essentially a cache that contains every row, so it never has the empty/wrong-data mode (DSQL's storage tier works this way). Second, a scalable backend (DSQL, DynamoDB) that achieves the needed scale and performance without a cache in front. Third, in Aurora's case, the leader constantly pre-warms failover targets' caches so a failover doesn't trigger the empty-cache mode. "Caching isn't a bad pattern, but it is a pattern with some significant downsides that are really best avoided in practice."

**How AI will change software engineering**

Brooker's core belief: "We have only just started to see the impact that software is going to have on the world." Software has always been supply-constrained, and the changing economics of building it open an almost unbounded opportunity for "more software, bigger software, better software, more personal software." This means careers will look different early and late, and success will hinge on "the ability to adapt to that change and to lead that change."

He models the future in three tiers. The first is the **craft tier**: like hand-saw joinery, building software the old way will persist as a hobby and a source of joy, even if it's not economically interesting. "Not everything we do has to be an economically interesting opportunity." The second is the **analog electronics tier**—a shrinking but real segment of the industry that runs on past techniques, languages, and technologies because driving change in the real world is always harder than it looks from the outside; it depends on people, politics, policy, and "our various irrationalities as humans." Real careers and economic opportunity remain there, as they still do in analog electronics for radio and power systems. The third is the **mainstream tier**, which adopts agentic development, AI-powered development, specification-driven development, and techniques "whose names we don't even know yet" to build software at speeds and costs unimaginable with old techniques. "It's the space I'd be in if I was building a company today."

**Advice for junior engineers**

Finding problems that matter will shift from senior-engineer territory to being part of even the earliest career stages. "Here's the context, here's the problem, here's the customer. Let's go off and work together and solve problems with all of this context." He acknowledges this will frustrate people who came for a pure software-development career—"open their IDE, start typing, and don't stop for eight hours"—as that mode becomes harder to build a career around. Simultaneously, there's enormous opportunity for the **extremely technically deep**: people deep on optimization, infrastructure, databases, or scientific domains, because the ability to ask the right questions is more valuable than ever. "There was too much sort of boilerplate to really use that leverage… and [now] be able to turn that into software and software products in a way that was really difficult before."

Ryan raises the fear that junior engineers will be expected to operate at senior levels immediately. Brooker's reassurance: hiring incentives are aligned—"it's not valuable to hire a bunch of people and set them up to fail"—and organizations will need to provide guardrails, mentorship, and feedback loops, just as he received when he first spoke to an AWS customer and found it "super scary." The emphasis on *what* people learn will change, but the need to learn on the job is universal. "It's a super exciting time. I think that just the scale of the opportunity is bigger than it's ever been."

**Thoughts for senior engineers**

For senior engineers, the challenge is retaining the value of hard-won experience without falling behind. Many have moved into influence and leadership roles where they're not hands-on every day, and Brooker believes "it's going to be harder and harder to be in that kind of role and be able to influence and advise in a relevant way." His advice is stark: "You kind of got to get building, you got to get back in, get back into it. You need to deeply understand how the practice of building software and the practice of designing software has changed." The good news is that time spent as a practitioner is vastly more leveraged now—less wasted time on boilerplate—and it reconnects senior people to why they got into the field in the first place: learning, building, solving customer problems.

He sees a clear divide between those using modern agentic-powered developer practices and those who aren't. The former have nuanced things to say about strengths, weaknesses, and what still needs work; the latter have "such a poor mental model of how they work… that the things they say about them tend to be essentially fiction." **"If you aren't doing it hands-on, your opinion about it is very likely to be completely wrong."** Admitting that takes humility, and it's tough for people with fancy titles and distinguished careers, but "it's a must." He points to the early builders of S3 and EC2 as models: their enduring success came from being "so deep in the details and so grounded on the use cases and so deep in the economics," working at both the strategic level and the "minute-to-minute details of what's fast now, what's slow, what's good, what's bad."

**Why engineers should write**

Writing is an "incredible multiplier" for technical people. Building a great product shares ideas with the world, and one-on-one mentorship does too, but writing scales across space and time: "there are so many more people that you can share that with and it lasts for a much longer period of time." A blog post from a decade ago can still be shared with someone today. Writing also forces a level of mental clarity that speaking and slide-decks don't—a core Amazon cultural belief that Brooker has found true in practice. He sometimes writes documents with no intention of sharing them, "just to sharpen my own thinking on a particular point," and references Ryan's conversation with Leslie Lamport, who takes that a step further into formal mathematics.

Engineers who complain that writing docs is busy-work are partly right: documentation that happens at the wrong semantic level (he picks on UML as an example) isn't valuable. What separates worthwhile writing is understanding what you're getting out of it—an artifact for your future self, for new team members, for customers, or as a tool to think through a **one-way-door technical decision** that's hard to reverse. Not every decision warrants this; many are easy, non-critical, or reversible. But for key decisions, writing is essential. He also notes a meta-benefit of design docs: they distinguish between decisions that were carefully chosen and thought through, and decisions that were arbitrary guesses made with no data. Future maintainers can confidently change the arbitrary ones but should "really engage with the reason that we made this decision" for the carefully chosen ones.

**The doing/discussing balance and apparent expertise**

Brooker's blog post "The Four Hobbies and Apparent Expertise" uses a two-by-two matrix of doing vs. discussing to map the tradeoff between expertise and visibility. Some engineers are 100% on the doing side—quiet, deeply knowledgeable, but under-leveraged because they never share what they know. Others are 100% on the discussing side—on every messaging channel, writing and speaking constantly, highly visible but with little grounding in actual practice. Brooker enjoys working with the doers more, but believes both extremes are failure modes: the pure doer misses signals about what problems are actually important because "if your head's down in your IDE all day, you could very likely be working on the wrong thing," while the pure discusser's opinions drift into fiction.

His personal sweet spot is roughly **75/25 practitioner-to-communicator**, with great colleagues he knows spanning from 90/10 to 50/50. In this moment of rapid change, he suggests swinging a bit more toward the practitioner side. Ryan asks whether it's better long-term to be overrated or underrated. Brooker is unequivocal: "It's probably better to be underrated. I think being overrated can feel great in the moment, but it's rarely sustainable." He draws an analogy to sports and crafts, where "you can't fool the world for very long. It very quickly becomes very obvious who can and who can't." In software, where so much is qualitative, it takes longer for reality to catch up with perception—but the people with careers he most admires are "people who are personally very honest about their level of knowledge and understanding and skill."

**People, books, and being bolder earlier**

The engineer Brooker most admires is Allan Vermeulen, an early AWS engineer who contributed heavily to S3's design and many database services, and briefly served as Amazon's CTO before realizing it wasn't the job he wanted. Brooker has a vivid memory from around 2010 of arguing with Vermeulen about edge cases in the Paxos paper at a deep technical level—and in the same breath, Vermeulen could discuss cloud strategy at the executive level. "That ability to work sort of almost at every level" is what Brooker has modeled his own career after. He also notes that "one of the other things that is really admirable about a lot of these folks is… they don't want to be celebrities."

For technical books, he recommends Martin Kleppmann's distributed-systems book (a second edition is coming), the *Quantitative Systems Design* book, and Hennessy and Patterson's *Computer Architecture*. He reads mostly papers rather than books for technical depth, noting that AI tools have made papers "way more accessible now"—"hey, Claude, summarize this for me and then I can dive into it and read the author's words." He also points to much older sources: the algorithms Lambda uses to manage traffic bursts come from Erlang's work on telephone call centers roughly a century ago. "Folks also shouldn't think that, oh well, the industry is changing super fast"—foundational insight endures, and deep understanding of those topics is "more leveraged than ever before."

His advice to his younger self, just joining AWS: "be a little bit bolder." He loved the teams he worked on in EC2 and EBS, but suspects he was more hesitant than optimal about leaving when his learning and impact had tapered off. He's changed organizations in a big way four times in his career; "maybe five or six would have been optimal." The principle is to continually ask: "What am I learning and who am I learning from, and is there a better environment to do that more quickly and to learn more things?" He's personally motivated by following his curiosity, and "every time I've done that in my career, I found that a valuable move."

---

His **outer loop** over the postmortem process—extracting patterns across thousands of incidents and channeling them into product design—is the same muscle that produced DSQL's lock-free architecture and his stance against caches. His **75/25 practitioner-to-communicator** sweet spot and his conviction that "if you aren't doing it hands-on, your opinion about it is very likely to be completely wrong" both flow from the same grounding principle he saw in S3's early builders: being deep in the details is what makes strategic judgment actually land. Across every chapter—the Wednesday-morning COE meeting, the craft/analog/mainstream three-tier model, the warning against **normalization of operational heroics**—the through-line is that being "extremely grounded in reality" is not just a learning tactic but the engine of durable technical leadership.
