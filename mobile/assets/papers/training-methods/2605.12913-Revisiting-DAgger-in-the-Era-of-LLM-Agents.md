# Revisiting DAgger in the Era of LLM-Agents

**ArXiv:** [2605.12913](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.12913)

## 🎯 Pitch

Training long-horizon LLM agents by purely imitating expert demonstrations causes catastrophic failures because the agent’s own early mistakes push it into states the expert never visited—covariate shift. This paper shows that a classic interactive imitation learning algorithm (DAgger), where a teacher occasionally takes over during student rollouts to provide corrective actions, outperforms sophisticated baselines like on-policy distillation by 3–4 points on SWE-Bench Verified, enabling a 4B model to beat published 8B systems and an 8B model to surpass a 32B-parameter competitor.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper revisits **Dataset Aggregation (DAgger)** for multi-turn LLM agents, introducing a post-training recipe that collects trajectories through a turn-level stochastic mixture of student and teacher policies—the student acts with probability $1 - \beta_i$ while the teacher intervenes with probability $\beta_i$, and the student is trained via cross-entropy loss against teacher-provided labels on every visited state. The approach is evaluated on software-engineering agents built from Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 and Qwen3-8B models operating under the OpenHands scaffold, trained on SWE-Gym and tested on SWE-Bench Verified. DAgger-style training improves over the strongest post-training baseline—on-policy distillation—by +3.9 points at 4B scale (27.3% resolution rate) and +3.6 points at 8B scale (29.8%), with the 4B agent surpassing representative published 8B SWE-agent systems and the 8B agent outperforming SWE-Gym-32B, establishing that explicit state-distribution correction yields substantial gains over SFT, RLVR, and on-policy distillation only when the base student model possesses non-trivial initial capability and a stronger teacher policy is available to provide recovery actions on student-induced states.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Long-Horizon LLM Agents Suffer from a Fundamental Distribution Mismatch

The paper addresses a structural flaw in how we currently train LLM agents for multi-turn, tool-using tasks. When an agent must operate over dozens or hundreds of turns—exploring a codebase, running commands, editing files, and recovering from errors—a single early mistake can cascade through the remaining trajectory, leading the agent into states the training data never covered. This is the **covariate shift** problem: the agent is trained on one distribution of states (expert demonstrations) but deployed on a different distribution (its own induced states), and the gap between these distributions widens with every compounding error.

The paper formalizes this in Section 2 using standard imitation learning notation: a policy $\pi_\theta$ trained via behavior cloning minimizes $\mathbb{E}_{s \sim d_{\pi_e}, a_e \sim \pi_e(\cdot|s)}[\ell(\pi_\theta(\cdot|s), a_e)]$, where $d_{\pi_e}$ is the state distribution induced by the expert. But during deployment, the student follows its own state distribution $d_{\pi_\theta}$, and the resulting mismatch causes imitation error to scale **quadratically** with horizon $T$ in the worst case (Section 2, citing Ross et al., 2010; Ross et al., 2011). For software-engineering tasks where trajectories routinely span 20–100 turns, this quadratic dependence is catastrophic.

This problem matters acutely because LLM agents are no longer experimental curiosities—they are being deployed in software engineering, web browsing, data analysis, and scientific computing (Section 1). SWE-Bench Verified tasks require agents to navigate real GitHub repositories, localize bugs, edit source files across multiple locations, and submit patches that pass hidden regression tests. The agents operate under scaffolds like OpenHands that provide tool interfaces (bash execution, file editing, git operations), creating an action space where wrong file edits, incorrect grep searches, or misunderstanding of repository structure can permanently derail a trajectory. The paper emphasizes that "minor early mistakes can alter the subsequent state distribution and derail the whole trajectory" (Section 1), and the experimental evidence bears this out: in the qualitative failure analysis (Table 4), the base Qwen3-4B model's unresolved trajectories show a 74.3% rate of "wrong solution" among submitted-but-unresolved failures, indicating that the model reaches states where it produces incorrect but plausible-looking patches—precisely the kind of failure that on-policy training should address.

### The Competing Approaches and Their Structural Shortcomings

The paper identifies three existing post-training paradigms, each with a fundamental limitation that becomes severe in long-horizon settings. Table 1 in the paper provides a crisp comparison matrix, but understanding *why* each approach fails requires unpacking their mechanisms.

**Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT): Dense Supervision, Wrong States**

SFT—also called behavior cloning in the imitation learning literature—trains the student by mimicking teacher-generated trajectories collected from the initial task prompt. The teacher (in this paper's experiments, Qwen3-Coder-30B-A3B-Instruct) solves each task once, producing a trajectory of actions. The student is then trained via cross-entropy loss to predict those actions given the corresponding prefix states.

The appeal of SFT is that it provides dense, token-level supervision at every turn, and the teacher trajectories are typically high-quality (especially when filtered to retain only successful trajectories via rejection sampling, as done in SWE-Gym). The problem, however, is that SFT trains the student exclusively on states visited by the expert. During deployment, the student makes decisions that deviate from the expert's choices—and once it deviates, it enters states the training data never covered. There is no teacher demonstration for what to do when the student has `grep`-ed the wrong directory, edited the wrong function, or misunderstood the issue description. The paper's covariate-shift analysis (Section 4.4, Figure 2) quantifies this directly: SFT's reverse KL divergence $D_{KL}(\pi_\theta \| \pi_e)$ on student-induced contexts initially drops from ~0.27 to ~0.10 but then **rebounds** to ~0.126 as training continues, indicating that expert-only trajectories fail to cover the states the learned policy actually visits.

This rebound is the signature of covariate shift: early SFT training brings the student closer to the teacher on the training distribution, but since the training distribution is $d_{\pi_e}$ rather than $d_{\pi_\theta}$, the student eventually learns behaviors that work on expert states but fail on its own induced states. In software engineering terms: the student learns to edit files exactly as the teacher did, but never learns what to do when it has already edited the wrong file.

**Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR): Right States, Sparse Signal**

RLVR—instantiated here as GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization) trained on executable test feedback—addresses SFT's distribution mismatch by training on the student's own rollouts. The student generates trajectories, each trajectory receives an outcome-level reward (pass/fail based on whether the final patch passes the unit tests), and the policy is updated via policy gradient with group-normalized advantages.

This solves the state distribution problem: the student trains on $d_{\pi_\theta}$, so there is no train-test mismatch. But it introduces a different problem: **sparse credit assignment**. In a 50-turn trajectory where the agent does 45 turns of productive exploration followed by a single incorrect edit that causes the tests to fail, the reward signal provides no information about which turn was the mistake. The outcome-level reward treats the entire trajectory as a monolithic failure, making it extremely difficult for the policy to identify and correct specific errors.

The paper identifies additional practical issues with RLVR in this setting (Section 1). First, **group sampling is computationally expensive**: GRPO requires sampling 8 trajectories per task (as configured in the SkyRL-v0 setup), multiplying the inference cost by 8× compared to SFT. Second, **advantage estimates collapse when samples lack diversity**: when all 8 rollouts on a task either succeed or all fail, there is no relative advantage signal to differentiate good actions from bad ones—a problem the paper notes is especially acute on very easy or very hard tasks. The experimental results bear this out: GRPO barely moves the needle, improving from 11.2% to 11.6% on SWE-Bench Verified for the 4B model and from 7.7% to 8.2% for the 8B model (Table 3). These near-zero gains likely reflect the difficulty of credit assignment over long horizons combined with the limited diversity in rollout outcomes.

**On-Policy Distillation (OPD): Right States, Dense Supervision, but Brittle Cold Start**

OPD represents a hybrid: it collects trajectories from the student (on-policy state distribution) but uses the teacher to provide dense supervision by minimizing the reverse KL divergence $D_{KL}(\pi_\theta \| \pi_e)$ on student-visited states. The paper formulates this in its unified framework (Section 3.3, Table 2) as training with context distribution $d_{\pi_\theta}$, label distribution $\pi_\theta$ (the student's own actions), and importance weight $w(s,a) = \log \frac{\pi_e(a|s)}{\pi_\theta(a|s)}$—essentially, the student is encouraged to increase probability on actions the teacher assigns higher probability, using the student's own rollouts as the sampling distribution.

This gives OPD the best of both worlds in principle: on-policy states + dense teacher signal. But the paper identifies a **cold-start bottleneck** that is especially severe in long-horizon tasks (Section 1): "early rollouts from weak students often fail prematurely, especially with long horizon tasks, forcing the teacher to supervise unsuccessful prefixes rather than productive trajectories." If the initial student model is weak (and the paper's base Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 achieves only 11.2% on SWE-Bench Verified, so 88.8% of its trajectories fail), then the early OPD batches consist primarily of failed trajectories. The teacher can provide supervision on these failed trajectories—"here is what you should have done at this state"—but the states visited are predominantly early-failure states, not the productive middle-and-late trajectory states where the student needs the most help.

The practical consequence is visible in Figure 1: at 3K effective training samples, OPD reaches only 9% on SWE-Gym Holdout and 13% on SWE-Bench Verified-100, substantially below DAgger-style training (12% and 20%, respectively). OPD eventually catches up somewhat on SWE-Gym Holdout (reaching 16% vs. DAgger's 17%) but lags significantly on SWE-Bench Verified (23.4% vs. 27.3%). This pattern—OPD doing reasonably on in-domain held-out tasks but struggling on out-of-domain generalization—suggests that cold-start rollouts bias the model toward early-trajectory corrections rather than robust long-horizon behavior.

A second limitation of OPD is **requirement for teacher logits**. The reverse KL objective requires access to the teacher's full token-level probability distribution, which is impossible when the teacher is a black-box API model (e.g., GPT-4, Gemini). The paper explicitly notes this in Table 1 as an advantage of their method over OPD.

### Where Existing Approaches Converge: The Unified Framework

The paper's Section 3.3 provides a valuable synthetic contribution: a unified objective that reveals how all post-training methods differ along only three axes:

$$\theta_{i+1} = \arg\max_\theta \mathbb{E}_{s \sim \text{sg}(p_s), a \sim \text{sg}(p_a)}[\text{sg}(w(s,a)) \log \pi_\theta(a|s)] - \lambda \Omega_i(\theta)$$

This framework clarifies exactly what each method gains and loses:

- **SFT**: $p_s = d_{\pi_e}$ (expert states, wrong distribution), $p_a = \pi_e$ (expert labels, right labels), $w \equiv 1$ (uniform weighting). The distribution mismatch in $p_s$ is the fatal flaw.

- **RLVR**: $p_s = d_{\pi_\theta}$ (student states, right distribution), $p_a = \pi_\theta$ (student actions, potentially wrong actions), $w = A(s,a)$ (sparse advantage). The label source and weighting are the fatal flaws.

- **OPD**: $p_s = d_{\pi_\theta}$ (student states, right distribution), $p_a = \pi_\theta$ (student actions, still wrong), $w = \log(\pi_e/\pi_\theta)$ (teacher-guided weighting). The cold-start quality of $p_s$ is the fatal flaw, and $p_a$ still being student actions limits the directness of supervision.

The paper's DAgger-style method makes a specific substitution: $p_s = d_{\text{mix}_i}$ (mixture of student and teacher states, interpolating toward on-policy), $p_a = \pi_e$ (teacher labels, direct supervision), $w \equiv 1$ (simple supervised learning). This substitution is what the paper means by "revisiting DAgger"—it is not a new algorithm but rather a recognition that the three-axis decomposition reveals a specific design point (mixture states + expert labels + uniform weights) that none of the existing LLM post-training methods occupy.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper explicitly frames itself as resolving the dilemma between SFT's dense-but-off-policy supervision and RLVR's on-policy-but-sparse feedback, while also addressing OPD's cold-start fragility (Section 1, Table 1, and the introduction's central question: "Is there any method that can simultaneously exploit dense feedback, while with on-policy coverage and early access to successful trajectories?").

This is not presented as a new algorithm invention but as a **principled adaptation** of DAgger (Ross et al., 2011) to the multi-turn LM agent setting. The key adaptation is the turn-level stochastic mixing protocol: rather than DAgger's original approach of alternating between full student trajectories and full expert trajectories across iterations, the paper proposes two protocols—**DAgger-style** (independent turn-level Bernoulli mixing with parameter $\beta_i$, decaying from 1.0 to 0.6 across iterations) and **AggreVaTe-style** (student generates a prefix of length $\kappa \sim \rho_i$, teacher completes the trajectory). Both achieve the same principle: early iterations are teacher-dominated, exposing the student to successful trajectories and recovery behaviors, while later iterations shift toward student-induced states, correcting the distribution mismatch.

The paper draws a direct line from classical imitation learning theory to modern LLM agent training. DAgger's original no-regret guarantee—reducing imitation error dependence on horizon from quadratic to linear—is cited in Section 2 as the theoretical motivation. The paper's contribution is showing that this theoretical benefit translates to practical gains in a challenging real-world domain (software engineering) at non-trivial scale (4B–8B parameter models, 2,338 training tasks, 500 evaluation instances).

Critically, the paper positions its method as **not requiring fundamentally new infrastructure**. It uses the same OpenHands scaffold, the same SWE-Gym training data, and the same optimization budget as SFT and OPD (5 rollout-update iterations, constant learning rate $3 \times 10^{-6}$, batch size 16). The only change is in how trajectories are collected: each turn flips a biased coin to decide whether the teacher or student acts, and the teacher provides labels regardless. This minimal implementation change is a strength: it means existing post-training pipelines can adopt DAgger-style mixing with essentially no additional engineering beyond what OPD already requires.

The paper also positions its method relative to concurrent work. It explicitly cites Lauffer et al. (2025), which "switches from student rollouts to expert completion midway" (Section 5), noting that this is similar to the AggreVaTe-style protocol, but that the DAgger-style variant additionally uses turn-level mixing and local teacher labels, providing finer-grained state distribution correction. The paper does not claim to be the first to apply imitation learning to LLM agents, but rather argues that previous applications (like OPD) missed the critical ingredient of teacher interleaving during data collection, settling for pure on-policy rollouts that fail to bootstrap from weak initial policies.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation (Approachable Technical Breakdown)

The system being built is a post-training pipeline that takes a moderately capable LLM student agent and improves its ability to solve multi-turn software-engineering tasks by having it practice on real GitHub issues while a stronger teacher model periodically intervenes to correct its trajectory. The paper solves the problem of distribution mismatch between training and deployment for LLM agents—specifically, the fact that supervised fine-tuning trains agents on expert-generated states that look nothing like the messy, error-prone states the agent actually encounters when acting on its own—by constructing training trajectories as a stochastic mixture of student and teacher actions, so the student learns from teacher-provided corrections on states it genuinely tends to visit.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The system has five interconnected components operating in a training loop:

1. **Task Distribution** — a pool of 2,338 SWE-Gym software-engineering tasks (GitHub issues with executable unit-test suites) from which 512 instances are sampled per training iteration.

2. **Rollout Engine (OpenHands Scaffold)** — the execution environment that processes agent actions (bash commands, file edits, git operations) and returns environment observations, running inside Docker containers with the target codebase checked out at `/testbed`.

3. **Stochastic Mixture Controller** — a turn-level Bernoulli selector with parameter $\beta_i \in [0, 1]$ that flips a biased coin at every turn to decide whether the teacher policy $\pi_e$ or the student policy $\pi_\theta$ executes the next action, producing trajectories where both policies contribute to the visited states.

4. **Teacher Labeler** — for every state visited during a mixed-policy rollout (regardless of who acted), the teacher policy $\pi_e$ is queried for its preferred action $\tilde{a}_t$, which becomes the training target for that state.

5. **Student Updater** — a standard supervised learning loop that optimizes the cross-entropy loss between the student's predicted action distribution and the teacher-provided labels on the collected states, using batches of teacher-labeled state–action pairs from the most recent mixed-policy rollout.

The information flow is: task instance enters → mixture controller determines per-turn execution policy → student or teacher executes action through OpenHands → environment returns observation → teacher provides label for the state → data aggregator collects state–label pairs → student trainer optimizes cross-entropy → updated student is used in next iteration → $\beta_i$ decays to shift distribution toward student-induced states.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the multi-turn agent setting (Section 3.1 in the paper), which formalizes the state–action–observation dynamics of LLM agents operating under scaffolds—this is the mathematical substrate everything else builds on.

- **Second**, the DAgger-style rollout protocol and the AggreVaTe-style rollout protocol (Section 3.2), because these are the core mechanisms that produce the training data and are the only components that differ from standard post-training pipelines.

- **Third**, the training objective (Section 3.2), which is straightforward but requires careful treatment of how token-level cross-entropy combines with packing multiple transitions into shared-prefix trajectories.

- **Fourth**, the unified framework (Section 3.3), which is not a component of the system but rather the lens through which all post-training methods are compared—understanding it reveals exactly what design choices DAgger-style training makes and why those choices matter.

- **Fifth**, the practical implementation details that bridge the algorithm description to the experimental results (drawn from Appendix F and the main text)—mixing schedules, batch sizes, learning rates, and the data filtering procedure.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **method adaptation paper** whose core idea is that the DAgger principle—train on student-induced states with expert-provided labels—can be realized for multi-turn LLM agents through a stochastic turn-level mixture policy, and that this realization combines the on-policy state coverage of RL/OPD with the dense supervision of SFT while avoiding the cold-start failures of pure on-policy methods.

#### Formalizing the Multi-Turn LLM-Agent Setting

The paper begins by setting up precise notation for the agent's interaction loop, which is necessary because the DAgger protocol operates at the granularity of individual turns within multi-turn trajectories.

A task instance is specified by an initial prompt $x \sim q$ drawn from a task distribution $q$—for software engineering, $x$ is the issue description, hints, and repository state. The agent then interacts with an environment over a sequence of turns indexed by $t = 1, 2, \ldots, T$. At each turn $t$, the policy observes the interaction history and samples an action $a_t$, which may include intermediate reasoning (chain-of-thought) and a tool invocation (bash command, file edit, finish signal). The environment executes the action and returns an observation $o_t$:

$$a_t \sim \pi(\cdot \mid x, a_{1:t-1}, o_{1:t-1})$$

$$o_t \sim \text{Env}(\cdot \mid x, a_{1:t}, o_{1:t-1})$$

where $\pi$ is either the student policy $\pi_\theta$ or the teacher policy $\pi_e$, $\text{Env}$ is the scaffold execution environment (OpenHands, providing tool outputs, error messages, file contents), $a_{1:t-1}$ is the sequence of all previous actions, and $o_{1:t-1}$ is the sequence of all previous observations.

**What these equations describe:** The first equation says that the policy is a conditional distribution over next actions given all prior context—the prompt plus every action–observation pair so far. The second equation says the environment is a (possibly deterministic) function that, given the prompt, all actions taken so far (including the latest one), and all observations returned so far, produces the next observation. The interaction terminates when either the agent emits a designated finish action or a maximum turn budget $T_{\text{max}}$ (set to 100 in experiments, Appendix F) is reached, producing a full trajectory:

$$\tau = (x, a_1, o_1, \ldots, a_T, o_T), \quad T \leq T_{\text{max}}$$

A verifier then assigns a terminal success signal $R(\tau, x) \in \{0, 1\}$—for SWE tasks, this is whether the submitted patch passes the hidden unit tests. The paper does not use $R$ directly for training in DAgger-style variants (unlike RLVR which treats $R$ as the reward), but $R$ is used for evaluation and for trajectory filtering (keeping only trajectories that produce valid patch submissions, Appendix F).

**A critical compression:** To make the notation manageable across turns, the paper defines the state $s_t$ as the observable interaction history at the start of turn $t$:

$$s_t \triangleq (x, a_{1:t-1}, o_{1:t-1})$$

This means the state at turn $t$ is everything the agent has seen before choosing its $t$-th action: the initial prompt $x$, all previous actions $a_1$ through $a_{t-1}$, and all previous observations $o_1$ through $o_{t-1}$. The policy can then be written compactly as $\pi(\cdot \mid s_t)$. This compression makes the state space explicit: $s_t$ grows with $t$ as the trajectory lengthens, and different agent behaviors produce different state histories, which is precisely why covariate shift matters—SFT trains on states $s_t \sim d_{\pi_e}$ while deployment sees states $s_t \sim d_{\pi_\theta}$.

**Why this formalism matters:** It establishes that a "state" in this setting is not a fixed-size vector (as in traditional RL) but an expanding sequence of tokens representing the full conversation and tool-use history. The DAgger principle of "collect states from the student and label them with the teacher" therefore means collecting these prefix histories—which can be thousands of tokens long—and querying the teacher to generate a complete next-action token sequence for each prefix. The paper's implementation handles this by literally constructing conversation histories up to each turn, passing them through the teacher model, and storing the teacher's generated response as the label.

#### DAgger-Style Rollout: Turn-Level Stochastic Mixture

The DAgger-style protocol is the paper's primary contribution and implements the core idea of DAgger (Ross et al., 2011) adapted to the turn structure of LLM agents. At each training iteration $i$, a mixing parameter $\beta_i \in [0, 1]$ controls the probability that any individual turn is executed by the teacher rather than the student.

**The mixing mechanism.** For a trajectory with maximum length $T_{\text{max}}$, a binary indicator sequence $b_{1:T_{\text{max}}}$ is sampled where each $b_t \in \{0, 1\}$ is drawn independently:

$$p_i^{\text{turn}}(b_{1:T_{\text{max}}}) = \prod_{t=1}^{T_{\text{max}}} \beta_i^{b_t} (1 - \beta_i)^{1-b_t}$$

where $b_t = 1$ means the teacher acts at turn $t$, and $b_t = 0$ means the student acts at turn $t$.

**What this product computes:** The probability of a specific indicator sequence $(b_1, b_2, \ldots, b_{T_{\text{max}}})$ as a product of independent Bernoulli terms. For each turn $t$, the exponent selects which factor contributes: if $b_t = 1$, the factor $\beta_i^{1} \cdot (1-\beta_i)^0 = \beta_i$ gives the probability the teacher acts; if $b_t = 0$, the factor $\beta_i^0 \cdot (1-\beta_i)^1 = 1-\beta_i$ gives the probability the student acts. The product over all turns gives a joint distribution where each turn's executor is chosen independently of all others.

**Why this form:** The independent turn-level mixing is what distinguishes DAgger-style from AggreVaTe-style. Independence means the teacher can intervene at any turn based purely on the coin flip, not based on any assessment of state quality. This has two important consequences: (1) it produces trajectories where teacher and student actions are interleaved at fine granularity, meaning the student encounters states reached partly through its own actions and partly through teacher actions—a more gradual distribution shift than binary "student prefix then teacher suffix"; (2) it ensures that even early in training when $\beta_i$ is high, some student actions appear, providing on-policy exposure from the very first iteration.

**Action execution.** At each turn $t$, given the indicator $b_t$, the system executes:

$$a_t \sim \begin{cases} \pi_e(\cdot \mid s_t), & \text{if } b_t = 1 \\ \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s_t), & \text{if } b_t = 0 \end{cases}$$

The environment then produces $o_t \sim \text{Env}(\cdot \mid x, a_{1:t}, o_{1:t-1})$, and the trajectory advances to state $s_{t+1} = (x, a_{1:t}, o_{1:t})$. Critically, $a_t$ might have been produced by the teacher or the student, but either way it becomes part of the history that the next turn's policy conditions on. When the teacher acts, it can "rescue" the trajectory from states the student would have failed in, producing observations that the student would never see on its own. When the student acts, it produces deployment-realistic states that may diverge from expert trajectories—and it is precisely these divergent states that need teacher labels.

**Teacher labeling regardless of executor.** The crucial DAgger principle that enables supervised learning on student-induced states is: at every visited state $s_t$, irrespective of whether the teacher or student executed the action, the teacher is queried for its preferred action:

$$\tilde{a}_t \sim \pi_e(\cdot \mid s_t)$$

This label $\tilde{a}_t$ is what the student will be trained to predict. The labeling happens even when $b_t = 1$ (teacher executed), which may seem redundant (labeling teacher states with teacher actions is just SFT), but the value is in the states where $b_t = 0$: the student produced an action (potentially a bad one), the environment responded with an observation (potentially a confusing or error-filled one), and then the teacher provides what the correct action would have been in that exact situation. These are the crucial data points that SFT never provides—what to do after you've already made a mistake.

The data collected from one trajectory is therefore:

$$B(\tau) = \{(s_t, \tilde{a}_t) : t = 1, \ldots, T\}$$

where $s_t$ is the actual state visited (which may have been reached through a mix of teacher and student actions) and $\tilde{a}_t$ is the teacher's action at that state. These pairs are aggregated across all trajectories in the rollout batch to form the training dataset $\mathcal{D}_i$ for iteration $i$.

**The mixing schedule decay.** The paper uses a specific annealing schedule for $\beta_i$ (Appendix F):

$$\beta_i = \max(0.6, 1.0 - 0.2(i - 1))$$

where $i = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5$ is the iteration index across 5 total rollout-update cycles. This produces the sequence: $\beta_1 = 1.0$, $\beta_2 = 0.8$, $\beta_3 = 0.6$, $\beta_4 = 0.6$, $\beta_5 = 0.6$.

**What this schedule achieves:** Iteration 1 ($\beta_1 = 1.0$) is pure teacher rollouts—every turn is executed by the teacher. At this stage, the student sees only expert states, identical to SFT's initial data distribution. Iteration 2 ($\beta_2 = 0.8$) introduces 20% student turns, exposing the student to states it reaches on its own 20% of the time while the teacher rescues 80% of turns. By iteration 3 ($\beta_3 = 0.6$), the distribution has shifted to 40% student turns, and the floor of 0.6 prevents the teacher intervention rate from dropping below 60%, ensuring that even in late iterations the teacher still provides substantial guidance and trajectory recovery. The floor is a practical design choice that the paper does not theoretically justify but which likely prevents the extreme case of $\beta_i \to 0$, where trajectories would be pure student rollouts that might fail too early to provide useful learning signal, reverting to OPD's cold-start problem.

**Why not decay all the way to zero?** This is an important empirical choice that distinguishes this work from classical DAgger. In classical DAgger (Ross et al., 2011), $\beta_i \to 0$ as $i \to \infty$, meaning eventually all data comes from the student's own policy. The paper's decision to floor at 0.6 reflects a practical reality of long-horizon software-engineering tasks: even a well-trained student still makes errors that derail trajectories, and without periodic teacher interventions, the collected data would consist mainly of early failure states rather than the full trajectory coverage needed for continued improvement. The teacher interventions serve as "reset" mechanisms: when the student takes a wrong turn, the teacher's next action steers the trajectory back toward productive states, ensuring that the student sees both the error state (valuable for learning) and the recovery path (valuable for learning how to fix mistakes). This floor at 0.6 means the method never becomes fully on-policy—it remains in a regime where 60% of visited states are teacher-induced, which is a deliberate trade-off: accept some residual off-policy-ness in exchange for ensuring productive trajectories.

#### AggreVaTe-Style Rollout: Trajectory-Level Prefix-Completion

The AggreVaTe-style protocol provides an alternative mixing strategy with a coarser granularity: rather than mixing teacher and student turns throughout the trajectory, the student controls the first $\kappa$ turns and the teacher completes the remainder.

**The prefix-length distribution.** At iteration $i$, a student-prefix length $\kappa$ is sampled from a distribution $\rho_i$ over $\{0, 1, \ldots, T_{\text{max}}\}$. Given $\kappa$, the indicator sequence is deterministic:

$$p_i^{\text{traj}}(b_{1:T_{\text{max}}} \mid \kappa) = \prod_{t=1}^{\kappa} \mathbb{I}\{b_t = 0\} \prod_{t=\kappa+1}^{T_{\text{max}}} \mathbb{I}\{b_t = 1\}$$

where $\mathbb{I}\{\cdot\}$ is the indicator function that equals 1 when the condition is true and 0 otherwise.

**What this product computes:** The first product $\prod_{t=1}^{\kappa} \mathbb{I}\{b_t = 0\}$ enforces that $b_t = 0$ for all $t \leq \kappa$—the student acts during the first $\kappa$ turns. The second product $\prod_{t=\kappa+1}^{T_{\text{max}}} \mathbb{I}\{b_t = 1\}$ enforces that $b_t = 1$ for all $t > \kappa$—the teacher acts from turn $\kappa+1$ onward until termination. Unlike the DAgger-style protocol where $b_t$ are independent, this is a trajectory-level mixture: there is exactly one switch point $\kappa$, after which the teacher takes full control.

**Why this form:** The AggreVaTe-style protocol is directly inspired by AggreVaTe (Ross and Bagnell, 2014), which uses student-prefix followed by expert-completion to provide trajectory-level correction. The key difference from DAgger-style is the **granularity of teacher intervention**: in DAgger-style, the teacher may intervene at any turn and the student may resume control afterward, creating interleaved trajectories; in AggreVaTe-style, the teacher takes over completely after the switch point, providing a full completion from $\kappa+1$ to termination. This means AggreVaTe-style provides longer contiguous blocks of student-induced states (the prefix of length $\kappa$) followed by teacher recovery, which can be more interpretable for analysis—the student's autonomous performance on the first $\kappa$ steps can be directly assessed.

**The distribution $\rho_i$.** The paper specifies (Appendix F) that $\rho_i = \text{Unif}\{0, \ldots, 40\}$, a uniform distribution over prefix lengths from 0 (pure teacher trajectory) to 40. The shift toward longer prefixes over training is implemented by "shifting probability mass toward longer student prefixes over training," though the exact schedule is not fully specified in the appendix. The maximum of 40 is motivated by the empirical observation that student rollouts typically terminate within about 40 turns, making this the practical range of interesting prefix lengths.

**Comparison with DAgger-style.** The paper's results show that AggreVaTe-style training achieves competitive but generally slightly lower performance than DAgger-style (Table 3: 24.5% vs. 27.3% on SWE-Bench Verified for 4B; 27.3% vs. 29.8% for 8B). The paper attributes AggreVaTe-style's advantage in one specific dimension—it achieves the lowest wrong-file rate (15.7%, Table 4)—to the fact that longer teacher continuations can improve repository localization. The disadvantage in overall resolution rate may stem from the coarser intervention granularity: if the student makes a mistake at turn 3 and then flounders until turn 20 before the teacher takes over, those 17 turns of floundering produce states that are not necessarily informative for learning (the student is essentially performing random exploration in a broken trajectory). DAgger-style's turn-level mixing allows the teacher to intervene sooner after a mistake, producing more targeted corrections.

#### Training Objective: Token-Level Cross-Entropy on Teacher Labels

After collecting the dataset $\mathcal{D}_i = \{(s_t, \tilde{a}_t)\}_{t=1}^N$ from mixed-policy rollouts (where $N$ sums over all transitions across all trajectories in the rollout batch), the student model is optimized using standard supervised learning. The loss at iteration $i$ is:

$$\mathcal{L}_i(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{(s_t, \tilde{a}_t) \sim \mathcal{D}_i} \left[ \sum_{t=1}^T \ell_{\text{CE}}(\theta; s_t, \tilde{a}_t) \right]$$

where the per-transition cross-entropy decomposes over the tokens of the teacher action. Specifically, for a teacher action $\tilde{a}_t$ consisting of $m_t$ tokens $(\tilde{a}_{t,1}, \tilde{a}_{t,2}, \ldots, \tilde{a}_{t,m_t})$:

$$\ell_{\text{CE}}(\theta; s_t, \tilde{a}_t) = -\sum_{j=1}^{m_t} \log \pi_\theta(\tilde{a}_{t,j} \mid s_t, \tilde{a}_{t,<j})$$

where $\tilde{a}_{t,<j}$ denotes the prefix of the teacher action up to (but not including) token $j$, and $\pi_\theta(\tilde{a}_{t,j} \mid s_t, \tilde{a}_{t,<j})$ is the student model's predicted probability of token $\tilde{a}_{t,j}$ given the state $s_t$ and the preceding action tokens.

**What this loss computes:** For each state–label pair in the dataset, the student model processes the state $s_t$ (the entire conversation and tool-use history up to turn $t$) and generates a probability distribution over its vocabulary for the next token position. The cross-entropy term $-\log \pi_\theta(\tilde{a}_{t,j} \mid s_t, \tilde{a}_{t,<j})$ penalizes the student for assigning low probability to the token the teacher actually produced. Summing over all $m_t$ tokens in the action gives the total per-turn loss. Summing over all $T$ turns in the trajectory gives the trajectory-level loss. The expectation $\mathbb{E}_{(s_t, \tilde{a}_t) \sim \mathcal{D}_i}$ averages over all state–label pairs in the aggregated dataset.

**Why this form:** This is the maximum-likelihood objective for autoregressive sequence prediction, which is the standard training objective for LLMs and the natural choice when the supervision signal is a target sequence (the teacher's action). The token-level decomposition matches the standard next-token prediction training used in pretraining and SFT, meaning no change to the optimizer, loss function, or training infrastructure is required—the only change is where the data comes from. An alternative would be to use a sequence-level loss (e.g., a reward-weighted objective as in RLVR), but that would sacrifice the dense per-token learning signal that makes supervised learning sample-efficient. Another alternative would be to use the reverse-KL distillation objective of OPD, but that would require training on the student's own action distribution with importance weights, which the paper shows (via the unified framework) provides a weaker and less direct learning signal than direct teacher action imitation.

**Computational efficiency through prefix packing.** The paper notes an important implementation detail (Section 3.2): transitions that share a common state prefix are packed into a single trajectory during training, with a loss mask applied to ensure the gradient remains equivalent to individual updates. For example, in a trajectory with states $s_1, s_2, s_3$, the state $s_2$ contains $s_1$ as a prefix (since $s_2 = (s_1, a_1, o_1)$), and $s_3$ contains $s_2$ as a prefix. Rather than processing each state–label pair as an independent forward pass—which would redundantly process the shared prefix tokens $T$ times for a length-$T$ trajectory—the implementation packs all transitions into one sequence and applies the cross-entropy loss only on the label tokens corresponding to each turn. This reduces the computational cost from $O(T \cdot \text{prefix\_length})$ to $O(\text{total\_sequence\_length})$, which is essential for training on long trajectories where $T$ can reach 100 turns with tens of thousands of tokens.

**The effective sample count.** The paper reports results in terms of effective training samples (Figure 1, 3K to 8K range), which counts the number of state–label pairs used for training after filtering out trajectories that do not produce valid patch submissions (Appendix F). This filtering step is important: trajectories where the mixture of student and teacher actions fails to produce a coherent final patch (e.g., the agent gets stuck in a loop, exhausts the context, or never calls the finish tool) are excluded from training because they provide no meaningful learning signal at the terminal states. The filtering is analogous to SFT's rejection sampling (keeping only successful expert trajectories) but differs in that it only requires a valid submission, not necessarily a passing one—the paper filters for trajectories that produce a parsable patch, not necessarily a correct one. This distinction matters because even incorrect patches contain valuable supervision: the teacher's actions leading up to and including the incorrect patch show the student what it should have done in those states, even if the trajectory ultimately failed.

#### The Unified Framework: Why DAgger Occupies a Specific Design Point

The paper's Section 3.3 provides a theoretical synthesis that is not a component of the training pipeline but rather a lens for understanding exactly what design choices DAgger-style training makes relative to alternatives. This framework is essential for grasping the paper's contribution because it shows that DAgger-style training is not an ad-hoc combination of SFT and OPD, but rather occupies a specific, principled design point in a three-dimensional space of post-training algorithms.

All post-training methods can be expressed as optimizing an objective of the form:

$$\theta_{i+1} = \arg\max_\theta \mathbb{E}_{s \sim \text{sg}(p_s), a \sim \text{sg}(p_a)} \left[ \text{sg}(w(s, a)) \log \pi_\theta(a \mid s) \right] - \lambda \Omega_i(\theta)$$

where $\text{sg}(\cdot)$ denotes the stop-gradient operator (meaning gradients are not propagated through the sampling distributions or importance weights), $p_s$ is the distribution over contexts (states) from which training examples are drawn, $p_a(\cdot \mid s)$ is the distribution from which action labels are sampled for a given context, $w(s, a)$ is a scalar importance weight assigned to each sample, and $\Omega_i(\theta)$ is an optional regularizer (e.g., KL penalty) with coefficient $\lambda$.

**What this objective framework reveals:** The three axes—where states come from ($p_s$), where labels come from ($p_a$), and how samples are weighted ($w$)—are the only degrees of freedom in post-training algorithm design (assuming the base objective is log-likelihood maximization). By specifying choices along these three axes for each method, the paper shows that existing approaches occupy specific points in this space, and that the DAgger point is a natural but previously unexplored combination.

**SFT / Behavior Cloning:** $p_s = d_{\pi_e}$ (states from expert trajectories), $p_a = \pi_e$ (labels are expert actions), $w(s,a) \equiv 1$ (all samples weighted equally). The plain-language consequence: train on states the expert visits, imitating exactly what the expert did on those states. The distribution mismatch in $p_s$ is the reason SFT degrades under covariate shift—the student never sees the states it will actually encounter.

**RLVR / Policy Gradient:** $p_s = d_{\pi_\theta}$ (states from student trajectories), $p_a = \pi_\theta$ (labels are student's own actions), $w(s,a) = A(s,a)$ (weighted by advantage, which can be positive, negative, or zero). The plain-language consequence: train on states the student visits, reinforce actions that led to good outcomes, penalize actions that led to bad outcomes—but the outcome signal is at trajectory level, not per-turn. The sparse weighting $w$ is the reason RLVR struggles with credit assignment.

**On-Policy Distillation:** $p_s = d_{\pi_\theta}$ (states from student trajectories), $p_a = \pi_\theta$ (labels are student's own actions—crucially, not teacher actions), $w(s,a) = \log \frac{\pi_e(a \mid s)}{\pi_\theta(a \mid s)} = -\log \frac{\pi_\theta(a \mid s)}{\pi_e(a \mid s)}$ (weighted by the log-ratio of teacher to student probabilities). The plain-language consequence: train on states the student visits, and for each action the student took, increase its probability if the teacher assigns it higher probability than the student currently does, decrease it otherwise. The weighting function comes from the score-function gradient of the reverse KL divergence $D_{\text{KL}}(\pi_\theta \| \pi_e)$, which has the property that the gradient is $\mathbb{E}_{a \sim \pi_\theta}[(\log \pi_e(a|s) - \log \pi_\theta(a|s)) \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a|s)]$.

**OPD's weight interpretation in plain language:** If the student took action $a$ and the teacher thinks $a$ is much more likely than the student does ($\pi_e(a|s) \gg \pi_\theta(a|s)$), then $\log(\pi_e/\pi_\theta)$ is large and positive, so this action gets strongly reinforced—"the teacher says you should be much more confident in this action." If the student took action $a$ and the teacher thinks it's unlikely ($\pi_e(a|s) \ll \pi_\theta(a|s)$), then $\log(\pi_e/\pi_\theta)$ is negative, so this action gets penalized—"you're overconfident in this action, the teacher would rarely choose it." If student and teacher agree ($\pi_e \approx \pi_\theta$), the weight is near zero and the sample contributes little to the gradient.

**Why OPD's form creates the cold-start problem:** The weights $w(s,a)$ depend on the student's own action distribution $\pi_\theta$. When the student is weak (early training), it takes many low-quality actions, and the teacher assigns very low probability to these actions, producing large negative weights. These negative-weight samples effectively teach the student "don't do what you just did," which is useful but provides limited positive guidance about what to do instead. More importantly, the student's early trajectories tend to fail early, so most samples come from early-trajectory states—the student learns to avoid egregious early mistakes but gets little supervision on middle and late trajectory states where successful task completion actually happens.

**DAgger-Style (Ours):** $p_s = d_i^{\text{turn}}$ (states from the turn-level mixture distribution induced by $p_i^{\text{turn}}$ with mixing parameter $\beta_i$), $p_a = \pi_e$ (labels are expert actions, regardless of who acted), $w(s,a) \equiv 1$ (all samples weighted equally).

**What changes relative to the alternatives:** Compared to SFT, DAgger-style changes $p_s$ from pure expert to a mixture that increasingly includes student-induced states—this is the covariate shift fix. Compared to RLVR, DAgger-style changes $p_a$ from student's own actions to expert actions and $w$ from sparse advantage to uniform weighting—this replaces sparse reward signal with dense supervised signal. Compared to OPD, DAgger-style makes two changes: (1) $p_s$ includes teacher-induced states through the mixing parameter $\beta_i$, providing productive trajectories even early in training and avoiding cold-start failures; (2) $p_a$ switches from student actions to teacher actions, providing direct rather than importance-weighted supervision. The uniform weight $w \equiv 1$ means every teacher-labeled state–action pair contributes equally to the gradient, which is appropriate because every teacher action on a visited state is a valid demonstration of what to do in that situation.

**The uni ed view's explanatory power for the cold-start advantage:** OPD's state distribution $d_{\pi_\theta}$ at iteration 1 is induced by the untrained student—for the 4B model with 11.2% base resolution rate, roughly 89% of these trajectories fail. The teacher can provide importance weights on these failed trajectories, but the states are predominantly early-failure states. DAgger-style with $\beta_1 = 1.0$ has state distribution $d_{\pi_e}$ at iteration 1—pure expert trajectories, all of which are successful (after filtering). The student gets full expert demonstrations on successful trajectories first, then gradually shifts toward its own states in iterations 2–5. This staged curriculum—pure expert first, then increasing student exposure—is what the paper means by "cold-start robustness."

**The uni ed view's explanatory power for the dense supervision advantage:** In RLVR, $p_a = \pi_\theta$ means the training signal is "the action you took was good/bad based on the final outcome." In OPD, $p_a = \pi_\theta$ means the training signal is "the action you took should be more/less likely based on teacher probabilities." In DAgger-style, $p_a = \pi_e$ means the training signal is "here is exactly what the expert would have done—predict this." The third signal is more direct because it provides a complete target sequence rather than a scalar modifier on the student's own sequence. When the student has taken a suboptimal action that the teacher would not have taken, RLVR provides no signal (because $a$ was not sampled from $\pi_e$, so the teacher's probability for $a$ is undefined), OPD provides a negative weight (discouraging the action), and DAgger-style provides a positive example (the teacher's alternative action). The positive example is more informative because it shows what to do, not just what not to do.

**AggreVaTe-Style (Ours) in the unified framework:** $p_s = d_i^{\text{traj}}$ (states from the trajectory-level mixture), $p_a = \pi_e$, $w(s,a) \equiv 1$. This is the same design point as DAgger-style in terms of label source and weighting, differing only in the context distribution $p_s$: trajectory-level mixture with a single switch point versus turn-level independent mixing. The paper's experimental finding that DAgger-style outperforms AggreVaTe-style overall (27.3% vs. 24.5% on SWE-Bench Verified for 4B) suggests that the finer-grained turn-level mixing provides more effective state distribution correction—the interleaved teacher interventions create a more diverse set of states where teacher rescue follows student error, compared to the binary "student acts, then teacher completes" pattern.

#### Practical Implementation Configurations

The paper provides detailed hyperparameter settings in Appendix F that govern how the abstract algorithm is realized in practice.

**Rollout collection configuration.** Each training iteration collects a fresh batch of 512 task instances from the SWE-Gym training set (2,338 tasks total, with 100 held out as SWE-Gym Holdout for evaluation). The 512 tasks are processed through mixed-policy rollouts to produce trajectories. For DAgger-style, the Bernoulli indicator sequence $b_{1:T_{\text{max}}}$ is sampled independently for each turn of each task. For AggreVaTe-style, a prefix length $\kappa$ is sampled from $\text{Unif}\{0, \ldots, 40\}$ for each task independently.

Rollout collection uses temperature 0.7 and top-p = 0.9 for both the student and teacher policies when generating actions. This temperature is moderately high, encouraging diversity in the student's actions (so the collected states cover a range of possible deviations from the expert) while the teacher's actions at the same temperature remain reliable (the teacher is substantially stronger, so temperature 0.7 does not significantly degrade its action quality). Evaluation, by contrast, uses greedy decoding (temperature 0) to maximize reproducibility and task-resolution performance.

**Context and horizon limits.** The maximum context length is 64K tokens, and the maximum number of environment interactions per trajectory is 100 turns ($T_{\text{max}} = 100$). The 64K context window is the model's native limit (Qwen3 models in this scale support 64K–128K context), and trajectories that exceed this limit are categorized as "context overflow" failures in the qualitative analysis (Table 4). The 100-turn limit is a practical bound: most successful trajectories terminate well before 100 turns (via the finish action), and trajectories that reach 100 turns are typically stuck in loops or unproductive exploration.

**Training hyperparameters.** The student model is trained for 3 epochs over each collected online batch, using batch size 16, constant learning rate $3 \times 10^{-6}$, and no learning rate warmup or decay. The optimizer is AdamW (assumed standard for LLM training, though the exact AdamW betas and epsilon are not specified). Training runs on 4 A100 GPUs. The total number of online rollout-update iterations is 5, producing 5 distinct training batches (each with up to 512 trajectories, filtered to retain only those producing valid patch submissions).

**The constant learning rate choice.** Unlike SFT, which uses a cosine schedule decaying from $10^{-5}$ to $10^{-6}$, DAgger-style training uses a constant $3 \times 10^{-6}$. The paper does not explain this choice in detail, but it is consistent with the online nature of the training: each iteration trains on a fresh batch of data from the current policy, so there is no natural "end of training" at which the learning rate should decay to zero. A constant rate ensures that later iterations—which see more student-induced and therefore potentially noisier data—receive as much learning capacity as earlier iterations. The lower absolute value ($3 \times 10^{-6}$ vs. SFT's peak $10^{-5}$) likely reflects the on-policy nature of the data: since the training data changes each iteration, aggressive learning rates could cause the model to overfit to one batch before the next batch arrives, destabilizing the iterative improvement cycle.

**Data filtering.** After each mixed-policy rollout, trajectories that do not produce a valid final patch submission are filtered out before training. A "valid submission" means the trajectory ended with a finish action that included a patch or the trajectory's state contains a parsable diff of changes. Trajectories that crash, loop infinitely, overflow context, or never call the finish tool are excluded. The paper reports "effective training samples" in Figure 1, which counts the number of state–label pairs in the retained trajectories. The filtering rate is not explicitly reported, but the fact that SFT retains 684 successful trajectories from the full SWE-Gym training set (Appendix F) gives context: the teacher-only trajectories have a limited success rate (~29% of the 2,338 tasks produce passing patches), and mixed trajectories likely have a higher submission rate (since the teacher can complete trajectories the student abandons) but not necessarily a higher pass rate.

**Comparison to SFT configuration.** SFT trains on the 684 expert trajectories that pass the tests after rejection sampling, for 3 epochs with cosine learning rate schedule. The total number of training examples is fixed (684 trajectories × average trajectory length). DAgger-style and OPD train on 5 batches of up to 512 trajectories each, filtered to retain only those with valid submissions. The total effective sample counts are shown in Figure 1, reaching up to ~8,000 effective samples across all iterations. This means DAgger-style sees more total training examples than SFT, but each example is from a different state distribution (shifting from expert toward student-induced). The paper's scaling curves (Figure 1) show performance as a function of cumulative effective samples, allowing fair comparison of sample efficiency.

**Comparison to GRPO configuration.** GRPO trains on the 293-instance SkyRL-v0 subset with 8 trajectories per task (2,344 rollouts per pass, for 3 passes, totaling ~7,032 rollouts), using online batch size 32, learning rate $1 \times 10^{-6}$. The stark difference in performance (11.6% vs 27.3% on SWE-Bench Verified for 4B) despite comparable or larger data volume suggests that the problem is not sample count but signal quality: GRPO's outcome-level binary reward provides dramatically less information per sample than DAgger-style's turn-level teacher action sequences.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: A Unified Post-Training Framework That Reveals a Missing Design Point

The paper's most intellectually distinctive contribution is not the DAgger adaptation itself, but the **three-axis decomposition of post-training algorithms** (Section 3.3, Table 2) that exposes a specific, principled design point—mixture-induced states + expert labels + uniform weights—that none of the existing LLM post-training methods occupied. This framework is more than a taxonomy; it is a diagnostic tool that explains *why* existing methods fail in complementary ways and *where* to intervene.

Prior to this work, the field treated SFT, RLVR, and on-policy distillation as fundamentally different algorithm classes—imitation learning versus reinforcement learning versus knowledge distillation—with separate literatures, separate hyperparameters, and separate failure modes. The paper shows that these methods differ along only three axes: where states come from (`p_s`), where labels come from (`p_a`), and how samples are weighted (`w`). Under this decomposition, SFT is `(expert, expert, 1)`, RLVR is `(student, student, advantage)`, and OPD is `(student, student, log-ratio)`. The three design points each have exactly one axis that causes their failure: SFT has the wrong `p_s` (off-policy states), RLVR has the wrong `p_a` and `w` (student actions, sparse advantage), OPD has `p_a =` student actions (indirect teacher signal through importance weights) and suffers from cold-start quality of `p_s`.

The insight that **no existing method occupied `(mixture, expert, 1)`**—a point that combines on-policy state coverage with direct expert supervision through simple cross-entropy—is what motivates the entire paper. This is a genuine conceptual advance because it converts a confusing landscape of competing methods into a clean design space with an obviously underexplored region. The paper is essentially arguing: "the field has been sampling from three corners of this space while ignoring the fourth, and that fourth corner is the natural home for DAgger in the LLM-agent setting."

The significance of this framework extends well beyond this paper's experiments. It provides a **generative grammar** for post-training algorithm design: any new method can be characterized by its choices on these three axes, and any combination that hasn't been tried is a candidate for exploration. The paper does not explore, for instance, `(mixture, mixture, advantage)` or `(student, expert, log-ratio)`, but the framework makes it obvious that these are coherent design points worth investigating. This is a rare case where a theoretical contribution (the unified objective in Equation 14 and Appendix A) genuinely simpli es a messy empirical landscape rather than just retroactively justifying existing practice.

**Fundamental vs. incremental:** This is a fundamental reframing. The framework does not depend on the specific DAgger implementation or the SWE domain; it applies to any sequential decision-making task where a student policy can be trained with access to a teacher. It is the kind of contribution that, if widely adopted, would change how researchers describe and compare post-training methods—moving from "we used GRPO on SWE-Gym" to "our method sets `(p_s = ` trajectory-mixture, `p_a = ` teacher, `w = 1)`".

**Evidence tie-in:** Table 2 directly instantiates the framework for five methods, and the experimental results validate its explanatory power: SFT (expert states only) shows covariate shift rebound in Figure 2 (reverse KL rises from 0.10 to 0.126), while DAgger-style and AggreVaTe-style (mixture states) remain stable near 0.10; OPD (student actions with importance weights) shows cold-start lag at 3K samples in Figure 1 (9% vs. DAgger's 12% on SWE-Gym Holdout), while DAgger-style (expert labels) does not.

### Innovation 2: Teacher Interleaving as a Cold-Start Solution That On-Policy Distillation Overlooked

The second major contribution is the empirical demonstration that **interleaving teacher and student actions during trajectory collection** solves the cold-start problem that makes pure on-policy methods (OPD, RLVR) struggle with long-horizon tasks, and that this solution is both simpler and more effective than the importance-weighting machinery of OPD.

The conceptual move here is subtle but important. OPD's premise is that training on student-induced states is sufficient to x SFT's covariate shift, and that the teacher's role is to provide a *scoring function* (`w(s,a) = log(π_e/π_θ)`) on the student's own actions. This makes sense if you think of the teacher as a veri er—a source of evaluative feedback on student behavior. The DAgger perspective inverts this: the teacher's primary role is as a **demonstrator on student-induced states**, providing complete alternative actions rather than scalar feedback. The interleaving then ensures that the student *sees productive trajectory continuations* even when its own policy would have derailed, because the teacher periodically takes over and steers toward successful outcomes.

This inversion has a concrete consequence that the paper quanti es but does not explicitly name as a separate mechanism: **teacher interleaving functions as an implicit curriculum**. At iteration 1 with `β_1 = 1.0`, the student trains on pure expert trajectories—this is exactly SFT, providing a strong initialization. At iteration 2 with `β_2 = 0.8`, 20% of turns are student-induced; the student sees states it reaches after making its own (potentially wrong) decisions, but the teacher rescues 80% of the time, ensuring trajectories remain productive. By iteration 5 with `β_5 = 0.6`, 40% of turns are student-induced, and the model has been gradually exposed to its own error states while always receiving teacher corrections. OPD has no such curriculum: from iteration 1, 100% of states are student-induced, and a student with 11.2% base resolution rate generates trajectories that are 89% failure states, providing limited signal on the productive middle-and-late trajectory regions where task completion actually happens.

This is a **diagnostic insight** rather than a new algorithm. The paper is saying: OPD is not wrong to use on-policy states, but it is wrong to go fully on-policy *immediately*. The diagnosis is that cold-start fragility in OPD comes not from on-policy-ness per se, but from the absence of a mechanism to ensure that early on-policy rollouts reach productive states. Teacher interleaving is the natural x, and it happens to be exactly what DAgger provides.

**Comparison to prior work:** Lauffer et al. (2025), cited in Section 5, proposes on-policy expert corrections but uses a trajectory-level switch (student prefix, then expert completion), which is similar to the AggreVaTe-style variant. The paper's DAgger-style protocol with turn-level Bernoulli mixing is a re nement: the interleaving at individual turn granularity means teacher interventions can be brief and targeted (one turn of correction, then back to the student) rather than taking over the entire trajectory remainder. This fine granularity likely matters for the student to learn *recovery* from its own mistakes—a teacher takeover at turn 10 that continues through turn 50 provides a 40-turn demonstration, but if the student only needed one turn of correction, the remaining 39 turns are effectively off-policy (teacher-induced) states that don't help the student learn to continue autonomously after recovery.

**Fundamental vs. incremental:** This is incremental relative to the classical DAgger algorithm (which already proposed training on mixture-induced states) but fundamental relative to the LLM post-training literature (which had not recognized the cold-start problem as a consequence of pure on-policy sampling). The contribution is in identifying *why* the classical solution applies to the modern setting and demonstrating that it yields substantial empirical gains.

**Evidence tie-in:** Figure 1 shows that DAgger-style training outperforms OPD at every sample budget, with the gap largest at the earliest checkpoint (3K samples: 12% vs. 9% on SWE-Gym Holdout, 20% vs. 13% on SWE-Bench Verified-100). Figure 2 shows that DAgger-style achieves lower reverse KL than OPD at 3K samples (0.10 vs. 0.126), con rming that mixture rollouts reduce deployment-time policy divergence faster than pure on-policy rollouts.

### Innovation 3: Covariate Shift as a Quantitatively Measurable Phenomenon in LLM Agent Deployment

The paper's third contribution is methodological: it demonstrates that **covariate shift in LLM agent training is not just a theoretical concern but a measurable, quantitative phenomenon** that can be tracked via reverse KL divergence on student-induced contexts and correlated with downstream task performance degradation.

This is significant because the imitation learning literature has long warned about covariate shift (Ross et al., 2010; 2011; 2014), but the LLM agent community has largely treated it as an abstract theoretical property rather than an empirically veri able failure mode. The standard argument for why RL or OPD should outperform SFT is "covariate shift"—but without measurement, this is a just-so story. The paper's Figure 2 provides the measurement: SFT's reverse KL `D_KL(π_θ || π_e)` on student-visited contexts initially decreases from ~0.27 to ~0.10 as training proceeds, then **rebounds** to ~0.126, while DAgger-style and AggreVaTe-style methods remain stable near 0.10. This rebound is the empirical signature of covariate shift: the student learns to match the teacher on expert states (decreasing KL), but as it becomes a better imitator, it produces increasingly con dent deviations from the expert distribution on deployment states (increasing KL), because those deployment states are outside the training support.

The rebound pattern is non-obvious and diagnostically powerful. If one only measured KL on the training distribution (expert states), SFT would appear to converge monotonically—the student gets better and better at predicting expert actions on expert states. The paper's decision to measure KL on **student-visited contexts** (`c ∼ ˆd_{π_θ}`), i.e., states sampled by deploying the current student on held-out tasks, is what exposes the mismatch. This is a methodological contribution that future work can adopt: rather than arguing about covariate shift in the abstract, measure the policy divergence on deployment trajectories and track whether it diverges as training proceeds.

The qualitative failure analysis (Table 4) provides a complementary, behavior-level view of covariate shift consequences. The base model fails primarily through "wrong solution" (74.3% of submitted-but-unresolved failures), indicating a fundamental capability gap. SFT largely xes this (wrong solution drops to 55.8%), but introduces a new failure mode: 20.3% syntax/runtime errors, the highest among all trained methods. The paper does not explicitly connect this to covariate shift, but the pattern is consistent with it: SFT trains the student to produce correct syntax on expert-visited states, but when the student takes a wrong turn and reaches an off-distribution state, it lacks the supervision to recover coherently, and its subsequent actions contain syntax errors that the expert never demonstrated because the expert never reached those states. DAgger-style training, which provides teacher labels on student-induced error states, reduces syntax/runtime errors to 15.9%—the teacher demonstrated how to recover gracefully from exactly these situations.

**Comparison to prior work:** Prior LLM post-training papers (SWE-Gym, SkyRL, SWE-smith) report aggregate resolution rates and sometimes per-module performance, but none provide a direct measurement of policy divergence under deployment-time state distributions. The closest analogue is OPD work (Agarwal et al., 2024; Lu, 2025) which uses teacher-student KL as a training objective, but measures it as a loss value rather than as a diagnostic of covariate shift. This paper repurposes KL divergence from a training signal to a **evaluation metric**, which is a subtle but important shift in perspective.

**Fundamental vs. incremental:** This is a methodological contribution—not a new algorithm or theory, but a new way of measuring a known problem. Its signi cance lies in making covariate shift empirically tractable, which could in uence how future work evaluates post-training methods. If every post-training paper reported reverse KL on student-visited contexts alongside resolution rates, the community would have a much clearer picture of which methods genuinely x covariate shift versus which methods achieve gains through other mechanisms.

**Evidence tie-in:** Figure 2 and its discussion in Section 4.4 provide the direct measurement. Table 4's per-method failure mode distributions provide the behavioral correlate. Together, they establish that covariate shift is real, measurable, and behaviorally consequential in this domain.

### Innovation 4: The Flooring of the Teacher Mixture Parameter as an Implicit Recognition of Persistent On-Policy Brittleness

The paper makes an empirical design choice that carries a signi cant conceptual implication: the teacher mixing parameter `β_i` is decayed from 1.0 to 0.6 and **oored there**, never reaching zero. This means DAgger-style training never becomes fully on-policy—at least 60% of turns in every training trajectory are teacher-executed, even in the nal iteration.

This is a departure from classical DAgger (Ross et al., 2011), where `β_i` → 0 as training proceeds. In the classical setting (e.g., Atari games, simulated robotics), after enough iterations, the student policy becomes suf ciently competent that pure on-policy rollouts produce meaningful trajectories, and the expert is no longer needed. The paper's decision to oor at 0.6 re ects a recognition that **long-horizon software-engineering tasks never reach this regime**: even a well-trained student (the nal DAgger-style 4B model achieves 27.3% on SWE-Bench Verified) still fails on ~73% of tasks, and those failures are not random exploration but systematic derailments that produce uninformative trajectories if the teacher is absent.

This ooring is a **negative nding in disguise**: it signals that test-time compute and on-policy training cannot fully substitute for teacher guidance in long-horizon settings, at least at the scale studied. The paper does not frame it this way, but the implication is clear: if `β_i` were decayed to 0, performance would likely degrade because later training batches would consist of increasingly failed trajectories, reverting to OPD's cold-start problem. The persistence of teacher interventions as a necessity (not just a cold-start crutch) suggests that **the student model's autonomous exploration capability has a ceiling that is well below the task dif culty**, and that crossing this ceiling would require either a stronger base model (scaling pretraining, not just test-time techniques) or different training algorithms.

This connects to a broader tension in the paper: the method works best when the student has suf cient initial capability to reach productive states some of the time and when the teacher is substantially stronger. But if the student is too weak (base 8B model: 7.7% resolution rate), even DAgger-style training only brings it to 29.8%—still failing on 70% of tasks. The DAgger framework provides no mechanism for the student to *exceed* the teacher's capability; it can only approach it on the states the student tends to visit. The ooring at 0.6 is an empirical admission that the student never becomes competent enough to generate useful training data on its own, which is a fundamental limitation of the imitation learning paradigm applied to tasks where the student's autonomous success rate remains low even after training.

**Comparison to prior work:** Classical DAgger assumes the student eventually converges to near-expert performance, making `β_i` → 0 both safe and desirable. OPD implicitly assumes the student can generate useful rollouts from the start (no cold-start mechanism). This paper's ooring at 0.6 is a practical compromise that acknowledges neither assumption holds for current LLM agents on SWE tasks. It is a design choice that future work should either justify through ablation (what happens at `β_i = 0.4`? `0.2`? `0.0`?) or overcome through better base models or improved training procedures.

**Fundamental vs. incremental:** This is an empirical observation with theoretical implications. It does not advance theory per se, but it identi es a boundary condition on imitation learning for LLM agents that the classical theory did not anticipate: when the task horizon is long and the student's autonomous success rate remains low, pure on-policy training is counterproductive, and persistent teacher guidance is necessary. This is a useful negative result that constrains the applicability of purely on-policy methods.

**Evidence tie-in:** The `β_i` schedule is speci ed in Appendix F but never ablated in the main experiments. The paper does not report results for `β_i` decayed below 0.6, meaning this innovation is more of a **diagnostic observation about the method's design** than a validated empirical claim. Future work should explicitly test whether the oor is necessary or merely conservative. The fact that OPD (which is effectively `β_i = 0.0` for all iterations) underperforms DAgger-style (Figure 1) provides indirect evidence that pure on-policy rollouts are indeed harmful at this scale, but a direct ablation of `β_i` values would strengthen the argument considerably.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The training corpus is SWE-Gym [25], a collection of 2,338 real-world GitHub issue-resolution tasks with executable unit-test suites, operating under the OpenHands scaffold. In-domain evaluation uses a fixed 100-instance held-out split called SWE-Gym Holdout. Out-of-domain evaluation uses SWE-Bench Verified [10], a curated 500-instance subset of SWE-Bench with human-validated problem statements and evaluation tests; the paper reports results on 466 instances because 34 Matplotlib instances fail to build in the Docker environment. The paper also reports results on SWE-Bench Verified-100, a fixed 100-task subset used for scaling analysis that "closely tracks the full benchmark" (Section 4.3).

- **Base model(s).** The student policy is instantiated at two scales from the Qwen3 family [39]: Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 and Qwen3-8B. The teacher policy is fixed across all experiments as Qwen3-Coder-30B-A3B-Instruct [5], a substantially larger model with stronger coding capabilities. The 4B base model achieves 11.2% resolution rate on SWE-Bench Verified without post-training, providing a moderately capable starting point where test-time training can make a meaningful difference. The 8B base model achieves only 7.7%, which the paper attributes to differences in the base instruction-tuning rather than model quality per se.

- **Metrics.** The primary metric is **task-resolution rate** (%), defined as the fraction of instances for which the agent's submitted patch passes the hidden evaluation tests. This is a binary per-instance metric: either all tests pass (resolved) or they do not. SWE-Bench Verified uses the standard grading function from the benchmark. For the covariate shift analysis (Section 4.4), the paper additionally reports average token-level reverse KL divergence $D_{KL}(\pi_\theta \| \pi_e)$ computed on student-visited contexts from 100 sampled SWE-Gym instances, using temperature 0.7 during rollout.

- **Baselines.** The paper compares against three post-training methods trained from the same student initialization on SWE-Gym (or specified subsets):
  - **SFT** (Supervised Fine-Tuning / Behavior Cloning): Following the SWE-Gym training recipe [25], the teacher generates one trajectory per training task, and rejection sampling retains only trajectories whose final patches pass executable tests, yielding 684 successful trajectories. The student is trained for 3 epochs with cosine learning rate schedule ($10^{-5}$ to $10^{-6}$).
  - **GRPO** (Group Relative Policy Optimization): An RLVR baseline following the SkyRL-v0 setup [6, 47], trained on a 293-instance subset emphasizing tasks of moderate difficulty. Each task samples 8 trajectories for group-normalized advantage estimation, with 3 full passes over the subset, online batch size 32, and constant learning rate $1 \times 10^{-6}$.
  - **OPD** (On-Policy Distillation): Following Lu (2025) [22], the student collects trajectories under its own policy, and the teacher supplies token-level supervision on student-visited states through a reverse-KL distillation objective. OPD uses the same optimization budget as DAgger-style: 5 rollout-update iterations, 512 tasks per batch, constant learning rate $3 \times 10^{-6}$.
  - **Published SWE-agent systems** (lower block of Table 3): SWE-Gym-7B and SWE-Gym-32B [25], SkyRL-Agent-7B-v0 and SkyRL-Agent-8B-v0 [6], SWE-smith-LM-7B [41], R2E-Gym-7B-Agent and R2E-Gym-32B-Agent [50], and SWE-Dev-32B [33]. These differ in training data, scaffold, and initialization, so the comparison is contextual rather than controlled.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper measures training cost in **effective training samples**, defined as the number of state–label pairs retained after filtering out trajectories that do not produce valid patch submissions. Rollout collection uses 512 tasks per iteration, with 5 total iterations for DAgger-style, AggreVaTe-style, and OPD, yielding up to ~8,000 effective samples across all iterations (Figure 1). All methods use 4 A100 GPUs for training. The paper does not report inference FLOPs or wall-clock time, making latency comparisons impossible from the reported data. Generation uses temperature 0.7 and top-p = 0.9 for rollouts, greedy decoding for evaluation.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not employ cross-validation in the traditional sense. The 100-instance SWE-Gym Holdout split is fixed and used for in-domain evaluation across all methods. SWE-Bench Verified serves as a completely out-of-domain evaluation set (different repositories, different issues, no training overlap). For the scaling analysis (Figure 1), performance is tracked at multiple sample budgets during a single training run, with checkpoints evaluated on both SWE-Gym Holdout and SWE-Bench Verified-100. The paper does not report confidence intervals, error bars, or statistical significance tests for any results—an important limitation given the relatively small evaluation sets (100 and 466/500 instances).

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Task Resolution: DAgger-Style vs. Post-Training Baselines

Table 3 (upper block) reports the headline results under matched scaffold and training data. For the Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 student:

- **Base model:** 5.0% on SWE-Gym Holdout, 11.2% on SWE-Bench Verified.
- **GRPO:** 8.0% on SWE-Gym Holdout, 11.6% on SWE-Bench Verified. The gains are minimal (+3.0 and +0.4 points, respectively), consistent with the paper's argument that sparse outcome rewards provide weak learning signal over long horizons.
- **SFT:** 15.0% on SWE-Gym Holdout, 22.9% on SWE-Bench Verified. SFT provides substantial gains, more than doubling the base model's performance on SWE-Bench Verified.
- **OPD:** 16.0% on SWE-Gym Holdout, 23.4% on SWE-Bench Verified. OPD improves over SFT by +1.0 point on SWE-Gym Holdout and +0.5 points on SWE-Bench Verified—modest gains that validate OPD's premise (on-policy states help) but also illustrate its cold-start limitation (the gains are incremental rather than transformative).
- **DAgger-style (Ours):** 17.0% on SWE-Gym Holdout, **27.3%** on SWE-Bench Verified. This represents improvements over the strongest non-DAgger baseline (OPD) of +1.0 points on in-domain and **+3.9 points** on out-of-domain evaluation. The asymmetric gain—modest in-domain, substantial out-of-domain—is notable and suggests that mixture rollouts improve generalization beyond the training distribution, consistent with the covariate-shift mitigation argument.
- **AggreVaTe-style (Ours):** 16.0% on SWE-Gym Holdout, 24.5% on SWE-Bench Verified. Improvements over SFT of +1.0 and +1.6 points respectively, but trailing DAgger-style by -1.0 and -2.8 points.

For the Qwen3-8B student:

- **Base model:** 2.0% on SWE-Gym Holdout, 7.7% on SWE-Bench Verified. The base 8B model is substantially weaker than the base 4B model (7.7% vs. 11.2% on SWE-Bench Verified), which the paper does not fully explain but likely reflects differences in instruction-tuning quality or coding-specific pretraining.
- **GRPO:** 4.0% on SWE-Gym Holdout, 8.2% on SWE-Bench Verified. Again, minimal gains.
- **SFT:** 12.0% on SWE-Gym Holdout, 23.4% on SWE-Bench Verified. Similar to the 4B SFT result on SWE-Bench Verified (22.9%), suggesting SFT brings both model scales to comparable performance despite the 8B's weaker start.
- **OPD:** 16.0% on SWE-Gym Holdout, 26.2% on SWE-Bench Verified. Stronger in-domain gain over SFT for the 8B (+4.0 points vs. +1.0 for 4B), and a larger out-of-domain gain (+2.8 points vs. +0.5 for 4B). This suggests OPD may be more beneficial when the base model starts weaker, perhaps because the on-policy correction is more necessary.
- **DAgger-style (Ours):** 19.0% on SWE-Gym Holdout, **29.8%** on SWE-Bench Verified. Improvements over OPD of +3.0 points in-domain and **+3.6 points** out-of-domain.
- **AggreVaTe-style (Ours):** 17.0% on SWE-Gym Holdout, 27.3% on SWE-Bench Verified. Improvements over SFT of +5.0 and +3.9 points, trailing DAgger-style by -2.0 and -2.5 points.

**Cross-scale pattern:** The absolute gains from DAgger-style over OPD are consistent across scales (+3.9 and +3.6 points on SWE-Bench Verified for 4B and 8B), while the gains over SFT are larger at 8B (+6.4 points vs. +4.4 points for 4B on SWE-Bench Verified). This suggests the method is robust to model scale within the tested range, and potentially more beneficial when the base model has more room for improvement.

#### Comparison Against Published SWE-Agent Systems

Table 3 (lower block) contextualizes the DAgger-style results against systems from the broader literature. These comparisons are not controlled—systems differ in training data, scaffold, and initialization—but they establish that DAgger-style post-training enables surprisingly strong performance from small models.

For the 4B DAgger-style model (27.3% on SWE-Bench Verified):

- Outperforms SkyRL-Agent-8B-v0 (9.4%) by +17.9 points—a dramatic gap, though SkyRL uses only the 293-instance SkyRL-v0 subset for training.
- Outperforms SWE-Gym-7B (10.6%) by +16.7 points, despite SWE-Gym-7B being trained on the same SWE-Gym data with SFT—the improvement comes entirely from the post-training method.
- Outperforms SkyRL-Agent-7B-v0 (14.6%) by +12.7 points.
- Outperforms SWE-smith-LM-7B (15.2%) by +12.1 points, despite SWE-smith using a custom data generation pipeline.
- Outperforms R2E-Gym-7B-Agent (19.0%) by +8.3 points—R2E-Gym uses a synthetic training environment, so this is a cross-method comparison.
- Outperforms SWE-Gym-32B (20.6%) by +6.7 points—the 4B DAgger-style model outperforms a 32B model trained with SFT on the same data distribution.

For the 8B DAgger-style model (29.8% on SWE-Bench Verified):

- Outperforms SWE-Gym-32B (20.6%) by +9.2 points.
- Trails R2E-Gym-32B-Agent (34.4%) by -4.6 points and SWE-Dev-32B (36.6%) by -6.8 points. The gap to the strongest 32B systems suggests there are capability ceilings that post-training alone cannot overcome—the 8B student with DAgger-style training still cannot match a 32B model trained with more sophisticated methods (R2E-Gym uses synthetic environments; SWE-Dev uses specialized training and inference scaling).

**Interpretation:** The 4B DAgger-style model competes in the 8B–32B performance range, and the 8B DAgger-style model approaches 32B systems. This is the paper's strongest evidence that state-distribution correction can substitute for model scale, at least within the SWE domain and the Qwen model family. However, the comparison to published systems confounds multiple variables (training data quality, scaffold differences, model family), so the specific contribution of DAgger-style training versus other factors cannot be isolated from these numbers alone.

#### Sample Scaling and Training Stability

Figure 1 tracks resolution rate as a function of cumulative effective training samples on SWE-Gym Holdout (left panel) and SWE-Bench Verified-100 (right panel). The y-axis for SWE-Gym Holdout ranges from ~6% to ~17%; for SWE-Bench Verified-100, from ~12.5% to ~25%. Both panels exclude GRPO because it was trained on the 293-instance SkyRL-v0 subset and its effective sample budget is not directly comparable.

The key trends:

- **SFT converges quickly but saturates early.** On SWE-Gym Holdout, SFT reaches ~13% at ~2,000 samples and plateaus around 15% by ~5,000 samples. On SWE-Bench Verified-100, SFT peaks at 20% around 4,000–5,000 samples, then **declines** to 19% by 8,000 samples. The decline on out-of-domain data while in-domain performance holds steady is the classical signature of overfitting to the training distribution—consistent with covariate shift: the model becomes increasingly specialized to expert-induced states that do not generalize to deployment.

- **OPD starts slowly but catches up in-domain.** At 3,000 effective samples, OPD achieves only ~9% on SWE-Gym Holdout and ~13% on SWE-Bench Verified-100—substantially below DAgger-style (12% and 20%) and even below SFT on SWE-Bench Verified-100. By 6,000–8,000 samples, OPD reaches ~16% on SWE-Gym Holdout (matching DAgger-style) but remains behind on SWE-Bench Verified-100 (peaking near 22% vs. DAgger-style's 25%). This pattern—OPD eventually matching DAgger-style in-domain but not out-of-domain—suggests that pure on-policy rollouts bias the model toward the training distribution in a way that mixture rollouts do not. The cold-start gap at 3,000 samples is clear evidence supporting the paper's claim that early student-only rollouts are unproductive.

- **DAgger-style and AggreVaTe-style show earlier gains and steadier scaling.** At 3,000 effective samples, DAgger-style reaches 12% on SWE-Gym Holdout and 20% on SWE-Bench Verified-100—beating OPD by +3 and +7 points respectively. At 8,000 samples, DAgger-style reaches 17% and 26%, continuing to improve while SFT plateaus or declines. AggreVaTe-style follows a similar trajectory, slightly below DAgger-style on SWE-Bench Verified-100 (peaking near 23%) but comparable on SWE-Gym Holdout. Neither mixture-rollout method shows the decline pattern that SFT exhibits.

**The training stability interpretation:** The paper frames these results as demonstrating "more stable training" (Section 4.3). The evidence for stability is (a) monotonic improvement for DAgger-style and AggreVaTe-style versus SFT's decline on SWE-Bench Verified-100, and (b) smaller early-iteration gap versus OPD. However, "stability" in the sense of run-to-run variance or sensitivity to hyperparameters is not measured—the paper reports single training runs, and the curves in Figure 1 are smooth interpolations, not raw per-checkpoint measurements with error bars. The claim of stability should be understood as "does not exhibit the performance degradation pattern seen in SFT" rather than "has low variance across seeds."

#### Covariate Shift: Reverse KL Divergence Analysis

Figure 2 tracks average token-level reverse KL divergence, $\mathbb{E}_{c \sim \hat{d}_{\pi_\theta}}[D_{KL}(\pi_\theta(\cdot|c) \| \pi_e(\cdot|c))]$, on student-visited contexts for the 4B model, as a function of effective training samples. Lower reverse KL indicates the student's predictions remain closer to the teacher's on the states the student actually encounters during deployment.

The key trends:

- **SFT exhibits a V-shaped pattern.** Reverse KL starts near 0.27, drops to approximately 0.10 at around 3,000–4,000 samples, then rises to approximately 0.126 by 8,000 samples. This rebound—divergence increasing after initially decreasing—is the paper's direct quantitative evidence for covariate shift in SFT. The interpretation: early training brings the student closer to the teacher on the training distribution (expert states), causing KL to drop; but as training continues, the student learns behaviors that work on expert states but diverge on its own induced states, causing KL to rise again because the training distribution never covers those deployment states.

- **OPD starts higher and decreases slowly.** At 3,000 effective samples, OPD's reverse KL is approximately 0.126—substantially higher than DAgger-style's 0.10 and even higher than SFT's value at that point. By 8,000 samples, OPD converges to approximately 0.10, matching DAgger-style but after a much slower descent. The high early KL supports the cold-start argument: at 3,000 samples, the OPD student has been trained on its own rollouts for 3 iterations, but those rollouts are of poor quality, so the model has not yet converged to a policy that stays close to the teacher on deployment states. The eventual convergence to 0.10 at 8,000 samples shows that OPD does eventually achieve the same deployment-time alignment as DAgger-style, but requires more samples to get there.

- **DAgger-style and AggreVaTe-style converge early and stay flat.** Both methods drop from ~0.27 to ~0.10 by approximately 3,000–4,000 samples and remain there through 8,000 samples, showing **roughly a 20% reduction relative to SFT at the largest sample budget** (0.10 vs. 0.126). The flat trajectory after convergence—no rebound—indicates that mixture-rollout training avoids the covariate-shift-induced divergence that SFT exhibits. The fact that DAgger-style and AggreVaTe-style closely track each other suggests that both mixture protocols provide comparable state-distribution correction, even though their resolution rates differ (Table 3). The paper does not explore why similar KL divergence does not translate to similar task performance.

**Connecting Figure 2 to Figure 1:** The KL divergence analysis provides a mechanistic explanation for the performance patterns. SFT's KL rebound at large sample budgets coincides with its SWE-Bench Verified-100 decline (Figure 1, right panel): as the student diverges from the teacher on deployment states, its resolution rate drops. OPD's slow KL convergence coincides with its slow early performance gains: the model needs more samples to reduce its deployment-time divergence because early on-policy rollouts are low-quality. DAgger-style's early KL convergence and stability coincide with its early performance advantage and sustained improvement.

**Important measurement detail:** The KL divergence is computed on states visited by the student when deploying with temperature 0.7—the same temperature used for rollout collection. This is a realistic deployment setting (not greedy decoding, which would produce less diverse and potentially less divergent trajectories). However, it means the KL values depend on the sampling temperature, and the paper does not explore sensitivity to this choice.

#### Qualitative Failure Analysis

Table 4 decomposes unresolved trajectories on SWE-Bench Verified into fine-grained failure modes, using Claude Opus 4.7 as an LLM judge to assign one primary failure mode per trajectory. The analysis covers the Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 backbone and all post-training variants. Results are reported for all 466 evaluated instances.

The submission-level breakdown distinguishes between submitted-but-unresolved (the agent produced a patch that did not pass tests) and no-submission (the agent never produced a valid patch). Within submitted-but-unresolved, failures are categorized as: wrong solution (the patch is logically incorrect), wrong file (the patch modifies the wrong part of the codebase), or syntax/runtime error (the patch contains bugs that prevent execution). Within no-submission, failures include: tool-use error (the agent misuses the scaffold tools), repetitive loop (the agent cycles through the same actions without progress), context overflow (the trajectory exceeds the 64K token limit), budget exhaustion (the agent reaches 100 turns without finishing), and no edit (the agent never attempts a code change).

The key behavioral patterns:

- **Base model and GRPO fail primarily through non-submission.** The base model submits on only 52.1% of instances, with 47.9% producing no submission. Among submitted instances, 74.3% have wrong solutions. GRPO barely changes this pattern: 53.4% submission rate, 72.3% wrong solutions. These methods produce agents that often cannot formulate a coherent patch at all, and when they do, the patch is usually incorrect.

- **SFT learns to submit but produces brittle patches.** SFT dramatically increases the submission rate to 97.6%—the model reliably produces patches. However, among submitted-but-unresolved failures, SFT has the highest syntax/runtime error rate of all trained methods: 20.3%. The paper does not explicitly explain this, but the covariate-shift framework suggests a mechanism: SFT trains the model to write correct code on expert-induced states, but when the model takes a wrong turn and enters an off-distribution state, it lacks supervision for how to write syntactically valid code in that context, and produces buggy patches. SFT also shows the highest wrong-file rate (23.8%) among the trained methods, suggesting localization suffers under covariate shift.

- **OPD has a repetitive-loop problem.** OPD achieves the highest submission rate (98.5%), but among no-submission failures, 44.3% are repetitive loops—substantially higher than SFT (30.5%), DAgger-style (22.7%), and AggreVaTe-style (23.7%). The paper does not discuss this finding in detail, but it is consistent with the cold-start hypothesis: early OPD training on failed student rollouts may teach the model to repeat exploratory actions without making progress, because early rollouts contain many loops and the teacher's supervision on loop states may inadvertently reinforce the looping behavior.

- **DAgger-style reduces syntax/runtime errors and nearly eliminates tool-use errors.** DAgger-style achieves the highest resolution rate (27.3%), maintains a high submission rate (97.9%), and shows zero tool-use errors (0.0%)—the only method besides SFT to achieve this. Syntax/runtime errors drop to 15.9%, the lowest among the methods that submit reliably. Repetitive loops among no-submission failures are lower than SFT and OPD (22.7% vs. 30.5% and 44.3%), though higher than AggreVaTe-style (23.7% vs. 22.7%—essentially tied). The dominant failure mode for DAgger-style no-submission cases is context overflow (57.5%), indicating that the residual bottleneck is long-context capacity rather than failure to engage with the task.

- **AggreVaTe-style has the best file localization.** AggreVaTe-style achieves the lowest wrong-file rate among all methods (15.7%), lower than DAgger-style (19.7%), SFT (23.8%), and OPD (19.1%). The paper attributes this to longer teacher continuations improving repository localization (Section 4.5). However, AggreVaTe-style also has the highest context overflow rate among no-submission failures (60.1%), suggesting a trade-off: longer teacher completions provide better search guidance but consume more context, increasing the risk of overflow.

**What these patterns reveal about DAgger-style's mechanism:** The reduction in syntax/runtime errors (20.3% → 15.9%) relative to SFT is direct evidence that teacher interleaving provides supervision on error-recovery states—the student learns to write valid code even after taking wrong turns. The near-elimination of tool-use errors (3.8% in base, 0.0% in DAgger-style) suggests that mixture rollouts cover a wider range of tool-use states than expert-only trajectories, preventing the model from entering tool-use states it cannot handle. The persistence of context overflow as the dominant failure mode (57.5% for DAgger-style, vs. 55.1% for SFT) indicates that state-distribution correction does not solve the fundamentally architectural limitation of finite context windows.

**Judge reliability caveat:** The paper uses an LLM judge (Claude Opus 4.7) to assign failure modes. No inter-annotator agreement, human validation, or judge calibration is reported. The fine-grained categories (wrong solution vs. wrong file vs. syntax error) may have ambiguous boundaries—a patch that modifies the wrong file and also has a syntax error could be categorized either way. The paper does not discuss judge reliability, which is a limitation of this analysis.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Teacher mixture coefficient schedule (`β_i` from 1.0 to 0.6, floored):** The paper specifies this schedule (Appendix F) but does not ablate it—no experiments vary the decay rate, the floor value, or compare alternative schedules. The choice to floor at 0.6 rather than decaying to 0.0 is motivated by the cold-start argument but is never empirically tested against other values. This is a notable missing ablation: the paper's central mechanism (teacher interleaving) has only one design parameter, and its sensitivity to that parameter is unexplored.

**Turn-level (DAgger-style) vs. trajectory-level (AggreVaTe-style) mixing:** This is the paper's primary algorithmic comparison, effectively an ablation over the granularity of teacher interleaving. DAgger-style consistently outperforms AggreVaTe-style on resolution rate (Table 3: 27.3% vs. 24.5% at 4B, 29.8% vs. 27.3% at 8B on SWE-Bench Verified) and on scaling trends (Figure 1). However, AggreVaTe-style achieves better file localization (Table 4: 15.7% vs. 19.7% wrong-file rate). The paper interprets this as a granularity trade-off: fine-grained turn-level mixing provides more targeted error correction (reducing syntax/runtime errors and loops), while coarse-grained trajectory-level mixing with long teacher completions provides better search and localization behavior. This is not a formal ablation but a comparison of two protocol variants.

**Student model scale (4B vs. 8B):** This is a robustness check over model size. The DAgger-style gains over the strongest baseline hold at both scales (+3.9 points on SWE-Bench Verified at 4B, +3.6 points at 8B), as do the gains over SFT (+4.4 points at 4B, +6.4 points at 8B). The qualitative failure patterns are broadly consistent across scales. The paper does not test larger student models (e.g., 14B, 32B), so the scaling trend beyond 8B is unknown—it is possible that with a stronger base model, pure on-policy methods (OPD) would catch up or surpass DAgger-style because cold-start becomes less severe.

**Base model quality (4B-Instruct at 11.2% vs. 8B at 7.7%):** The 4B base model starts stronger than the 8B base model, likely due to differences in instruction-tuning. Despite the 8B's weaker start, SFT brings both to comparable performance (~23% on SWE-Bench Verified), and DAgger-style brings the 8B to 29.8% vs. the 4B's 27.3%. The paper does not explicitly control for base model quality, making it impossible to separate the effect of parameter count from the effect of instruction-tuning quality. This is an uncontrolled variable that complicates the cross-scale comparison.

**Data filtering (valid submission requirement):** Both DAgger-style and OPD filter out trajectories that do not produce valid patch submissions (Appendix F). SFT uses rejection sampling to keep only passing patches. The paper does not ablate the filtering threshold—what if all trajectories were kept, including those without submissions? What if only passing trajectories were kept, as in SFT? The filtering rate is not reported, so it is unclear how much data is discarded in each iteration. This is a significant missing detail because it affects the effective sample efficiency comparison: if DAgger-style retains a higher fraction of trajectories than OPD (because teacher interleaving produces more valid submissions), then the sample efficiency advantage may partly reflect better data yield rather than better per-sample learning.

**PRM/ORM choice (not applicable—the paper does not use process reward models):** All methods use raw task-resolution rate as the evaluation metric and cross-entropy or policy-gradient objectives for training, without learned verifiers or process reward models. This is a design choice that distinguishes the paper from the PRM-heavy test-time compute literature, but it also means the paper does not investigate whether adding verifier-guided selection at inference time would amplify or diminish the DAgger-style gains.

**Negative result: GRPO does not improve meaningfully over the base model.** Table 3 shows GRPO improving from 11.2% to 11.6% on SWE-Bench Verified for the 4B model and from 7.7% to 8.2% for the 8B model. These are near-zero gains despite substantial compute investment (3 full passes over the 293-instance SkyRL-v0 subset with 8 trajectories per task). The paper does not deeply analyze this negative result, but the implication is clear: RLVR with outcome-level rewards is fundamentally limited in long-horizon settings, and future RL-based SWE-agent training would need either denser rewards (process rewards, rubrics) or better credit assignment mechanisms to be competitive with imitation-based methods.

**Negative result: SFT's SWE-Bench Verified performance declines with continued training.** Figure 1 (right panel) shows SFT peaking at 20% on SWE-Bench Verified-100 around 4,000–5,000 effective samples, then declining to 19% by 8,000 samples, while SWE-Gym Holdout performance holds steady at 15%. This overfitting-to-distribution pattern is precisely what the covariate-shift theory predicts—and the fact that mixture-rollout methods do not exhibit this decline is strong evidence that DAgger-style training mitigates the problem as intended.

**Missing ablation: the floor value of `β_i = 0.6`.** The DAgger-style mixing schedule decays `β_i` from 1.0 by -0.2 per iteration to a floor of 0.6. The paper never tests lower floors (0.4, 0.2, 0.0) to determine whether further shifting toward on-policy states would improve or degrade performance. The floor at 0.6 is a conservative choice that maintains majority teacher control throughout training; testing lower values would reveal whether the student ever becomes competent enough to benefit from fully on-policy data, or whether the teacher guidance remains necessary even after 5 iterations.

**Missing ablation: number of rollout-update iterations.** The paper uses 5 iterations for DAgger-style, AggreVaTe-style, and OPD. It does not test whether more iterations would continue to yield gains or whether performance saturates. The scaling curves in Figure 1 suggest continued improvement from 3K to 8K effective samples, but it is unclear whether this corresponds to more iterations or larger batch sizes. Testing 10 or 15 iterations with the same `β_i` floor would reveal whether the method plateaus or continues improving.

**Missing ablation: batch size per iteration.** The paper uses 512 tasks per rollout batch. No experiments vary this number (e.g., 256 or 1024) to assess whether the mixture-rollout benefits depend on batch diversity. Since teacher interleaving aims to cover a wide range of student-induced states, batch size could matter: too few tasks might not provide enough state diversity for the mixture distribution to be effective.

**Missing comparison: SFT initialized, then OPD or DAgger-style.** The paper trains DAgger-style from scratch starting with the base model. An alternative would be to initialize with the SFT-trained model (which already achieves 22.9% on SWE-Bench Verified) and then apply DAgger-style mixing. This would test whether mixture rollouts provide gains beyond what SFT already captures, or whether the DAgger-style advantage is primarily in the early training phase. The cold-start argument suggests DAgger-style should still help on top of an SFT initialization by correcting the residual covariate shift, but this experiment is not run.

### Critical Assessment

#### Does DAgger-style training achieve +3.9 points at 4B and +3.6 points at 8B over the strongest post-training baseline?

**Yes, but the choice of "strongest baseline" matters.** Table 3 shows DAgger-style at 27.3% vs. OPD at 23.4% for 4B (+3.9 points) and 29.8% vs. 26.2% for 8B (+3.6 points). These numbers come from matched experiments: same initialization, same scaffold, same training data (SWE-Gym), same optimization budget (5 iterations, 512 tasks/batch). The comparison is fair and the gap is substantial.

However, the "strongest baseline" is context-dependent. SFT achieves 22.9% (4B) and 23.4% (8B)—the gap to SFT is +4.4 and +6.4 points respectively, which is even larger. The paper frames OPD as the strongest baseline because OPD outperforms SFT on average, but the gap between OPD and SFT is modest (+0.5 points at 4B, +2.8 points at 8B on SWE-Bench Verified), meaning the choice of baseline does not dramatically change the reported improvement. The more important framing is that DAgger-style meaningfully improves over *both* SFT and OPD, with the gains over OPD validating the cold-start mitigation argument (since OPD already uses on-policy states, the remaining gap must be attributed to teacher interleaving and expert labels).

A limitation: the paper does not compare against an ensemble or combination of baselines—for example, initializing from the SFT checkpoint and then running OPD, or training DAgger-style with the SFT model as initialization. Such a comparison would isolate whether DAgger-style's advantage comes from better initialization (the `β_1 = 1.0` pure-expert first iteration) or from the continued teacher interleaving in later iterations. The cold-start argument predicts that initialization matters; the covariate-shift argument predicts that continued interleaving matters. The paper's design confounds these two effects.

#### Does DAgger-style training enable small models to surpass significantly larger models?

**Conditionally yes, with important caveats.** The 4B DAgger-style model (27.3%) outperforms SWE-Gym-32B (20.6%) by +6.7 points—a 4B model beating a 32B model on the same benchmark. The 8B DAgger-style model (29.8%) similarly outperforms SWE-Gym-32B. This is genuine evidence that post-training method choice can compensate for a factor of ~4–8× in parameter count.

However, the comparison confounds multiple variables beyond model scale. SWE-Gym-32B was trained with SFT only (the SWE-Gym paper's standard recipe), not with OPD or any other advanced post-training method. It is possible—even likely—that applying DAgger-style training to a 32B base model would yield even stronger results. The paper's claim is not "DAgger-style beats any 32B system" but rather "DAgger-style enables a 4B/8B system to beat a representative 32B system trained with SFT." This is a meaningful but narrower claim.

Additionally, the base model comparison is confounded by instruction-tuning quality. The 4B model starts at 11.2% on SWE-Bench Verified while the 8B starts at 7.7%—the 4B is a better coding model out of the box, likely due to training data differences rather than architecture. The 8B DAgger-style model reaches 29.8%, still trailing R2E-Gym-32B-Agent (34.4%) and SWE-Dev-32B (36.6%). So while DAgger-style narrows the gap to 32B systems, it does not close it—there remains a ~5–7 point gap to the strongest 32B agents, which use specialized training environments (R2E-Gym) or training-and-inference scaling (SWE-Dev) that go beyond what DAgger-style alone provides.

#### Does DAgger-style training mitigate covariate shift?

**Yes, with direct quantitative evidence.** Figure 2 provides the strongest evidence: SFT's reverse KL exhibits a V-shaped rebound pattern, while DAgger-style and AggreVaTe-style converge to a lower value (~0.10) and stay flat. This is a clean, interpretable measurement of covariate shift and its mitigation. The 20% reduction in deployment-time KL divergence relative to SFT at 8,000 samples (~0.10 vs. ~0.126) is meaningful.

However, the paper does not demonstrate a causal link between KL reduction and resolution-rate improvement. The two metrics are correlated (lower KL coincides with higher resolution rate), but the paper does not show that the KL reduction *causes* the performance gain. It is possible that both are consequences of a third factor—for instance, the mixture-rollout data might simply be more diverse or higher-quality in ways that improve both KL and resolution rate through independent mechanisms. A causal analysis (e.g., showing that within a single method, checkpoints with lower KL have higher resolution rate, or that explicitly optimizing for lower KL improves resolution rate) would strengthen this claim.

Additionally, the KL measurement uses temperature 0.7 rollouts on only 100 SWE-Gym instances. The small sample (100 instances × 1 trajectory each = 100 trajectories) means the KL estimate has non-trivial variance. The paper does not report confidence intervals, so the statistical reliability of the 20% reduction is unclear. A larger sample or multiple rollouts per instance would make the measurement more robust.

#### Does DAgger-style training provide "cold-start robustness"?

**Partially supported.** The paper's cold-start argument has two components: (1) that OPD suffers from cold-start failures (early student rollouts are unproductive), and (2) that DAgger-style's teacher interleaving avoids this problem. The evidence for (1) is: OPD's lower early performance in Figure 1 (9% vs. 12% for DAgger-style at 3K samples on SWE-Gym Holdout; 13% vs. 20% on SWE-Bench Verified-100), and OPD's higher early KL divergence in Figure 2 (0.126 vs. 0.10 at 3K samples). The evidence for (2) is: DAgger-style's higher early performance and lower early KL. This is correlational support.

What is missing is a direct demonstration of the mechanism: an analysis showing that early OPD rollouts indeed contain more early-failure states (e.g., shorter average trajectory length, lower rate of reaching tool interactions, higher rate of early termination) compared to DAgger-style rollouts. The paper never characterizes the content of the collected trajectories—it reports only the downstream effects on policy divergence and resolution rate. Showing that DAgger-style rollouts at iteration 1–2 are longer, reach more diverse states, or have higher teacher-action quality than OPD rollouts would make the cold-start argument empirically grounded rather than inferential.

#### Does DAgger-style training improve long-horizon behaviors (search, editing, recovery)?

**Partially supported by the qualitative failure analysis.** Table 4 shows that DAgger-style training reduces syntax/runtime errors (from SFT's 20.3% to 15.9%), reduces wrong-file errors (from SFT's 23.8% to 19.7%), and shifts no-submission failures away from tool-use errors and repetitive loops toward context overflow. These are behavioral changes consistent with improved long-horizon capabilities: the model makes fewer execution-level mistakes and gets further in trajectories before failing.

However, the paper does not provide trajectory-level behavioral metrics (e.g., average number of turns before first error, frequency of recovery actions after errors, distribution of trajectory lengths for successful vs. unsuccessful runs). The LLM-judge failure categorization is a coarse summary that collapses rich trajectory dynamics into a single label. A more detailed analysis—for instance, measuring how often the agent successfully recovers from a wrong file edit, or how often teacher interleaving prevents an early mistake from cascading—would provide stronger evidence for the specific mechanism the paper claims.

Additionally, the judge-based categorization introduces a reliability concern: Claude Opus 4.7 may have systematic biases in how it categorizes failures (e.g., it may be more likely to label certain agent behaviors as "wrong solution" vs. "wrong file" depending on superficial features of the patch). Without human validation or inter-judge agreement metrics, the fine-grained categories should be treated as suggestive rather than definitive.

#### Missing experiments that would strengthen the paper

1. **Ablation over `β_i` schedule.** Testing floors of 0.4, 0.2, 0.0 would reveal whether DAgger-style benefits from persistent teacher guidance or whether it could eventually transition to fully on-policy training. This is the single most important missing experiment because it tests the core design choice.

2. **SFT → DAgger-style transfer.** Initializing DAgger-style training from the SFT checkpoint would separate the cold-start benefit (early iteration with `β_1 = 1.0`) from the ongoing state-distribution correction benefit. If DAgger-style still improves over SFT initialization, the covariate-shift mitigation is validated independently of cold start.

3. **Direct trajectory characterization.** Reporting statistics on the collected trajectories—average length, turn at which first teacher intervention occurs, fraction of states where student and teacher disagree, diversity of visited states—would make the mechanism concrete rather than inferential.

4. **Evaluation on a non-SWE domain.** The paper's claims about DAgger for "multi-turn LM agents" are general, but all experiments are on software engineering. Testing on web navigation, data analysis, or another long-horizon agentic domain would establish domain generality.

5. **Multiple random seeds.** The paper reports single training runs. Without error bars or seed variance, it is impossible to assess whether the +3.9 and +3.6 point gains are statistically reliable or within the noise range of training variance. Given the relatively small evaluation sets (100 and 466 instances), seed variance could be substantial.

6. **Teacher quality ablation.** The method relies on a substantially stronger teacher (Qwen3-Coder-30B-A3B-Instruct). Testing with a weaker teacher (e.g., a 14B model) or a teacher from a different family would reveal how dependent the gains are on teacher quality. If DAgger-style requires a ~4× larger teacher, its practical applicability is more constrained than the paper suggests.

7. **Larger-scale experiments.** Testing at 14B or 32B student scale would reveal whether the relative gains from DAgger-style diminish as the base model becomes stronger (cold-start becomes less severe) or persist (covariate shift remains a problem regardless of scale).

#### Overall assessment

The paper's experimental evidence supports its core narrative—DAgger-style training improves over SFT, GRPO, and OPD on SWE tasks—but the evidence is narrower than the paper's framing suggests. The gains are demonstrated on a single domain (software engineering), with a single model family (Qwen3), at two scales (4B and 8B), using a single teacher (Qwen3-Coder-30B), and evaluated on benchmarks that, while standard, have relatively small instance counts (100 and 466). The quantitative evidence for covariate-shift mitigation (Figure 2) is clean and interpretable, but the causal link to resolution-rate improvement is correlational. The cold-start argument is inferential rather than mechanically demonstrated. The missing ablations—especially over `β_i` schedule and SFT-initialized DAgger-style—leave important questions about the method's operating principles unanswered.

The strongest empirical claims—that a 4B DAgger-style model surpasses published 8B systems, and that an 8B DAgger-style model approaches 32B systems—are supported but confounded by cross-system differences in training data, scaffold, and base model quality. These comparisons establish that DAgger-style post-training produces competitive agents, but they do not isolate the contribution of the method from other factors.

In sum, the experiments demonstrate that teacher-interleaved rollout collection is a practically effective post-training strategy for SWE agents at the tested scales, and the covariate-shift measurement provides a plausible mechanism. The paper would benefit from more systematic ablations, broader domain testing, and statistical rigor, but the core empirical findings are internally consistent and support the paper's central argument.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Difficulty Estimation Cost Is Unaccounted For in Headline Efficiency Gains

The paper's central DAgger protocol requires collecting teacher-labeled data on states visited by the student at each iteration, which in turn requires running a substantially larger teacher model (Qwen3-Coder-30B-A3B-Instruct, ~30B active parameters with 3B activated via mixture-of-experts) on **every turn of every trajectory** in the rollout batch—512 tasks × up to 100 turns per trajectory × 5 iterations, for a maximum of ~256,000 teacher forward passes across training. Additionally, the teacher is queried at all visited states **regardless of whether the teacher or student executed the action** (Algorithm 1, line 5: "Query teacher label `ã_t ~ π_e(· | s_t)`"), meaning that even when the student acts, the teacher processes the same state to produce a label. This effectively doubles the per-state inference cost for student-induced turns: one student forward pass to generate the action, plus one teacher forward pass to generate the label.

The paper does not account for this cost in any budget calculation. The headline comparisons in Table 3, Figure 1, and Figure 2 are all presented in terms of effective training samples (state–label pairs retained after filtering invalid submissions), not total FLOPs or inference cost. The teacher's inference cost is not subtracted from the student's training budget or amortized across performance improvements. To put numbers on the asymmetry: the student is a 4B or 8B parameter dense model, while the teacher is a 30B-parameter mixture-of-experts model—the teacher is approximately an order of magnitude more expensive per forward pass. Yet the paper reports that DAgger-style training uses "the same optimization budget" as SFT and OPD (Section 4.1), counting only student training FLOPs and student rollout generation, not teacher labeling.

The consequence is that the reported +3.9 and +3.6 point gains over OPD at 4B and 8B scale respectively (Table 3) are **upper bounds on achievable efficiency** from the student's perspective but do not reflect the total compute cost of the training procedure. A practitioner comparing DAgger-style to SFT would need to weigh whether the +4.4 point improvement (at 4B) justifies roughly 2× the inference FLOPs (student + teacher vs. student-only or precomputed expert trajectories). The paper demonstrates performance gains but does not provide the cost–benefit analysis that a deployment decision requires.

The paper partially acknowledges the teacher cost issue in Section 5 (Related Work), citing Lauffer et al. (2025) whose trajectory-level switch approach is similar to AggreVaTe-style and noting that DAgger-style uses "turn-level teacher-student mixing" which "reduces reliance on long expert completions"—but this acknowledges only the trajectory-level teacher computation, not the per-state teacher labeling cost. Appendix B (Limitations) states that "its effectiveness may depend on teacher quality, availability, and inference cost," but this is a general caution, not a quantitative accounting.

No ablation or analysis studies the sensitivity of DAgger-style performance to teacher model size or cost. The paper does not test whether a smaller (cheaper) teacher would preserve the gains, or whether teacher labeling can be subsampled (e.g., labeling only student-induced states and not teacher-induced states) without degrading performance. The teacher cost therefore represents an **unquantified practical overhead** that could make the method less attractive in resource-constrained settings.

---

### All Results Are on a Single Domain, a Single Model Family, and a Single Teacher

The paper's entire empirical validation is conducted on software-engineering agent tasks (SWE-Gym for training, SWE-Bench Verified for out-of-domain evaluation) using Qwen3 student models (4B-Instruct-2507 and 8B) and a single Qwen3-Coder-30B-A3B-Instruct teacher (Section 4.1). The authors explicitly acknowledge this in Appendix B:

> "our experiments focus on software-engineering agents under the OpenHands scaffold, and further work is needed to validate whether the same gains transfer to other long-horizon agentic domains such as web navigation, data analysis, or scientific computing."

This is a significant limitation because the DAgger principle (train on mixture-induced states with expert labels) makes no domain-specific assumptions—it should apply to any sequential decision-making task with a teacher policy. But the empirical demonstration is confined to a single task family where (a) trajectories are particularly long (20–100 turns), (b) the action space involves code editing with immediate environment feedback, (c) errors are highly path-dependent (a wrong grep search can permanently derail a trajectory), and (d) the verifiable reward signal (unit tests) is sparse and binary. These domain characteristics may make DAgger especially beneficial in SWE relative to other domains where trajectories are shorter, errors are more recoverable, or reward signals are denser.

The model-family confinement is equally concerning. The Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 base model starts at 11.2% on SWE-Bench Verified, while the Qwen3-8B base model starts at only 7.7% (Table 3). The paper attributes this to "differences in the base instruction-tuning rather than model quality per se" but does not control for it. Both models share the same pretraining corpus, tokenizer, and architecture family. It is unknown whether the DAgger-style gains would replicate on a different model family (e.g., Llama, DeepSeek, or Gemma) with different pretraining data, different tool-use capabilities, or different sensitivity to distribution shift. The single teacher model (Qwen3-Coder-30B-A3B-Instruct) further narrows the evidence: the gains may depend on the specific teacher's coding expertise, its calibration on SWE tasks, or its compatibility with the Qwen3 student family (shared tokenizer, similar pretraining distribution).

The consequence is that the paper's claims about DAgger as a general post-training recipe for "multi-turn LM agents" (title, Section 1) are **extrapolations from a single data point**. A practitioner working in web navigation, where trajectories involve DOM interactions rather than code editing and where the verifiable reward is whether a purchase was completed or a form was submitted, cannot assume that DAgger-style mixing will yield +3–4 point gains. The method might transfer, or it might not; the paper provides no evidence either way.

No cross-domain or cross-model-family experiments are attempted, and the paper does not propose a theoretical framework for predicting when DAgger will be most beneficial (e.g., as a function of trajectory length, action space complexity, or base model competence). The generalizability of the findings is therefore **entirely untested**.

---

### The Method Requires a Substantially Stronger Teacher Policy That Provides Turn-Level Labels

DAgger-style training fundamentally depends on access to a teacher policy that is (a) available at training time to provide action labels on every visited state, and (b) substantially more capable than the student on the target task. The paper's experiments use a teacher (Qwen3-Coder-30B-A3B-Instruct) that is ~8× larger than the 4B student in total parameters and ~4× larger than the 8B student, and is a code-specialized model (Appendix B acknowledges the requirement: "our method relies on a stronger teacher policy to provide action labels on visited states").

The implication is that DAgger-style training is **inapplicable in settings where no stronger teacher exists**—for example, when training a state-of-the-art model to push the frontier on a new task, or when the available teacher is only marginally better than the student, or when the teacher is a black-box API that charges per token and cannot be queried hundreds of thousands of times. The paper's comparison in Table 1 explicitly markets compatibility with black-box teachers as an advantage of DAgger over OPD ("Compatibility with Black-Box Teacher: DAgger ✓, OPD ✗"), but this is misleading: while DAgger does not require teacher logits (unlike OPD, which needs `π_e(a|s)` probabilities), it does require generating complete teacher action sequences at every state, which still requires API access and per-token generation costs. A GPT-4 or Gemini teacher queried on 256,000 states for a training run would incur substantial financial cost that the paper does not estimate.

Furthermore, the paper never ablates teacher quality. It does not test whether a smaller teacher (e.g., a 14B model) would provide sufficient supervision, or whether the teacher needs to be a code-specialized model, or whether a teacher from a different model family would work (e.g., using DeepSeek-Coder as the teacher for a Qwen3 student). The possibility that the gains are specific to the Qwen3 teacher–student pair—which may share similar pretraining distributions, tokenization, and coding style—cannot be ruled out. If a practitioner does not have access to a teacher that is both substantially stronger and architecturally compatible with their student model, the paper provides no guidance on whether DAgger-style training would still be beneficial or how much teacher quality matters.

The consequence is that the method's applicability is **gated by teacher availability**, and the paper understates this constraint. The "cold-start robustness" advantage over OPD (Table 1) is only realized if a stronger teacher exists to provide the initial `β_1 = 1.0` expert trajectories and the ongoing teacher interventions. Without such a teacher, the method reduces to pure on-policy rollouts—which is exactly OPD, and the paper shows OPD underperforms DAgger-style. The method's headline gains thus depend on a resource (a much stronger teacher) whose availability the paper does not treat as a limitation, but which practitioners will immediately recognize as one.

### The Teacher Mixing Schedule Is Not Ablated, Leaving the Core Mechanism Unexplored

The DAgger-style protocol has exactly one critical hyperparameter that controls the tradeoff between on-policy state coverage and teacher guidance: the mixing coefficient `β_i` and its annealing schedule. The paper specifies `β_i = max(0.6, 1.0 - 0.2(i-1))` (Appendix F), which decays from 1.0 to 0.6 in steps of 0.2 over 5 iterations and then floors at 0.6. This choice means that **at least 60% of all turns across all training iterations are teacher-executed**, and the student never trains on a batch where more than 40% of states are student-induced.

The paper provides no empirical justification for this specific schedule and performs **no ablation** over alternative choices. It does not test:
- Lower floor values (0.4, 0.2, 0.0) to assess whether further on-policy shifting improves or degrades performance.
- Slower decay rates (e.g., staying at `β = 1.0` for more iterations before decaying) to assess whether extended expert-only pretraining helps.
- Faster decay rates (e.g., dropping to 0.6 in 2 iterations and staying there) to assess whether rapid on-policy shifting is beneficial.
- Different floor values combined with more iterations (e.g., `β` decaying to 0.2 over 10 iterations) to assess whether eventual full on-policy training becomes viable with more gradual shifting.
- Task- or difficulty-adaptive mixing (e.g., using higher `β` for harder tasks), analogous to the difficulty-conditioned allocation in compute-optimal test-time scaling.

The consequence is that the paper cannot answer the most natural follow-up question: **does the 40% student-turn ceiling represent a genuine requirement for stable training, or is it a conservatively chosen hyperparameter that leaves performance on the table?** If `β_i` could be decayed further (to 0.4 or 0.2) without degradation, the method would achieve more on-policy state coverage and potentially larger gains. If lower `β` values cause training collapse (analogous to OPD's cold-start problem), the paper would have identified a principled boundary on how on-policy DAgger can be in this setting. Without this ablation, the floor at 0.6 is an unexplained design choice that future practitioners cannot reason about when adapting the method to new domains or model scales.

Additionally, the `β_i` schedule is the primary knob controlling the exploration–exploitation tradeoff in DAgger-style training: higher `β` means more exploitation of teacher guidance (safe trajectories, dense supervision) but less exploration of student-induced states (the states where covariate shift actually matters). The paper does not analyze this tradeoff quantitatively—for example, by measuring how the diversity of visited states or the rate of student errors in collected trajectories varies with `β`. Without such analysis, the paper provides a recipe (`β_i` from 1.0 to 0.6, floored) but not an understanding of the recipe's sensitivity or generalizability.

### Test Sets Are Small and Statistical Reliability Is Unreported

The paper evaluates on two test sets: SWE-Gym Holdout (100 instances) and SWE-Bench Verified (466 instances after excluding 34 that fail to build in the Docker environment, as noted in the footnote to Table 3). The scaling analysis in Figure 1 further uses SWE-Bench Verified-100, a fixed 100-task subset. All main results (Table 3, Figures 1–2) are reported as point estimates without confidence intervals, error bars, standard deviations, or statistical significance tests. The paper runs single training runs for each method and scale; no seed variance is reported.

With a test set of 100 instances, **a single-instance resolution or failure shifts the reported resolution rate by 1 percentage point**. The headline +3.9 point gain for DAgger-style over OPD at 4B (27.3% vs. 23.4% on SWE-Bench Verified) corresponds to a difference of approximately 18 instances being resolved—a meaningful absolute gap, but one whose statistical significance depends on the variance of the resolution rate under resampling or across seeds. The +1.0 point in-domain gain on SWE-Gym Holdout (17.0% vs. 16.0%) corresponds to a single-instance difference on the 100-instance set—this could easily be noise.

The paper's within-method trends (e.g., SFT declining from 20% to 19% on SWE-Bench Verified-100 in Figure 1) are also single-percentage-point movements that could reflect sampling noise rather than genuine overfitting, especially since the subset is only 100 instances. Without error estimates, the reader cannot distinguish between a robust trend and a random fluctuation.

The consequence is that **the precision of the reported gains is unknown**. A practitioner comparing DAgger-style to OPD or SFT cannot assess whether the +3.9 point improvement is reliably achievable or whether it might vary by ±2–3 points depending on the specific test set split, training seed, or data ordering. This is especially problematic for smaller-scale results like the SWE-Gym Holdout numbers (where the gap between DAgger-style and OPD is only 1 point) and the fine-grained failure analysis in Table 4 (where failure mode percentages sum over unresolved instances, which for some categories represent only a handful of trajectories). For example, DAgger-style's "wrong solution" rate of 64.4% is reported to one decimal place but is based on the subset of instances that were both unresolved and submitted, which is approximately 127 instances (466 × (1 - 0.273) × 0.979 ≈ 127), so each failure mode percentage point represents roughly 1.3 trajectories.

The paper does not discuss statistical methodology beyond describing the evaluation protocol (Section 4.1). The SWE-agent literature more broadly has not converged on standard practices for statistical reporting, but the small test sets make this gap more consequential than in domains with larger evaluation corpora.

---

### Hard Instances Remain Essentially Unsolved, and the Method Does Not Address Capability Gaps

Across all methods in the paper, the absolute resolution rates on SWE-Bench Verified remain low: the best method (DAgger-style, 8B) achieves 29.8%, meaning **more than 70% of tasks are not resolved**. The base 4B model resolves 11.2%, so the +16.1 point gain from DAgger-style training is substantial in relative terms, but the absolute ceiling is low. The paper does not provide a difficulty-stratified analysis (unlike the compute-optimal test-time scaling work in the reference example, which bins problems into quintiles), so it is impossible to determine whether the unresolved 70% are uniformly hard, whether there is a subset of tasks on which no method makes progress, or whether certain task characteristics (repository size, issue complexity, number of files to modify) are predictive of failure.

The qualitative failure analysis (Table 4) provides suggestive evidence: the dominant failure mode for DAgger-style no-submission cases is context overflow (57.5%), and among submitted-but-unresolved cases, wrong solution dominates (64.4%). This pattern—either the agent can't fit the task in its context window, or it produces a plausible-looking but incorrect patch—suggests that the residual failures are not merely problems of state-distribution mismatch that more DAgger iterations could fix, but rather **fundamental capability limitations**: insufficient context length to handle large codebases, and insufficient reasoning depth to identify the correct edit among many plausible candidates.

This limitation is important because the paper's framing (Section 1) positions DAgger as resolving the dilemma between SFT's covariate shift and RLVR's sparse rewards, implying that the method addresses the key bottleneck in LLM agent post-training. But if 70% of failures persist after DAgger-style training, the method has not addressed the dominant failure modes—it has only improved performance on the ~16% of tasks that were amenable to improvement through state-distribution correction. The remaining failures may require fundamentally different interventions: larger context windows (architectural change), better retrieval mechanisms (system-level change), or more capable base models (pretraining scaling). The paper acknowledges this obliquely in Appendix B:

> "the remaining errors are increasingly dominated by long-context limitations such as context overflow, indicating that improvements in memory, retrieval, or context management may be necessary for further gains."

The consequence is that **DAgger-style training is a partial solution that improves performance within the base model's capability envelope but does not expand that envelope**. A practitioner whose base model resolves 11% of tasks can expect to reach ~27% with DAgger-style training—still failing on nearly three-quarters of tasks. Whether this level of performance is useful depends on the deployment context, but the paper's narrative of resolving a fundamental post-training dilemma should be tempered by the observation that the dilemma's resolution leaves the majority of tasks unresolved. This is not a failure of the method per se—no post-training method can create capability ex nihilo—but it is a limitation of the paper's scope that constrains the practical impact of the contribution.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper introduces a **unified design-space framework** for post-training algorithms that reframes what had been treated as fundamentally different approaches—SFT, RLVR, and on-policy distillation—as points in a three-dimensional space defined by context distribution, label distribution, and sample weighting (Section 3.3, Table 2). This is a **reframing contribution** rather than a paradigm shift: it does not introduce new training objectives or architectures, but it provides a lens through which the field's existing methods become comparable, diagnosable, and combinable. The framework's power lies in revealing that a specific design point—mixture-induced states + expert labels + uniform weights—was unoccupied in the LLM agent post-training literature despite being the natural home for DAgger's classical insight that imitation learners should train on their own induced state distribution.

The practical consequence of this reframing is a concrete recipe that **combines the strengths of SFT and on-policy methods while avoiding their characteristic failures**. SFT fails because it trains on `d_{π_e}` (expert states) while deploying on `d_{π_θ}` (student states); OPD fixes the state distribution but suffers from cold-start because `d_{π_θ}` at iteration 1 is induced by a weak policy with ~11% success rate; RLVR fixes the state distribution but provides only sparse outcome-level feedback. DAgger-style training occupies the design point that solves all three: `p_s` interpolates from `d_{π_e}` toward `d_{π_θ}` through the annealing parameter `β_i`, `p_a` is always `π_e` (direct expert supervision), and `w ≡ 1` (simple, stable learning). The gains over OPD (+3.9 points at 4B, +3.6 points at 8B on SWE-Bench Verified) and over SFT (+4.4 and +6.4 points respectively) validate that this design point is not merely theoretically elegant but empirically superior.

The paper also introduces a **measurable diagnostic for covariate shift** in LLM agent training: reverse KL divergence `D_{KL}(π_θ || π_e)` computed on student-visited deployment contexts rather than on the training distribution (Section 4.4, Figure 2). Prior work invoked covariate shift as a theoretical justification for on-policy methods without measuring it. The paper demonstrates that SFT exhibits the classical signature—KL initially decreases to ~0.10 as the student learns to imitate the expert on training states, then **rebounds** to ~0.126 as training continues because the learned policy encounters deployment states outside its training support. DAgger-style and AggreVaTe-style methods do not exhibit this rebound, remaining stable near ~0.10. This measurement transforms covariate shift from a theoretical concern into an empirically trackable phenomenon, enabling future work to quantify, rather than merely assert, whether a post-training method mitigates distribution mismatch.

The comparison against published SWE-agent systems (Table 3, lower block) establishes a **ceiling-calibration point** that shifts expectations about what small models can achieve with appropriate post-training. The 4B DAgger-style model at 27.3% outperforms SWE-Gym-32B (20.6%) and approaches within ~7 points of specialized 32B systems like R2E-Gym-32B-Agent (34.4%). This does not mean post-training replaces pretraining—the gap to 32B systems persists, and the hardest tasks remain largely unsolved (70%+ failure rate across all methods). But it does mean that **practitioners with access to a strong teacher should invest heavily in state-distribution correction before scaling model size**, because the returns from DAgger-style training on a 4B model can exceed the returns from quadrupling parameter count while using SFT alone.

The work also **resolves a latent tension** between the imitation learning and RL communities in agent post-training. The near-zero gains from GRPO (11.2% → 11.6% at 4B, 7.7% → 8.2% at 8B on SWE-Bench Verified, Table 3) suggest that pure RLVR with outcome-level rewards is not competitive with imitation-based methods on long-horizon SWE tasks at this scale. This does not mean RL is useless for agents—it means that outcome-level rewards alone provide insufficient signal over 20–100 turn trajectories where credit assignment is extremely challenging. The paper does not claim imitation learning is universally superior, but its results redirect attention toward methods that provide dense, turn-level supervision, whether from teachers (DAgger), rubrics, or learned process reward models. The GRPO negative result serves as an important calibration: if a practitioner's task has trajectories longer than ~20 turns and only binary outcome feedback, imitation-based methods should be the default starting point.

However, the paper's **flooring of `β_i` at 0.6** (Section 3, Appendix F)—meaning the student never trains on batches with more than 40% student-induced states—introduces a subtle but important hedge on the DAgger narrative. Classical DAgger assumes `β_i → 0` because the student eventually becomes competent enough to generate useful trajectories autonomously. The fact that the paper empirically chose to floor at 0.6 (without ablation to justify this specific value) suggests that **fully on-policy training remains unstable for SWE agents at this scale**, even after 5 iterations of curriculum. This is not a failure of the method but a boundary condition: for tasks where the student's autonomous success rate remains below ~30% even after training, persistent teacher guidance is necessary to prevent training data collapse into uninformative failure trajectories. The paper does not frame it this way, but the implication is clear: DAgger helps, but it does not solve the fundamental problem that weak policies produce unproductive rollouts—it only mitigates it by keeping the teacher in the loop.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Systematic ablation of the DAgger mixing schedule `β_i`.** The paper specifies `β_i = max(0.6, 1.0 - 0.2(i-1))` and floors at 0.6, but never ablates over the decay rate, floor value, or number of iterations. A direct follow-up would train DAgger-style agents with floors of {0.0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8} on the same SWE-Gym setup, tracking both resolution rate on SWE-Bench Verified and reverse KL divergence on student-visited contexts. The key question: does performance degrade at lower floors because cold-start returns (pure on-policy rollouts become unproductive), improve because the state distribution shifts further toward deployment conditions, or plateau at the 0.6 floor? If degradation occurs, the experiment identifies a principled minimum teacher-intervention rate for long-horizon SWE tasks; if improvement occurs, the paper's reported gains are conservative. This ablation also directly tests whether the student ever becomes competent enough to sustain fully on-policy training—a question with implications for whether DAgger is a permanent crutch or a temporary curriculum.

**SFT-initialized DAgger to isolate cold-start from covariate-shift benefits.** The paper's DAgger-style training starts from the base model, so the first iteration (`β_1 = 1.0`) provides pure expert trajectories that serve as both cold-start initialization and the beginning of state-distribution correction. A clean follow-up would initialize DAgger-style training from the SFT checkpoint (which already achieves 22.9% on SWE-Bench Verified for the 4B model, Table 3), then run the same 5-iteration mixture-rollout schedule. If DAgger-style still improves over the SFT initialization (e.g., from 22.9% to ~27% on SWE-Bench Verified), the gains can be attributed to ongoing state-distribution correction rather than better initialization—validating the covariate-shift mechanism independent of cold start. If DAgger-style does not improve over SFT initialization, then the paper's headline gains are primarily a cold-start effect and the method is essentially a better SFT initialization procedure rather than a fundamentally different training paradigm. This experiment is cheap (the SFT checkpoint already exists from the paper's SFT baseline) and would resolve the central mechanistic uncertainty in the paper's narrative.

**Cross-domain transfer to web navigation or data analysis agents.** The paper's entire empirical validation is on SWE tasks. To test whether the DAgger principle generalizes beyond code-editing domains, a follow-up would replicate the 4B-scale experiment on WebArena or a comparable web-navigation benchmark where trajectories involve DOM interactions, form filling, and navigation rather than code editing. The key adaptation is the teacher: a strong web-navigation model must serve as `π_e` to provide action labels on visited pages. The experiment would measure: (1) whether DAgger-style training produces gains of comparable magnitude (+3–5 points) in the web domain, (2) whether the optimal `β_i` schedule differs (web trajectories may be shorter or have different error-recovery patterns), and (3) whether the covariate-shift diagnostic (reverse KL on deployment contexts) replicates the V-shaped rebound for SFT and stability for DAgger. A negative result—DAgger providing no benefit in web navigation—would indicate that the method's gains are specific to SWE's combination of long trajectories, path-dependent errors, and code-editing action spaces, significantly narrowing the paper's claimed generality.

**Teacher-quality scaling analysis.** The paper uses a single teacher (Qwen3-Coder-30B-A3B-Instruct) that is ~8× larger than the 4B student and code-specialized. A follow-up would train the 4B student with a range of teachers: the same 30B teacher (replication), a 14B teacher from the same Qwen3-Coder family (to test whether a ~3× larger teacher preserves gains), a 7B teacher (roughly peer-level, to test whether DAgger helps when the teacher is not substantially stronger), and a 30B general-purpose teacher (Qwen3-30B, not code-specialized) to test whether domain specialization matters more than raw scale. The dependent variables are SWE-Bench Verified resolution rate, training stability (reverse KL trajectory), and effective sample efficiency. If DAgger-style gains persist with a peer-level or general-purpose teacher, the method is far more broadly applicable than the paper's setup suggests. If gains collapse with weaker teachers, the method's practical scope is limited to settings where a much stronger, domain-matched teacher is available—an important constraint for practitioners and a signal that teacher quality should be reported alongside method comparisons in future work.

**Combining DAgger with process reward models for inference-time search.** The paper uses DAgger purely for training; evaluation uses greedy decoding with no test-time compute scaling. A natural extension would train a process reward model (PRM) on the teacher-labeled states collected during DAgger rollouts—since every visited state has a teacher-provided action and an eventual trajectory outcome (pass/fail), these states are automatically labeled with correctness signals—and then use this PRM to guide beam search or best-of-N weighted selection at inference time. The hypothesis is that DAgger's state-distribution correction (training) and PRM-guided search (inference) are complementary: DAgger ensures the model's proposal distribution covers deployment-realistic states, while the PRM helps select the best among multiple sampled trajectories. The experiment would compare DAgger-style training alone (current paper) vs. DAgger-style training + PRM inference-time search, measuring whether the combination pushes the 8B model closer to the ~34–37% range of 32B specialized systems. If the gains compound, this establishes a full pipeline—train with mixture rollouts, verify with learned PRMs—that combines the paper's training insight with the test-time compute scaling literature.

**Investigation of why GRPO fails and what would make RL competitive.** The paper's GRPO baseline produces near-zero gains (Table 3), which is a striking negative result given the compute invested (3 passes over 293 tasks, 8 trajectories per task). A diagnostic follow-up would analyze *why* GRPO fails: is it credit assignment (outcome rewards provide no per-turn signal), advantage collapse (all 8 rollouts on a task either succeed or fail, yielding zero relative advantage), or exploration failure (the student never discovers successful trajectories to reinforce)? The experiment would instrument GRPO training to measure per-turn advantage variance, the fraction of batches with non-zero advantages, and the diversity of trajectory outcomes within each group. If advantage collapse is the dominant failure mode, the result motivates research on denser reward signals (rubrics, process rewards, learned value functions) rather than better policy-gradient algorithms. If credit assignment is the bottleneck, it motivates incorporating turn-level feedback—potentially from the same teacher used in DAgger—into the RL objective, blurring the line between the paper's imitation approach and RL. This diagnostic work is essential because the paper's results imply that outcome-level RLVR is not a viable post-training strategy for long-horizon SWE agents at this scale, a finding that should either be explained and generalized or challenged with improved RL methods.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Cost-efficient post-training for small SWE-agent deployments.** Organizations training SWE agents on proprietary codebases with limited compute budgets can apply DAgger-style training to achieve resolution rates competitive with much larger models. The paper's 4B DAgger-style agent reaches 27.3% on SWE-Bench Verified—outperforming published 7–8B systems (e.g., SWE-smith-LM-7B at 15.2%, R2E-Gym-7B-Agent at 19.0%) and a 32B SFT-trained system (SWE-Gym-32B at 20.6%). For a team that has access to a strong teacher model (e.g., an internal 30B+ code model or API access to a frontier model) but wants to deploy a smaller, cheaper agent, DAgger-style training provides a concrete recipe: collect 5 rounds of mixed-policy rollouts with `β_i` annealing from 1.0 to 0.6, train on teacher-labeled states with a constant `3 × 10^{-6}` learning rate, and deploy the resulting 4B–8B agent. The inference cost savings from a 4B agent versus a 32B agent are roughly 8× per token, which compounds significantly in continuous integration pipelines that evaluate agents on hundreds or thousands of issues.

**Data-efficient bootstrapping of agent capabilities from black-box API teachers.** Because DAgger-style training requires only teacher-generated action sequences (not logits or internal states), it is compatible with black-box API models like GPT-4, Claude, or Gemini—models to which OPD cannot be applied because OPD requires token-level log-probabilities for the reverse-KL objective. A team wanting to distill SWE capabilities from a frontier API model into a smaller open-weight model for on-premise deployment can: (1) use the API model as `π_e` to generate labels on SWE-Gym or a proprietary task distribution, (2) run DAgger-style training with the smaller open model as `π_θ`, and (3) deploy the resulting agent without any API dependency at inference time. The paper's results with a 30B teacher producing a 4B student that outperforms a 32B SFT-trained model suggest that this distillation pathway can be surprisingly effective—the student can surpass models trained with SFT at 4–8× its parameter count, even when the teacher is only used for labeling and not for distillation in the traditional sense. The main practical cost is the API inference expense for ~250,000 teacher queries across training (512 tasks × ~100 max turns × 5 iterations, with filtering reducing this in practice), which a team should weigh against the alternative of training a larger model from scratch.

**Improved training stability for long-horizon agent fine-tuning.** Practitioners who have experienced SFT performance degradation with extended training—the SWE-Bench Verified decline from 20% to 19% shown in Figure 1 (right) for SFT, attributed to covariate shift—can adopt the mixture-rollout protocol as a drop-in replacement for standard SFT data collection. The implementation change is minimal: instead of generating expert trajectories once and training on them, generate trajectories with teacher-student interleaving (using the `β_i` schedule) and train on teacher labels at all visited states. The paper's Figure 2 shows that this stabilizes the deployment-time policy divergence, and Figure 1 shows monotonic improvement without the SFT decline pattern. For teams already running OPD pipelines (which require on-policy rollouts and teacher access), switching to DAgger-style requires no additional infrastructure—only changing the trajectory collection protocol to include `β_i`-probability teacher turns and labeling all states with teacher actions rather than computing reverse-KL weights. The paper's results suggest this switch yields faster early convergence (3K sample advantage in Figure 1) and better final performance (+3.6–3.9 points on SWE-Bench Verified at 4B–8B). The practical risk is the teacher inference overhead (querying `π_e` at every state, including teacher-executed turns), but for teams already paying teacher inference costs for OPD, this overhead may be acceptable given the performance gains.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper explicitly positions DAgger-style training against SFT, GRPO (RLVR), and on-policy distillation (OPD), with the tradeoffs enumerated in Table 1 and the unified framework in Table 2. The decision rules that emerge from the paper's evidence and design choices are:

- **Prefer DAgger-style training over SFT when** the student's autonomous success rate on the target task is below ~30% (cold-start matters) and the task requires long trajectories (≥20 turns) where covariate shift compounds. SFT remains simpler and cheaper when a large corpus of pre-collected expert trajectories already exists and the student's deployment-time behavior is not expected to diverge significantly from expert demonstrations—but the paper's Figure 2 shows that divergence is likely even for moderately capable students, and the SWE-Bench Verified decline in Figure 1 shows SFT overfits to the training distribution. The paper's evidence suggests that for long-horizon agentic tasks, SFT should be treated as a baseline that DAgger-style training will likely improve upon, not as a co-equal option.

- **Prefer DAgger-style training over OPD when** a stronger teacher model is available and its inference cost during training is acceptable. DAgger-style outperforms OPD at every sample budget (Figure 1) and achieves lower deployment-time policy divergence earlier in training (Figure 2). The paper identifies OPD's cold-start fragility as the mechanism: early student-only rollouts are dominated by failure trajectories, limiting early learning signal. DAgger-style's teacher-interleaved rollouts avoid this by ensuring productive trajectories from iteration 1 (`β_1 = 1.0`) while gradually introducing student-induced states. The one scenario where OPD might be preferred is when teacher logits are available and the practitioner wants to avoid the additional per-state teacher generation cost—OPD uses the student's own actions as the label source, so it only requires teacher forward passes for importance-weight computation (which can be done with a single pass per state), whereas DAgger requires full teacher action generation at every state (which may require autoregressive decoding). The paper does not quantify this cost difference, so practitioners should measure it in their specific setting.

- **Prefer DAgger-style training over GRPO / RLVR when** the task provides only sparse outcome-level rewards and the trajectory horizon exceeds ~10–20 turns. The paper's GRPO baseline produces near-zero gains (Table 3), and the paper attributes this to sparse credit assignment and advantage collapse. DAgger-style provides dense, turn-level supervision through teacher labels, which is fundamentally more informative per sample when the outcome signal is binary and delayed. GRPO or other RL methods may be preferred when (a) dense per-step rewards are available (e.g., rubrics, process rewards, or learned value functions), (b) the base model is strong enough that its rollouts have non-trivial success rates (reducing advantage collapse), or (c) exploration beyond teacher-demonstrated behaviors is desired (DAgger is fundamentally limited to imitating the teacher's action distribution). The paper does not test DAgger against RL methods with dense rewards, so this tradeoff is based on the paper's RLVR results and the method's design, not on direct empirical comparison with improved RL.

- **Prefer the DAgger-style variant over the AggreVaTe-style variant when** maximizing overall resolution rate is the goal. DAgger-style achieves +2.8 and +2.5 points higher on SWE-Bench Verified at 4B and 8B respectively (Table 3). However, AggreVaTe-style achieves better file localization (Table 4: 15.7% wrong-file rate vs. DAgger-style's 19.7%), suggesting that trajectory-level teacher completions provide stronger search and navigation guidance. A practitioner prioritizing repository exploration accuracy over end-to-end resolution might prefer AggreVaTe-style despite its lower aggregate score, or might combine the two by using AggreVaTe-style rollouts in early iterations (for localization) and DAgger-style rollouts in later iterations (for fine-grained error correction).

These decision rules are conditioned on the paper's specific experimental setup: Qwen3 model family, SWE tasks under the OpenHands scaffold, a teacher ~4–8× larger than the student, and the specific `β_i` schedule with a 0.6 floor. The paper does not provide evidence on how these tradeoffs shift under different teachers, domains, or model scales, so practitioners should treat the rules as **empirically grounded for the tested setting** rather than universally validated.
