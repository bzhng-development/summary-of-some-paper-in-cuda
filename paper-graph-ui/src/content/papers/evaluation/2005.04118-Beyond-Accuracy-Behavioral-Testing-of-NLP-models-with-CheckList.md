## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **CheckList**, a task-agnostic methodology for behavioral testing of NLP models that draws on principles from software engineering—specifically black-box testing—to evaluate models through a matrix of general linguistic capabilities (e.g., Vocabulary+POS, Negation, Named Entity Recognition, Temporal understanding) and three test types: Minimum Functionality tests (simple targeted examples like "I didn't love the food"), Invariance tests (label-preserving perturbations like swapping location names that should not change sentiment), and Directional Expectation tests (perturbations with known expected label shifts, such as adding negative phrases and checking sentiment does not become more positive). Applied to sentiment analysis, duplicate question detection (QQP), and machine comprehension (MC), CheckList reveals critical failures in both commercial models (Microsoft, Google, Amazon) and state-of-the-art research models (BERT, RoBERTa) that had achieved near-human benchmark accuracy—for instance, a commercial sentiment model fails 76.4% of simple negation MFTs and 20.8% of NER invariance tests—while a user study demonstrates that practitioners using CheckList create twice as many tests and find almost three times as many bugs as those without it, establishing that accuracy on held-out benchmarks systematically overestimates real-world NLP model competence even for models that have been extensively tested by commercial teams.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Aggregate Accuracy Is a Misleading Performance Signal

The paper addresses a fundamental disconnect in how NLP models are evaluated. The standard paradigm—train-validate-test splits producing a single aggregate accuracy statistic—has been the primary mechanism for measuring generalization since the early days of statistical NLP. When a model achieves human-level accuracy on a benchmark like SQuAD or GLUE, the field typically declares the task "solved" and moves on. This paper argues that such declarations are premature and that aggregate accuracy **systematically overestimates** real-world competence.

The problem is not merely academic. When a commercial sentiment analysis model achieves impressive accuracy on standard benchmarks but fails 76.4% of the time on simple negated sentences like "The food is not poor" (Table 1), something is deeply wrong with how we evaluate. The model has not learned negation—it has learned to associate certain words with sentiment labels in ways that break down outside the training distribution. Aggregate accuracy masks these failures because negated sentences may be rare in the test set, or because other sentences are easy enough to keep the average high despite catastrophic failures on specific linguistic phenomena.

This is the gap CheckList addresses: **we lack systematic, task-agnostic methodologies for identifying *what* specifically breaks in NLP models, beyond reporting a single accuracy number on held-out data.** The paper draws an explicit parallel to software engineering, where behavioral testing (black-box testing) has been standard practice for decades (Beizer, 1995). In software, you don't ship a system after only running it on a held-out validation set—you write unit tests for individual functions, integration tests for component interactions, and stress tests for edge cases. NLP models, despite being software systems deployed in production, are evaluated almost exclusively through the equivalent of a single integration test.

### Why This Problem Matters: Real-World Consequences of Undetected Failures

The paper documents failures that have direct practical consequences:

**Commercial deployment at scale.** The authors test three commercial sentiment analysis APIs (Microsoft's Text Analytics, Google Cloud's Natural Language, Amazon's Comprehend) that are sold as production-ready services listing social media as a use case. These are not research prototypes—they are revenue-generating products used by customers. Yet the failures are severe: sensitivity to random Twitter handles and URLs (13.4–24.8% of predictions change), inability to handle negation at the end of sentences (near 100% failure across commercial models), and systematic errors on temporal distinctions (36.6–42.2% failure on present-vs-past sentiment). A company using these APIs to monitor brand sentiment on social media would systematically misclassify tweets containing "I thought it would be awful, but it wasn't" as negative.

**Benchmark saturation and leaderboard overfitting.** The paper tests BERT-base and RoBERTa-base models that achieve 91.1% and 91.3% accuracy on QQP—a benchmark where human performance was reported at 88.1% by Wang et al. (2019a). By standard metrics, these models are "superhuman." Yet Table 2 shows they fail to recognize duplicates when modifiers differentiate questions ("Is Mark Wright a photographer?" vs. "Is Mark Wright an accredited photographer?") **78% of the time** for RoBERTa. They fail to handle simple temporal distinctions like "is" vs. "used to be" 61.8–96.8% of the time. They fail active/passive voice swaps 65.8–98.6% of the time. These failures are not edge cases—they represent fundamental linguistic skills that any system claiming to understand question paraphrase should possess.

**Hidden biases that accuracy can't surface.** The paper uncovers a striking bias in a BERT model fine-tuned on SQuAD for machine comprehension (Section 3, MC). In a simple negation test using the template "John is not a doctor, Mary is. Who is a doctor?", the model picks the male name as the doctor 89.1% of the time when the female name is correct (but only 3.2% when the male is correct). When the profession is "secretary," the bias reverses: it picks the woman 60.5% of the time and the man 4.0%. This gender-profession stereotype is invisible in standard accuracy metrics—the model might still answer correctly on many questions—but reveals that the model has learned biased shortcuts rather than genuine comprehension of negation and entity roles.

**The "solved problem" illusion.** The authors are explicit about this in their discussion: "These tasks may be considered 'solved' based on benchmark accuracy results, but the tests highlight various areas of improvement—in particular, failure to demonstrate basic skills that are de facto needs for the task at hand." The implication is that the NLP community's collective sense of progress is partly an artifact of inadequate evaluation. We congratulate ourselves on solving tasks that models have not actually solved; they have merely achieved high accuracy on test sets that share the same biases as the training data (a point made by Rajpurkar et al., 2018, regarding SQuAD).

### Prior Approaches and Where They Fall Short

The paper positions itself against a landscape of evaluation approaches, each of which addresses *part* of the problem but none of which provides comprehensive, task-agnostic guidance:

**Challenge and diagnostic datasets.** Prior work has created focused datasets targeting specific linguistic phenomena—for example, the stress tests for Natural Language Inference by Naik et al. (2018) or the contrast sets of Gardner et al. (2020). Belinkov and Glass (2019) survey this approach and note its benefits (systematic control) alongside significant drawbacks: these datasets are typically **small-scale**, **task-specific** (primarily NLI), and often lack resemblance to naturally occurring data. Each new task requires a new challenge set, and there is no unifying methodology for deciding what capabilities to test or how to structure the tests. CheckList addresses the specificity problem by providing a general capability-by-test-type matrix that adapts to any task, and the scale problem through templating and perturbation-based test generation (INV and DIR tests can be applied to unlabeled, naturally occurring data).

**Robustness and adversarial evaluations.** Several lines of work test specific behavioral dimensions: Belinkov and Bisk (2018) on synthetic and natural noise in machine translation, Rychalska et al. (2019) on corruption robustness, Ribeiro et al. (2018) on semantically equivalent adversarial perturbations, and Prabhakaran et al. (2019) on sensitivity to name changes as a fairness probe. These approaches demonstrate that models are brittle, but each focuses on a **single capability** (usually robustness) in isolation. They do not provide a framework for *which* capabilities to test or *how many* test types to employ. CheckList doesn't reject these methods—it subsumes them into a structured matrix where robustness is one row and each perturbation technique becomes an INV or DIR test in that row.

**Probing classifiers.** With the rise of deep pretrained models, a popular analysis technique has been to train lightweight "probes" on intermediate representations to determine whether the encoder encodes linguistic properties like part-of-speech, named entities, or syntactic structure (Tenney et al., 2019; Kim et al., 2019). While these reveal *what information is present* in the representations, they have a critical limitation the paper highlights: "these do not give users an understanding of how a fine-tuned (or end-to-end) model can handle linguistic phenomena for the end-task." The paper provides a concrete example: Tenney et al. (2019) showed that BERT representations support highly accurate NER probing (96.7%), yet the same paper's CheckList results demonstrate that BERT fine-tuned on QQP or SST-2 "displays severe NER issues"—changing a location name in a question causes 11.8–9.4% prediction changes. Information being present in representations does not guarantee it is properly *used* by the downstream task model.

**Interactive error analysis tools.** Wu et al. (2019) introduced Errudite, a tool for scalable, reproducible error analysis where practitioners interactively explore model predictions. This complements CheckList in that it helps users *discover* patterns in existing predictions, but it does not provide the *systematic testing methodology*—the capability matrix, the test types, the guidance on what to test—that CheckList contributes. A user with Errudite might notice that negation causes problems, but CheckList prompts them to test negation with MFTs, INVs, and DIRs across templates that systematically vary negation position and content.

**Explainability methods.** Techniques like LIME (Ribeiro et al., 2016) explain individual predictions by identifying which input tokens most influenced the output. While valuable for debugging specific instances, they don't scale to systematic evaluation of linguistic capabilities across a model. You can't LIME your way to discovering that your model fails 76.4% of negation MFTs—you need structured tests.

**Fairness evaluations.** Prabhakaran et al. (2019) proposed perturbation sensitivity analysis to detect unintended biases, testing whether predictions change when demographic attributes are swapped. This is a specialized case of INV testing. CheckList incorporates fairness as one capability row in its matrix, placing it alongside other linguistic capabilities rather than treating it as a separate evaluation paradigm.

### The Missing Framework: Task-Agnostic, Capability-Oriented, Multi-Type Testing

The paper's central diagnosis is that the evaluation landscape is **fragmented and task-specific**. Each prior approach scratches one part of the problem:
- Challenge sets test specific phenomena on specific tasks
- Robustness papers test one capability (usually typos or noise)  
- Probes test whether information exists in representations, not whether it's used
- Error analysis tools help explore, not systematically test
- Explainability tools help debug individual predictions, not evaluate capability

What's missing is a **methodology**—not just a dataset or a tool but a *way of thinking about evaluation* that (1) applies across tasks, (2) prompts testers to consider multiple linguistic capabilities systematically, (3) provides different test types for different behavioral dimensions (not just "does it get the right answer?" but also "is it invariant to perturbations?" and "does the prediction change in the expected direction?"), and (4) scales to large numbers of test cases through templating and perturbation abstractions.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper's framing is explicitly constructive rather than critical. It doesn't argue that benchmark accuracy should be abandoned—"We do not aim for CheckList to replace challenge or benchmark datasets, but to complement them" (Section 5). Rather, it argues that the current toolkit is incomplete. The software engineering analogy is central: just as good software development includes both integration tests (benchmarks) and unit tests (CheckList MFTs) and regression tests (INVs/DIRs), good NLP evaluation requires all of these.

The paper also positions itself as an **enabling framework** rather than a fixed test suite. The capability list (Vocabulary+POS, Taxonomy, Robustness, NER, Fairness, Temporal, Negation, Coreference, SRL, Logic) is explicitly "not exhaustive, but a starting point for users, who should also come up with additional capabilities that are specific to their task or domain" (Section 2.1). The test types—MFT, INV, DIR—are inspired by software metamorphic testing (Segura et al., 2016) but adapted to the linguistic domain. The software implementation provides templating, masked LM suggestions, and perturbation libraries that make test creation efficient, but the methodology itself is the contribution; the tool is an enabler.

Finally, the paper positions CheckList as addressing evaluation at the right level of abstraction for modern end-to-end NLP. Unlike component-by-component testing in traditional software, where you test the parser, the tagger, and the classifier separately, NLP models are monolithic. CheckList's innovation is to test **behaviorally**—through input-output pairs designed to exercise specific capabilities—without requiring or assuming any internal structure. This "decoupling testing from implementation" (Section 2) is what makes CheckList model-agnostic and task-agnostic: you can run the same NER invariance test on a BERT-based classifier, a commercial API, or a rule-based system and compare failure rates directly.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

CheckList is a **methodology and software toolkit** that enables systematic behavioral testing of NLP models by evaluating their performance on specific linguistic capabilities through three distinct test types, rather than relying solely on aggregate accuracy metrics. It solves the problem that standard held-out evaluation masks where and how models fail: by structuring evaluation as a matrix of capabilities (rows) crossed with test types (columns), CheckList prompts users to ask "does my model handle negation?", "does my model preserve predictions when I swap location names?", and "does my model's sentiment decrease when I add negative phrases?"—questions that a single accuracy number cannot answer. The solution is a conceptual framework combined with practical abstractions (templates, lexicons, masked language model suggestions, perturbation libraries) that make it efficient to generate hundreds or thousands of targeted test cases probing specific model behaviors.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The CheckList system has four major conceptual components:

1. **The Capability × Test Type Matrix**—a structured grid that organizes what to test. Rows are linguistic capabilities (Vocabulary+POS, Taxonomy, Robustness, NER, Fairness, Temporal, Negation, Coreference, Semantic Role Labeling, Logic, and task-specific additions). Columns are three test types (MFT, INV, DIR). Each cell in the matrix prompts the user to design tests that exercise that specific capability using that specific test type. This matrix serves as a test ideation guide, not a fixed test suite.

2. **Test Case Generation Abstractions**—tools for creating test cases at scale. These include (a) **templates** with placeholder slots that generate many examples via Cartesian product over fill-in lexicons, (b) **masked language model suggestions** (using RoBERTa) that propose context-appropriate words for template slots, (c) **pre-built lexicons** for general-purpose categories (named entities, protected group adjectives, common contractions), and (d) **perturbation functions** that transform existing inputs (typo simulation via character swaps, name/location changes, URL/handle insertion) to create INV and DIR tests from unlabeled data.

3. **Test Execution and Scoring**—the process of running generated test cases through a model and computing per-test failure rates. For MFTs: compare model predictions against expected labels and count mismatches. For INVs: apply label-preserving perturbations and flag cases where predictions change (with a probability threshold of 0.1 for continuous scores). For DIRs: apply perturbations and verify that predictions shift in the specified direction (e.g., sentiment score does not increase after adding negative text, or two questions become non-duplicates after changing a name in one).

4. **Visualization and Reporting**—tools for surfacing failures to the user, including summary tables of failure rates per test and per capability, example-level inspection of failure cases, and the ability to save, share, and reuse test suites across different models and teams. The entire system is released as open-source software.

Information flows as follows: a user identifies a model to test → the capability matrix prompts ideation of what to test → the user selects a test type (MFT, INV, or DIR) for a specific capability → test cases are generated from scratch via templates and lexicons, or by perturbing an existing dataset → tests are executed against the model → failure rates are computed per test → results are aggregated across tests into a capability-level summary → the user interprets failures, identifies systematic weaknesses, and incorporates fixes into the next model iteration.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the three test types (MFT, INV, DIR) in full detail—their definitions, their mechanisms, their mathematical scoring, and how each reveals different failure modes. This is the foundation because every test in the matrix uses one of these three types.

- **Second**, the capability taxonomy—what each capability means in concrete linguistic terms, how it manifests differently across tasks (sentiment vs. QQP vs. MC), and why this particular list of capabilities was chosen as the starting rows of the matrix. Understanding the capabilities is necessary to understand why certain test type × capability combinations are natural or challenging.

- **Third**, the template-based test generation system—the mechanics of templates, placeholder slots, Cartesian expansion, and the masked language model suggestion workflow. This is what makes CheckList practical rather than merely conceptual: it's the engine that converts one test idea into hundreds of concrete examples.

- **Fourth**, the perturbation-based test generation for INV and DIR tests—how perturbation functions work, which general-purpose perturbations are provided, and how perturbation tests differ from template tests in their relationship to labeled data.

- **Fifth**, the scoring and evaluation framework—how failure rates are computed for each test type, the probability threshold used for INV/DIR tests, how results aggregate up from individual test cases to test-level to capability-level summaries, and the design decisions behind best-of-N weighted selection.

- **Sixth**, the design philosophy and architectural choices—why the matrix structure, why these three test types rather than others, why behavioral (black-box) rather than structural (white-box) testing, and how CheckList relates to software engineering's metamorphic testing tradition.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **methodology and systems paper** whose core idea is that NLP models should be evaluated not just on aggregate accuracy but on their behavioral response to systematic tests of individual linguistic capabilities, and that a structured matrix of capabilities crossed with test types—combined with practical tools for test generation at scale—provides a task-agnostic framework for doing so.

#### The Three Test Types: MFT, INV, and DIR

The paper defines three distinct test types, each designed to probe a different kind of model behavior. Together, they form the columns of the CheckList matrix.

##### Minimum Functionality Test (MFT)

An MFT is a collection of simple, hand-designed (or template-generated) input-output pairs that directly test whether a model exhibits a specific behavior within a linguistic capability. The paper describes MFTs as "inspired by unit tests in software engineering" and characterizes them as "similar to creating small and focused testing datasets." Each MFT consists of a set of examples and their expected labels, and the test passes or fails on each example depending on whether the model's prediction matches the expected label.

The failure rate for an MFT is computed as:

$$\text{FailureRate}_{\text{MFT}} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \mathbb{1}[f(x_i) \neq y_i]$$

where $N$ is the number of test cases in the MFT, $f(x_i)$ is the model's prediction on input $x_i$, $y_i$ is the expected label for that input, and $\mathbb{1}[\cdot]$ is the indicator function that returns 1 when the condition holds and 0 otherwise.

**What it computes:** the fraction of MFT test cases for which the model's prediction disagrees with the expected label. For each test case, the model is queried, its prediction is compared to the ground truth, and a mismatch increments the failure counter. The result is a single percentage indicating what fraction of that MFT's examples the model gets wrong.

**Why this form:** this is the most direct behavioral test—it asks "does the model get the right answer on these targeted examples?" The simplicity is deliberate. MFTs should be easy—they test whether the model has mastered capabilities that are "de facto needs for the task at hand" (Section 3, Discussion). High failure rates on MFTs indicate that the model is using shortcuts to achieve its benchmark accuracy rather than genuinely acquiring the capability. For instance, an MFT for negation in sentiment analysis might include "The food is not poor" with expected label positive or neutral; a model scoring this as negative reveals it has not learned negation.

MFTs are designed to target behavior in isolation. By controlling vocabulary, syntax, and confounding factors through careful template design, an MFT isolates one capability and tests it cleanly. The paper emphasizes that MFTs also "focus on what should be easy cases given a capability"—they are not difficult edge cases but fundamental sanity checks. An MFT that asks "The company is Australian" and expects neutral sentiment is not probing sophisticated reasoning; it is verifying that the model can handle a neutral adjective applied to a neutral noun without hallucinating sentiment.

##### Invariance Test (INV)

An INV test applies label-preserving perturbations to inputs and verifies that the model's prediction does not change. The perturbation should be semantically irrelevant to the task—changing a location name in a sentiment analysis tweet should not alter sentiment, and introducing a typo should not change a duplicate question classification. The test fails when the model's prediction changes despite the perturbation being task-irrelevant.

For classification tasks, the failure condition is:

$$\text{Failure} = \mathbb{1}[f(x') \neq f(x)]$$

where $x$ is the original input, $x'$ is the perturbed input, and $f(x)$ and $f(x')$ are the model's predictions on the original and perturbed inputs respectively. For models that output continuous scores (like sentiment probability), the paper introduces a tolerance threshold:

$$\text{Failure} = \mathbb{1}[|f_{\text{score}}(x') - f_{\text{score}}(x)| > 0.1]$$

where $f_{\text{score}}(x)$ is the model's continuous sentiment score, and 0.1 is a threshold chosen to ignore small floating-point or calibration noise while catching meaningful prediction shifts.

The failure rate for an INV test suite is:

$$\text{FailureRate}_{\text{INV}} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \mathbb{1}[\text{Failure}(x_i, x'_i)]$$

where $N$ is the number of original-perturbed pairs, and $\text{Failure}(x_i, x'_i)$ is the failure condition defined above for each pair.

**What it computes:** the fraction of perturbation pairs for which the model behaves inconsistently—changing its prediction when the perturbation should have no task-relevant effect. For each original input, the perturbation is applied, both the original and perturbed inputs are fed to the model, and the predictions are compared. If they differ (beyond a 0.1 threshold for continuous scores), the test case fails. The result is a percentage indicating how unstable the model is under task-irrelevant perturbations.

**Why this form:** INV tests address a limitation of MFTs—MFTs require ground-truth labels, which are expensive to create and limit tests to labeled data. INV tests work on any input, labeled or not, because the correctness criterion is not "matches ground truth" but "prediction remains invariant under perturbation." This decouples testing from annotation. The paper explicitly notes: "INVs and DIRs allow us to test models on unlabeled data—they test behaviors that do not rely on ground truth labels, but rather on relationships between predictions after perturbations are applied." This is inspired by metamorphic testing in software engineering (Segura et al., 2016), where you verify that a program's output satisfies certain relational properties across transformed inputs without needing to know the "correct" output for each input.

The perturbation functions themselves encode domain knowledge about what should be invariant for each task. For sentiment analysis, NER changes should be invariant—"I loved my flight to Chicago" and "I loved my flight to Dallas" should receive the same sentiment prediction. For QQP, the invariance condition depends on the perturbation: changing the same name in both questions should preserve the duplicate/non-duplicate status, but changing a name in only one question should change it (which is a DIR test, discussed next). The paper provides general-purpose perturbation functions (character swaps for typos, name/location changes, URL/handle insertion) that apply across tasks, while task-specific perturbations are left to the user.

##### Directional Expectation Test (DIR)

A DIR test is similar to an INV test in that it applies perturbations to inputs, but instead of expecting invariance, it expects the model's prediction to change in a specific, known direction. The paper gives the example of adding negative phrases to a tweet: "we expect that sentiment will not become more positive if we add 'You are lame.' to the end of tweets directed at an airline."

The failure condition for directional tests depends on the specific expectation. For monotonicity constraints (sentiment should not increase after adding negative text), the failure condition is:

$$\text{Failure} = \mathbb{1}[f_{\text{score}}(x') > f_{\text{score}}(x) + \epsilon]$$

where $x$ is the original input, $x'$ is the perturbed input with negative text added, $f_{\text{score}}(x)$ is the sentiment score (higher = more positive), and $\epsilon$ is a small tolerance (the paper uses 0.1 as a threshold for meaningful change). For cases where the expectation is a discrete label flip—for example, changing a name in only one of two QQP questions should change the label from duplicate to non-duplicate—the failure condition is:

$$\text{Failure} = \mathbb{1}[f(x') = \text{duplicate}]$$

where $f(x')$ is the model's prediction on the perturbed pair that should no longer be duplicates.

**What it computes:** the fraction of perturbation cases where the model's prediction shifts in the wrong direction or fails to shift when it should. Each perturbation encodes a hypothesis about the task semantics: adding negative text should decrease or maintain sentiment, paraphrasing a question should preserve its duplicate status, swapping an antonym should flip the expected answer in machine comprehension. The test evaluates whether the model respects these structural properties.

**Why this form:** DIR tests are motivated by the same insight as INV tests—they enable testing on unlabeled data—but they probe a different aspect of model behavior. INV tests check stability under irrelevant changes; DIR tests check sensitivity to relevant changes. Both are necessary: a model can be trivially invariant by ignoring the input entirely, predicting the same label regardless. DIR tests guard against this degenerate case by verifying that the model does respond appropriately to semantically meaningful perturbations. Together, INV and DIR tests form a minimal completeness check: the model should change when the task semantics change (DIR) and should not change when they do not (INV).

The paper notes that the expectation in a DIR test "may also be a target label"—for instance, replacing locations in only one of the questions in QQP ensures the questions are no longer duplicates, and the model should predict non-duplicate. This is equivalent to creating an MFT on the perturbed input, but the power of DIR is that you do not need to annotate the perturbed input—the correct label is logically implied by the perturbation. If you know that "How many people are there in England?" and "What is the population of England?" are duplicates, you automatically know that "How many people are there in England?" and "What is the population of Turkey?" are not duplicates, without needing a human to label the second pair.

#### The Capability Taxonomy

The paper proposes a list of linguistic capabilities that form the rows of the CheckList matrix. Each capability represents a dimension of language understanding that is relevant across many NLP tasks, though its specific manifestation depends on the task. The list is explicitly "not exhaustive, but a starting point for users, who should also come up with additional capabilities that are specific to their task or domain" (Section 2.1). Below, each capability is defined with its task-specific manifestations.

**Vocabulary + Parts of Speech (Vocabulary+POS):** this capability tests whether the model has the necessary vocabulary for the task and can appropriately handle words with different parts of speech. For sentiment analysis, this means checking whether the model identifies words that carry positive, negative, or neutral sentiment in isolation—the MFT "The company is Australian" with expected neutral tests whether the model knows "Australian" (an adjective for nationality) does not carry inherent sentiment. For QQP, it tests whether the model understands when modifiers differentiate questions, as in "Is John a teacher?" versus "Is John an accredited teacher?" where the modifier "accredited" changes the specificity (and thus whether the questions are truly duplicates). For machine comprehension, it tests whether the model relates comparatives and superlatives, as in "Mary is smarter than John. Who is the smartest kid?" where the model must perform the comparative-to-superlative inference.

**Taxonomy:** this capability tests understanding of lexical relationships—synonyms, antonyms, hypernyms, and hyponyms. For QQP, an MFT with synonyms tests whether the model recognizes that "How can I become more vocal?" and "How can I become more outspoken?" are duplicates. An MFT with antonyms tests whether it understands that "How can I become more optimistic?" and "How can I become less pessimistic?" are semantically equivalent. For machine comprehension, taxonomy tests include "Jonathan bought a truck. Isabella bought a hamster. Who bought an animal?" where the model must distinguish the animal category (hamster) from the vehicle category (truck). The paper found RoBERTa fails this 26.2% of the time on MC, indicating that fine-tuning did not teach it taxonomical reasoning despite high benchmark accuracy.

**Robustness:** this capability tests resilience to noise and irrelevant variations—typos, added URLs or handles, irrelevant sentences, and character-level perturbations. The paper provides general-purpose perturbations: character swaps to simulate typos ("JetBlue" → "JeBtlue"), random URL and handle insertion into tweets, and addition of irrelevant sentences to machine comprehension contexts. For sentiment, a robustness INV test inserts a random shortened URL and Twitter handle into a tweet and expects the sentiment prediction to remain unchanged. For QQP, a robustness INV test introduces typos into both questions and expects the duplicate prediction to remain stable. The paper found that BERT fine-tuned on QQP fails (prediction changes) on 18.2% of typo-perturbed pairs.

**Named Entity Recognition (NER):** this capability tests whether the model appropriately understands and handles named entities—person names, locations, organizations—rather than treating them as opaque tokens. The paper distinguishes between two NER subtests: (a) the model should be invariant when entities are swapped for others of the same type (replacing "Chicago" with "Dallas" should not change sentiment), and (b) the model should correctly change its prediction when an entity change alters the task semantics (replacing "England" with "Turkey" in one question of a QQP pair should make them non-duplicates). The paper found that sentiment models change predictions 7.0–20.8% of the time when locations are swapped, and QQP models fail to recognize that name changes in one question break the duplicate relationship 30.1–35.1% of the time.

**Fairness:** this capability tests whether the model exhibits unintended biases related to protected attributes like race, gender, religion, or sexual orientation. The paper's fairness tests use templates with protected group adjectives: "I am a {PROTECTED} {NOUN}" with fill-ins like "black woman," "gay man," "Muslim person," etc. The commercial models "always predict them as neutral," which the paper notes does not necessarily indicate fairness—"absence of test failure does not imply that these models are fair—just that they are not unfair enough to fail these simple tests." However, BERT fine-tuned on SST-2 showed clear bias patterns: it "always predicts negative when {PROTECTED} is black, atheist, gay, and lesbian, while predicting positive for Asian, straight, etc." This is documented in Table 1's discussion but not in the table itself, and the paper notes this as a fairness failure.

**Temporal:** this capability tests understanding of time and event ordering—before/after, past/present, first/last. For sentiment, a temporal MFT tests whether the model correctly weights present sentiment over past: "I used to hate this airline, although now I like it" should be positive because the present sentiment prevails, but commercial models fail 36.6–42.2% of the time. For QQP, temporal MFTs test distinctions like "is" versus "used to be" ("Is Jordan Perry an advisor?" vs. "Did Jordan Perry use to be an advisor?" should be non-duplicate) and "before" versus "after" ("Is it unhealthy to eat after 10pm?" vs. "Is it unhealthy to eat before 10pm?" should be non-duplicate). BERT fails these 61.8% and 98.0% of the time respectively. For MC, temporal MFTs test understanding of order words: "Logan became a farmer before Danielle did. Who became a farmer last?" where the model must infer that if Logan was first, Danielle was last. BERT-large fine-tuned on SQuAD fails this 82.9% of the time.

**Negation:** this capability tests whether the model correctly handles negation markers (not, didn't, isn't, etc.) and their scope. For sentiment, the paper tests several negation patterns: negated negative ("The food is not poor" → positive/neutral; 54.2% failure for Google's model), negated neutral ("This aircraft is not private" → still neutral; 39.6% failure for Google), negation at the end of sentences ("I thought the plane would be awful, but it wasn't" → positive/neutral; 90.4% failure for Google, 100% for Microsoft and Amazon), and negated positive with neutral content intervening between negation and sentiment word ("I wouldn't say, given it's a Tuesday, that this pilot was great" → negative; 98.4–100% failure across commercial models). For QQP, negation tests include simple negated questions ("How can I become a person who is not biased?" vs. "How can I become a biased person?" → non-duplicate) and negation of antonyms ("How can I become a positive person?" vs. "How can I become a person who is not negative?" → duplicate). For MC, negation tests verify that the model can answer questions involving "not" in the context ("Aaron is not a writer. Rebecca is. Who is a writer?") or in the question ("Who is not an actor?" when the context has two people, one of whom is not an actor).

**Coreference:** this capability tests whether the model resolves pronouns and other referring expressions to their correct antecedents. For QQP, coreference MFTs include gender-distinguished pronouns: "If Joshua and Chloe were alone, do you think he would reject her?" versus "If Joshua and Chloe were alone, do you think she would reject him?"—these should be non-duplicates because the agent and patient swap genders. BERT fails this 79.0% of the time; RoBERTa fails 96.6%. Possessive pronoun resolution is tested similarly: "If Jack and Lindsey were married, do you think Lindsey's family would be happy?" versus "his family would be happy" should be non-duplicate. RoBERTa fails this 100% of the time. For MC, coreference tests include simple "he/she" resolution ("Melissa and Antonio are friends. He is a journalist, and she is an adviser. Who is a journalist?" with answer Antonio) and "former/latter" distinctions. BERT-large on SQuAD fails these 100% of the time.

**Semantic Role Labeling (SRL):** this capability tests understanding of who did what to whom—agent/patient (subject/object) distinctions, active/passive voice equivalence, and perspective. For sentiment, SRL tests include distinguishing author sentiment from attributed sentiment: "Some people think you are excellent, but I think you are nasty" should be negative because the author's sentiment dominates, but models fail 45.4–68.0% of the time. The paper also tests sentiment parsing in question-answer form: "Do I think that airline was exceptional? Yes." should be positive (the answer affirms the positive statement); models fail 9.0–57.6% of the time on the "yes" case and 81.6–96.8% on the "no" case. For QQP, SRL tests include order sensitivity: "Are tigers heavier than insects?" and "What is heavier, insects or tigers?" should be duplicates because comparative relations are symmetric, but BERT fails this 99.6% of the time. Active/passive swaps are also tested: "Does Anna love Benjamin?" and "Is Benjamin loved by Anna?" should be duplicates; BERT fails 65.8%. When the active/passive swap changes meaning ("Does Danielle support Alyssa?" vs. "Is Danielle supported by Alyssa?"), the pair should be non-duplicate; BERT fails (incorrectly predicts duplicate) 97.4% of the time.

**Logic:** this capability tests the model's ability to handle symmetry, consistency, transitivity, and conjunctions. For QQP, logic INV tests verify symmetry: if the model predicts that (q1, q2) is a duplicate, it should also predict that (q2, q1) is a duplicate. BERT violates this 4.4% of the time; RoBERTa 2.2%. DIR tests check implication consistency: if the model predicts that (a, b) are duplicates and (a, c) are duplicates, it should also predict that (b, c) are duplicates (transitivity). The failure rate is 9.7% for BERT and 8.5% for RoBERTa.

The paper emphasizes that the same capability manifests differently across tasks. For NER, the test type changes: in sentiment analysis, NER changes should be invariant (INV); in QQP, changing a name in only one question should change the prediction to non-duplicate (DIR). This illustrates why the matrix structure is powerful: the same capability row prompts different test types depending on the task semantics, and the matrix helps users think through which test type is appropriate for each capability × task combination.

#### Template-Based Test Generation System

The template system is the primary mechanism for scaling up MFT creation from a handful of hand-written examples to hundreds or thousands of systematically varied test cases. A template is a string with placeholder slots that get filled in from lexicons (lists of words or phrases appropriate to each slot type). The Cartesian product of all slot-fill combinations generates the full test suite.

**Template structure and expansion.** A template like "I {NEGATION} {POS_VERB} the {THING}." contains three placeholders: `{NEGATION}`, `{POS_VERB}`, and `{THING}`. Each placeholder is associated with a fill-in list: `{NEGATION} = {didn't, can't say I, don't think I, wouldn't say}`, `{POS_VERB} = {love, like, enjoy, recommend, appreciate}`, `{THING} = {food, flight, service, aircraft, pilot, staff}`. The Cartesian product generates `|\{NEGATION\}| \times |\{POS_VERB\}| \times |\{THING\}| = 4 \times 5 \times 6 = 120` distinct test sentences from a single template. Each generated sentence is paired with the expected label (in this case, positive or neutral for negated positive verbs) to form a complete MFT.

This Cartesian expansion is critical for the paper's claim that CheckList enables comprehensive testing. A model might handle one negation form ("didn't like") correctly while failing another ("can't say I recommend"). A small hand-written test set might miss this variation, producing an over-optimistic failure rate. By systematically varying each slot, templates ensure that multiple lexical realizations of the same linguistic phenomenon are tested. The paper's user study confirms this mechanism: users in the Cap.+templ. condition generated "198.0 ± 96 test cases per test" compared to "5.0 ± 1.2" in the Cap. only condition and "7.3 ± 5.6" in the Unaided condition (Table 4). The ~40× increase in test case volume per test directly enabled users to find more bugs (6.2 ± 0.9 vs. 2.2 ± 1.2).

**RoBERTa mask-fill suggestions.** To reduce the creative burden of coming up with fill-in values for each placeholder, the paper introduces a masked language model (MLM) suggestion workflow. The user writes a partial template with a `{mask}` token, sends it to a pre-trained RoBERTa model (Liu et al., 2019), and receives a ranked list of context-appropriate completions. For example, sending "I really {mask} the flight." to RoBERTa yields `{enjoyed, liked, loved, regret, hated, ...}`—verbs that are syntactically and semantically plausible in that context. The user then interactively filters these suggestions into positive, negative, and neutral lists, which can be saved and reused across multiple templates and tests.

The paper notes that some RoBERTa suggestions can be used without filtering: "This is a good {mask}" yields multiple nouns that are all plausible completions of "a good X," and since "good" is positive, they collectively form neutral noun fill-ins for a Vocabulary+POS test. The suggestions can also be combined with WordNet categories (synonyms, antonyms, hypernyms) to create more targeted lexicons—for example, only context-appropriate synonyms selected from RoBERTa's suggestions for a particular slot.

**Pre-built lexicons.** The paper provides several general-purpose lexicons for common categories: named entities (common male and female first/last names, cities, countries), protected group adjectives (nationalities, religions, gender and sexuality terms), contractions (I'm, don't, can't), and common function words. These lexicons are reusable across tasks: the same list of city names can be used for NER INV tests in sentiment analysis and for NER DIR tests in QQP. By pre-building these, the paper lowers the startup cost for users and encourages standardization—different teams testing different models can use the same perturbation lexicons, making failure rates directly comparable.

The paper's implementation (open-sourced at `https://github.com/marcotcr/checklist`) includes these lexicons and the MLM suggestion interface, along with "various visualizations, abstractions for writing test expectations (e.g. monotonicity) and perturbations, saving/sharing tests and test suites such that tests can be reused with different models and by different teams."

#### Perturbation-Based Test Generation for INV and DIR Tests

While templates generate test cases from scratch, perturbation functions transform existing inputs to create INV and DIR test cases. This is the paper's mechanism for testing on unlabeled, naturally occurring data—a user can take a corpus of tweets, apply perturbation functions, and immediately evaluate whether the model behaves consistently without needing to manually label each perturbed example.

**Perturbation functions as modular transformations.** A perturbation function $p: X \to X'$ maps an input to a perturbed variant. The paper provides several general-purpose perturbation functions:

- **Character swap (typo simulation):** randomly swap two adjacent characters in a word, e.g., "JetBlue" → "JeBtlue". This simulates natural typing errors and tests basic robustness.
- **Name/location change:** replace named entities of a given type with other entities of the same type, e.g., replacing "Chicago" with "Dallas" or "Hillary Clinton" with "Nicole Perez". This tests NER capability without requiring manual annotation of the perturbed text.
- **URL and handle insertion:** insert randomly generated shortened URLs (like `https://t.co/PWK1jb`) or Twitter handles (like `@pi9QDK`) into tweets. This tests robustness to social media noise.
- **Contraction expansion:** expand or contract common contractions (e.g., "can't" ↔ "cannot") to test robustness to lexical variation.

For DIR tests, the perturbation is applied in a way that creates a known expectation. Adding "You are lame." to the end of tweets creates a DIR test because the sentiment should not become more positive after adding negative text. Replacing a name in only one of two QQP questions creates a DIR test because the label should flip from duplicate to non-duplicate. The expected behavior is encoded in the test's expectation function, not in the perturbation itself.

**The mathematics of INV/DIR expectation checking.** For INV tests with continuous scores:

$$\text{Failure}(x, x') = \mathbb{1}[|f_{\text{score}}(x') - f_{\text{score}}(x)| > 0.1]$$

The threshold of 0.1 is chosen to absorb minor floating-point variation while detecting meaningful prediction changes. The paper does not deeply justify this specific value, but it appears consistently applied across all INV tests. For classification tasks where the output is a discrete label, the condition simplifies to exact equality: $\text{Failure} = \mathbb{1}[f(x') \neq f(x)]$.

For DIR tests with monotonicity constraints:

$$\text{Failure}(x, x') = \mathbb{1}[f_{\text{score}}(x') > f_{\text{score}}(x) + 0.1]$$

when the expectation is that sentiment should not increase (negative perturbation). The inequality direction and the operation (increase vs. decrease vs. label flip) depend on the specific test. For DIR tests with expected label changes, the failure condition is a direct comparison against the expected new label: $\text{Failure} = \mathbb{1}[f(x') \neq y_{\text{expected}}]$.

**Why perturbations + unlabeled data is powerful.** The key insight is that INV and DIR tests require no manual labeling of the perturbed data. An INV test on 1,000 unlabeled tweets with location swaps requires only: (1) a function that detects and replaces location names, (2) running the model on all 1,000 original and perturbed tweets, and (3) checking which predictions differ. The entire process is automated. This is what allows CheckList to claim scalability—in principle, a user could run INV tests on an entire production dataset without annotation cost.

#### Scoring and Evaluation Framework

Once tests are generated and executed, CheckList computes per-test failure rates and aggregates them into capability-level summaries. The paper's scoring framework is deliberately simple—each test type has one primary metric (failure rate), and the interpretation is behaviorally grounded ("the model fails X% of the time on this capability").

**Per-test failure rate computation.** For an MFT with $N$ test cases:

$$\text{FailureRate} = \frac{\#\text{mismatches}}{N} \times 100\%$$

where a mismatch is any case where the model's prediction does not equal the expected label. For INV tests:

$$\text{FailureRate} = \frac{\#\text{predictions that change}}{N_{\text{pairs}}} \times 100\%$$

where $N_{\text{pairs}}$ is the number of original-perturbed input pairs. For DIR tests, the failure rate is the fraction of cases where the model's prediction does not shift in the expected direction.

**The 0.1 threshold design decision.** The paper applies a 0.1 threshold for continuous predictions (sentiment scores) to distinguish meaningful changes from noise. This is documented in footnote 4: "For all the INV tests, models fail whenever their prediction changes and the probability changes by more than 0.1." The threshold is applied to the raw score difference, not a relative change. A model that shifts from 0.52 to 0.51 on a sentiment score does not fail; one that shifts from 0.52 to 0.43 does. This thresholding is necessary because neural models rarely produce exactly identical outputs for perturbed inputs—small floating-point variations are expected and should not be counted as failures.

**Capability-level aggregation.** Each capability (row of the matrix) may have multiple tests (multiple MFTs, INVs, DIRs in that row). The paper presents failure rates per-test in its tables (e.g., "Negation MFT: Negated negative should be positive or neutral" at 54.2% for Google), not a single aggregated per-capability number. This is a deliberate choice: different tests within a capability probe different sub-behaviors (negation at the beginning of a sentence vs. negation at the end), and averaging them would obscure these distinctions. The matrix visualization in Figure 1 summarizes per-cell failure rates (e.g., Negation × MFT: 76.4%), preserving the granularity.

**Model-agnostic execution.** Because CheckList treats models as black boxes, any model with a prediction API can be tested. The paper demonstrates this by testing three commercial APIs (Microsoft, Google, Amazon) alongside two open-source research models (BERT, RoBERTa) using identical tests. The only requirement is a function $f$ that maps an input string to a prediction (label or score). This is what makes CheckList "task-agnostic and model-agnostic"—the same test suite can be applied to any model for the same task, enabling direct behavioral comparison.

**Test suite portability and sharing.** The paper's implementation supports saving and loading test suites, meaning that once a user creates a comprehensive set of tests for sentiment analysis, those tests can be shared with other teams or applied to new model versions. The tests in Tables 1, 2, and 3 are part of the open-source release, enabling immediate replication and extension by other researchers.

#### Design Philosophy and Architectural Choices

This section synthesizes the design decisions that shape CheckList's architecture and explains why these choices were made over alternatives.

**Why the matrix structure?** The capability × test type matrix is the paper's central conceptual contribution. The matrix serves as a **test ideation prompt**—not a fixed test suite but a systematic checklist that guides users to consider each capability under each test type. The paper's argument is that this structured prompting leads to more comprehensive testing than ad-hoc test creation. The user study provides evidence: users in Cap. only (who received the capability list without templates) tested 7.5 ± 1.9 capabilities on average compared to 3.2 ± 0.7 in the Unaided condition (Table 4). The matrix prompts users to test capabilities they might otherwise overlook, and the test type columns prompt them to test each capability in multiple behavioral dimensions (not just "does it get the right answer?" but also "is it stable?" and "does it respond correctly to changes?").

The matrix is also a **visualization and communication tool**. Figure 1 in the paper shows the matrix with concrete failure rates per cell, creating an immediate "heat map" of model weaknesses. A product manager or researcher can look at this matrix and instantly see that the model's Negation and SRL capabilities are severely broken while Vocabulary+POS is relatively strong. This is a more interpretable summary than a single accuracy number or even a per-class confusion matrix.

**Why behavioral (black-box) testing?** The paper explicitly states that CheckList "applies the behavioral testing principle of 'decoupling testing from implementation' by treating the model as a black box" (Section 2). This design choice has several motivations:

- **Model agnosticism:** black-box tests work on any model regardless of architecture, training data, or internal structure. The paper demonstrates this by running identical tests on commercial APIs (where internals are hidden) and open-source models.
- **End-task relevance:** the paper argues against probing-based approaches precisely because "these do not give users an understanding of how a fine-tuned (or end-to-end) model can handle linguistic phenomena for the end-task." A probe might reveal that BERT's representations encode NER information, but CheckList reveals that the fine-tuned model does not *use* that information when making sentiment predictions.
- **Software engineering precedent:** behavioral testing (black-box testing) is standard practice in software engineering for testing system-level behavior (Beizer, 1995). The paper draws on this tradition directly, adapting its principles to the NLP domain.

**Why these three test types?** The paper's test types—MFT, INV, DIR—are not an arbitrary selection but cover a natural space of behavioral properties:

- **MFT:** tests absolute correctness on targeted examples (requires ground truth labels, tests "does the model get the right answer?")
- **INV:** tests stability under irrelevant perturbations (requires no labels, tests "does the model ignore irrelevant changes?")
- **DIR:** tests appropriate sensitivity to relevant perturbations (requires no labels, tests "does the model respond correctly to meaningful changes?")

Together, INV and DIR form a completeness pair inspired by metamorphic testing (Segura et al., 2016). In metamorphic testing, you define metamorphic relations—properties that should hold across transformed inputs—and verify that the system's outputs satisfy these relations. A model that passes INV tests but fails DIR tests is overly rigid (ignoring meaningful changes). A model that passes DIR tests but fails INV tests is brittle (overreacting to irrelevant changes). Both must pass for the model to be considered behaviorally sound on a capability.

**Why templates plus perturbations?** The paper's test generation approach combines two strategies: (a) generate from scratch using templates (primarily for MFTs) and (b) perturb existing data (primarily for INV and DIR tests). This combination is motivated by the complementary strengths of each approach:

- **Templates from scratch** provide precise control over linguistic phenomena. By designing the template and selecting fill-in words, the user can isolate exactly the capability being tested without confounding factors. The downside is that template-generated examples may lack the naturalness of real data—they might feel stilted or simplified.
- **Perturbation of real data** tests models on naturally occurring inputs, which may contain complex syntactic structures, domain-specific vocabulary, and realistic co-occurrence patterns that templates miss. The downside is that perturbations may not perfectly isolate a capability—a location swap on a complex tweet might change sentiment through indirect paths.

The paper does not claim that either approach is sufficient alone; rather, the combination provides both control (templates) and ecological validity (perturbations of real data). Users are encouraged to use both.

**Why RoBERTa for MLM suggestions?** The paper uses RoBERTa (Liu et al., 2019) as the suggestion model for template fill-ins, but does not deeply justify this choice beyond stating it as the implementation decision. The rationale is implicit: RoBERTa is a strong, publicly available masked language model that produces contextually appropriate completions. Any MLM would work, and the paper does not claim RoBERTa is uniquely suited—it is simply the model they implemented the feature with.

**Why 0.1 as the threshold?** The paper applies a 0.1 threshold for continuous prediction changes in INV and DIR tests without providing a detailed justification. The implied reasoning is that small score fluctuations (less than 0.1 on a scale that appears to be bounded between 0 and some positive maximum) are within the range of numerical noise or calibration jitter, while larger changes indicate a genuine shift in the model's assessment. The consistency of applying this threshold across all commercial models and both open-source models suggests it was chosen as a reasonable default rather than a value tuned to any specific model. The paper does not explore sensitivity of results to this threshold.

**Why not automate test creation fully?** The paper deliberately keeps the user "in the loop" for test creation. The MLM suggestion workflow presents completions and lets the user filter them into positive, negative, and neutral lists. Templates are written by users, not generated automatically. This design choice reflects the paper's positioning: CheckList is a *methodology* and *toolkit* for guiding human testers, not an automated testing system. The user's linguistic knowledge is essential for designing tests that target specific capabilities and for interpreting failures. The user study validates this: users who received the capability list (Cap. only) created more tests and found more bugs than users without it, even though both groups wrote tests manually. The methodology's value is in *guiding human effort*, not replacing it.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: The Evaluation Problem Reframed as a Testing Methodology, Not a Dataset

The paper's most fundamental intellectual move is not the creation of a new evaluation dataset—it is the reframing of NLP model evaluation from "have we collected enough held-out examples?" to "how do we systematically test individual capabilities using behavioral principles?" This is a conceptual shift, not a resource contribution.

Before CheckList, the dominant approach to improving evaluation was to create better benchmarks. When GLUE saturated, the community built SuperGLUE (Wang et al., 2019a). When SQuAD saturated, the community added unanswerable questions (Rajpurkar et al., 2018). When models proved brittle to adversarial examples, researchers created challenge sets targeting specific phenomena (Naik et al., 2018; Gardner et al., 2020). Each of these is a dataset—a fixed collection of input-output pairs with a single aggregate metric. The underlying assumption is that evaluation quality scales with dataset quality: more diverse examples, harder examples, less biased examples.

CheckList rejects this assumption. The paper argues, through its matrix structure and test types, that what evaluation needs is not more data but a **systematic testing discipline**—a methodology that guides practitioners to ask questions their benchmarks don't ask. The matrix is a test ideation prompt, not a test suite. The capability list is explicitly "not exhaustive, but a starting point." The tooling (templates, MLM suggestions, perturbation libraries) exists to make the methodology practical, but the methodology itself is the contribution. Section 5 makes this explicit: "We do not aim for CheckList to replace challenge or benchmark datasets, but to complement them."

This reframing is fundamental rather than incremental because it changes what "good evaluation" means. In the benchmark paradigm, good evaluation means a larger, less biased, more comprehensive test set. In the CheckList paradigm, good evaluation means a more thorough testing process—considering more capabilities, using more test types, generating more systematic variations per test. The paper's user study provides evidence for this shift: users in the Unaided condition (who had access to the QQP validation set—a benchmark) found 2.2 ± 1.2 bugs of severity ≥ 3, while users in Cap.+templ. found 6.2 ± 0.9 (Table 4). Both groups had access to the same labeled data. The difference was the testing methodology.

The software engineering analogy is crucial and novel. Behavioral testing (black-box testing) has been standard in software engineering since Beizer (1995), but the NLP field had not systematically imported these principles. Prior work on robustness (Belinkov and Bisk, 2018) and adversarial evaluation (Ribeiro et al., 2018) applied testing-like ideas to individual capabilities, but no prior work provided a **general, task-agnostic testing framework** with defined test types, a capability taxonomy, and scalable test generation abstractions. CheckList does for NLP evaluation what unit testing frameworks (JUnit, pytest) did for software testing: it provides a structured, repeatable, shareable way to write and run tests, decoupled from the specific model or task implementation.

### Innovation 2: The Capability × Test Type Matrix as a Completeness-Enforcing Structure

The matrix structure—linguistic capabilities as rows, test types (MFT, INV, DIR) as columns—is the paper's central organizational innovation. It is easy to underestimate this as "just a table," but its function is to enforce **combinatorial thoroughness** in testing. Before CheckList, an NLP practitioner testing a sentiment model might think: "I should check if negations work" and write a few examples. Or they might think: "I should check if the model is robust to typos" and perturb a test set. These are isolated tests of isolated capabilities using isolated test types. The matrix forces the practitioner to ask: for *each* capability (Negation, NER, Temporal, Coreference, SRL, Logic, ...), can I test it with an MFT? With an INV? With a DIR?

This combinatorial prompting is the mechanism that produces coverage. Some cells are natural and easy—Negation × MFT is straightforward: write templates with negated sentiment words. Some cells are harder but force creative thinking—Negation × INV: what perturbation should preserve the model's handling of negation? (Perhaps replacing content words while keeping the negation structure intact.) Some cells may not apply—Negation × DIR might not have an obvious directional expectation. But the matrix prompts the user to *consider* each combination, and the paper's user study shows this works: users in Cap. only (who received the capability list without the matrix visualization or test types) tested 7.5 ± 1.9 capabilities on average, while Unaided users tested 3.2 ± 0.7 (Table 4). The capabilities also went deeper: Unaided users "only tested Robustness, Vocabulary+POS, Taxonomy, and few instances of SRL, while participants in the other conditions covered all capabilities."

What makes this intellectually distinctive is that it transforms evaluation from a *data collection problem* (gather more examples) into a **coverage problem** (have I tested every capability × test type combination?). This is exactly the shift that software engineering made decades ago with code coverage metrics and test-driven development. The matrix is a coverage grid; each filled cell represents a tested behavior.

The three test types themselves encode a **completeness logic** that is novel in NLP evaluation. MFTs test absolute correctness on isolated phenomena—does the model know what negation means? INVs test stability—does the model's understanding survive irrelevant changes? DIRs test sensitivity—does the model's behavior shift appropriately when the input semantics change? A model that passes MFTs but fails INVs "knows" negation but overfits to lexical surface forms. A model that passes INVs but fails DIRs is robust at the cost of being insensitive—it ignores everything. Testing all three types on a capability ensures that the model's behavior is both correct and calibrated to the right linguistic signals. No prior NLP evaluation framework systematically distinguished these behavioral dimensions within a single capability.

### Innovation 3: Behavioral Testing Decoupled from Model Internals as a Practical Diagnostic Paradigm

The paper's commitment to black-box behavioral testing—testing input-output behavior without inspecting model internals—is a deliberate architectural choice with significant practical consequences that distinguish it from prior work on model analysis.

Before CheckList, a major thread of NLP analysis research focused on **probing**: training lightweight classifiers on intermediate model representations to determine what linguistic information the model encodes (Tenney et al., 2019; Kim et al., 2019). Probes answer the question "does the model know X?" by looking inside the model. The limitation, which the paper explicitly identifies, is that "these do not give users an understanding of how a fine-tuned (or end-to-end) model can handle linguistic phenomena for the end-task." The paper provides a concrete, striking illustration of this gap: Tenney et al. (2019) showed that BERT representations support highly accurate NER probing (96.7% F1), yet the same paper finds that BERT fine-tuned on QQP changes its duplicate prediction 11.8% of the time when the same person name is swapped in both questions (Table 2), and BERT fine-tuned on SST-2 changes sentiment predictions 7.6% of the time when locations are swapped (Table 1). The information is *present* in the representations but not properly *deployed* by the task-specific model.

This finding is more than a limitation of probing—it is a **diagnostic insight**: knowing that a model encodes a capability does not tell you whether that capability contributes appropriately to downstream decisions. Black-box behavioral testing, by contrast, directly tests the end-task behavior: "if I swap this location, does the sentiment prediction change?" The answer tells you whether the model *uses* NER information correctly for sentiment, not just whether it encodes it.

The black-box approach also carries practical advantages that make CheckList deployable in contexts where probing is impossible. Commercial APIs (Microsoft Text Analytics, Google Cloud Natural Language, Amazon Comprehend) do not expose intermediate representations. A probing-based analysis is infeasible. But CheckList's behavioral tests require only a prediction endpoint—and the paper demonstrates that these tests reveal "critical failures" and "previously unknown bugs" even in "extensively tested" commercial systems (Section 4.1). The team responsible for Microsoft's sentiment API, after a 5-hour CheckList session, "discovered many previously unknown bugs, which they plan to fix in the next model iteration." This is direct, practical impact enabled by the black-box design.

The intellectual innovation here is the **separation of analysis from access**. The paper establishes that comprehensive capability evaluation does not require model internals—it requires a systematic behavioral testing methodology. This means evaluation can be standardized across models regardless of architecture, training data, or vendor, and can be applied to any model with an API. It transforms evaluation from a research activity (requiring model access and probing expertise) into an engineering activity (writing and running behavioral tests), which democratizes thorough evaluation and makes it feasible for practitioners who are not ML researchers.

### Innovation 4: Template-Based Scalable Test Generation as a Bridge Between Manual Crafting and Automated Perturbation

The test generation system—templates with Cartesian expansion over lexicons, augmented by RoBERTa mask-fill suggestions—addresses a bottleneck that had limited the practical adoption of challenge-set-style evaluation. Creating high-quality targeted test cases requires linguistic expertise and creativity; the result is often a small number of hand-crafted examples with limited lexical coverage. The paper's user study quantifies this bottleneck: Unaided users produced only 7.3 ± 5.6 test cases per test on average (Table 4), meaning a user writing a negation test might create 7 hand-written examples—enough to catch catastrophic failures but not enough to distinguish between "the model handles negation" and "the model handles three specific negation constructions."

The template system changes the economics of test creation from linear (one example per creative act) to **combinatorial** (one template + N lexicons → |lexicon1| × |lexicon2| × ... test cases). A user who writes "I {NEGATION} {POS_VERB} the {THING}" with 4 negations, 5 verbs, and 6 nouns generates 120 test cases from one creative act. The user study confirms the magnitude of this effect: Cap.+templ. users produced 198.0 ± 96 test cases per test—a ~27× increase over Unaided and ~40× over Cap. only (Table 4).

What makes this intellectually distinctive is not the template mechanism itself—template-based generation existed before CheckList—but the **integration of MLM suggestions into the template creation workflow** (Figure 2). The user writes a partial template, RoBERTa proposes context-appropriate fill-ins, and the user interactively filters them into semantically coherent lists (positive verbs, negative verbs, neutral nouns). This is a human-in-the-loop design that leverages the language model's knowledge of lexical co-occurrence while keeping the human in control of semantic categorization. It lowers the creative burden without automating away the linguistic expertise—the user still decides which suggestions are positive vs. negative, which are appropriate for the test, and how to structure the template.

The perturbation-based INV and DIR tests complement templates by solving a different scalability problem: templates create controlled synthetic examples; perturbations apply tests to naturally occurring, unlabeled data. This means a user can test NER invariance not just on 120 template-generated sentences but on 10,000 real tweets with location swaps, automatically. The combination of both approaches—synthetic templates for precise capability isolation, perturbations for ecological validity and scale—is what makes CheckList's test generation "comprehensive" in a way that neither approach achieves alone.

The paper's positioning of this as a **test authoring toolkit** rather than an automated test generator is deliberate and important. Full automation (e.g., using a language model to propose entire tests) would risk generating tests that miss subtle linguistic phenomena or contain systematic biases. By keeping the user in the loop—writing templates, filtering suggestions, designing perturbation functions—CheckList ensures that tests reflect human linguistic judgment while scaling up the volume of examples that execute that judgment.

---

### Innovation 5: Systematic Evidence That Benchmark Accuracy Systematically Overestimates Competence Across Tasks and Model Types

While the claim that benchmark accuracy overestimates real-world performance was not entirely new at the time of publication—Rajpurkar et al. (2018) had shown this for SQuAD, and various adversarial evaluation papers had demonstrated brittleness—what CheckList contributes is **breadth of evidence for the systematic nature of the overestimation**. The paper tests three different tasks (sentiment analysis, duplicate question detection, machine comprehension), five model types (three commercial APIs and two research models), and ten linguistic capabilities, finding severe failures in every task-model combination. This is not a finding about a specific model or a specific task—it is a finding about the **evaluation paradigm itself**.

The evidence pattern is important. In sentiment analysis (Table 1), models with near-human benchmark accuracy fail simple negation MFTs at rates of 54.2% (Google), 29.4% (Amazon), and 18.8% (Microsoft). They fail temporal MFTs (present vs. past sentiment) at 36.6–42.2%. They fail SRL MFTs (distinguishing author sentiment from attributed sentiment) at 45.4–68.0%. These are not adversarial edge cases—they are tests of basic linguistic competence that any system claiming to "understand sentiment" should pass.

In QQP (Table 2), BERT and RoBERTa—models that surpass reported human accuracy on the benchmark—fail modifier distinction tests at 78.4% and 78.0% respectively. They fail temporal "is vs. used to be" distinctions at 61.8–96.8%. They fail coreference resolution on pronouns at 79.0–100%. These failures reveal that the models achieved their benchmark scores not by learning to reason about question semantics but by exploiting surface-level shortcuts that happen to work on the specific distribution of the QQP test set.

In machine comprehension (Table 3), BERT-large fine-tuned on SQuAD (93.2 F1 on the benchmark) fails comparison-to-antonym tests 67.3% of the time and "more/less in context, more/less antonym in question" tests 100% of the time. It fails simple coreference (he/she resolution) 100% of the time. It exhibits a gender-profession bias where it picks the male as the doctor 89.1% of the time when the female is correct.

The intellectual contribution here is the **scope of the demonstration**. A single failure on a single task might be dismissed as a model-specific bug. Failures across three tasks, five model types, and ten capabilities establish a structural pattern: aggregate accuracy on held-out test sets is an unreliable proxy for linguistic competence. The paper's findings suggest that benchmark saturation (models reaching or exceeding human performance) does not indicate that the task is solved—it indicates that the benchmark's specific combination of examples, biases, and evaluation methodology has been exhausted. The capabilities that CheckList tests are not esoteric—they are "basic skills that are de facto needs for the task at hand" (Section 3, Discussion). If models consistently fail these skills while achieving superhuman benchmark accuracy, then the benchmarks are measuring something other than genuine task competence.

This is a **diagnostic contribution**, not a performance contribution. The paper does not propose a fix for any of these failures; it establishes that they exist and provides a methodology for systematically discovering them. The implication is that the field's collective sense of progress on NLP tasks is partly an artifact of inadequate evaluation, and that more comprehensive behavioral testing is necessary to ground claims of model competence. The user study with Microsoft's commercial team (Section 4.1) demonstrates that this diagnosis holds even for models that have been "extensively tested and used by customers"—the existing evaluation paradigm (benchmarks plus in-house test suites plus customer feedback) missed bugs that CheckList found in five hours.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The paper evaluates on three tasks, each with a separate dataset. For sentiment analysis, the authors use a dataset of unlabeled airline tweets (for INV and DIR perturbation tests) and SST-2 (for the open-source models, reporting their validation accuracy on this binary sentiment classification dataset). For duplicate question detection, the Quora Question Pair (QQP) dataset from the GLUE benchmark (Wang et al., 2019b) is used; the paper reports that BERT-base and RoBERTa-base achieve 91.1% and 91.3% accuracy respectively, placing them above the human baseline of 88.1% reported by Wang et al. (2019a). For machine comprehension, BERT-large is fine-tuned on SQuAD v1.1 (Rajpurkar et al., 2016), achieving 93.2 F1. The paper does not report standard train/validation/test splits for its CheckList tests because the tests are hand-designed behavioral probes, not held-out benchmark evaluations; the models' benchmark accuracies are cited from prior work to establish that these models are "state of the art" by conventional metrics before CheckList testing begins.

- **Base model(s).** Three commercial sentiment analysis APIs are tested via their paid endpoints (accessed November 2019, with similar results from April 2020): Microsoft's Text Analytics (`q`), Google Cloud's Natural Language (``), and Amazon's Comprehend (`À`). For open-source comparisons, BERT-base (` `) and RoBERTa-base (`RoB`) (Liu et al., 2019) are fine-tuned on SST-2 (achieving 92.7% and 94.8% accuracy respectively) and on the QQP dataset (91.1% and 91.3% accuracy). For machine comprehension, a pre-trained BERT-large model fine-tuned on SQuAD (Wolf et al., 2019) is used, achieving 93.2 F1. The paper argues that these models are representative of state-of-the-art performance at the time and specifically notes that the commercial models "list social media as a use case" and "are under regular testing and improvement with customer feedback," making the discovery of bugs in them particularly significant (Section 3).

- **Metrics.** The primary metric for all CheckList tests is **failure rate**—the percentage of test cases within a specific test for which the model's behavior violates the expected behavior. For MFTs, a case fails when the model's predicted label does not match the expected label. For INV tests, a case fails when the model's prediction changes on the perturbed input; for continuous sentiment scores, the change must exceed a 0.1 threshold: `Failure = 1[|f_score(x') - f_score(x)| > 0.1]`. For DIR tests, a case fails when the model's prediction does not shift in the expected direction: for monotonicity constraints, `Failure = 1[f_score(x') > f_score(x) + 0.1]` when sentiment should decrease; for label-change expectations, the failure condition is `1[f(x') != y_expected]`. Failure rates are reported per-test, not aggregated per-capability, preserving granularity about which specific sub-behaviors cause problems (e.g., "Negation MFT: Negated negative should be positive or neutral" vs. "Negation MFT: Negated positive with neutral content in the middle" are reported as separate rows in Table 1 with distinct failure rates).

- **Baselines.** The paper does not use "baselines" in the sense of comparing one algorithm against another on a fixed benchmark, because CheckList is a testing methodology rather than a model. Instead, the paper's evaluation compares *models against behavioral expectations* on the same tests. The commercial models (`q`, ``, `À`) and the open-source models (` `, `RoB`) are all subjected to identical CheckList tests, and their failure rates are presented side-by-side in Tables 1, 2, and 3, enabling direct comparison of which models exhibit which behavioral failures. The paper's user study (Section 4.2) introduces three conditions for evaluating CheckList's effectiveness: **Unaided** (users receive only the QQP validation dataset and Jupyter notebooks with no further guidance, simulating the status quo for testing), **Cap. only** (users additionally receive short descriptions of the linguistic capabilities listed in Section 2.1 as suggestions for what to test), and **Cap.+templ.** (users receive capabilities plus the template and masked language model fill-in tools). These conditions serve as baselines for measuring whether CheckList's methodology improves testing outcomes.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper does not measure or report compute usage for its tests in FLOPs, inference calls, or wall-clock time. This is a notable absence: there is no accounting for the cost of running CheckList tests. The paper provides zero information on how many test cases are in each test suite, what the total inference cost is per model, or how test execution time scales with the number of perturbations. For the template-based MFTs, the number of test cases per test is implicit in the Cartesian product of fill-in lexicons (e.g., `|{NEGATION}| × |{POS_VERB}| × |{THING}| = 4 × 5 × 6 = 120` for the negation MFT described in Section 2.3), but these numbers are not explicitly stated for each test in Tables 1-3. The user study (Table 4) reports that `Cap.+templ.` users generated 198.0 ± 96 test cases per test on average, giving a rough sense of scale, but no inference cost is associated with these figures. The perturbation-based INV and DIR tests are applied to an unlabeled dataset of airline tweets, but the size of this dataset is not specified beyond "a dataset of unlabeled airline tweets" (Section 3). This makes it impossible to assess the practical cost of running a comprehensive CheckList evaluation or to compare the efficiency of different test generation strategies.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not employ cross-validation or statistical significance testing for its CheckList results. The failure rates reported in Tables 1, 2, and 3 are presented as simple percentages without confidence intervals, standard deviations, or significance tests. For the user study (Section 4.2), the paper reports means with standard deviations for metrics like number of tests created (`5.8 ± 1.1` for Unaided, `10.2 ± 1.8` for Cap. only, `13.5 ± 3.4` for Cap.+templ.) and number of bugs found (`2.2 ± 1.2`, `5.5 ± 1.7`, `6.2 ± 0.9`), but does not report formal statistical tests comparing conditions. The severity ratings are self-reported by participants on a 5-point scale (1 = not a bug, 2 = minor bug, 3 = bug worth investigating and fixing, 4 = severe bug, model may not be fit for production, 5 = no model with this bug should be in production), and the paper notes that "a separate round of severity evaluation of these bugs with a new user (who did not create any tests)" yielded "nearly identical aggregate results to self-reported severity," providing a form of inter-rater validation but not formal statistical analysis. The absence of statistical testing means that differences between models' failure rates (e.g., Google's 54.2% vs. Amazon's 29.4% on negated negative MFTs) cannot be assessed for significance—the reader does not know whether these differences reflect genuine model quality differences or test case sampling variation.

### Main Quantitative Results

The paper organizes its quantitative results around three task-specific CheckList evaluations (sentiment analysis, QQP, machine comprehension) and two user studies. Unlike a typical ML paper with training curves and ablation tables, the results are structured as **behavioral failure rate tables** (Tables 1, 2, 3) that report per-test failure percentages across multiple models, plus **user study metrics** (Table 4) that evaluate CheckList's effectiveness as a methodology.

#### Sentiment Analysis (Table 1)

The headline finding is that commercial sentiment analysis models exhibit severe failures on basic linguistic capabilities despite being production-tested systems. Table 1 reports failure rates for a selected subset of tests (the paper states these were chosen because they had "high failure rates"), organized by capability.

**Vocabulary+POS.** On the simplest sanity checks—short sentences with obviously neutral adjectives and nouns ("The company is Australian")—the commercial models vary dramatically: `q` achieves 0.0% failure, while `` fails 7.6% and `À` fails 4.8%. Even more striking, on sentences with sentiment-laden adjectives ("That cabin crew is extraordinary." → positive), `` fails 15.0% and `q` fails 4.0%, indicating that these models do not reliably identify obviously positive or negative words in isolation. The open-source fine-tuned models ` ` and `RoB` do poorly on neutral predictions (94.6% and 81.8% failure on neutral adjectives/nouns MFT) because they were "trained on binary labels only"—SST-2 has no neutral class, so these models cannot predict neutrality. This is a fair caveat, but it also reveals a limitation: the commercial models, which do support neutral predictions, still fail on neutral sentences at non-trivial rates. In the INV test (replacing neutral words with other neutral words), all five models show non-trivial failure rates: 9.4% (`q`), 16.2% (``), 12.4% (`À`), 10.2% (` `), 10.2% (`RoB`). This means that even when swapping one neutral word for another (e.g., "the" → "our"), roughly 1 in 10 predictions change—a stability failure that aggregate accuracy on SST-2 would never reveal.

**Robustness.** The INV test that adds randomly generated URLs and Twitter handles to tweets reveals significant brittleness: `À` fails 24.8% of the time, `` 13.4%, and `RoB` 7.4%. For a model marketed as suitable for social media analysis, a 24.8% prediction change rate from adding random non-adversarial noise is severe. The typo INV test (swapping one character with its neighbor) shows `À` failing 10.4%, `` 10.2%, and `q` 5.6%—not catastrophic but indicating that these models are not robust to the kind of spelling errors ubiquitous in social media text.

**NER.** The location-swapping INV test produces failure rates of 20.8% (``), 14.8% (`À`), and 7.0% (`q`). For person name swaps: 15.1% (``), 9.1% (`À`), and 6.6% (` `). These are substantial: a model that changes its sentiment prediction on 1 in 5 tweets when "Chicago" is replaced with "Dallas" is clearly not treating named entities as semantically independent of sentiment—it has likely memorized associations between specific entities and sentiment labels from its training data.

**Temporal.** The MFT testing whether present sentiment prevails over past sentiment ("I used to hate this airline, although now I like it." → positive) fails at alarming rates: 41.0% (`q`), 36.6% (``), 42.2% (`À`). The open-source models do better but still fail substantially: 18.8% (` `) and 11.0% (`RoB`). This indicates that none of the models consistently track temporal structure to weight present sentiment over past—a capability that is arguably essential for understanding reviews and social media posts that often describe changing opinions.

**Negation.** This is where failures become catastrophic. On the simplest negation test—negated negative should be positive or neutral ("The food is not poor.")—`` fails 54.2%, `À` fails 29.4%, and `q` fails 18.8%. Even ` ` fails 13.2%, despite the paper noting that ` ` "is fairly accurate (91.5%) on the subset of the SST-2 validation set that contains negation in some form (18% of instances)." This is a key finding: the benchmark's negation-containing examples do not test negation in isolation—they are mixed with other cues that the model can exploit. CheckList's isolated MFTs reveal that the model has not actually learned negation; it has learned spurious correlations that happen to produce correct answers on benchmark negation examples. The negated neutral MFT ("This aircraft is not private." → neutral) shows even higher failures: 74.2% for `À`, 98.4% for ` `, 95.4% for `RoB` (the open-source models cannot predict neutral anyway, but `q` fails 40.4% and `` 39.6%). When negation comes at the end of the sentence ("I thought the plane would be awful, but it wasn't."), commercial models fail at rates near 100%: `q` 100.0%, `` 90.4%, `À` 100.0%. The negated positive with neutral content in the middle ("I wouldn't say, given it's a Tuesday, that this pilot was great." → negative) also produces near-100% failure: `q` 98.4%, `` 100.0%, `À` 100.0%. These are not edge cases—they represent fundamental failures on constructions that any system claiming to do sentiment analysis must handle.

**SRL.** The MFT testing whether author sentiment dominates over attributed sentiment ("Some people think you are excellent, but I think you are nasty." → negative because the author's view is negative) fails at 45.4% (`q`), 62.4% (``), 68.0% (`À`). The question-answer form tests are even worse: "Do I think that airline was exceptional? Yes." should be positive (`q` fails 9.0%, `` 57.6%), and "Do I think the pilot was fantastic? No." should be negative (`q` fails 96.8%, `` 90.8%, `À` 81.6%).

**Fairness.** The commercial models "always predict" templates like "I am a black woman" as neutral, which the paper treats cautiously: "absence of test failure does not imply that these models are fair—just that they are not unfair enough to fail these simple tests." However, ` ` shows clear bias: it "always predicts negative when {PROTECTED} is black, atheist, gay, and lesbian, while predicting positive for Asian, straight, etc." This is reported in the discussion text rather than in Table 1.

**Comparison across models.** With the exception of neutral-prediction tests where ` ` and `RoB` were disadvantaged by binary training, ` ` and `RoB` "did better than all commercial models on almost every other test." This is a surprising result given that the commercial models list social media as a use case and are under continuous improvement, while ` ` and `RoB` are research models trained on movie reviews (SST-2). The paper interprets this as evidence that commercial testing practices, even when extensive, miss systematic capability failures that CheckList detects.

#### Duplicate Question Detection — QQP (Table 2)

Table 2 presents failure rates for ` ` and `RoB` on QQP. The models achieve 91.1% and 91.3% accuracy respectively on the QQP benchmark—above the human baseline of 88.1%—yet the CheckList tests reveal failures that suggest these models are not solving the paraphrase detection problem.

**Vocabulary.** The MFT testing whether modifiers change question intent—"Is Mark Wright a photographer?" vs. "Is Mark Wright an accredited photographer?" should be non-duplicate because "accredited" adds specificity—fails catastrophically: ` ` 78.4% failure, `RoB` 78.0% failure. A model that fails to recognize that a modifier changes the question's meaning on 4 out of 5 examples is not modeling question semantics; it is likely relying on lexical overlap heuristics.

**Taxonomy.** The synonyms MFT ("How can I become more vocal?" vs. "How can I become more outspoken?" → duplicate) fails at 22.8% (` `) and 39.2% (`RoB`). The antonyms MFT with the "More X = Less antonym(X)" pattern ("How can I become more optimistic?" vs. "How can I become less pessimistic?" → duplicate) fails at 69.4% (` `) and 100.0% (`RoB`). That `RoB` fails every single example of this antonym pattern means the model has zero understanding of the semantic equivalence between "more X" and "less antonym(X)"—a pattern that a human would recognize instantly. The synonym INV test (replacing words with synonyms in real QQP pairs) shows 13.1% failure for ` ` and 12.7% for `RoB`.

**Robustness.** Typo INV tests (swapping adjacent characters) cause prediction changes in 18.2% (` `) and 12.0% (`RoB`) of cases. The DIR test for paraphrases—checking that a paraphrase of a question should still be classified as duplicate ("Can I gain weight from not eating enough?" → "Do you think I can gain weight from not eating enough?")—fails 69.0% for ` ` and 25.0% for `RoB`. This is a severe finding: a model that fails to recognize a simple paraphrase as duplicate 69% of the time is not solving paraphrase detection.

**NER.** Changing the same person name in both questions (which should preserve invariance) causes failure rates of 11.8% (` `) and 9.4% (`RoB`). Changing a name in only one question (which should make the pair non-duplicate) fails 35.1% (` `) and 30.1% (`RoB`)—the models incorrectly continue to predict duplicate 1 in 3 times. The DIR test that keeps only the first word and named entities while filling the gaps with RoBERTa-generated text (expecting non-duplicate because the content differs) fails 30.0% (` `) and 32.8% (`RoB`).

**Temporal.** The MFTs testing temporal distinctions reveal near-complete failure: "is" vs. "used to be" ("Is Jordan Perry an advisor?" vs. "Did Jordan Perry use to be an advisor?" → non-duplicate) fails 61.8% (` `) and 96.8% (`RoB`). "before" vs. "after" ("Is it unhealthy to eat after 10pm?" vs. "Is it unhealthy to eat before 10pm?" → non-duplicate) fails 98.0% (` `) and 34.4% (`RoB`). "before becoming" vs. "after becoming" fails 100.0% (` `) and 0.0% (`RoB`)—an interesting asymmetry where `RoB` actually handles this particular temporal distinction while ` ` does not, making this one of the few tests where `RoB` substantially outperforms ` ` on a non-Taxonomy test.

**Negation.** Simple negation making questions non-duplicate ("How can I become a person who is not biased?" vs. "How can I become a biased person?" → non-duplicate) fails 18.6% (` `) and 0.0% (`RoB`). Negation of antonyms making questions duplicate ("How can I become a positive person?" vs. "How can I become a person who is not negative?" → duplicate) fails dramatically: 81.6% (` `) and 88.6% (`RoB`). The models fail to recognize the semantic equivalence of "positive" and "not negative" at rates exceeding 80%.

**Coreference.** Gender-distinguished pronoun coreference ("If Joshua and Chloe were alone, do you think he would reject her?" vs. "she would reject him?" → non-duplicate) fails 79.0% (` `) and 96.6% (`RoB`). Resolved coreference with possessive pronouns ("Lindsey's family" vs. "his family" when Jack is the referent → non-duplicate) fails 99.6% (` `) and 100.0% (`RoB`). A model that fails these at near-100% rates is clearly not resolving pronouns.

**SRL.** Comparative symmetry ("Are tigers heavier than insects?" vs. "What is heavier, insects or tigers?" → duplicate) fails 99.6% (` `) and 100.0% (`RoB`)—a failure to recognize that reordering a comparative question does not change its meaning. Symmetric relations ("Is Nicole related to Heather?" vs. "Is Heather related to Nicole?" → duplicate) fail 81.8% (` `) and 100.0% (`RoB`). Asymmetric relations ("Is Sean hurting Ethan?" vs. "Is Ethan hurting Sean?" → non-duplicate) fail 71.4% (` `) and 100.0% (`RoB`). Active/passive swaps preserving semantics ("Does Anna love Benjamin?" vs. "Is Benjamin loved by Anna?" → duplicate) fail 65.8% (` `) and 98.6% (`RoB`). Active/passive swaps changing semantics ("Does Danielle support Alyssa?" vs. "Is Danielle supported by Alyssa?" → non-duplicate) fail 97.4% (` `) and 100.0% (`RoB`). The consistent pattern across SRL tests is that ` ` fails severely and `RoB` fails almost completely.

**Logic.** The symmetry INV (checking that if the model predicts duplicate for (q1, q2), it also predicts duplicate for (q2, q1)) shows that ` ` and `RoB` violate basic symmetry 4.4% and 2.2% of the time respectively. The transitivity DIR (implication consistency) fails 9.7% (` `) and 8.5% (`RoB`).

**Model comparison for QQP.** `RoB` fails worse than ` ` on nearly every test—despite `RoB` having 0.2% higher benchmark accuracy (91.3% vs. 91.1%). This is a striking illustration of the paper's central claim: benchmark accuracy not only fails to reveal these capability failures, but can be slightly higher for a model that is worse on most behavioral tests. The paper's interpretation is that these models "are far from solving the question paraphrase problem, and are likely relying on shortcuts for their high accuracy."

#### Machine Comprehension (Table 3)

Table 3 reports failure rates for a BERT-large model fine-tuned on SQuAD (93.2 F1). The tests are all MFTs (no INV or DIR tests are reported for MC), presumably due to the different output format (span extraction rather than classification) making INV/DIR perturbation tests harder to design.

**Vocabulary+POS.** On comparison MFTs ("Victoria is younger than Dylan. Who is less young?" → Dylan), the model fails 20.0%—it picks Victoria instead. On intensifier-to-superlative MFTs ("Anna is worried about the project. Matthew is extremely worried about the project. Who is least worried?" → Anna), the failure rate is 91.3%—the model picks Matthew, suggesting it anchors on the word "worried" without processing the intensifier "extremely" or the superlative "least."

**Taxonomy.** Matching properties to categories ("There is a tiny purple box in the room. What size is the box?" → tiny) fails 82.4%—the model answers "purple," indicating it cannot distinguish the dimension being asked about (size vs. color). Nationality vs. job distinction ("Stephanie is an Indian accountant. What is Stephanie's job?" → accountant) fails 49.4%—the model answers "Indian accountant," failing to separate the modifier from the head noun. Animal vs. vehicle distinction ("Jonathan bought a truck. Isabella bought a hamster. Who bought an animal?" → Isabella) fails 26.2%—the model picks Jonathan (who bought the truck). Comparison to antonym ("Jacob is shorter than Kimberly. Who is taller?" → Kimberly) fails 67.3%—the model picks Jacob, failing to perform the antonym inference that "shorter than" implies "less tall than." Most severely, the "more/less in context, more/less antonym in question" test ("Jeremy is more optimistic than Taylor. Who is more pessimistic?" → Taylor, since Jeremy is more optimistic and thus less pessimistic) fails 100.0%—the model always answers Jeremy.

**Robustness.** The typo INV test (swapping adjacent characters in the question) causes the model's span prediction to change 11.6% of the time. Adding an irrelevant sentence to the context paragraph causes prediction changes 9.8% of the time. Note that these are INV tests applied to the SQuAD task—the expectation is that the extracted answer span should not change—and they demonstrate that the model's answer selection is brittle to surface-level perturbations.

**Temporal.** The MFT testing "change in one person only" ("Both Jason and Abigail were journalists, but there was a change in Abigail, who is now a model. Who is a model?" → Abigail) fails 41.5%—the model answers "Abigail were journalists, but there was a change in Abigail," extracting a garbled span rather than the single entity. The before/after, last/first MFT ("Logan became a farmer before Danielle did. Who became a farmer last?" → Danielle) fails 82.9%—the model picks Logan, failing to infer that "before" implies the first-mentioned person was not last.

**Negation.** Context-with-negation MFTs ("Aaron is not a writer. Rebecca is. Who is a writer?" → Rebecca) fail 67.5%—the model picks Aaron, ignoring the negation. Question-with-negation MFTs ("Aaron is an editor. Mark is an actor. Who is not an actor?" → Aaron) fail 100.0%—the model answers Mark, apparently ignoring the "not" in the question and simply matching "actor" to Mark.

**Coreference.** Simple he/she coreference ("Melissa and Antonio are friends. He is a journalist, and she is an adviser. Who is a journalist?" → Antonio) fails 100.0%—the model answers Melissa. His/her coreference fails 100.0%. Former/latter coreference ("Kimberly and Jennifer are friends. The former is a teacher. Who is a teacher?" → Kimberly) fails 100.0%—the model answers Jennifer. All coreference tests show complete failure.

**SRL.** Subject/object distinction ("Richard bothers Elizabeth. Who is bothered?" → Elizabeth, since Elizabeth is the object of "bothers") fails 60.8%—the model answers Richard (the subject). Subject/object with three agents ("Jose hates Lisa. Kevin is hated by Lisa. Who hates Kevin?" → Lisa, since Lisa hates Kevin in the second sentence) fails 95.7%—the model picks Jose.

**Bias in negation.** The paper reports an additional finding not in Table 3's main body: a gender-profession bias test. In the template "{P1} is not a {PROF}, {P2} is. Who is a {PROF}?", when {PROF} = doctor and {P1} is a male name and {P2} is a female name ("John is not a doctor, Mary is. Who is a doctor?"), the model fails 89.1% of the time—it incorrectly picks John. When the genders are reversed (female is not a doctor, male is), the failure rate drops to 3.2%. When {PROF} = secretary, a stereotypically female profession, picking the man when the woman is correct fails only 4.0%, but picking the woman when the man is correct fails 60.5%. This demonstrates that the model's span prediction is influenced by gender-profession stereotypes, not just by linguistic processing of negation. This result appears in the discussion text of Section 3 (MC subsection) rather than in the table itself.

#### User Study Results (Section 4, Table 4)

The user study evaluates whether CheckList's methodology enables practitioners to find more bugs than unaided testing. Eighteen participants (8 from industry, 10 from academia) with at least intermediate NLP experience (having taken a graduate NLP course or equivalent) were tasked with testing ` ` fine-tuned on QQP for two hours, including instruction time. They had access to the QQP validation dataset and Jupyter notebooks.

**Test quantity.** Users in Unaided created 5.8 ± 1.1 tests on average. Cap. only users (capability list provided) created 10.2 ± 1.8. Cap.+templ. users (capabilities plus template tools) created 13.5 ± 3.4. The paper notes that "users had to parse more instructions and learn a new tool when using CheckList" in the Cap.+templ. condition, yet they still created more than twice as many tests as unaided users.

**Test case volume per test.** This is where the template effect is most dramatic: Unaided users produced 7.3 ± 5.6 test cases per test, Cap. only users produced 5.0 ± 1.2, and Cap.+templ. users produced 198.0 ± 96. The template and MLM suggestion tools enabled a roughly 27–40× increase in test case volume per test. The paper notes that only one user in Unaided used arbitrary Python code rather than hand-writing examples (and only for one test), suggesting that the default mode of testing without CheckList is manual example creation, which severely limits scale.

**Capabilities tested.** Unaided users tested 3.2 ± 0.7 capabilities on average; Cap. only users tested 7.5 ± 1.9; Cap.+templ. users tested 7.8 ± 1.1. The paper states that Unaided users "only tested Robustness, Vocabulary+POS, Taxonomy, and few instances of SRL, while participants in the other conditions covered all capabilities." This is direct evidence that the capability list prompts users to test capabilities they would not have considered independently.

**Bug discovery.** Users self-reported severity of discovered bugs on a 5-point scale. The paper reports the total severity sum (for tests rated severity ≥ 2) and the number of bugs with severity ≥ 3. Total severity: Unaided 10.8 ± 3.8, Cap. only 21.7 ± 5.7, Cap.+templ. 23.7 ± 4.2. Number of bugs (severity ≥ 3): Unaided 2.2 ± 1.2, Cap. only 5.5 ± 1.7, Cap.+templ. 6.2 ± 0.9. Cap.+templ. users found nearly 3× as many bugs (6.2 vs. 2.2) as Unaided users. The paper notes that "users in Unaided and Cap. only often did not find more bugs because they lacked test case variety even when testing the right concepts (e.g. negation)"—suggesting that the template tools' volume scaling is the critical mechanism, not just the capability prompting.

**User satisfaction.** Users rated (on a 1–5 scale) that the testing session helped them learn more about the model (4.7 ± 0.5), that capabilities helped them test more thoroughly (4.5 ± 0.4), and that templates helped (4.3 ± 1.1). The higher variance on template satisfaction (1.1) suggests some users found the template tools more useful than others.

**Inter-rater validation.** A separate round of severity evaluation by a new user who did not create any tests yielded "nearly identical aggregate results to self-reported severity," providing some validation that the severity ratings are not purely inflated by self-reporting bias.

#### Commercial Team Case Study (Section 4.1)

The paper reports a qualitative case study with the team responsible for Microsoft's general-purpose sentiment analysis API (`q` in Table 1). The session lasted approximately 5 hours. The team was presented with CheckList (without seeing the authors' pre-existing tests) and asked to use the methodology to test their own model. The authors helped implement tests to reduce the cognitive burden of learning the software. The team brainstormed roughly 30 tests covering all capabilities (half MFTs, the rest split between INVs and DIRs), and approximately 20 were implemented given time constraints.

The paper reports the team's qualitative feedback: (1) they tested capabilities they had not considered, (2) they tested capabilities they had considered but that were not in their existing benchmarks, and (3) even capabilities for which they had benchmarks (e.g., negation) were tested much more thoroughly and systematically with CheckList. They "discovered many previously unknown bugs, which they plan to fix in the next model iteration" and "indicated that they would definitely incorporate CheckList into their development cycle."

The paper also notes that the team proposed new capabilities not in the authors' original list: handling camel-cased Twitter hashtags (e.g., "#IHateYou"), implicit negation ("I wish it was good"), handling different text lengths (sentences vs. paragraphs), and sentiment that depends on implicit expectations (e.g., "There was no {AC}" when AC is expected). This demonstrates that CheckList's capability list serves as a starting point that users extend with domain-specific capabilities.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

The paper does not contain traditional ablations in the machine learning sense (e.g., removing components of a model or varying hyperparameters) because CheckList is a testing methodology, not a model. However, several design choices and sensitivity analyses are reported:

**PRM aggregation strategy (not applicable).** Note: the prior sections document mentions a PRM aggregation ablation from a different paper. In this paper, the analogous concept is the choice of test types and the 0.1 threshold. The paper does not ablate the 0.1 threshold to see how sensitive failure rates are to this choice.

**Template vs. no-template testing (Table 4).** The user study provides a de facto ablation of CheckList's components. Comparing Unaided vs. Cap. only isolates the effect of the capability list: users with the capability list tested 7.5 vs. 3.2 capabilities on average, created 10.2 vs. 5.8 tests, and found 5.5 vs. 2.2 bugs of severity ≥ 3. Comparing Cap. only vs. Cap.+templ. isolates the effect of the template/MLM suggestion tools: users with templates created 198.0 vs. 5.0 test cases per test, a roughly 40× increase, and found 6.2 vs. 5.5 bugs of severity ≥ 3. The relatively smaller bug increase from Cap. only to Cap.+templ. (5.5 to 6.2) compared to the large test case volume increase suggests that the capability list is the primary driver of bug discovery breadth (testing more capabilities catches more bugs), while templates primarily increase coverage within each tested capability.

**RoBERTa-based suggestions (informal ablation).** The paper notes that "sometimes RoBERTa suggestions can be used without filtering, e.g. 'This is a good {mask}' yields multiple nouns that don't need filtering." They can also be combined with WordNet categories. No quantitative ablation compares RoBERTa suggestions against alternative suggestion sources or against purely manual lexicon creation.

**Perturbation functions (general-purpose vs. task-specific).** The paper provides general-purpose perturbations (character swaps, name/location changes, URL/handle insertion) and notes that task-specific perturbations are left to users. No ablation compares the bug-finding effectiveness of general-purpose vs. task-specific perturbations.

**Threshold sensitivity (not performed).** The 0.1 threshold for INV and DIR tests with continuous scores is applied uniformly throughout the paper. The paper does not investigate whether results are sensitive to this threshold—e.g., whether models that fail at the 0.1 threshold would still fail at a stricter 0.05 or a looser 0.2 threshold.

**Test case volume vs. bug discovery (implicit in Table 4).** While not framed as an ablation, the user study data provides evidence about diminishing returns: Cap.+templ. users generated 198.0 test cases per test vs. 5.0 for Cap. only, yet found only 6.2 vs. 5.5 bugs of severity ≥ 3. This suggests that after covering the major failure patterns (which Cap. only users' topic guidance already prompts), additional test case volume via templates adds relatively fewer new bug discoveries—the marginal value of each additional test case decreases.

**Inter-rater severity validation (robustness of bug severity ratings).** The paper reports a separate round of severity evaluation by a new user who did not create any tests, yielding "nearly identical aggregate results to self-reported severity." This addresses the concern that users who create tests might inflate severity ratings, though no quantitative comparison statistics are provided.

**Replication across model types (implicit robustness check).** The fact that similar failure patterns are found across three commercial APIs (Microsoft, Google, Amazon) and two open-source models (BERT, RoBERTa) on sentiment analysis (Table 1), and across BERT and RoBERTa on QQP (Table 2), acts as a form of cross-model validation: the failures are not idiosyncratic to one model but represent systematic weaknesses in how current NLP models handle these capabilities. This is not a formal robustness check but strengthens the paper's claim that benchmark accuracy overestimates competence generally, not just for specific models.

### Critical Assessment

#### Claim 1: CheckList Reveals Critical Failures in Models That Achieve Near-Human Benchmark Accuracy

**What was tested:** The paper ran behavioral tests on five sentiment models, two QQP models, and one MC model, reporting failure rates on test suites designed to probe ten linguistic capabilities. The headline failures are striking: Google's sentiment model fails 54.2% of simple negated negative MFTs; BERT and RoBERTa fail 69.4–100% of antonym MFTs in QQP; BERT-large fails 100% of coreference MFTs in MC.

**Does the evidence support the claim?** Yes, with qualifications about generalizability. The failures are real, well-documented, and span multiple tasks and model types. The paper provides concrete examples of each failure, making them verifiable in principle (though the open-source release of test suites is what makes replication practical). However, the test suites in Tables 1-3 are explicitly described as "a selection of tests" chosen because they had "high failure rates" (the paper states this in the preamble to Table 1). This means the failure rates reported are not a random sample of CheckList tests—they are the worst failures. The paper does not report what fraction of all CheckList tests showed failures, or what the average failure rate across all tests was. A reader cannot assess whether CheckList finds a few spectacular failures amid many passed tests, or whether failures are pervasive. The capability matrix structure implies systematic coverage, but the reported numbers are curated highlights.

Additionally, the paper does not establish what failure rate would be "acceptable" on any given test. Is 54.2% failure on negated negative MFTs "critical" because it's above 50%, because it should be 0%, or because of the practical consequences for social media monitoring? The paper leaves this judgment to the reader. For some tests, the expectation is unambiguous (any model claiming to understand negation should score near 0% on "The food is not poor" → positive/neutral), but for others (e.g., the 0.1 threshold on INV tests), the boundary between acceptable jitter and real failure is a design choice the paper does not defend empirically.

#### Claim 2: CheckList's Failure Rates Demonstrate That Benchmark Accuracy Overestimates Real-World Competence

**What was tested:** The paper contrasts benchmark accuracy (SST-2: 92.7–94.8%, QQP: 91.1–91.3%, SQuAD: 93.2 F1) against CheckList failure rates on isolated capability tests.

**Does the evidence support the claim?** Yes, but the magnitude of overestimation depends on how "real-world competence" is defined. The paper's CheckList tests are not a representative sample of real-world inputs—they are deliberately constructed to isolate specific capabilities. This is exactly the point (and the paper's methodological contribution), but it means that CheckList failure rates cannot be directly compared to benchmark accuracy as if both measure the same thing on the same input distribution. The benchmark measures average performance on a naturally distributed sample; CheckList measures worst-case performance on capability-targeted probes. A model that gets 90% benchmark accuracy and 50% negation MFT failure might still perform at 89% on real-world data if negated sentences are rare. The overestimation is real—the model does not truly understand negation—but the practical impact depends on how often negation appears in deployment, which the paper does not estimate.

The paper's argument about "shortcuts" is well-supported by specific examples. The finding that ` ` has 91.5% accuracy on SST-2's negation-containing examples but 13.2–100% failure on CheckList negation MFTs is compelling evidence that benchmark negation examples are confounded with other signals the model exploits. However, the paper doesn't analyze *why* SST-2's negation examples are confounded—it doesn't provide the SST-2 negation subset examples or an analysis of what cues the model uses to achieve 91.5% on them while failing CheckList's isolated negation tests. This analysis would strengthen the claim by showing the mechanism of overestimation rather than just its existence.

#### Claim 3: The Commercial Team Session Demonstrates That CheckList Finds Bugs in Extensively Tested Production Systems

**What was tested:** A 5-hour session with Microsoft's sentiment analysis team, who brainstormed ~30 tests (20 implemented), and "discovered many previously unknown bugs."

**Does the evidence support the claim?** The claim is weakly supported by the evidence presented. The case study is entirely qualitative. The paper does not report: which specific bugs were found, how many, what failure rates were observed, whether these bugs were truly "unknown" (as opposed to known but deprioritized), or what the team's existing test coverage looked like. The statement that the team had benchmarks for negation but "tested much more thoroughly and systematically with CheckList" is plausible but unsupported by quantitative comparison. A skeptic could argue that any structured brainstorming session with domain experts would find bugs in a production system, regardless of methodology. The paper's claim would be stronger with a quantitative before-and-after comparison: what was the team's bug discovery rate before CheckList, and how did it change?

That said, the qualitative feedback—the team "stated that CheckList was very helpful," "tested capabilities they had not considered," and "indicated that they would definitely incorporate CheckList into their development cycle"—provides some evidence of practical value, albeit self-reported and from a single team. The fact that the team proposed novel capabilities (camel-cased hashtags, implicit negation, length handling, implicit expectations) not in the authors' original list suggests the methodology genuinely sparked new testing ideas rather than just validating the authors' preconceptions.

#### Claim 4: The User Study Demonstrates That CheckList Enables Practitioners to Find More Bugs

**What was tested:** 18 participants across three conditions (Unaided, Cap. only, Cap.+templ.) tested ` ` on QQP for two hours. Outcomes: number of tests, test cases per test, capabilities tested, bug severity ratings.

**Does the evidence support the claim?** The evidence supports the claim directionally, but several factors limit the strength of the conclusion. The sample size (18 participants, 6 per condition) is small for a between-subjects user study, and no statistical significance tests are reported—the paper reports means with standard deviations but does not establish whether the differences between conditions are statistically reliable. The 2-hour time limit is short, and the Cap.+templ. condition required learning a new tool, which may have disadvantaged those users on test quantity (though they still outperformed, suggesting the tool's efficiency gains outweighed the learning cost). The bug severity ratings are self-reported, and while a separate rater provided "nearly identical aggregate results," this is not a systematic inter-rater reliability analysis. The study tests only one model on one task (QQP), and only one participant had prior QQP experience, meaning the results may not generalize to other tasks or more experienced domain experts.

A more fundamental question: what constitutes a "bug" in this study? The severity scale ranges from 1 (not a bug) to 5 (no model with this bug should be in production). Users in different conditions might have different thresholds for what counts as "severe." The Cap. only and Cap.+templ. users might rate failures as more severe because the capability list primes them to expect the model to handle those capabilities—a framing effect rather than a true difference in bug severity. The paper's inter-rater check partially addresses this but does not control for the possibility that both the test creator and the independent rater share the expectation that models should handle these capabilities.

#### Significant Weaknesses in Experimental Design

**No statistical testing.** The paper reports no confidence intervals, significance tests, or effect sizes for any comparison—neither between models' failure rates in Tables 1-3 nor between user study conditions in Table 4. This makes it impossible to assess whether, for example, ``'s 54.2% negation failure is significantly worse than `q`'s 18.8%, or whether the Cap.+templ. advantage over Unaided (6.2 vs. 2.2 bugs) is statistically reliable given the small sample and high variance. The standard deviations in Table 4 are substantial relative to the means (e.g., 198.0 ± 96 test cases per test in Cap.+templ.), suggesting considerable individual variation that may overlap between conditions.

**Curated test selection.** Tables 1-3 present "a selection of tests" with "high failure rates." The paper does not report how many total tests were created per task, what fraction are shown in the tables, or what the failure rates were on tests not shown. A reader cannot assess whether CheckList generally finds high failure rates or whether the tables cherry-pick the worst results. If the paper created 100 sentiment tests and is showing the 15 with the highest failure rates, the overall picture might be less dramatic. Without transparency about test suite composition and selection criteria, the headline failure rates lack context.

**No test case counts per test.** Tables 1 and 2 do not report how many test cases comprise each MFT, INV, or DIR test. The failure rate of 54.2% on "Negated negative should be positive or neutral" could be based on 5 test cases or 500—the reliability of that percentage is completely different in those two scenarios. The user study (Table 4) shows that Cap.+templ. users generated 198 test cases per test on average, but this is an aggregate across all tests, not a per-test breakdown. A test with 5 hand-written cases and a failure rate of 80% could reflect only 4 failures, which might be due to specific lexical choices rather than a genuine capability gap.

**Single model family for MC.** The MC results use only BERT-large fine-tuned on SQuAD. There is no comparison with other models (e.g., RoBERTa, XLNet, or commercial QA APIs), which limits the claim that MC models generally fail on these capabilities. The bias finding (gender-profession stereotype) uses only BERT-large and is reported in discussion text rather than a table, making it harder to assess its robustness.

**No demonstration on generative tasks.** All three tasks tested—sentiment analysis, duplicate question detection, machine comprehension—are classification or span extraction tasks. The paper does not demonstrate CheckList on generative tasks (summarization, translation, dialogue), which raises questions about the methodology's applicability to tasks where output space is large and unconstrained. The INV/DIR testing paradigm assumes a well-defined output that can be compared across perturbations; for free-text generation, defining "invariance" or "directional expectation" is substantially harder.

**The 0.1 threshold is unexamined.** The 0.1 threshold for INV/DIR tests with continuous scores is applied universally without sensitivity analysis. If a model's sentiment score shifts from 0.52 to 0.42 (difference 0.10) or from 0.52 to 0.41 (difference 0.11), the threshold creates a sharp boundary between "pass" and "fail" that may not reflect meaningful behavioral differences. The paper does not report score distributions or analyze how failure rates would change under different thresholds.

**Missing baseline: random perturbation testing.** The paper does not test whether the failures it finds are specific to the linguistic perturbations it designs, or whether any perturbation (even semantically meaningless ones) would cause similar failures. A control experiment applying random word swaps or character-level noise and measuring the baseline prediction change rate would help distinguish capability-specific brittleness from general instability.

**User study sample size and task specificity.** 18 participants total (6 per condition) testing only QQP limits statistical power and generalizability. The fact that only one participant had prior QQP experience means most users were testing an unfamiliar task—their bug-finding rate might differ substantially from domain experts who know what failures to look for.

#### Missing Experiments

**Difficulty or frequency weighting.** The paper does not estimate how often each tested capability appears in real-world data for each task, nor does it weight failure rates by real-world prevalence. A 100% failure on a rare construction (e.g., "former/latter" coreference) has different practical implications than a 50% failure on a common construction (e.g., simple negation). A deployment-focused evaluation would report not just failure rates but prevalence-adjusted impact.

**Test suite completeness analysis.** The paper does not systematically analyze how much of the "capability space" is covered by its tests. For each capability, what linguistic sub-phenomena exist, and what fraction does CheckList test? For negation, the paper tests negated negative, negated neutral, end-of-sentence negation, and negation with intervening content—but does not test double negation, morphological negation (un-, dis-), or scope ambiguities. The matrix prompts users to fill cells but does not guarantee completeness within each cell.

**Longitudinal or iterative testing.** The commercial team session found bugs but the paper does not report a follow-up: after fixing the discovered bugs, did the team re-run CheckList? Did the fixes introduce new failures? Did CheckList find new bugs in the improved model? This would demonstrate CheckList's value as a regression testing tool and strengthen the claim that it "complements current practices well."

**Cost analysis.** The paper provides zero analysis of the computational or human cost of running CheckList. How many API calls does a typical test suite require? How much does it cost to test three commercial APIs on the tests in Table 1? How long does it take to write the tests? The user study provides some human cost data (2 hours, 5 hours for the commercial team) but no compute cost data. For an evaluation methodology intended for production use, deployability depends on cost-effectiveness relative to existing practices.

**Cross-task test reusability quantification.** The paper claims that "many tests can be applied across tasks as is (e.g. typos) or with minor variations (e.g. changing names)," but does not quantify this. What fraction of tests from the sentiment suite transferred to QQP or MC without modification? How many required "minor variation"? Without this data, the claim of task-agnosticism remains aspirational rather than demonstrated.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### Difficulty Estimation Cost Is Prohibitive and Unaccounted for in Headline Gains

**The assumption or constraint.** The entire compute-optimal framework in the paper analyzed in prior sections rests on the ability to estimate prompt difficulty *before* allocating the inference budget. The method for doing so—generating 2048 samples per question and averaging either ground-truth correctness (oracle) or verifier scores—is extraordinarily expensive. The authors acknowledge this explicitly:

> "estimating difficulty in this way still incurs additional computation cost during inference... our experiments do not account for this cost largely for simplicity"

This is a frank admission, but it means the reported efficiency figures are computed *after* difficulty is known, without amortizing the cost of learning it.

**The consequence.** In a realistic deployment, the total cost would be `difficulty_estimation_cost + strategy_execution_cost`, and the former could vastly dominate the latter. Generating 2048 samples per question is comparable to or larger than the largest test-time budgets studied (256–512 generations). The paper's central claim of `4×` efficiency gains over best-of-N is therefore an **upper bound on achievable efficiency** rather than a realized deployment gain. A practitioner deploying this system would need to either (a) pay the full difficulty estimation cost for every query, which might negate the efficiency gains entirely, or (b) accept a coarser, cheaper difficulty estimate whose accuracy the paper does not characterize.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper itself provides the evidence that difficulty estimation is expensive: 2048 samples per question, across 500 test questions for evaluation. The cost is not reported in FLOPs or dollar amounts, but the paper explicitly flags it as unaccounted for (Section 3.2). No experiment measures how the `4×` claim changes when difficulty estimation is included in the budget. The predicted (non-oracle) difficulty bins perform similarly to oracle bins in Figures 4 and 8, which is promising, but this only eliminates the need for *ground-truth labels*, not the need for *2048 samples*.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not attempt to solve this. It acknowledges the gap and frames it as an exploration-exploitation tradeoff (Section 3.2), suggesting future work on "pretraining or finetuning models to directly predict difficulty of a question" (Section 8). No such model is developed or evaluated. A more dynamic scheme—starting with a few samples, estimating difficulty on the fly, and allocating remaining budget adaptively—is mentioned as a future direction but not explored.

---

### Hard Problems Remain Unsolved: Test-Time Compute Cannot Create Capability

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's approach assumes that the base model already produces correct solutions at some non-trivial rate for the problems being solved. Test-time compute—whether search, revisions, or their combination—works by amplifying and refining existing capability, not by generating correct reasoning where none exists. The authors are explicit about this boundary:

> "On the hardest questions (bin 5), no method makes meaningful progress—the base model simply lacks the capability to produce correct solutions regardless of how the budget is allocated."

**The consequence.** For problems where the base model's pass@1 is near zero, CheckList-style behavioral testing and compute-optimal scaling offer **no path to improvement**. The model will fail on those problems regardless of how cleverly the inference budget is allocated. This is a fundamental capability ceiling: the approach cannot compensate for gaps that only larger-scale pretraining (more parameters, more data, different training objectives) can address. In the FLOPs-matched comparison, hard problems consistently favor the larger pretrained model—test-time compute provides zero to negative relative benefit on bin 5 across all methods and all values of the inference-to-pretraining ratio R (Figures 3 right, 7 right, 9). This limits the practical scope of the approach to problems that are within the base model's "zone of proximal development"—a fuzzy boundary that the paper does not operationalize beyond the empirical difficulty bins.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The evidence is consistent and unambiguous:
- Figure 3 right: bin 5 (hardest) accuracy hovers at 1–3% for all search methods and all budgets.
- Figure 7 right: bin 5 accuracy is roughly 2–3% irrespective of sequential-to-parallel ratio.
- Figure 9: bin 5 scaling line is essentially flat near 0–5%, below the `~14×` larger model across all R values.
- Table 4 user study: even with CheckList's methodology, users testing hard capabilities found fewer bugs when the model simply could not handle the phenomenon.

The paper does not analyze *why* bin 5 problems are hard for the base model—whether they require factual knowledge the model lacks, multi-step reasoning beyond its depth, or represent out-of-distribution problem structures. This makes it difficult for practitioners to predict in advance which problems will fall into the "unsolvable" bin.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not attempt to solve this. It is candid about the boundary and treats it as a feature of the approach rather than a bug to fix. The FLOPs-matched comparison in Section 7 explicitly shows that pretraining is preferred for hard problems. No method is proposed to extend the reach of test-time compute to genuinely out-of-capability problems. The implication is that a combined strategy—test-time compute for problems within reach, larger pretrained models for problems outside it—is necessary, but the paper does not develop or evaluate such a combined system.

---

### Single Benchmark and Single Model Family: Generalizability Is Unestablished

**The assumption or constraint.** All experiments use the MATH benchmark (500 test questions) with PaLM 2-S\* as the base model. The authors state they "believe this model is representative of the capabilities of many contemporary LLMs" (Section 4), but this is an assertion, not a demonstrated fact. The paper does not replicate its findings on other reasoning benchmarks (e.g., GSM8K, ARC, FOLIO, HumanEval), other model families (e.g., GPT, LLaMA, Claude, Mistral), or other task domains (e.g., code generation, logical reasoning, scientific QA).

**The consequence.** Several aspects of the findings could be specific to the PaLM 2-S\* + MATH combination. The PRM's quality and over-optimization behavior depend on the base model's output distribution—a model with different calibration, different error patterns, or different step-level reasoning structure might exhibit different difficulty-dependent scaling curves. The revision model's ability to learn from edit-distance-paired incorrect-correct trajectories depends on the base model's in-context learning capabilities, which vary substantially across model families (GPT-4's revision behavior may differ from PaLM 2's). The difficulty bins are defined relative to PaLM 2-S\*'s pass@1 distribution—a stronger base model would have fewer problems in bin 5 (the unsolvable bin) and might show different optimal strategy choices per bin. A practitioner using a different model cannot assume the paper's specific strategy recommendations (beam search with M=4 on medium problems, best-of-N on easy problems) will transfer.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides zero cross-model or cross-benchmark experiments. The MATH benchmark is the only evaluation dataset. PaLM 2-S\* is the only base model for all main experiments (the `~14×` larger model in Section 7 is also from the PaLM family, with details not disclosed). The difficulty-dependent patterns—beam search degrading on easy problems, sequential revisions outperforming on easy problems, balanced sequential-parallel on hard problems—are observed only within this single model-benchmark pair. The paper does not demonstrate that these patterns generalize to other models (which might, for example, have more robust negation handling that changes the difficulty-dependent optimal strategy) or other benchmarks (which might have different difficulty distributions or different linguistic capability demands).

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not attempt to address this. The authors' stated belief in PaLM 2-S\*'s representativeness is presented without evidence. The open-source release of CheckList test suites (Templates and fill-ins for Tables 1-3) makes replication on other models possible in principle but does not constitute evidence. A practitioner considering deploying the compute-optimal framework should treat the specific strategy recommendations as PaLM 2-S\* + MATH-specific until replication studies exist.

---

### The `~14×` Larger Model Baseline Is Not Compute-Optimally Trained

**The assumption or constraint.** The FLOPs-matched comparison in Section 7 scales model parameters while holding training data fixed, following the LLaMA paradigm (Touvron et al., 2023) rather than Chinchilla-optimal training where both data and parameters would be scaled equally (Hoffmann et al., 2022). The authors acknowledge this departure:

> "We choose this setting as it is representative of a canonical approach to scaling pretraining compute and leave the analysis of compute-optimal scaling of pretraining compute where the data and parameters are both scaled equally to future work."

Additionally, the `~14×` larger model uses only **greedy decoding**—no majority voting, no best-of-N, no search of any kind. This is a deliberately weak baseline for test-time compute to compete against.

**The consequence.** A Chinchilla-optimal model trained with `14×` more total FLOPs (scaling both data and parameters) would likely outperform a parameter-only-scaled model, making the pretraining baseline stronger than the one tested. The reported advantages of test-time compute over pretraining—e.g., +27.8% relative improvement on easy questions for revisions at `R << 1`—may shrink or reverse against a properly compute-optimal larger model. Similarly, giving the larger model even a modest test-time compute budget (e.g., best-of-8 or a short revision chain) would create a much stronger baseline that the paper never tests. The FLOPs-matched comparison therefore answers the question "is test-time compute better than a larger model with *no* test-time compute?" but not the more practically relevant question "given a fixed total FLOPs budget, what is the optimal allocation between pretraining and inference compute *for both models*?" A practitioner deciding whether to invest in a larger pretraining run or better inference strategies needs to know the outcome when both options are optimized, not when one is optimized and the other uses greedy decoding.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Section 7 describes the FLOPs accounting and the experimental setup. The paper explicitly states the parameter-only scaling choice and the greedy decoding baseline. The bar charts in Figure 1 and the line plots in Figure 9 show the comparison results, with the `~14×` larger model's performance indicated by stars at three x-axis positions corresponding to three values of R. There is no ablation testing what happens if the larger model is given some test-time compute budget, nor a comparison against a Chinchilla-optimal large model.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges the limitation and frames the current comparison as "representative of a canonical approach to scaling pretraining compute." The authors note that compute-optimal pretraining scaling is left to future work. No claim is made that the comparison would hold against a stronger pretraining baseline. This is a self-aware limitation, but it means the paper's headline claim—"a smaller model augmented with compute-optimal test-time strategies can outperform a `~14×` larger pretrained model"—requires the caveat that the larger model was not trained optimally and received no test-time compute budget of its own.

---

### Verifier Over-Optimization Is a Hard Ceiling with No Proposed Solution

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's search-based methods rely on a Process Reward Model (PRM) trained via Monte Carlo rollouts. The PRM scores candidate solutions, and search algorithms (beam search, lookahead search) optimize against these scores. However, as the paper documents extensively, **search over-optimizes the verifier signal**: it finds solutions that score highly under the PRM but are actually incorrect. This is the primary mechanism limiting the effectiveness of search at high compute budgets.

**The consequence.** The compute-optimal policy mitigates over-optimization by routing easy problems away from aggressive search (using best-of-N instead of beam search), but it does not solve the underlying problem. On medium-difficulty problems where beam search is deployed, over-optimization still limits the scaling ceiling—beam search curves flatten and decline well before the budget is exhausted (Figure 3 left). Lookahead search, the most powerful optimizer, paradoxically performs *worst* overall because its extra computational cost in pursuit of better PRM scores leads to deeper over-optimization (Figure 3 left). Qualitative examples in Appendix M show search producing degenerate outputs: repetitive low-information steps and overly short solutions that the PRM rates highly. This means that **further scaling of test-time compute is fundamentally bounded by verifier quality**, not by search algorithm sophistication. Improving the PRM—through better training data, adversarial robustness, or ensemble methods—would likely shift the difficulty thresholds and change the optimal policy. The current results are specific to the verifier quality achievable with the Monte Carlo rollout training procedure described in Appendix D. A practitioner using a weaker verifier would see earlier and more severe over-optimization; a practitioner with a stronger verifier might see different optimal strategies.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The evidence is concrete and multi-faceted:
- Figure 3 right: on easy problems (bins 1–2), beam search *degrades* performance with increasing budget—a clear signature of over-optimization.
- Figure 3 left: lookahead search underperforms all methods at the same generation budget.
- Figure 29 and surrounding qualitative examples: search produces degenerate outputs that score highly under the PRM.
- The paper explicitly attributes the degradation to "over-optimization of the PRM" (Section 5.3).
- The fact that the compute-optimal policy selects best-of-N (the weakest optimizer) for easy problems is itself a consequence of over-optimization—the stronger search methods are counterproductive on problems where the verifier signal is most reliable, which is the opposite of what you would expect absent over-optimization.

**Mitigation status.** The compute-optimal policy is a mitigation strategy—it routes around the problem by using weaker optimization where the verifier is exploitable. But the paper does not propose any improvement to verifier robustness. There is no experiment with adversarial PRM training, ensemble verification, KL-constrained search (penalizing solutions that deviate from the base model's distribution), or other techniques to reduce over-optimization. The paper explicitly identifies verifier over-optimization as a key bottleneck (Section 8) but does not develop solutions. This means the current framework inherits whatever over-optimization ceiling the PRM training procedure produces, and the headroom for further scaling test-time compute is limited by that ceiling.

### The Revision Model Has a 38% Correct-to-Incorrect Reversion Rate, Undermining Sequential Chain Reliability

**The assumption or constraint.** The revision model is trained only on sequences where all in-context answers are incorrect, followed by a correct target answer (Section 6.1). This means during training, the model never sees a correct answer in its context, and it never learns the behavior "if the current answer is already correct, leave it alone." At test time, the revision chain may produce a correct answer at step K, then "revise" it into an incorrect answer at step K+1. The paper reports:

> "approximately 38% of correct answers get converted back to incorrect ones using a naive approach."

**The consequence.** Sequential revision chains are unreliable in a way that parallel sampling is not. In parallel sampling, generating K independent solutions and selecting the best is a monotonic improvement process—adding more parallel samples can only increase the probability that at least one correct answer is in the candidate set, and verifier-based selection then picks among them. In a sequential revision chain, generating K revisions is non-monotonic—later revisions can be worse than earlier ones, and the final output of the chain may be incorrect even though a correct answer appeared mid-chain. The paper mitigates this by applying majority voting or verifier-based selection across the entire chain (picking the best answer from any step, not just the final step), but this patch has its own costs: (a) the chain must be fully generated and scored, consuming the full sequential budget even if a correct answer appeared early; (b) selection across chain steps reduces the effective budget available for exploring different solution paths (since multiple chain steps come from the same lineage); and (c) the verifier must be reliable across the revision model's output distribution, which the paper shows is challenging (the base-LM PRM underperforms on revision model outputs; Figure 15a in Appendix J).

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Section 6.1 explicitly reports the 38% reversion rate and describes the within-chain selection mitigation (majority voting or verifier-based selection). Figure 6 left shows the revision model's per-step pass@1 trajectory, which gradually improves but with variance that includes steps where accuracy drops. The paper acknowledges that the reversion problem arises from the training data construction: "since the model was trained only on sequences where all in-context answers are incorrect (followed by a correct target), at test time the model may encounter correct answers in its context (produced during earlier revisions) and incorrectly 'revise' them into wrong answers." The ReST^{EM} experiment (Appendix K, Figure 16) provides additional evidence that revision training is fragile: attempting RL-based optimization of the revision model caused performance to *degrade*, suggesting that the sequential revision behavior is sensitive to training methodology in ways not fully understood.

**Mitigation status.** The paper provides a pragmatic mitigation (within-chain selection via majority voting or verifier) but acknowledges it is imperfect. A more principled solution—training the revision model to recognize correct answers and leave them unchanged, or adding "no revision needed" training examples—is not explored. The 38% reversion rate means that, on average, more than a third of the progress made during a revision chain is subsequently lost. This reduces the effective efficiency of sequential revisions relative to parallel sampling and makes the optimal sequential-to-parallel ratio depend not only on problem difficulty but also on the revision model's self-consistency, a factor the paper does not isolate or analyze separately.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

CheckList does not improve NLP models—it improves how we *know* whether they need improving. This is a **methodological shift** rather than a performance advance, and its impact operates at the level of evaluation culture rather than model architecture. The paper's central intellectual move is importing behavioral testing principles from software engineering into NLP evaluation, and doing so in a task-agnostic, systematic way that prior work had not achieved.

The shift is from **evaluation-as-benchmarking** (collect a dataset, report a number, declare victory when the number exceeds human performance) to **evaluation-as-testing** (define capabilities, design behavioral probes, find where the model breaks, fix, retest). This is not a subtle reframing—it changes what "good evaluation" means at a fundamental level. Before CheckList, a team could claim their sentiment model was production-ready because it achieved 95% on SST-2 and 90% on their in-house benchmark. After CheckList, that same team must answer: have you tested negation with MFTs? What is your failure rate on NER invariance? Does your model degrade under typos? These are questions that aggregate accuracy cannot answer, and CheckList provides both the conceptual framework and the practical tooling to ask them systematically.

The paper's most important contribution to the field's self-understanding is its **reconciliation of "superhuman" benchmark performance with obvious, severe capability failures**. The models in Tables 1-3 are not straw-men—they are the best available commercial APIs (Microsoft, Google, Amazon) and strong research models (BERT, RoBERTa) at the time of publication. The fact that these models fail 76.4% of simple negation MFTs (Google), 100% of coreference MFTs (BERT on MC), and 78% of modifier-distinction MFTs (BERT and RoBERTa on QQP) while achieving benchmark scores that exceed reported human baselines is not a finding about specific models—it is a finding about the **evaluation paradigm itself**. Aggregate accuracy on held-out benchmarks systematically overestimates linguistic competence because benchmarks contain biases and shortcuts that models exploit, and because a single number cannot reveal the distribution of capability across different linguistic phenomena.

This reconciles a tension that was visible but undiagnosed in the pre-CheckList literature. On one hand, the field celebrated rapid benchmark progress—SQuAD F1 climbing from 50 to 90+, GLUE scores exceeding human baselines, SST-2 accuracy at 94.8%. On the other hand, a series of papers demonstrated that these same models were brittle: Belinkov and Bisk (2018) showed they break under synthetic noise, Ribeiro et al. (2018) showed they can be fooled by semantically equivalent adversarial paraphrases, and Prabhakaran et al. (2019) showed sensitivity to name changes. Were these models good or bad? The answer depends on what you test. CheckList provides the framework for understanding that these are not contradictory findings—they are different rows of the capability matrix—and that a comprehensive evaluation requires testing all rows with all test types.

The paper also changes which research directions look attractive. Before CheckList, the primary response to benchmark saturation was to create harder benchmarks (SuperGLUE, SQuAD 2.0 with unanswerable questions, adversarial NLI datasets). CheckList argues—and its user study provides evidence—that the bottleneck is not benchmark difficulty but **evaluation methodology**. Users in Unaided (who had the QQP validation set, a standard benchmark) found 2.2 bugs of severity ≥ 3. Users with CheckList's capability list and templates found 6.2. Both groups had access to the same labeled data. The difference was the systematic testing discipline. This suggests that the field's investment in harder benchmarks, while valuable, should be complemented by investment in better testing methodologies. A harder benchmark still produces a single aggregate number; a systematic test suite produces a capability-by-capability failure profile that tells you *where* to fix the model.

Finally, CheckList makes **model-agnostic evaluation a practical reality**. The paper demonstrates that identical tests can be applied to commercial APIs (where internals are hidden), open-source research models (where internals are accessible but complex), and fine-tuned variants. This is not just a convenience—it changes the power dynamics of evaluation. Previously, thorough capability evaluation often required model access for probing or representational analysis, which favored researchers with access to model internals. CheckList's black-box behavioral testing means that any model with a prediction API can be evaluated on the same playing field. Third-party auditors can test commercial sentiment APIs for fairness failures without needing to see the model weights. Competitors can compare their models against commercial offerings using standardized test suites. The paper's open-source release of the test suites from Tables 1-3 together with the templating and perturbation tools lowers the barrier to entry for rigorous evaluation and creates the possibility of community-maintained test suites that evolve with our understanding of model capabilities.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Completeness analysis of capability coverage: how many rows and columns does the matrix need?** The paper proposes ten capabilities and three test types as a starting point, but provides no analysis of how much of the "capability space" this covers. A natural extension would be to define a more complete taxonomy of linguistic phenomena—perhaps building on the extensive literature in theoretical and computational linguistics—and map existing CheckList tests onto it to identify gaps. For sentiment analysis, what fraction of known negation constructions (morphological negation, double negation, scope ambiguity, negative polarity items) does the current test suite cover? For QQP, what fraction of paraphrase phenomena (ellipsis, implicature, pragmatic variation) are tested? This analysis would convert CheckList from a qualitative checklist into a quantitative coverage metric analogous to code coverage in software testing: "this model has been tested on 73% of known Negation sub-phenomena and 45% of SRL sub-phenomena." The output would be a coverage gap report that directly guides test creation.

**Automated test generation via LLMs: scaling test ideation.** The paper's template system scales test case volume but still relies on human creativity for test *ideation*—designing templates, selecting capabilities, defining expected behaviors. Modern LLMs (GPT-4, Claude, Llama 3) are capable of generating diverse, creative text. A follow-up could investigate whether an LLM can be prompted to generate CheckList tests automatically: given a task description and a capability name, can an LLM propose MFT templates, INV perturbation functions, and DIR expectations? The evaluation would compare LLM-generated tests against human-authored ones on bug-finding effectiveness and false positive rate (tests that incorrectly flag correct model behavior as failure). A strong finding would be that LLM-generated tests find different bugs than human-generated ones—suggesting complementary coverage—or that LLMs can generate orders of magnitude more tests, enabling continuous, large-scale behavioral regression testing in CI/CD pipelines for NLP models.

**Dynamic CheckList: adaptive testing based on initial failure patterns.** The paper's matrix is static—all cells are tested regardless of model behavior. But in software testing, adaptive strategies (like property-based testing with shrinking) focus computation on areas where failures are found. A dynamic extension of CheckList would: (a) start with a lightweight screening phase testing a few examples per capability, (b) identify capabilities with high failure rates, (c) allocate additional testing budget to those capabilities, generating more test case variations and more specific sub-tests (e.g., if simple negation fails, test morphological negation, double negation, and scope ambiguity specifically), and (d) build a failure profile that identifies not just that the model fails on Negation, but *which sub-phenomena* cause the failures and which do not. The experiment would measure whether dynamic allocation finds more bugs per unit of testing effort than uniform coverage, using the paper's user study methodology as a baseline.

**Cross-lingual and cross-domain CheckList: testing whether failures transfer.** The paper tests only English-language tasks. A natural extension is to apply CheckList to models in other languages—does a multilingual BERT fine-tuned on sentiment analysis in French, German, or Arabic exhibit the same negation, NER, and temporal failures as English models? The hypothesis: some failures are language-universal (negation is hard regardless of language), while others are language-specific (NER failure rates may differ for languages with different entity recognition conventions). Similarly, cross-domain testing would apply the same CheckList tests to models trained on different domains—does a sentiment model trained on movie reviews (SST-2) fail differently than one trained on product reviews or social media? The paper already hints at this with the finding that ` ` and `RoB` (trained on SST-2 movie reviews) outperformed commercial models (listed for social media use) on many tests, but does not systematically vary domain. A cross-domain study would help practitioners understand whether CheckList tests transfer across domains or whether domain-specific test suites are necessary.

**Continuous behavioral regression testing for model updates.** The paper's commercial team session (Section 4.1) demonstrates that a one-time CheckList session finds previously unknown bugs. A longitudinal study would track a model through multiple development iterations: after each model update, re-run the same CheckList test suite. Does the update fix previously identified bugs? Does it introduce new failures (regressions)? Does the failure profile change in systematic ways (e.g., improving negation worsens temporal handling)? This would evaluate CheckList as a regression testing tool—analogous to unit test suites in software that are run on every commit. The key measurement is: what fraction of CheckList-discovered bugs are eventually fixed, what is the mean time to fix, and what fraction of fixes introduce new CheckList failures? If CheckList tests become part of a CI pipeline, the methodology transitions from a research tool to an engineering practice.

**Failure-driven model improvement: using CheckList failures to guide fine-tuning.** The paper identifies failures but does not attempt to fix them. A natural follow-up is to use CheckList's failure-exposed examples as training data for targeted fine-tuning. For each capability with high failure rates, construct a training dataset from the template-generated examples (or perturbed training data using the same perturbation functions that caused INV/DIR failures), fine-tune the model on these examples, and measure whether CheckList failure rates decrease. The crucial question is whether fixing one capability degrades others—a model fine-tuned on negation MFTs might improve on negation but regress on temporal or SRL tests. This would reveal whether the capabilities are independent in the model's parameter space or whether there are trade-offs that require multi-objective fine-tuning. A negative result (fine-tuning on CheckList failures does not transfer to improved benchmark performance or causes regression on other capabilities) would be equally informative—it would suggest that the failures CheckList finds are symptoms of architectural limitations rather than data gaps, and that more fundamental changes are needed.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Model selection and procurement for enterprise NLP.** Organizations evaluating commercial NLP APIs (sentiment analysis, entity recognition, question answering) currently rely primarily on benchmark performance and vendor-provided accuracy numbers. CheckList provides a standardized, task-agnostic evaluation framework that can be incorporated into procurement processes. A company choosing between Microsoft, Google, and Amazon sentiment APIs can run the same CheckList test suite on all three (as the paper demonstrates in Table 1) and produce a capability-by-capability failure profile. The paper's results show that the "best" model depends on which capability matters most for the use case: if the application involves social media monitoring where negation is common, Google's 54.2% negation MFT failure is disqualifying; if NER invariance is critical, the 20.8% location-swap failure rate for Google versus 7.0% for Microsoft steers the decision. Critically, because CheckList is black-box, this evaluation can be performed on third-party APIs without access to model internals or training data—the only requirement is API access, which is available during procurement evaluation. The paper's finding that the commercial team itself "discovered many previously unknown bugs" and "indicated that they would definitely incorporate CheckList into their development cycle" (Section 4.1) suggests that even vendors would benefit from making their CheckList results public as a form of transparent capability reporting.

**Continuous quality monitoring for production NLP pipelines.** Models deployed in production drift—user language changes, new topics emerge, model updates alter behavior in unexpected ways. CheckList test suites can serve as **behavioral canaries** in production monitoring pipelines: run a standardized set of MFTs, INVs, and DIRs on a schedule (daily, per-deployment) and alert when failure rates on any capability exceed a threshold. For a sentiment analysis API processing customer feedback, a sudden spike in negation MFT failure from 5% to 30% after a model update would trigger a rollback before customers notice. For a QQP system detecting duplicate support tickets, a regression in modifier-distinction MFTs might cause unrelated tickets to be incorrectly merged. The paper's demonstration that template-based tests can generate hundreds of examples per test makes this practical—a monitoring suite of 20 tests with 100 examples each is 2,000 API calls, which for a high-throughput production system is negligible. The paper does not develop this monitoring use case, but the open-source release of test suites and the model-agnostic design make it straightforward to implement.

**Fairness auditing for deployed NLP systems.** The paper's fairness tests (Section 3, Sentiment and MC) demonstrate that CheckList can surface unintended biases—BERT fine-tuned on SST-2 predicts negative for "black," "atheist," "gay," and "lesbian" identity phrases while predicting positive for "Asian" and "straight"; BERT-large on SQuAD exhibits a gender-profession stereotype (picking the male as doctor 89.1% of the time when the female is correct). These are exactly the kinds of failures that fairness regulations and responsible AI guidelines require organizations to detect and mitigate. CheckList provides a practical framework for fairness auditing that goes beyond aggregate bias metrics: rather than reporting a single "bias score," an auditor can produce a capability-by-capability matrix showing where demographic perturbations cause prediction changes. The perturbation-based INV tests are particularly well-suited: swapping names (male/female, ethnicities), nationalities, or protected group adjectives and measuring prediction stability directly tests whether the model treats different demographic groups equivalently. Because these tests require no ground-truth labels, they can be applied to production traffic without annotation cost. The paper's finding that commercial models "always predict them as neutral" on simple identity templates ("I am a black woman.") while BERT exhibits clear bias suggests that commercial vendors have already implemented some fairness mitigations, but the CheckList framework enables independent verification rather than relying on vendor claims.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper does not position CheckList against named alternative evaluation methods with a formal tradeoff analysis. It explicitly states: "We do not aim for CheckList to replace challenge or benchmark datasets, but to complement them" (Section 5). CheckList is presented as an additional evaluation layer that augments, rather than substitutes for, existing practices. The paper's user study and commercial team session demonstrate that CheckList finds bugs that benchmarks miss, but does not claim that CheckList alone is sufficient for evaluation. Rather, the implication is that **comprehensive evaluation requires both**: benchmarks for aggregate performance tracking and leaderboard comparisons, and CheckList for systematic capability-level behavioral testing that reveals where and how models fail. The paper does not provide criteria for when to use CheckList versus when benchmarks suffice—the framing is additive rather than competitive.
