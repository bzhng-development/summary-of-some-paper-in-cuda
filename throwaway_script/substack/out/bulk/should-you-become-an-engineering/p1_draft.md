Here are the takeaways from this Ryan Peterman article on the IC vs. engineering manager decision (drawn from his own transition at Meta and interviews with senior leaders):

**The fundamental trap of manager career growth**

Manager career growth scales with the size of your team — in most cases, promotion comes from the sustained impact of a larger organization. The catch is that your team only grows if your business area needs it to; without organic headcount expansion, your career will stagnate regardless of how well you perform. "Managers have much less control over their career growth than ICs do." There is an upside: you can catch outlier growth if you get lucky. Ryan saw early Facebook employees get promoted to Director "within just a year or two because the company was growing like crazy — 'a rising tide lifts all boats.'" That same trajectory would take far longer on the IC track. The downside is structural: managers have less career mobility because there are fewer management roles overall. A good senior IC, by contrast, "can go almost anywhere because they create scope and don't rely on having a team for impact."

**The working style tradeoff: fewer hours, less control**

Managers work almost entirely through delegation and influence, which means people and meetings dominate the day. The cons are stark — almost no time for deep work since the schedule is fragmented, less control of your calendar (meetings dictate when you work), and "politics and drama inevitably come up." But there is a compensating upside that surprised Ryan: "work usually stops after business hours." And your people skills grow since they become your primary tool for getting things done, which spills over into personal life. The high-level tradeoff he distilled from every manager he surveyed is that you work fewer hours but have less control over your time. "This makes sense given managers don't trade their time for impact. You scale your judgment through delegation and influence."

**Why anyone would choose this path — and who shouldn't**

Three legitimate reasons to become an EM: your strengths and interests genuinely align with growing others (some ICs he met were "ok at engineering but where they shined was in growing others and using their people skills"); your long-term career aspirations point toward executive roles or starting a company; or you've been an IC long enough that you're bored and want to learn something new. What you should not do is become a manager because you think it's the fastest path upward. Some used to argue management was better for career growth since there were more managers at IC7+, but Ryan notes "that's not true anymore after the 2023 manager layoffs throughout the industry." His own experience validated the warning: "I learned a lot from switching to management and enjoyed it but I didn't get any closer to the next level. I had a much more clear path to the next level as an IC in my org."

**The practical path to making the switch**

Ryan's playbook for becoming an EM has three steps. First, get promoted to Staff (IC6) — "most companies don't allow transition before then." Second, try it out and show you're ready: manage an intern ("one of the best ways to try management out without taking on a full team") or step into a tech lead role, which is similar to being an EM just without performance reviews. Third, express interest to your manager explicitly — EM roles are limited, and when an opportunity opens, "you'll want to be on your manager's mind as someone who has the skillset and is interested in the role." Opportunities to transition are situational: growing areas that are hiring aggressively will have the most openings for management based on organizational need.
