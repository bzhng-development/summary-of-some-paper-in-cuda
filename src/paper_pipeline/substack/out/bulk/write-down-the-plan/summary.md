Here are the takeaways from this Ryan Peterman essay (on how the simple habit of writing and sharing the plan became an unfair career advantage):

**Writing as an unfair career advantage**

Ryan opens with a Marc Andreessen quote that crystallized something he’d been doing intuitively since his mid-level IC4 days: “One of the ways you find the up-and-comers at a tech company is by seeing who wrote down the plan.” He reflects that, looking back, his own promotions were “much smoother” because he did this. The habit is deceptively simple—write the plan and share it—yet he calls it an “unfair advantage” precisely because it takes so little time and “many don’t” do it. He admits to being lucky in one respect: “I actually enjoy writing.” That baseline comfort with putting words on a page snowballed into “many more opportunities throughout my career,” a through-line he returns to as he unpacks the specific mechanics of why the habit works.

**Three mechanisms: clarity, alignment, and visibility**

The benefits of writing down a plan, as he lays them out, operate on three distinct levels. First, **improving your own thinking**: writing “forces your thoughts into a coherent, logical narrative,” and the act of condensing that narrative gives you deeper understanding—he regularly discovers optimizations or edge cases he hadn’t considered until he tried to put them in prose. Second, **building alignment**: as you write and share drafts, you incorporate feedback iteratively until everyone agrees. The document becomes a coordination surface that gets a team or org onto the same page. Third, **increasing your visibility**: the shared plan “markets your work.” Everyone sees the impactful plan and associates you with it, which means performance conversations are smoother because people already know what you’ve done. And the downstream effect is self-reinforcing: “the stronger your internal brand, the more opportunities will come your way.” He links out to a concrete story illustrating that flywheel in action. The three mechanisms stack—better thinking produces sharper plans, alignment reduces friction in execution, and visibility turns that execution into recognized impact. For something that takes “so little time,” the return is outsized, and his closing nudge is simply: “Next planning season, write down the plan and share it.”

---

The habit loops back on itself: the **visibility** created by a shared plan strengthens an **internal brand** that surfaces more opportunities, which in turn demand more plans. Writing-as-unfair-advantage isn't a single tactic but a self-reinforcing cycle—clarity, alignment, and visibility each feeding the next—and the barrier to entry is simply sitting down and doing it.
