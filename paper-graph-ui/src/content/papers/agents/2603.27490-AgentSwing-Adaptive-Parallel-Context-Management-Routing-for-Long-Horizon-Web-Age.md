# AgentSwing: Adaptive Parallel Context Management Routing for Long-Horizon Web Agents

**ArXiv:** [2603.27490](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.27490)

## 🎯 Pitch

Long-horizon web agents can achieve the same accuracy with up to 3× fewer steps simply by dynamically choosing when to discard accumulated context rather than following a fixed strategy throughout. A new probabilistic framework reveals that success depends separately on whether the agent survives its context window and whether it answers correctly upon arrival—two dimensions that static methods trade off but adaptive routing can jointly optimize.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper proposes **AgentSwing**, a state-aware adaptive parallel context management routing framework for long-horizon web agents that dynamically switches among heterogeneous context management strategies at each trigger point rather than committing to a single static policy throughout the trajectory. The authors introduce the first probabilistic framework decomposing long-horizon agent success into two complementary dimensions—search efficiency η (the probability of reaching a stopping point before exhausting resources) and terminal precision ρ (the probability of answering correctly conditioned on reaching a stopping point)—providing a unified lens for understanding why static strategies occupy different points on an efficiency-precision trade-off frontier and why adaptive routing can move beyond it. Evaluated across BrowseComp, BrowseComp-ZH, and Humanity's Last Exam with three open-source agent backbones (GPT-OSS-120B, DeepSeek-v3.2, and Tongyi-DR-30B-A3B), AgentSwing consistently outperforms static context management methods, often matching or exceeding their performance with up to 3× fewer interaction turns (reaching 60.0% on BrowseComp with GPT-OSS-120B versus 52.5% for the best static strategy at the same budget) while also achieving a higher ultimate performance ceiling, establishing that the gains from adaptive context management routing depend critically on using short-horizon lookahead to evaluate downstream consequences before selecting a continuation.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Static Context Management Cannot Adapt to Trajectory Evolution

The fundamental problem this paper addresses is that **long-horizon web agents exhaust their finite context windows before completing sufficiently informative searches**, and existing solutions — which commit to a single fixed context management strategy — cannot adapt as the quality and reliability of accumulated context changes over the course of a trajectory.

To understand why this matters, we need to recognize what a "long-horizon" information-seeking task actually looks like. Consider the example from Figure 7: an agent trying to identify a specific viral song must search for a rapper born in October in the 1990s who joined a gang at 14, verify that rapper's identity against multiple clues, identify a featured artist whose father served 15 years in prison, and cross-reference discographies against album release windows. This requires visiting Wikipedia pages, searching for birth dates, checking discographies, verifying collaboration timelines, and backtracking when initial hypotheses (like "Lil Durk") prove incorrect. The trajectory in that example spans 31 turns, accumulating both useful clues (e.g., the successful identification of "$tupid Young") and substantial noise (e.g., dead-end investigations into Nipsey Hussle, Lil Durk, and Hit-Boy collaborations that fell outside the required timeframe).

Under a fixed context budget — typically 128K tokens for the models used in this paper — an agent that simply appends every interaction turn to its working memory will eventually hit this ceiling. When that happens, one of two failure modes occurs: either the agent must stop prematurely without sufficient evidence to answer correctly (a **search efficiency** failure — it couldn't reach a stopping point), or it manages to produce an answer but the accumulated noise in its context degrades the quality of that answer (a **terminal precision** failure — it reached a stopping point but answered incorrectly). The paper's probabilistic framework in Section 2.1 formalizes exactly these two failure modes.

The practical significance of this bottleneck is enormous. As the paper notes in Section 1, recent frontier systems — from OpenAI's DeepResearch to Anthropic's Claude, from DeepSeek to various open-source efforts — have demonstrated that deep information-seeking tasks routinely require tens or even hundreds of interaction steps. BrowseComp, one of the three benchmarks used in this paper, was specifically designed by Wei et al. (2025) to be "simple yet challenging" for browsing agents precisely because its questions require synthesizing evidence across multiple web pages that cannot be found through a single search query. Similarly, Humanity's Last Exam (Phan et al., 2025) contains questions deliberately constructed to resist solution by models that cannot perform extended, multi-step web research. In both cases, the **context window is the scarce resource that determines the performance ceiling**, not the underlying reasoning capability of the model.

### Where Existing Approaches Fall Short: The Static Strategy Trap

Prior to this work, context management for long-horizon agents fell into a recognizable pattern: researchers or practitioners would select a single strategy — Discard-All, Keep-Last-N, Summary, or some variant — and apply that same strategy uniformly at every trigger point throughout the entire trajectory. The paper identifies three representative static strategies that capture the dominant approaches in prior work (Section 3, expanded in Section 5):

- **Discard-All** (Liu et al., 2025a; Team et al., 2026; Zeng et al., 2026a): When the context exceeds a threshold, discard the entire accumulated trajectory and restart from only the original user prompt. This is the most aggressive reset-based policy, giving the agent a completely clean working context at the cost of losing all intermediate progress.

- **Keep-Last-N** (Liu et al., 2025a; Zeng et al., 2026a): Preserve only the most recent N interaction turns (N = 5 in this paper's experiments) and discard everything earlier. This retains recent local context — which is often the most relevant for the current search direction — while freeing up space.

- **Summary** (Liu et al., 2025a; Anthropic, 2025b): Compress the accumulated trajectory into a summarized text and keep the context as the original user prompt plus the summary. This attempts to preserve the "gist" of what has been learned while reducing token consumption.

The paper also compares against a **baseline without context management (w/o CM)**, where the agent simply appends turns until it either produces an answer or exhausts the maximum context length, at which point it is counted as failed.

The problem with all of these static approaches is that **the optimal context management decision depends on the current trajectory state**, and that state varies dramatically over the course of a single long-horizon search. The paper provides compelling evidence for this claim in Section 2.3 and Figure 3:

**Static strategies occupy different points on an efficiency-precision trade-off frontier.** Figure 3b plots search efficiency η (x-axis) against terminal precision ρ (y-axis) for each strategy on BrowseComp. The baseline (w/o CM) sits in the **high-efficiency, low-precision** corner: because it never resets, it reaches stopping points on many tasks (high η), but the accumulated noise in its context makes those answers less reliable (low ρ). Discard-All sits near the opposite corner: **low-efficiency, high-precision**. Each individual reset-based attempt has a smaller effective context budget and is therefore less likely to finish on its own (lower η), but when it does finish, the cleaner context produces more reliable answers (higher ρ). Summary and Keep-Last-N fall between these extremes, trading off efficiency and precision in different ways.

This trade-off is not merely an empirical curiosity — it is a **structural consequence of how context quality evolves during search**. Section 2.2 makes this explicit through the Discard-All case study. When the context budget is small (aggressive resetting), each individual attempt has a cleaner working memory and therefore higher terminal precision, but also a lower probability of finishing because it has fewer tokens to work with. Increasing the number of reset opportunities N partially compensates for the efficiency loss (Equation 10), but the fundamental tension remains: you cannot simultaneously have the search efficiency of a large context and the terminal precision of a small, clean context with any single static strategy.

**The same strategy that helps in one trajectory state can hurt in another.** The case study in Section 4.4 and Figure 7 makes this concrete. At Turn 23 of the "Mando" case, the trajectory contains a mixture of useful recent information (a newly surfaced clue about "$tupid Young") and substantial accumulated noise (extended dead-end exploration of Lil Durk, Nipsey Hussle, and Hit-Boy collaborations that violated timeline constraints). In this state:

- **Keep-Last-N** succeeds because it preserves the recent "$tupid Young" clue while discarding the earlier noise. The lookahead continuation immediately verifies the rapper's identity and identifies "Mando" as the candidate song.
- **Discard-All** fails because it resets entirely, losing the "$tupid Young" breakthrough and falling back to broad, unfocused exploration that makes no progress within the lookahead horizon.
- **Summary** fails because it preserves the dominant but incorrect "Lil Durk" hypothesis, compressing the trajectory in a way that inherits the misleading focus.

But the paper also provides a complementary case in Appendix C (Tables 7–9) where **Discard-All is the correct choice**. In the "live-crickets" case with GPT-OSS-120B, the trajectory becomes trapped in a loop of repeatedly trying and failing to extract text from an inaccessible PDF. The accumulated context is dominated by noise, repetition, and failed local exploration. Here, Discard-All's aggressive reset allows the agent to recover a cleaner search state and approach the extraction problem from a different angle, while Keep-Last-N preserves the recent context — which is precisely the dead-end loop — and therefore remains trapped.

These contrasting cases reveal why static strategies are fundamentally limited: **no single strategy dominates across all trajectory states**. A strategy that works well when the recent context contains a promising breakthrough (Keep-Last-N in the "Mando" case) will fail when the recent context is dominated by noise and dead ends (the "live-crickets" case). Conversely, a strategy that works well when a clean restart is needed (Discard-All in the "live-crickets" case) will discard valuable intermediate progress in other situations.

### Conflicting Signals in Prior Work

The paper is motivated by a genuine tension in the existing literature and practitioner experience. On one hand, multiple frontier systems have shown that **aggressive context management substantially improves long-horizon performance**. Liu et al. (2025a) demonstrated that Discard-All enabled DeepSeek agents to sustain more interaction turns and achieve higher accuracy on deep search benchmarks. Team et al. (2026) and Zeng et al. (2026a) reported similar findings for the Kimi and GLM agent families, respectively. Anthropic (2025b) published engineering guidance advocating for context management as a key mechanism for effective AI agents.

On the other hand, the choice of *which* strategy to use remains largely **ad hoc and heuristic**. Different systems commit to different strategies — some use Discard-All, others use Keep-Last-N, still others use summarization — without a principled framework for deciding when each is appropriate. The paper cites these approaches as evidence that context management is valuable, but notes that all of them suffer from the same limitation: "once a strategy is selected, the same operation is repeatedly applied throughout the entire trajectory" (Section 5).

This limitation is not just a matter of suboptimal performance in the average case. It represents a **missed opportunity for compositional gains**. If different strategies are complementary — with Keep-Last-N excelling at preserving recent breakthroughs and Discard-All excelling at recovering from noise-dominated states — then a system that can dynamically switch between them should outperform any individual strategy. The key question is whether the switching decision can be made reliably enough at test time to realize these gains, or whether the overhead of decision-making erases the benefits.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper positions itself as providing **both a theoretical framework and a practical mechanism** for moving beyond static context management:

**The probabilistic framework (Section 2) provides the theoretical lens.** By decomposing end-to-end success into η × ρ, the paper gives the field a vocabulary for discussing *why* different strategies behave differently and *what* an ideal strategy would achieve. This framework is not merely descriptive — it is prescriptive. It shows that the goal of context management should be to **simultaneously achieve high search efficiency and high terminal precision**, moving to the upper-right region of the efficiency-precision plane in Figure 3b. Static strategies are constrained to operate along a trade-off frontier; an adaptive strategy that selects the right operation for each trajectory state can, in principle, push beyond that frontier.

**AgentSwing (Section 3) provides the practical mechanism.** Rather than committing to a single strategy, AgentSwing treats context management as a **state-dependent routing problem**: at each trigger point, expand multiple context-managed branches in parallel (applying different strategies to the same raw trajectory), let each branch continue for K additional turns (the lookahead phase), and then use the agent model itself to select the most promising continuation based on both the managed context and its short-term downstream behavior under real environment feedback.

This design differs from prior work in two crucial ways. First, it makes the context management decision **contingent on future behavior**, not just on properties of the current context. A strategy that preserves a seemingly promising clue is only valuable if that clue actually leads to progress in subsequent turns — the lookahead mechanism evaluates this directly. Second, it **leverages the complementary strengths of heterogeneous strategies** within a single trajectory, switching between them as the trajectory state evolves. The transition matrices in Figure 9 confirm that this switching is non-random and model-dependent — different backbones exhibit different preferences for which strategy to transition to from which current strategy, suggesting that AgentSwing is genuinely adapting to state rather than defaulting to a single dominant choice.

The paper explicitly frames this as a **test-time scaling mechanism** for long-horizon agents. Just as inference-time compute scaling (best-of-N, beam search, revisions) can improve LLM performance on reasoning tasks without modifying model weights, adaptive context management can improve agent performance on long-horizon tasks without changing the underlying agent model. The key difference is that here the "compute" being scaled is not merely more samples from the same distribution, but rather **more sophisticated context curation** that changes the information available to the agent at each step.

This positioning connects to a broader trend in the literature that the paper cites: the recognition that test-time design choices — context engineering (Anthropic, 2025b), interaction protocol design (Yao et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2025a), and search strategy (Li et al., 2025a) — can be as consequential as model capability for long-horizon performance. AgentSwing contributes to this trend by showing that context management itself can be made adaptive, and that doing so yields gains beyond what any single static design can achieve.

### The Significance of the Contribution

The practical importance of this problem is difficult to overstate given the trajectory of the field. As of early 2026, autonomous web agents are transitioning from research prototypes to deployed systems (OpenAI DeepResearch, Anthropic's Claude with computer use, Kimi Researcher, and various open-source efforts). All of these systems face the same fundamental tension: the web is vast, complex questions require synthesizing information across many pages, and context windows — while growing — remain finite. Context management is therefore not an optional optimization but a **hard requirement for viability** in production settings.

The theoretical significance is equally important. The paper's η × ρ decomposition is the first formal probabilistic characterization of long-horizon agent success that separates the two distinct failure modes (failure to finish vs. failure to answer correctly given finishing). This framework provides a language for future work to reason about context management trade-offs and to design strategies that explicitly target one dimension or the other. It also explains why commonly reported metrics like Pass@1 or accuracy should not be treated as monolithic in long-horizon settings — they conflate two processes that may respond differently to intervention.

Finally, the paper fills a gap that is both empirical and conceptual. Empirically, no prior work had systematically compared static context management strategies against an adaptive alternative on challenging long-horizon benchmarks with multiple model backbones. Conceptually, no prior work had provided a framework for understanding *why* a fixed strategy cannot be optimal across all trajectory states, or *how* to design an adaptive alternative that can realize compositional gains from heterogeneous strategies. AgentSwing and its accompanying probabilistic framework address both gaps simultaneously.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader orientation

AgentSwing is a **test-time control framework** that sits on top of an existing LLM-based web agent and decides, at each point where the agent's context window is filling up, which context management strategy to apply next—rather than always applying the same one. The problem it solves is that long-horizon web search produces trajectories where the "right" way to compress or reset the accumulated history depends on what has been discovered so far: sometimes the recent context contains a valuable breakthrough that should be preserved (calling for Keep-Last-N), sometimes the trajectory is spiraling into noise and repetition (calling for Discard-All), and sometimes a compressed summary of progress is the best compromise (calling for Summary). The solution shape is a **parallel-branching router**: at each trigger point, apply multiple candidate strategies simultaneously, let each branch run ahead for a few turns to see where it leads, and then select the most promising branch—turning context management from a fixed compression heuristic into a state-dependent routing decision over future continuations.

### 3.2 Big-picture architecture (diagram in words)

The AgentSwing framework has two major components that operate in sequence at each context-management trigger point:

1. **Parallel Context Management:** When the accumulated context length exceeds a predefined fraction `$r$` of the model's maximum context length (e.g., `$r = 0.2$` or `$r = 0.4$` of 128K tokens), the framework takes the current raw trajectory and applies multiple candidate strategies to it **independently and in parallel**. In the paper's implementation, three strategies are used: **Keep-Last-N** (retain only the most recent N interaction turns), **Summary** (compress the trajectory into a summary text appended to the original user prompt), and **Discard-All** (discard everything except the original user prompt). Each strategy produces a **managed context**—a cleaned, compressed, or reset version of the working memory that the agent will use for subsequent turns.

2. **Lookahead Routing Mechanism:** After generating the managed contexts, the framework does not immediately select one. Instead, it lets each branch continue interacting with the environment for `$K$` additional turns (the **lookahead horizon**, with `$K = 3$` as the default in experiments). This produces `$K$` new `$\langle\text{thinking}, \text{tool call}, \text{tool response}\rangle$` tuples per branch, generated under real environment feedback. After the lookahead phase, the agent model is presented with the original raw context plus all candidate continuations (the managed context plus its `$K$` lookahead turns) and asked to select the most promising branch. The selected continuation becomes the new main trajectory, and the other branches are discarded. The framework then resumes normal operation until the next trigger point, at which point the process repeats—potentially selecting a different strategy than the previous trigger point.

Information flows as follows: **raw trajectory** → **trigger detection** (context exceeds `$r \times$` max length) → **parallel strategy application** (Keep-Last-N, Summary, Discard-All applied independently) → **parallel lookahead rollout** (`$K$` turns per branch with real tool calls) → **routing decision** (agent model selects best branch based on managed context + lookahead continuation) → **selected branch becomes new main trajectory** → **continue until next trigger or task completion**.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the deep dive

- **First**, the probabilistic framework from Section 2, because it defines the formal objective that AgentSwing is designed to optimize—understanding η and ρ is prerequisite to understanding *why* the routing decision matters and what it is trying to achieve.
- **Second**, the trigger mechanism and parallel context management component, because these define the action space: what strategies are available, how they transform the raw context, and when the framework intervenes.
- **Third**, the lookahead routing mechanism in detail, because this is the core intellectual contribution—how short-horizon rollouts enable state-dependent strategy selection, what the routing prompt looks like, and how the lookahead horizon `$K$` affects performance.
- **Fourth**, the design choices and their justifications: why three strategies, why `$K = 3$`, why the agent model itself serves as the router, and how the framework connects back to the efficiency-precision trade-off from Section 2.

### 3.4 Detailed, sentence-based technical breakdown

This is primarily a **systems and empirical analysis paper** whose core idea is that context management for long-horizon agents should be treated as a **state-dependent routing problem** rather than a fixed compression policy, and that short-horizon lookahead rollouts provide sufficient signal to make this routing decision reliably at test time.

#### The Probabilistic Framework: Formalizing What AgentSwing Optimizes

The paper's probabilistic framework (Section 2) is not merely a descriptive tool—it defines the optimization objective that AgentSwing is designed to improve. Understanding this framework is essential for understanding *why* the routing mechanism works the way it does.

The framework decomposes the end-to-end success probability of a long-horizon agent into two multiplicative factors, each capturing a distinct failure mode. Let `$\tau$` be a task sampled from a distribution `$\mathcal{T}$`, and let `$\pi$` be a test-time strategy (specifying context management, stopping rules, and resource constraints). Define two events under strategy `$\pi$`:

- `$S_\pi$`: the event that the agent reaches a stopping point and emits a final answer (before exhausting its resource budget).
- `$C_\pi$`: the event that this emitted answer is correct.

The paper then defines two task-level quantities:

$$\eta^\pi_\tau := P(S_\pi \mid \tau)$$

where `$\eta^\pi_\tau$` is the **search efficiency** for task `$\tau$` under strategy `$\pi$`—the probability that the agent manages to produce *any* final answer before running out of turns or context.

$$\rho^\pi_\tau := P(C_\pi \mid S_\pi, \tau)$$

where `$\rho^\pi_\tau$` is the **terminal precision** for task `$\tau$` under strategy `$\pi$`—the probability that the answer is correct, *conditioned on* the agent having reached a stopping point.

**What these equations compute:** Given a specific task and a specific strategy, `$\eta^\pi_\tau$` computes the fraction of execution runs (or, in the empirical estimate, the binary indicator across tasks) where the agent successfully terminates with an answer rather than exhausting its budget. `$\rho^\pi_\tau$` computes, among only those runs where the agent terminated, the fraction where the answer was correct. The product `$\eta^\pi_\tau \times \rho^\pi_\tau$` gives the overall probability of successful task completion under strategy `$\pi$`.

Task-level success follows from the chain rule:

$$P(\text{Success}_\pi \mid \tau) = P(S_\pi \cap C_\pi \mid \tau) = \eta^\pi_\tau \cdot \rho^\pi_\tau$$

**Why this decomposition matters:** The two factors are *structurally different* and respond differently to context management interventions. A strategy that never resets context (the baseline without context management) achieves high `$\eta$` because the agent always has a full working memory and rarely gets stuck—but low `$\rho$` because the accumulated noise, drift, and "context rot" (Hsieh et al., 2024; Modarressi et al., 2025) degrades the quality of the final answer. A strategy that aggressively resets (Discard-All) achieves lower `$\eta$` because each reset-based attempt operates under a smaller effective context budget and is less likely to finish—but higher `$\rho$` when it does finish because the cleaner context produces more reliable answers. This decomposition reveals that Pass@1 or accuracy is a *conflated* metric in long-horizon settings—it obscures whether a strategy's strength comes from reaching more stopping points or from answering more reliably at those stopping points, two properties that can trade off against each other.

At the population level, the paper defines:

$$\eta^\pi := P(S_\pi) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \mathcal{T}}[\eta^\pi_\tau]$$

$$\rho^\pi := P(C_\pi \mid S_\pi) = \frac{P(C_\pi \cap S_\pi)}{P(S_\pi)} = \frac{\mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \mathcal{T}}[\eta^\pi_\tau \rho^\pi_\tau]}{\mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \mathcal{T}}[\eta^\pi_\tau]}$$

**What these compute:** `$\eta^\pi$` is the expected search efficiency across the task distribution—the fraction of all tasks where the agent manages to terminate with an answer. `$\rho^\pi$` is a weighted average of terminal precision, where the weights are proportional to how often each task reaches a stopping point. This weighting is important because tasks where the agent never finishes contribute zero weight to `$\rho^\pi$`, meaning `$\rho^\pi$` reflects precision *conditional on the strategy's own finishing pattern*.

The overall population-level success probability is:

$$\text{Pass@1}_\pi = P(\text{Success}_\pi) = \eta^\pi \cdot \rho^\pi$$

**Empirical estimation.** For a benchmark with `$M$` tasks, let `$N^\pi_\text{finish}$` be the number of tasks where the agent reaches a stopping point and emits a final answer under strategy `$\pi$`, and let `$N^\pi_\text{correct}$` be the number of tasks where that final answer is correct. Tasks that exhaust the allowed resources (maximum context length or maximum interaction turns) before producing a final answer are counted as failed. The empirical estimates are:

$$\eta^\pi \approx \frac{N^\pi_\text{finish}}{M}, \quad \rho^\pi \approx \frac{N^\pi_\text{correct}}{N^\pi_\text{finish}}, \quad \text{Pass@1}_\pi \approx \frac{N^\pi_\text{correct}}{M}$$

**Why this estimation protocol?** This follows the convention established by Team et al. (2026) and Zeng et al. (2026a) for long-horizon agent evaluation. The key design choice is that resource exhaustion is treated as failure—the agent does not get partial credit for partial progress. This makes `$\eta$` a genuine measure of whether the strategy enables sufficient exploration within the available budget.

**Aligned terminal precision.** Because different strategies finish on different subsets of tasks, directly comparing `$\rho^\pi$` across strategies can be misleading—a strategy with high `$\rho$` might simply be finishing on easier tasks. To enable fairer cross-strategy comparison, the paper defines **aligned terminal precision** `$\rho^\pi_\text{align}$`. Let `$N_\text{aligned-finish}$` be the number of tasks that finish under *all* compared strategies or settings, and let `$N^\pi_\text{aligned-correct}$` be the number of those shared-finished tasks answered correctly by strategy `$\pi$`. Then:

$$\rho^\pi_\text{align} \approx \frac{N^\pi_\text{aligned-correct}}{N_\text{aligned-finish}}$$

**What this computes:** By restricting to tasks that all strategies manage to finish, this metric isolates the precision difference on a held-constant task set, removing the confounding effect of different finishing patterns. If strategy A has higher `$\rho$` than strategy B but lower `$\rho_\text{align}$`, it means strategy A's apparent precision advantage came from finishing on easier tasks, not from genuinely more reliable answers on comparable tasks.

**What this framework implies for context management design:** An ideal context management strategy would simultaneously achieve high `$\eta$` (reaching stopping points reliably) *and* high `$\rho$` (answering correctly when it stops). As Figure 3b empirically demonstrates, static strategies occupy a trade-off frontier—none simultaneously achieves both. AgentSwing's design goal, therefore, is to break through this frontier by adaptively selecting the strategy that best balances `$\eta$` and `$\rho$` for the current trajectory state, with the routing decision informed by lookahead information about which continuation is most likely to lead to successful termination with a correct answer.

#### The Discard-All Case Study: Why Efficiency-Precision Trade-offs Exist

The paper uses Discard-All as a concrete case study (Section 2.2) to illustrate *why* the efficiency-precision trade-off emerges and how it can be partially mitigated by increasing the number of reset attempts.

Under Discard-All, the agent operates in **reset-based segments**: when the context exceeds the trigger threshold, the entire accumulated history is discarded, and the agent restarts from only the original user prompt. This means that a single task execution under Discard-All may contain multiple independent attempts (segments), each operating under a smaller effective context budget than the baseline. If the maximum turn budget is exhausted before any segment produces a final answer, the task is counted as failed.

The paper examines how the **trigger threshold** (the fraction `$r$` of maximum context length at which Discard-All activates) affects performance. Figure 2 shows that, for both Tongyi-DR-30B-A3B and GPT-OSS-120B on BrowseComp, **aligned terminal precision consistently decreases as the context budget increases**. In other words, larger working contexts lead to more severe "context rot" at termination—the accumulated noise, irrelevant history, and competing hypotheses in a large context degrade the agent's ability to reason correctly about the final answer. Since the baseline without context management corresponds to the largest possible context regime (it never resets), it is the least favorable for terminal precision.

However, smaller contexts also mean each individual reset-based attempt is less likely to finish on its own, because the agent has fewer tokens to work with before hitting the trigger threshold again. This creates the trade-off. But Discard-All partially compensates for this through **multiple reset opportunities**: the agent gets `$N$` independent attempts (one per reset segment) rather than a single uninterrupted attempt. For a task `$\tau$`, let `$S_i$` denote the event that the agent reaches a stopping point during the `$i$`-th reset-based segment, with at most `$N$` such segments allowed. Then:

$$\eta^{\text{DA}}_\tau = P\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{N} S_i \;\middle|\; \tau\right)$$

**What this computes:** the probability that the agent finishes during *any* of the `$N$` reset-based attempts. This is the probability of the union of `$N$` events—if any one segment reaches a stopping point, the task counts as finished.

Under a conditional independence approximation across reset-based segments (each segment's chance of finishing is roughly independent, since each starts from a clean context with only the user prompt):

$$\eta^{\text{DA}}_\tau = 1 - \prod_{i=1}^{N} \left(1 - \eta^{\text{DA}}_{\tau,i}\right) \approx 1 - \left(1 - \eta^{\text{DA}}_{\tau,\text{single}}\right)^N$$

where `$\eta^{\text{DA}}_{\tau,\text{single}}$` is the probability that a single reset-based segment finishes on task `$\tau$`.

**What this approximation reveals:** Even though each individual segment has lower search efficiency than the baseline (because `$\eta^{\text{DA}}_{\tau,\text{single}} < \eta^{\text{baseline}}_\tau$`), the overall search efficiency grows toward 1 as `$N$` increases—each additional reset opportunity provides another chance to finish. For example, if `$\eta^{\text{DA}}_{\tau,\text{single}} = 0.3$` (a 30% chance per segment) and `$N = 5$`, then `$\eta^{\text{DA}}_\tau \approx 1 - (0.7)^5 \approx 0.83$`. The efficiency loss from smaller individual contexts is compensated by multiple independent attempts.

**Why this matters for AgentSwing:** This analysis shows that the efficiency-precision trade-off is not a fixed law of nature—it can be manipulated by changing the number of attempts. But more importantly, it reveals that the trade-off is *state-dependent*: the value of a reset depends on whether the current trajectory contains useful intermediate progress that would be lost. AgentSwing generalizes this insight by making the reset-vs-preserve decision explicit and adaptive, rather than always resetting (Discard-All) or never resetting (baseline) or always preserving the last N turns (Keep-Last-N).

#### Trigger Mechanism: When AgentSwing Intervenes

AgentSwing does not modify the agent's behavior during normal operation. It only activates when a **trigger condition** is met: the accumulated context length exceeds a predefined fraction `$r$` of the model's maximum context length.

**What "context length" means here:** The context is the full sequence of tokens fed to the agent model at each turn, including the system prompt, user prompt, and all accumulated `$\langle\text{thinking}, \text{tool call}, \text{tool response}\rangle$` tuples. The trigger threshold is measured in tokens, and `$r$` is the fraction of the model's maximum context window (128K tokens for all models in this paper).

**Model-specific trigger ratios.** The paper uses different values of `$r$` for different backbones:
- `$r = 0.2$` for GPT-OSS-120B (triggers at approximately 25.6K tokens)
- `$r = 0.4$` for both Tongyi-DR-30B-A3B and DeepSeek-v3.2 (triggers at approximately 51.2K tokens)

**Rationale for model-specific thresholds:** The paper states that these settings follow "the officially recommended or empirically optimal settings of each agent backbone" (Section 4.1), and the rationale is discussed in the Discard-All analysis of Section 2.2. Different models have different sensitivities to context length—some degrade more rapidly with accumulated history (lower optimal `$r$`), while others can tolerate larger working contexts before context rot becomes severe (higher optimal `$r$`). The trigger threshold essentially determines the **effective context budget per segment** under Discard-All, and by extension affects how often context management is triggered under any strategy.

**What happens when the trigger fires:** The current raw trajectory—the complete sequence of all interaction turns up to that point—is passed to the parallel context management component. The trigger fires repeatedly throughout a single task execution: each time the context grows past `$r \times$` max length after the previous context management operation, AgentSwing intervenes again. This means a single task may trigger context management multiple times, and the strategy selected at trigger point `$t$` may differ from the strategy selected at trigger point `$t+1$`.

**Why a fractional trigger rather than a fixed token count?** Using a fraction of maximum context length makes the mechanism portable across models with different context windows. If the maximum context length changes (e.g., upgrading from a 128K model to a 256K model), the same `$r$` value automatically scales the trigger threshold. It also ensures that context management always activates before the context is completely full, leaving some headroom for the managed context and subsequent turns.

#### Parallel Context Management: The Action Space

At each trigger point, AgentSwing applies multiple candidate context management strategies to the same raw context **independently and in parallel**, producing a set of alternative managed contexts. Each managed context is a transformed version of the accumulated trajectory, designed to fit within the remaining context budget while preserving different aspects of the search history.

The paper uses three representative strategies that capture the dominant approaches in prior work:

**Strategy 1: Keep-Last-N (KLN)**

This strategy preserves only the most recent `$N$` interaction turns and discards everything earlier. An interaction turn is a complete `$\langle\text{thinking}, \text{tool call}, \text{tool response}\rangle$` tuple. The paper uses `$N = 5$` throughout all experiments.

**What it produces:** The managed context consists of the original user prompt `$q$` followed by the last 5 complete turns from the trajectory. All earlier turns—potentially dozens or hundreds—are discarded. The agent continues from a context that contains only recent local information.

**Mechanism:** This is implemented as a simple truncation operation: count turns backward from the most recent, keep the last `$N$`, drop the rest. No summarization, no reranking, no selection—purely recency-based retention.

**What it preserves:** Recent search direction, recently discovered clues, and the immediate context needed to continue the current line of investigation. In the "Mando" case study (Figure 7), Keep-Last-N successfully preserved the recently surfaced "$tupid Young" clue while discarding the earlier noise from dead-end Lil Durk investigations.

**What it loses:** Any broader context about why the agent is searching in this direction, earlier clues that might be needed later, and the history of hypotheses that have already been tested and rejected—potentially leading to repeated exploration of the same dead ends.

**Why N = 5?** The paper does not provide a detailed ablation of `$N$`, but the choice appears motivated by prior work (Liu et al., 2025a; Zeng et al., 2026a) and the empirical observation that 5 turns typically provide enough local context for the agent to understand its current state while being small enough to significantly reduce token consumption. Five turns in a deep information-seeking setting typically include 5 search queries or page visits, which is usually sufficient to capture a coherent local investigation thread.

**Strategy 2: Summary (SUM)**

This strategy compresses the entire accumulated trajectory into a summarized text and constructs the managed context as the original user prompt `$q$` plus the summary, i.e., `$(q, \text{Sum})$`.

**What it produces:** A condensed narrative describing what the agent has searched for, what it has found, which hypotheses have been tested and rejected, and what the current state of knowledge is. The summary is generated by a language model—in all experiments, the paper uses GPT-OSS-120B as the summarizer, regardless of which agent model is being used for the main task.

**Mechanism:** The summarization step is implemented as a prompting task: the full trajectory is provided to GPT-OSS-120B with instructions to produce a concise summary capturing the key findings, tested hypotheses, and current search direction. The exact summarization prompt is not provided in the paper, but the description in Section 3 indicates it follows the approach of Liu et al. (2025a) and Anthropic (2025b).

**What it preserves:** The high-level semantic content of the search—what has been learned, which directions have been explored, what remains to be investigated. This is the most information-dense form of compression, aiming to preserve the "gist" while dramatically reducing token count.

**What it loses:** Specific details that the summarizer may deem unimportant but that could become relevant later, the precise wording of clues that may be needed for exact matching, and the sequential structure of the investigation (which turn led to which discovery). There is also a risk of **compression bias**: the summarizer may over-emphasize the dominant hypothesis and under-represent alternative clues, as seen in the "Mando" case where the Summary branch preserved the incorrect "Lil Durk" focus because that had dominated the trajectory up to the trigger point.

**Why use GPT-OSS-120B as the summarizer for all models?** This is a deliberate design choice to decouple summarization quality from the agent model's capability. By using a fixed, strong summarizer across all experiments, differences in performance can be attributed to the routing mechanism and the agent model's ability to use different context types, rather than to differences in summarization quality. This also means that the Summary strategy has a fixed computational cost (one summarization call to GPT-OSS-120B) regardless of the agent backbone.

**Strategy 3: Discard-All (DA)**

This is the most aggressive strategy: discard the entire accumulated interaction history and keep only the original user prompt `$q$`.

**What it produces:** A completely clean context containing only the original task description. The agent effectively restarts its search from scratch, with no memory of what it has already tried—but also with no accumulated noise, drift, or misleading hypotheses.

**Mechanism:** Simple truncation: remove all turns after the user prompt. No summarization, no retention. This is equivalent to spawning a new agent instance with the same task.

**What it preserves:** Only the task specification. The agent must reconstruct its search strategy from first principles, but it does so with a fully clean working memory.

**What it loses:** Everything. All intermediate discoveries, all clues that have been accumulated, all hypotheses that have been tested. If the agent had made genuine progress that would be useful for answering the question, Discard-All throws it away.

**When it helps:** When the accumulated context is dominated by noise, repetition, or unproductive search patterns. The "live-crickets" case in Appendix C (Tables 7–9) illustrates this: the trajectory becomes trapped in a loop of repeatedly trying and failing to extract text from an inaccessible PDF. Discard-All allows the agent to break out of this loop and approach the problem from a fresh angle, while Keep-Last-N preserves the recent context (which is precisely the dead-end loop) and therefore remains trapped.

**Why include such an extreme strategy?** Despite its apparent wastefulness, Discard-All has been shown to substantially improve long-horizon performance in prior work (Liu et al., 2025a; Team et al., 2026; Zeng et al., 2026a) because the precision gains from clean context can outweigh the efficiency losses from restarting, especially when the agent gets multiple reset opportunities (as formalized in Equation 10). Including it in the candidate set ensures that AgentSwing has the option to "cut its losses" when the current trajectory is going nowhere.

**Why these three strategies specifically?** The paper states that these are "representative" strategies covering the main approach families in prior work: recency-based retention (Keep-Last-N), semantic compression (Summary), and full reset (Discard-All). They span the efficiency-precision trade-off frontier identified in Figure 3b: Keep-Last-N and Summary are more efficiency-oriented (higher `$\eta$`, moderate `$\rho$`), while Discard-All is more precision-oriented (lower `$\eta$`, higher `$\rho$`). The diversity of this candidate set is what enables AgentSwing to adapt to different trajectory states—if all strategies had similar efficiency-precision profiles, routing would provide little benefit.

**Parallel execution model.** The paper states that the strategies are applied "in parallel," meaning the managed contexts are generated independently and simultaneously. In practice, Keep-Last-N and Discard-All are trivial operations (truncation at different points), so they have negligible computational cost. Summary requires an LLM call, which has non-trivial latency and cost. The paper does not provide latency measurements, but the parallelism means that the slowest strategy (Summary) determines the wall-clock time of the context management phase, and the other two strategies can be prepared concurrently at no additional time cost.

#### Lookahead Routing: Selecting the Best Continuation

This is the core intellectual contribution of AgentSwing: rather than selecting a context management strategy based solely on properties of the managed context itself (e.g., "does the summary look coherent?" or "how many tokens did we save?"), the framework evaluates each candidate by **observing its short-term downstream behavior under real environment feedback** and then routes to the most promising branch.

**Step 1: Lookahead rollout.** After parallel context management produces the managed contexts, each branch continues interacting with the environment for `$K$` additional turns. The paper uses `$K = 3$` as the default based on ablation results (Table 3). During these `$K$` turns, the agent model:

- Receives the managed context as its working memory.
- Generates a `$\langle\text{thinking}\rangle$` block with its reasoning.
- Issues a `$\langle\text{tool call}\rangle$` (e.g., search, visit, Google Scholar, Python).
- Receives a `$\langle\text{tool response}\rangle$` from the environment.
- Repeats for `$K$` total turns.

Critically, these lookahead turns use **real tool calls and real environment feedback**. The agent is actually searching the web, visiting pages, and receiving results—not simulating or hallucinating. This means the continuation reflects genuine downstream behavior, including whether the managed context enables the agent to find relevant information, make progress toward the answer, or get stuck.

**What the lookahead produces:** For each candidate strategy `$s \in \{\text{KLN}, \text{SUM}, \text{DA}\}$`, a continuation trace consisting of `$K$` new interaction turns appended to the managed context. This trace includes both the agent's reasoning (showing what it thinks is promising) and the environment's responses (showing what information is actually available).

**Step 2: Routing decision.** After all branches complete their lookahead rollouts, AgentSwing presents the candidate continuations to the agent model and asks it to select the most promising branch. The prompt includes:

- The **original raw context** (the full trajectory up to the trigger point, before any context management).
- Each candidate branch's **managed context plus its `$K$` lookahead turns**, labeled with the strategy name (e.g., "Branch 0: discard_all", "Branch 1: keep_last_n", "Branch 3: summary").
- An instruction to select the branch that is most likely to lead to successful task completion.

The agent model then outputs a reasoning trace explaining which branch it selects and why, followed by the selection.

**What the router considers:** The routing decision is based on both the managed context itself (e.g., "the summary preserves the key hypothesis about Lil Durk") and the downstream behavior (e.g., "but the Discard-All branch made no progress in its lookahead turns, while the Keep-Last-N branch identified the correct rapper and began verifying his discography"). This is fundamentally different from selecting a strategy based on a static quality metric—the router sees *where each path leads* before committing.

**Step 3: Branch selection and continuation.** The selected branch becomes the new main trajectory. The raw context is replaced with the managed context, and the `$K$` lookahead turns are prepended to the continuation. The agent then continues from this point, generating additional turns until either the task is completed or another trigger point is reached. The unselected branches are discarded, and their computational cost is not recoverable.

**Why the agent model serves as the router:** The paper uses the same agent model for both the lookahead rollouts and the routing decision (unless otherwise specified). This is a deliberate simplicity choice: no separate router model needs to be trained, and the agent model's own reasoning capabilities are leveraged to evaluate trajectory quality. The routing prompt effectively asks the model to perform a meta-reasoning task—"given these three possible futures, which one should I pursue?"—which is a natural extension of the model's existing reasoning capability.

**Why lookahead depth K = 3:** Table 3 reports an ablation comparing `$K = 1$`, `$K = 3$`, and `$K = 5$` for GPT-OSS-120B and Tongyi-DR-30B-A3B on BrowseComp:

| Routing Mechanism | GPT-OSS-120B | Tongyi-DR-30B-A3B |
|---|---|---|
| Random | 51.0 | 56.5 |
| w/o Lookahead | 50.0 | 57.0 |
| Lookahead (K = 1) | 52.5 | 58.0 |
| Lookahead (K = 3) | 60.0 | 60.5 |
| Lookahead (K = 5) | 55.0 | 59.0 |

**What this ablation reveals:**
- **Lookahead provides substantial gains over no lookahead.** The "w/o Lookahead" variant performs parallel context management but removes rollout, selecting solely based on the managed contexts themselves (essentially asking the model "which compressed context looks best?" without seeing future behavior). This performs similarly to random selection, confirming that the gains come from evaluating downstream consequences, not merely from having multiple candidate contexts.
- **Moderate lookahead (K = 3) is optimal.** `$K = 1$` provides only a single turn of future information, which may not be enough to distinguish promising branches from unpromising ones—the first turn after context management is often a broad search or a continuation of the previous direction, and the difference between branches may not be apparent until the second or third turn. `$K = 3$` provides enough future trajectory to see meaningful differences (e.g., one branch identifies the correct entity, another remains stuck). `$K = 5$` sometimes degrades performance, likely because the longer rollouts risk exceeding the maximum context length, and the additional turns may introduce noise or diverge in ways that make routing harder rather than easier.

**Why not train a dedicated router?** The paper acknowledges in Section 7 (Limitations) that the current routing mechanism is performed by the agent model itself, and that "a stronger dedicated router, verifier, or trajectory evaluator with better foresight may further improve branch selection quality." The choice to use the agent model as the router is a simplicity-performance trade-off: it avoids the need for additional training data, model serving infrastructure, and potential distribution shift between the router and the agent. However, it also means that routing quality is bounded by the agent model's own meta-reasoning capability—if the model is poor at evaluating trajectory quality, the routing decisions will be poor regardless of how informative the lookahead rollouts are.

**Why parallel branches rather than sequential trial-and-error?** A natural alternative would be to try one strategy, see if it works, and if not, backtrack and try another. AgentSwing's parallel design is motivated by two considerations: (1) **latency**: running branches in parallel means the total wall-clock time is determined by the slowest branch (the one with Summary, which requires an LLM call) plus `$K$` turns of interaction, rather than the sum of trying multiple strategies sequentially; (2) **information quality**: seeing all continuations side-by-side may enable better comparative reasoning than seeing them sequentially, especially since the router can directly contrast "this branch found the correct rapper in 3 turns" versus "this branch is still searching broadly."

**Connection to the efficiency-precision framework:** The routing decision can be understood as an implicit optimization over the efficiency-precision trade-off. A branch that makes concrete progress toward the answer within the lookahead horizon (e.g., identifying the correct entity and beginning verification) is likely to have both high `$\eta$` (it's on track to finish) and high `$\rho$` (the managed context preserved the right information to enable correct reasoning). A branch that shows no progress or gets stuck is likely to have low `$\eta$` (it may exhaust the turn budget before finishing) or low `$\rho$` (it's pursuing a wrong hypothesis). By selecting the branch with the most promising lookahead continuation, the router is effectively selecting the strategy that maximizes the expected product `$\eta \times \rho$` for the remainder of the trajectory.

#### Design Choices and Their Justifications

**Why three strategies rather than two or four?** The paper's ablation in Appendix A (Figure 8) tests various combinations with Tongyi-DR-30B-A3B on BrowseComp. Key findings:

- Single strategies: Discard-All (58.0%), Keep-Last-N (53.0%), Summary (55.0%).
- Two-strategy combinations: KLN+SUM (55.5%), DA+KLN (56.5%), DA+SUM (60.0%).
- Three identical copies of Discard-All: DA+DA+DA (59.0%). This is an important control—it tests whether gains come merely from having multiple branches or from strategy diversity. The result (59.0% vs. 58.0% for single Discard-All) suggests a small benefit from multiple branches even without strategy diversity (perhaps from multiple independent lookahead rollouts), but the gain is modest.
- All three strategies: DA+KLN+SUM (60.5%).

**Interpretation:** Adding more strategies generally helps, but with diminishing returns. The jump from the best single strategy (DA at 58.0%) to the best two-strategy combination (DA+SUM at 60.0%) is substantial. Adding Keep-Last-N to reach all three strategies yields a further small gain (60.5%). The authors do not test combinations beyond three, but the pattern suggests that strategy diversity matters more than the absolute number of branches—DA+SUM benefits from combining the high-precision reset strategy with the information-preserving compression strategy, covering complementary failure modes.

The choice of three strategies thus represents a practical sweet spot: enough diversity to cover the main efficiency-precision trade-off points (high-precision Discard-All, moderate-efficiency Keep-Last-N, efficiency-oriented Summary), but not so many branches that the computational cost of lookahead rollouts becomes prohibitive.

**Why the same agent model for both rollout and routing?** This is a deliberate simplicity choice with several justifications:
- **No additional model serving infrastructure:** Using the same model means no separate router needs to be deployed, trained, or maintained.
- **Calibration alignment:** The agent model's own reasoning about trajectory quality is naturally calibrated to its own capabilities—it knows what kinds of continuations it can successfully execute, whereas a separate router might overestimate or underestimate the agent's abilities.
- **Proven effectiveness:** The empirical results show this design works well across three different backbones, suggesting the meta-reasoning capability is sufficiently general.
- **Acknowledged limitation:** The paper explicitly states in Section 7 that a dedicated router could potentially improve further, and leaves this to future work.

**Why apply strategies in parallel rather than having the router choose a strategy first?** If the router could select the best strategy without needing to execute all of them, the computational cost would be lower (no wasted lookahead rollouts on branches that won't be selected). However, the paper's ablation shows that selecting without lookahead ("w/o Lookahead" in Table 3) performs no better than random. This suggests that **static properties of the managed context are insufficient for reliable strategy selection**—you need to see the downstream behavior to know whether a strategy is actually good for the current state. The parallel design accepts the computational overhead of wasted rollouts in exchange for reliable routing decisions.

**Why use real environment feedback during lookahead rather than simulated rollouts?** The paper does not discuss simulated rollouts, but the design choice to use real tool calls is significant. Real environment feedback means the lookahead continuation reflects genuine information availability—if a managed context leads the agent to search for something that doesn't exist or visit a page that's inaccessible, the environment will respond accordingly, and the router can observe this failure. Simulated rollouts (where the agent hallucinates tool responses) would be cheaper but would not provide reliable signal about whether the branch is actually making progress. The cost of real tool calls (web search API calls, page visits) is the price of reliable routing signal.

**Why doesn't AgentSwing always select the same strategy?** Figure 9 shows the empirical strategy-transition probabilities, and they are clearly non-uniform and model-dependent. GPT-OSS-120B transitions to Discard-All roughly 49% of the time regardless of the current strategy. DeepSeek-v3.2 and Tongyi-DR-30B-A3B both favor Summary (55–58% transition probability). This indicates that the routing behavior is not simply defaulting to the single best strategy—different models have different preferences, and the transitions depend on the current strategy (the matrices are not uniform rows). The fact that transition probabilities vary by model suggests that AgentSwing is genuinely adapting to model-specific context management needs, not just learning a fixed "best strategy."

**Connection to the broader test-time compute scaling paradigm.** The paper explicitly frames AgentSwing as a test-time scaling mechanism (Section 1, Figure 1 caption). The analogy is: just as best-of-N sampling spends inference compute on generating multiple candidate answers and selecting the best one, AgentSwing spends inference compute on generating multiple candidate context management decisions and selecting the best one. The "compute" here is the cost of the lookahead rollouts (real tool calls and model inference for `$K$` turns per branch), and the "scaling" is the ability to increase `$K$` or the number of candidate strategies to improve routing quality. The ablation in Table 3 shows that increasing `$K$` from 1 to 3 improves performance, consistent with a compute-scaling interpretation—more lookahead compute leads to better routing decisions, up to a point of diminishing returns.

#### Summary of the Full AgentSwing Pipeline

To consolidate, here is the complete execution flow for a single task under AgentSwing:

1. **Initialization:** The agent receives the user prompt `$q$` and begins standard ReAct-style interaction with the environment, accumulating `$\langle\text{thinking}, \text{tool call}, \text{tool response}\rangle$` turns.

2. **Trigger detection:** After each turn, the framework checks whether the total context length exceeds `$r \times$` the model's maximum context length. If not, normal operation continues.

3. **Parallel context management (trigger fired):** The current raw trajectory is processed by three strategies in parallel:
   - **Keep-Last-N:** Truncate to last 5 turns + user prompt.
   - **Summary:** Call GPT-OSS-120B to summarize the full trajectory; output is user prompt + summary.
   - **Discard-All:** Truncate to user prompt only.

4. **Parallel lookahead rollout:** Each of the three managed contexts is fed to the agent model, which continues interacting with the environment for `$K = 3$` additional turns using real tool calls. This produces three continuation traces, each containing the managed context plus 3 new `$\langle\text{thinking}, \text{tool call}, \text{tool response}\rangle$` tuples.

5. **Routing decision:** The agent model receives the original raw context plus all three continuation traces (labeled by strategy) and is prompted to select the most promising branch. The model outputs reasoning followed by a branch selection.

6. **Branch adoption:** The selected branch's managed context replaces the raw context. The lookahead turns for that branch are retained as part of the trajectory. Unselected branches are discarded.

7. **Continuation:** The agent continues normal operation from the selected branch's state. Steps 2–6 repeat if the context again exceeds the trigger threshold before the task is completed.

8. **Termination:** The task ends when the agent emits a final answer or exhausts the maximum interaction budget (400 turns, unless otherwise specified). If the budget is exhausted without an answer, the task is counted as failed.

**Computational cost accounting:** The additional cost of AgentSwing over a static strategy comes from the parallel lookahead rollouts. For each trigger event, AgentSwing executes `$3 \times K$` additional interaction turns (3 branches × `$K$` lookahead turns) that would not be executed under a static strategy. However, the paper's token efficiency analysis (Figure 6) shows that this overhead is modest in practice because AgentSwing tends to finish tasks in fewer total turns than static strategies (especially compared to Discard-All), partially offsetting the per-trigger overhead through fewer triggers overall. The aligned-case analysis in Table 2 shows AgentSwing achieving substantially lower average turn counts than Discard-All (e.g., 190.3 vs. 297.2 for GPT-OSS-120B on the shared triggered subset), confirming that the routing mechanism's turn savings outweigh its lookahead costs.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Decomposing Long-Horizon Success into η × ρ Reveals That Pass@1 Obscures Two Fundamentally Different Failure Modes

The paper's most conceptually distinctive contribution is not AgentSwing itself, but the **probabilistic framework that precedes and motivates it**. Prior to this work, the field evaluated long-horizon agents using monolithic metrics — Pass@1, accuracy, success rate — that collapse two structurally different processes into a single number. An agent that fails because it never reaches a stopping point (it wanders until its turn budget expires) and an agent that fails because it terminates with a wrong answer are treated identically, even though the interventions needed to fix them are completely different.

**What the field did before:** The dominant evaluation paradigm in web agent research — spanning benchmarks like WebArena, Mind2Web, BrowseComp, and HLE — treats end-to-end task success as an atomic event. An agent either gets the answer right or it doesn't. Context management strategies were compared solely by their final accuracy numbers, with no diagnostic vocabulary for understanding *why* one strategy outperformed another. When Liu et al. (2025a) showed that Discard-All improved DeepSeek's BrowseComp performance, the interpretation was straightforward but shallow: "resetting context helps." No framework existed to ask *how* it helps (by improving precision at the cost of efficiency) or *when* it helps (when the trajectory is noise-dominated but not when it contains valuable intermediate progress).

The paper's decomposition changes this. By defining `$\eta$` (search efficiency) as the probability of reaching a stopping point and `$\rho$` (terminal precision) as the probability of answering correctly conditioned on stopping, the framework provides a **diagnostic lens** that transforms "Discard-All improves accuracy" into "Discard-All sacrifices `$\eta$` to gain `$\rho$`, and the net effect on `$\eta \times \rho$` depends on the trajectory state." This is not just a relabeling — it makes testable predictions. If a strategy truly improves `$\rho$` at the cost of `$\eta$`, then its advantage should shrink under tight turn budgets (where `$\eta$` dominates) and grow under generous turn budgets (where `$\rho$` dominates). Figure 5 confirms exactly this: under small turn budgets (100–200 turns), some static context management strategies actually *underperform* the baseline without context management, because the baseline's high `$\eta$` is more valuable than the managed strategies' high `$\rho$` when there aren't enough turns to amortize the efficiency loss. Under large budgets (300–400 turns), all context management strategies surpass the baseline because `$\rho$` becomes the binding constraint.

**Why this is significant beyond performance numbers:** This framework provides the field with a **shared language for reasoning about context management trade-offs**. Before this paper, choosing between Discard-All, Keep-Last-N, and Summary was largely a matter of empirical trial-and-error, with different groups arriving at different conclusions depending on their specific benchmarks and turn budgets. After this paper, the choice can be framed in terms of where each strategy sits on the `$\eta$`-`$\rho$` plane. Figure 3b maps this explicitly: the baseline occupies the high-`$\eta$`, low-`$\rho$` corner; Discard-All occupies the low-`$\eta$`, high-`$\rho$` corner; Summary and Keep-Last-N occupy intermediate points. An ideal strategy would occupy the upper-right region (high `$\eta$` *and* high `$\rho$`), which static strategies cannot reach because they're constrained to operate along a trade-off frontier. This immediately suggests that the goal of future context management research should be to *break through this frontier* — and AgentSwing is presented as a first demonstration that adaptive routing can do so.

The **aligned terminal precision** metric (`$\rho_\text{align}$`, Equation 8) is a methodological contribution in its own right. Because different strategies finish on different task subsets, naive comparison of `$\rho$` across strategies can be misleading — a strategy with high `$\rho$` might simply be finishing on easier tasks while abandoning harder ones. By computing terminal precision only on the intersection of tasks that all strategies finish, `$\rho_\text{align}$` isolates the genuine precision difference without the confounding effect of differential finishing patterns. This is a small but principled refinement that future work should adopt as standard practice.

**What makes this a fundamental rather than incremental contribution:** The `$\eta \times \rho$` decomposition is not merely a new metric — it is a **conceptual reframing** of what context management is trying to optimize. The paper shows that the two dimensions respond differently to the same intervention, trade off against each other in predictable ways, and can be targeted independently by different strategies. This transforms context management from a heuristic engineering problem ("which compression method works best?") into an optimization problem ("how do we jointly maximize `$\eta$` and `$\rho$` given the current trajectory state?"). The fact that static strategies are constrained to a trade-off frontier while adaptive routing can push beyond it is a theoretical prediction of the framework that is borne out empirically in Figure 3b, where AgentSwing occupies a more favorable region of the plane than any static strategy.

---

### Innovation 2: Context Management as State-Dependent Routing — Not a Fixed Compression Policy

The second major intellectual move is the shift from treating context management as a **compression heuristic** (apply the same transformation to the accumulated history at every trigger point) to treating it as a **routing problem** (given the current trajectory state, which of several possible futures should we pursue?). This reframing is conceptually distinct from the specific mechanism (parallel branches + lookahead) that implements it, and it has implications that extend beyond AgentSwing's particular design.

**What the field did before:** All prior context management methods — Discard-All (Liu et al., 2025a; Team et al., 2026; Zeng et al., 2026a), Keep-Last-N (Liu et al., 2025a; Zeng et al., 2026a), various summarization approaches (Anthropic, 2025b; Yu et al., 2025; Ye et al., 2026), and context folding (Ye et al., 2026) — share a common structure: once a strategy is selected, **the same operation is applied at every trigger point throughout the entire trajectory**. The design question was "which single compression function is best?" and the answer was evaluated by aggregate benchmark performance. The implicit assumption was that the optimal compression function is invariant across trajectory states — that what works well on average works well everywhere.

AgentSwing challenges this assumption directly. The case studies in Section 4.4 and Appendix C provide the empirical counterexample: within a *single trajectory*, the optimal strategy changes. At Turn 23 of the "Mando" case (Figure 7), Keep-Last-N is optimal because the recent context contains a newly surfaced correct clue ("$tupid Young") while the earlier context is dominated by noise from incorrect hypotheses. At a comparable point in the "live-crickets" case (Appendix C, Tables 7–9), Discard-All is optimal because the entire recent context is a dead-end loop of failed PDF extraction attempts. A static strategy forced to apply the same operation at both trigger points would necessarily fail at one of them.

**Why this is a fundamental shift, not a small refinement:** The routing perspective changes what we mean by "a good context management strategy." Under the static paradigm, a good strategy is one whose compression function preserves the right information on average across all trajectory states — a property of the *function*. Under the routing paradigm, a good strategy is one that makes good *decisions* about which compression function to apply at each state — a property of the *decision policy*. This is a higher-level capability: it requires not just knowing how to compress, but knowing *when* each type of compression is appropriate.

The routing perspective also changes how we should evaluate context management. A static strategy that achieves 55% Pass@1 might be doing so by performing well on 55% of trajectory states and poorly on 45%. An adaptive strategy that achieves 60% Pass@1 might be doing so by selecting the right strategy 80% of the time and failing 20% of the time. The distinction matters for diagnosing failure modes: improving a static strategy requires redesigning the compression function (which may help some states while hurting others), while improving an adaptive strategy requires improving the routing decisions (which can be targeted at the specific states where poor choices are made).

**Tie to evidence:** The transition matrices in Figure 9 provide the most direct evidence that routing is genuine rather than degenerate. If AgentSwing were simply learning that one strategy is best and always selecting it, the transition matrices would have uniform rows (the same probability distribution over next strategies regardless of current strategy). Instead, they show model-specific, non-uniform patterns: GPT-OSS-120B transitions to Discard-All roughly 49% of the time from any current strategy, while DeepSeek-v3.2 and Tongyi-DR-30B-A3B favor Summary (55–58%). Moreover, the transition probabilities *do* depend on the current strategy — for GPT-OSS-120B, the probability of staying in Summary is 0.31 while the probability of transitioning from Keep-Last-N to Summary is 0.24 — suggesting that the routing decision is conditioned on trajectory state information that correlates with the current strategy. If routing were random or degenerate, these matrices would look very different.

---

### Innovation 3: Lookahead Information, Not Context Properties, Is What Makes Routing Decisions Reliable

The paper's third conceptual contribution is a **diagnostic finding about what information is necessary for effective strategy selection**. The natural intuition — and the approach one might first try — is to evaluate context management strategies based on the managed contexts themselves: does the summary look coherent? Does the truncated context preserve the most important clues? How many tokens did we save? The paper's ablation in Table 3 refutes this intuition decisively.

**The key result:** When AgentSwing removes the lookahead rollout and selects a branch based solely on the managed contexts ("w/o Lookahead" in Table 3), performance collapses to roughly the level of random selection (50.0% vs. 51.0% for GPT-OSS-120B, 57.0% vs. 56.5% for Tongyi-DR-30B-A3B). In other words, **static properties of the managed context contain essentially no signal about which strategy will lead to successful task completion**. The agent model cannot reliably distinguish a good compression from a bad one by inspecting the compressed output alone.

This is a negative result with substantial implications. It means that the quality of a context management decision is not an intrinsic property of the managed context — it is a property of how that context **interacts with the environment in subsequent turns**. A summary that perfectly captures the dominant hypothesis may lead to failure if that hypothesis is incorrect (as in the "Mando" case, where the Summary branch preserved the wrong "Lil Durk" focus). A Discard-All reset that seems wasteful may lead to success if the accumulated context was mostly noise (as in the "live-crickets" case). The only way to evaluate a context management decision is to **see where it leads** — to observe the downstream behavior under real environment feedback.

**What this tells us about the nature of the problem:** Context management is not like text summarization, where we can judge the quality of the output by its coherence, coverage, and faithfulness to the source. It is a **control decision** in a partially observable environment: the value of a managed context depends on what the agent will discover next, which depends on what information is actually available on the web, which cannot be predicted from the managed context alone. The lookahead mechanism is therefore not an optimization — it is a **necessary condition** for reliable routing. Without it, the routing decision is essentially blind.

**Why this is significant beyond AgentSwing's specific design:** This finding has implications for any system that needs to make state-dependent decisions about context curation. It suggests that approaches based on training a classifier to predict "good" vs. "bad" context management decisions from static features of the trajectory state would likely fail, because the features that determine downstream success are not present in the trajectory state — they emerge from the interaction between the managed context and the environment. The paper's lookahead mechanism is one way to surface these downstream features (by actually generating the future turns), but future work might explore alternatives: learned value functions that predict expected downstream success from trajectory state, process reward models trained on lookahead outcomes, or more efficient rollouts that don't require full environment interaction.

**Tie to evidence:** Table 3 is the critical ablation. The jump from "w/o Lookahead" (50.0%) to "Lookahead K=3" (60.0%) for GPT-OSS-120B represents a 10 percentage point gain attributable purely to the inclusion of downstream behavior information in the routing decision. The fact that Lookahead K=1 (52.5%) provides only a small gain over no lookahead while K=3 (60.0%) provides a large gain suggests that a single turn of future information is insufficient — meaningful differences between branches typically don't emerge until the second or third turn after context management, when the agent has had time to act on the information preserved (or lost) by the compression.

### Innovation 4: The Efficiency-Precision Trade-off Is a Frontier That Adaptive Routing Can Break Through — But Only with Sufficient Turn Budgets

The paper's fourth contribution is an empirical characterization of the **conditions under which adaptive context management provides gains over static strategies**, and what those conditions reveal about the structure of the problem.

**The key empirical pattern (Figure 5):** Under small maximum turn budgets (100–200 turns), context management provides limited gains over the baseline without context management, and some static strategies may even underperform it. Under large budgets (300–400 turns), all context management strategies consistently surpass the baseline, and AgentSwing maintains a widening advantage over static strategies. This pattern is not a quirk of one model — it replicates across GPT-OSS-120B, DeepSeek-v3.2, and Tongyi-DR-30B-A3B on BrowseComp.

**What this pattern means:** The efficiency-precision decomposition explains this directly. Under tight turn budgets, `$\eta$` (search efficiency) is the binding constraint — the primary failure mode is that the agent runs out of turns before finding sufficient evidence. The baseline without context management has the highest `$\eta$` because it never resets and therefore wastes no turns re-exploring, so it performs competitively despite low `$\rho$`. Context management strategies sacrifice `$\eta$` to gain `$\rho$`, but when turns are scarce, this trade-off is unfavorable — the precision gain cannot compensate for the efficiency loss because there aren't enough turns to amortize it.

Under generous turn budgets, the binding constraint flips: `$\rho$` (terminal precision) becomes the bottleneck. The baseline's large, noise-contaminated context degrades answer quality, and giving it more turns doesn't help because the problem isn't insufficient exploration — it's that the accumulated context is unreliable. Context management strategies gain `$\rho$` by maintaining cleaner working contexts, and with enough turns to compensate for their lower per-segment `$\eta$`, this precision advantage translates into higher overall Pass@1.

AgentSwing benefits from both regimes: it can select efficiency-oriented strategies (Keep-Last-N, Summary) when the trajectory contains useful progress, and precision-oriented strategies (Discard-All) when the trajectory is noise-dominated. This allows it to maintain competitive `$\eta$` while achieving higher `$\rho$` than any static strategy operating at a single point on the trade-off frontier.

**What makes this a diagnostic insight rather than just an empirical observation:** This pattern predicts that the **relative advantage of adaptive context management should grow with the turn budget**. Figure 5 confirms this: the gap between AgentSwing and the best static strategy widens as max turns increase from 100 to 400, because adaptive routing can increasingly exploit the `$\rho$` advantage of clean contexts without being penalized by the `$\eta$` disadvantage (since more turns provide more opportunities to finish). This is not an obvious prediction — one might have expected the opposite (that all strategies converge at high budgets) or that the advantage is constant. The fact that the gap widens suggests that adaptive routing is genuinely capturing a compounding benefit: better context management at early trigger points leads to more informative trajectories, which leads to better routing decisions at later trigger points, which leads to even better context management.

**Tie to evidence:** Table 2 provides the most granular evidence. On the subset of BrowseComp tasks where context management is triggered under all strategies (the "aligned" cases), AgentSwing achieves the highest Pass@1 across all three models (41.8% for GPT-OSS-120B vs. 35.2% for the best static strategy, 35.6% for DeepSeek-v3.2 vs. 32.9%, 31.1% for Tongyi-DR-30B-A3B vs. 20.0%). Critically, it achieves this with substantially fewer average turns than Discard-All (190.3 vs. 297.2 for GPT-OSS-120B) while matching or exceeding Discard-All's `$\rho$`. This is the signature of a strategy that has broken through the trade-off frontier: it achieves the precision of Discard-All with the efficiency of Summary/Keep-Last-N, something no static strategy can do.

### Innovation 5: Strategy Complementarity, Not Just Strategy Quality, Drives Adaptive Gains — With Implications for How We Design Candidate Sets

The final conceptual contribution is the empirical finding that **the composition of the candidate strategy set matters independently of the quality of individual strategies**. This is demonstrated most clearly in the combination ablation (Appendix A, Figure 8), but its implications extend beyond AgentSwing to any adaptive system that routes among heterogeneous options.

**The key result:** Adding a second strategy to the candidate set can provide gains even when the added strategy is individually weaker than the existing one. Discard-All alone achieves 58.0% on BrowseComp with Tongyi-DR-30B-A3B. Summary alone achieves 55.0%. But DA+Summary achieves 60.0% — higher than either individually. This is a **compositional gain**: the combination outperforms its best constituent because the router can select Discard-All when the trajectory state calls for aggressive resetting and Summary when it calls for information preservation, covering failure modes that neither strategy covers alone.

The control experiment with DA+DA+DA (three identical Discard-All branches, achieving 59.0%) is particularly revealing. This tests whether gains come from strategy diversity or merely from having multiple independent lookahead rollouts (which might provide better routing signal through ensemble-like effects). The result — 59.0% vs. 58.0% for single Discard-All — shows a small benefit from multiple branches even without strategy diversity, but this benefit is dwarfed by the gain from adding a *different* strategy (DA+Summary: 60.0%). The diversity of the candidate set is the primary driver of adaptive gains.

**Why this matters for how we think about test-time scaling:** In the standard best-of-N or beam search paradigm, scaling means generating more samples from the same distribution — more branches, but all using the same strategy. AgentSwing's results suggest a different scaling axis: **strategy diversity**. Two branches with different strategies can outperform three branches with the same strategy, because different strategies cover different regions of the `$\eta$`-`$\rho$` plane and are therefore complementary. This has direct implications for how to allocate a test-time compute budget: rather than spending all compute on more samples from the best-known strategy, invest some compute in evaluating alternative strategies, because the routing mechanism can extract value from their complementarity.

**What this suggests for future work:** The paper uses three strategies (Discard-All, Keep-Last-N, Summary) that span the main approach families in prior work. But the combination ablation suggests that exploring **richer or more specialized candidate strategies** could yield further gains — perhaps strategies that are optimized for specific trajectory states (e.g., a "hypothesis-preserving" summary that extracts only the current leading hypothesis, or a "breakthrough-detecting" truncation that identifies the most recent informative turn). The routing framework can accommodate any number of candidate strategies, and the gains come from covering more diverse points on the `$\eta$`-`$\rho$` frontier.

**Tie to evidence:** Figure 8 in Appendix A provides the direct ablation. The progression from single strategies (53.0–58.0%) to two-strategy combinations (55.5–60.0%) to the full three-strategy set (60.5%) shows consistent gains from increased diversity. The fact that DA+Summary (60.0%) is nearly as good as DA+KLN+Summary (60.5%) suggests diminishing returns — two complementary strategies may already capture most of the available gain — but the specific optimal combination likely depends on the model backbone and benchmark. The transition matrices in Figure 9 show that different models benefit from different strategies (GPT-OSS-120B favors Discard-All, DeepSeek-v3.2 and Tongyi-DR favor Summary), so the ideal candidate set should be tuned per backbone rather than assumed universal.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The paper evaluates on three long-horizon information-seeking benchmarks: **BrowseComp** (Wei et al., 2025; 200 randomly sampled tasks from the full set), **BrowseComp-ZH** (Zhou et al., 2025; full 289-task set), and **Humanity's Last Exam (HLE)** (Phan et al., 2025; 500 text-only tasks sampled following prior work by Li et al., 2025d and Nguyen et al., 2025). BrowseComp and BrowseComp-ZH assess deep web search and cross-page evidence synthesis; HLE additionally tests integration of academic search and computational reasoning.

- **Base model(s).** Three open-source models spanning different parameter scales and architectures: **GPT-OSS-120B** (OpenAI, 2025b; a 120B-parameter dense model), **DeepSeek-v3.2** (Liu et al., 2025a; a mixture-of-experts model), and **Tongyi-DeepResearch-30B-A3B** (Team, 2025b; a 30B-parameter model with 3B active parameters, referred to as Tongyi-DR-30B-A3B). The authors select these because they exhibit "strong tool-use capability for deep information-seeking tasks" (Section 4.1) and span a wide capability range—from frontier-scale (GPT-OSS-120B, DeepSeek-v3.2) to smaller, more efficient architectures (Tongyi-DR-30B-A3B)—enabling assessment of whether AgentSwing's benefits generalize across model scales and families. All models are invoked under their official function-calling protocol.

- **Metrics.** The primary metric is **Pass@1** (end-to-end task accuracy, equivalently `$\eta \times \rho$`). The paper additionally reports **search efficiency `$\eta$`** (fraction of tasks where the agent reaches a stopping point and emits a final answer before exhausting resources), **terminal precision `$\rho$`** (fraction of finished tasks answered correctly), and **aligned terminal precision `$\rho_\text{align}$`** (terminal precision computed only on tasks that finish under *all* compared strategies, to control for differential finishing patterns). All evaluations use the **LLM-as-a-Judge** protocol (Gu et al., 2024) with the official evaluation prompts and judging models released by each benchmark. The paper also reports **total interaction turns** and **cumulative token counts** for efficiency analysis.

- **Baselines.** The paper compares AgentSwing against four baselines:
  - **Baseline without context management (w/o CM):** The agent appends all interaction turns to its context until it either produces a final answer or exhausts the maximum context length (128K tokens for all models). Tasks that exhaust resources without an answer are counted as failed. This is the standard ReAct-style execution (Yao et al., 2023) with no context intervention.
  - **Discard-All** (Liu et al., 2025a; Team et al., 2026; Zeng et al., 2026a): When the context exceeds a trigger threshold, discard the entire accumulated trajectory and continue from only the original user prompt.
  - **Keep-Last-N** with N=5 (Liu et al., 2025a; Zeng et al., 2026a): When triggered, preserve only the last 5 `$\langle$`thinking, tool call, tool response`$\rangle$` tuples and discard earlier history.
  - **Summary** (Liu et al., 2025a; Anthropic, 2025b): When triggered, compress the accumulated trajectory into a summarized text using GPT-OSS-120B as the summarizer, and keep the context as the original user prompt plus the summary.

  For context, Table 1 also reports results from several proprietary foundation models with tools (Claude-4.5-Opus, Gemini-3.0-Pro, GPT-5.1 High, OpenAI-o3) and specialized deep information-seeking agents (OpenAI DeepResearch, ASearcher-Web-32B, DeepMiner-32B-RL, IterResearch-30B-A3B, AgentFold-30B-A3B, AgentFounder-30B-A3B, MiroThinker-v1.5-30B-A3B) as reference points, though these are not directly compared to AgentSwing under the same context management settings.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The primary resource constraint is **maximum interaction turns** (set to 400 for all context management strategies unless otherwise specified) and **maximum context length** (128K tokens for all models). Context management is triggered when the accumulated context exceeds a fraction `$r$` of the 128K maximum: `$r = 0.2$` (≈25.6K tokens) for GPT-OSS-120B, and `$r = 0.4$` (≈51.2K tokens) for both Tongyi-DR-30B-A3B and DeepSeek-v3.2. These thresholds follow "officially recommended or empirically optimal settings" (Section 4.1), with rationale discussed in the Discard-All analysis of Section 2.2. For AgentSwing specifically, the additional compute cost comes from the lookahead rollouts: at each trigger point, 3 branches × `$K = 3$` additional interaction turns are executed with real tool calls before the routing decision. The paper compares total interaction turns and cumulative token counts (Figure 6) to assess whether AgentSwing's overhead is offset by faster task completion.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not employ cross-validation—all evaluations are on fixed benchmark test sets. The aligned-case analyses (Table 2, Figures 3c, 6) restrict comparisons to the subset of tasks that trigger context management under *all* evaluated strategies within the same model backbone, enabling controlled comparison on shared hard cases. Statistical significance testing is not reported. The ablation of candidate strategy combinations (Appendix A, Figure 8) sweeps multiple strategy subsets on BrowseComp with Tongyi-DR-30B-A3B. The lookahead depth ablation (Table 3) tests `$K = 1, 3, 5$` plus random and no-lookahead controls for two model backbones on BrowseComp.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Overall Performance Across Benchmarks and Backbones

Table 1 reports the headline results. AgentSwing consistently outperforms all static baseline strategies across all three benchmarks and all three agent backbones. Specific numbers:

- **GPT-OSS-120B on BrowseComp:** AgentSwing achieves 60.0%, compared to 52.5% for the best static strategy (Keep-Last-N), 50.5% for Discard-All, 48.0% for Summary, and 39.5% for the baseline without context management. The gain over the best static strategy is 7.5 percentage points.
- **DeepSeek-v3.2 on BrowseComp:** AgentSwing achieves 62.5%, compared to 58.0% for the best static strategy (Discard-All), 52.0% for Keep-Last-N, 48.5% for Summary, and 43.5% for the baseline (the paper also reports the full-benchmark results: 51.4% baseline, 62.5% AgentSwing on the 200-task subset).
- **Tongyi-DR-30B-A3B on BrowseComp:** AgentSwing achieves 60.5%, compared to 58.0% for Discard-All, 55.0% for Summary, 53.0% for Keep-Last-N, and 48.0% for the baseline. The gap between AgentSwing and the best static strategy is 2.5 percentage points—smaller than for larger models, but still consistent.
- **Cross-benchmark pattern:** On BrowseComp-ZH, AgentSwing pushes DeepSeek-v3.2 to 71.3 (vs. 70.2 for Discard-All, the best static strategy) and GPT-OSS-120B to 38.0 (vs. 33.6 for Keep-Last-N). On HLE, gains are more modest: AgentSwing achieves 44.4 with DeepSeek-v3.2 (vs. 43.5 for Summary), 35.1 with GPT-OSS-120B (vs. 34.4 for Summary), and 33.1 with Tongyi-DR-30B-A3B (vs. 32.7 for Discard-All). The HLE gains are narrower, suggesting either that HLE's difficulty profile makes context management less impactful or that the 400-turn budget is insufficient for adaptive routing to compound.

The paper also notes that AgentSwing establishes leading performance for Tongyi-DR-30B-A3B "among deep information-seeking agents of comparable scale" (Table 1), and pushes DeepSeek-v3.2 to scores "surpassing several proprietary foundation models" on BrowseComp-ZH (71.3 vs. Claude-4.5-Opus at 62.4, Gemini-3.0-Pro at 66.8, OpenAI-o3 at 58.1) and HLE (44.4 vs. Claude-4.5-Opus at 43.4, Gemini-3.0-Pro at 45.8, GPT-5.1 High at 42.7). However, these comparisons are across different evaluation protocols (full benchmarks vs. sampled subsets, different tool configurations), so they should be treated as approximate reference points rather than controlled head-to-head comparisons.

#### Scaling with Interaction Budget

Figure 5 shows how performance scales with the maximum interaction budget (100, 200, 300, 400 turns) on BrowseComp for all three backbones. The key patterns:

- **At small budgets (100–200 turns):** Context management strategies provide limited gains over the baseline without context management, and some static strategies underperform it. For GPT-OSS-120B at 100 turns, the baseline achieves approximately 38% while Discard-All and Summary achieve roughly 35–37%. This is consistent with the efficiency-precision framework: under tight turn budgets, the baseline's high `$\eta$` (search efficiency) is the dominant factor, and context management's `$\rho$` advantage cannot compensate for its `$\eta$` disadvantage.

- **At moderate budgets (200–300 turns):** Context management strategies begin to consistently surpass the baseline. For GPT-OSS-120B, the crossover occurs around 200 turns, after which all static strategies remain above the baseline. AgentSwing maintains a widening lead over static strategies across this range.

- **At large budgets (300–400 turns):** AgentSwing's advantage over static strategies is largest. For GPT-OSS-120B at 400 turns, AgentSwing reaches 60.0% while the best static strategy (Keep-Last-N) reaches 52.5%—a gap of 7.5 points. For DeepSeek-v3.2, AgentSwing at 62.5% leads Discard-All at 58.0%. For Tongyi-DR-30B-A3B, AgentSwing at 60.5% leads Discard-All at 58.0%. The widening gap at higher budgets supports the interpretation that adaptive routing captures compounding benefits: better early context management enables more informative later trajectories, which enables better subsequent routing decisions.

- **AgentSwing's early-budget advantage:** Unlike static strategies, AgentSwing outperforms the baseline even at 100 turns for GPT-OSS-120B and DeepSeek-v3.2, suggesting that its adaptive routing can select efficiency-oriented strategies when turns are scarce, avoiding the efficiency penalty that hurts static precision-oriented strategies under tight budgets.

#### Efficiency-Precision Decomposition

Figure 3 provides the `$\eta$` vs. `$\rho$` analysis on BrowseComp (400-turn budget):

- **Figure 3a (Pass@1):** AgentSwing achieves the highest Pass@1 for both GPT-OSS-120B (≈61%) and Tongyi-DR-30B-A3B (≈60%), above all static strategies.

- **Figure 3b (efficiency-precision plane):** The baseline (w/o CM) occupies the high-`$\eta$` (≈90–95%), low-`$\rho$` (≈40–50%) region. Discard-All occupies the low-`$\eta$` (≈65–75%), high-`$\rho$` (≈60–80%) region. Summary and Keep-Last-N occupy intermediate points. AgentSwing moves to a more favorable region with `$\eta$` comparable to Summary/Keep-Last-N (≈80–90%) and `$\rho$` approaching Discard-All (≈60–70%), effectively breaking through the trade-off frontier that constrains static strategies.

- **Figure 3c (aligned terminal precision):** On the shared subset of tasks that finish under all strategies, AgentSwing achieves the highest `$\rho_\text{align}$` for both models. For GPT-OSS-120B, AgentSwing reaches approximately 77%, compared to roughly 73% for Discard-All, 68% for Keep-Last-N, and 63% for Summary. For Tongyi-DR-30B-A3B, AgentSwing reaches approximately 82%, compared to roughly 75% for Discard-All and 70% for Summary. This shows that AgentSwing's precision advantage is not an artifact of finishing on easier tasks—it genuinely produces more reliable answers on the same hard cases.

#### Aligned Hard-Case Analysis

Table 2 restricts analysis to the subset of BrowseComp tasks where context management is triggered under *all* compared strategies within each model backbone. This subset represents the hardest cases—tasks long enough that every strategy must intervene. On these aligned cases:

- **GPT-OSS-120B (122 aligned tasks):** AgentSwing achieves 41.8% Pass@1, compared to 35.2% for Keep-Last-N, 28.7% for both Discard-All and Summary. AgentSwing's `$\eta$` (73.8%) is close to the efficiency-oriented Keep-Last-N (74.6%), while its `$\rho$` (56.7%) approaches the precision-oriented Discard-All (68.6%). The combination yields the highest Pass@1. Critically, AgentSwing's average turn count (190.3) is substantially lower than Discard-All (297.2) and comparable to Keep-Last-N (205.4), showing that its gains do not come from paying a higher interaction cost.

- **DeepSeek-v3.2 (73 aligned tasks):** AgentSwing achieves 35.6% Pass@1, compared to 32.9% for Discard-All, 31.5% for Keep-Last-N, and 30.1% for Summary. AgentSwing's `$\eta$` (93.2%) is the highest among all strategies, while its `$\rho$` (38.2%) is lower than Discard-All (60.0%) but substantially higher than Summary (30.6%). Average turns: 151.9 for AgentSwing vs. 268.3 for Discard-All.

- **Tongyi-DR-30B-A3B (45 aligned tasks):** AgentSwing achieves 31.1% Pass@1, compared to 20.0% for all three static strategies. This is the largest relative gain (11.1 percentage points over the static best). AgentSwing's `$\eta$` (75.6%) is lower than Keep-Last-N (93.3%) but its `$\rho$` (41.2%) is substantially higher than any static strategy (Discard-All at 81.8% `$\rho$` but only 24.4% `$\eta$`, yielding lower Pass@1). Average turns: 203.6 for AgentSwing vs. 340.8 for Discard-All.

The consistent pattern across models: AgentSwing achieves Pass@1 gains by combining the efficiency of one strategy family (Keep-Last-N/Summary) with the precision of another (Discard-All), and it does so with lower turn counts than Discard-All, refuting the hypothesis that its gains simply come from executing more turns.

#### Lookahead Routing Ablation

Table 3 ablates the routing mechanism on BrowseComp with GPT-OSS-120B and Tongyi-DR-30B-A3B:

- **Random routing** (uniform random branch selection after context management): GPT-OSS-120B 51.0%, Tongyi-DR-30B-A3B 56.5%.
- **Without lookahead** (parallel context management but selects based solely on managed contexts, no rollout): GPT-OSS-120B 50.0%, Tongyi-DR-30B-A3B 57.0%.
- **Lookahead K=1:** GPT-OSS-120B 52.5%, Tongyi-DR-30B-A3B 58.0%.
- **Lookahead K=3:** GPT-OSS-120B 60.0%, Tongyi-DR-30B-A3B 60.5%.
- **Lookahead K=5:** GPT-OSS-120B 55.0%, Tongyi-DR-30B-A3B 59.0%.

The critical finding: removing lookahead entirely collapses performance to roughly random-selection levels (50.0% vs. 51.0% for GPT-OSS-120B, 57.0% vs. 56.5% for Tongyi-DR-30B-A3B). A single lookahead turn (K=1) provides only modest gains over random (52.5% vs. 51.0% for GPT-OSS-120B), suggesting that one turn of future information is insufficient to distinguish promising branches from dead ends. K=3 provides the optimal trade-off, with K=5 sometimes degrading performance—the paper attributes this to the risk of longer rollouts exceeding maximum context length constraints.

#### Token Efficiency Analysis

Figure 6 plots total interaction turns against cumulative token counts for each finished task on the aligned cases from Table 2. Each point represents one task. The key observation:

- **Discard-All** tends to require more turns to finish (points shifted rightward on the x-axis) but accumulates fewer tokens per turn (lower slope) because it resets context frequently.
- **Keep-Last-N and Summary** tend to finish in fewer turns but accumulate more tokens because they retain more trajectory history.
- **AgentSwing** points are distributed across the space, with many tasks finishing at low-to-moderate turn counts and moderate token counts—closer to the efficiency-oriented strategies on the turn axis but not as token-heavy. The paper states that "although AgentSwing introduces additional token usage due to lookahead routing, the overhead remains modest in practice" (Section 4.3).

The implication: AgentSwing's gains are not achieved by simply spending more tokens or more turns than static strategies. Its adaptive routing enables it to finish tasks with comparable or lower total resource consumption than the most efficient static strategies while achieving higher accuracy.

#### Strategy Transition Analysis

Figure 9 reports empirical strategy-transition probabilities under AgentSwing on BrowseComp:

- **GPT-OSS-120B:** Transitions to Discard-All approximately 49% of the time regardless of current strategy. Transitions to Summary ~31% and Keep-Last-N ~20%. The matrix rows are nearly uniform, suggesting that for this model, the router strongly prefers Discard-All across most trajectory states, with occasional selections of Summary or Keep-Last-N.

- **DeepSeek-v3.2:** Strongly favors Summary (55% from Summary, 52% from Keep-Last-N, 55% from Discard-All). Discard-All is selected 18–20% of the time, Keep-Last-N 26–28%. The preference for Summary is consistent across all current strategies.

- **Tongyi-DR-30B-A3B:** Similar to DeepSeek-v3.2, favors Summary (55–58%). Discard-All is selected more frequently when the current strategy is Discard-All (31%) than when the current is Summary (20%) or Keep-Last-N (25%).

These non-uniform, model-dependent patterns confirm that routing behavior is not random and not simply defaulting to a single best strategy. The fact that transition probabilities differ across backbones suggests that different models have different context management needs—likely reflecting differences in context utilization efficiency, sensitivity to noise, or capability to recover from resets.

#### Comparison with Foundation Models and Specialized Agents

Table 1 contextualizes AgentSwing's performance against several reference systems, though the comparisons are not controlled (different tools, different protocols, different evaluation subsets):

- On BrowseComp, AgentSwing with DeepSeek-v3.2 (62.5%) substantially exceeds Claude-4.5-Opus (37.0%), Gemini-3.0-Pro (37.8%), and GPT-5.1 High (50.8%)—all foundation models with tools but without specialized context management. It also exceeds specialized agents: OpenAI DeepResearch (51.5%), AgentFounder-30B-A3B (39.9%), and IterResearch-30B-A3B (37.3%), though it trails MiroThinker-v1.5-30B-A3B (56.1%).

- On BrowseComp-ZH, AgentSwing with DeepSeek-v3.2 (71.3%) exceeds all listed systems including Claude-4.5-Opus (62.4%), Gemini-3.0-Pro (66.8%), and MiroThinker-v1.5-30B-A3B (66.8%).

- On HLE, AgentSwing with DeepSeek-v3.2 (44.4%) is competitive with foundation models (Claude-4.5-Opus 43.4%, Gemini-3.0-Pro 45.8%, GPT-5.1 High 42.7%) and exceeds all listed specialized agents except MiroThinker-v1.5-30B-A3B (31.0% vs. AgentSwing's 44.4%).

The paper does not claim these as direct apples-to-apples comparisons—they serve as approximate reference points for situating AgentSwing's performance in the broader landscape.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Candidate strategy combination:** Figure 8 (Appendix A) ablates different subsets of candidate strategies within AgentSwing on BrowseComp with Tongyi-DR-30B-A3B. Single strategies: Discard-All 58.0%, Summary 55.0%, Keep-Last-N 53.0%. Two-strategy combinations: DA+Summary 60.0%, DA+Keep-Last-N 56.5%, Keep-Last-N+Summary 55.5%. Three identical Discard-All branches (DA+DA+DA): 59.0%. All three strategies (DA+KLN+Summary): 60.5%. The key finding: adding a complementary strategy (DA+Summary: 60.0%) substantially outperforms either constituent alone (DA: 58.0%, Summary: 55.0%), while adding three identical branches (DA+DA+DA: 59.0%) provides only a marginal gain over a single branch (58.0%). This confirms that strategy diversity, not merely branch count, drives adaptive gains.

**Lookahead depth:** Table 3. Discussed in detail above. K=3 is optimal; K=1 provides minimal gain over no lookahead; K=5 can degrade performance relative to K=3 (GPT-OSS-120B: 55.0% vs. 60.0%; Tongyi-DR: 59.0% vs. 60.5%).

**Without lookahead vs. random:** Table 3. Removing lookahead (selecting based on managed context alone) performs no better than random selection for both tested models (GPT-OSS-120B: 50.0% vs. 51.0%; Tongyi-DR: 57.0% vs. 56.5%). This is a strong negative result showing that static properties of managed contexts contain essentially no signal for predicting downstream success.

**Interaction budget scaling (implicit ablation):** Figure 5. All context management strategies show a characteristic scaling pattern: limited gains (or even underperformance) at low turn budgets, consistent gains at moderate-to-high budgets, with AgentSwing showing the widest advantage at the highest budgets. This pattern is consistent across all three backbones.

**Context budget threshold (Discard-All analysis):** Figure 2. Varying the trigger ratio `$r$` for Discard-All on BrowseComp shows that aligned terminal precision consistently decreases as context budget increases, while Pass@1 shows a non-monotonic pattern with an optimal intermediate budget. This analysis motivates the model-specific trigger thresholds (`$r = 0.2$` for GPT-OSS-120B, `$r = 0.4$` for others) and explains why "one-size-fits-all" context budgets are suboptimal.

**Aligned-case analysis:** Table 2. Discussed in detail above. By restricting to tasks that trigger context management under all strategies, this ablation controls for strategy-specific finishing patterns and isolates genuine precision and efficiency differences on the hardest shared cases.

**Model-specific trigger ratios:** The paper uses different trigger thresholds for different models (`$r = 0.2$` for GPT-OSS-120B, `$r = 0.4$` for DeepSeek-v3.2 and Tongyi-DR-30B-A3B) but does not provide a systematic ablation of `$r$` for AgentSwing specifically—only for Discard-All (Figure 2). The sensitivity of AgentSwing's performance to `$r$` is therefore not fully characterized.

**Summarizer model:** All Summary operations use GPT-OSS-120B as the summarizer, even when the agent model is DeepSeek-v3.2 or Tongyi-DR-30B-A3B. The paper does not ablate the choice of summarizer model, so it is unclear whether summarization quality (and thus the Summary strategy's contribution to AgentSwing's gains) depends on using a strong, fixed summarizer or would transfer to using the agent model itself for summarization.

### Critical Assessment

#### Claim 1: AgentSwing consistently outperforms static context management strategies.

**Assessment:** Supported, with meaningful caveats about conditionality. The evidence in Table 1 shows consistent Pass@1 gains across all three benchmarks and all three backbones. However, the magnitude of gains varies substantially:

- Large gains on BrowseComp (7.5 percentage points over best static for GPT-OSS-120B, 4.5 for DeepSeek-v3.2, 2.5 for Tongyi-DR-30B-A3B) and BrowseComp-ZH (4.4 points for GPT-OSS-120B, 1.1 for DeepSeek-v3.2, 2.8 for Tongyi-DR-30B-A3B).
- Modest gains on HLE (0.7–1.4 percentage points over best static). The paper does not analyze why HLE gains are smaller, but several hypotheses are plausible: HLE questions may require more domain-specific reasoning where context rot is less severe, the 400-turn budget may be insufficient for HLE's longer required trajectories, or HLE's question distribution may skew toward difficulty levels where context management provides less leverage.

The Figure 5 scaling curves reveal that AgentSwing's advantage is **conditioned on having a sufficiently large turn budget**—at 100 turns, some static strategies underperform the baseline, and AgentSwing's advantage is minimal. The "up to 3× fewer interaction turns" claim in the abstract (matching static strategy performance at lower budgets) is supported by Figure 5: AgentSwing at roughly 200 turns matches or exceeds static strategies at 400 turns for GPT-OSS-120B and DeepSeek-v3.2, though this specific 3× ratio is a rough visual estimate rather than a precisely quantified metric.

A limitation: the paper reports only aggregate Pass@1 per benchmark. It does not report per-difficulty-bin breakdowns (analogous to the quintile analysis in the reference example paper), which would reveal whether AgentSwing's gains are concentrated on specific types of tasks (e.g., tasks requiring extensive backtracking, tasks with clear vs. ambiguous clues) or are uniform. The per-difficulty analysis that the probabilistic framework enables is not conducted.

#### Claim 2: AgentSwing breaks through the efficiency-precision trade-off frontier that constrains static strategies.

**Assessment:** Supported by the `$\eta$` vs. `$\rho$` analysis in Figure 3b, but with important nuances. Figure 3b shows AgentSwing occupying a region of the plane with higher `$\eta$` than Discard-All and higher `$\rho$` than Summary/Keep-Last-N, consistent with the claim. However:

- The frontier itself is not formally derived—it is an empirical observation from four data points (baseline, Summary, Keep-Last-N, Discard-All). Three static strategies plus a baseline do not define a frontier in any rigorous sense. The paper's language about "breaking through the trade-off frontier" should be understood as an empirical observation that AgentSwing achieves a combination of `$\eta$` and `$\rho$` not achieved by any individual static strategy tested, rather than a proof that static strategies *cannot* achieve such combinations.
- The `$\eta$` and `$\rho$` estimates are computed from a single run per task per strategy (the binary finish/correct indicators), not from multiple runs that would provide per-task probability estimates. The probabilistic framework defines `$\eta^\pi_\tau$` and `$\rho^\pi_\tau$` as probabilities, but the empirical estimates collapse to binary indicators at the task level, aggregated across tasks. This means the efficiency-precision decomposition is computed across-task rather than within-task—it captures which tasks a strategy fails to finish vs. which finished tasks it answers incorrectly, but does not capture per-task stochasticity.

#### Claim 3: Lookahead information (not static context properties) is necessary for effective routing.

**Assessment:** Strongly supported by Table 3. The "w/o Lookahead" variant performing at random-selection levels is a striking and clean result. This is one of the paper's most robust findings, and it has clear implications for future work on adaptive context management. However:

- The "w/o Lookahead" variant still uses the agent model to make the selection—it's possible that a different selection mechanism (e.g., a trained classifier using features of the managed context) could extract signal that the agent model's zero-shot judgment misses. The paper's result shows that **zero-shot model judgment of managed contexts** contains no signal, not that **no possible static signal** exists.
- The comparison to "random" as a baseline is appropriate, but it would be informative to also compare against an "oracle" upper bound (always select the best branch, as determined by which branch would have led to task success). Without an oracle comparison, we don't know how much room for improvement remains in the routing mechanism.

#### Claim 4: Strategy diversity (not just branch count) drives AgentSwing's gains.

**Assessment:** Supported by Appendix A, Figure 8. The DA+DA+DA control (59.0%) vs. DA+Summary (60.0%) cleanly isolates the diversity effect. However:

- The experiment only tests duplicate Discard-All branches, not duplicate Summary or duplicate Keep-Last-N branches. The small gain from multiple identical branches (59.0% vs. 58.0%) might be specific to Discard-All—multiple independent lookahead rollouts from the same managed context might provide more benefit for strategies with higher-variance lookahead behavior.
- The paper tests only the three canonical strategies. It does not test whether adding *more* diverse strategies (e.g., Keep-Last-N with different N values, or different summarization prompts) yields further gains, or whether a small set of carefully chosen complementary strategies captures most of the available benefit. The DA+Summary combination (60.0%) being close to the full three-strategy set (60.5%) suggests diminishing returns, but this is not systematically explored.

#### Genuine Weaknesses and Missing Experiments

**No per-difficulty analysis.** Given that the probabilistic framework decomposes success into `$\eta$` and `$\rho$`, and that these respond differently to context management, the most natural analysis—how AgentSwing's gains vary with task difficulty—is absent. Are the gains concentrated on tasks where the baseline has low `$\rho$` (noise-dominated trajectories)? On tasks where the baseline has low `$\eta$` (premature context exhaustion)? The paper has the machinery to answer these questions but does not.

**Small sample sizes for some analyses.** The aligned-case analysis in Table 2 uses subsets of 45–122 tasks per model (from a 200-task BrowseComp sample). The `$\rho$` estimates on these subsets have wide confidence intervals. For Tongyi-DR-30B-A3B with only 45 aligned tasks, the difference between AgentSwing's 31.1% Pass@1 and the static strategies' 20.0% corresponds to a difference of only 5 tasks—meaningful in percentage terms but based on a small absolute number. Confidence intervals or statistical tests would substantially strengthen these comparisons.

**The trigger threshold `$r$` is not systematically optimized for AgentSwing.** The paper uses model-specific `$r$` values motivated by the Discard-All analysis in Figure 2, but does not verify that these are optimal when the agent is using adaptive routing rather than a single static strategy. AgentSwing's ability to switch strategies might change the optimal trigger threshold—for instance, if the router can detect when the current context is noise-dominated, it might benefit from triggering *earlier* (lower `$r`) to give Discard-All more opportunities.

**No latency or wall-clock time analysis.** AgentSwing's parallel context management and lookahead rollouts introduce additional latency per trigger event: three LLM calls for context management (one for Summary, two trivial truncations) plus 3 × K = 9 additional interaction turns with real tool calls and environment responses. For K=3 and a trigger event occurring, say, 5 times per trajectory, this adds ~45 extra turns of latency. The paper reports turn-count efficiency (Table 2, Figure 6) but not wall-clock time. For latency-sensitive applications, this overhead could be prohibitive even if total turn counts are lower.

**The reference comparisons in Table 1 are not controlled.** The comparisons to foundation models (Claude-4.5-Opus, Gemini-3.0-Pro, etc.) use different tool configurations, different evaluation subsets, and different prompting protocols. The paper does not claim these as controlled comparisons, but their presentation in Table 1 without explicit caveats about protocol differences could be misleading. For instance, the HLE results for AgentSwing (44.4 with DeepSeek-v3.2) and for Claude-4.5-Opus (43.4) appear comparable, but Claude's result is on the full HLE benchmark while AgentSwing's is on a 500-question text-only subset—these are not directly comparable.

**No experiment combining AgentSwing with a stronger agent model or different tool configurations.** All experiments use the standard Search + Visit tool configuration (plus Google Scholar and Python for HLE). It would be informative to see whether AgentSwing's relative gains persist or change with richer tool suites, or with agent models that have been specifically fine-tuned for long-horizon search (rather than the general-purpose GPT-OSS-120B and DeepSeek-v3.2).

**No analysis of when routing fails.** The case studies (Figure 7, Appendix C) show successful routing decisions. But what happens when the router makes a poor choice? Does the trajectory degrade gracefully, or does a wrong routing decision at one trigger point cascade into failure? The paper's transition matrices (Figure 9) show that the router does not always select the same strategy—some transitions presumably lead to worse outcomes than alternatives. An analysis of routing errors (how often the selected branch was actually worse than an unselected branch, and by how much) would characterize the reliability of the routing mechanism and its failure modes.

**The `$\eta$` and `$\rho$` estimates conflate strategy-specific finishing patterns with strategy quality.** Because `$\rho$` is computed only on tasks that a given strategy finishes, a strategy that finishes on only the easiest tasks will have high `$\rho$` even if it answers those tasks no better than other strategies. The aligned `$\rho_\text{align}$` metric addresses this partially, but only for the subset of tasks that *all* strategies finish—which is a small and potentially unrepresentative subset (e.g., 45 out of 200 BrowseComp tasks for Tongyi-DR-30B-A3B). The paper's efficiency-precision framework is elegant, but the empirical estimates are noisy and confounded in ways the paper does not fully address.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### Latency Overhead from Parallel Lookahead Rollouts Is Unmeasured and May Be Prohibitive for Interactive Deployments

**The assumption or constraint:** AgentSwing's core operational loop introduces substantial per-trigger latency: at each context management trigger point, the framework executes 3 parallel branches × K lookahead turns (K = 3 in the default configuration) using real tool calls with real environment feedback, plus one additional LLM call for the Summary strategy's context compression. The paper measures efficiency in *interaction turns* and *cumulative token counts* (Figure 6, Table 2) but never reports wall-clock time or end-to-end latency. In Section 4.3, the authors state only that "although AgentSwing introduces additional token usage due to lookahead routing, the overhead remains modest in practice," with no quantitative latency characterization.

**The consequence:** For a typical trajectory that triggers context management, say, 5 times over the course of a task, AgentSwing executes approximately 5 × (3 × 3) = 45 additional interaction turns that a static strategy would not execute—plus the Summary LLM call at each trigger point. Each of these turns involves a full agent model inference (thinking + tool call generation), a real web search or page visit, and processing the environment response. In a latency-sensitive deployment (interactive assistant, real-time research tool), this overhead could easily add minutes of wall-clock time per task. A user waiting for an answer may find the latency unacceptable even if the answer quality is higher. The turn-count analysis in Table 2 shows AgentSwing finishes aligned hard cases in fewer total turns than Discard-All (e.g., 190.3 vs. 297.2 for GPT-OSS-120B), but this comparison *excludes* the lookahead turns from the count—those turns are spent on branches that are ultimately discarded and do not contribute to the final answer trajectory. In wall-clock terms, those discarded turns still consume time, API calls, and web search quota.

**What evidence exists in the paper:** None. The paper provides no latency measurements, no wall-clock comparisons to static strategies, and no analysis of how much time the lookahead rollouts add per trigger event. The token efficiency analysis (Figure 6) plots total tokens vs. total turns for finished tasks, but token count is a poor proxy for latency—a single Summary call to GPT-OSS-120B may have different latency characteristics than three parallel search-and-visit turns executed by a smaller model. The ablation in Table 3 shows that K = 3 lookahead turns provides substantially better routing accuracy than K = 1 (60.0% vs. 52.5% for GPT-OSS-120B), but the latency implications of this choice are not discussed—a practitioner cannot determine whether the accuracy gain from K = 3 justifies the ~3× increase in per-trigger latency relative to K = 1.

**Mitigation status:** Not addressed. The paper acknowledges only token overhead ("additional token usage") without mentioning latency. Future work could explore whether the lookahead turns can be executed with cheaper models or simulated rollouts, or whether an asymmetric design (where the router evaluates branches with fewer or cheaper turns than the main agent uses) could reduce latency while preserving routing quality. The paper does not suggest these directions.

### Difficulty Estimation and Per-Difficulty Analysis Are Absent, Leaving the Framework's Diagnostic Promise Partially Unfulfilled

**The assumption or constraint:** The probabilistic framework in Section 2 decomposes end-to-end success into η × ρ and argues that this decomposition enables diagnostic reasoning about *why* strategies succeed or fail, and *under what conditions* adaptive routing provides gains. However, the paper never applies this framework to analyze **how AgentSwing's benefits vary with task difficulty**. All reported analyses are aggregate (per-benchmark Pass@1) or aligned-case (restricted to tasks that trigger context management under all strategies). There is no difficulty binning, no analysis of whether AgentSwing's gains are concentrated on easy, medium, or hard tasks, and no characterization of which failure modes (η failures vs. ρ failures) AgentSwing primarily addresses at different difficulty levels.

**The consequence:** A practitioner cannot determine whether AgentSwing is worth deploying for their specific task distribution. If a deployment's task mix skews toward very hard problems (where the baseline's Pass@1 is near zero—analogous to difficulty bin 5 in the reference example paper's analysis), AgentSwing may provide negligible benefit because the agent lacks the underlying capability to solve those tasks regardless of context management quality. Conversely, if the task mix is predominantly easy, the baseline without context management may already perform adequately, and the latency overhead of adaptive routing may not be justified. Without per-difficulty characterization, the paper's claim that AgentSwing "consistently outperforms strong static methods" (Section 1) provides no guidance about *where* in the difficulty spectrum these gains materialize.

**What evidence exists in the paper:** None. The framework defines η and ρ at the task level (equations 1–4) and provides population-level estimates (equations 5–8), but all empirical analyses aggregate across tasks. The aligned-case analysis (Table 2) restricts to tasks where context management is triggered under all strategies—these are necessarily harder-than-average tasks (since they are long enough to require intervention under even the most aggressive strategy), but this is a coarse binary split rather than a difficulty spectrum. The transition matrices (Figure 9) show that different models have different strategy preferences, but this is not linked to task difficulty. The paper's own framework provides the vocabulary for difficulty-conditioned analysis (pass@1 rates could be used to bin tasks into difficulty quintiles, directly analogous to the reference example paper's approach), but this analysis is not conducted.

**Mitigation status:** Not addressed. The framework's diagnostic potential—understanding *which* tasks benefit from adaptive routing and *why*—remains unrealized. Future work could use the η × ρ decomposition to bin tasks by baseline pass@1 and analyze how AgentSwing's gains vary across bins, which would directly address the deployment-relevant question of where adaptive context management is worth its overhead.

### The Summary Strategy Uses a Fixed External Model, Creating a Dependency That Limits Reproducibility and Complicates Attribution

**The assumption or constraint:** In all experiments, the Summary context management strategy uses **GPT-OSS-120B as the summarizer**, regardless of which model serves as the agent backbone. This is stated explicitly in Section 4.1: "For Summary, the summarization step is always performed by GPT-OSS-120B." The paper does not ablate this choice—there is no experiment where DeepSeek-v3.2 or Tongyi-DR-30B-A3B serves as its own summarizer, nor any experiment using a different external summarizer.

**The consequence:** Two distinct problems arise. First, **reproducibility**: GPT-OSS-120B is a specific 120B-parameter model released by OpenAI. A researcher attempting to replicate AgentSwing's results in a setting where GPT-OSS-120B is unavailable (e.g., due to API access restrictions, cost, or deployment constraints) cannot determine whether the Summary strategy's contribution to AgentSwing's gains depends on this specific summarizer or would transfer to a locally available model. If GPT-OSS-120B's summaries are substantially higher quality than what DeepSeek-v3.2 or Tongyi-DR-30B-A3B could produce themselves, then the reported gains for those backbones may partially reflect the summarizer's capability rather than AgentSwing's routing mechanism.

Second, **attribution**: When AgentSwing outperforms static strategies with DeepSeek-v3.2 or Tongyi-DR-30B-A3B as the agent backbone, it is unclear how much of the gain comes from the routing mechanism versus from access to a stronger model (GPT-OSS-120B) for the summarization subtask. The Summary branch is one of three candidate strategies at each trigger point, and the transition matrices in Figure 9 show that DeepSeek-v3.2 and Tongyi-DR-30B-A3B select Summary roughly 55–58% of the time. If these models are selecting Summary *because* its GPT-OSS-120B-generated summaries are higher quality than what they could produce themselves, then AgentSwing's routing mechanism is partly a vehicle for leveraging a stronger auxiliary model—a different claim than "adaptive context management routing improves performance."

**What evidence exists in the paper:** The combination ablation in Appendix A (Figure 8) shows that the Summary strategy contributes meaningfully to AgentSwing's gains: DA+Summary achieves 60.0% vs. 58.0% for Discard-All alone with Tongyi-DR-30B-A3B. However, this ablation does not vary the summarizer model, so it cannot distinguish whether Summary's contribution comes from the *type* of context management (semantic compression) or from the *quality* of the specific summarizer used. The paper does not report any experiment where the agent model serves as its own summarizer, nor any comparison of summarization quality across models.

**Mitigation status:** Not addressed. The paper does not acknowledge this as a limitation, and the summarizer choice is stated as a fixed experimental parameter without justification or ablation. Future work should test whether AgentSwing's gains persist when the agent model serves as its own summarizer, which would clarify whether the routing mechanism itself or access to a stronger auxiliary model drives the reported improvements. A weaker but still informative alternative would be to use the same external summarizer for the static Summary baseline—this is already the case—but to also report results with a matched-capability summarizer for each backbone.

### Gains on HLE Are Modest and Unexplained, Suggesting the Approach May Not Generalize to All Long-Horizon Task Types

**The assumption or constraint:** The paper evaluates on three benchmarks—BrowseComp, BrowseComp-ZH, and HLE—and claims that AgentSwing "consistently outperforms strong static context management methods" (Section 1) and "demonstrate[s] the effectiveness and generalization of AgentSwing" (Section 1). However, the results on HLE reveal a substantially different pattern from BrowseComp and BrowseComp-ZH, which the paper does not analyze or explain.

**The consequence:** On BrowseComp, AgentSwing's advantage over the best static strategy ranges from 2.5 to 7.5 percentage points across backbones (Table 1). On BrowseComp-ZH, the range is 1.1 to 4.4 points. On HLE, the advantage narrows to 0.4–1.4 points: GPT-OSS-120B: AgentSwing 35.1% vs. Summary 34.4% (+0.7); DeepSeek-v3.2: 44.4% vs. Summary 43.5% (+0.9); Tongyi-DR-30B-A3B: 33.1% vs. Discard-All 32.7% (+0.4). These gains are small enough that they could plausibly fall within the noise of the evaluation (500 tasks, no confidence intervals reported). A practitioner evaluating whether to deploy AgentSwing on HLE-like tasks—which involve academic search, computational reasoning, and domain-specific knowledge integration—would conclude from these numbers that the benefit is marginal at best.

The paper's silence on *why* HLE gains are small leaves several important hypotheses untested. HLE tasks may require fundamentally different search patterns than BrowseComp tasks (e.g., more single-source fact retrieval and less cross-page evidence synthesis), making context rot less severe. HLE's 400-turn budget may be insufficient for AgentSwing's routing mechanism to compound its benefits (analogous to Figure 5's finding that gains are minimal at low turn budgets). HLE tasks may have a difficulty distribution skewed toward problems where the baseline already has high accuracy, leaving little room for context management to help. Without analysis, the generalization claim remains unqualified and potentially misleading.

**What evidence exists in the paper:** Table 1 reports the HLE numbers. No further analysis of HLE is provided—no efficiency-precision decomposition on HLE, no aligned-case analysis, no scaling curves by interaction budget, no difficulty binning. The paper does not acknowledge the discrepancy between HLE and BrowseComp results, nor does it offer hypotheses for why the pattern differs. This is a missed opportunity: analyzing *where* AgentSwing fails to provide gains would be as informative as analyzing where it succeeds.

**Mitigation status:** Not addressed. The paper's claims of generalization and consistency are not qualified by the HLE results. Future work should characterize the task properties that determine whether adaptive context management provides value—e.g., trajectory length distribution, prevalence of misleading clues requiring backtracking, ratio of search to reasoning turns—and use this characterization to predict when AgentSwing will help vs. when static strategies suffice.

### Turn Budget Dictates Whether Adaptive Routing Helps or Hurts, Creating a Deployment Regime Where the Approach Underperforms the Baseline

**The assumption or constraint:** The paper's primary experimental configuration uses a 400-turn maximum interaction budget for all context management strategies. Figure 5 reveals that **the relative performance of context management strategies—including AgentSwing—depends critically on the available turn budget**, and that under sufficiently small budgets, static strategies can underperform the baseline without context management, and AgentSwing's advantage shrinks substantially.

**The consequence:** Figure 5 shows that at 100-turn budgets on BrowseComp, the baseline without context management achieves roughly 38% for GPT-OSS-120B and approximately 43% for DeepSeek-v3.2. Several static strategies score *below* these baselines at 100 turns. AgentSwing outperforms the baseline at 100 turns for both models, but the margin is small—approximately 2–4 percentage points—compared to the 7.5-point gap at 400 turns. At 200 turns, the crossover becomes clearer, but the gap remains substantially smaller than at 300–400 turns.

This creates a **practical deployment constraint**: if an application has a tight latency budget that limits total interaction turns (e.g., a user-facing tool where each turn adds seconds of waiting time), AgentSwing's additional per-trigger overhead (see Latency limitation above) may combine with the reduced per-turn accuracy advantage to make the approach net-negative relative to a simpler baseline. A practitioner with a hard constraint of, say, 150 turns per task cannot determine from the paper's results whether AgentSwing at 150 turns outperforms the baseline—Figure 5 shows only 4 budget points (100, 200, 300, 400), and the region between 100 and 200 turns is not characterized. For Tongyi-DR-30B-A3B at 100 turns, the baseline and AgentSwing appear nearly identical (the curves cross near 100).

**What evidence exists in the paper:** Figure 5 provides the scaling curves for BrowseComp at four budget levels. The paper does not analyze the budget-dependent behavior in terms of the η × ρ framework (e.g., showing how η and ρ each scale with budget for different strategies), which would provide insight into *why* the baseline performs well at low budgets (high η dominates when turns are scarce). No scaling analysis is provided for BrowseComp-ZH or HLE. The paper does not report the minimum turn budget at which AgentSwing statistically significantly outperforms the best static strategy—this "break-even budget" would be a practically useful summary statistic.

**Mitigation status:** Not addressed. The paper's abstract and introduction present AgentSwing's gains without qualifying their dependence on turn budget, and the "up to 3× fewer interaction turns" framing (in the abstract and Figure 1) refers to matching static strategy performance at lower budgets, not to outperforming the baseline. The paper does not provide guidance on minimum recommended turn budgets for deployment, nor does it characterize how the budget requirement scales with task difficulty or benchmark characteristics. Future work should establish budget-conditioned performance curves and identify the budget regimes where AgentSwing's overhead is justified.

### The Efficiency-Precision Framework's Empirical Estimates Are Noisy and Confounded, Limiting Its Diagnostic Utility

**The assumption or constraint:** The probabilistic framework in Section 2 defines search efficiency `$\eta^\pi_\tau$` and terminal precision `$\rho^\pi_\tau$` as task-level probabilities—quantities that would, in principle, be estimated by running strategy `$\pi$` multiple times on the same task and measuring the fraction of runs that finish and the fraction of finished runs that are correct. In practice, the paper estimates η and ρ from a **single run per task per strategy**, collapsing the probabilistic quantities to binary indicators: a task either finishes or doesn't, and if it finishes, the answer is either correct or not. The population-level estimates (Equations 6, 8) then aggregate these binary indicators across tasks.

**The consequence:** The empirical η and ρ estimates conflate two sources of variation: genuine task-level differences in the probability of finishing/answering correctly, and **single-run stochasticity** (the agent might finish on 60% of runs for a given task, but the paper observes only one binary outcome). This means the η and ρ reported in Figures 3b, 3c, and Table 2 are not pure measures of the strategy's efficiency and precision properties—they are contaminated by run-level noise. The aligned terminal precision `$\rho_\text{align}$` (Equation 8) is particularly vulnerable: it restricts analysis to tasks that finish under *all* compared strategies in their single respective runs. A task that would finish under Discard-All on 80% of runs but happened not to finish in the single observed run is excluded from the aligned set, even though it is genuinely "finishable" by Discard-All. This creates a selection bias: the aligned set over-represents tasks that are deterministically easy to finish and under-represents tasks where finishing is noisy.

**What evidence exists in the paper:** The small sample sizes for aligned-case analyses (Table 2) make this concern concrete. For Tongyi-DR-30B-A3B on BrowseComp, only 45 out of 200 tasks (22.5%) finish under all four strategies (Discard-All, Summary, Keep-Last-N, AgentSwing). The aligned Pass@1 for the static strategies is uniformly 20.0% on these 45 tasks, while AgentSwing achieves 31.1%—a difference of 5 tasks (14 vs. 9 correct). With only 45 tasks in the denominator, a shift of 5 tasks could plausibly arise from run-level noise rather than a genuine precision difference. Confidence intervals are not reported.

The paper acknowledges the need for aligned metrics (§2.1, Equation 8) as a cross-strategy comparison tool, but does not discuss the single-run limitation or its implications for the reliability of the aligned estimates. The η vs. ρ scatterplot (Figure 3b) plots each strategy as a single point without error bars, masking the uncertainty in both coordinates.

**Mitigation status:** Not addressed. The paper's probabilistic language (defining η and ρ as probabilities) creates an expectation of stochastic estimation that the empirical protocol does not fulfill. Multiple runs per task would be computationally expensive—400-turn trajectories with web search API calls are costly—but even a small number of repeated runs (e.g., 3–5) on a subset of tasks would provide estimates of run-level variance and enable confidence intervals. The paper does not discuss this trade-off or suggest it as future work. A more modest mitigation would be to report binomial confidence intervals for the Pass@1 estimates, which would help readers assess whether the reported differences (particularly the small HLE gains) are statistically meaningful.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper changes the conversation around context management for long-horizon agents in one specific but consequential way: it recasts context management from a **compression design problem** ("which single compression function should we apply everywhere?") into a **state-dependent control problem** ("given the current trajectory state, which of several possible futures should we pursue?"). This reframing is not merely terminological—it has direct empirical consequences, and the paper provides the evidence to back them.

**What shifts conceptually.** Prior to this work, the dominant paradigm for context management was best captured by the engineering guidance from Anthropic (2025b): choose a strategy (Discard-All, Keep-Last-N, summarization, or some hybrid), apply it uniformly at every trigger point, and evaluate it by aggregate benchmark accuracy. The implicit assumption—rarely stated but universally operationalized—was that the optimal compression function is invariant across trajectory states. Under this assumption, the research question was "which function works best on average?", and progress meant finding better compression functions.

AgentSwing demonstrates that this assumption is false in a way that matters for performance. The paper's two contrasting case studies (Section 4.4 and Appendix C) are the core evidence: within a *single trajectory*, the optimal strategy at Turn 23 (Keep-Last-N, because recent context contains a newly surfaced correct clue) differs from the optimal strategy at a comparable trigger point in a different trajectory state (Discard-All, because recent context is a dead-end loop). A static strategy forced to apply the same operation at both points necessarily fails at one. The routing perspective makes this trade-off explicit and provides a mechanism for resolving it.

This conceptual shift has downstream consequences for how researchers should think about context management:

- **Designing better compression functions is not the only path to improvement.** The field has invested substantially in developing more sophisticated compression methods—better summarization prompts, hierarchical memory structures, retrieval-augmented context curation. AgentSwing's results suggest an orthogonal improvement axis: routing among *existing* compression functions. The combination ablation in Appendix A (Figure 8) shows that adding a second strategy to the candidate set can provide gains even when the added strategy is individually weaker than the existing one (Summary alone: 55.0%; Discard-All alone: 58.0%; DA+Summary: 60.0%). This means that even with today's imperfect compression methods, better allocation decisions can extract performance that no single method achieves alone.

- **Evaluating context management strategies requires looking at downstream behavior, not static context properties.** The ablation in Table 3—where "w/o Lookahead" routing performs at random-selection levels (50.0% vs. 51.0% for GPT-OSS-120B)—is a negative result with positive implications. It tells the field that static quality metrics for managed contexts (coherence, coverage, token savings) are insufficient proxies for context management quality, because the value of a managed context depends on how the agent *interacts with the environment* in subsequent turns. This finding should redirect evaluation efforts away from intrinsic context quality metrics and toward extrinsic, behavior-based evaluation—measuring whether the managed context enables the agent to find the right information, not whether it looks coherent to a human or a judge model.

- **The efficiency-precision decomposition provides a shared diagnostic language.** The η × ρ framework (Section 2) is not just a metric—it is a vocabulary for discussing *why* a strategy succeeds or fails, and what kind of intervention might help. Before this paper, the only way to compare Discard-All and Keep-Last-N was by their final accuracy numbers, which provide no insight into mechanism. After this paper, one can say: "Discard-All sacrifices η to gain ρ; Keep-Last-N preserves η at the cost of ρ; an ideal strategy would achieve both." This diagnostic language makes it possible to design interventions that target specific failure modes—for instance, a strategy that aims to improve η without sacrificing ρ by being more selective about when to reset. This is a genuine contribution to how the field reasons about long-horizon agent design, independent of AgentSwing's specific mechanism.

**What this work resolves.** The paper resolves a latent tension in prior work that was visible but unarticulated. Multiple frontier systems had demonstrated that aggressive context management (particularly Discard-All) substantially improved long-horizon performance (Liu et al., 2025a; Team et al., 2026; Zeng et al., 2026a). Yet different systems committed to different strategies, and there was no principled framework for understanding *why* Discard-All helped in some cases, *why* Keep-Last-N helped in others, or *when* one should be preferred over the other. Practitioners were left with an empirical trial-and-error process: try Discard-All on your benchmark, try Keep-Last-N, pick whichever scores higher.

AgentSwing resolves this by showing that the question "which strategy is best?" is ill-posed—the answer depends on trajectory state, and the right question is "how do we decide which strategy to use at each state?" The efficiency-precision framework explains why static strategies occupy different points on a trade-off frontier (Figure 3b): Discard-All gains ρ at the cost of η; the baseline gains η at the cost of ρ; Summary and Keep-Last-N compromise between them. Once this structure is understood, the apparent contradiction in prior work—some groups found Discard-All best, others preferred Keep-Last-N—resolves naturally: they were testing on different benchmark distributions that implicitly weighted η vs. ρ differently, or using different turn budgets that changed which factor was the binding constraint (as Figure 5 demonstrates directly).

**What research directions become more attractive.** Several lines of inquiry that were previously underexplored now have clear motivation:

- **Learned routing policies.** The paper uses the agent model itself as a zero-shot router, a deliberately simple choice that the authors acknowledge may not be optimal (Section 7). The empirical demonstration that routing *can* provide substantial gains (7.5 percentage points over the best static strategy for GPT-OSS-120B on BrowseComp) makes the case for investing in better routers—trained verifiers, process reward models, or trajectory evaluators that can predict downstream success more reliably than the agent model's own zero-shot judgment. This was not an obviously promising direction before AgentSwing, because it was unclear whether routing among heterogeneous strategies could provide gains beyond simply picking the best single strategy. The DA+DA+DA control in Appendix A (59.0% vs. 58.0% for single Discard-All) quantifies the "multiple identical branches" benefit separately from the "strategy diversity" benefit, showing that routing quality matters independently of candidate set composition.

- **Strategy design for complementarity rather than standalone quality.** The traditional approach to context management research is to design a new strategy and evaluate it against existing strategies on benchmark accuracy. AgentSwing's results suggest a different research objective: design strategies that are *complementary* to existing strategies—that cover different regions of the η-ρ plane, or that excel in trajectory states where existing strategies fail. A new strategy that scores 55% as a standalone might be more valuable than one that scores 58%, if the 55% strategy covers failure modes that the 58% strategy misses and can be combined via routing. This changes the optimization objective for context management research.

**What research directions become less attractive.** Conversely, some previously active directions now appear less promising:

- **Universal context management strategies that aim to dominate all others.** The paper's evidence that the optimal strategy depends on trajectory state (case studies) and that strategy complementarity drives routing gains (Appendix A, Figure 8) suggests that the search for a single "best" compression function is unlikely to yield the same returns as investing in better routing over a diverse candidate set. This doesn't mean compression research is obsolete—better compression functions expand the frontier and provide new candidates for routing—but it does mean that evaluating compression methods solely by their standalone accuracy misses the potential value they could provide as part of an adaptive ensemble.

- **Static quality metrics for context management decisions.** The "w/o Lookahead" result in Table 3 (routing based on managed context properties performs at chance level) is a strong signal that intrinsic evaluation of context management quality is insufficient. Research effort invested in developing better metrics for evaluating managed contexts (coherence scores, faithfulness metrics, information retention measures) is unlikely to directly translate into better routing decisions, because the signal for routing quality comes from downstream behavior, not from static context properties. This doesn't mean such metrics are useless—they may be valuable for debugging or for training supervised routers—but they are not sufficient as the primary evaluation criterion.

**Magnitude of the shift.** This is best characterized as a **reframing with supporting evidence**, not a paradigm shift. The core idea—that context management decisions should be state-dependent—is conceptually straightforward and likely intuitive to many practitioners. The paper's contribution is not the idea itself but rather: (1) the formal probabilistic framework that makes the idea precise and testable, (2) the empirical demonstration that state-dependent routing provides gains beyond any static strategy, (3) the specific mechanism (parallel branches + lookahead) that implements the routing decision reliably at test time, and (4) the diagnostic finding that lookahead information is *necessary* for effective routing (not just helpful). The combination of framework, mechanism, and evidence elevates the idea from intuition to demonstrated principle, which is what makes the paper more than an incremental systems contribution.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**1. Difficulty-conditioned analysis of adaptive context management gains.** The paper's probabilistic framework decomposes success into η × ρ and argues that different strategies affect these dimensions differently, but never applies this decomposition to analyze *where* AgentSwing's gains materialize across the difficulty spectrum. A natural follow-up would bin BrowseComp tasks by the baseline model's pass@1 rate (analogous to the quintile analysis in the reference example paper's Section 5), compute η and ρ per bin for each strategy, and characterize how AgentSwing's advantage varies with difficulty. The prediction from the framework is clear: AgentSwing should provide largest gains on medium-difficulty tasks where the baseline has non-trivial pass@1 but suffers from context rot (high η, low ρ), because those are the tasks where adaptive routing can maintain efficiency while improving precision. On very easy tasks (baseline already near-ceiling), gains should be minimal; on very hard tasks (baseline near-zero pass@1), no context management strategy should help. This analysis would directly address the deployment-relevant question of *which tasks* benefit from the overhead of adaptive routing, and would test whether the η × ρ framework has genuine predictive power beyond aggregate metrics.

**2. Training a dedicated router with supervised or RL-based objectives.** The paper uses the agent model itself as a zero-shot router and acknowledges in Section 7 that "a stronger dedicated router, verifier, or trajectory evaluator with better foresight may further improve branch selection quality." The lookahead rollouts naturally generate training data for a router: for each trigger event, all three branches are executed for K turns, and the eventual task outcome (success/failure) is known after the fact. This means the oracle "correct" routing decision is available post-hoc—simply identify which branch ultimately led to task completion (or which would have, if the selected branch failed). A follow-up could train a lightweight classifier or process reward model on (trajectory state, managed context, lookahead trace) → (success probability) pairs, using the lookahead rollouts as training signal. The key experiment would compare the trained router against the zero-shot agent-model router and against an oracle upper bound (always select the best branch as determined by post-hoc outcome). The gap between the trained router and the oracle would quantify how much room remains for routing improvement, while the gap between zero-shot and trained routing would quantify how much the agent model's own meta-reasoning capability limits current performance.

**3. Generalization to other agent architectures, tool suites, and domains.** All experiments use the ReAct interaction pattern (thinking → tool call → tool response) with a standard Search + Visit tool suite. The paper does not test whether AgentSwing's gains transfer to agents using different interaction protocols (e.g., code-acting agents that generate executable actions, tree-search agents that explore multiple branches simultaneously, or agents with structured memory separate from the context window) or different tool configurations (e.g., agents with access to databases, APIs, or domain-specific search engines). A systematic follow-up would test AgentSwing with at least two alternative agent architectures—for example, a code-acting agent (where the context includes both natural language and code) and an agent with explicit memory management separate from the LLM context—on the same BrowseComp tasks. The prediction is that AgentSwing's routing mechanism should provide gains whenever (a) the agent accumulates trajectory history that can become noisy or misleading, and (b) the candidate context management strategies have complementary strengths (different points on the η-ρ plane). If gains disappear for agents with explicit memory architectures (which already separate "working memory" from "long-term memory"), that would clarify that AgentSwing is primarily compensating for the absence of architectural memory rather than providing a general context management solution.

**4. Dynamic trigger policies that adapt the intervention threshold per trajectory state.** The paper uses a fixed trigger ratio r (context exceeds r × max length) for all trigger events, with model-specific r values (0.2 for GPT-OSS-120B, 0.4 for DeepSeek-v3.2 and Tongyi-DR-30B-A3B) motivated by the Discard-All analysis in Figure 2. But the optimal trigger threshold likely depends on trajectory state—when the current context contains a promising breakthrough, triggering context management early risks losing that information; when the context is noise-dominated, triggering earlier (lower r) gives Discard-All more reset opportunities. A follow-up could implement a dynamic trigger policy: after each turn, use a lightweight classifier (trained on trajectory features—diversity of visited domains, rate of new information discovery, presence of contradictory findings) to predict whether the trajectory is in a "productive" or "unproductive" state, and vary the trigger threshold accordingly (higher r when productive, lower r when unproductive). The experiment would compare the dynamic trigger policy against the fixed threshold on BrowseComp scaling curves (Figure 5 analog), testing whether dynamic triggering recovers some of the gains lost at low turn budgets (where fixed-threshold context management underperforms the baseline).

**5. Lookahead efficiency: cheaper rollouts that preserve routing signal.** The paper's lookahead rollouts use real tool calls with real environment feedback, which provides reliable signal for routing but adds substantial latency and API cost. Table 3 shows that K = 3 lookahead turns provide substantially better routing than K = 1 (60.0% vs. 52.5% for GPT-OSS-120B), suggesting that multiple turns of future behavior are needed for reliable routing decisions. A follow-up could explore whether cheaper rollouts can preserve most of this signal: for instance, using a smaller/faster model for the lookahead turns (asymmetric rollout), using simulated rather than real tool responses (where the agent model hallucinates what a search or visit would return, based on its training knowledge), or using only the first tool call per branch and then predicting downstream success from that single action. The key experiment would measure the correlation between routing decisions made with cheap rollouts vs. routing decisions made with full real-environment rollouts, and report the Pass@1 achieved with each approach. A strong positive result—cheap rollouts achieve, say, 90% of the full-rollout routing accuracy at 10% of the cost—would make adaptive context management practical for latency-sensitive deployments.

**6. Combining AgentSwing with model-level improvements for long-horizon robustness.** The paper positions AgentSwing as a test-time control mechanism that operates without modifying the agent model. The Limitations section (Section 7) suggests that "a more fundamental direction is to translate these principles into model-level competence, for example, by training agents that are intrinsically more efficient under smaller context budgets or more reliable under long-horizon noisy trajectories." A concrete follow-up would train an agent model using trajectories augmented with AgentSwing's routing decisions as training data: for each training task, run AgentSwing, collect the trajectory including the selected context management branches and discarded branches, and fine-tune the agent model to (a) predict which context management strategy is appropriate given a trajectory state (distilling the routing policy into model weights), and (b) generate more robust continuations from managed contexts (reducing the ρ penalty of small contexts). The experiment would compare this fine-tuned model against the base model with AgentSwing as an external controller, testing whether internalizing the routing policy eliminates the need for explicit lookahead rollouts at test time. The success metric would be whether the fine-tuned model with a static context management strategy matches or exceeds the base model with AgentSwing's full adaptive routing.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Cost-efficient batch evaluation of long-horizon agent systems.** Organizations developing or benchmarking deep information-seeking agents routinely run thousands of evaluation trajectories, each consuming hundreds of LLM inference calls and web search API requests. At current pricing, a single 400-turn BrowseComp trajectory with a 120B-parameter model can cost tens of dollars in API fees alone. AgentSwing's finding that it matches static strategy performance with up to 3× fewer interaction turns (Figure 5: AgentSwing at ~200 turns roughly matches Discard-All at 400 turns for GPT-OSS-120B) translates directly to cost savings in batch evaluation pipelines. Specifically, if an organization currently evaluates a new agent checkpoint on 200 BrowseComp tasks at 400 turns per task using Discard-All (200 × 400 = 80,000 total turns), switching to AgentSwing could achieve comparable or better accuracy at roughly 200 turns per task (40,000 total turns), halving the inference and API cost per evaluation run. The additional cost of AgentSwing's lookahead rollouts partially offsets this saving, but the aligned-case analysis in Table 2 shows AgentSwing finishing tasks in substantially fewer turns than Discard-All (190.3 vs. 297.2 average for GPT-OSS-120B on aligned cases), suggesting net turn-count reductions of roughly 35–40% even accounting for lookahead overhead. For organizations running weekly or daily evaluations across multiple benchmarks and model variants, this efficiency gain compounds meaningfully.

**Deployment of smaller, cheaper models for long-horizon tasks via test-time context management.** The paper demonstrates that AgentSwing with Tongyi-DR-30B-A3B (a 30B-parameter model with 3B active parameters, substantially smaller and cheaper to serve than 120B+ frontier models) achieves 60.5% on BrowseComp—competitive with GPT-OSS-120B under the best static strategy (Keep-Last-N at 52.5%) and approaching DeepSeek-v3.2 under Discard-All (58.0%). This suggests a deployment architecture where a smaller, cost-efficient model handles long-horizon information-seeking tasks at quality levels previously achievable only with much larger models, by investing test-time compute in adaptive context management rather than in model scale. Concretely, a service that currently routes BrowseComp-difficulty tasks to a 120B-parameter model (inference cost: ~$X per million tokens) could instead route them to a 30B-parameter model with AgentSwing (inference cost: ~$X/4 per million tokens, plus lookahead overhead). The 60.5% vs. 52.5% accuracy comparison suggests this substitution would *improve* quality while reducing per-query model inference cost, though the lookahead rollouts and Summary calls to GPT-OSS-120B would partially offset the savings. The break-even point depends on the relative pricing of the models and web search API calls, which the paper does not analyze, but the performance numbers establish the viability of the approach.

**Context-management-aware agent design for self-improvement pipelines.** Several recent works (Gao et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2026; Su et al., 2026) train long-horizon agents using trajectories collected from the agent's own exploration, with successful trajectories used as training data and failed trajectories discarded or used as negative examples. In these pipelines, the *quality* of collected trajectories determines downstream model capability. AgentSwing's routing mechanism can be applied during trajectory collection to improve the ratio of successful to unsuccessful trajectories: rather than collecting trajectories under a single static context management strategy (which produces a mix of successes and failures depending on trajectory state), collect trajectories under AgentSwing's adaptive routing, which selects the most promising strategy at each trigger point. The aligned-case results in Table 2 suggest this could improve the success rate on hard tasks substantially—for Tongyi-DR-30B-A3B, AgentSwing achieves 31.1% Pass@1 on aligned cases vs. 20.0% for all static strategies, a 55% relative improvement in successful trajectory yield on the hardest subset of tasks. In a self-improvement loop where each percentage point of successful trajectory yield translates to more and better training data, this improvement could compound over multiple iterations. The implementation is straightforward: wrap AgentSwing around the agent model during the trajectory collection phase of any existing self-improvement pipeline, without modifying the training procedure itself.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper does not articulate explicit decision rules for choosing between AgentSwing and static context management strategies. The results provide implicit guidance, which can be summarized as follows:

- **Favor AgentSwing when turn budgets are generous (300+ turns) and task difficulty is moderate-to-high.** Figure 5 shows AgentSwing's advantage over static strategies widens with turn budget, reaching 2.5–7.5 percentage points at 400 turns across backbones. The aligned-case analysis (Table 2) shows the largest relative gains on the hardest task subset (tasks long enough to trigger context management under all strategies). Conversely, at 100-turn budgets, some static strategies underperform the baseline, and AgentSwing's advantage is minimal—suggesting that tight turn budgets erode the value of adaptive routing.

- **Consider static strategies (particularly Discard-All) when latency is the primary constraint and turn budgets are moderate.** AgentSwing's parallel lookahead rollouts add per-trigger latency from real tool calls and model inferences that the paper does not quantify. In latency-sensitive deployments where each additional second of waiting degrades user experience, the unmeasured wall-clock overhead may outweigh the accuracy gains, particularly if the accuracy advantage at the available turn budget is small (e.g., the ~0.7 percentage point gain on HLE for GPT-OSS-120B).

- **The choice depends on the task distribution's difficulty profile, which the paper does not characterize.** Without per-difficulty analysis, a practitioner cannot determine whether their specific task mix—e.g., predominantly factual lookup questions vs. multi-hop synthesis questions requiring backtracking—will benefit from adaptive routing. The case studies suggest that AgentSwing helps most on tasks where trajectories alternate between productive exploration and noise-dominated drift, because those are the states where strategy switching provides value. Tasks that are uniformly easy (always productive) or uniformly impossible for the agent (never productive) likely see minimal benefit from any context management strategy.
