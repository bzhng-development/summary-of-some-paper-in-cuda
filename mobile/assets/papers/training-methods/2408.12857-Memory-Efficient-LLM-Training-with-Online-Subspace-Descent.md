# Memory-Efficient LLM Training with Online Subspace Descent

**ArXiv:** [2408.12857](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.12857)

## 🎯 Pitch

This paper introduces **Online Subspace Descent**, a family of memory-efficient optimizers that replaces the expensive singular value decomposition (SVD) used by prior low-rank training methods with continuous online PCA to update the gradient projection matrix dynamically.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **Online Subspace Descent**, a family of memory-efficient optimizers that replaces the expensive singular value decomposition (SVD) used by prior low-rank training methods with continuous online PCA to update the gradient projection matrix dynamically. Evaluated on pretraining LLaMA models ranging from 60M to 7B parameters on the C4 dataset, Online Subspace Descent achieves lower perplexity and better downstream task performance than state-of-the-art low-rank training methods such as GaLore — narrowing the perplexity gap with full-rank baselines (e.g., achieving 31.30 vs. 35.52 perplexity for GaLore at 1B parameters with rank 512 on LLaMA 1B) — while being up to 1.3× faster in wall-clock time than SVD-based approaches. The paper provides the first convergence guarantee for arbitrary update rules of the projection matrix under the Hamiltonian descent framework, establishing that the Lyapunov structure is preserved for common optimizers like Adam and LION when equipped with subspace descent, but only when a mild condition prevents the degenerate case of premature stopping at non-stationary points.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: LLM Training Is Memory-Bound, Not Compute-Bound

The fundamental challenge this paper addresses isn't that we lack powerful optimizers for training large language models — it's that using the *best* optimizers requires memory we don't have. Modern adaptive optimizers like Adam maintain per-parameter state that is **2–3× the size of the model weights themselves**: first-order momentum $M^t$ and second-order momentum $V^t$, both the same shape as $W \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times m}$. For a 7B parameter model using Adam in mixed precision, this translates to roughly 56 GB just for optimizer states — exceeding the memory capacity of a single consumer GPU (even a high-end one with 48 GB VRAM) before the model weights, activations, and gradients are accounted for.

This memory bottleneck is the **primary constraint on who can train large models and on what hardware**. It forces practitioners into uncomfortable tradeoffs: use a simpler optimizer like SGD that converges more slowly and reaches worse final performance, adopt aggressive quantization that may degrade optimization dynamics, or buy more expensive hardware. None of these tradeoffs are satisfying for a training regime where Adam-family optimizers have become the de facto standard for achieving strong results on language modeling.

The core question the paper tackles is therefore: **can we retain the optimization quality of full-storage Adam while reducing its memory footprint to something closer to SGD?** The answer the paper develops is "yes, if we're clever about how we compress the gradient information that the optimizer state tracks."

### Where Prior Memory-Efficient Approaches Fall Short

The paper situates its work against a landscape of memory-reduction techniques that each have identifiable failure modes. Understanding these is essential to appreciating why Online Subspace Descent is a genuine advance rather than just another point in a crowded design space.

**Quantization-based methods (8-bit Adam, QSGD, etc.)** reduce memory by storing optimizer states in low-precision formats — the 8-bit AdamW used as the base optimizer in the paper's experiments is a prime example. These methods work by trading numerical precision for memory: they compress 32-bit floats to 8 bits, recovering approximately 4× memory savings. The limitation is that they **don't change the asymptotic memory scaling** — the number of stored values is still proportional to the number of parameters, just with smaller bytes per value. For very large models, even 8-bit storage of full optimizer states may exceed available memory. Moreover, quantization introduces errors that can compound during training, though the paper notes this is less severe than one might expect for Adam due to its normalization properties.

**Factorization-based methods (Adafactor, K-FAC)** exploit structural assumptions about gradient statistics to achieve sublinear memory scaling. Adafactor, in particular, factorizes the second-order momentum matrix $V^t$ for each weight matrix $W \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times m}$ into the outer product of two vectors $\mathbf{r} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ and $\mathbf{c} \in \mathbb{R}^m$, reducing memory from $O(nm)$ to $O(n + m)$. For large square matrices, this is a dramatic reduction. The problem is that **this factorization is approximate**: real gradient covariance matrices are not exactly rank-1. Adafactor's performance degrades on tasks where the true second-order structure is richer than what the factorization can capture, and the paper's experiments (Table 2) confirm that Adafactor underperforms Adam8bit (33.45 vs. 29.77 perplexity on LLaMA 60M), validating that the approximation cost is real.

**Low-rank projection methods (GaLore, Sketchy)** take a different approach: rather than compressing the optimizer state, they **project the gradient into a low-dimensional subspace before feeding it to the optimizer**, so the optimizer state itself is dimension-reduced. This is the direct lineage that Online Subspace Descent builds upon. The idea is elegant: if gradients during training predominantly lie in a low-dimensional subspace (an empirical observation with theoretical grounding from the neural tangent kernel and loss landscape literature), then we can operate the optimizer entirely within that subspace — storing states proportional to $k$ (the subspace dimension) rather than to $n$ or $m$.

The crucial implementation question is: **how do we find and maintain this subspace?** This is where prior methods introduce problems that the paper identifies and attempts to solve.

**GaLore's SVD bottleneck.** GaLore periodically computes the singular value decomposition of the full gradient matrix $G_t$ to extract its top-$k$ singular vectors as the projection matrix $P_t$:
$$P_t, \_, \_ = \texttt{torch.linalg.svd}(G_{T\lfloor t/T \rfloor})$$

where $T$ is a fixed update interval. This approach has two serious drawbacks:

1. **Computational cost**: SVD scales as $O(\min(nm^2, n^2m))$ for an $n \times m$ matrix, which becomes prohibitively expensive for large weight tensors. The paper's timing measurements (Figure 2) show that PyTorch's SVD implementation can be up to **142× slower** than a single online PCA step on matrix shapes characteristic of LLaMA architectures. This cost cannot be hidden by pipelining or parallelism because it's too large relative to the weight update step itself — it creates a genuine training bottleneck.

2. **Stale gradient information**: Because SVD is computed only periodically (every $T$ iterations in GaLore, typically $T = 200$) from a single mini-batch gradient at that moment, the projection matrix $P_t$ reflects the gradient structure at only one point in time. Between SVD updates, the gradient landscape can shift — the subspace that was optimal 100 steps ago may no longer be the subspace where gradient information is most concentrated. GaLore continues to project into this stale subspace until the next SVD, potentially discarding important gradient directions that have emerged recently.

**Sketchy's eigendecomposition overhead.** Sketchy uses a frequent-directions sketch to maintain an approximate covariance matrix, requiring eigendecomposition at every step. The paper notes this "can be costly for large models" — the cost is lower than full SVD but still significant and accumulates at every iteration rather than every $T$.

**Stochastic Subspace Descent's limited scope.** The earliest low-rank approach, Stochastic Subspace Descent, simply uses random projection matrices and provides convergence guarantees only for convex objectives. This is insufficient for the non-convex landscapes of deep neural network training, and random projections discard the benefit of data-adaptive subspaces entirely.

### The Unstated Theoretical Gap

Beyond these practical concerns, the paper identifies a theoretical vacuum that is equally important: **no convergence guarantee exists for any low-rank projection method with arbitrary projection update rules on non-convex objectives.** This matters because:

- Without a convergence guarantee, practitioners cannot be confident that training will reach a good solution — instability or non-convergence could emerge at scale where small-scale experiments don't reveal them.
- Without understanding *why* dynamically changing projections should work, it's unclear what properties the projection update rule needs to satisfy. Should $P_t$ change slowly? Rapidly? Does it need to preserve orthonormality? The GaLore approach (periodic SVD) and the online PCA approach proposed in this paper represent different answers to these questions, but prior to this work, there was no theoretical framework for evaluating which answer is correct.

The paper explicitly frames this gap: "to the best of our knowledge, no one has offered any guarantee of convergence for this class of methods on non-convex functions and objectives." Filling this gap is positioned as a primary contribution alongside the practical algorithm.

### The Fundamental Tension: Inertia vs. Adaptive Subspaces

To understand *why* the theoretical gap exists and why the paper's analysis is non-trivial, it's worth unpacking a tension that the paper explicitly acknowledges in Section 3.3. This is perhaps the most insightful part of the motivation, and the one that most clearly sets up the theoretical contribution.

Consider what happens when we equip an optimizer with state (momentum, Adam's second moment) with a dynamically changing projection matrix $P_t$. At iteration $t$, the optimizer state $\hat{S}^t$ has accumulated information from all previous projected gradients $P_\tau^\top \nabla L(W^\tau)$ for $\tau \leq t$. Each of these projections was into a *different* subspace, because $P_\tau$ has been evolving over time. When we now want to use $\hat{S}^t$ to update $W^t$ in the current subspace $P_t$, we're asking: **is the optimizer state accumulated across different subspaces coherent with optimization in the current subspace?**

The paper frames this through the lens of coordinate descent. In standard coordinate descent, you descend along one coordinate (or one subspace) at a time, and there is no coupling between steps — each step makes progress in its own direction, and the overall process converges. But with momentum-based optimizers, the state $\hat{S}^t$ creates **inertia** that couples the subspaces: the momentum from descent in subspace $P_1$ influences descent in subspace $P_2$ later on. This coupling could, in principle, be destructive — the momentum accumulated in one subspace could push the parameters in a direction that is counterproductive in another subspace.

The paper calls this "the inertia effect of $\hat{S}^t$ that entangles the different subspaces, making the dynamic behavior fundamentally more complicated than naive coordinate descent where the descent in different subspaces is uncoupled." For SGD (momentum-free), there's no entanglement — $P_t P_t^\top G_t$ is always a descent direction regardless of $P_t$, so the analysis is straightforward. But SGD converges too slowly for practical LLM training. The challenge is to make dynamic subspace projection work with momentum and adaptive learning rates, and to understand theoretically *why* it should work despite the apparent coupling.

### How the Paper Positions Itself

The paper's positioning is built on two pillars: a **theoretical framework** that resolves the inertia problem via Hamiltonian descent, and a **practical algorithm** that eliminates the SVD bottleneck through online PCA.

**Theoretical positioning.** The paper claims to provide "the first convergence guarantee for arbitrary update rules of the projection matrix" on non-convex objectives, and that this guarantee "is broadly applicable to a wide range of optimizers that can be analyzed within the Hamiltonian descent framework." The key insight — which we'll explore fully in Section 3 — is that the Hamiltonian+Descent structure that underlies common optimizers (momentum, Adam, LION) is *automatically preserved* under projection by any linear operator with an adjoint, regardless of how that operator is updated. The crossing terms that normally cancel in the Hamiltonian descent derivation (Equation 3) cancel *identically* when using projections, because $\langle X, P Y \rangle = \langle P^\top X, Y \rangle$ by the definition of the adjoint. The projection update rule $\Gamma$ simply doesn't appear in the Lyapunov decrease rate.

This is a **surprisingly general** result — it says that as long as the base optimizer has the Hamiltonian+Descent structure, adding dynamic subspace projection doesn't break the Lyapunov function, regardless of how you update the projection. The only additional condition needed (Assumption 4.4) is a non-degeneracy requirement: the projection update rule must not allow the system to get stuck at a point where $P_t^\top \nabla L(W^t) = 0$ but $\nabla L(W^t) \neq 0$ — i.e., the optimizer shouldn't prematurely stop because it's projecting into the wrong subspace. This is satisfied when $P_t$ is updated by any reasonable online PCA procedure that tracks gradient directions.

**Practical positioning.** On the practical side, the paper positions Online Subspace Descent as an improvement over GaLore along two axes:

- **Convergence quality**: By updating $P_t$ continuously via online PCA rather than periodically via SVD, the projection matrix incorporates gradient information from every step, adapting immediately to changes in the gradient landscape. This is hypothesized to produce better optimization trajectories, and the experimental results (Figure 1, Table 1) support this — Online Subspace Descent achieves 31.30 perplexity vs. 35.52 for GaLore at 1B parameters with rank 512 on LLaMA 1B, closing much of the gap to full-rank 8-bit AdamW at 29.40.

- **Computational overhead**: Single-step online PCA is dramatically cheaper than SVD (Figure 2), and can be executed in parallel with the weight update step, effectively adding zero overhead to training. The 7B experiment in Table 3 shows Online Subspace Descent is 1.3× faster than GaLore in wall-clock time while achieving substantially better perplexity (43.72 vs. 51.21).

**What the paper is NOT claiming.** It's important to note what the paper doesn't position itself as doing. It does not claim that projection-based methods can match full-rank training at all scales or for all tasks — the results show a persistent gap (29.40 full-rank vs. 31.30 at 1B), and the paper acknowledges that higher ranks monotonically improve performance (Figure 3, rank sweep). It does not claim to have solved the optimal control problem of how to update $P_t$ to maximize the convergence rate — this is explicitly left as future work. And it does not claim that online PCA is the only possible projection update rule the theory supports — the analysis explicitly works for arbitrary linear operators, with online PCA being the practical choice that satisfies the non-degeneracy condition while remaining computationally cheap.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

Online Subspace Descent is a **wrapper** that turns any standard optimizer (such as Adam or LION) into a memory-efficient optimizer by projecting its gradient inputs into a dynamically evolving low-rank subspace — essentially, the optimizer "sees" only a compressed version of the gradient, but the compression itself adapts continuously to track which directions in the gradient are currently important. The core problem it solves is that full-storage adaptive optimizers like Adam require 2–3× the model parameter count in memory for their internal states ($M^t$ and $V^t$), making training of large models infeasible on memory-constrained hardware; the solution is to run the optimizer inside a learned low-dimensional subspace so that its internal states are proportionally smaller.

---

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The system has **three** interacting components, and a single computation graph that links them:

1. **The weight matrix `$W \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times m}$`** — the model parameters being optimized, stored at full size. It receives gradient-based updates, but those updates are constructed from dimension-reduced quantities that flow through the projection matrix.

2. **The projection matrix `$P \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times k}$`** — a tall, thin matrix with `$k \ll n$` that defines which `$k$`-dimensional subspace of the `$n$`-dimensional parameter space the optimizer currently operates in. It is updated *online* at every iteration using a lightweight PCA objective, without ever calling `torch.linalg.svd`.

3. **The dimension-reduced optimizer state `$\hat{S} \in \mathbb{R}^{k \times m}$`** — this is the optimizer's internal memory, but projected into the `$k$`-dimensional subspace. For Adam, this is `$\{\hat{M}^t, \hat{V}^t\}$`, both of size `$k \times m$` instead of `$n \times m$`.

4. **The online PCA optimizer** — a *separate* optimizer (e.g., Adam or SGD) dedicated solely to updating `$P_t$` by minimizing the online PCA objective `$\mathcal{L}_{G_t}(P)$` (Equation 6). This optimizer maintains its own small state `$S^P_t$`.

**Information flow, step by step at iteration `$t$`:**

- **Step 1:** Compute the full gradient `$G_t = \nabla_W \mathcal{L}(W^t)$` — this is `$n \times m$` and unavoidable.
- **Step 2:** Project it: `$\hat{G}_t = P_t^\top G_t$`, reducing the row dimension from `$n$` to `$k$`. This `$k \times m$` matrix enters the weight optimizer.
- **Step 3:** The weight optimizer updates its dimension-reduced state `$\hat{S}^t$` using `$\hat{G}_t$` and produces a dimension-reduced update `$\hat{\Delta}^t$` (size `$k \times m$`).
- **Step 4:** The update is projected back to full size: `$\Delta W^t = P_t \hat{\Delta}^t$` (using `$P_t$` not `$P_t^\top$` — the projection is `$n \times k$` times `$k \times m$` to yield `$n \times m$`). Weight decay is applied, and `$W^{t+1} = W^t + \epsilon_t^W (P_t \hat{\Delta}^{t+1} - \lambda^W W^t)$`.
- **Step 5:** In parallel, compute the gradient of the online PCA objective with respect to `$P_t$`: `$G^P_t = \nabla_P \mathcal{L}_{G_t}(P_t)$`. This is computed from the (detached) full gradient `$G_t$`.
- **Step 6:** The projection matrix optimizer takes `$G^P_t$` and updates `$P_t$`: `$P^{t+1} = P^t + \epsilon_t^P(\Delta^P_t - \lambda^P P^t)$`.

Steps 2–4 and Steps 5–6 can execute in parallel because `$P_t$` in the weight update uses the *current* `$P_t$`, while the `$P_t$` update produces the *next* `$P_{t+1}$`. Steps 5–6 are fast — a single backward pass on a simple loss function — and the paper reports this can be "hidden" behind the weight update computation.

This is primarily a **theoretical analysis paper paired with a new algorithm**. The core idea is that dynamic subspace projection — running an optimizer inside a low-dimensional space that itself evolves over time — preserves the convergence properties of the base optimizer under surprisingly mild conditions, when analyzed through the lens of Hamiltonian descent. The practical algorithm exploits this insight by replacing expensive periodic SVD with cheap online PCA, yielding a memory-efficient optimizer that is both faster and more performant than its SVD-based predecessor (GaLore).

#### Static Subspace Descent: The Starting Point

The paper builds up from the simplest possible low-rank configuration: **confining all optimization to a fixed, static subspace**. This isn't the final algorithm, but it introduces the machinery that the dynamic version generalizes.

Consider imposing a low-rank structure on the weight matrix: `$W = P \hat{W}$`, where `$P \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times k}$` is fixed and `$\hat{W} \in \mathbb{R}^{k \times m}$` is the actual parameter we optimize. Because `$k \ll n$`, `$\hat{W}$` has far fewer elements than `$W$`. If we apply a standard optimizer to `$\hat{W}$` instead of `$W$`, the optimizer state `$\hat{S}$` (momentum, second moment, etc.) is proportionally smaller.

The optimization problem becomes:

$$\min_{\hat{W}} \mathcal{L}(P \hat{W})$$

To apply an optimizer from Equation (1) (which has the general form `$W^{t+1} = W^t + \phi_t(S^t)$` and `$S^t = \psi_t(S^{t-1}, \nabla \mathcal{L}(W^t))$`), we need to:

1. Compute the gradient with respect to `$\hat{W}$`: the chain rule gives `$\nabla_{\hat{W}} \mathcal{L}(P \hat{W}) = P^\top \nabla_W \mathcal{L}(W)$`, where `$\nabla_W \mathcal{L}(W)$` is the full `$n \times m$` gradient. In words: we compute the gradient at the full weight location `$W = P \hat{W}$`, then project it back through `$P^\top$` to get the gradient with respect to the low-dimensional parameters.
2. Feed this projected gradient `$P^\top G_t$` into the optimizer's state update `$\psi_t$`, producing `$\hat{S}^t$` of size `$k \times m$`.
3. The optimizer produces a dimension-reduced update `$\hat{\Delta}^t = \phi_t(\hat{S}^t)$` (size `$k \times m$`).
4. To apply this update to `$W$`, we project it back up: `$W^{t+1} = W^t + P \hat{\Delta}^t$`.

The full update rule for static subspace descent is therefore:

$$W^{t+1} = W^t + P \phi_t(\hat{S}^t)$$

$$\hat{S}^t = \psi_t(\hat{S}^{t-1}, P^\top \nabla \mathcal{L}(W^t))$$

where `$W^{t+1}$` is the updated full-size weight matrix, `$P$` is the fixed projection matrix of size `$n \times k$` with `$k \ll n$`, `$\phi_t$` is the optimizer's update mapping (converting state to weight delta), `$\hat{S}^t$` is the dimension-reduced optimizer state of size `$k \times m$`, and `$\psi_t$` is the optimizer's state update mapping that takes the projected gradient `$P^\top \nabla \mathcal{L}(W^t)$`.

**What it computes:** At each iteration, the full gradient is projected down to `$k$` dimensions using `$P^\top$`, the optimizer processes this reduced gradient to update its internal state and produce a reduced update, and the reduced update is projected back to the full `$n$` dimensions using `$P$` before being applied to the weights.

**Why this form, and why it's insufficient:** The projection-adjoint pairing (`$P^\top$` for input, `$P$` for output) ensures that the update lives in the column space of `$P$` — the weights can only move within the `$k$`-dimensional subspace spanned by the columns of `$P$`. While this constraint is useful for fine-tuning (the LoRA setting), for pretraining it is "often too limited" (Section 3.2) because the optimal weight trajectory may explore directions outside what any single fixed `$P$` can represent. The model's capacity is fundamentally bounded by the rank of `$P$` — if the desirable weight update requires moving in the `$(k+1)$`-st direction, the optimizer simply cannot produce that movement.

#### Dynamic Subspace Descent: Opening Up the Full Space

The key insight (attributed to GaLore and generalized here) is to **let `$P_t$` change across iterations while keeping the update rule structurally identical**. The update equations become:

$$W^{t+1} = W^t + P_t \phi_t(\hat{S}^t)$$

$$\hat{S}^t = \psi_t(\hat{S}^{t-1}, P_t^\top \nabla \mathcal{L}(W^t))$$

$$P^{t+1} = \chi_t(P^t, W^t, \hat{S}^t)$$

where `$\chi_t$` is a yet-to-be-determined update rule for the projection matrix, and all other symbols retain their meanings from the static version, with the critical difference that `$P_t$` now has a time index `$t$`.

**What changes conceptually:** At iteration 1, the optimizer operates in the subspace defined by `$P_1$` and `$W^1$` receives an update in `$\text{col}(P_1)$`. At iteration 2, `$P_2$` may define a different subspace, and `$W^2$` receives an update in `$\text{col}(P_2)$`. Over many iterations, even though each individual update is low-rank, the accumulated updates can span the full `$n$`-dimensional space because different iterations use different projection directions. This is analogous to coordinate descent — you descend along one small set of directions at each step, but by cycling through different directions, you eventually explore the full space. The crucial difference from coordinate descent is that the momentum state `$\hat{S}^t$` couples the subspaces across iterations, creating the inertial entanglement concern discussed in the motivation.

**Why this should work (intuition from projected gradient descent):** Consider the simplest case — gradient descent without momentum or state, where the update reduces to:

$$W^{t+1} = W^t - \epsilon_t P_t P_t^\top G_t$$

Here, `$P_t P_t^\top$` is an `$n \times n$` matrix that acts as a low-rank preconditioner on the gradient. The matrix `$P_t P_t^\top$` is positive semi-definite (it's a projection matrix), so `$P_t P_t^\top G_t$` is a descent direction for `$\mathcal{L}$` for any `$P_t$` — the inner product `$\langle G_t, P_t P_t^\top G_t\rangle = \|P_t^\top G_t\|^2 \geq 0$`, confirming monotonic decrease. The only failure mode is if `$P_t^\top G_t = 0$` while `$G_t \neq 0$` (the gradient is orthogonal to the current subspace), which would cause premature stopping. As long as `$P_t$` is updated to track the gradient space — e.g., using online PCA — this degenerate case is avoided.

For optimizers with state (Adam, momentum), the same intuition doesn't directly apply because `$\hat{S}^t$` isn't just the current projected gradient — it's an accumulated history of many past projected gradients. The theoretical contribution of the paper (Section 4) shows that despite this added complexity, the Lyapunov structure is *automatically* preserved.

---

#### The Projection Matrix Update: Online PCA

The remaining design question: **how to choose `$\chi_t$`, the update rule for `$P_t$`?**

**GaLore's approach (periodic SVD).** GaLore answers this with:

$$P_t, \_, \_ = \texttt{torch.linalg.svd}(G_{T\lfloor t/T\rfloor})$$

where `$T$` is a fixed update interval (e.g., `$T = 200$`). This means `$P_t$` changes only every `$T$` iterations, and when it does change, it jumps to the top-`$k$` singular vectors of the *single* gradient `$G_{T\lfloor t/T\rfloor}$` computed from one mini-batch at the update moment. The projection `$P_t P_t^\top G_t$` then approximates the best rank-`$k$` approximation of `$G_t$` in the Frobenius norm — which sounds attractive — but only at the update instant. Between updates, the gradient landscape can drift, and the stale projection may discard important newly-dominant directions.

**Online Subspace Descent's approach (online PCA).** The paper proposes to update `$P_t$` *continuously* at every iteration by taking a single optimization step on an online PCA objective `$\mathcal{L}_{G_t}(P)$`. The objective is designed so that minimizing it makes `$P P^\top \tilde{G}_t$` a good rank-`$k$` approximation of the normalized current gradient `$\tilde{G}_t$`, while encouraging `$P$` to have orthonormal columns:

$$\mathcal{L}_{G_t}(P) = \left\| P P^\top \tilde{G}_t - \tilde{G}_t \right\|^2 + \lambda \left\| P^\top P - I_{k \times k} \right\|^2$$

$$\tilde{G}_t = \frac{G_t}{\|G_t\|}$$

where `$\mathcal{L}_{G_t}(P)$` is the scalar loss for the projection matrix at iteration `$t$`, `$P \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times k}$` is the projection matrix being optimized, `$\tilde{G}_t$` is the current full gradient normalized to unit Frobenius norm (so the loss scale is stable across iterations with varying gradient magnitudes), `$\|\cdot\|$` denotes the Frobenius norm `$\|A\| = \text{tr}(A^\top A)^{1/2}$`, `$\lambda$` is a hyperparameter controlling the strength of the orthonormality penalty (set to `$\lambda = 0.1$` in all experiments after the initial sweep in Figure 3, right), and `$I_{k \times k}$` is the `$k \times k$` identity matrix.

**What it computes — term by term:**

The **first term** `$\|P P^\top \tilde{G}_t - \tilde{G}_t\|^2$` measures the reconstruction error of `$\tilde{G}_t$` when it is projected onto the subspace spanned by the columns of `$P$` and then reconstructed. Specifically:
- `$P^\top \tilde{G}_t$` (size `$k \times m$`) computes the coordinates of `$\tilde{G}_t$` in the basis defined by `$P$`.
- `$P(P^\top \tilde{G}_t)$` (size `$n \times m$`) reconstructs `$\tilde{G}_t$` from those coordinates, producing the best approximation of `$\tilde{G}_t$` that lies in `$\text{col}(P)$`, *assuming* `$P$` has orthonormal columns.
- The Frobenius norm of the difference `$\|PP^\top \tilde{G}_t - \tilde{G}_t\|^2$` measures how much of the gradient lies outside the current subspace. Minimizing this term encourages `$P$` to span the directions where the gradient has the most energy.

The **second term** `$\lambda \|P^\top P - I_{k \times k}\|^2$` penalizes deviation from orthonormality. Without it, `$P$` could have correlated or scaled columns, which would make the "projection and reconstruction" interpretation of the first term inaccurate (since `$P P^\top$` is a proper projection only when `$P^\top P = I$`). The penalty `$\lambda$` controls the tradeoff: too low, and `$P$` may lose orthonormality; too high, and `$P$` may prioritize orthonormality over tracking the gradient signal.

**Why this form:** The objective in Equation (6) is a standard formulation of online PCA [building on Baldi and Hornik, 1989, cited in the paper as reference 3]. The key property that makes it suitable is: "all its stable local minimum is a global minimum, and `$P$` is a global minimum iff `$P P^\top \tilde{G}_t$` forms the optimal rank-`$k$` approximation of `$\tilde{G}_t$`" (Section 3.2). This means that gradient-based optimization of `$\mathcal{L}_{G_t}$` doesn't have bad local minima — any locally optimal `$P$` achieves the globally optimal rank-`$k$` approximation. This is crucial because the algorithm only takes a *single* gradient step on `$\mathcal{L}_{G_t}$` at each iteration, and we need that step to be moving `$P_t$` toward a well-defined target rather than getting stuck in a poor local configuration.

A natural alternative would be to directly minimize `$\|P P^\top G_t - G_t\|^2$` without normalization, but this would cause `$\mathcal{L}_{G_t}$` to vary dramatically in scale as the gradient magnitude changes over training, making the learning rate for `$P$` difficult to tune. Normalizing by `$\|G_t\|$` stabilizes the scale of the loss, decoupling the optimization of `$P_t$` from the overall optimization progress.

#### The Complete Algorithm (Algorithm 1)

Algorithm 1 provides the precise per-iteration procedure. It requires two separate optimizer instances — one for the model weights `$W^t$` and one for the projection matrix `$P^t$` — with their own learning rates, weight decay coefficients, and internal states.

**The weight update (Step 3 of Algorithm 1):**

$$\hat{\Delta}^t, \hat{S}^t = \text{Optimizer}_W(P_t^\top G_t, \hat{S}^{t-1})$$

$$W^{t+1} = W^t + \epsilon_t^W (P_t \hat{\Delta}^{t+1} - \lambda^W W^t)$$

where `$\text{Optimizer}_W$` is the base optimizer (e.g., 8-bit AdamW), `$P_t^\top G_t$` is the projected gradient of size `$k \times m$`, `$\hat{S}^{t-1}$` is the previous optimizer state of size `$k \times m$`, `$\hat{\Delta}^t$` is the optimizer's proposed update of size `$k \times m$`, `$\epsilon_t^W$` is the weight learning rate schedule, `$\lambda^W$` is the weight decay coefficient, and `$W^{t+1}$` is the updated full-size weight matrix.

**The projection matrix update (Step 4 of Algorithm 1):**

$$G^P_t = \nabla_P \mathcal{L}_{G_t}(P_t) \quad \text{where } \mathcal{L}_{G_t}(\cdot) \text{ is defined in Equation (6)}$$

$$\Delta^P_t, S^P_t = \text{Optimizer}_P(G^P_t, S^P_{t-1})$$

$$P^{t+1} = P^t + \epsilon_t^P (\Delta^P_t - \lambda^P P^t)$$

where `$\text{Optimizer}_P$` is a (potentially different) optimizer for the projection matrix, `$\nabla_P \mathcal{L}_{G_t}(P_t)$` is the gradient of the online PCA loss with respect to `$P$` (computed by backpropagation through Equation 6), `$G^P_t$` is this gradient of size `$n \times k$`, `$S^P_{t-1}$` is the optimizer state for `$P$` (size proportional to `$n \times k$`), `$\epsilon_t^P$` is the projection matrix learning rate, and `$\lambda^P$` is its weight decay.

**Key design choices and hyperparameter coupling:**

The paper recommends specific relationships between the weight and projection optimizers that reduce the hyperparameter search space:

- **Learning rate ratio `$\alpha = \epsilon_t^P / \epsilon_t^W = 5$`**: The projection matrix should be updated faster than the weights. The sweep in Figure 3 (middle panel) shows that setting `$\alpha = 5$` provides a good balance — "`$\alpha$` must be kept small to avoid instability." At `$\alpha = 1$`, the projection matrix lags too far behind the changing gradient landscape; at very high `$\alpha$`, the projection matrix oscillates and destabilizes training.

- **Symmetric weight decay `$\lambda^W = \lambda^P$`**: Both optimizers use the same weight decay coefficient. This symmetry is recommended in the algorithm remark but the paper does not extensively ablate it.

- **Base optimizer choice**: The paper's primary configuration uses 8-bit AdamW for `$\text{Optimizer}_W$` (following standard LLM training practice for its strong performance) and 8-bit AdamW for `$\text{Optimizer}_P$`. However, Section 5.4 explores alternatives — using SGD to update `$P_t$` "can achieve similar performance" (Table 2: Ours AdamW8bit + SGD at 53.53 vs. Ours AdamW8bit + AdamW8bit at 49.01 perplexity — a non-trivial gap, so AdamW remains the recommendation). The key finding is that the `$P_t$` update is not highly sensitive to the choice of optimizer; even simple SGD suffices to track the PCA objective, though Adam provides some improvement.

- **Learning rate for larger models**: For 60M parameter models, the learning rate sweep finds flexibility. For 350M and 1B models, the paper recommends "using a learning rate that is 10 times smaller, specifically 0.001" for the weight optimizer, as "larger learning rates cause unrecoverable spikes and instability."

**Why two separate optimizers:** The paper explicitly distinguishes between `$\text{Optimizer}_W$` and `$\text{Optimizer}_P$` because they operate on structurally different quantities. The weight optimizer processes gradients that are `$k \times m$` (reduced row dimension) with states proportional to that size. The projection optimizer processes gradients that are `$n \times k$` (the shape of `$P_t$`), with states proportional to `$n \times k$`. The total memory is `$O(km + nk + km + nk) = O(nk + km)$`, where the first `$km$` is for `$\hat{S}$`, the `$nk$` is for `$P_t$` itself, and the additional `$nk$` is for `$S^P$`. When `$k \ll \min(n, m)$`, this is substantially less than the `$O(nm)$` memory of full Adam. For example, with `$n = m = 4096$` and `$k = 512$`, the subspace method stores states proportional to `$512 \times 4096 \times 4 \approx 8$` million elements vs. `$4096 \times 4096 \times 2 \approx 33$` million for full Adam — roughly a `$4\times$` reduction for the optimizer states.

**Weight decay is a heuristic addition:** The paper explicitly notes that weight decay is "a common heuristic" and is not part of the theoretical analysis. The update `$W^{t+1} = W^t + \epsilon_t^W (P_t \hat{\Delta}^{t+1} - \lambda^W W^t)$` applies weight decay in the full `$n \times m$` space, not the projected space. This is important because it means that even directions currently outside the column space of `$P_t$` are slowly decayed toward zero, which could interact with the projection mechanism in subtle ways — the theoretical analysis in Section 4 does not account for weight decay.

#### The Hamiltonian Descent Framework: Why Dynamic Projections Don't Break Convergence

This is the paper's central theoretical contribution and the most technically dense part of the paper. The claim is that when a base optimizer has the Hamiltonian+Descent structure (Equation 2), equipping it with dynamic subspace projection *automatically preserves* the Lyapunov function — regardless of how `$P_t$` is updated. This resolves the "inertia entanglement" concern from Section 3.3.

**Recap: The base Hamiltonian+Descent structure.** The continuous-time limit of many common optimizers (momentum, Adam, LION) can be expressed as:

$$\frac{d}{dt}W^t = \partial_S H(W^t, S^t) - \Phi(\partial_W H(W^t, S^t))$$

$$\frac{d}{dt}S^t = -\partial_W H(W^t, S^t) - \Psi(\partial_S H(W^t, S^t))$$

where `$H(W, S)$` is a Hamiltonian (or Lyapunov) function satisfying `$\min_S H(W, S) = \mathcal{L}(W)$` for all `$W$` (so minimizing `$\mathcal{L}(W)$` reduces to minimizing `$H(W, S)$`), `$\Phi(\cdot)$` and `$\Psi(\cdot)$` are monotone operators satisfying `$\langle X, \Phi(X)\rangle = \|X\|^2_\Phi \geq 0$` and `$\langle X, \Psi(X)\rangle = \|X\|^2_\Psi \geq 0$` for all `$X$` (they provide the "descent" or friction in the system), `$\partial_W H$` and `$\partial_S H$` are the partial derivatives of the Hamiltonian with respect to the weights and optimizer states respectively, and `$S^t$` denotes the optimizer state (momentum, second moment, etc.).

**What this structure guarantees:** Without the `$\Phi$` and `$\Psi$` terms (setting both to zero), the system would be a pure Hamiltonian system that conserves `$H(W^t, S^t)$` exactly — like a frictionless pendulum that swings forever. The `$\Phi$` and `$\Psi$` terms add "friction" that *monotonically decreases* `$H$`:

$$\frac{d}{dt}H(W^t, S^t) = \langle \partial_W H^t, \frac{d}{dt}W^t \rangle + \langle \partial_S H^t, \frac{d}{dt}S^t \rangle$$

Substituting the dynamics:
$$= \langle \partial_W H^t, \partial_S H^t - \Phi(\partial_W H^t) \rangle + \langle \partial_S H^t, -\partial_W H^t - \Psi(\partial_S H^t) \rangle$$

The cross terms `$\langle \partial_W H^t, \partial_S H^t \rangle$` and `$\langle \partial_S H^t, -\partial_W H^t \rangle$` cancel (they are negatives of each other), leaving:

$$= -\|\partial_W H^t\|^2_\Phi - \|\partial_S H^t\|^2_\Psi \leq 0$$

The inequality holds because `$\Phi$` and `$\Psi$` are monotone. The key insight is that the Hamiltonian decreases at a rate proportional to the squared norms of the partial derivatives, weighted by the monotone operators' "strength." A larger decrease means faster convergence, but any non-negative decrease guarantees that the system is moving toward a local minimum of `$H$` (and hence of `$\mathcal{L}$`).

**The dynamic projection system (Equation 7).** When we apply subspace descent to this base system, we replace the state `$S^t$` (size `$n \times m$`) with a dimension-reduced state `$\hat{S}^t$` (size `$k \times m$`), and insert projections `$P_t$` and `$P_t^\top$` accordingly:

$$\frac{d}{dt}W^t = P_t \partial_{\hat{S}} H(W^t, \hat{S}^t) - \Phi(\partial_W H(W^t, \hat{S}^t))$$

$$\frac{d}{dt}\hat{S}^t = -P_t^\top \partial_W H(W^t, \hat{S}^t) - \Psi(\partial_{\hat{S}} H(W^t, \hat{S}^t))$$

$$\frac{d}{dt}P^t = \Gamma(P^t, \nabla \mathcal{L}(W^t))$$

where the first two equations are the subspace-projected versions of the base Hamiltonian descent dynamics, `$\partial_{\hat{S}} H$` is the derivative of the Hamiltonian with respect to the *reduced* state (still computed from the same Hamiltonian `$H$`, just evaluated at `$\hat{S}$` instead of `$S$`), and the third equation expresses the projection matrix dynamics through an arbitrary update function `$\Gamma$`.

**The crucial Lyapunov derivation (Equation 8).** Computing `$\frac{d}{dt}H(W^t, \hat{S}^t)$` for this system:

$$\frac{d}{dt}H(W^t, \hat{S}^t) = \langle \partial_W H^t, \frac{d}{dt}W^t \rangle + \langle \partial_{\hat{S}} H^t, \frac{d}{dt}\hat{S}^t \rangle$$

Substituting the dynamics:
$$= \langle \partial_W H^t, P_t \partial_{\hat{S}} H^t - \Phi(\partial_W H^t) \rangle + \langle \partial_{\hat{S}} H^t, -P_t^\top \partial_W H^t - \Psi(\partial_{\hat{S}} H^t) \rangle$$

The first term expands to `$\langle \partial_W H^t, P_t \partial_{\hat{S}} H^t \rangle - \langle \partial_W H^t, \Phi(\partial_W H^t) \rangle$`. The second term expands to `$-\langle \partial_{\hat{S}} H^t, P_t^\top \partial_W H^t \rangle - \langle \partial_{\hat{S}} H^t, \Psi(\partial_{\hat{S}} H^t) \rangle$`.

Now observe the cross terms: `$\langle \partial_W H^t, P_t \partial_{\hat{S}} H^t \rangle$` and `$-\langle \partial_{\hat{S}} H^t, P_t^\top \partial_W H^t \rangle$`. By the definition of the adjoint, `$\langle X, P Y \rangle = \langle P^\top X, Y \rangle$` for any matrices `$X, Y$` of compatible dimensions. Applying this with `$X = \partial_W H^t$` and `$Y = \partial_{\hat{S}} H^t$`:
$$\langle \partial_W H^t, P_t \partial_{\hat{S}} H^t \rangle = \langle P_t^\top \partial_W H^t, \partial_{\hat{S}} H^t \rangle = \langle \partial_{\hat{S}} H^t, P_t^\top \partial_W H^t \rangle$$

The inner product is symmetric, so these two terms are *exactly equal* and cancel identically, leaving:

$$= -\|\partial_W H^t\|^2_\Phi - \|\partial_{\hat{S}} H^t\|^2_\Psi \leq 0$$

**What this reveals — and why it's surprising:**

The final expression `$-\|\partial_W H^t\|^2_\Phi - \|\partial_{\hat{S}} H^t\|^2_\Psi$` is **structurally identical** to what we had for the base system, except that the second norm uses `$\partial_{\hat{S}} H$` instead of `$\partial_S H$`. The projection matrix `$P_t$` has *completely disappeared* from the Lyapunov decrease rate (except implicitly through how `$\hat{S}^t$` evolves), and the update rule `$\Gamma$` does not appear at all. This means:

- **The Lyapunov property is preserved regardless of `$\Gamma$`**: Whether `$P_t$` is updated by SVD, online PCA, random perturbations, or any other rule, the Hamiltonian `$H(W, \hat{S})$` will never increase — the system always dissipates "energy." The only requirement is that the derivatives in the chain rule exist (so `$P_t$` must be differentiable with respect to time, which is satisfied by any smooth update rule).

- **The projection works because of the adjoint relationship**: The cancellation `$\langle \partial_W H, P_t \partial_{\hat{S}} H \rangle = \langle P_t^\top \partial_W H, \partial_{\hat{S}} H \rangle$` is the linchpin. This holds for any `$P_t$` — it's a property of matrix algebra, not of optimization. This explains *why* the coupling between subspaces (via the momentum state `$\hat{S}$`) doesn't cause destructive interference: the adjoint structure mathematically forces the cross terms to cancel regardless of which specific `$P_t$` is used or how it was obtained.

- **The entanglement concern from Section 3.3 is resolved**: The worry was that `$\hat{S}^t$` accumulates projected gradients from many different `$P_\tau$` for `$\tau \leq t$`, and that using this accumulated state with a different `$P_t$` at the current step might produce an update that isn't coherent. The Lyapunov analysis shows that at the continuous-time level, this coherence is automatically maintained by the adjoint structure — the optimizer state `$\hat{S}^t$` is the *correct* state for the current `$P_t$` because the Hamiltonian `$H(W, \hat{S})$` is defined in terms of `$\hat{S}$` (not `$S$`), and the dissipation rate only depends on whether `$\partial_{\hat{S}} H$` is zero, not on whether the historical projections were consistent.

**Concrete examples of the framework.** The paper provides explicit continuous-time forms and Hamiltonians for the three major optimizer families equipped with subspace descent:

**Example 4.1 (Momentum + Online Subspace Descent):**
$$\frac{d}{dt}W^t = -P_t \hat{M}^t, \quad \hat{G}_t = P_t^\top \nabla \mathcal{L}(W^t), \quad \frac{d}{dt}\hat{M}^t = a(\hat{G}_t - \hat{M}^t)$$

where `$\hat{M}^t \in \mathbb{R}^{k \times m}$` is the dimension-reduced momentum, `$a > 0$` is the momentum decay rate (related to the discrete-time `$\beta$` parameter by `$a = (1-\beta)/\epsilon$`), and `$\hat{G}_t$` is the projected gradient. The Hamiltonian is:

$$H(W, \hat{M}) = \mathcal{L}(W) + \frac{\|\hat{M}\|^2}{2a}$$

where `$\mathcal{L}(W)$` is the training loss and `$\frac{\|\hat{M}\|^2}{2a}$` is the kinetic energy of the reduced momentum. The Lyapunov decrease rate works out to `$\frac{d}{dt}H = -\|\hat{M}^t\|^2 \leq 0$` — the dissipation comes entirely from the momentum decay term `$-a\hat{M}^t$` in the `$\hat{M}$` dynamics, with the projections cancelling via the adjoint as shown.

**Example 4.2 (Adam + Online Subspace Descent):**
$$\frac{d}{dt}W^t = P_t \frac{\hat{M}^t}{\sqrt{\hat{V}^t} + e}, \quad \frac{d}{dt}\hat{M}^t = a(\hat{G}_t - \hat{M}^t), \quad \frac{d}{dt}\hat{V}^t = b(\hat{G}_t^{\odot 2} - \hat{V}^t)$$

where `$\hat{V}^t \in \mathbb{R}^{k \times m}$` is the dimension-reduced second moment, `$b > 0$` is the second-moment decay rate, and the division/ square root are elementwise. The Hamiltonian is:

$$H(W, \hat{M}, \hat{V}) = \mathcal{L}(W) + \frac{1}{2a}\left\langle \frac{\hat{M}}{\sqrt{\hat{V}} + e}, \hat{M} \right\rangle$$

where `$\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle$` is the Frobenius inner product and the second term generalizes the kinetic energy to account for the per-parameter adaptive scaling. The decrease rate involves the condition `$a \geq b/4$` (a standard requirement for Adam's continuous-time analysis) and yields `$\frac{d}{dt}H \leq 0$`.

**Example 4.3 (LION-K + Online Subspace Descent):**
$$\frac{d}{dt}W^t = P_t \nabla K((1-b)\hat{M}^t - b\hat{G}_t), \quad \frac{d}{dt}\hat{M}^t = -a(\hat{G}_t + \hat{M}^t)$$

with Hamiltonian `$H(W, \hat{M}) = a\mathcal{L}(W) + \frac{1}{1-b}K((1-b)\hat{M})$` where `$K$` is any convex function with `$\nabla K(0) = 0$` (for standard LION, `$K(X) = \|X\|_{1,1}$` and `$\nabla K(X) = \text{sign}(X)$`). The decrease proof uses convexity of `$K$` via the `$[X; Y]_{\nabla K} \geq 0$` property.

**The non-obvious takeaway:** The paper's analysis shows that the Hamiltonian+Descent property — which is the theoretical basis for the convergence of the base optimizers — is **closed under projection by bounded linear operators with adjoints**. This is much stronger than proving convergence for a specific projection update rule; it means that any method of updating `$P_t$` (SVD, online PCA, random projections with a decay schedule, etc.) inherits the Lyapunov property, as long as the update rule is smooth enough that the time derivative exists.

#### The Non-Degeneracy Condition and Convergence Guarantee

The Lyapunov analysis guarantees that `$H(W^t, \hat{S}^t)$` never increases, but this alone doesn't guarantee convergence to a *stationary point of `$\mathcal{L}$`* — the system could converge to a point where `$P_t^\top \nabla \mathcal{L}(W^t) = 0$` but `$\nabla \mathcal{L}(W^t) \neq 0$`, which would mean the optimizer has stalled in a "bad" projection where the gradient is orthogonal to the current subspace. To rule this out, we need Assumption 4.4.

**Assumption 4.4, Part (i):** "`$\frac{d}{dt}H(W^t, \hat{S}^t) = 0$` implies `$\hat{G}_t = P_t^\top \nabla \mathcal{L}(W^t) = 0$` and `$\frac{d}{dt}W^t = 0$`."

This says that when the Hamiltonian stops decreasing (the system reaches an equilibrium), the projected gradient must be zero *and* the weights must stop changing. This is a property of the *base optimizer*, not the projection mechanism, and the paper states it is "easy to verify for the common optimizers like momentum, Adam, Lion-K." For momentum, `$\frac{d}{dt}H = -\|\hat{M}^t\|^2 = 0$` implies `$\hat{M}^t = 0$`, which from the `$\hat{M}$` dynamics `$\frac{d}{dt}\hat{M}^t = a(\hat{G}_t - \hat{M}^t) = a\hat{G}_t$` at equilibrium gives `$\hat{G}_t = 0$`, and then `$\frac{d}{dt}W^t = -P_t \hat{M}^t = 0$`. Similar reasoning applies to Adam and LION.

**Assumption 4.4, Part (ii):** "When `$G_t \equiv G \neq 0$`, the set `$\{P : P^\top G = 0\}$` is not a positive invariant set of `$\frac{d}{dt}P_t = \Gamma(P_t, G_t)$`."

This is the critical condition that prevents premature stopping. It says: if the true gradient `$G$` is non-zero but constant (a "stuck" situation), the set of projection matrices that are orthogonal to `$G$` — i.e., those that would give `$P^\top G = 0$` and mask the non-zero gradient — cannot be an *invariant set* of the `$P_t$` dynamics. If the `$P_t$` dynamics would eventually leave any such set, then the system cannot remain trapped at a point where `$G \neq 0$` but `$\hat{G} = 0$`.

**Why online PCA satisfies this:** If `$P_t$` is updated by gradient descent on `$\mathcal{L}_{G_t}(P)$` from Equation (6), and `$G_t \equiv G \neq 0$`, then at any `$P$` satisfying `$P^\top G = 0$`, the gradient `$\nabla_P \mathcal{L}_G(P)$` will *not* be zero — because the reconstruction error `$\|P P^\top \tilde{G} - \tilde{G}\|^2 = \|\tilde{G}\|^2 \neq 0$` and the gradient will push `$P$` toward directions that span `$G$`. Therefore, the system cannot remain in the degenerate state; the projection matrix will be pushed out of the orthogonal subspace, eventually allowing the optimizer to "see" the non-zero gradient and continue descending.

**Theorem 4.5 (Convergence to stationary points):** "Assume Assumption 4.4 holds. Let `$(W^t, \hat{S}^t, P^t)$` be a bounded solution of (7), then all the accumulation points `$\{W^t\}$` as `$t \to +\infty$` are stationary points of `$\mathcal{L}(W)$`."

**What this guarantees:** In the continuous-time limit, the weight trajectory converges to a set of points where `$\nabla \mathcal{L}(W) = 0$` — i.e., local minima, maxima, or saddle points. Combined with the Lyapunov decrease (which rules out maxima), this essentially guarantees convergence to (generalized) local minima. The boundedness condition prevents divergent trajectories.

**Proof sketch (via LaSalle's invariance principle):** LaSalle's invariance principle states that for a dynamical system with a Lyapunov function, all bounded trajectories converge to the largest invariant set contained in the set where `$\frac{d}{dt}H = 0$`. From Part (i) of Assumption 4.4, `$\frac{d}{dt}H = 0$` implies `$\hat{G}_t = 0$` and `$\frac{d}{dt}W^t = 0$`, which further implies `$\frac{d}{dt}G_t = 0$` (since `$G_t = \nabla \mathcal{L}(W^t)$` and `$W^t$` is constant, `$G_t$` is constant). So in the invariant set, we have `$G_t \equiv G$` constant and `$P_t^\top G = 0$`. Part (ii) of Assumption 4.4 says that if `$G \neq 0$`, the set `$\{P : P^\top G = 0\}$` is not invariant — but we're inside an invariant set, so this is a contradiction. Therefore, `$G$` must be zero. Thus, all trajectories in the limit set have `$\nabla \mathcal{L}(W) = 0$`, and since `$\frac{d}{dt}W = 0$`, these are stationary points.

**Practical significance:** This theorem tells practitioners that Online Subspace Descent won't get stuck in a "bad" subspace that hides the true gradient — as long as the online PCA update continues to track gradient directions, the system will eventually find a true stationary point. It also provides a criterion for designing alternative projection update rules: any rule that satisfies Parts (i) and (ii) of Assumption 4.4 inherits the convergence guarantee. This is what makes the guarantee "generally applicable to arbitrary update rules of the projection matrix."

#### Generalization to Arbitrary Linear Operators

The paper extends the framework from simple left-multiplication by `$P_t$` to more general linear operators, enabling richer projection structures.

**The generalized system (Section 4.3):**

$$\frac{d}{dt}W^t = \mathcal{P}_t(\partial_{\hat{S}} H(W^t, \hat{S}^t)) - \Phi(\partial_W H(W^t, \hat{S}^t))$$

$$\frac{d}{dt}\hat{S}^t = -\mathcal{P}^*_t(\partial_W H(W^t, \hat{S}^t)) - \Psi(\partial_{\hat{S}} H(W^t, \hat{S}^t))$$

$$\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{P}_t = \Gamma(\mathcal{P}_t, \nabla \mathcal{L}(W^t))$$

where `$\mathcal{P}_t$` is any linear operator (not just a matrix) with adjoint `$\mathcal{P}^*_t$` satisfying `$\langle X, \mathcal{P}_t(Y) \rangle = \langle \mathcal{P}^*_t(X), Y \rangle$` for all `$X, Y$`.

**What the adjoint condition means concretely:** The adjoint `$\mathcal{P}^*_t$` is the unique linear operator that makes the inner product identity hold. If `$\mathcal{P}_t$` maps inputs forward (from the reduced space to the full space), `$\mathcal{P}^*_t$` maps inputs backward (from the full space to the reduced space) such that the "energy" `$\langle X, \mathcal{P}_t(Y) \rangle$` computed in the full space equals the "energy" `$\langle \mathcal{P}^*_t(X), Y \rangle$` computed in the reduced space.

**Example: Two-sided projection `$\mathcal{P}_t(X) = P_t X Q_t$`.** This is the operator form that GaLore uses for 2D weight matrices, where `$P_t \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times k}$` projects the row space and `$Q_t \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times \ell}$` (or `$Q_t \in \mathbb{R}^{\ell \times m}$` depending on convention) projects the column space. The adjoint is `$\mathcal{P}^*_t(X) = P_t^\top X Q_t^\top$`, verified by:
$$\langle P_t X Q_t, Y \rangle = \text{tr}(P_t X Q_t Y^\top) = \text{tr}(X Q_t Y^\top P_t) = \text{tr}(X (P_t^\top Y Q_t^\top)^\top) = \langle X, P_t^\top Y Q_t^\top \rangle$$

The subspace descent system with this operator becomes:

$$\frac{d}{dt}W^t = P_t \partial_{\hat{S}} H(W^t, \hat{S}^t) Q_t - \Phi(\partial_W H(W^t, \hat{S}^t))$$

$$\frac{d}{dt}\hat{S}^t = -P_t^\top \partial_W H(W^t, \hat{S}^t) Q_t^\top - \Psi(\partial_{\hat{S}} H(W^t, \hat{S}^t))$$

with `$P_t$` and `$Q_t$` each updated by their own online PCA-like objectives (or jointly via an online SVD on `$G_t$`).

**Example: Sum-of-projections `$\mathcal{P}_t(X) = P_t X + X Q_t$`.** The adjoint is `$\mathcal{P}^*_t(X) = P_t^\top X + X Q_t^\top$`. This form appears in some structured matrix optimization contexts.

**Why the generalized framework matters:** The simple `$P_t$` projection reduces memory along one dimension (the rows), which is effective when weight matrices are very tall (large output dimension) or when the gradient's low-rank structure is predominantly along one axis. The two-sided projection `$P_t X Q_t$` reduces memory along both dimensions simultaneously, potentially achieving much larger compression ratios for large square weight matrices. The Lyapunov analysis in Section 4.1 showing that `$\frac{d}{dt}H = -\|\partial_W H\|^2_\Phi - \|\partial_{\hat{S}} H\|^2_\Psi$` carries through identically for all these forms because the cancellation `$\langle \partial_W H, \mathcal{P}_t(\partial_{\hat{S}} H) \rangle = \langle \mathcal{P}^*_t(\partial_W H), \partial_{\hat{S}} H \rangle$` relies only on the adjoint property, not on the specific form of `$\mathcal{P}_t$`. The paper's experiments use only the one-sided projection `$P_t$`, but this generalization shows that the theoretical guarantee covers GaLore's two-sided projections and other more complex structures.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: The Adjoint Cancellation — Why Dynamic Subspace Projection Works for *Any* Projection Update Rule

The paper's deepest conceptual move is not the algorithm itself, but the theoretical discovery of **why** dynamic subspace projection should work for momentum-based optimizers at all. Prior to this work, there was no framework for understanding the interaction between a dynamically changing projection matrix and an optimizer state that accumulates information across different projections. The field's implicit assumption — visible in the design of GaLore and Sketchy — was that the projection matrix needed to be computed carefully (via expensive SVD or eigendecomposition) to ensure that the projected gradients at different times were coherent with each other. The worry, articulated explicitly in the paper's own Section 3.3, was that momentum accumulated in one subspace might interfere destructively with descent in a different subspace — an "inertia entanglement" that could degrade or even break convergence.

The paper's adjoint cancellation result (Section 4.1, Equation 8) shows that this worry is **mathematically unfounded** for any optimizer with a Hamiltonian descent structure. The key identity `⟨∂_W H, P_t ∂_Ŝ H⟩ = ⟨P_t^⊤ ∂_W H, ∂_Ŝ H⟩` causes the cross terms in the Lyapunov derivative to cancel *identically*, regardless of what `P_t` is or how it was obtained. The projection matrix update rule Γ does not appear in the decrease rate `dH/dt = −∥∂_W H∥²_Φ − ∥∂_Ŝ H∥²_Ψ ≤ 0`. This means the Lyapunov property is **closed under projection by any linear operator with an adjoint** — a structural invariance that is completely independent of the specific update mechanism for `P_t`.

This is a fundamental theoretical advance rather than an incremental refinement. It transforms subspace descent from a heuristic that "seems to work" into a principled framework with a convergence guarantee that covers an entire *family* of possible projection update rules, including GaLore's periodic SVD, the paper's online PCA, random projections, or any future method. The guarantee's generality — "the first convergence guarantee for arbitrary update rules of the projection matrix" on non-convex objectives — means that future researchers designing new projection update mechanisms don't need to re-prove convergence from scratch; they only need to verify the mild non-degeneracy condition (Assumption 4.4) to inherit the guarantee.

The significance extends beyond the specific result. The paper provides a **diagnostic lens** for evaluating projection update rules: the only thing that can go wrong is premature stopping at `P_t^⊤ ∇L = 0` while `∇L ≠ 0` (the degenerate case ruled out by Assumption 4.4 Part ii). This reframes the design problem away from "is this projection update stable?" to the much simpler "does this update rule eventually escape subspaces orthogonal to the true gradient?" — a question that admits clear answers (online PCA escapes because the reconstruction loss is non-zero; random periodic resampling escapes trivially; a fixed stale projection does not escape, explaining why static subspace descent fails for pretraining).

The empirical anchor for this insight is indirect but essential: the paper's experiments show that online PCA (a cheap, approximate update) matches or exceeds the performance of exact SVD (an expensive, optimal update) — precisely what the theory predicts, since the theory says the update rule quality matters only through the non-degeneracy condition, not through approximation accuracy of the subspace. The 31.30 vs. 35.52 perplexity gap between Online Subspace Descent and GaLore at 1B parameters (Table 1) is evidence that continuous approximate updates can outperform periodic exact ones, consistent with the theoretical claim that update *frequency* and *recency* matter more than update *precision*.

### Innovation 2: The Inertia Problem Was an Illusion — Hamiltonian Descent as the Unifying Framework

A less explicit but equally important conceptual contribution is the paper's reframing of what the "hard problem" in dynamic subspace descent actually is. Before this work, the natural framing — which the paper itself uses in Section 3.3 to motivate the theory — was that the optimizer state `Ŝ^t` creates an inertia problem: it couples optimization across different subspaces, and the central question is how to manage that coupling. This framing leads naturally to design questions like "how slowly should `P_t` change to prevent destructive interference?" and "should we reset the optimizer state when the projection changes?"

The Hamiltonian descent analysis reveals that this framing is **backwards**. The coupling between subspaces through the optimizer state is not a problem to be managed — it is *exactly what makes the system work*, because the Hamiltonian `H(W, Ŝ)` is defined directly in terms of the reduced state `Ŝ`, and the adjoint structure ensures that the optimizer state is always the "correct" state for the current projection, regardless of how it was accumulated. The kinetic energy term `∥M̂∥²/(2a)` in the momentum Hamiltonian doesn't care whether `M̂` was built from consistent projections — it only cares about the current value of `M̂` and whether it's decreasing.

This is a reframing of the problem that changes what questions researchers should ask. Instead of "how do we prevent harmful interference between subspaces?", the right question is "how do we prevent the system from getting stuck in a subspace orthogonal to the true gradient?" (Assumption 4.4). Instead of "should the projection change slowly?", the right question is "how do we ensure the projection tracks the dominant gradient directions quickly enough to avoid premature stopping?" This shifts attention from stability concerns (which the Lyapunov analysis guarantees are automatically satisfied) to responsiveness concerns (which drive the design of the projection update rule).

The paper's explicit connection to Hamiltonian descent — drawing on the framework of Maddison et al. (2018) and Chen et al. (2023) — is itself a conceptual contribution. Prior work on memory-efficient optimization (GaLore, Sketchy, Adafactor) operated in the discrete algorithmic domain, analyzing update rules rather than continuous-time dynamics. By lifting the analysis to continuous time, the paper exposes the underlying geometric structure (the Hamiltonian, the adjoint cancellation, the Lyapunov function) that is obscured by step sizes, discretization, and stochastic noise. This is analogous to how the continuous-time analysis of gradient descent as gradient flow reveals properties (convex optimization convergence rates, the role of curvature) that are harder to see in the discrete case.

The concrete payoff is the unification of multiple optimizer families (momentum, Adam, LION) under a single theorem. Rather than proving separate convergence results for "Adam with subspace descent" and "LION with subspace descent" and "momentum with subspace descent," the paper proves one theorem for any optimizer with the Hamiltonian+Descent structure, and then verifies (Examples 4.1–4.3) that the major optimizers satisfy it. This is not just notational convenience — it reveals that the subspace projection mechanism interacts with the optimizer through a single mathematical property (the adjoint relationship), not through optimizer-specific details.

### Innovation 3: Online PCA as a Computationally-Negligible, Theoretically-Grounded Alternative to SVD

The practical innovation — replacing periodic SVD with continuous single-step online PCA — might appear to be a straightforward engineering optimization, but its significance is deeper when viewed through the theoretical lens the paper provides. The paper doesn't just propose online PCA as a cheaper approximation to SVD; it establishes that **the approximation quality of the projection update is not the bottleneck for convergence**. Since the Lyapunov property holds regardless of how `P_t` is updated, and convergence only requires the non-degeneracy condition (escape from orthogonal subspaces), the design objective for the projection update shifts from "compute the best possible low-rank approximation of the current gradient" (what SVD does) to "provide a cheap, timely signal that keeps `P_t` tracking the dominant gradient directions" (what online PCA does).

This is a **qualitative shift in what "good" means for the projection update**. GaLore's design philosophy — compute the exact top-k SVD of the gradient periodically — implicitly assumes that approximation accuracy is what matters. The paper's theory shows that what actually matters is *responsiveness* (how quickly `P_t` adapts when the gradient distribution shifts) and *non-degeneracy* (not getting stuck orthogonal to the gradient). A stale exact SVD (GaLore at iteration 199 of a 200-step update cycle) may be a worse choice than a slightly-noisy but current online PCA step, even though the SVD would achieve lower reconstruction error on the gradient *at the time it was computed*.

The empirical evidence for this shift is the timing measurements in Figure 2: SVD can be up to 142× slower than a single online PCA step on representative LLaMA weight tensor shapes. But the more important number is in Table 3: Online Subspace Descent achieves 1.3× faster wall-clock time than GaLore at 7B scale *while also achieving better perplexity* (43.72 vs. 51.21). This simultaneously-better-and-faster result is rare in systems optimization and signals that the SVD was not just expensive but *counterproductive* — its computational cost forced infrequent updates that degraded optimization quality, creating a lose-lose tradeoff between cost and performance. Online PCA breaks this tradeoff entirely: it's so cheap that it can run every iteration, and running every iteration improves optimization.

The flexibility of the online PCA framework — any optimizer can update `P_t`, the learning rate ratio α is the main tuning knob, weight decay symmetry is recommended but not mandatory — makes it a practical drop-in replacement for SVD in any projection-based training method. This is an enabling contribution: it lowers the barrier to experimenting with subspace descent by removing the computational bottleneck that made SVD-based approaches unattractive for large-scale training.

### Innovation 4: The Difficulty-as-Sufficient-Statistic Insight (Retrospective Justification for Why This Works)

A subtler conceptual move embedded in the paper's design is the implicit claim that **the current gradient `G_t` is a sufficient signal for updating the projection matrix** — that tracking the PCA of the instantaneous gradient stream is adequate for maintaining a useful subspace, without needing to model the gradient covariance across time explicitly. This stands in contrast to methods like Sketchy (Feinberg et al., 2023), which maintain an explicit sketch of the gradient covariance matrix, and to GaLore, which recomputes the subspace from scratch periodically rather than tracking it incrementally.

The online PCA objective (Equation 6) optimizes `P_t` to reconstruct the *current* normalized gradient `G̃_t` well, not to reconstruct the average gradient or the historical gradient covariance. This is a myopic objective — it doesn't look ahead or behind. Yet it works, and the theoretical framework explains why: the Lyapunov structure ensures that as long as `P_t` doesn't get stuck orthogonal to `G_t`, the system converges. There's no requirement that `P_t` span the historically dominant directions or approximate the true gradient covariance — only that it not be orthogonal to the current gradient when the gradient is non-zero.

This is a **minimalist sufficient condition**, and recognizing it as such is a conceptual contribution. It means that the projection update can be dramatically simpler than what prior work assumed was necessary. You don't need to maintain a running covariance estimate (Sketchy). You don't need to periodically solve an expensive eigenvalue problem on the full gradient (GaLore). You just need to nudge `P_t` in the direction that would better capture the current gradient, and the optimizer dynamics handle the rest. This minimalism is what enables the computational efficiency gains — if the projection update needed to be sophisticated to work, the method would inherit the same computational bottlenecks it seeks to eliminate.

The empirical anchor is the rank sweep in Figure 3 (left), which shows that higher ranks monotonically improve perplexity but with diminishing returns — a pattern consistent with the interpretation that the projection is providing a coarse-grained gradient summary, not a precise covariance estimate. If precise covariance tracking were necessary, we would expect a sharper threshold where performance degrades catastrophically below some critical rank; the smooth saturation suggests the system is tolerant of approximation, consistent with the theory's claim that only non-degeneracy is required.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** All pretraining experiments use the C4 dataset (Raffel et al., 2019), a large-scale corpus of cleaned web text commonly used for language model pretraining evaluation. The paper uses the default C4 training split for all runs.

- **Base model(s).** The paper trains LLaMA models (Touvron et al., 2023) from scratch at scales ranging from 60M to 7B parameters. The 60M, 350M, and 1B experiments are conducted on a single NVIDIA A100 GPU; the 7B experiment uses multiple GPUs (not explicitly specified, but the wall-clock timing comparison with GaLore implies multi-GPU setup). LLaMA is chosen because it is a standard open architecture for language model pretraining research, enabling direct comparison with GaLore and other memory-efficient training methods that also evaluate on LLaMA.

- **Metrics.** The primary metric throughout is **perplexity** on the C4 validation set, measured as the exponential of the cross-entropy loss. For the 1B model experiments in Table 1, perplexity is reported as "the training average of the last 10 steps." For the 7B model, the paper additionally reports **GLUE benchmark scores** (MRPC, RTE, SST2, MNLI, QNLI, QQP) using the eval-harness framework to assess downstream task performance of the pretrained checkpoints. **Wall-clock time** is reported for the 7B experiment (Table 3) as a direct measure of training throughput.

- **Baselines.** The paper compares against:
  - **8-bit AdamW (full rank)**: The standard full-storage adaptive optimizer with block-wise quantization (Dettmers et al., 2021), serving as the upper-bound performance reference.
  - **GaLore** (Zhao et al., 2024): The state-of-the-art low-rank projection method that uses periodic SVD to update the projection matrix. GaLore is tested with its recommended hyperparameters, including an update gap of 200 iterations and rank matched to Online Subspace Descent for fair comparison.
  - **Other base optimizers without subspace descent**: LION (Chen et al., 2023), Adafactor (Shazeer and Stern, 2018), and SGD, tested in Table 2 and Table 6 (Appendix) to assess how Online Subspace Descent performs when wrapping different base optimizers.
  - **GaLore with alternative base optimizers**: GaLore + LION and GaLore + Adafactor (Table 6, Appendix), to compare the subspace descent wrapper effect across optimizer families.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper does not use a "generation budget" in the sense of inference-time sampling (unlike the reference example). Instead, compute is measured in terms of **wall-clock time** (Table 3, Figure 2) and **memory consumption** (8.64 GB vs. 9.01 GB quoted in Section 5.1 for LLaMA 1B at rank 512). All methods are compared at equal training steps (10K steps for all experiments) with matched batch sizes (512) and sequence lengths (256 or 1024 depending on the experiment). For timing comparisons, SVD and online PCA are measured as isolated operations on representative tensor shapes (Figure 2).

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not report cross-validation or statistical significance testing. Results are reported from single training runs at each configuration. The rank sweep (Figure 3, left) and hyperparameter sweeps (Figure 3, middle and right) show smooth trends that suggest results are not dominated by noise, but no standard deviations, confidence intervals, or multiple seeds are reported. For the 7B experiment (Tables 3 and 4), a single training run is reported.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Aggregate Performance: Online Subspace Descent vs. GaLore vs. Full-Rank

**Headline result (Table 1):** For LLaMA 1B pretrained with sequence length 256 and rank 512 for 10K steps, Online Subspace Descent achieves **31.30 perplexity** compared to **35.52 for GaLore** and **29.40 for full-rank 8-bit AdamW**. This represents a 4.22 perplexity point improvement over GaLore, closing more than half the gap between GaLore and the full-rank baseline. The memory consumption is comparable: Online Subspace Descent uses 8.64 GB vs. GaLore's 9.01 GB — a slight memory advantage despite the continuous projection updates, likely because online PCA requires less working memory than batched SVD computation.

At smaller scales (LLaMA 60M), the gap narrows: Online Subspace Descent achieves 56.12 vs. GaLore's 57.03 at rank 512, with full-rank at 32.75. At 350M, Online Subspace Descent achieves 43.67 vs. GaLore's 44.34, with full-rank at 30.43. The trend is clear: **as model size increases, Online Subspace Descent's advantage over GaLore grows**, from 0.91 perplexity points at 60M to 4.22 at 1B. The paper hypothesizes (Section 5.1) that this is "due to the higher intrinsic rank of the underlying optimization problem in larger models," making the positive impact of online (frequent) projection updates more pronounced.

**Supporting evidence (Figure 1):** The validation perplexity curves for LLaMA 1B with sequence length 256 and rank 512 show Online Subspace Descent diverging from GaLore starting around step 2,000, with the gap widening consistently through the full 10K steps. Both methods track closely for the first ~1,500 steps, suggesting the benefit of online updates emerges as the gradient landscape shifts and GaLore's stale SVD-based projections become increasingly misaligned.

#### Computational Overhead: Online PCA vs. SVD

**Headline result (Figure 2):** The paper measures the execution time of `torch.svd` versus a single-step backward pass for the online PCA objective on matrices of typical shapes found in LLaMA linear layers (ranging from 60M to 7B scale). On an A100 GPU, SVD is up to **142× slower** than a single online PCA step for representative weight tensor shapes. On a consumer RTX 3090, the gap is similarly large. The paper notes that "each step of online PCA can be cleverly scheduled and hidden in the weight optimization step when executed in parallel, whereas SVD is too expensive to be hidden."

**Supporting evidence (Table 3):** For the 7B model pretrained on C4 for 10K steps, Online Subspace Descent (with P_t updated by SGD) achieves wall-clock time of **7.14 hours** compared to **9.74 hours** for GaLore — a **1.36× speedup** — while simultaneously achieving lower perplexity (43.72 vs. 51.21). This is the crucial "better and faster" result that distinguishes Online Subspace Descent from a mere approximation tradeoff: the SVD was not just expensive but counterproductive, because its cost forced infrequent updates that degraded optimization quality.

#### Rank Ablation: How Much Compression Is Acceptable?

**Headline result (Figure 3, left; Table 5):** Sweeping rank across {32, 128, 512, full} for LLaMA 60M with sequence length 1024 reveals that **higher ranks monotonically improve perplexity**, but with diminishing returns. At rank 32, Online Subspace Descent achieves 85.90 vs. GaLore's 86.16 (near-identical, both far from full-rank at 37.18). At rank 128, Online Subspace Descent achieves 49.01 vs. GaLore's 48.05 (GaLore slightly better). At rank 512, Online Subspace Descent achieves 37.41 vs. GaLore's 36.93 (GaLore still slightly better at this small scale). Full-rank achieves 37.18 for Online Subspace Descent and 36.51 for GaLore.

The paper's interpretation of the saturation pattern is: "high-frequency tokens can be effectively learned with low-rank training. However, learning lower-frequency tokens requires higher ranks. Once these lower-frequency tokens are adequately learned, further increasing the rank does not significantly decrease perplexity." The recommendation is to "select the highest rank until the perplexity reduction saturates."

A nuance that complicates the rank comparison: at 60M scale, GaLore is actually slightly *better* than Online Subspace Descent at ranks 128 and 512 (48.05 vs. 49.01; 36.93 vs. 37.41). The Online Subspace Descent advantage emerges at larger scales (350M and 1B) where GaLore's stale projections become more costly. This scale-dependent crossover is not explicitly discussed by the authors but is visible in the data.

#### Hyperparameter Sensitivity: Learning Rate Ratio α and Orthonormality Penalty λ

**Headline result (Figure 3, middle and right; Section 5.3):** For LLaMA 60M training:

- **α (projection learning rate ratio `ε_t^P / ε_t^W`):** Swept values produce loss curves that diverge meaningfully. The paper reports that "α must be kept small to avoid instability" and sets `α = 5` for all subsequent experiments. The loss curves in Figure 3 (middle) show that higher α values lead to faster initial descent but eventual instability or divergence, while very low α underperforms due to the projection matrix lagging behind gradient changes.

- **λ (orthonormality penalty):** The paper finds that "the result is not sensitive to λ for small models (60M)" and sets `λ = 0.1` for all subsequent experiments. The loss curves (Figure 3, right) are nearly overlapping across the swept λ values, confirming the insensitivity claim at this scale.

- **Learning rate (Section 5.3):** For the 60M model, "learning rate choices are more flexible, producing similar results." However, for larger models (350M, 1B), the paper recommends "using a learning rate that is 10 times smaller, specifically 0.001," noting that "larger learning rates cause unrecoverable spikes and instability, a general characteristic observed across all methods." This scale-dependent learning rate sensitivity is a practical consideration for practitioners scaling up.

#### Compatibility with Different Base Optimizers

**Headline result (Table 2; Table 6 in Appendix):** Online Subspace Descent is tested with multiple base optimizers for both weight updates and projection matrix updates on LLaMA 60M with sequence length 1024:

- **Ours AdamW8bit + AdamW8bit**: 49.01 perplexity (the primary configuration)
- **Ours Adafactor + AdamW8bit**: 34.15 perplexity — notably better than the primary configuration at this scale, and substantially better than full-rank Adafactor at 33.45
- **Ours LION + AdamW8bit**: 44.76 — worse than the primary but better than full-rank LION at 52.65
- **Ours LION + LION**: 57.97 — worse than full-rank LION, suggesting LION is a poor choice for projection matrix updates
- **Ours Adafactor + Adafactor**: 47.61 — worse than using AdamW8bit for the projection
- **Ours AdamW8bit + SGD**: 53.53 — using simple SGD for the projection matrix degrades performance but is still functional, consistent with the theory's claim that the projection update rule matters only through non-degeneracy

The key finding is that **AdamW8bit is the recommended optimizer for the projection matrix** regardless of what optimizer is used for the weights — the combination "Adafactor + AdamW8bit" outperforms "Adafactor + Adafactor" (34.15 vs. 47.61) and "LION + AdamW8bit" outperforms "LION + LION" (44.76 vs. 57.97). This suggests the projection matrix optimization benefits from Adam's adaptive learning rates and momentum more than the weight optimization does.

**Comparison with GaLore variants:** GaLore + Adafactor achieves 34.32 vs. Ours Adafactor + AdamW8bit at 34.15 (near-identical at this scale). GaLore + LION achieves 46.90 vs. Ours LION + AdamW8bit at 44.76 (Online Subspace Descent better). GaLore + AdamW8bit at 48.05 vs. Ours AdamW8bit + AdamW8bit at 49.01 (GaLore slightly better at 60M — consistent with the scale-dependent crossover seen in Table 5).

#### Scaling to 7B Parameters and Downstream Evaluation

**Headline result (Table 3):** Pretraining a 7B LLaMA model on C4 for 10K steps (with P_t updated by SGD, not AdamW8bit, suggesting computational constraints at this scale), Online Subspace Descent achieves **43.72 perplexity** vs. **51.21 for GaLore** — a 7.49 perplexity point improvement, the largest absolute gap reported in the paper. Wall-clock time is 7.14 hours vs. 9.74 hours (1.36× faster).

**Downstream evaluation (Table 4):** On the GLUE benchmark using eval-harness, the Online Subspace Descent checkpoint outperforms the GaLore checkpoint on 4 of 6 tasks: MRPC (0.6982 vs. 0.6838), SST2 (0.5233 vs. 0.5183), MNLI (0.3654 vs. 0.3506), QNLI (0.5142 vs. 0.4946), and QQP (0.3795 vs. 0.3682). GaLore wins on RTE (0.5018 vs. 0.4901). The average across tasks is 0.4951 for Online Subspace Descent vs. 0.4862 for GaLore — a 0.89 point advantage. These are low absolute scores (all well below state-of-the-art), consistent with a 7B model pretrained for only 10K steps (far from convergence), but the relative comparison is meaningful: better perplexity during pretraining translates to better downstream representations.

The paper notes in the 7B experiment that P_t is updated by SGD rather than AdamW8bit. This is a practical concession to memory constraints at 7B scale (maintaining a separate AdamW state for P_t would consume additional memory proportional to `n × k`, which becomes substantial when `n` is large). The fact that SGD-updated P_t still outperforms GaLore by a wide margin reinforces the theoretical claim that the projection update rule matters primarily through non-degeneracy, not through optimization quality.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Rank sweep across model scales (Table 5, Figure 3 left):** Sweeping rank from 32 to full rank on LLaMA 60M (sequence length 1024) shows monotonic improvement with diminishing returns. At rank 32, both methods are near 86 perplexity (far from full-rank at ~37). At rank 512, both methods approach full-rank performance (37.41 for Ours, 36.93 for GaLore, 37.18 for full-rank). The key non-obvious finding is the **scale-dependent crossover**: GaLore is slightly better at 60M across most ranks, but Online Subspace Descent is substantially better at 1B. This is not highlighted by the authors but is clearly visible in the data (Table 1 vs. Table 5).

**Base optimizer sweep (Table 6 in Appendix A.3):** Exhaustive testing of {SGD, LION, Adafactor, AdamW8bit} for both weight and projection optimizers on LLaMA 60M. Finding: AdamW8bit as the projection optimizer consistently yields the best or near-best results regardless of the weight optimizer choice. SGD alone achieves 3469.14 perplexity — essentially non-functional — confirming that base optimizer quality matters enormously. The combination "Adafactor weights + AdamW8bit projection" (34.15) nearly matches full-rank Adafactor (33.45), suggesting Online Subspace Descent can recover most of Adafactor's performance while reducing memory (since the Adafactor state is applied only in the reduced `k × m` space).

**Learning rate sensitivity at scale:** The paper reports (Section 5.3) that for 60M models, learning rate choices are flexible, but for 350M and 1B models, using a learning rate of 0.001 is required — 10× smaller than what works at 60M. This is described as a general characteristic across all methods, not specific to Online Subspace Descent, but it represents a practical tuning burden when scaling up.

**Sequence length scaling (implied comparison):** The paper reports results at sequence lengths 256 (Table 1, Figure 1) and 1024 (Table 5, Figure 3 rank sweep, Table 2). While not presented as a formal ablation, the results show Online Subspace Descent is effective at both sequence lengths. The 1B results with sequence length 256 (Table 1) show the largest advantage over GaLore, while the 60M results with sequence length 1024 (Table 5) show GaLore slightly ahead, suggesting the sequence length × model size interaction may be complex.

**Projection matrix optimizer choice at 7B scale:** The 7B experiment uses SGD for P_t updates while all smaller-scale experiments use AdamW8bit. This is a practical necessity (memory constraints), but the fact that performance remains strong (43.72 vs. 51.21 for GaLore) serves as an implicit ablation showing that the projection update optimizer is not critical — consistent with the theory's claim that the projection update rule matters mainly through non-degeneracy, not optimization precision.

**Orthonormality penalty sensitivity (Figure 3, right):** The loss curves for different λ values on LLaMA 60M are nearly overlapping, confirming that the orthonormality regularizer in Equation (6) is not a sensitive hyperparameter at this scale. The paper sets λ = 0.1 for all experiments based on this sweep.

### Critical Assessment

The experimental section provides substantial evidence for the paper's practical claims but leaves several important questions unaddressed.

**Does Online Subspace Descent genuinely outperform GaLore?** The answer is **scale-dependent in ways the paper does not fully characterize.** At 60M parameters (Tables 5 and 6), GaLore is often slightly better than Online Subspace Descent (e.g., 36.93 vs. 37.41 at rank 512; 48.05 vs. 49.01 at rank 128). At 350M, the gap shifts to Online Subspace Descent's favor (43.67 vs. 44.34). At 1B, Online Subspace Descent is substantially better (31.30 vs. 35.52). At 7B, the advantage is largest (43.72 vs. 51.21). This monotonic trend with model size is compelling, but the paper only reports two intermediate scales (350M, 1B) and the endpoints (60M, 7B). A finer-grained scaling curve (e.g., 150M, 700M, 3B) would clarify where and why the crossover occurs. The hypothesis that "higher intrinsic rank" in larger models favors online updates is plausible but untested — no measurement of effective rank or gradient spectrum is provided across model scales.

**Does the advantage come from update frequency or from avoiding SVD staleness?** The paper attributes Online Subspace Descent's advantage to replacing periodic SVD with continuous online updates, which improves both computational efficiency (faster updates) and optimization quality (more timely gradient tracking). These two factors are confounded in all experiments: Online Subspace Descent updates P_t every iteration and is faster; GaLore updates only every 200 iterations and is slower. An ablation that would disentangle these would be: run GaLore with more frequent SVD (e.g., T=10 or T=50) to test whether the staleness or the SVD cost is the primary factor. If more frequent SVD closes the perplexity gap but increases wall-clock time, then SVD staleness is the bottleneck; if more frequent SVD doesn't help, then SVD approximation quality is the issue. This ablation is not present.

**Does the theoretical guarantee translate to practice?** The convergence analysis (Section 4) operates in continuous time with infinitesimal step sizes and no stochastic gradient noise — a setting far removed from the discrete, stochastic training of 10K-step LLM pretraining runs. The paper provides no empirical verification that the Lyapunov property holds in practice (e.g., measuring whether the Hamiltonian-like quantity decreases monotonically during training, or whether the non-degeneracy condition is ever violated). The theory serves as motivation and plausibility argument, not as a directly tested hypothesis. This is not necessarily a weakness — theoretical analyses of optimization often operate at this level of abstraction — but the claim of "the first convergence guarantee" should be understood as applying to the continuous-time ODE limit, not to the actual stochastic discrete algorithm.

**How does performance scale with training duration?** All experiments are limited to 10K steps — a tiny fraction of typical LLM pretraining (which often runs for hundreds of thousands of steps). At 10K steps, the models are far from convergence (full-rank 8-bit AdamW achieves 29.40 at 1B after 10K steps — a strong LLM at this scale would be closer to 10–15 perplexity). It is unclear whether the gap between Online Subspace Descent and GaLore would persist, widen, or narrow over a full training run. The diminishing returns in the rank sweep (Figure 3, left) suggest that the benefits of online updates might be most pronounced early in training when the gradient landscape changes rapidly, and might diminish as training stabilizes — or conversely, GaLore might catch up if given enough time to periodically recompute the SVD. A longer training run (e.g., 50K or 100K steps) is a conspicuous absence.

**Are the memory savings accurately characterized?** The paper reports memory for LLaMA 1B: 8.64 GB (Online Subspace Descent) vs. 9.01 GB (GaLore). This is a ~4% memory reduction. The theoretical memory advantage should be more substantial: GaLore must materialize the full gradient for SVD computation, while online PCA only requires the gradient for a single backward pass through a small loss function. The small measured difference might reflect implementation details (e.g., PyTorch memory caching, gradient accumulation buffers) rather than algorithmic memory requirements. A detailed memory breakdown (model weights, optimizer states, projection matrix, SVD workspace vs. online PCA workspace, activations) would clarify where memory is actually saved.

**What about two-sided projection (P_t X Q_t)?** The theory in Section 4.3 explicitly covers two-sided projection operators, and GaLore uses these for 2D weight matrices. The paper's experiments appear to use only one-sided projection (P_t), which reduces memory along only one dimension of each weight matrix. For large square weight matrices (e.g., 4096 × 4096), two-sided projection could achieve much larger compression ratios. The absence of two-sided projection experiments means the method may be using more memory than necessary for such layers, and the comparison with GaLore (which uses two-sided projections) may not be at equal compression ratios.

**Missing baselines and comparisons:**

- **No comparison with Sketchy** (Feinberg et al., 2023): The paper cites Sketchy as prior work that uses eigendecomposition at every step, positioning online PCA as a cheaper alternative. But no direct experimental comparison is provided. Since Sketchy also uses frequent (every-step) subspace updates, comparing against it would test whether online PCA's approximation quality is competitive with eigendecomposition-based updates.

- **No comparison with simple heuristics:** Could updating P_t by simply taking the top-k gradient directions via randomized SVD (a fast approximation) achieve similar results? Could a running average of gradient outer products with periodic eigendecomposition match online PCA? The design space of cheap projection updates is large, and the paper only compares against expensive exact SVD.

- **No comparison with quantization-only methods at matched memory:** What if one used a larger model with full-storage Adam but aggressive 4-bit quantization? The memory budget could potentially match the subspace approach. The paper doesn't explore this dimension of the memory-accuracy tradeoff space.

- **No Adafactor with matched parameter count:** Since Adafactor achieves sublinear memory scaling, one could train a *larger* model with Adafactor using the same memory as a smaller model with Online Subspace Descent + Adam. The paper compares only at equal model sizes, not equal memory budgets — a missed opportunity to demonstrate practical value.

**Where the claims hold and where they are conditional:**

- **"Better perplexity than GaLore"** holds at 350M, 1B, and 7B scales, but *not* at 60M scale where GaLore is competitive or slightly better. The claim should be conditioned on model scale.

- **"Up to 1.3× faster than GaLore"** holds at 7B (Table 3). The paper does not report timing for smaller scales, so the speedup's scale-dependence is unknown. At small scales where SVD is less dominant, the speedup may be smaller or absent.

- **"Closes the gap with full-rank baselines"** is true in the sense that Online Subspace Descent is closer to full-rank than GaLore is, but there remains a substantial gap: 31.30 vs. 29.40 at 1B (Table 1), or 1.9 perplexity points. This is meaningfully better than GaLore's 6.12 point gap, but it's not "closed" in any absolute sense.

- **"Minimum overhead to training"** is supported by Figure 2 (online PCA is fast) and the claim that it can be hidden behind weight updates, but no end-to-end profiling data is provided showing that the overhead is truly zero in practice (e.g., measuring throughput with and without the P_t update at various scales).

**What would strengthen the paper:**

- Training runs beyond 10K steps to assess whether advantages persist or diminish over realistic training horizons.
- Direct measurement of gradient subspace effective rank across training to test the hypothesis about why online updates help more at larger scales.
- Ablation of GaLore with more frequent SVD updates to isolate staleness from update mechanism.
- Memory profiling that breaks down exactly where bytes are saved.
- Two-sided projection experiments to test the generalized operator framework empirically.
- Evaluation on a standard downstream benchmark beyond GLUE for the 1B and 350M checkpoints to verify that perplexity improvements translate to task performance at those scales (the GLUE evaluation is only for 7B).

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### 6.1 The Convergence Guarantee Applies to the Continuous-Time ODE Limit, Not the Actual Discrete Stochastic Algorithm

**The assumption or constraint.** The theoretical analysis in Section 4 operates entirely in continuous time with infinitesimal step sizes and full-batch gradients — the standard "ODE limit" approach to optimization dynamics. The Lyapunov function `$H(W, \hat{S})$`, the adjoint cancellation argument (Equation 8), the LaSalle invariance proof of Theorem 4.5, and the non-degeneracy condition (Assumption 4.4) all describe the behavior of the system `$d/dt (W^t, \hat{S}^t, P^t)$` as a set of coupled ordinary differential equations. The actual algorithm (Algorithm 1) is a discrete-time, finite-step-size, stochastic-gradient procedure — the gap between the ODE analysis and the discrete implementation is not bridged theoretically.

The paper is transparent that this is how Hamiltonian descent analyses work, citing Maddison et al. (2018) and Chen et al. (2023), and the standard logic in this literature is that "a sound optimizer should be guaranteed to converge to local optima of the loss, at least when using sufficiently small step sizes" (Section 2). But the phrase "sufficiently small step sizes" is doing heavy lifting: in practice, LLM pretraining uses the largest stable learning rate (often found by grid search and tuned near the edge of divergence), and stochastic gradient noise can qualitatively change the dynamics (e.g., the role of noise in escaping saddle points or favoring flat minima).

**The consequence.** The theoretical guarantee — convergence to stationary points of `$\mathcal{L}(W)$` — does not necessarily apply to any practical training run. The paper provides no discrete-time convergence rate, no bound on how small the step size must be for the ODE approximation to be valid, and no analysis of how stochastic gradient noise interacts with the subspace projection (e.g., could the noise in `$\hat{G}_t$` cause the online PCA objective to track spurious directions from a noisy mini-batch rather than the true gradient subspace?).

Two specific failure modes are particularly concerning in the discrete-stochastic regime:

- **The non-degeneracy condition (Assumption 4.4 Part ii) may be violated transiently.** In the continuous deterministic limit, the online PCA update is guaranteed to eventually push `$P_t$` out of any set where `$P_t^\top G = 0$` with `$G \neq 0$`. In the discrete stochastic setting, a single mini-batch gradient `$G_t$` is a noisy estimate of `$\nabla \mathcal{L}(W^t)$`. If the noise happens to produce a `$G_t$` that is nearly orthogonal to the true gradient, and the projection matrix `$P_t$` is updated based on this noisy `$G_t$`, the projection could drift into a degenerate configuration where `$P_t^\top \nabla \mathcal{L}(W^t) \approx 0$` despite `$\nabla \mathcal{L}(W^t) \neq 0$`. The ODE analysis provides no guarantee that the discrete system escapes this transiently — it could stall for many iterations, wasting compute.

- **Discretization errors in the adjoint cancellation.** The Lyapunov derivation in Equation (8) relies on the continuous-time chain rule `$\frac{d}{dt}H = \langle \partial_W H, \frac{d}{dt}W \rangle + \langle \partial_{\hat{S}} H, \frac{d}{dt}\hat{S} \rangle$`. In discrete time, the weight update and state update are computed at slightly different moments (the state is updated using `$P_t^\top G_t$`, then the weight is updated using the new state). The cross-term cancellation `$\langle \partial_W H, P_t \partial_{\hat{S}} H \rangle = \langle P_t^\top \partial_W H, \partial_{\hat{S}} H \rangle$` is exact in continuous time but gains a discretization error of `$O(\epsilon_t)$` in discrete time. For the large learning rates used in LLM pretraining (the paper recommends 0.01 for 60M models, 0.001 for larger ones — Section 5.3), this discretization error could be non-negligible.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** **None directly.** The paper provides no empirical measurement of any Lyapunov-like quantity during training (e.g., tracking a discretized Hamiltonian to verify it decreases monotonically). The convergence analysis is presented purely as theory (Section 4), and the experiments (Section 5) demonstrate that Online Subspace Descent achieves lower perplexity than GaLore, but do not verify that the theoretical mechanism (adjoint cancellation, non-degeneracy, Lyapunov decrease) is actually operating in the discrete system. The loss curves (Figure 1, Figure 3) show monotonic improvement, which is consistent with convergence but does not distinguish between the proposed mechanism and other explanations.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not discuss the ODE-discrete gap or its implications for practice. The convergence guarantee is stated without caveats about the continuous-time assumption (e.g., Theorem 4.5 is presented as a guarantee for "the system (7)" which is the continuous-time system, but the algorithm the paper proposes is the discrete-time Algorithm 1 — the relationship is not interrogated). The paper suggests future work on "optimal rules of `$P_t$` to maximize the decreasing rate as an optimal control problem" (Section 4.1), which would further deepen the continuous-time theory, but does not flag bridging the discrete-continuous gap as a priority.

### 6.2 All Experiments Are Limited to 10K Training Steps — a Tiny Fraction of Realistic LLM Pretraining

**The assumption or constraint.** Every training run in the paper — from the 60M parameter experiments through the 7B parameter scaling experiment — is conducted for exactly 10,000 steps (Section 5). For context, realistic LLM pretraining runs typically span hundreds of thousands to millions of steps. The 7B LLaMA model trained for 10K steps in Table 3 achieves 43.72 perplexity on C4; a fully-trained 7B model on C4 would be expected to reach well below 15 perplexity. The models are far from convergence at the point where all comparisons are made.

The paper does not explicitly justify the 10K-step horizon. Section 5 states the training protocol but does not discuss why this duration was chosen or whether the results are expected to generalize to longer training.

**The consequence.** The relative performance of Online Subspace Descent vs. GaLore vs. full-rank could shift substantially over a full training run in at least three ways:

- **The advantage could narrow.** If the primary benefit of online PCA over periodic SVD is more timely tracking of the changing gradient landscape (Section 5.1 hypothesis), then this benefit is largest early in training when the loss landscape changes rapidly (the model is moving far from its random initialization). As training converges, the gradient distribution may stabilize, making GaLore's periodic SVD less "stale" relative to online PCA. The gap observed at step 10K might overstate the advantage at step 100K.

- **The advantage could widen.** If the projection matrix update rule matters for escaping suboptimal regions (e.g., saddle points or narrow valleys), then the benefit of continuous updates might compound over longer training — early small advantages in gradient tracking lead to better optimization trajectories that diverge further over time. The loss curves in Figure 1 show the gap between Online Subspace Descent and GaLore widening from step ~2,000 through step 10,000 without plateauing, suggesting the trend could continue.

- **The rank scaling could change.** The paper's rank sweep (Figure 3, left; Table 5) shows that higher ranks monotonically improve 10K-step perplexity with diminishing returns. Over longer training, the effective rank required for good performance might change — early training might benefit from lower-rank projections that capture the dominant gradient signal, while later fine-grained optimization might require higher ranks to learn low-frequency patterns (consistent with the paper's own interpretation in Section 5.2). The optimal rank might shift over the course of training, but the paper only evaluates fixed-rank configurations.

The GLUE evaluation in Table 4 provides a weak signal of transfer to downstream tasks, but at 10K steps these scores (e.g., 0.3654 MNLI, 0.5233 SST2) are substantially below what a converged 7B model would achieve, and it's unclear whether the 0.89-point average advantage over GaLore would persist, grow, or reverse at convergence.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The loss curves (Figure 1) show the gap widening through step 10,000 without plateauing, suggesting the results are not at steady state. The paper provides no extrapolation, no checkpoint at intermediate scale (50K, 100K steps), and no discussion of how training duration might affect the conclusions. This is a missing experiment, not a negative result.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed, and not acknowledged as a limitation. The paper treats the 10K-step results as sufficient to support its claims of improved performance vs. GaLore. In the context of the paper's scope (a first demonstration of a new method with theoretical foundations), 10K steps may be reasonable as a proof of concept, but a practitioner deciding whether to adopt Online Subspace Descent for a full pretraining run would need evidence at realistic training horizons.

### 6.3 The Difficulty Estimation Cost Analogy: Difficulty in Predicting Effective Rank Without Exhaustive Sweeps

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper provides *no method* for determining the appropriate rank `$k$` for a given model, task, and training budget beyond "sweep it and pick the largest rank until perplexity reduction saturates" (Section 5.2). The rank is a critical hyperparameter that directly controls the memory-accuracy tradeoff: lower rank means less memory and faster iteration but potentially worse final performance. The paper's experiments use ranks 32, 128, 512, and full, but the choice of these specific values appears arbitrary rather than derived from any analysis of the weight matrices' spectral properties or the gradient's effective rank.

This is analogous to the "difficulty estimation cost" flagged in the reference example — the paper demonstrates that the method works once the rank is correctly set, but doesn't account for the cost of *finding* the correct rank. A practitioner would need to run multiple training experiments at different ranks to determine the saturation point, each of which could be expensive for large models.

**The consequence.** The headline efficiency gains (e.g., 8.64 GB for Online Subspace Descent vs. presumably substantially more for full-rank Adam at 1B) assume that the chosen rank achieves acceptable performance. But if the required rank is significantly higher than expected — perhaps because the gradient's effective rank is larger for certain architectures, datasets, or training phases — the memory savings shrink. At the extreme, if the optimal rank approaches the full dimension, the subspace method offers no memory benefit (and introduces overhead from the projection matrix optimization).

The paper's rank sweep results (Figure 3, left; Table 5) show meaningful differences between adjacent ranks: at LLaMA 60M/SS 1024, rank 128 achieves 49.01 perplexity vs. 37.41 at rank 512. If a practitioner chooses rank 128 to save memory, they sacrifice ~11.6 perplexity points. If they choose rank 512 to be safe, they may be using more memory than necessary. Without a method for predicting the required rank — perhaps from the gradient spectrum measured early in training, or from model architecture properties — the practical deployment requires expensive trial and error.

Additionally, the paper's hypothesis that larger models have higher "intrinsic rank" of the optimization problem (Section 5.1) implies that the rank budget should scale with model size, but the paper provides no scaling law or heuristic for how `$k$` should grow with parameter count. At 60M, rank 512 nearly matches full-rank; at 1B, rank 512 still leaves a 1.9 perplexity gap to full-rank (Table 1); at 7B, the gap with rank 512 is presumably larger (though not directly measured, since the 7B experiment doesn't report full-rank performance). The practitioner is left guessing whether rank 512 is sufficient for their target model scale.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The rank sweep (Figure 3, left; Table 5) demonstrates the sensitivity at one scale (60M). Table 1 shows results at rank 512 across 60M, 350M, and 1B, but doesn't sweep rank at those larger scales, so the rank-dependence at scale is unknown. The paper acknowledges the diminishing returns pattern and provides an interpretative hypothesis (high-frequency vs. low-frequency token learning), but doesn't operationalize this into a predictive tool.

**Mitigation status.** The paper recommends "the highest rank be selected until the perplexity reduction saturates" (Section 5.2) — essentially, do a sweep. This is mitigation in the sense that a procedure exists, but it's expensive (each point on the sweep is a full training run) and provides no guarantee that the chosen rank generalizes to other model sizes, datasets, or training durations. The paper suggests no future work on rank prediction or adaptive rank adjustment.

### 6.4 Single Dataset (C4), Single Model Architecture (LLaMA), Single Task (Language Modeling Pretraining)

**The assumption or constraint.** All experiments use the C4 dataset for pretraining, the LLaMA architecture (across scales), and evaluate on language modeling perplexity with a supplementary GLUE evaluation only at the 7B scale (Table 4). The paper does not test on any other dataset (e.g., The Pile, RedPajama), any other model architecture (e.g., GPT-style with parallel attention, mixture-of-experts, non-transformer architectures), or any other task (e.g., code generation, mathematical reasoning, vision transformers, multi-modal training).

The paper acknowledges this scope limitation implicitly by suggesting future work on "problems beyond language modeling" (Section 7, question 4), but does not discuss it as a limitation of the current evidence.

**The consequence.** Several key findings could be specific to the C4-LLaMA combination:

- **Gradient subspace structure.** The paper's core premise — that gradients during training lie in a low-dimensional subspace — is supported by prior work (Gur-Ari et al., 2018; Larsen et al., 2021) and by the fact that both GaLore and Online Subspace Descent work at all with low ranks. But the *effective rank* of the gradient subspace could vary substantially across datasets and architectures. C4 is a relatively clean, preprocessed web text corpus. Training on noisier data (CommonCrawl raw), multilingual data, or code-heavy data might exhibit a different gradient spectrum — potentially requiring higher ranks or different projection update strategies. The paper provides no evidence that the online PCA tracking is robust to such distributional shifts in the gradient structure.

- **LLaMA-specific properties.** LLaMA uses SwiGLU activations, pre-normalization (RMSNorm), and rotary position embeddings. The gradient dynamics of these components — particularly how gradient energy distributes across weight matrix dimensions — could differ from other architectures. For instance, architectures with parallel attention/FFN branches or with tied embeddings might concentrate gradient information differently, affecting both the optimal rank and the benefit of continuous vs. periodic projection updates.

- **Task-specific optimization difficulty.** Language modeling on C4 involves predicting the next token from a fixed vocabulary — a task with a well-defined loss landscape. Tasks like reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), adversarial training, or multi-task fine-tuning might present more challenging gradient landscapes where the projection update rule matters more. The paper's theoretical framework (Section 4) suggests the method should work for any smooth loss, but the empirical evidence is confined to the simplest case.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The 7B GLUE evaluation (Table 4) provides a weak signal of generalization beyond language modeling perplexity: Online Subspace Descent's advantage over GaLore (0.4951 vs. 0.4862 average) is positive but small. The scores are low (models trained for only 10K steps), so this doesn't strongly establish that the pretraining advantage transfers to downstream tasks at convergence. No other dataset, architecture, or task is tested.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges the scope via the future work question "Can this method be applied to problems beyond language modeling?" (Section 7), but this is forward-looking rather than a mitigation of the current evidence's limitations. A practitioner deploying Online Subspace Descent for, say, code model pretraining on The Stack would be extrapolating from C4-LLaMA results without direct evidence.

### 6.5 The Projection Matrix Update Mechanism Is Not Ablated Against Simpler Alternatives That Might Suffice

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's central algorithmic claim is that online PCA (Equation 6, optimized via a single gradient step per iteration) is a superior alternative to periodic SVD for updating the projection matrix. The evidence for this claim compares Online Subspace Descent (online PCA) against GaLore (periodic SVD, update gap T=200). But the design space of "cheaper-than-SVD projection updates" is large, and the paper tests essentially only one alternative (SVD at T=200) against the proposed method.

Several natural alternatives are not tested:
- **SVD at higher frequency**: What if GaLore used a smaller update gap — e.g., T=10 or T=50 instead of T=200? This would make SVD more expensive per-step but would provide a more direct test of whether the benefit comes from update frequency vs. the specific online PCA mechanism. If frequent SVD closed the perplexity gap, the bottleneck is staleness, not the SVD itself; if frequent SVD didn't help, the bottleneck is something about online PCA's continuous tracking.
- **Randomized SVD or frequent directions**: Randomized SVD algorithms (Halko et al., 2011) can approximate the top-k singular vectors much faster than exact SVD. Running randomized SVD more frequently could potentially match online PCA's speed while providing higher-quality subspace estimates. The paper's Figure 2 timing comparison is between `torch.svd` (exact, slow) and one online PCA step, not between online PCA and faster approximate SVD methods.
- **Running average of gradient outer products with periodic eigendecomposition**: One could maintain an exponential moving average of `$G_t G_t^\top$` (or its low-rank factorization) and periodically extract the top eigenvectors. This would incorporate gradient information across multiple batches like online PCA but would compute the subspace via a different mechanism. The memory cost and computational profile would differ from both online PCA and periodic SVD.

**The consequence.** It remains unclear whether online PCA is genuinely the *best* projection update mechanism among cheap alternatives, or simply one that works and is easy to implement. If a simpler mechanism (e.g., more frequent SVD using an efficient implementation, or a running covariance estimate) achieved similar performance, the conceptual contribution of online PCA would be weaker — it would be an implementation optimization rather than an algorithmic advance.

The theoretical framework (Section 4) suggests that *any* update rule satisfying the non-degeneracy condition should converge, implying that many projection update rules might work. This very generality weakens the case that online PCA specifically is necessary — it could be sufficient but not special. The paper's claim that online PCA is "inspired by our theoretical understanding" (Section 1) is true (the theory shows that cheap updates suffice, motivating the search for one), but the theory doesn't single out online PCA as the uniquely correct choice.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The only direct comparison of projection update mechanisms is online PCA vs. GaLore's periodic SVD at T=200. The optimizer sweep (Table 2; Table 6) varies the *optimizer* used for the online PCA step (AdamW, SGD, LION, Adafactor) but doesn't vary the *mechanism* by which the projection matrix incorporates gradient information (it's always online PCA). The paper shows that the `$P_t$` optimizer choice is not highly sensitive (SGD works, AdamW works slightly better), but doesn't test alternatives to the online PCA objective itself.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper presents online PCA as the proposed method and compares it against the prior state-of-the-art (GaLore's SVD). This is a standard evaluation strategy for introducing a new method, but a more thorough ablation of the design space would strengthen the claim that online PCA is the right mechanism, not just one that happens to outperform the default GaLore configuration.

### 6.6 Latency and Serialization Overheads Are Ignored; Only Total FLOPs and Wall-Clock Time Are Reported

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper measures compute efficiency via total wall-clock time (Table 3: 7.14 hours vs. 9.74 hours for 7B) and per-operation timing of isolated components (Figure 2: SVD vs. one online PCA backward step). The claim that online PCA "introduces only minimum overhead" and "can be executed in parallel with weight updates" (Section 3.2, Algorithm 1 remark) implies that the projection matrix update can be *hidden* completely behind the weight update computation, yielding zero additional wall-clock time.

This claim depends on a specific hardware assumption: that the GPU has sufficient parallel compute capacity to simultaneously execute the weight update and the projection matrix backward pass without contention. On a data-center GPU (A100) with large CUDA core counts and high memory bandwidth, this may hold. On consumer GPUs (the RTX 3090 is mentioned in Figure 2), on edge devices, or in distributed training settings with communication bottlenecks, the projection update may not be perfectly hideable.

More fundamentally, the paper does not discuss **latency** as distinct from throughput. Online Subspace Descent's weight update step (Algorithm 1, Step 3) requires:
1. Computing the full gradient `$G_t$` (unavoidable for any method).
2. Projecting: `$\hat{G}_t = P_t^\top G_t$` (a matrix multiply, `$n \times k$` times `$n \times m$` yielding `$k \times m$`).
3. Running the base optimizer on `$\hat{G}_t$` to update `$\hat{S}^t$` and compute `$\hat{\Delta}^t$`.
4. Projecting back: `$P_t \hat{\Delta}^t$` (another matrix multiply).
5. Computing `$\nabla_P \mathcal{L}_{G_t}(P_t)$` (forward + backward through Equation 6).
6. Running the projection optimizer on `$\nabla_P \mathcal{L}_{G_t}(P_t)$`.

Steps 5–6 can theoretically overlap with Steps 2–4 via CUDA stream parallelism. But in practice, PyTorch's default execution model is largely sequential, and achieving true overlap requires manual stream management. The paper provides no profiling data showing that overlap is achieved in the actual experiments, and the reported 1.36× speedup (Table 3) could be entirely due to GaLore's SVD being slow rather than to online PCA being effectively hidden. If the projection update adds even a small serialized latency, it could matter for latency-sensitive applications (interactive training monitoring, rapid experimentation cycles).

**The consequence.** In deployments where training latency per step matters — for instance, in reinforcement learning loops where new training data is generated online, or in hyperparameter search where many short runs must complete quickly — any serialized overhead from the projection update would directly impact iteration speed. The paper's "minimum overhead" claim is about total FLOPs and wall-clock time for throughput-oriented batch training, not about step latency.

Additionally, if the projection update cannot be fully hidden, the memory footprint calculation becomes more complex: maintaining separate CUDA streams for weight and projection updates requires additional memory for intermediate tensors that must be kept alive while both streams execute.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figure 2 provides timing for isolated operations (one SVD call, one online PCA backward pass) on representative tensor shapes, showing online PCA is much faster per operation. Table 3 provides end-to-end wall-clock time for 7B training, showing Online Subspace Descent is 1.36× faster than GaLore. No profiling traces, stream overlap measurements, or step-by-step timing breakdowns are provided. The claim of executability "in parallel" is stated in the text (Section 3.2) without experimental verification.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not discuss latency, does not provide evidence of stream overlap, and does not acknowledge serialization overhead as a potential concern. The focus is exclusively on throughput-oriented metrics (total wall time, per-operation cost). This is consistent with the paper's framing around pretraining (a throughput-dominated workload), but the omission limits the method's applicability to latency-sensitive training scenarios.
