Here are the takeaways from this Ryan Peterman interview (the host of The Peterman Post reflects on growing his newsletter from zero to 100,000 subscribers and what it taught him about writing, attention, and consistency):

**Committing to quantity to unlock quality**

When Ryan started writing two years ago, he had a theory: even though quality was his number one priority, he believed that "focusing on quantity was how I'd get there." So he forced himself to publish every single week—a brutal commitment that often meant staying up until 3:31am on Thursday nights to hit the Friday deadline. The results, he says, speak for themselves when you compare an early post against a recent one. The deliberate repetition wasn't just about output; it was a practice regimen. He also points to the first section of *On Writing Well* (called "Principles") as essential reading that improved his delivery regardless of style. The core advice: if you want to improve your writing, commit to a high-frequency cadence and treat it like deliberate practice.

**Topic selection matters more than polish**

After months of writing, Ryan felt confident in his delivery but realized that "how well you structure your message is just a small part of writing something people want to read." Far more important is what you write about. The analytics made this obvious—he put the same effort into polishing every post, yet some dramatically outperformed others. His post on LLM-assisted dev tooling, for instance, rode a hot topic to unusually high engagement. His approach to finding those topics has two prongs. First, **write about what you find interesting**: rather than chasing audience demand, he thinks about "what I wish I could have read earlier in my career"—which produced some of his best pieces on switching to management, scaling yourself, and onboarding. The career-story interviews (like those with Rahul and Evan) were inspired by similar stories that fired him up when he joined Meta. Second, **engage on social media**: LinkedIn and Twitter serve as both an idea source (observing what people engage with) and a testing ground where immediate feedback tells you what resonates.

**Managing attention is an exponential game**

The most impactful lesson of writing online was understanding attention as a fragile, line-by-line proposition. "People will only read the N+1th line if the Nth line interests them," so the first few lines are "exponentially more important than the rest." His formula for an intro: tell the reader why they should care right away, establish credibility with results (he mentions 100k readers in the first paragraph), and promise to condense a large effort into a brief, skimmable post. If he launched straight into the topic without making the reader care, "few people would read until the end." Understanding the audience isn't just a soft skill—it's the structural constraint around which all effective online writing is built.

**Substack recommendations as a network-effect flywheel**

Substack kept all his metrics from day one, so he can trace exactly where ~100,000 subscribers came from. The overwhelming driver was **Substack recommendations**, which accounted for ~69,000 subscribers—roughly 69% of total growth. Ryan was intentional about this: he realized that "piggybacking off the growth of others would create a powerful network effect." His tactic was to subscribe to other newsletters in his niche, reply to the welcome email, and ask to exchange recommendations with authors of a similar audience size. The second-largest channel was social media (~20% of total subs, split as ~15.5k from LinkedIn, ~4k from Twitter, and ~0.5k from Instagram/Threads). He describes platforms like LinkedIn and Twitter as "natural discovery engines" and notes that building a brand on social media has a **snowball effect**—the few accounts at the top grow easily because followers provide engagement and social proof, while most people at the bottom get almost nothing. Growth at the beginning is tough, but worth it if you invest enough to break out.

**Cross-promotions and the ByteByteGo outlier**

Newsletter collaborations and cross-promotions drove ~7,300 subscribers but turned out to be high-effort for mediocre returns. Ryan wrote guest articles for other publications expecting a meaningful subscriber bump, but "the results were underwhelming relative to their effort." The one exception was a collaboration with **ByteByteGo** (Alex Xu's massive engineering newsletter), which drove roughly 2,000 subscribers at a 0.2% conversion rate. His lesson: don't write fresh content for cross-promotion given the low conversion rate. But if someone wants to repost something you've already written, "that's a no-brainer." The remaining subscriber channels were referrals (~2.7k), Google searches (~1k), and Instagram/Threads (~0.5k).

**Why he chose not to monetize**

Ryan has thought about monetization extensively but never pulled the trigger, partly because his full-time job pays him enough that it wasn't a pressing need. He evaluated four paths and rejected each on principle or practicality. **Sponsorships and ads** felt like a conflict of interest between credibility and money, and he didn't want to degrade content quality by inserting ads into the writing (though he leaves the door open: "maybe one day I'll find a way"). **Selling courses** never appealed because "it felt like a cash grab that'd extract more value than it added," and he didn't think it would be interesting or fun. A **paid newsletter or community**—specifically a Q&A column where paid subscribers could submit questions for tailored responses—seemed decent (many readers were already asking for one-on-one mentorship), but he was "drowning in work" and couldn't add the commitment. **Writing a book** carries enormous time cost and uncertain payout: best-sellers can earn $1M+, but "most books flop." Given the risk and his already strapped schedule, he passed—though he might write one "for fun rather than the anticipated payout" someday.

**Breaking the weekly cadence**

Committing to weekly publishing was "a hell of an accountability mechanism"—the promise to readers gave him the motivation to stick it out through countless late Thursday nights. But recently he polled his audience, and the results were "overwhelmingly in favor of not caring when the newsletter arrives as long as the quality is high." So he's dropping the rigid Friday schedule and shifting toward "less frequent, more interesting posts." Part of the freed-up time will go to a passion project on his other Substack and the podcast he's been developing, where he knows he has "a lot of room for improvement in my speaking ability" and wants to reach a point of genuine confidence.
