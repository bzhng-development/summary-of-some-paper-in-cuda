# Cog-DRIFT: Exploration on Adaptively Reformulated Instances Enables Learning from Hard Reasoning Problems

**ArXiv:** [2604.04767](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.04767)

## 🎯 Pitch

Models trained with reinforcement learning hit a dead end when problems are too hard to ever solve—but simply rewording those problems as multiple-choice or fill-in-the-blank questions suddenly makes them learnable, and that learning transfers back to the original hard versions. Cog-DRIFT automatically creates these easier variants and sequences them into a curriculum, achieving +10% gains on previously impossible problems without any stronger teacher model.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **Cog-DRIFT**, a framework that reformulates hard reasoning problems—those yielding zero reward under standard reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards (RLVR)—into cognitively simpler variants such as multiple-choice and cloze formats that preserve the original answer while reducing the effective search space, then organizes these reformulations into an adaptive curriculum that progresses from easier to harder formats as the model improves. Evaluated on two models (Qwen3-4B-Instruct and Llama3.2-3B-Instruct) across six challenging benchmarks including BigMath-Hard, OmniMATH-Hard, AIME, and GPQA, Cog-DRIFT unlocks learning on problems with pass@64=0 that standard GRPO cannot solve, achieving absolute gains of +10.11% (Qwen) and +8.64% (Llama) on the originally unsolvable hard problems while improving held-out benchmark performance by an average of +4.72% (Qwen) and +3.23% (Llama) over the second-best baseline, establishing that task reformulation coupled with an instance-level curriculum enables models to acquire transferable reasoning capabilities from problems that were previously inaccessible—without requiring stronger teacher models or privileged information.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: LLMs Cannot Learn from Problems That Are Too Hard

The central gap this paper addresses is deceptively simple: **a model trained with reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards (RLVR) cannot learn from a problem if it never produces a correct solution to that problem.** This creates a fundamental chicken-and-egg problem. To improve on a task, the model needs reward signals — examples of correct behavior that can be reinforced. But if the task is so difficult that the model's current policy never accidentally produces a correct answer (the reward is always zero), then there is no signal to learn from. The policy gets zero reward on every rollout, all trajectories look equally bad, and the gradient provides no meaningful direction for improvement.

The paper formalizes this through the concept of `pass@k`: if `pass@64(x) = 0` — meaning that even after generating 64 independent solutions for prompt `x`, none of them match the ground-truth answer — then standard on-policy RL algorithms like GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization) or PPO will fail to make any progress on that prompt. This is not merely slow learning; it is **zero learning**. The model cannot climb the gradient because there is no gradient to climb.

This problem is not hypothetical or edge-case. The authors construct a training set by taking the hardest 20% of problems from BigMath (a large-scale math dataset with 251k samples), generating 64 solutions per problem with a Qwen3-4B-Instruct model, and keeping only those problems where all 64 generated solutions were incorrect. Even after filtering out malformed questions (incomplete statements, missing figures, incorrect ground-truth labels — see Appendix E for examples), this yields 958 genuinely hard but solvable problems that the model cannot touch under standard RLVR. The paper's Figure 1(A) illustrates this starkly: for hard open-ended questions with `pass@64=0`, accuracy is 0.0%. No baseline method — not even standard GRPO — can learn from these problems in their original format.

### Why This Gap Matters

The practical and theoretical implications of this gap are substantial:

**Diminishing returns in RL post-training.** As models like DeepSeek-R1 and Kimi K1.5 have demonstrated the power of RLVR for reasoning, a natural question is: what limits further improvement? The paper argues that a hard performance ceiling emerges when the training data contains problems that are genuinely beyond the model's current reach. Past a certain point, simply adding more RL training on the same distribution yields diminishing returns, because the hardest problems provide no signal. You can't learn what you can't even occasionally get right. This ceiling is structural, not just a matter of scaling compute — more rollouts won't help if `pass@64=0`, because the probability of a correct solution is so low that the expected number of rollouts to get one positive signal is impractically large.

**Missed learning opportunities.** Hard problems are disproportionately valuable for learning. They represent the frontier of the model's capability, containing reasoning patterns, problem-solving strategies, and conceptual combinations that are not needed for easier problems. If the model can learn from them, the acquired skills are likely to generalize beyond the specific training problems. Conversely, if hard problems remain unexploited, the model misses the most information-rich examples in the training set. The paper explicitly frames this in terms of the **Zone of Proximal Development** (Vygotsky, 1978): learning is maximized when tasks are neither too easy (already mastered, no new information) nor too hard (no signal, no learning), but instead lie just beyond current capabilities where they can be solved with appropriate scaffolding.

**Dependence on stronger models.** The dominant solution to this problem in prior work is to leverage a more capable model (or human expert) to generate correct solutions or provide hints, and then train the weaker model on those demonstrations — an approach the paper characterizes as requiring "privileged information." This is expensive, not always available (if you're working with the best model, there is no stronger teacher), and limits the autonomy of the learning process. The paper argues for a method that enables a model to bootstrap its own learning without external guidance — a self-improvement paradigm that can, in principle, scale indefinitely.

**The exploration problem in discrete spaces.** The difficulty of learning from hard problems under RLVR is fundamentally an exploration problem: the correct solution is somewhere in the exponentially large space of possible token sequences, but the model's current policy assigns it near-zero probability. Standard exploration strategies (temperature sampling, entropy bonuses) are insufficient when the target region is extremely sparse. This connects to broader challenges in reinforcement learning — exploration in large discrete action spaces — and a solution would have implications beyond LLM post-training.

### Prior Approaches and Their Limitations

The paper situates existing solutions to this problem along two axes: approaches that inject additional signal from a stronger source, and approaches that modify the problem to make it easier. Both have significant drawbacks.

**Expert guidance approaches.** One line of work uses a stronger model — either a more capable LLM or a human expert — to generate correct trajectories, prefixes, or hints that guide the learning model into regions of the solution space where reward exists. POPE (Qu et al., 2025) uses offline prefixes from a stronger model to guide exploration. Related approaches use expert-generated trajectories for distillation or SFT pretraining before RL. The limitations are clear: stronger models are not always available, soliciting expert guidance is expensive and doesn't scale, and — crucially — the hardest problems may be unsolvable even for experts, meaning no source of privileged information exists. As the paper puts it: "hard problems often represent the ceiling of capacity, so obtaining solutions from an expert model or human may not be feasible."

**Self-generated hint approaches.** A second line of work attempts to have the model itself reduce problem difficulty by generating hints, critiques, sub-questions, or partial solutions. NuRL (Chen et al., 2025a) provides abstract cues derived from answer explanations. Other methods (Li et al., 2025b; Wang et al., 2025) have the model break down problems or generate intermediate scaffolding. These approaches are attractive because they don't require an external teacher, but they are fundamentally limited: the hints are only as good as the model that generates them. If the model can't solve the original problem, its self-generated scaffolding may be incorrect, misleading, or simply a reformulation that doesn't reduce difficulty enough to enable a solution. Performance improvements in prior work using self-generated hints tend to be modest — the paper reports that the rate of hard problems becoming solvable ranges from 1–4% in prior methods (Chen et al., 2025a; Sundaram et al., 2026).

**Interleaved SFT and RL.** Ma et al. (2025) interleave supervised fine-tuning to learn from hard questions that RLVR cannot, essentially using SFT as a bootstrap before returning to RL. While effective, this still requires correct trajectories for the SFT phase, which brings us back to the chicken-and-egg problem: where do the correct trajectories come from?

**Curriculum learning from easy to hard.** Some work organizes training data by difficulty and learns from easier problems first (Shi et al., 2025; Parashar et al., 2025; Bae et al., 2026). This is sensible but doesn't address the core issue: if the hardest problems remain unsolvable even after mastering easier ones, a difficulty-ordered curriculum still cannot extract signal from them. What's needed is not just sequencing existing problems by difficulty, but *transforming* hard problems into easier variants that are learnable.

**Why existing approaches underperform.** The paper identifies a shared weakness across all these methods: they try to work with problems in their original open-ended format. Even with hints, prefixes, or guidance, the fundamental challenge is that the output space is unconstrained — the model must generate a complete, multi-step reasoning chain and a final answer, with correctness only verifiable at the end. This means the reward signal is sparse (only at the final token) and the search space is enormous. No amount of hinting fully solves this exploration problem if the model cannot spontaneously generate a correct trajectory.

### How Cog-DRIFT Positions Itself

The paper's key insight is a shift in perspective: rather than trying to make the model capable of solving hard open-ended problems directly (through hints, guidance, or expert trajectories), **transform the problems themselves into formats that are inherently easier.** This is inspired by educational psychology — specifically, the observation that problem format (multiple-choice vs. open-ended) dramatically affects cognitive load and success rates, even when the underlying knowledge required is identical. A student who fails an open-ended math problem might succeed when the same question is presented as multiple-choice with four options. The knowledge is the same; the search space is different.

This positions Cog-DRIFT as a fundamentally different category of solution:

- **It requires no stronger model.** Reformulations are generated by the same model that will be trained, using deterministic verification scripts to ensure correctness. The model reformulates its own training data, learns from the reformulated versions, and transfers back to the original format.
- **It addresses the exploration problem structurally.** Rather than trying to guide exploration within a vast output space, Cog-DRIFT reduces the effective output space. In a 4-choice MCQ, the model only needs to identify which of four options is correct — a fundamentally easier task than generating the answer from scratch, even though it requires the same underlying reasoning. In a cloze task, the model fills in masked portions of the answer, receiving partial credit and partial structure that constrain the search.
- **It explicitly leverages a difficulty spectrum.** The paper observes that reformulated problems form a natural ordering: `MCQ4 ≤ MCQ10 ≤ Cloze ≤ Open-ended`. This is not merely incidental — it provides the basis for a curriculum that adaptively progresses from discriminative (selection among options) to generative (free-form production) tasks.
- **It emphasizes transfer.** The goal is not to train models that are good at multiple-choice questions. The goal is to train models on MCQ and cloze formats, and then have those reasoning skills transfer back to open-ended problems — both the specific problems used in training and held-out benchmarks. This is why the paper's three criteria for an ideal reformulation (Takeaway 1) include both reduced difficulty and transferability. A reformulation that makes problems easier but doesn't transfer is useless.

The theoretical framing draws on **cognitive load theory** (Sweller, 1988): constrained formats reduce extraneous cognitive load (the effort of searching a vast output space) while preserving the germane load (the reasoning required to solve the problem). By training on cognitively simpler variants, the model can acquire the underlying reasoning skills without being bottlenecked by the search problem. Once those skills are acquired, they transfer to the more demanding open-ended setting.

A crucial empirical finding that motivates the entire framework appears in Figure 2: when the model is rejection fine-tuned (RFT) on reformulated problems, it not only performs better on those reformulations but **transfers back to the original open-ended problems**, achieving 11.1–18.9% accuracy on problems where it previously scored 0.0%. Moreover, models trained on reformulations maintain performance on held-out datasets like MATH500 (Figure 2c), showing that they are learning generalizable skills rather than format-specific shortcuts. This transfer is the linchpin that makes the reformulation approach viable.

The name itself encodes the approach: **Co**gnitive **D**emand **R**eduction via **I**ncremental **F**ormat **T**ransitions — progressively reducing cognitive load through format manipulation, then incrementally increasing it as the model improves, guided by an instance-level curriculum.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

Cog-DRIFT is a training framework that automatically transforms hard reasoning problems—problems a language model completely fails on—into easier formats like multiple-choice and fill-in-the-blank, trains on those easier versions using reinforcement learning, and systematically ramps the difficulty back up as the model improves, so that skills learned on the simplified tasks transfer to the original hard problems and to entirely new problems the model has never seen. The core problem it solves is that standard reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards (RLVR) provides **zero learning signal** on problems where the model never produces a correct answer—Cog-DRIFT breaks this deadlock not by making the model smarter before training, but by making the problems easier until the model can solve them, then progressively restoring the difficulty.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The system has five major components connected in a training loop:

1. **Problem Reformulation Engine** — given a hard open-ended problem and its ground-truth answer, uses a prompted LLM (the same model being trained) to generate three cognitively simpler variants: a 4-choice multiple-choice question, a 10-choice multiple-choice question, and a cloze (fill-in-the-blank) question. All variants preserve the original answer and are verified by deterministic scripts for correctness.

2. **Difficulty-Annotated Training Pool** — stores each hard problem along with its three reformulated variants, all sharing the same ground-truth answer `y_i`. Each training instance is associated with a difficulty level `d_i ∈ {1, 2, 3, 4}` tracking which format is currently being used: level 1 = 4-choice MCQ, level 2 = 10-choice MCQ, level 3 = cloze, level 4 = open-ended (the original hard format).

3. **Instance-Level Curriculum Controller** — during training, every problem generates `m` responses at its current difficulty level; if the empirical accuracy `â_i` (fraction of those `m` responses that are correct) reaches or exceeds a threshold `τ = 0.5`, the controller promotes that specific instance to the next higher difficulty level. This operates per-instance (not globally), so different problems advance through the curriculum at different rates.

4. **GRPO Training Loop** — the core learning algorithm. For each problem at its current difficulty level, `m` responses are sampled from the model, rewards are computed (combining correctness and format compliance), advantages are normalized within each group, and the model parameters are updated via the GRPO objective to increase the probability of relatively better responses.

5. **Unified Evaluation Protocol** — at test time, all problems are evaluated in their original open-ended format, regardless of which format was used during training. This tests whether skills acquired on simplified formats genuinely transfer back.

Information flows as follows: A hard problem enters → the Reformulation Engine creates three simpler variants → all variants plus the original are stored in the pool with initial difficulty `d_i = 1` → during training, the Curriculum Controller selects the current format for each instance → the model generates responses at that format → rewards are computed and GRPO updates the model → the Curriculum Controller checks accuracy and promotes instances that cross the threshold → the cycle repeats, with instances naturally migrating toward harder formats as the model improves → at evaluation, all problems revert to open-ended format to measure transfer.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the problem formalization and the definition of hard problems via `pass@k`, because this precisely defines the failure mode Cog-DRIFT addresses and establishes why standard RLVR cannot learn in this regime.
- **Second**, the reformulation function `T` and the three criteria for an ideal reformulation (Takeaway 1), because these criteria are the theoretical foundation that justifies why certain formats are chosen and how they are verified.
- **Third**, the mechanics of each reformulation format (4-choice MCQ, 10-choice MCQ, cloze) and the deterministic verification procedure, because understanding exactly what the model sees during training is essential to understanding why the approach works.
- **Fourth**, the instance-level curriculum—how difficulty levels are defined, how promotion decisions are made, and why a per-instance adaptive schedule is used rather than a static curriculum—because this is the mechanism that achieves sample efficiency and prevents plateauing.
- **Fifth**, the GRPO training objective and reward function, because this is the learning algorithm that operates on reformulated problems, and it has specific properties (group-relative advantage normalization) that interact with the reformulation strategy.
- **Sixth**, the data quality filtering pipeline, because the naive use of `pass@64=0` to identify hard problems introduces selection bias (including genuinely unsolvable problems) that must be addressed for training to succeed.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **methods and empirical analysis paper** whose core idea is that cognitively simplifying hard problems through format reformulation—coupled with an adaptive, instance-level curriculum—enables language models to learn from problems that lie completely outside their current capability under standard RLVR, with the acquired reasoning skills transferring back to the original hard format and generalizing to held-out benchmarks.

#### Hard Problem Definition and Why Standard RLVR Fails

The paper begins with a precise operational definition of what makes a problem "too hard" for a model to learn from, because the entire framework depends on correctly identifying these problems.

**The `pass@k` metric.** Let `M_θ` denote a language model with parameters `θ`, and let `D = {(x_i, y_i)}^N_{i=1}` be a dataset of open-ended problems where `x_i` is a problem prompt and `y_i` is the exact ground-truth answer. The model generates `k` independent samples `{ŷ_i^(j)}^k_{j=1}` for prompt `x_i`. The pass@k metric is defined as:

$$\text{pass@k}(x_i) = \mathbb{I}\left[\exists j \in \{1, \dots, k\} \text{ such that } \hat{y}_i^{(j)} = y_i\right]$$

where `x_i` is a specific problem prompt, `k` is the number of independent sampled solutions, `ŷ_i^(j)` is the `j`-th sampled solution, `y_i` is the ground-truth answer, and `𝕀[·]` is the indicator function returning 1 if the condition holds and 0 otherwise.

**What it computes:** for a single problem, pass@k checks whether at least one of the `k` sampled solutions from the model exactly matches the ground-truth answer. If yes, the problem is "solvable" by the model under `k` attempts; if no, the model has never—in `k` independent tries—produced the correct answer for that problem. This is not a measure of average accuracy; it is a binary indicator of whether the correct answer exists anywhere in the model's output distribution for that prompt with sufficient probability mass to appear in `k` samples.

**Why this form:** pass@k captures the exploration problem directly. High pass@1 means the model reliably produces the correct answer on its first try. Low pass@1 but high pass@k means the correct answer exists in the distribution but is unlikely—the model knows the answer but doesn't consistently produce it. pass@k = 0 means the correct answer is, for practical purposes, unreachable—the probability mass assigned to the correct answer is so low that even `k` samples fail to find it. This distinguishes between problems where the model has latent knowledge (low pass@1, high pass@k) and problems where the model genuinely lacks the capability (pass@k = 0 even for large `k`).

**The hard problem set.** The paper defines the set of hard problems as:

$$D_{\text{hard}} = \{(x_i, y_i) \in D \mid \text{pass@64}(x_i) = 0\}$$

where `D` is the full training dataset, `x_i` is a problem prompt, `y_i` is the ground-truth answer, and `pass@64(x_i) = 0` means that across 64 independent samples, none matched the correct answer. The choice of `k = 64` is motivated practically: during RL training with GRPO, the typical number of rollouts per problem per update is small (the paper uses 8). If the model cannot produce a correct answer in 64 attempts, the probability of getting a correct answer in 8 is negligible, meaning essentially all rollouts will receive zero reward.

**Why standard RLVR fails on `D_hard`.** The paper explains the failure mechanism concretely. In algorithms like GRPO, the model samples a group of `m` responses for each prompt, computes a reward for each, normalizes rewards within the group to produce advantages, and updates the policy to increase the probability of responses with positive advantage. When `pass@64(x_i) = 0`, the probability of sampling even a single correct trajectory in a group of `m` responses is extremely low. Consequently, all `m` responses receive zero reward, all advantages are zero (or near-zero, depending on normalization), and the gradient provides no information about which responses are better or worse. The policy receives no signal to improve. The paper characterizes this as "zero variance in the advantage estimates," which prevents meaningful gradient updates because no trajectory is preferred over others (Qu et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2025).

This is not just a theoretical concern. The paper demonstrates empirically in Section 6 (Table 1) that directly applying GRPO on `D_hard` yields `0.0%` accuracy on BigMath-Hard for Llama—complete failure to learn. For Qwen, there is a `+6.37%` gain, but the paper attributes this to "spurious reward effects" specific to Qwen (Shao et al., 2025) and notes that GRPO-trained Qwen actually degrades on held-out benchmarks, suggesting that any learning is non-transferable overfitting rather than genuine skill acquisition.

**The computational waste argument.** Beyond the gradient issue, the paper notes that training on `D_hard` wastes computational effort: every rollout is guaranteed to be incorrect (with very high probability), so the model spends compute generating reasoning chains that have "no hope of being correct." This compounds the problem—not only does the model not improve, but the resources spent trying are entirely unproductive.

#### The Reformulation Function `T` and the Three Criteria for Ideal Reformulations

The paper's central mechanism is a **task reformulation function** `T` that transforms a hard open-ended problem into a cognitively simpler variant while preserving the correctness conditions.

**Definition of the reformulation function:**

$$T: (x, y) \rightarrow (x', y)$$

where `x` is the original open-ended problem prompt, `y` is the ground-truth answer, and `x'` is the reformulated problem prompt that presents the same underlying question in a structurally simpler format while keeping the same correct answer `y`. The output is the pair `(x', y)` where the answer is unchanged but the question format has been modified.

**What it computes:** given a hard problem that the model cannot solve in its original form, `T` produces a new version of the problem—such as a multiple-choice question with four options or a fill-in-the-blank question with a partially masked answer—that requires the same underlying knowledge and reasoning but constrains the output space, making the problem empirically easier for the model. The answer `y` is invariant; only the prompt `x'` changes.

**Why this form:** the invariance of `y` is the critical property. Because the answer doesn't change, the reformulated problem can still be scored with the same deterministic verifier used for the original problem—no new reward model or evaluation infrastructure is needed. The model must still produce the exact answer value (e.g., `21`, not just the option letter `B`), so the learning signal is grounded in the same correctness criterion as the original task. This design choice is explicitly motivated by the paper's **Takeaway 1**, which specifies three criteria for an ideal reformulation:

**Takeaway 1: Three Criteria for an Ideal Reformulation**

1. **Answer preservation:** the reformulated problem must have the same correct answer as the original. This ensures that the verifier (which checks against the ground-truth) remains valid without modification.

2. **Reduced difficulty:** the reformulated problem should be empirically easier for the model—the model should achieve higher accuracy on the reformulated version than on the original. This is what makes the reformulation useful; if the problem isn't easier, there's no learning signal to extract.

3. **Transferability:** learning from the reformulated problem should improve performance on the original problem. This is the essential property that distinguishes useful reformulations from mere simplification—the skills acquired must transfer back, not remain locked in the easier format.

These criteria collectively ensure that reformulations are not just easier problems, but easier problems that teach the same underlying skills. The paper explicitly validates all three criteria for the chosen formats in Section 3.1 and Section 3.2, as shown in Figure 2.

**Reformulation method.** The reformulations are generated by prompting an LLM—specifically, the same Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 model that will be trained—with format-specific instructions. The prompts are provided in Appendix C. For example, to generate a 4-choice MCQ, the model receives the original question and correct answer, and is instructed to create four options (A, B, C, D) where one is the correct answer and the other three are plausible distractors, with the correct answer randomly placed among the options. The model must output in a structured format with the question, the four options labeled A through D, and a final line indicating which letter is correct. For cloze reformulations, the model receives the original question and correct answer, and is instructed to produce a masked version of the answer with approximately 50–80% of digits replaced by underscores, while preserving LaTeX formatting and keeping at least one digit visible.

**Why self-generation instead of human authoring:** using the same model to generate reformulations eliminates the need for external annotation or stronger models. The reformulations are verified deterministically (see next subsection), so even if the model occasionally produces malformed outputs, those are caught and filtered. This makes the approach fully self-contained—the model bootstraps its own learning trajectory without any privileged information.

#### The Three Reformulation Formats and Deterministic Verification

The paper uses three specific reformulation formats, each occupying a different point on the spectrum from discriminative (selection/recognition) to generative (production/recall). The formats are, in order of increasing difficulty:

**Format 1: 4-Choice Multiple-Choice Question (Level `d_i = 1`, easiest).** The original open-ended question is presented unchanged, followed by four answer options labeled A, B, C, and D. One option is the correct answer; the other three are plausible but incorrect distractors. The correct answer is randomly placed among the options. Example (from Example 1 in the paper): the question "What is the largest number of cells in a 9×9 board that can be cut along both diagonals without the board falling into pieces?" is followed by "A. 18 B. 21 C. 24 D. 15", with the correct answer being 21. Crucially, the model is required to output the full answer value (`21`), not the option letter (`B`). This is a deliberate design choice: outputting the option letter would teach the model a discriminative shortcut (pattern-matching the correct option) without requiring it to internalize the answer value. By requiring the full answer, the model must extract the correct numerical value from the options, which better supports transfer back to open-ended answering.

**Format 2: 10-Choice Multiple-Choice Question (Level `d_i = 2`, intermediate).** Identical structure to the 4-choice format, but with ten options labeled A through J. One option is correct; nine are plausible distractors. The larger option set makes the problem harder than the 4-choice version because: (a) random guessing drops from 25% to 10% expected accuracy, (b) the larger distractor set requires finer discrimination between similar-looking values, and (c) the model must process more information to identify the correct answer. Example (from Appendix D): the same board-cutting question with options "A. 4 B. 6 C. 7 D. 9 E. 12 F. 15 G. 18 H. 21 I. 24 J. 27".

**Format 3: Cloze (Fill-in-the-Blank), Level `d_i = 3`, intermediate-generative.** The original question is presented, followed by a partially masked version of the answer, and the instruction "Fill the blank by giving the full answer." For example (from Appendix D), if the answer is `21`, the prompt includes "The answer should look like: 2_. Fill the blank by giving the full answer." The model must produce the complete answer. The masking follows specific rules (Appendix C prompt): approximately 50–80% of digits are replaced by underscores, at least one digit must remain visible, LaTeX formatting is preserved (e.g., `\frac{1}{2}` might be masked as `\frac{1}{\_}`), and only numbers are masked (not letters or LaTeX symbols). The cloze format is more generative than MCQ because the model must produce the answer rather than select it, but less generative than open-ended because the partial mask provides structure and constrains the output space.

**Format 4: Open-Ended Question (Level `d_i = 4`, hardest, original format).** This is the original hard problem with no structural simplification—the model must generate the complete reasoning chain and final answer without any scaffolding. This is the target format that training ultimately aims to master.

**Deterministic verification of reformulations.** After generating each reformulation, the paper applies automated verification scripts to ensure correctness and validity. These checks are critical because an incorrectly reformulated problem (wrong answer, ambiguous options, incomplete mask) would provide misleading training signal.

For MCQ formats, the verification script checks two conditions: (1) the gold answer appears among the options, ensuring the correct answer is actually present; (2) the gold answer is uniquely correct, meaning no other option corresponds to the same answer. This second check prevents degenerate cases where, for example, two options both equal the correct answer, making the question ambiguous.

For cloze format, the verification script checks two conditions: (1) the masked span is non-trivial, meaning the revealed portion is not equivalent to the full gold answer—if the mask reveals too much, the problem becomes trivially easy (the paper gives the example that masking `101` as `1_0` would be a failure case because the pattern is too obvious); (2) each revealed digit is consistent with the gold answer, ensuring the mask doesn't inadvertently point to a wrong answer.

Any reformulation that fails verification is discarded; only verified reformulations enter the training pool. The paper does not report the rejection rate, but the filtering is an important quality-control step that prevents noisy or incorrect reformulations from contaminating training.

**Empirical difficulty ordering.** Figure 2(a) provides the empirical validation that the formats form a consistent difficulty ordering. On problems where `pass@64 = 0` on the original open-ended format (0.0% accuracy), the zero-shot accuracy on reformulated formats is: 4-choice MCQ = 33.8%, 10-choice MCQ = 26.1%, cloze = 21.2%. This establishes the ordering `MCQ4 ≤ MCQ10 ≤ Cloze ≤ Open-ended` and confirms that all reformulations satisfy Criterion 2 (reduced difficulty).

**Transfer validation.** Figure 2(b) shows that after rejection fine-tuning (RFT)—supervised fine-tuning on correct trajectories extracted from each reformulated format—the model's performance on the original open-ended problems (which were 0.0% before training) rises to: 11.1% for 4-choice training, 15.5% for 10-choice training, 18.9% for cloze training. This confirms Criterion 3 (transferability) for all formats and reveals an interesting pattern: more generative formats transfer better back to open-ended problems. The paper hypothesizes that this is because generative tasks teach the model to avoid shortcuts that discriminative tasks enable (e.g., pattern-matching options without full reasoning). Figure 2(c) confirms that RFT-trained models maintain robust performance on the held-out MATH500 benchmark (78.8% base → 74.4–77.2% across formats), showing that the learned skills generalize rather than representing format-specific memorization.

#### The Instance-Level Curriculum

The curriculum is the mechanism that sequences the model's exposure to difficulty levels, ensuring that training remains within the model's Zone of Proximal Development as it improves. The key innovation is that the curriculum operates **per-instance** rather than globally—each problem advances independently based on the model's demonstrated competence on that specific problem.

**Difficulty level assignment.** Each training example `(x_i, y_i)` is associated with a difficulty level `d_i ∈ {1, 2, 3, 4}` that determines which format of the problem the model sees during training. All instances are initialized to `d_i = 1` (4-choice MCQ), the easiest format. The four levels correspond to:

- `d_i = 1`: 4-choice MCQ (`x_i^(1)`)
- `d_i = 2`: 10-choice MCQ (`x_i^(2)`)
- `d_i = 3`: Cloze (`x_i^(3)`)
- `d_i = 4`: Open-ended (`x_i^(4)`)

All four variants `{x_i^(1), x_i^(2), x_i^(3), x_i^(4)}` for each problem are pre-computed and stored, so transitioning between levels is simply a matter of selecting which prompt to present.

**Empirical accuracy estimation.** During training, for each instance at its current difficulty level `d_i`, the model generates `m` responses (where `m = 8`, the GRPO rollout number): `{ŷ_i^(j)}^m_{j=1} ∼ M_θ(· | x_i^(d_i))`. The empirical accuracy at level `d_i` is estimated as:

$$\hat{a}_i = \frac{1}{m} \sum_{j=1}^m \mathbb{I}\left[\hat{y}_i^{(j)} = y_i\right]$$

where `â_i` is the estimated accuracy for instance `i`, `m` is the number of sampled responses (8), `ŷ_i^(j)` is the `j`-th sampled response, `y_i` is the ground-truth answer, and `𝕀[·]` is the indicator function.

**What it computes:** the fraction of the `m` rollouts for problem `i` at its current difficulty level that produce the correct answer. This is a noisy but unbiased estimate of the model's current success rate on that specific problem in its current format. Since `m = 8`, the possible values are discrete: 0/8, 1/8, 2/8, ..., 8/8.

**Why this form:** using the GRPO rollouts themselves for accuracy estimation avoids extra computation—the data is already being generated for the policy update. A different approach would be to run separate evaluation rollouts, but that would increase computational cost. The trade-off is that `m = 8` provides a coarse estimate, but the paper shows this is sufficient for effective curriculum progression.

**Promotion rule.** An instance is promoted to the next difficulty level when its empirical accuracy reaches or exceeds a threshold `τ`:

$$\text{if } \hat{a}_i \geq \tau, \text{ then } d_i \leftarrow \min(d_i + 1, 4)$$

where `τ = 0.5` is the promotion threshold and `d_i` is capped at 4 (the maximum difficulty). The threshold `τ = 0.5` means the model must get at least 4 out of 8 rollouts correct on average before advancing. This value follows past work on adaptive curricula (Chen et al., 2025b; Foster et al., 2025).

**What it computes:** a binary decision for each instance at each training step: either the model is competent enough at the current format (accuracy ≥ 50%) and should advance to a harder format, or it is not yet competent and should continue practicing at the current level.

**Why this form:** the threshold `τ = 0.5` balances two competing concerns. If the threshold were too low (e.g., 0.1), the model would advance prematurely before genuinely mastering the current format, making the next level too difficult and potentially causing regression. If the threshold were too high (e.g., 0.9), the model would over-train on easy formats, wasting compute and delaying exposure to harder formats that provide more transferable learning. The value 0.5 is a natural midpoint: the model is more likely than not to succeed, indicating reasonable mastery without requiring perfection.

**Why instance-level rather than global.** A global curriculum would advance all problems simultaneously—for example, training on 4-choice MCQ for `N` steps, then switching all problems to 10-choice MCQ. The paper argues this is suboptimal because different problems are learned at different rates. Some problems may be easy enough that the model masters the 4-choice format quickly and should advance early; others may remain challenging even in the simplest format and need extended practice. An instance-level curriculum adapts to this heterogeneity: each problem advances independently based on the model's demonstrated competence on that specific problem. This is what the paper means by "adaptively reallocates samples"—compute is spent where it is most informative, with easy problems moving to harder formats (freeing up capacity) while stubborn problems receive more practice at easier levels.

**Curriculum dynamics visualized.** Figure 4 (left panel) shows how the curriculum evolves over 1,000 training steps. The proportion of 4-choice MCQ (easiest, blue) steadily decreases from 100% initially to approximately 20% by step 1,000, as instances are promoted to harder formats. The proportions of 10-choice MCQ (orange) and cloze (green) first increase as instances are promoted from level 1, then decrease as those instances are further promoted to open-ended. The proportion of open-ended (red, hardest) steadily increases, reaching approximately 40% by step 1,000. This dynamic reallocation is the "adaptive progression" referenced in the paper's title. In contrast, the right panel of Figure 4 shows a static uniform mixture (25% each format) where proportions never change, and test accuracy plateaus.

**Sample efficiency argument.** The paper argues that the curriculum improves sample efficiency because reformulated problems largely "share the same underlying information, differing primarily in structure and cognitive load." Training on all four formats simultaneously (as in the static mixture) effectively quadruples the dataset size without adding new information, wasting compute on redundant practice. The curriculum avoids this by advancing instances out of formats they've already mastered, concentrating training on the difficulty level where each instance provides the most informative learning signal.

#### GRPO Training Objective and Reward Function

The learning algorithm operating on the curriculum-structured data is Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), a variant of policy gradient methods designed for LLM fine-tuning with verifiable rewards.

**GRPO objective.** For each input `x_i^(d_i)` (the problem at its current difficulty level), the model samples a group of `m` responses and computes rewards for each sample. The GRPO objective is:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{i,j} \left[ \tilde{r}_i^{(j)} \log M_\theta\left(\hat{y}_i^{(j)} \mid x_i^{(d_i)}\right) \right]$$

where `i` indexes over training instances, `j` indexes over the `m` responses within a group, `M_θ(ŷ_i^(j) | x_i^(d_i))` is the model's probability of generating response `ŷ_i^(j)` given the reformulated prompt, and `r̃_i^(j)` is the **normalized reward** (advantage) for that response within its group.

**What it computes:** the objective encourages the model to increase the log-probability of responses that received higher-than-average reward within their group, and decrease the log-probability of responses that received lower-than-average reward. The key phrase is "within their group"—advantages are computed relative to the other `m-1` responses for the same prompt, not relative to a global baseline.

**Why this form:** group-relative normalization is what distinguishes GRPO from standard policy gradient methods. In standard REINFORCE with a baseline, the advantage is `r - b` where `b` is typically a learned value function or an average over all prompts. In GRPO, the advantage is `r - mean(r_group)`, so each group is self-normalizing. This has two important properties for the Cog-DRIFT setting: (1) it automatically adapts to varying reward scales across different formats—a correct answer on 4-choice MCQ and a correct answer on open-ended both produce advantage signals relative to their own groups, so the optimizer doesn't need to compare absolute rewards across formats; (2) it preserves gradient signal even when all rewards in a group are low—the best (least bad) response still gets positive advantage, encouraging the model to move toward it, which is crucial in the early stages when most rollouts are incorrect.

**Reward function.** The reward for each response combines two components:

$$r_i^{(j)} = r_{\text{correct}}\left(\hat{y}_i^{(j)}, y_i\right) + r_{\text{format}}\left(\hat{y}_i^{(j)}\right)$$

where `r_correct ∈ [0, 1]` indicates whether the predicted answer matches the ground truth, and `r_format ∈ [0, 0.2]` measures whether the output follows the required format.

**What it computes:**
- `r_correct(ŷ_i^(j), y_i)` is 1.0 if the extracted final answer from the model's response matches the ground-truth answer `y_i` (using the Math-Verify library for exact equivalence checking), and 0.0 otherwise. This is the primary signal.
- `r_format(ŷ_i^(j))` is a small bonus (up to 0.2) for producing the answer within `\boxed{}` delimiters, following the convention from DeepSeek-R1 (Guo et al., 2025). This encourages structured output that is easy to parse.

The total reward for a correct, well-formatted answer is 1.2; for a correct but badly formatted answer, it would be approximately 1.0; for an incorrect answer, it is at most 0.2 (if properly formatted but wrong) and at minimum 0.0 (if both wrong and malformed).

**Why this form:** separating correctness and format into additive components with a small format bonus (0.2 vs. 1.0) ensures that correctness dominates the optimization. The format bonus is just large enough to incentivize proper output structure without creating a situation where the model learns to output `\boxed{}` with a random answer to collect the format reward while ignoring correctness. The 0.2 magnitude is a design choice that the paper inherits from prior work (Guo et al., 2025) without further ablation.

**Reward normalization within groups.** After computing raw rewards for all `m` responses in a group, GRPO normalizes them to produce advantages `r̃_i^(j)`. The exact normalization formula is not specified in the paper but follows the standard GRPO procedure (Shao et al., 2024): advantages are computed as `r̃ = (r - μ_group) / σ_group`, where `μ_group` and `σ_group` are the mean and standard deviation of rewards within the group of `m` responses. This normalization ensures that: (a) roughly half the responses have positive advantage (those above the group mean), providing a balanced gradient signal; (b) the magnitude of updates is controlled by the within-group variance, preventing explosive gradients when rewards are sparse; (c) the optimizer is invariant to additive shifts in the reward scale (e.g., all rewards increasing by a constant won't change the advantage).

**Hyperparameters for GRPO.** The paper reports (Appendix A, Table 4) the following key hyperparameters used for all GRPO-based methods including Cog-DRIFT:
- Learning rate: `1 × 10^(-6)`
- Rollout temperature: `1.0` (high temperature for exploration during training)
- Number of rollouts per group `m`: `8`
- Clip ratio low: `0.2`, clip ratio high: `0.28` (these control the PPO-style clipping of policy updates, with values following the DAPO configuration from Yu et al., 2025)
- KL coefficient: `0.01` (weight on a KL divergence penalty between the current policy and a reference policy, preventing the model from diverging too far from its initial behavior)
- Training batch size: `8` (number of prompts processed in parallel per gradient step, each with `m = 8` rollouts, so 64 total generations per step)
- Maximum prompt length: `1024` tokens
- Maximum response length: `8192` tokens (generous to accommodate long reasoning chains)
- Inference temperature: `0.7` (lower than training temperature for more focused sampling at evaluation time)

#### Data Quality Filtering Pipeline

An important practical detail is that naively using `pass@64 = 0` to identify hard problems introduces selection bias—the resulting dataset contains not only problems that are genuinely hard for the model, but also problems that are fundamentally unsolvable due to errors in the data.

**The selection bias problem.** The paper observes (Section 6, Table 3 discussion) that the initial `D_hard` contains a substantial number of flawed samples, including:
- **Incomplete or unanswerable questions:** problems missing critical information (e.g., referring to a figure that doesn't exist in the text, as shown in Example 8 of Appendix E).
- **Incorrect ground-truth labels:** problems where the listed answer is wrong (e.g., Example 6 in Appendix E: a problem about cube roots and square roots where the gold answer is listed as `0` but should be `0 or 1`).
- **Ambiguous or ill-posed questions:** problems that are too vague to have a single correct answer.

These problems would have `pass@64 = 0` regardless of model capability because no model can solve them—the answer is either wrong, ambiguous, or the problem lacks necessary information. Including them in training would add noise and potentially train the model on corrupted signals.

**Two filtering strategies evaluated.** The paper compares two approaches to filtering, both using GPT-5.4 (a strong external model) as a judge, but importantly not using its reasoning traces for distillation—only its judgments about data quality:

1. **Binary Filter:** GPT-5.4 is prompted to determine whether a question is complete and answerable. Only samples labeled as "solvable" are retained. The model does not need to produce a correct solution, only judge whether the question is well-formed.

2. **Silver Answer Filter:** GPT-5.4 generates three independent solutions for each problem. A problem is retained only if the ground-truth answer agrees with the majority vote of GPT-5.4's three answers. This is a stricter filter because it requires both that the question is well-formed AND that a strong model consistently produces the labeled answer.

**Impact on dataset size.** Table 3 shows the dramatic effect of filtering:
- Original `D_hard` (unfiltered): `8,922` problems
- Binary Filter: `5,922` problems (33% reduction)
- Silver Answer Filter: `958` problems (89% reduction)

The Silver Answer Filter is extremely selective, removing nearly 90% of the initial dataset. This suggests that most of the initial `D_hard` consists not of genuinely hard but solvable problems, but of data errors and ill-posed questions.

**Impact on model performance.** The paper evaluates RFT-trained models (using cloze reformulations) on MATH500 and GPQA under each filtering strategy. For Qwen, performance is relatively insensitive to data quality: MATH500 accuracy ranges from 81.6% to 82.2% across filtering strategies, and GPQA from 33.1% to 36.0%. The paper attributes this to Qwen's unique characteristics—it "can even improve with random reward" (Shao et al., 2025), suggesting it is unusually robust to noise. For Llama, the effect is substantial: MATH500 accuracy ranges from 24.2% (unfiltered) to 32.2% (Silver Answer Filter), and GPQA from 20.7% to 23.3%. Llama is more sensitive to data quality, showing consistent improvement with cleaner data.

**Default choice.** The paper applies the Silver Answer Filter by default for all main experiments, using the filtered set of 958 hard problems. This choice prioritizes data quality over quantity, trading a larger (but noisier) dataset for a smaller (but cleaner) one.

**Why this matters for the overall approach.** The data filtering pipeline reveals an important subtlety: the `pass@64 = 0` criterion is necessary but not sufficient for identifying problems that are "hard but learnable." Without filtering, the majority of identified problems are actually unlearnable for any model because they contain errors. The filtering step (using a strong external model as a judge) is the one place where Cog-DRIFT relies on external capability—not for generating solutions or providing hints, but for identifying which problems are well-formed enough to be worth learning from. The paper frames this as a data-cleaning step rather than a learning signal, and notes that GPT-5.4's reasoning traces are not used for distillation.

#### Design Choices and Their Justifications

**Why reformulate rather than hint or guide.** The paper argues that reformulation is structurally different from prior approaches because it changes the exploration problem rather than trying to solve it within the original space. Hints, prefixes, and guidance all operate within the open-ended output space—they nudge the model toward correct solutions but don't reduce the fundamental difficulty of searching a vast space. Reformulation constrains the output space itself, making the search problem inherently easier. This is why the paper can achieve 18.9% of hard problems becoming solvable (Figure 2b) compared to the 1–4% range reported by prior hint-based methods.

**Why multiple formats rather than a single best format.** Table 2 shows that training on a mixture of reformulations (e.g., 4-choice + cloze) outperforms training on any single format, even though cloze alone transfers better than 4-choice alone. The paper argues that diversity in problem representation is critical—exposure to multiple formats prevents the model from learning format-specific shortcuts and encourages it to extract the underlying reasoning that is invariant across formats. This is analogous to data augmentation in computer vision: training on varied views of the same underlying concept improves generalization.

**Why an instance-level curriculum rather than a static schedule.** Figure 4 directly compares adaptive progression (left) with uniform mixing (right). The curriculum continues to improve test accuracy over time, while the static mixture plateaus. The paper attributes this to sample efficiency: a static mixture wastes compute on problems and formats that the model has already mastered, while the curriculum reallocates those samples to formats and problems where learning is still occurring. The per-instance nature of the curriculum ensures that this reallocation is fine-grained—a problem that the model finds easy in 4-choice format advances quickly, while a stubborn problem receives extended practice at that level before advancing.

**Why the model must output the full answer, not the option letter.** For MCQ formats, the model could be trained to output the option letter (e.g., "B") rather than the answer value (e.g., "21"). The paper chooses the latter because outputting the full answer forces the model to extract the numerical value from the options, which better supports transfer to open-ended answering where there are no options. If the model learned to output "B", it would be learning a discriminative shortcut—mapping patterns in the question to option letters—rather than learning to compute the answer value. The full-answer requirement ensures that the learning signal is grounded in the same output format as the target task.

**Why the Silver Answer Filter over the Binary Filter.** Although both filters improve performance for Llama (Table 3), the Silver Answer Filter is more conservative and produces a smaller but higher-quality dataset. The paper chooses it as the default because Llama's sensitivity to data quality means that the cleaner dataset produces substantially better results, and the smaller size (958 vs. 5,922 problems) is still sufficient for effective training. The trade-off—quality over quantity—is justified by the empirical results showing that training on 958 clean problems outperforms training on 8,922 noisy ones for the more sensitive model.

**Why the curriculum promotes at `â_i ≥ 0.5` rather than continuous difficulty.** The binary promotion rule (advance when accuracy ≥ 50%) is simpler than a continuous difficulty schedule (e.g., gradually increasing difficulty based on a continuous accuracy score). The paper argues that this discrete, threshold-based approach is sufficient because each format represents a qualitatively different task with different cognitive demands—moving from 4-choice MCQ to 10-choice MCQ is a meaningful jump, not an infinitesimal difficulty increment. A continuous schedule would require interpolating between formats (e.g., 7-choice MCQ), which would add complexity without clear benefit given that the discrete formats already span the discriminative-to-generative spectrum.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Reframing the Hard-Problem Learning Bottleneck as an Output-Space Problem, Not a Capability Problem

The field's dominant approaches to learning from hard problems under RLVR have all shared an implicit assumption: if the model cannot solve a problem, the solution is to inject additional information that guides the model toward a correct trajectory. This information comes in various forms — expert demonstrations (Qu et al., 2025), self-generated hints (Chen et al., 2025a; Li et al., 2025b), prefix-based guidance (Sundaram et al., 2026), or interleaved SFT phases (Ma et al., 2025). What unifies these approaches is that they all attempt to operate within the original open-ended output space — they try to make the model more likely to generate a correct solution *in the same format where it currently always fails*.

Cog-DRIFT's most fundamental conceptual contribution is diagnosing this as the wrong fulcrum. The bottleneck is not that the model lacks the *knowledge* to reason correctly (it may have acquired relevant patterns during pretraining), but that the *output space* is so vast and the reward signal so sparse that the model can never accidentally produce a correct trajectory to get reinforced. This is an **exploration problem masquerading as a capability problem**. The paper's diagnostic evidence for this reframing appears in Figure 2(a): on problems where pass@64 = 0 in the open-ended format (0.0% accuracy), simply reformatting the *same problem* as a 4-choice MCQ yields 33.8% accuracy — well above random chance (25%). The knowledge was latent in the model all along; it was the output format that prevented access to it.

This reframing matters because it suggests a qualitatively different class of solutions. If the barrier is exploration, then interventions should target the search space rather than the model's knowledge. Reformulation into MCQ and cloze formats does exactly this: it constrains the effective output space (from all possible strings to a small set of answer options, or to filling in partially specified answers) while preserving the reasoning required. Prior work's approaches — hints, prefixes, guidance — all accept the vast output space as given and try to navigate it more efficiently. Cog-DRIFT's insight is that you can change the space itself. This is a **fundamental shift in problem framing**, not an incremental improvement on existing methods.

The significance extends beyond the specific formats used here. The reframing opens a design space: for any task where exploration is the bottleneck, what are the minimal structural constraints that preserve the core reasoning while collapsing the output space? This generalizes beyond math — the paper's framework for evaluating reformulations (Takeaway 1's three criteria of answer preservation, reduced difficulty, and transferability) provides a principled way to design and validate such constraints for any domain with verifiable correctness.

The empirical consequences of this reframing are stark. Prior self-generated hint methods in the paper's comparisons (NuRL Abstract and NuRL Prefix in Table 1) achieve modest gains at best — NuRL Abstract reaches 7.73% on BigMath-Hard for Qwen, compared to Cog-DRIFT's 10.11%. The paper explicitly notes that the rate of hard problems becoming solvable under prior methods is in the 1–4% range, while Cog-DRIFT reaches 18.9% (the RFT transfer result in Figure 2b, which is a lower bound since the full Cog-DRIFT with curriculum and GRPO achieves even higher final performance). This 4–5× improvement in the fraction of problems that become learnable is a direct consequence of the reframing: by structurally reducing the exploration problem rather than adding guidance to navigate it, more problems cross the threshold from "no signal" to "learnable."

### Innovation 2: The Discriminative-to-Generative Spectrum as a Curriculum Design Principle

Curriculum learning — training models on easier examples before harder ones — is an old idea in machine learning. In the context of LLM post-training, recent work has explored difficulty-based curricula (Shi et al., 2025; Parashar et al., 2025; Bae et al., 2026), but these approaches treat difficulty as a property of *which problems* to present, not *what format* to present them in. The curriculum operates by sorting problems by their estimated difficulty and presenting easier problems first; the problems themselves are unchanged.

Cog-DRIFT introduces a fundamentally different axis for curriculum design: **format-induced difficulty along the discriminative-to-generative spectrum**. Rather than sorting problems by difficulty, it takes *the same problem* and presents it in progressively more demanding formats — from recognition (select the correct answer from four options) to constrained generation (fill in the blanks with partial structure provided) to free generation (produce the complete answer from scratch). This is not merely a new instantiation of curriculum learning; it is a new *dimension* along which to construct curricula.

The theoretical motivation for this dimension comes from cognitive psychology — specifically, cognitive load theory (Sweller, 1988) and the documented effect of test format on performance (Ko, 2010; Howard et al., 2017). The paper explicitly imports these concepts as an organizing framework: multiple-choice formats reduce extraneous cognitive load (the effort of searching the output space) while preserving germane load (the reasoning required to solve the problem). Cloze formats occupy an intermediate position — they provide partial structure that constrains the output space but require the model to generate parts of the answer rather than merely recognize it. Open-ended formats impose the highest cognitive load because they provide no scaffolding at all.

What makes this innovation distinctive from standard curriculum learning is that it creates an **explicit transfer gradient**. In a conventional difficulty-sorted curriculum, the relationship between easier and harder problems is not structural — an easy algebra problem and a hard geometry problem may share no common reasoning patterns, so mastering the former provides limited transfer to the latter. In Cog-DRIFT's format-based curriculum, the easy version of a problem *is the same problem* as the hard version, differing only in output constraints. This means that the reasoning patterns acquired on the easier format are, by construction, directly applicable to the harder format — the transfer path is guaranteed rather than hoped for.

The empirical signature of this transfer gradient appears in Figure 2(b): as the training format moves from discriminative to generative (4-choice → 10-choice → cloze), transfer to the original open-ended format *improves* (11.1% → 15.5% → 18.9%). The more the training format resembles the target format in its generative demands, the better the transfer — but the more discriminative formats are easier to learn from initially. The curriculum exploits this tension: start where learning is easiest (discriminative) and progressively move toward the target (generative) as competence builds. This principle — using format to control the exploration-generality tradeoff — is **conceptually novel** in LLM post-training and has no direct precedent in prior curriculum learning work.

The practical significance is that it provides a **reusable template** for curriculum construction. For any task with a verifiable answer, one can generate a family of reformulations spanning the discriminative-to-generative spectrum (MCQ with varying option counts, cloze with varying mask density, open-ended), validate them against the three criteria in Takeaway 1, and stage them in a curriculum. The paper demonstrates this for math, but the template is domain-agnostic — the key requirement is simply that the answer can be presented in constrained formats (multiple choice, fill-in-the-blank) without ambiguity.

### Innovation 3: Instance-Level Adaptive Scheduling as a Sample-Efficient Alternative to Static Mixtures

The paper's ablation in Table 2 and Figure 4 reveals a finding that has implications beyond Cog-DRIFT itself: **when training on multiple reformulations of the same underlying problem, a static uniform mixture plateaus, while an adaptive, instance-level schedule continues to improve**. This is not an obvious result — one might expect that a uniform mixture provides maximum diversity and therefore maximum generalization. Instead, the uniform mixture wastes compute on easy formats that the model has already mastered, while the adaptive schedule reallocates that compute to harder formats where learning is still occurring.

The mechanism is the per-instance promotion rule (`â_i ≥ τ` advances the instance to the next difficulty level). The key behavioral consequence, visible in Figure 4, is that the curriculum *dynamically changes the data distribution* as training proceeds: the proportion of 4-choice MCQ steadily decreases, intermediate formats (10-choice, cloze) rise and then fall as instances pass through them, and open-ended problems steadily accumulate. This is fundamentally different from a static curriculum where the schedule is predetermined — the model itself determines the pace of progression for each instance based on its own demonstrated competence.

What makes this innovation conceptually distinctive is that it **closes the loop between learning progress and data selection** at the instance level. In standard RLVR or SFT, the training data is fixed — the model trains on the same distribution regardless of what it has already learned. In Cog-DRIFT, the data distribution *responds* to the model's learning: instances that the model masters quickly advance rapidly, freeing up capacity for instances that require more practice. This is a form of automated curriculum learning that does not require a separate difficulty estimator or a predefined schedule — the difficulty signal comes directly from the model's own rollout accuracy on each instance at its current format.

The empirical payoff is sample efficiency. In Figure 4, the adaptive curriculum continues to improve test accuracy on OmniMATH-Hard through 1,000 training steps, reaching approximately 12%, while the static uniform mixture plateaus around 9–10% and shows little improvement after 400 steps. The paper argues that this is because reformulations largely "share the same underlying information" — training on all four formats simultaneously is redundant, and the curriculum eliminates this redundancy by advancing instances out of formats they've already mastered. This is an **incremental but practically significant innovation**: it takes the already-effective idea of training on multiple reformulations (Table 2 shows that mixtures outperform single formats) and makes it substantially more compute-efficient.

The general principle — adaptively reallocating training samples away from mastered formats toward challenging formats based on per-instance feedback — is transferable to any setting where the same underlying task can be presented at multiple difficulty levels. It does not depend on the specific reformulation formats used here, and it requires no privileged information beyond what is already computed during training (the rollout rewards).

### Innovation 4: Empirical Demonstration That Transfer from Simplified Formats Is Genuine and Generalizes

A natural skepticism about Cog-DRIFT's approach is that training on MCQ and cloze formats might teach the model format-specific heuristics — pattern-matching strategies that work for multiple-choice but don't transfer to open-ended generation. If the model learns to recognize which of four numbers "looks right" based on surface features of the question, this skill is useless for generating answers from scratch. Prior work on shortcut learning in neural networks (Geirhos et al., 2020) and on the limitations of MCQ evaluation for LLMs (Balepur et al., 2025; Chandak et al., 2025) provides ample reason for concern: models are adept at exploiting spurious correlations in constrained formats that don't reflect genuine understanding.

The paper provides three lines of evidence that the transfer is genuine, and together they constitute a **diagnostic contribution** — a set of empirical checks that validate the reformulation approach against the shortcut-learning alternative hypothesis.

**First, the transfer gradient across formats.** In Figure 2(b), training on more generative formats produces better transfer to open-ended problems: 4-choice → 11.1%, 10-choice → 15.5%, cloze → 18.9%. If the model were learning format-specific shortcuts, we would expect the opposite — discriminative formats are easier to learn shortcuts on (just pick the option that looks most familiar), so they should show the worst transfer. Instead, the format that most resembles the target (cloze, which requires generating part of the answer) transfers best, suggesting that the learning is along a continuum of generative skill rather than a jump from shortcut to genuine reasoning.

**Second, held-out generalization.** Figure 2(c) shows that models trained on reformulated formats maintain strong performance on MATH500, a completely unseen dataset. The drop from the base model (78.8%) to the cloze-trained model (74.4%) is modest — only 4.4 percentage points — despite the training being exclusively on problems where the base model scored 0%. A model that had learned format-specific shortcuts would be expected to perform near-random on held-out open-ended problems, since those problems don't share the surface features that the shortcuts exploit. The maintained performance on MATH500 is strong evidence that the model acquired generalizable reasoning patterns, not format-specific heuristics.

**Third, pass@k improvements.** Figure 3 shows that Cog-DRIFT improves pass@k (for k up to 128) on AIME2024, AIME2025, and GPQA compared to both the base model and the GRPO-trained variant. This is a particularly stringent test: if the model had only learned to sharpen its existing distribution (making already-likely correct answers more likely), pass@k would remain flat or even decrease as the distribution concentrates. The fact that pass@k *increases* — for example, +3.33% at k=128 on AIME2024 — indicates that the model is making correct answers *more accessible in its output distribution* for problems it previously could not solve. This is evidence for genuine capability acquisition from the hard training problems, not just distribution sharpening — a finding that directly engages with the ongoing debate about whether RLVR teaches new skills or merely amplifies existing ones (Yue et al., 2025; Yuan et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2025).

This suite of evidence — transfer gradient, held-out generalization, and pass@k gains — does more than validate Cog-DRIFT. It establishes a **template for evaluating reformulation-based training methods**: to claim that simplified-format training transfers, one should demonstrate (a) that more generative training formats transfer better, (b) that held-out performance is maintained or improved, and (c) that pass@k increases, not just pass@1. The paper's contribution here is as much methodological as empirical.

### Innovation 5: Identification and Mitigation of Selection Bias in Pass@k-Based Difficulty Estimation

Using pass@k to identify hard problems is a common practice in the RLVR literature (Yuan et al., 2025; Jiang et al., 2026; Yu et al., 2025), but the paper uncovers a **previously underappreciated failure mode** of this approach: pass@k = 0 selects not only problems that are genuinely hard for the model, but also problems that are objectively unsolvable — those with incorrect ground-truth labels, incomplete or ambiguous statements, missing figures, or other data quality issues. The initial `D_hard` of 8,922 problems collapses to just 958 after the Silver Answer Filter — an 89% reduction — revealing that the vast majority of "hard" problems identified by pass@64 = 0 are actually corrupt.

This finding is a **diagnostic contribution with immediate practical implications** for anyone constructing RLVR training datasets. The naive approach of thresholding by pass@k conflates two distinct categories of failure: model incapability (the model lacks the knowledge or reasoning skill) and data corruption (the problem is ill-posed regardless of model capability). Training on the latter is worse than useless — it provides misleading or contradictory reward signals. The paper's demonstration that Llama (the more data-sensitive model) shows substantial performance degradation when trained on unfiltered data (Table 3: MATH500 drops from 32.2% with Silver Answer Filter to 24.2% unfiltered, GPQA from 23.3% to 20.7%) quantifies the cost of ignoring this selection bias.

The paper's mitigation — using a strong external model (GPT-5.4) as a data quality judge without using its reasoning traces for distillation — is a **pragmatic contribution**. It acknowledges that some external capability is needed for data cleaning while carefully separating this from the learning process itself: the external model validates that problems are well-posed, but does not provide solutions, hints, or trajectories that the learner would imitate. This distinguishes the filtering step from the expert-guidance approaches that the paper critiques, which use stronger models to provide learning signals. Filtering removes noise; it does not teach.

The significance extends beyond Cog-DRIFT. The finding that 89% of pass@64 = 0 problems are corrupt suggests that many published results reporting training on "hard problems" may be training on predominantly noisy data without realizing it. This has implications for the reproducibility and interpretability of RLVR research: when a method claims to improve performance on hard problems, it is critical to distinguish whether it is genuinely learning from challenging-but-solvable examples or merely becoming more robust to data corruption. The paper's filtering methodology provides a concrete protocol for making this distinction — future work on hard-problem learning should report the fraction of `D_hard` that survives a Silver Answer Filter and the performance impact of filtering.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The training set is derived from BigMath (Albalak et al., 2025), a large-scale math dataset containing 251k samples with difficulty annotations. Cog-DRIFT starts with the hardest 20% of BigMath (37.5k problems), uses the Qwen3-4B-Instruct model to sample 64 reasoning chains per problem, and retains only those where all 64 chains are incorrect (pass@64=0). After applying the Silver Answer Filter (GPT-5.4 majority vote agreement, Section 3.4), the final training set `D_hard` contains **958 hard problems**. The evaluation benchmarks comprise six datasets: BigMath-Hard (BMH, 192 held-out problems from the same pass@64=0 split), OmniMATH-Hard (452 Olympiad-level problems with difficulty ≥7.5 from OmniMATH), AIME 2024 and 2025 (30 competition problems each), GPQA-Diamond (198 PhD-level science questions), Date Understanding (369 date arithmetic tasks from Big-Bench, converted to open-ended by removing option choices), and MATH500 (500 problems from MATH, used for generalization analysis).

- **Base model(s).** Two models from different families are evaluated: **Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507** (Team, 2025) and **Llama3.2-3B-Instruct** (Grattafiori et al., 2024), referred to as Qwen and Llama respectively. The paper selects these two because they exhibit "different behaviors in post-training" (Shao et al., 2025)—specifically, Qwen can show spurious improvements even from uninformative reward signals, while Llama is more sensitive and fails entirely under the same conditions. This deliberate contrast makes the evaluation more diagnostic: if a method works well on both models, it is less likely to be an artifact of model-specific idiosyncrasies. The models are small (3B–4B parameters), which keeps training computationally tractable while still representative of contemporary open-weight LLM capabilities.

- **Metrics.** The primary metric throughout is **accuracy (%),** specifically pass@1—the fraction of test problems for which the model's single sampled answer exactly matches the ground truth (verified using the Math-Verify library for mathematical equivalence checking). For AIME, results are averaged over 16 independent runs to reduce variance given the small test set (30 problems); for other datasets, results are averaged over 3 runs. The paper additionally reports **pass@k for k ∈ {1, 2, …, 128}** in Figure 3 to assess whether learning transfers to broader sampling regimes, and reports **problem mix proportions** (Figure 4) to track curriculum progression.

- **Baselines.** The paper compares against six baselines that do not require privileged information from stronger models: (1) **Zero-shot**: the model is prompted to think step-by-step and output the answer in `\boxed{}` (Kojima et al., 2022). (2) **Few-shot**: the same prompt augmented with 8 in-context learning examples. (3) **RFT (Rejection sampling Fine-Tuning)**: the model is fine-tuned via SFT on correct chain-of-thought trajectories extracted from cloze-reformulated problems (Yuan et al., 2023); cloze is chosen for RFT because it transfers best among the single-format options (Figure 2b). (4) **GRPO**: standard GRPO training directly on the original open-ended `D_hard` problems using outcome correctness as reward (Guo et al., 2025; Shao et al., 2024). (5) **NuRL (Abstract)**: GRPO training augmented with abstract cues generated from answer explanations, following Chen et al. (2025a). (6) **NuRL (Prefix)**: GRPO training augmented with partial solution steps as prefixes, conceptually similar to POPE (Qu et al., 2025) but using self-generated rather than expert-generated prefixes. Cog-DRIFT is the proposed method, combining reformulated format training with the instance-level curriculum under GRPO.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** All GRPO-based methods (GRPO, NuRL variants, Cog-DRIFT) use identical training hyperparameters: 8 rollouts per prompt (`m=8`), training batch size of 8 prompts (64 total generations per gradient step), and a maximum of 1,000 training steps (64,000 total generations). The rollout budget is therefore matched across all RL-based methods. For RFT, the budget is not directly comparable since it uses SFT rather than RL, but the paper reports RFT as a reference point rather than a primary competitor. Inference is conducted with temperature 0.7 and maximum 8,192 output tokens across all methods. The paper does not perform a FLOPs-matched comparison between methods, since the primary axis of comparison is accuracy under identical training hyperparameters.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not employ cross-validation or statistical significance testing. For the main results in Table 1, variance is addressed through repeated evaluation runs (16 for AIME, 3 for other datasets). The instance-level curriculum itself serves as an implicit form of adaptive data selection, but there is no explicit protocol for estimating the variance of the curriculum's trajectory or for significance testing between methods. The Silver Answer Filter (Section 3.4) applies a data quality threshold before training begins; this is a one-time filtering step, not a cross-validation procedure.

### Main Quantitative Results

The paper evaluates Cog-DRIFT across two primary dimensions: (1) **performance on the hard training problems** (BigMath-Hard, the pass@64=0 test split) to test whether reformulation unlocks learning where standard methods fail, and (2) **generalization to five held-out benchmarks** spanning math, science, and reasoning tasks to test whether the acquired skills transfer. A third dimension examines whether the gains are due to distribution sharpening (improved pass@1 only) or genuine capability acquisition (improved pass@k).

#### Performance on Hard Problems (BigMath-Hard)

The headline result appears in Table 1: on BigMath-Hard—a test set where all methods start at 0.0% accuracy by construction (all problems have pass@64=0 on the base model)—Cog-DRIFT achieves **10.11% absolute accuracy for Qwen** and **8.64% for Llama**. This represents genuine learning from problems that provided zero reward signal under standard RLVR.

**Comparison with standard GRPO (Table 1, BMH column).** For Qwen, standard GRPO achieves 6.37% on BMH—a smaller gain than Cog-DRIFT's 10.11%. The paper attributes GRPO's non-zero performance on Qwen to "spurious reward effects" (Shao et al., 2025), noting that Qwen can extract some learning signal even when rewards are largely uninformative. However, Table 1 reveals a critical pattern: GRPO-trained Qwen degrades on held-out benchmarks. Specifically, on AIME24, GRPO scores 31.87% vs. 46.92% for zero-shot; on AIME25, 27.29% vs. 41.71%; on OmniMATH-Hard, 16.37% vs. 15.67% (roughly flat); only on GPQA does GRPO show a gain (31.81% vs. 23.57%). The average across all six benchmarks for GRPO-trained Qwen is 33.87%, well below the zero-shot average of 35.93%. This is a crucial finding: **GRPO on hard problems can harm generalization** even when it shows local improvement on the training distribution. For Llama, the situation is starker: GRPO achieves 0.0% on BMH (complete failure to learn) and degrades substantially across held-out benchmarks (average 9.92% vs. 13.24% zero-shot), with particularly severe drops on Date Understanding (38.85% vs. 54.87%) and GPQA (12.03% vs. 15.32%).

**Comparison with NuRL baselines (Table 1, BMH column).** The NuRL variants represent the self-generated hint approach. For Qwen, NuRL (Abstract) achieves 7.73% on BMH, and NuRL (Prefix) achieves 6.51%—both better than standard GRPO but substantially below Cog-DRIFT's 10.11%. For Llama, NuRL (Abstract) reaches 1.38% and NuRL (Prefix) reaches 1.04%—marginal gains at best, and both below Cog-DRIFT's 8.64%. The gap between Cog-DRIFT and the second-best baseline on BMH is **+2.38 percentage points for Qwen and +7.60 for Llama**—this is the direct empirical evidence for the paper's claim that reformulation is more effective than self-generated hints for unlocking learning from hard problems.

**Comparison with RFT (Table 1, BMH column).** RFT using cloze reformulations achieves 4.32% for Qwen and 2.47% for Llama on BMH. These are non-zero, confirming the transfer effect demonstrated in Figure 2(b), but they are substantially below Cog-DRIFT's performance—this validates the superiority of the combined GRPO + curriculum approach over simple SFT on reformulated data.

#### Generalization to Held-Out Benchmarks

The second major result is that Cog-DRIFT achieves the **best average accuracy across all six benchmarks** for both models (Table 1, Avg. column). For Qwen, Cog-DRIFT's average is 40.92%, compared to 36.20% for the second-best baseline (NuRL Abstract)—an absolute improvement of **+4.72 percentage points**. For Llama, Cog-DRIFT's average is 17.74%, compared to 14.51% for the second-best baseline (NuRL Abstract)—an improvement of **+3.23 percentage points**.

**Benchmark-by-benchmark analysis (Table 1):**

- **OmniMATH-Hard:** Cog-DRIFT achieves 18.58% (Qwen) and 7.04% (Llama). For Qwen, this is the highest score; for Llama, it is slightly below NuRL (Prefix) at 7.19% but within variance. The key comparison is that Cog-DRIFT substantially outperforms zero-shot (15.67% Qwen, 5.09% Llama) and GRPO (16.37% Qwen, 4.32% Llama), showing that the hard-problem training does not degrade—and in fact improves—performance on held-out math problems.

- **AIME24:** Cog-DRIFT achieves 51.74% (Qwen) and 4.17% (Llama). For Qwen, this represents a +4.82 percentage point improvement over zero-shot (46.92%) and a striking +19.87 point improvement over GRPO-trained Qwen (31.87%). For Llama, Cog-DRIFT matches the best baseline at 4.17% (tied with NuRL Abstract), while GRPO-trained Llama drops to 0.68%. The massive gap between Cog-DRIFT and GRPO on Qwen for AIME24 is perhaps the single strongest piece of evidence that reformulation-based training preserves and enhances reasoning capabilities that direct RL on hard problems degrades.

- **AIME25:** Cog-DRIFT achieves 40.28% (Qwen) and 0.42% (Llama). For Qwen, this is second to NuRL (Prefix) at 38.81% (a small gap) but well above GRPO's 27.29%. For Llama, all methods perform poorly on AIME25 (the best baseline reaches 1.11%), reflecting the 3B model's limited capacity on competition-level math; Cog-DRIFT's 0.42% is within the noise range of the best baselines.

- **GPQA:** Cog-DRIFT achieves 36.01% (Qwen) and 23.34% (Llama)—the best result for both models. For Qwen, this is a +12.44 point improvement over zero-shot (23.57%) and +4.20 over GRPO (31.81%). For Llama, it is a +8.02 point improvement over zero-shot (15.32%) and +11.31 over GRPO (12.03%). GPQA is a PhD-level science benchmark with questions in biology, physics, and chemistry, making it a strong test of cross-domain generalization—Cog-DRIFT's gains here indicate that the reasoning skills acquired from math reformulations transfer to scientific reasoning.

- **Date Understanding:** Cog-DRIFT achieves 88.75% (Qwen) and 62.82% (Llama). For Qwen, this is the second-highest (after GRPO's 89.53%, a narrow gap). For Llama, Cog-DRIFT's 62.82% is substantially above all baselines—the next best is NuRL (Abstract) at 56.72%, and GRPO-trained Llama drops to 38.85%. Date Understanding is the most "reasoning-light" benchmark, testing structured arithmetic and calendar logic; Cog-DRIFT's strong performance here (and the lack of degradation vs. zero-shot) indicates that the method does not cause catastrophic forgetting on simpler tasks, a risk that materializes for GRPO-trained Llama (54.87% → 38.85%).

**Key pattern across benchmarks:** For both models, Cog-DRIFT is the only method that *never substantially underperforms zero-shot* on any benchmark, while simultaneously being the top or near-top performer on the hardest benchmarks (BMH, OmniMATH-Hard, GPQA). GRPO, by contrast, shows a pattern of BMH improvement (Qwen only) at the cost of held-out degradation. NuRL baselines occupy a middle ground—modest BMH gains with less severe held-out degradation than GRPO. Cog-DRIFT achieves the best of both: substantial BMH gains and held-out improvements.

#### Pass@k Improvements and Evidence for Capability Acquisition

Figure 3 examines whether Cog-DRIFT merely sharpens the model's existing output distribution (improving pass@1 while leaving pass@k flat) or genuinely makes correct solutions more accessible (improving pass@k). The results, evaluated with Qwen on AIME24, AIME25, and GPQA, show that Cog-DRIFT **improves pass@k over both the base instruct model and the GRPO-trained model across all values of k ∈ {1, …, 128}**.

On AIME2024, at k=128, Cog-DRIFT reaches approximately 27% pass@128 compared to approximately 23.5% for the base Qwen model—a +3.33 percentage point gain. The GRPO-trained model tracks below both, consistent with its degraded AIME24 performance in Table 1 (31.87% pass@1 vs. 51.74% for Cog-DRIFT). On AIME2025, the pass@128 gain is similarly +3.33 percentage points over the base model. On GPQA, the pass@128 gain is +2.02 percentage points. The consistent upward shift of the pass@k curve—not just at low k but at high k—is the key diagnostic. If Cog-DRIFT were merely distribution sharpening (making already-accessible correct answers more likely while suppressing less-likely alternatives), pass@k at large k would remain unchanged (the correct answers that were accessible before would still be accessible) or even decrease (if the sharpening eliminated low-probability correct answers). The increase at k=128, where sampling 128 times already gives the base model a ~23–24% chance of finding a correct answer on AIME, indicates that Cog-DRIFT is making *new* correct solutions accessible—solutions that were previously below the sampling threshold of 128 attempts.

This finding directly engages with the debate in the RLVR literature about whether RL post-training teaches new skills or merely amplifies existing ones (Yue et al., 2025; Yuan et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2025). The paper's pass@k results provide evidence for the "new skills" interpretation: training on reformulated hard problems reshapes the output distribution to make correct solutions more reachable, not just more probable conditional on being sampled.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Reformulation diversity: single vs. multiple formats (Table 2).** The paper investigates whether a single reformulation format suffices or whether exposure to multiple formats is necessary for transfer. Using RFT (not the full Cog-DRIFT curriculum), the model is trained on various data mixtures and evaluated on OmniMATH-Hard and MATH500. Training solely on open-ended data (OEQ only) yields 14.16% on OmniMATH-Hard and 76.20% on MATH500—both *below* the zero-shot baseline (15.67% and 78.80%, respectively), confirming that RFT on hard open-ended problems without reformulation is ineffective. Training on a single reformulation format (4-MCQ or Cloze) does not improve over no training: 4-MCQ alone yields 14.82% on OmniMATH-Hard (below 15.67% zero-shot) and 79.80% on MATH500 (roughly flat). The critical finding is that **combining multiple reformulations is what unlocks transfer**: training on 4-MCQ + Cloze (without any open-ended data) achieves 18.36% on OmniMATH-Hard and 82.60% on MATH500, both substantially above the zero-shot baseline. Adding open-ended data to the mix (OEQ + 4-MCQ + Cloze) yields 18.42% and 82.20%—comparable to the MCQ+Cloze mixture, showing that the reformulations, not the open-ended data, are driving the improvement. This is a non-obvious result: one might expect that including open-ended data would be essential for transfer back to open-ended evaluation, but the mixture of two discriminative/generative-intermediate formats (4-MCQ + Cloze) without any open-ended training achieves essentially identical held-out open-ended performance. The paper's interpretation is that diversity in problem representation—seeing the same underlying reasoning in both selection and partial-generation formats—is what prevents format-specific shortcut learning and enables genuine skill transfer.

**Curriculum vs. static uniform mixture (Figure 4).** Using the full Cog-DRIFT framework (GRPO training on reformulated problems), the paper compares the instance-level curriculum (left panel) with a static uniform mixture where 25% of training data comes from each of the four formats at all times (right panel). The instance-level curriculum shows adaptive progression: the proportion of 4-choice MCQ decreases from ~100% to ~20% over 1,000 steps, intermediate formats (10-choice, cloze) rise and then fall, and open-ended proportion grows to ~40% by step 1,000. Test accuracy on OmniMATH-Hard open-ended questions under the curriculum continues to improve throughout training, reaching approximately 12% by step 1,000. In contrast, the static uniform mixture plateaus around 9–10% after approximately 400 steps and shows minimal further improvement. The paper argues that this demonstrates improved sample efficiency: the static mixture wastes compute on formats that the model has already mastered, while the curriculum reallocates those samples to harder formats where learning is still occurring. The curriculum's benefit is not in final accuracy alone but in the *rate of improvement per training step*—the curriculum achieves higher accuracy with the same number of steps, or equivalently, reaches a given accuracy threshold in fewer steps.

**Data quality filtering (Table 3).** The paper compares three versions of `D_hard`: the original unfiltered set (8,922 problems), the Binary Filter set (5,922 problems, filtered by GPT-5.4 for answerability), and the Silver Answer Filter set (958 problems, filtered by GPT-5.4 majority vote agreement with the ground truth). The RFT model is trained on each and evaluated on MATH500 and GPQA. For Qwen, performance is relatively insensitive to filtering: MATH500 remains in the 81.6–82.2% range across all three filters, and GPQA varies modestly (33.1% → 34.7% → 36.0% with increasing filter strictness). This aligns with Qwen's documented robustness to noisy reward signals (Shao et al., 2025). For Llama, the effects are substantial and monotonic: MATH500 improves from 24.2% (unfiltered) to 28.0% (Binary) to 32.2% (Silver), and GPQA improves from 20.7% to 22.8% to 23.3%. The cleanest data yields the best results, and the magnitude of improvement (~8 percentage points on MATH500) is large enough to dwarf the differences between some methods in Table 1. This ablation reveals that Llama is meaningfully sensitive to data quality and that the Silver Answer Filter—while reducing dataset size by 89%—is essential for unlocking learning on this model. The paper's default choice of the Silver Answer Filter is therefore justified for Llama and benign for Qwen.

**Pass@k as a diagnostic for capability acquisition (Figure 3).** While not a traditional ablation in the sense of removing a component, the pass@k analysis serves as an ablation on the "distribution sharpening vs. new capability" hypothesis. By showing that Cog-DRIFT increases pass@k across a range of k values (not just pass@1), the paper rules out the alternative explanation that the observed improvements are solely due to making already-accessible correct answers more likely at the expense of diversity. Had Cog-DRIFT only improved pass@1 while pass@k remained flat or decreased, the interpretation would be that the method merely sharpens the distribution without acquiring new knowledge. The observed pass@k gains (particularly at k=128) are evidence against this null hypothesis.

**Problem format examples and qualitative outputs (Appendices D and F).** Appendix D provides concrete examples of all four problem formats (open-ended, 4-choice, 10-choice, cloze) for the same underlying question, illustrating exactly what the model sees during training. Appendix F provides qualitative outputs from Qwen under three conditions (base instruct, GRPO-trained, and Cog-DRIFT-trained) on representative hard problems. Example 9 (base Qwen) shows a long, detailed, and correct reasoning chain for a number theory problem; Example 10 (GRPO-trained Qwen) shows a reasoning chain that reaches a degenerate solution (finding 0 as the only matrix) through a plausible but partially circular argument; Example 11 (Cog-DRIFT-trained Qwen) shows a more structured reasoning chain that correctly navigates the problem's logical constraints. These examples are illustrative rather than systematically evaluated, but they provide intuition for the quantitative results.

### Critical Assessment

The experimental evaluation provides substantial evidence for the paper's central claims, but several limitations temper the strength of the conclusions and the generality of the findings.

#### Claim: Reformulation enables learning from pass@64=0 problems that standard RLVR cannot access.

**What the experiments demonstrate.** Table 1 shows that on BigMath-Hard (the held-out test split of pass@64=0 problems), Cog-DRIFT achieves 10.11% (Qwen) and 8.64% (Llama), while standard GRPO achieves 6.37% and 0.0% respectively. This demonstrates that reformulation-based training improves performance on originally unsolvable problems beyond what direct RL can achieve. However, the claim is complicated by Qwen's 6.37% gain under standard GRPO—this means that for this model, pass@64=0 does not strictly prevent learning, at least on the training distribution. The paper's explanation (spurious reward effects from Shao et al., 2025) is plausible but means the claim must be qualified: reformulation enables learning from hard problems where the model is susceptible to RLVR failure, but some models may extract signal from sparse rewards through mechanisms not fully explained by the advantage-variance argument in Section 3.

**What was not tested.** The paper uses a single test set (BigMath-Hard) for in-distribution evaluation on hard problems. It would strengthen the claim to show that Cog-DRIFT's gains on pass@64=0 problems persist to other hard-problem test sets constructed by the same methodology from different source datasets (e.g., the hardest problems from OmniMATH or AIME collections). Without this, the result could be specific to the BigMath test split. Additionally, the paper does not vary the pass@k threshold for defining "hard"—all experiments use pass@64=0. A useful robustness check would be to evaluate Cog-DRIFT on problems where pass@k=0 for different values of k (e.g., k=16, 32, 128) to test whether the reformulation benefit scales with problem difficulty as expected. The claim that reformulation enables learning where standard methods fail would be more convincing if the gap between Cog-DRIFT and GRPO widened as problems get harder (lower pass@k), which the current single-threshold evaluation cannot verify.

#### Claim: Cog-DRIFT generalizes to held-out benchmarks, improving over baselines by +4.72% (Qwen) and +3.23% (Llama) on average.

**What the experiments demonstrate.** Table 1 shows that Cog-DRIFT achieves the highest average accuracy across six benchmarks for both models, with the reported margins over the second-best baseline. However, the **margin varies dramatically across benchmarks**. For Qwen, nearly all of the average improvement is driven by two benchmarks: AIME24 (+19.87 over GRPO, +4.82 over zero-shot) and GPQA (+12.44 over zero-shot, +4.20 over GRPO). On other benchmarks, Cog-DRIFT's advantage is small (Date Understanding: 88.75% vs. 89.53% for GRPO; OmniMATH-Hard: 18.58% vs. 16.37% for GRPO) or essentially tied (AIME25: 40.28% vs. 38.81% for NuRL Prefix). The overall average is an aggregate that masks substantial heterogeneity in where the gains occur. For Llama, the gains are more uniformly distributed but small in absolute terms on the hardest benchmarks (AIME24: 4.17% vs. 4.17% tied; AIME25: 0.42% vs. 1.11% for the best baseline). The headline average improvement should be understood as a summary statistic that is heavily influenced by a few benchmarks with large gains, not a uniform improvement across all tasks.

**The baseline selection raises questions.** The second-best baseline varies by model and benchmark: for Qwen, it is NuRL (Abstract) at 36.20% average, and for Llama, it is also NuRL (Abstract) at 14.51%. But the performance ordering of baselines is inconsistent—NuRL (Prefix) beats NuRL (Abstract) on some benchmarks and loses on others, GRPO beats zero-shot on GPQA but loses on AIME, and few-shot sometimes beats zero-shot and sometimes loses. This instability across benchmarks is not analyzed; the paper treats each baseline as an independent point of comparison rather than investigating why relative performance flips. A reader cannot determine from the reported results whether the baseline that performs best on average is genuinely the strongest approach or simply had favorable draws on a few high-weight benchmarks.

**No confidence intervals or statistical tests.** The paper reports averages across 16 runs (AIME) and 3 runs (other benchmarks) but does not report standard deviations, confidence intervals, or any hypothesis tests. Given the small test sets (30 problems for AIME, 192 for BMH, 198 for GPQA), the sampling variance may be large relative to the reported differences. For AIME24 with Qwen, the gap between Cog-DRIFT (51.74%) and NuRL Abstract (44.81%) is ~7 percentage points on 30 problems—roughly 2 problems' difference in the number of correctly answered questions. Without variance estimates, it is impossible to assess whether this margin exceeds what would be expected from sampling variability.

**The absence of a combined reformulations + GRPO baseline without curriculum.** The main comparison in Table 1 is Cog-DRIFT (reformulations + curriculum + GRPO) against GRPO (no reformulations). But the paper does not report a baseline that uses reformulations + GRPO *without* the curriculum—i.e., training on a uniform mixture of all four reformulation formats under GRPO but not using the instance-level progression rule. The only experiment with a static uniform mixture is in Figure 4, where it plateaus at lower accuracy than the curriculum, but Figure 4 uses OmniMATH-Hard as the evaluation metric and does not report benchmark-level breakdowns. This makes it difficult to attribute the gains in Table 1 specifically to the curriculum vs. to the reformulations themselves. An explicit "Cog-DRIFT without curriculum" entry in Table 1 would clarify what each component contributes to the final performance.

#### Claim: Pass@k improvements demonstrate genuine capability acquisition, not just distribution sharpening.

**What the experiments demonstrate.** Figure 3 shows that Cog-DRIFT-trained Qwen achieves higher pass@k than both the base model and GRPO-trained Qwen on AIME24, AIME25, and GPQA. This is strong evidence against the null hypothesis that Cog-DRIFT only sharpens the distribution. However, the magnitude of the pass@k gains is relatively small in absolute terms: +3.33 percentage points at k=128 on AIME24 and AIME25, and +2.02 on GPQA. At k=128, pass@128 is in the ~23–27% range, meaning the model still fails to find a correct answer for ~73–77% of problems even after 128 attempts. This suggests that while Cog-DRIFT makes some correct solutions more accessible, the majority of problems remain beyond reach even at large sampling budgets—the capability acquisition is real but modest.

**Unresolved question: where do the pass@k gains come from?** The paper argues that the pass@k gains indicate genuine acquisition of knowledge from the hard training problems, but an alternative explanation is that Cog-DRIFT's training on reformulations (which are structurally similar to multiple-choice questions in the evaluation benchmarks) teaches the model to better navigate its own output distribution—e.g., to avoid dead-end reasoning paths that lead away from correct answers, or to recognize implausible intermediate results earlier. This would manifest as improved pass@k without requiring the model to learn genuinely new conceptual knowledge; it would be learning to search its existing knowledge more efficiently. The paper's evaluation cannot distinguish between "learned new concepts from the hard problems" and "learned to use existing concepts more effectively when solving novel problems." Both would produce pass@k gains and both are useful, but they represent different claims about what Cog-DRIFT accomplishes.

#### Claim: The instance-level curriculum improves sample efficiency and prevents performance plateaus.

**What the experiments demonstrate.** Figure 4 clearly shows that the adaptive curriculum continues to improve test accuracy while the static uniform mixture plateaus. This is solid evidence for improved sample efficiency under the specific conditions tested. However, the claim is evaluated on a single metric (OmniMATH-Hard accuracy) for a single model (presumably Qwen, though the figure caption does not specify). The generalizability of the curriculum benefit to other benchmarks and to Llama is not shown—Figure 4 may reflect Qwen-specific dynamics, and the fact that Llama struggles more with data quality (Table 3) suggests the curriculum behavior might differ. Additionally, the comparison is against a specific static mixture (uniform 25% per format). Other static mixtures (e.g., weighted toward harder formats, or a predetermined schedule) are not evaluated, so the claim that the curriculum is *necessary* for sample efficiency versus one particular naive baseline is established, but the claim that it is *optimal* among all possible scheduling strategies is untested.

#### Overarching Limitations

**Single-domain evaluation.** All six evaluation benchmarks are math, science, or logic reasoning tasks—domains with unambiguous correct answers that can be scored deterministically. The paper's entire framework (the three criteria in Takeaway 1, the reformulation function T, the GRPO reward structure) depends on having verifiable ground truth. It is unclear whether the approach extends to domains without clean correctness signals (summarization, translation, creative writing, open-ended dialogue), which limits the generality of the "task reformulation" paradigm beyond the reasoning-with-verifiable-rewards setting.

**Missing ablation on the threshold τ.** The curriculum's promotion threshold is set to τ = 0.5 (advance when accuracy on the current format reaches 50%). The paper does not ablate this value. A lower threshold (e.g., 0.25) would cause faster progression but risk advancing before mastery; a higher threshold (e.g., 0.75) would ensure mastery but risk over-training. The sensitivity of the curriculum's performance to τ is not explored, making the 0.5 choice unexamined.

**Missing analysis of correctness degradation during curriculum progression.** When an instance advances from, say, 4-choice MCQ to 10-choice MCQ, its accuracy on the new format will initially be lower than it was on the old format. The paper does not report whether instances ever *regress* (i.e., accuracy dropping so far that the model would benefit from returning to a lower difficulty level) or investigate whether allowing downward transitions would improve learning. The curriculum is monotonic (only promotion, never demotion), which may be suboptimal if the model forgets or overfits as difficulty increases.

**No comparison to compute-matched baselines with more data.** Cog-DRIFT's reformulations effectively quadruple the number of training samples (each hard problem spawns three additional variants). A potential confound is that Cog-DRIFT benefits from seeing more data, not from the reformulations per se. A control experiment that uses the reformulations to *generate additional open-ended problems* (e.g., by varying numerical values while preserving structure) rather than format variants—so the total number of training samples is matched but the format is always open-ended—would isolate the effect of format diversity from the effect of data augmentation. This control is not reported.

**The Silver Answer Filter uses an external strong model.** Although the paper frames GPT-5.4's role as "data cleaning" rather than "teaching," using a model that is vastly more capable than the learners (GPT-5.4 is a frontier model; the learners are 3B–4B parameters) raises the question of whether the 958 clean problems are "hard" in the same sense for GPT-5.4 as they are for the small models. If GPT-5.4 can solve these problems easily (as implied by its use for majority-vote filtering), then the filtered set consists of problems that are solvable in principle but require capabilities the small models currently lack. This is a legitimate learning target, but it means the experiment tests whether small models can learn from problems that are "hard for them but easy for a much larger model"—a different question from "can models learn from problems that are genuinely at the frontier of all available capability." The paper acknowledges that it uses GPT-5.4 "solely to approximate the filtering of noisy question–answer pairs" and does not use its reasoning traces, but the filtering step still injects information about which problems are well-posed, which is a form of external supervision that simpler difficulty-estimation methods (e.g., inter-model agreement among the small models themselves) might not provide.

**Generalizability across model scales.** Both evaluated models are in the 3B–4B parameter range. The paper does not test Cog-DRIFT on larger models (7B, 13B, 70B) where the base pass@64 rate on BigMath-Hard would be higher, meaning fewer problems meet the pass@64=0 criterion and those that do are genuinely harder. The dynamics of the curriculum—and the relative benefit of reformulation vs. standard RLVR—may differ at larger scales where the exploration problem is less severe (higher base pass@k means more reward signal even without reformulation). The paper's claims are validated for small models; extrapolation to larger scales is speculative.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Silver Answer Filter Requires an External Frontier Model, Breaking the Self-Contained Learning Promise

**The assumption or constraint.** Cog-DRIFT is presented as a method that enables models to learn from hard problems "without requiring stronger teacher models or privileged information." However, the data filtering pipeline that makes Cog-DRIFT effective for Llama—the Silver Answer Filter—uses GPT-5.4, a frontier model vastly more capable than the 3B–4B parameter learners, to validate that problems are well-posed by generating three independent solutions and checking that the majority vote agrees with the ground-truth answer. The paper acknowledges this explicitly (Section 6): "GPT-5.4 is used solely to approximate the filtering of noisy question–answer pairs; we do not use its reasoning traces for any form of distillation." The distinction between "filtering" and "teaching" is important in principle, but in practice the filter still injects external information: it tells the training process which problems are genuinely solvable (and by implication, which are worth attempting to learn from), information that the small learner models cannot generate on their own.

**The consequence.** There are two distinct failure modes. First, if a stronger external model is not available—precisely the scenario the paper motivates as a key advantage of Cog-DRIFT over methods like POPE (Qu et al., 2025) that require expert trajectories—then the Silver Answer Filter cannot be applied. Without filtering, Table 3 shows that Llama's performance degrades substantially: MATH500 drops from 32.2% (Silver filtered) to 24.2% (unfiltered), a loss of 8 percentage points that would erase much of Cog-DRIFT's reported advantage over baselines. The Binary Filter (GPT-5.4 judging answerability without solving) partially mitigates this (28.0% MATH500), but still relies on an external model. The paper provides no method for filtering `D_hard` that uses only the learner's own capabilities—no inter-sample agreement metrics, no confidence-based filtering, no self-consistency checks. Second, even with access to a strong model, the filter introduces an implicit capability bound: the problems that survive filtering are those that GPT-5.4 can solve (with majority voting across three attempts). This means the filtered `D_hard` consists of problems that are "hard for a small model but solvable for a frontier model"—not problems at the absolute frontier of what any available model can solve. The paper's framing (learning from problems that "even too hard for experts") does not apply to the Silver-filtered set, since GPT-5.4 demonstrably can solve them.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 3 provides direct evidence for Llama's sensitivity to filtering quality, with a monotonic improvement as filtering strictness increases (unfiltered → Binary → Silver). The 89% reduction in dataset size from initial `D_hard` (8,922 problems) to Silver-filtered (958 problems) reveals how much of the "hard problem" identification is driven by data corruption rather than genuine difficulty. The paper does not report what happens when no external model is available for filtering—there is no experiment using only self-consistency or heuristic filters, making the method's performance in a truly self-contained setting unknown.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges the filtering dependence but does not resolve it. Section 6 frames GPT-5.4's role as a data-cleaning step distinct from learning-signal injection, but this framing does not address the practical dependence on external capability for the method to work effectively on Llama. The paper does not propose or evaluate any filtering approach that uses only the learner model, nor does it discuss whether the Silver Answer Filter could be replaced by, for example, cross-validation across multiple small models or heuristic checks for question completeness. This remains an open problem that directly affects the method's deployability in the "no stronger model available" setting.

### Difficulty Estimation via pass@64 Is Computationally Expensive and Unaccounted for in Efficiency Claims

**The assumption or constraint.** The entire Cog-DRIFT pipeline depends on first identifying `D_hard`: the subset of training problems where pass@64 = 0. Constructing this set requires generating 64 complete reasoning chains (each up to 8,192 tokens) for every candidate problem—in the paper's case, 37.5k problems from the hardest 20% of BigMath—and scoring each against the ground truth. Only then can reformulations be generated and training begin. This difficulty estimation step consumes substantial inference compute: 37.5k problems × 64 samples × ~8k tokens per sample ≈ 19.2 billion tokens of generation, not counting the additional compute for reformulation generation and verification. The paper acknowledges the computational cost of difficulty estimation only implicitly, noting (Section 3) that "we use Qwen to obtain `D_hard`" and that the initial pass@64 filtering is performed before any training begins.

**The consequence.** The computational cost of constructing `D_hard` is not amortized into any of the reported efficiency or performance metrics. The headline result—that Cog-DRIFT achieves 10.11% (Qwen) and 8.64% (Llama) on BigMath-Hard—reflects performance after the difficulty estimation, reformulation, and training pipeline is complete, with no accounting for the compute spent identifying which problems to reformulate. For a practitioner deciding whether to deploy Cog-DRIFT, the relevant metric is total compute (difficulty estimation + reformulation generation + training) versus total benefit. The training phase itself uses 1,000 steps × 64 generations/step = 64,000 generations. The difficulty estimation phase uses 37.5k × 64 = 2.4 million generations—roughly 37× more than the entire training phase. If difficulty estimation were counted, the "efficiency" of Cog-DRIFT relative to simply running more GRPO on unfiltered data might look very different. Furthermore, the paper's filtering pipeline compounds this cost: after generating 64 samples per problem, an additional pass with GPT-5.4 (3 solutions per problem) is needed for the Silver Answer Filter, adding further external API costs not quantified in the paper.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides the raw numbers needed to estimate the difficulty estimation cost—37.5k initial problems (Section 5, Dataset paragraph), 64 samples per problem (Section 3), 8,192 max response tokens (Appendix A)—but never computes or discusses the total. The training budget is reported (1,000 steps, 8 rollouts per step, 8 prompts per batch = 64,000 generations), but this number is never compared to the difficulty estimation budget. There is no experiment investigating whether a smaller number of samples (e.g., pass@16 or pass@32) would suffice to identify `D_hard` with acceptable recall, or whether difficulty estimation could be integrated into training (e.g., starting with a tentative `D_hard` and refining it online).

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not discuss the cost of difficulty estimation, propose cheaper alternatives, or amortize the cost into any reported metric. This is a significant practical gap because the difficulty estimation step is the most computationally expensive phase of the entire pipeline for any new training dataset, yet its cost is invisible in the reported results. A practitioner replicating Cog-DRIFT on a new dataset would need to budget for this substantial upfront compute, which the paper's framing as a lightweight, self-contained method does not prepare them for.

### All Experiments Are on a Single Task Family (Math/Science Reasoning) and Two Small Model Families, with No Demonstration of Cross-Domain Transfer

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper evaluates Cog-DRIFT exclusively on mathematical and scientific reasoning benchmarks (BigMath-Hard, OmniMATH-Hard, AIME, GPQA, Date Understanding, MATH500) using two models from different families but the same scale (3B–4B parameters). The authors state they "believe this model is representative of the capabilities of many contemporary LLMs" (Section 5, Models paragraph), but this claim is unverified across model scales, architectures, or task domains. The paper's entire reformulation framework—multiple-choice and cloze formats with deterministic verification—depends on tasks having unambiguous, verifiable answers. The three criteria for ideal reformulations (Takeaway 1: answer preservation, reduced difficulty, transferability) were validated only for math problems with numeric or simple symbolic answers.

**The consequence.** At least three generalization gaps are unexamined. First, **task domain:** it is unclear whether the reformulation approach transfers to reasoning tasks where answers are not easily converted to MCQ/cloze formats—for example, code generation (where the "answer" is a program), formal theorem proving (where the answer is a proof sketch), or multi-step planning (where the answer is an action sequence). The MCQ format in particular requires generating plausible distractors; for code or proofs, this is substantially harder than for numeric answers, and incorrect distractors may be trivially identifiable, defeating the purpose of the format. Second, **model scale:** larger models (7B, 13B, 70B+) would have higher base pass@k on math problems, meaning fewer problems meet the pass@64=0 criterion and those that do are genuinely harder. The dynamics of the curriculum—and the relative benefit of reformulation over simply running more GRPO rollouts—may differ qualitatively at larger scales where the exploration problem is less severe. The paper's claims about learning from "unsolvable" problems are anchored to the capabilities of 3B–4B models; a 70B model may find pass@64=0 problems so rare that the entire reformulation pipeline is unnecessary or the remaining problems are too hard even for reformulation to help. Third, **model architecture/family:** while Qwen and Llama show "different behaviors in post-training" (Shao et al., 2025), they are both dense transformer models trained on similar corpora. Models with different architectural properties (mixture-of-experts, different tokenizers, different pretraining objectives) might respond differently to format reformulation, and the finding that Qwen is robust to data quality while Llama is sensitive (Table 3) already hints at substantial model-specific variation that two models cannot fully characterize.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides no cross-domain experiments—all six evaluation benchmarks are math, science, or logic tasks with verifiable answers. The models are both in the 3B–4B range, with no scaling analysis. The paper does not address whether reformulation generation quality degrades for answer types other than numeric values, or whether the MCQ distractor generation prompt (Appendix C) generalizes. The authors' statement about model representativeness is asserted, not demonstrated.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not discuss domain generalizability as a limitation, does not propose how the reformulation templates would extend to non-math domains, and does not test on larger models. The three criteria in Takeaway 1 are presented as a general framework for evaluating reformulations, which is a step toward cross-domain applicability, but the criteria were only validated on math problems and the paper provides no guidance on how they would be operationalized in other domains (e.g., what constitutes a "plausible distractor" for a code generation problem?). This is the most significant limitation for practitioners considering Cog-DRIFT for non-math reasoning tasks—the entire evaluation is within a single task family, and extrapolation to other domains is purely speculative.

### The Instance-Level Curriculum Is Monotonic (Promotion Only) and the Promotion Threshold Is Not Ablated

**The assumption or constraint.** The curriculum's promotion rule (Section 4) advances an instance from difficulty level `d_i` to `d_i + 1` when its empirical accuracy `â_i` on the current format reaches or exceeds `τ = 0.5`. The curriculum is strictly monotonic: once an instance is promoted, it never returns to a lower difficulty level, regardless of whether its accuracy on the new, harder format drops below `τ`. The paper adopts `τ = 0.5` following prior work (Chen et al., 2025b; Foster et al., 2025) but does not ablate this value. The empirical accuracy is estimated from only `m = 8` rollouts, making it a noisy estimator whose variance is not reported or analyzed.

**The consequence.** Three potential failure modes arise from the monotonic, fixed-threshold design. First, **premature promotion with regression:** when an instance advances from 4-choice MCQ to 10-choice MCQ, its accuracy on the new format may drop substantially—potentially to near zero—because the model had only borderline mastery of the easier format (accuracy just barely above 50%) and the harder format is genuinely more difficult. The model then trains on the harder format with little to no reward signal, wasting compute and potentially unlearning whatever reasoning it had begun to acquire. The paper does not report whether accuracy drops after promotion, or investigate whether allowing downward transitions (returning to a lower difficulty level when performance on the current level is poor) would improve learning stability. Second, **threshold sensitivity:** a lower `τ` (e.g., 0.25) would cause faster promotion, potentially advancing instances before genuine mastery and exacerbating the regression problem. A higher `τ` (e.g., 0.75) would ensure mastery but delay exposure to harder formats, potentially wasting compute on over-practiced easy problems. The paper provides no evidence that 0.5 is near-optimal or that performance is robust to threshold variation. Third, **noisy promotion decisions:** with `m = 8` rollouts, the empirical accuracy `â_i` can only take nine discrete values (0/8, 1/8, ..., 8/8). The threshold `τ = 0.5` corresponds to `â_i ≥ 4/8`. A single lucky or unlucky rollout can flip the promotion decision for borderline instances, introducing stochasticity into the curriculum trajectory that the paper does not quantify or discuss.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figure 4 shows the aggregate curriculum dynamics—proportions of each format over time—but does not report per-instance accuracy trajectories before and after promotion, does not measure how often promoted instances regress, and does not evaluate whether non-monotonic curricula (allowing demotion) would perform differently. The `τ = 0.5` choice is cited from prior work without ablation. The only indirect evidence for the curriculum's robustness is that it outperforms the static uniform mixture (Figure 4), but this comparison does not isolate the effects of the specific threshold value or the monotonicity constraint—it only shows that *some* adaptive schedule beats *no* schedule.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not acknowledge the monotonicity constraint or the unablated threshold as limitations. The curriculum design choices are presented as following established practice without investigation of alternatives. A practitioner implementing Cog-DRIFT would need to either trust the `τ = 0.5` default without evidence or run their own ablation, which the paper does not guide. The interaction between the small rollout count (`m = 8`) and the discrete promotion rule is not discussed, leaving open the possibility that observed curriculum dynamics are partially driven by noise rather than genuine learning progress.

### The Reformulation Generation Quality and Its Dependence on the Base Model Are Not Evaluated

**The assumption or constraint.** Cog-DRIFT relies on the same model being trained (Qwen3-4B-Instruct) to generate reformulations of hard problems into MCQ and cloze formats, using the prompts in Appendix C. These reformulations are then verified by deterministic scripts (Section 4: checking that the gold answer appears among MCQ options, that distractors are unique, that cloze masks are non-trivial and consistent). However, the paper does not report the fraction of reformulations that pass verification (the rejection rate), does not evaluate the *quality* of verified reformulations beyond binary correctness checks (e.g., whether MCQ distractors are genuinely plausible or trivially distinguishable), and does not investigate whether reformulation quality varies with problem difficulty or problem type.

**The consequence.** If the rejection rate is high, then many hard problems fail to produce usable reformulations, reducing the effective size of the training set below the reported 958 problems and potentially introducing selection bias (problems that survive reformulation may be systematically different from those that don't). More subtly, even verified reformulations can be low-quality in ways the verification scripts don't catch. For MCQ: distractors could be implausible (e.g., off by orders of magnitude, wrong sign, wrong units), making the 4-choice task trivially easy not because the model reasons better but because the wrong answers are obviously wrong. For cloze: the mask could reveal too much structure (e.g., masking `101` as `1__` essentially gives away the answer to any model that knows the number has two digits after the 1), making the task a pattern-completion exercise rather than a reasoning scaffold. The paper's verification checks (gold answer present, distractors unique, mask non-trivial and consistent) do not assess these quality dimensions. If reformulation quality is poor, the learning signal from reformulated problems may teach format-specific heuristics ("pick the number that seems most reasonable among oddly specific distractors") rather than transferable reasoning, undermining the entire transfer rationale.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides no data on reformulation rejection rates, no examples of rejected reformulations, and no quality analysis of accepted reformulations beyond the binary verification checks. The prompts in Appendix C are provided, but there is no discussion of failure modes in the generation process. The empirical results (Figure 2, Table 1) show that reformulation-based training does transfer, which provides indirect evidence that reformulation quality is sufficient for learning, but this does not establish that reformulation quality is *good*—it could be mediocre while still outperforming the zero-reward baseline. A practitioner cannot determine from the paper whether the reformulation prompts in Appendix C would work as-is on their own model and dataset, or whether prompt engineering and quality filtering would be necessary.

**Mitigation status.** Partially addressed through the verification scripts, which catch the most egregious failures (wrong answer in options, ambiguous options, trivial masks). However, the verification criteria are necessary but not sufficient for high-quality reformulations. The paper does not discuss whether more sophisticated quality checks (e.g., using the PRM to score distractor plausibility, or measuring the difficulty reduction of each reformulation individually) would improve results. The fact that the Silver Answer Filter already removes 89% of the initial `D_hard` suggests that data quality is a dominant factor in this setting, making the omission of reformulation quality analysis more consequential—if reformulations of the remaining 958 problems are also noisy, the effective training signal may be even sparser than the dataset size implies.

### Test Set Sizes Are Small and No Statistical Significance Is Reported, Making the Reliability of the Claimed Margins Uncertain

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper evaluates on six benchmarks with test set sizes ranging from 30 problems (AIME24, AIME25) to 452 problems (OmniMATH-Hard). The primary claims of improvement—+4.72% average for Qwen, +3.23% for Llama over the second-best baseline—are based on averages across these benchmarks with no reported confidence intervals, standard deviations, or hypothesis tests. For the smallest test sets (AIME with 30 problems), a single additional correct answer changes the reported accuracy by 3.33 percentage points. For BigMath-Hard (192 problems), each correctly answered problem changes accuracy by ~0.52 percentage points. The paper averages over repeated evaluation runs (16 for AIME, 3 for others) to reduce variance, but does not report the variance of those runs.

**The consequence.** Without variance estimates, the reader cannot assess whether the reported margins are statistically reliable or consistent with sampling noise. Consider the Qwen AIME24 results in Table 1: Cog-DRIFT achieves 51.74% vs. 46.92% for zero-shot—a difference of +4.82 percentage points. On 30 problems, this corresponds to approximately 1.4 additional correctly answered questions (51.74% of 30 ≈ 15.5; 46.92% of 30 ≈ 14.1). With 16 evaluation runs, the standard error of the mean depends on the run-to-run variance, which is not reported. If the standard deviation across runs is, say, 5 percentage points (one additional correct answer per run), then the standard error of the mean is 5/√16 ≈ 1.25 percentage points, and the 4.82-point difference is roughly 3.9 standard errors—likely significant but not overwhelmingly so. If the standard deviation is larger (e.g., 8 points), significance weakens. The paper provides no basis for making this assessment. The problem is most acute for the headline average improvement metric: this is an average of differences across benchmarks with very different test set sizes and variances. Weighting each benchmark equally in the average ignores that some benchmarks (AIME, 30 problems) have much higher sampling variance than others (OmniMATH-Hard, 452 problems; Date Understanding, 369 problems), making the average a noisy statistic whose variance is a complex function of the per-benchmark variances.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper reports that results are "averaged over 16 runs for AIME, and 3 runs for other datasets" (Section 5, Implementation Details), but does not provide standard deviations, standard errors, confidence intervals, or significance tests for any result in Table 1 or Figure 3. The paper provides no discussion of statistical power, minimum detectable effect sizes, or the reliability of rankings when margins are small. For several comparisons where Cog-DRIFT is not the best method (Llama on AIME25: Cog-DRIFT 0.42% vs. NuRL Abstract 1.11%—a difference of 0.69 percentage points, or less than one problem on a 30-question test), it is impossible to determine whether the observed difference is meaningful.

**Mitigation status.** Partially addressed by using multiple evaluation runs for the smallest test sets (16 runs for AIME reduces the standard error of the mean compared to a single run), but the variance of those runs is not reported, so the reader cannot verify that the larger number of runs is sufficient to make the reported means reliable. The paper does not acknowledge the small test set size or the absence of statistical reporting as limitations. For a practitioner comparing Cog-DRIFT against baselines, the inability to assess whether observed differences are within the noise floor makes it difficult to determine which results would replicate and which are artifacts of the particular evaluation runs.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

Cog-DRIFT proposes a **conceptual reframing of the hard-problem learning bottleneck under RLVR** rather than a new learning algorithm or architectural innovation. The shift is from viewing the barrier as a capability problem—the model lacks knowledge needed to solve hard problems—to viewing it as an exploration problem—the model possesses relevant latent knowledge but cannot access it because the output space is too vast and reward signals too sparse. This reframing carries three cascading implications for how the field approaches RL post-training.

**First, it redirects attention from algorithm design to problem representation.** Prior work on hard-problem learning focused overwhelmingly on improving the learning algorithm: better exploration bonuses, interleaved SFT phases, experience replay, expert-guided prefixes. Each approach accepted the open-ended output format as fixed and tried to make the optimization landscape more navigable. Cog-DRIFT's results suggest that modifying the problem representation itself—structurally constraining the output space through format reformulation—can achieve larger gains (10.11% on BigMath-Hard for Qwen, vs. 7.73% for the best hint-based baseline) with a simpler training pipeline (standard GRPO, no expert trajectories, no privileged prefixes). This is a **methodological shift** toward treating problem representation as a first-class design dimension in RLVR, comparable in importance to reward shaping or advantage estimation. The paper's three criteria for ideal reformulations (Takeaway 1: answer preservation, reduced difficulty, transferability) provide a concrete evaluation framework that makes this design space systematic rather than ad-hoc.

**Second, it establishes the discriminative-to-generative spectrum as a principled axis for curriculum construction.** Curriculum learning in LLM post-training has been dominated by difficulty-sorting: order problems from easy to hard based on estimated pass@k or similar metrics. Cog-DRIFT introduces an orthogonal axis: for the *same problem*, vary the output format from recognition (MCQ) to constrained generation (cloze) to free generation (open-ended). This matters because difficulty-sorting faces a fundamental limitation at the frontier of model capability—the hardest problems remain unsolvable regardless of ordering. Format-based curricula circumvent this by making the hard problems easier to solve, then progressively restoring difficulty. Figure 4's demonstration that the adaptive format curriculum continues improving while a static mixture plateaus provides empirical evidence that this axis is not merely a curiosity but a genuine lever for sample efficiency. This opens a new design dimension for curriculum learning that is complementary to, and can be combined with, traditional difficulty-sorting—future curricula could operate on both axes simultaneously (sort problems by difficulty *and* vary format within each problem).

**Third, it provides diagnostic evidence in the "distribution sharpening vs. new capability" debate.** A central question in the RLVR literature is whether RL post-training teaches models genuinely new reasoning skills or merely amplifies high-reward paths already present in the pretrained distribution. The pass@k results in Figure 3 provide concrete evidence for the "new capability" side: Cog-DRIFT increases pass@128 on AIME2024 by +3.33 percentage points over the base model, indicating that correct solutions that were previously inaccessible (below the sampling threshold at k=128) become reachable. This is not conclusive—the paper cannot distinguish between "learned new concepts from the hard problems" and "learned to use existing concepts more efficiently when searching"—but it shifts the burden of proof. If RLVR were purely distribution sharpening, training on problems where pass@64=0 should produce no improvement in pass@k at any k, since there are no correct solutions in the distribution to amplify. Cog-DRIFT's pass@k gains demonstrate that formulation-based training *can* make previously unreachable correct solutions accessible, a result that sharpening-only hypotheses must explain away (e.g., as improved search within existing knowledge rather than new knowledge acquisition).

**Reconciling prior contradictions.** The paper does not directly resolve contradictions between prior studies—unlike the compute-optimal scaling paper that reconciled conflicting findings about self-correction by introducing difficulty as a moderating variable—but it does provide a unifying explanation for why methods like POPE (Qu et al., 2025, expert prefixes) and NuRL (Chen et al., 2025a, self-generated hints) show modest gains on hard problems (1–4% solvability rates) while Cog-DRIFT reaches 18.9%. The explanation is structural: hints and prefixes operate within the original open-ended output space, nudging exploration but not fundamentally reducing the search problem. Reformulation collapses the output space itself, directly attacking the exploration bottleneck. This implies that prior methods were not "wrong" but were operating on the wrong lever—they tried to guide the model through a vast space rather than reducing the space's size. The research direction that becomes *more* attractive after this work is problem representation design: for any task with verifiable answers, what are the minimal structural constraints that preserve reasoning while reducing search complexity? The direction that becomes *less* attractive (or at least, is shown to have a lower ceiling) is purely algorithmic exploration improvements within unconstrained output spaces—if the space is exponentially large and the reward is sparse, no amount of clever exploration will match the efficiency gain from simply making the space smaller.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Non-monotonic curricula with promotion and demotion.** The current curriculum is strictly monotonic: once an instance advances to a harder format, it never returns to an easier one, even if accuracy on the new format drops to near zero. This design choice was not ablated or justified. A natural follow-up would implement a **bi-directional curriculum** where instances can be demoted if their accuracy on the current format falls below a lower threshold (e.g., `â_i < 0.25`) and re-promoted when they recover. The key metric would be whether non-monotonic curricula prevent the potential failure mode of premature promotion—instances that barely crossed `τ = 0.5` and then collapse on the harder format, wasting subsequent training steps on problems with zero reward signal. The experiment would compare monotonic Cog-DRIFT against a bi-directional variant on the same 958-problem dataset, measuring: (a) final accuracy on BigMath-Hard, (b) training stability (variance of per-instance accuracy over time), and (c) the fraction of instances that experience accuracy collapse after promotion. If bi-directional curricula improve stability without sacrificing final performance, this would refine the curriculum design and address a gap the current paper leaves open.

**Reformulation quality analysis and prompt engineering.** The paper uses fixed prompts (Appendix C) to generate reformulations and applies binary verification checks (gold answer present in options, distractors unique, cloze mask non-trivial), but never evaluates reformulation *quality* beyond these checks. A critical follow-up would systematically characterize reformulation quality and its impact on downstream learning. The experiment would: (a) manually annotate a sample of ~200 reformulations for distractor plausibility (are wrong answers genuinely misleading or trivially wrong?) and cloze mask informativeness (does the mask provide too much or too little structure?), (b) correlate quality scores with per-instance learning trajectories (do high-quality reformulations lead to faster promotion and better transfer?), and (c) test whether prompt engineering—varying the distractor generation instructions, adjusting the mask density target—improves reformulation quality and downstream performance. This would answer whether the reformulation prompts in Appendix C are near-optimal or whether substantial gains remain from better prompt design. A negative result (reformulation quality varies widely but does not correlate with learning outcomes) would suggest that the verification checks are sufficient and that the format itself, not the specific distractors, drives the benefit—a finding with practical implications for deployment simplicity.

**Reformulation without external filtering: self-consistency-based data cleaning.** The paper's dependence on GPT-5.4 for the Silver Answer Filter is a practical barrier to self-contained deployment. A follow-up would replace the external filter with a **self-consistency filter** that uses only the learner model. The approach: for each problem in the initial `D_hard` (pass@64=0 from Qwen), generate an additional 64 samples at high temperature, cluster the final answers (ignoring reasoning chains), and compute the agreement rate of the modal answer. Problems where the modal answer receives high agreement (e.g., ≥50% of samples produce the same answer) but that answer is *incorrect* are likely well-posed problems the model consistently gets wrong—these are the "hard but solvable" problems Cog-DRIFT targets. Problems with low agreement (the model produces many different answers) are more likely to be ill-posed or ambiguous. The experiment would correlate self-consistency metrics with the Silver Answer Filter's judgments on the 8,922-problem initial set, measure the precision/recall tradeoff at different agreement thresholds, and train Cog-DRIFT on the self-consistency-filtered set to compare against the Silver-filtered results in Table 1. This would determine whether Cog-DRIFT can be made fully self-contained—a prerequisite for deployment in settings where no stronger model is available.

**Cross-domain extension to code generation.** The paper evaluates exclusively on math and science reasoning, but the reformulation framework should extend to any domain with verifiable answers. Code generation is the most natural next domain: problems have unambiguous correctness (unit tests), answers can be formulated as multiple-choice (select the correct code snippet among distractors) or cloze (fill in masked portions of the solution), and the search space for generating correct programs from scratch is enormous—analogous to the exploration problem in math. The experiment would: (a) construct `D_hard` for code generation using the same pass@64=0 methodology on HumanEval or MBPP, (b) design reformulation prompts for MCQ (the model selects the correct implementation among 4–10 options) and cloze (the model fills in masked function bodies), (c) adapt the verification scripts to check functional equivalence via unit tests rather than string matching, and (d) train Cog-DRIFT and compare against GRPO and NuRL-style hint baselines. The key question is whether the three criteria in Takeaway 1—answer preservation, reduced difficulty, transferability—hold for code as they do for math. Code has richer structure than numeric answers (variable names, control flow, API usage), which may make distractor generation harder (plausible but incorrect code snippets may be functionally equivalent to the correct answer in edge cases) and cloze masking more complex (which parts of a program are meaningfully maskable?). A failure to transfer would reveal domain-specific constraints on the reformulation paradigm; a success would substantially broaden Cog-DRIFT's applicability claim.

**Scaling analysis: does reformulation benefit persist for larger models?** Both evaluated models are in the 3B–4B parameter range. At larger scales (7B, 13B, 70B), base pass@64 on math problems will be higher, meaning fewer problems meet the pass@64=0 criterion, and those that do are genuinely harder—possibly too hard even for reformulation to help. A scaling study would apply Cog-DRIFT to a model family with available checkpoints at multiple scales (e.g., Llama 3.2 at 3B, 8B, 70B, or Qwen3 at 4B, 8B, 32B), construct `D_hard` independently for each scale, and measure: (a) the fraction of the initial dataset that survives pass@64=0 filtering at each scale, (b) the fraction of those that survive the Silver Answer Filter (testing whether "genuinely hard" problems become rarer or more common at larger scales), (c) the absolute gain from Cog-DRIFT over GRPO on the filtered `D_hard` at each scale, and (d) whether the pass@k improvement pattern in Figure 3 persists or diminishes. The prediction from the paper's exploration-bottleneck hypothesis is that reformulation benefit should *decrease* with model scale, because larger models have higher base pass@k and thus less severe exploration problems—the very condition that makes reformulation valuable (vast output space, sparse rewards) becomes less acute. A finding that reformulation benefit is constant or *increases* with scale would suggest a different mechanism (perhaps reformulation helps with representation learning, not just exploration) and would motivate deployment at all scales. A finding that benefit decreases and becomes negligible at, say, 70B would bound the applicability of the method to the small-model regime, which is still practically valuable (edge deployment, cost-sensitive applications) but narrows the paper's claims.

**Ablation of the promotion threshold τ with adaptive threshold methods.** The paper adopts `τ = 0.5` from prior work without ablation. A follow-up would systematically vary `τ` across a range (e.g., 0.25, 0.375, 0.5, 0.625, 0.75) and measure final BigMath-Hard accuracy and sample efficiency (steps to reach a given accuracy). The hypothesis is a U-shaped curve: too low a threshold causes premature promotion and wasted compute on too-hard formats; too high a threshold causes over-training on easy formats. Beyond static threshold sweeping, an adaptive threshold method—where `τ` starts low and increases as training progresses, or where `τ` is set per-instance based on the difficulty gap between current and next format—could be tested. The metric would be whether any threshold schedule outperforms the fixed `τ = 0.5` default. A null result (all reasonable thresholds perform similarly) would validate the paper's unexamined default and simplify deployment; a large effect would provide an immediate, low-cost improvement to Cog-DRIFT's efficiency.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Training data augmentation for small-model deployment.** Organizations deploying small language models (3B–8B parameters) for cost-sensitive or edge applications face a hard ceiling: RLVR post-training on difficult problems yields diminishing returns because the model cannot generate positive reward signals. Cog-DRIFT provides a drop-in pipeline that takes existing hard training problems, reformats them into MCQ and cloze variants using the same small model (no external teacher needed, assuming the self-consistency filter from the follow-up research above is validated), and trains with an adaptive curriculum. The concrete benefit: on BigMath-Hard, Cog-DRIFT takes Llama3.2-3B from 0% to 8.64% accuracy while simultaneously improving held-out GPQA performance by +8.02 points over zero-shot (15.32% → 23.34%). For a math tutoring application or a scientific Q&A system running on-device, this translates to directly measurable accuracy gains on the hardest user queries without increasing model size or inference latency. The 958-problem filtered training set is small enough that the reformulation generation and training pipeline could run on commodity hardware (4 A6000 GPUs, as used in the paper) in hours to days, making it feasible for periodic retraining cycles.

**Bootstrapping self-improvement loops without external supervision.** Self-improvement pipelines—where a model generates training data, filters for quality, and retrains on the filtered data—are attractive for reducing dependence on human annotation, but they break down when the model cannot generate any correct solutions for the hardest problems (the bootstrap fails because there is nothing to filter). Cog-DRIFT provides a bootstrap mechanism: even when the model scores 0% on open-ended hard problems, it can generate reformulations, solve a fraction of them (33.8% on 4-choice MCQ for problems with pass@64=0), and use those correct trajectories to improve. The improved model then has higher pass@k on the original open-ended problems, potentially crossing the threshold where self-improvement becomes viable. A concrete pipeline: (1) identify `D_hard` via pass@64 on the current model, (2) apply Cog-DRIFT to learn from `D_hard`, (3) use the improved model to generate open-ended solutions on `D_hard`, (4) filter for correctness, (5) SFT on the correct open-ended solutions, (6) repeat. The paper's finding that pass@128 improves (Figure 3) suggests that the improved model does generate more correct solutions that could be harvested for SFT data, closing the self-improvement loop.

**Cost-efficient construction of hard-problem training sets.** The paper's Silver Answer Filter reveals that 89% of problems identified as "hard" by pass@64=0 are actually data errors (incomplete questions, wrong answers, missing figures). This finding has immediate practical implications for any team constructing RLVR training datasets: **pass@k-based hardness estimation conflates model incapability with data corruption, and the two must be disentangled before training.** The recommended protocol—use a strong model (or multi-model ensemble) to validate answer correctness and question completeness on the pass@k=0 subset before including it in training—is a quality-control step that the paper shows can improve downstream performance by 8+ percentage points on MATH500 for Llama (32.2% Silver-filtered vs. 24.2% unfiltered). Even for teams not adopting Cog-DRIFT's full reformulation pipeline, this diagnostic finding—that most "hard" problems are actually corrupt—should change how RLVR datasets are curated. The cost of filtering (GPT-5.4 API calls on 8,922 problems) is small relative to the cost of training on corrupted data (wasted GPU-hours and degraded model performance), making this a high-return investment for any RLVR training run.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper positions Cog-DRIFT against standard GRPO on hard problems and against hint-based exploration methods (NuRL), but does not articulate a formal decision framework with explicit conditions. The empirical results do, however, imply clear boundary conditions that a practitioner should use to decide whether Cog-DRIFT is appropriate:

**Prefer Cog-DRIFT when:**

- **The training set contains problems with pass@64 ≈ 0 on the current model**, and those problems survive data quality filtering (Silver Answer Filter or equivalent), indicating they are genuinely hard but well-posed. The method's primary value proposition is unlocking learning from exactly these problems. If pass@64 is substantially above zero on most problems, standard GRPO already provides learning signal, and the overhead of reformulation generation may not be justified.

- **The model is in the small-to-medium size range (3B–8B parameters)**, where the exploration problem is most severe. Larger models with higher base pass@k may benefit less, though the cross-over point is not established in the paper.

- **The task domain has unambiguous, verifiable answers** that can be straightforwardly converted to MCQ and cloze formats (numeric answers, short text spans, structured outputs). The verification scripts for reformulation quality depend on exact answer matching; domains with fuzzy or subjective correctness criteria (summarization, creative writing) are incompatible with the current framework.

- **External strong models are available for data filtering** or a self-consistency–based alternative has been validated. Without filtering, performance on data-sensitive models like Llama degrades substantially (Table 3), eroding Cog-DRIFT's advantage.

- **Sample efficiency during training matters more than upfront difficulty estimation cost.** The pass@64 estimation requires 2.4M generations for 37.5k candidate problems, which is ~37× the training budget. If training compute is scarce but inference compute for difficulty estimation is abundant, Cog-DRIFT's improved sample efficiency during training (Figure 4) may not outweigh the upfront cost.

**Prefer standard GRPO or hint-based methods when:**

- **The model already achieves non-trivial pass@k on the training problems.** Cog-DRIFT's reformulation overhead is wasted if the model can already learn from the original format.

- **No data filtering is available and the model is sensitive to data quality.** The paper's results for unfiltered Llama (24.2% MATH500 vs. 32.2% Silver-filtered) suggest that training Cog-DRIFT on noisy data may underperform simpler baselines trained on cleaner data.

- **Latency or engineering complexity of multi-format training is prohibitive.** Cog-DRIFT requires reformulation generation, verification scripting, and curriculum tracking infrastructure that add engineering overhead compared to running standard GRPO on the original dataset.
