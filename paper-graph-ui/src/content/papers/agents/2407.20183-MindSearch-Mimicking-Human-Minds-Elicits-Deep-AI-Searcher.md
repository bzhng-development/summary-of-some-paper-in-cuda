# MindSearch: Mimicking Human Minds Elicits Deep AI Searcher

**ArXiv:** [2407.20183](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.20183)

## 🎯 Pitch

This paper introduces **MindSearch**, an LLM-based multi-agent framework for complex web information seeking and integration that models human problem-solving as a dynamic graph construction process.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **MindSearch**, an LLM-based multi-agent framework for complex web information seeking and integration that models human problem-solving as a dynamic graph construction process. The system consists of a **WebPlanner**—which decomposes user queries into atomic sub-questions as nodes in a directed acyclic graph and progressively extends the graph via Python code generation (planning as coding)—and multiple **WebSearcher** agents, each performing hierarchical information retrieval with search engines (coarse-to-fine page selection with multi-query generation) to collect and summarize relevant content for their assigned sub-questions. Evaluated on both closed-set QA tasks (Bamboogle, Musique, HotpotQA) and open-set human-curated queries using GPT-4o and InternLM2.5-7B models, MindSearch achieves a 6.3% absolute improvement on closed-set QA with the open-source model over a ReAct search baseline while demonstrating that its responses are preferred by human evaluators over ChatGPT-Web and Perplexity.ai Pro in 70–83% of cases across depth, breadth, and factuality dimensions. The multi-agent design processes information from more than 300 web pages in under 3 minutes—a workload requiring roughly 3 hours of human effort—establishing that structured decomposition into parallelizable search tasks enables open-source LLMs to deliver competitive AI search engine solutions comparable to proprietary alternatives.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Information Seeking and Integration at Human-Level Complexity

The fundamental problem MindSearch addresses is deceptively simple to state but extraordinarily complex to solve: **how do you build a system that can take a nuanced, multi-faceted human question, search the open web to gather relevant information spread across dozens or hundreds of pages, filter out the noise, reason about how the pieces fit together, and produce a coherent, accurate, well-structured answer?**

This is what the authors call "information seeking and integration"—a cognitive task that humans perform routinely (a research analyst preparing a competitive landscape report, a journalist investigating a complex story, a student writing a term paper) but that consumes "enormous human efforts and time." The paper frames this as a problem that sits at the intersection of multiple capabilities: query decomposition, strategic search, relevance filtering, cross-document synthesis, and structured response generation. Each of these capabilities is challenging on its own; combining them into a single pipeline that works reliably across diverse, open-ended queries is substantially harder.

The authors ground their problem statement in a concrete observation about existing tools. Search engines, from their inception (Brin & Page, 1998) to modern deployments, fundamentally operate as **document retrieval systems**: you type keywords, you get a ranked list of links. They are extraordinarily effective at *retrieval* but provide no *integration*. If your question requires synthesizing information from ten different sources, reconciling contradictions, and drawing inferences that aren't explicitly stated in any single document, a traditional search engine leaves all of that cognitive work to the human user.

Large Language Models, conversely, are excellent at synthesis, reasoning, and generating fluent, structured text—but they "struggl[e] to deliver accurate knowledge in responses" (Section 1, citing Ji et al., 2023; Gu et al., 2024). LLMs are trained on static corpora with knowledge cutoffs; they cannot access current information, verify claims against authoritative sources, or retrieve domain-specific details that fall outside their training distribution. The problem of factual hallucination is well-documented and fundamentally limits the trustworthiness of pure LLM responses for knowledge-intensive tasks.

The authors identify this **complementary capability gap** as the core opportunity: "The complementary advantages of LLMs and search engines highlights a compelling opportunity for their combination, where the reasoning prowess of LLMs can be complemented by the extensive web information accessible via search engines." The challenge lies in how to combine them effectively—and this is where prior approaches fall short.

### Why This Problem Matters: Practical and Theoretical Significance

**Practical significance.** The paper argues that solving complex information seeking and integration has direct economic and societal value. The authors provide a concrete efficiency metric: MindSearch processes information from more than 300 web pages in under 3 minutes, a workload that "could take human experts about 3 hours to finish a similar cognitive workload" (Section 2.3; see also Appendix B for detailed time measurements: human labelers spent an average of 47 minutes searching and reading plus 74 minutes writing per question, approximately 121 minutes total). This 60× efficiency improvement over human effort—if it generalizes beyond the evaluated domains—has enormous implications for knowledge workers across industries: market research, competitive intelligence, scientific literature review, legal research, medical case analysis, policy analysis, and any domain where professionals spend substantial time gathering and synthesizing information from distributed sources.

Beyond the time savings, the quality dimensions matter equally. The paper evaluates responses along three axes—**depth** (thoroughness, addressing intricacies), **breadth** (covering multiple perspectives and related fields), and **factuality** (accuracy, grounding in reliable data)—and argues that AI-powered search systems can potentially exceed human performance on the first two dimensions by systematically exploring more sources than a human would have time to read. The fact that human evaluators prefer MindSearch's responses to those from commercial products (ChatGPT-Web, Perplexity.ai Pro) on 70–83% of queries (Figure 4) is a strong signal that the system is producing genuinely useful outputs, not just faster ones.

**Theoretical significance.** From a research perspective, the information seeking and integration problem serves as a stress test for several active areas in LLM research. It requires:

- **Multi-step planning and decomposition**: breaking an abstract, compound question into atomic sub-questions that can be independently researched.
- **Parallel and sequential reasoning management**: understanding which sub-questions depend on answers to previous sub-questions (sequential) and which can be pursued simultaneously (parallel).
- **Long-context management**: processing information from dozens or hundreds of web pages without hitting context window limits or suffering from the "lost in the middle" phenomenon that degrades LLM performance on long inputs.
- **Noise filtering and relevance assessment**: distinguishing useful information from the "massive information noise" present on the open web (Section 1, challenge 2).
- **Cross-document synthesis and conflict resolution**: reconciling contradictory information from different sources and building a coherent narrative.

Progress on this problem therefore advances multiple subfields simultaneously, and failures provide clear diagnostic signals about which capabilities remain bottlenecked.

### Where Prior Approaches Fall Short

The paper identifies three specific failure modes in how previous work has attempted to combine LLMs with search engines. These are not merely implementation issues—they represent fundamental architectural limitations that arise from treating the problem as simple retrieve-then-read rather than as a multi-step reasoning task.

#### Failure Mode 1: Single-Shot Retrieval Cannot Handle Complex Queries

The most basic approach—and the one implicitly adopted by many commercial AI search products—is to take the user's question, issue a single search query (possibly after some rewording), retrieve the top N results, feed them into the LLM's context window, and generate an answer. The authors argue this fails for precisely the reason that information seeking is cognitively complex: **"complex requests often cannot be accurately and completely retrieved by the search engine once"** (Section 1, challenge 1).

A user query like "How has China's Chang'e-6 mission contributed to international space exploration, and how does it compare to Apollo 11?" (Section 2, Figure 1 example; also Appendix F) cannot be answered from a single search results page. It requires researching: (1) the technical details of Chang'e-6, (2) the specific challenges of lunar far-side missions, (3) how each challenge was overcome, (4) the details of Apollo 11, (5) points of comparison between the two missions, and (6) China's broader role in international space collaboration. A single search query—even if well-constructed—will return results that are relevant to only a subset of these information needs, likely missing important dimensions entirely. Prior approaches that "simply treat the information seeking and integration task as a vanilla retrieve-augmented generation (RAG) task" (Section 1) inherit this fundamental limitation: RAG assumes a single retrieval step is sufficient to gather the necessary context.

The authors cite Self-RAG (Asai et al., 2023) and RQ-RAG (Chan et al., 2024) as representative examples of retrieval-augmented approaches, noting that they obtain "sub-optimal performance due to a superficial engagement with the depth and complexity of web-based information retrieval."

#### Failure Mode 2: Information Overload and Noise Overwhelms LLM Context Windows

Even if a system *could* identify all the relevant web pages for a complex query, feeding them directly into the LLM's context window creates a second-order problem: **"corresponding information to be integrated is spread over multiple web pages along with massive noise"** (Section 1, challenge 2). A typical web search for a moderately complex topic returns hundreds of results—many of which are partially relevant, outdated, contradictory, or low-quality. The useful information is distributed across dozens of pages, each containing substantial amounts of irrelevant content (navigation elements, advertisements, tangentially related discussions, boilerplate text).

Simply concatenating all retrieved pages and passing them to the LLM creates two problems: the model wastes capacity processing irrelevant content, and the signal-to-noise ratio degrades as more pages are added. The authors note this specifically as challenge 3: **"a large number of web pages with long contents may quickly exceed the maximum context length of LLMs"** (Section 1). Even with modern LLMs that support 128K+ token context windows, processing hundreds of full web pages would exceed practical limits, and even if it didn't, LLM performance on long-context reasoning degrades with input length—the model struggles to attend to relevant information buried in a sea of text.

The implication is that **intelligent filtering and summarization must happen *before* the LLM's integration step**, not as part of it. Prior RAG approaches that rely on the LLM to do both retrieval reading and synthesis in one pass place an unreasonable burden on the model's context processing capabilities.

#### Failure Mode 3: No Structured Reasoning About Information Dependencies

The most subtle failure mode is that prior approaches lack any explicit model of how pieces of information relate to each other. When a human researches a complex question, they don't just amass a pile of relevant documents—they build a mental model of how sub-questions depend on each other. You need to understand the Chang'e-6 relay satellite system before you can evaluate how the communication challenges were overcome; you need to know both missions' technical details before you can compare them meaningfully.

The authors argue that existing LLM+search frameworks lack this **topological reasoning** capability. A ReAct-style agent (Yao et al., 2022b), for instance, interleaves reasoning steps with tool calls, but the reasoning is linear—each step conditions on the previous step's result—without explicit modeling of parallelizable sub-tasks. This leads to inefficiency (sequential execution of tasks that could be parallelized) and potential reasoning errors (the model may lose track of how sub-findings relate to each other when processed in a flat sequence). The paper frames this as a limitation of "prompting the LLM to plan the entire data workflow architecture" (Section 2.1), noting that "current LLMs struggle with decomposing complex questions and understanding their topological relationships, leading to coarse-grained search queries."

### How MindSearch Positions Itself Relative to Prior Work

MindSearch's core architectural innovation can be understood as addressing all three failure modes through a single design principle: **structured decomposition with explicit dependency modeling**.

**Against single-shot retrieval**: MindSearch replaces one monolithic search with a directed acyclic graph (DAG) of atomic sub-queries, where each node represents a single, focused question that can be answered through targeted web search. This directly addresses challenge 1 by ensuring that each retrieval operation has a well-scoped information need that a search engine can satisfy with high precision. The WebPlanner's role is to decompose the user's complex question into this graph structure, determining which sub-questions need to be asked and in what order.

**Against information overload**: MindSearch introduces a two-level hierarchy that separates *search management* from *reasoning and integration*. The WebSearcher agents handle the noise-filtering problem locally—each one uses a hierarchical retrieval process (query rewriting → search aggregation → page selection → summarization) to extract only the relevant information for its specific sub-question before returning a concise summary to the WebPlanner. The WebPlanner never sees raw web pages; it sees curated summaries. This multi-agent separation of concerns means that both the WebPlanner and each WebSearcher operate within manageable context windows, addressing challenges 2 and 3 simultaneously.

**Against flat reasoning**: The DAG formalism with code-as-planning is MindSearch's most distinctive positioning move. Rather than relying on the LLM to implicitly reason about information dependencies through chain-of-thought, the WebPlanner explicitly constructs a graph through Python code generation using predefined atomic functions (`add_node`, `add_edge`). This "planning as coding" approach (Section 2.1) leverages the well-documented strength of LLMs on code generation tasks (Guo et al., 2024; Roziere et al., 2023) while providing an interpretable, verifiable representation of the reasoning structure. The Python interpreter serves as both an execution environment and a validation mechanism—if the WebPlanner generates invalid graph operations, the interpreter returns error messages that enable self-correction (demonstrated in Appendix E.1, Figure 7).

The authors position this as inspired by human cognitive processes: "mimic[ing] the human minds in web information seeking and integration" (Abstract, Section 1). The analogy is that humans break complex research questions into sub-questions, research them (potentially in parallel if independent), and synthesize findings into a coherent answer. MindSearch operationalizes this cognitive model as a multi-agent system where the WebPlanner is the "high-level planner, orchestrating the reasoning steps and coordinating other agents" while WebSearchers are the specialized researchers "tasked with each sub-problem" who "employs a hierarchical retrieval process to extract valuable data" (Section 1, between Figure 1 and Section 2.1).

**Positioning within the broader literature.** MindSearch sits at the intersection of several active research threads:

- **Tool utilization with LLMs** (Section 4.1): the WebSearcher is an instance of an LLM using search engines as external tools (Qin et al., 2023; Schick et al., 2024), but MindSearch goes beyond single-tool-call frameworks by orchestrating multiple coordinated tool-using agents within a graph-structured planning framework.

- **RAG with LLMs** (Section 4.2): the WebSearcher's hierarchical retrieval pipeline is a sophisticated RAG design, but unlike standard RAG (Lewis et al., 2020) or iterative RAG (Asai et al., 2023; Chan et al., 2024), the retrieval is *distributed* across multiple sub-agents, each with a narrow information need.

- **Web agents** (Section 4.3): prior work on web automation agents (Nakano et al., 2021; Deng et al., 2024; Gur et al., 2023; Zheng et al., 2024) has explored LLMs interacting with web pages through browsing and clicking. MindSearch differs in focus: it is specifically about **information seeking and integration** rather than web interaction (form-filling, clicking, navigating), and it solves the coordination problem through multi-agent decomposition rather than end-to-end agent training.

- **Multi-agent LLM systems**: the paper contributes to the growing literature on multi-agent LLM architectures (though this literature is not separately sectioned in the related work) by demonstrating that explicit role distribution (planner vs. searcher) with structured inter-agent communication (graph edges carrying context) can handle problems that overwhelm monolithic LLM approaches.

The paper's positioning can be summarized as: **existing LLM+search combinations are architecturally insufficient for complex information seeking—they either retrieve once and hope for the best, or reason linearly without modeling task structure. MindSearch introduces structured, graph-based decomposition with hierarchical retrieval as a principled alternative that better mirrors how humans actually solve complex research problems.** The empirical goal is to demonstrate that this architectural choice yields measurable improvements in answer quality (depth, breadth, factuality) while remaining efficient enough for practical deployment (300+ pages in under 3 minutes), and that the approach works with open-source models (InternLM2.5-7B) competitive with proprietary systems, making advanced AI search accessible without dependence on closed-source LLM APIs.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

MindSearch is an LLM-based multi-agent system that takes a complex user question, autonomously researches it across the open web, and produces a detailed, sourced answer. The system solves the problem of web information seeking and integration by decomposing the user's question into a structured graph of atomic sub-questions—mimicking how a human would break down a research project—executing targeted searches for each sub-question in parallel where possible, collecting and filtering the results, and synthesizing everything into a coherent final response.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The system has two major component types organized in a planner-worker hierarchy:

1. **WebPlanner** — a single high-level reasoning agent that takes the user's complex question, decomposes it into a directed acyclic graph (DAG) of atomic sub-questions (nodes) with explicit dependency relationships (edges), dispatches each node to a WebSearcher for execution, and synthesizes the collected sub-question answers into a final response. It operates by generating Python code that constructs and manipulates the graph through a predefined API.

2. **WebSearcher** — multiple independent search agents, each tasked with one atomic sub-question from the WebPlanner. Each WebSearcher performs a hierarchical retrieval process: rewriting the sub-question into multiple similar queries to broaden recall, executing these queries via a search engine API (Bing), aggregating results by URL, selecting the most valuable pages for detailed reading based on titles and snippets, fetching and reading the full content of selected pages, and producing a concise, cited summary response back to the WebPlanner.

Information flows as follows: a user query enters the WebPlanner → the WebPlanner adds a root node containing the question → the WebPlanner iteratively generates code to add sub-question nodes (with edges indicating dependencies) → each newly added node triggers a WebSearcher to execute its assigned search → the WebSearcher returns a summarized answer with citations → the WebPlanner reads the WebSearcher's response, reasons about what additional sub-questions are now needed, and adds more nodes → this cycle repeats until the WebPlanner determines sufficient information has been gathered → the WebPlanner adds a final "response" node that synthesizes all collected information into the answer.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the WebPlanner's graph-based planning mechanism — why a DAG, why code as the planning interface, and how the predefined API functions (`add_root_node`, `add_node`, `add_edge`, `add_response_node`) enable structured decomposition and parallel execution.
- **Second**, the WebSearcher's hierarchical retrieval pipeline — how it rewrites queries, aggregates search results, selects pages, and produces cited summaries, and why this coarse-to-fine approach is necessary for handling the noise and volume of web search results.
- **Third**, the context management strategy — how the multi-agent design naturally isolates context windows (the WebPlanner never sees raw web pages, each WebSearcher only sees pages relevant to its sub-question) and how graph edges serve as the mechanism for propagating relevant context between agents without information loss.
- **Fourth**, the "planning as coding" interface in detail — the exact API specification, how code execution with a Python interpreter enables error correction (syntax errors detected by the interpreter, semantic errors detected by WebSearcher "not found" responses), and why generating structured code outperforms generating natural language action plans.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is an **architectural design paper** whose core idea is that complex web information seeking can be solved by decomposing it into a directed acyclic graph of atomic search tasks, executed by specialized agents within a planner-worker hierarchy, with code generation serving as the planning interface.

---

#### WebPlanner: Planning via Directed Acyclic Graph Construction

**The Core Insight: Problem-Solving as Graph Construction**

The WebPlanner does not simply generate a static plan and then execute it. Instead, it **dynamically constructs a reasoning graph** by interleaving planning (deciding what to search for next) with execution (receiving search results and using them to inform subsequent planning). The authors formalize this process using a directed acyclic graph (DAG) representation:

Given a user question $Q$, the solution trajectory is represented as:

$$G(Q) = \langle V, E \rangle$$

where $V$ is a set of nodes $v$, each representing an independent web search (an atomic sub-question), and $E$ is a set of directed edges representing the reasoning dependencies between nodes—specifically, an edge from node $A$ to node $B$ means that the information obtained from searching $A$ was necessary to formulate the sub-question represented by $B$.

**What this graph captures:** it is a computational representation of the reasoning structure implicit in the user's question. The nodes are the individual information needs that must be satisfied to answer the question; the edges encode which pieces of information logically depend on which others. The DAG constraint (no cycles) ensures that the reasoning is acyclic—there is no infinite loop of mutually dependent sub-questions. The graph includes two special nodes: a `START` node (also called the root node) containing the original user question, and an `END` node (also called the response node) containing the final synthesized answer.

**Why a DAG rather than a tree or a linear sequence:** a tree would prevent sharing intermediate results across branches (a piece of information might be relevant to multiple downstream sub-questions), and a linear sequence would force all sub-questions to be asked sequentially even when they are independent and could be searched in parallel. A DAG allows both: sub-questions that depend on the same upstream information can share parent nodes (DAG structure), and sub-questions with no mutual dependencies can be added simultaneously and executed in parallel (the DAG's partial ordering determines what must wait for what).

**Why Code as the Planning Interface**

The authors identify a specific limitation of natural language planning: "merely prompting the LLM to plan the entire data workflow architecture does not yield satisfactory performance" (Section 2.1). The problem is that LLMs, when asked to decompose complex questions and describe their topological relationships in natural language, "struggle with decomposing complex questions and understanding their topological relationships, leading to coarse-grained search queries."

The solution is to **use Python code as the planning language**. Rather than asking the LLM to describe its plan in prose, the WebPlanner generates executable Python code that calls a predefined API to manipulate the graph. The authors justify this design choice with two observations:

1. LLMs demonstrate "superior performance... on code tasks" (Section 2.1, citing Guo et al., 2024 and Roziere et al., 2023). Generating structured, syntax-constrained code is an easier task for current LLMs than generating natural language plans because code has rigid syntactic rules that constrain the output space and reduce ambiguity.

2. Code execution provides a built-in validation mechanism. If the WebPlanner generates syntactically invalid code (e.g., referencing a non-existent node name, calling a function with wrong argument types), the Python interpreter raises an exception with an error message, which can be fed back to the LLM for self-correction in the next turn (see Appendix E.1 for examples of this error recovery loop). Natural language plans have no equivalent validation—the system cannot detect whether a plan is internally inconsistent until a WebSearcher fails to execute a poorly formulated query.

**The Predefined Graph API**

The WebPlanner operates within a Jupyter-style code execution environment and has access to a `WebSearchGraph` class with a specific interface (defined in detail in Appendix G, the System Prompt for WebPlanner). The class manages an internal state consisting of a nodes dictionary and an adjacency list. The API exposes six methods:

1. **`__init__()`**: Initializes an empty `WebSearchGraph` instance. The internal state is `nodes` (a dictionary mapping node names to dictionaries containing content, type, and metadata) and `adjacency_list` (a dictionary mapping node names to lists of adjacent node names representing outgoing edges).

2. **`add_root_node(node_content: str, node_name: str = 'root')`**: Adds the user's original question as the root node of the graph. The `node_content` is the full user query text. This is called exactly once at the beginning of the session.

3. **`add_node(node_name: str, node_content: str) -> str`**: Adds a sub-question node to the graph. The `node_name` is a unique string identifier for this node (the LLM must choose a descriptive name). The `node_content` is the atomic sub-question to be researched. The method returns a string containing the search results for that sub-question (obtained by invoking a WebSearcher internally). This is the core mechanism by which the WebPlanner dispatches work: calling `add_node` both adds the node to the graph's structural representation and triggers the actual web search.

4. **`add_response_node(node_name: str = 'response')`**: Adds the final response node. When the WebPlanner calls this method, it signals that information gathering is complete and the final answer should be generated. No other nodes should be added after this call.

5. **`add_edge(start_node: str, end_node: str)`**: Adds a directed edge from `start_node` to `end_node` in the graph's adjacency list. This encodes the dependency: the search for `end_node` was informed by the results from `start_node`. Edges are critical for context propagation between agents (see Section 2.3 on context management): when executing a WebSearcher for a node, the system prefixes the responses from that node's parent nodes (and the root node) to provide necessary context.

6. **`reset()`**: Resets all nodes and edges, returning the graph to an empty state.

7. **`node(node_name: str) -> str`**: Returns a dictionary containing the specified node's information (content, type, thought process if any, list of predecessor nodes). This is used at the end of each code block to retrieve and confirm the newly added node's information.

**The Planning Loop: Step-by-Step Graph Construction**

The WebPlanner operates in a multi-turn loop. At each turn, the following sequence occurs (illustrated in Figure 2 with a concrete example):

1. **Context reading**: The LLM receives the full dialogue history, which includes: the original user question, all previously generated code blocks, all search results returned by previous `add_node` calls, and any error messages from failed code executions. This provides the LLM with complete context about what has been discovered so far and what the current state of the reasoning graph is.

2. **Thinking and code generation**: The LLM outputs a "thought" (natural language reasoning about what information is still needed and what sub-questions to formulate) followed by a Python code block enclosed in markers (`<|action start|><|interpreter|>```python ... ```<|action end|>`). The code block contains one or more calls to the `WebSearchGraph` API methods. The system prompt explicitly instructs the LLM to "place each code block within a code block marker" and to include "only one code block per output" (Appendix G, System Prompt for WebPlanner, Consideration 5).

3. **Code execution**: The Python interpreter executes the generated code. If the code is syntactically valid, each `add_node` call triggers a WebSearcher to execute the specified sub-question's search. The search results (summaries from WebSearcher) are captured as the return value of `add_node` and become part of the conversation history for subsequent turns. If the code has a syntax error or runtime exception, the error message is captured and fed back to the LLM in the next turn, enabling self-correction (see Appendix E.1 for examples).

4. **Parallel execution of independent nodes**: If the LLM generates code that adds multiple nodes in a single code block, and those nodes have no dependencies on each other (i.e., their search tasks are independent), they can be executed in parallel. The WebSearcher instances for independent nodes run concurrently, significantly reducing total wall-clock time. This is a key advantage of the DAG formalism: the graph structure explicitly encodes which tasks are parallelizable, and the multi-agent architecture naturally supports parallel execution.

5. **Iteration**: Steps 1–4 repeat until the LLM determines that sufficient information has been collected. The LLM then calls `add_response_node`, which signals termination and triggers the final answer synthesis.

**What the LLM Sees and Decides**

The system prompt (detailed in Appendix G) provides the LLM with explicit constraints on how to formulate sub-questions:

- "Each search node's content must be a single question; do not include multiple questions" (Consideration 1). This enforces atomicity: each `add_node` call should target one specific information need that a search engine can address precisely. Compound questions like "what are the differences between A, B, and C" should be decomposed into separate nodes for A, B, and C.

- "Do not fabricate search results; wait for the code to return results" (Consideration 2). This prevents the LLM from hallucinating information and ensures it only reasons based on actually retrieved web content.

- "Do not repeat the same question; continue asking based on existing questions" (Consideration 3). This prevents redundant searches and encourages progressive exploration.

- "When adding a response node, add it separately; do not add a response node and other nodes simultaneously" (Consideration 4). This ensures clean termination semantics.

The LLM must also manage the tradeoff between breadth (how many parallel sub-questions to explore) and depth (how many sequential follow-up questions are needed). The constraint that the maximum interaction turn is limited to 10 (mentioned in Section 3.4 as a hyperparameter beyond which "limited performance gains" are observed) bounds the total number of planning iterations.

#### WebSearcher: Hierarchical Information Retrieval

**The Problem: From Hundreds of Pages to a Concise Answer**

When a WebSearcher receives a sub-question from the WebPlanner, it faces the information overload problem at a local scale. A single search query—even one targeting a narrow, atomic question—can return dozens or hundreds of web pages, each containing substantial amounts of irrelevant content along with the targeted information. The WebSearcher must filter this flood of data down to a concise, accurate, well-sourced answer that the WebPlanner can effectively use.

The WebSearcher solves this through a **four-stage coarse-to-fine retrieval pipeline** (illustrated in Figure 3):

**Stage 1: Multi-Query Generation and Execution (Query Rewriting)**

The sub-question received from the WebPlanner is not used as a single search query. Instead, the WebSearcher's LLM **generates several similar queries** that express the same information need in different ways. The authors describe this as a mechanism to "broaden the search content and thus improve the recall of relevant information" (Section 2.2).

Why multiple queries for a single sub-question? A single formulation of a query may miss relevant pages that use different terminology, focus on different aspects, or are indexed under different keywords. By generating multiple query variants—paraphrases, synonyms, different angles on the same topic—the WebSearcher increases the probability that at least one query formulation retrieves each truly relevant page. This is analogous to query expansion in traditional information retrieval but is done generatively by the LLM rather than through synonym dictionaries or relevance feedback.

The generated queries are then executed through the configured search API. The paper specifies that all experiments use the Bing Search API (Section 3, introduction paragraph: "all models only have access to the Internet through BING search API"). The method notes that the approach is not tied to a specific search engine—it can use "various search APIs, such as Google, Bing, and DuckDuckGo" (Section 2.2)—but Bing is the concrete choice for reproducibility.

**Stage 2: Search Content Aggregation**

Each query returns a set of results, with each result typically containing: a web URL, a page title, and a short text snippet/summary. Since multiple queries may return the same URL (especially if they are semantically similar), the WebSearcher **automatically merges results based on web URLs** to deduplicate entries. The aggregation produces a single deduplicated list of candidate web pages, each with its title and snippet.

This stage addresses the raw volume problem: the initial search may return hundreds of raw results across multiple queries, but many are duplicates or near-duplicates. Aggregation by URL collapses the candidate set to a manageable size of unique pages.

**Stage 3: Detailed Page Selection (Coarse-to-Fine Filtering)**

From the aggregated list of unique candidate pages, the WebSearcher's LLM is prompted to **select the most valuable pages for detailed reading**. The selection is based on the available metadata: URL, title, and snippet. The LLM reads these lightweight descriptors (which are much shorter than full page content) and makes a relevance judgment about which pages are likely to contain the specific information needed to answer the sub-question.

This is the critical filtering step that prevents context window overflow. If the WebSearcher attempted to read the full content of every candidate page, even for a narrow sub-question, the total text volume could quickly exceed practical context limits (the paper references 8K tokens as an example context window constraint in Section 2.2). By using the LLM to make a preliminary relevance assessment based on snippets and titles—which are orders of magnitude smaller than full page content—the system discards the vast majority of pages before incurring the cost of full-content reading.

The paper does not specify the exact number of pages selected for detailed reading, but the intent is clear: only pages that the LLM deems highly likely to contain directly relevant information proceed to the next stage.

**Stage 4: Final Summarization with Citations**

The full content of the selected web pages is fetched and added to the LLM's input context. The WebSearcher's LLM then reads these pages and generates a **detailed and complete reply** to the assigned sub-question. Critically, the system prompt for WebSearcher (Appendix G) mandates citation formatting:

> "Each key point in the response should be marked with the source of the search results to ensure the credibility of the information. The citation format is [[int]]. If there are multiple citations, use multiple [[]] to provide the index, such as [[id 1]][[id 2]]."

This citation requirement serves multiple purposes. It forces the LLM to ground its response in specific retrieved sources rather than relying on its parametric knowledge (reducing hallucination risk). It provides the WebPlanner with provenance information, enabling it to assess source credibility when synthesizing multiple WebSearcher responses. And it makes the final answer auditable—a human reader can trace every factual claim back to its originating web page.

The final output of the WebSearcher is a **self-contained, cited summary** that answers the specific sub-question. This summary—not the raw web pages—is what gets returned to the WebPlanner as the return value of `add_node`.

**Design Justification for Hierarchical Retrieval**

The hierarchical structure directly addresses the context-length challenge. If the WebSearcher attempted to process all candidate pages in a single pass, it would face the same information overload problem the overall MindSearch system is designed to solve. By breaking the retrieval into stages—metadata-based filtering followed by full-content reading of only the selected best pages—the WebSearcher operates within tractable context windows at each stage. The metadata-based filtering stage processes many items of small size; the full-content reading stage processes few items of large size. This decomposition matches the LLM's capabilities: good at relevance judgments on short texts (snippets), and good at detailed synthesis on long texts (full pages), but poor at doing both simultaneously across hundreds of documents.

#### Context Management in the Multi-Agent Architecture

**The Problem: Information Isolation vs. Information Sharing**

The multi-agent design of MindSearch distributes work across independent WebSearcher agents, which naturally creates a tension: each WebSearcher operates with a local context (the search results for its specific sub-question), but answering a complex question often requires that a WebSearcher be aware of information discovered by other agents working on related sub-questions. For example, if one WebSearcher is researching "Chang'e-6 communication challenges" and another is researching "Queqiao-2 relay satellite specifications," the latter's work is more effective if it knows what the former already discovered about communication requirements.

The naive approach—giving every WebSearcher access to all previous search results—would undermine the context isolation that makes the multi-agent design efficient. If each WebSearcher had to process all prior search results, the total context length would grow rapidly with each node, eventually exceeding practical limits and causing the same information overload problem the system is designed to avoid.

**The Solution: Graph-Edge-Based Context Propagation**

MindSearch uses the **topological relationships encoded in the DAG edges** to determine what context each WebSearcher should receive. Specifically (Section 2.3):

> "we simply prefix the response from its father node as well as the root node when executing each search agent"

When a WebSearcher is invoked for a node $v$, its input context includes two components beyond the sub-question itself:

1. **The response from $v$'s immediate parent node(s)** — specifically, the summarized answer produced by the WebSearcher(s) that handled the parent node(s). These are the nodes from which directed edges point to $v$, representing the information that was necessary to formulate $v$'s sub-question. The parent context ensures that the WebSearcher has the upstream information that motivated its assignment.

2. **The root node's content** — the original user question. This ensures that every WebSearcher, regardless of how deep it is in the graph, remains aware of the ultimate goal. The authors note that this prevents a common failure mode where "simply focusing the decomposed query from the Planner may lose useful information during the information collection phase due to the local receptive field inside the search agent" (Section 2.3).

**What is NOT included:** The WebSearcher does NOT receive the search results from sibling nodes (nodes at the same depth in the graph that were dispatched in parallel), from more distant ancestor nodes (grandparents beyond the immediate parent), or from nodes in unrelated branches of the graph. This selective context propagation means that each WebSearcher operates with a context window that contains only directly relevant prior information—not the full history of all searches performed so far.

**Why This Works: The Principle of Local Relevance**

The graph-edge-based context propagation relies on the assumption that **the dependency structure encoded in the graph edges accurately captures which information is relevant to which sub-questions**. If the WebPlanner correctly identifies that sub-question $B$ depends on sub-question $A$ (and therefore adds an edge $A \rightarrow B$), then the summary from $A$ is relevant context for $B$. If the WebPlanner correctly identifies that sub-questions $C$ and $D$ are independent (no edge between them), then their WebSearchers don't need each other's context.

This assumption is maintained by the WebPlanner's explicit reasoning at each step. Before generating code to add a new node, the WebPlanner's "thought" process evaluates what information has been discovered so far and determines what additional information is needed. The edges it adds reflect this reasoning. The authors empirically validate this approach by demonstrating that removing the parent-context prefixing (`w/o PPC` in Table 3) causes a performance drop from 64.0 to 63.3 on HotpotQA, confirming that the edge-based context propagation provides useful information beyond what the sub-question alone contains.

**Natural Context Isolation**

The multi-agent design provides context isolation as a **structural property**, not something that requires active management:

- The **WebPlanner** never sees raw web page content at all. It only sees the concise, cited summaries returned by WebSearchers. This means the WebPlanner's context window contains only high-level reasoning and curated information—the "massive information noise" of raw web pages is handled entirely by the WebSearcher agents and never reaches the planner. This allows the WebPlanner to focus purely on decomposition and synthesis without being "distracted by the over-length web search results" (Section 2.3).

- Each **WebSearcher** only sees web pages relevant to its specific sub-question. It doesn't have to process pages relevant to other sub-questions that are being handled by other WebSearcher instances. This means the total information that must fit within any single LLM's context window at any point in time is bounded by the maximum information needed to answer one atomic sub-question, not the total information needed to answer the entire complex user query.

The authors frame this as a fundamental advantage of the multi-agent paradigm for long-context tasks: "Such a paradigm also naturally enables long-context management among different agents, which improves the overall efficiency of the framework, especially under circumstances that require the model to quickly read plenty of web pages" (Section 2.3). The distributed nature of the computation means no single component becomes a context bottleneck.

#### The "Planning as Coding" Interface in Detail

**API Specification and Execution Model**

The WebPlanner's interaction with the DAG is mediated entirely through the `WebSearchGraph` class API. The system prompt (Appendix G) defines this API precisely, including parameter types, return types, and behavioral contracts. This specification serves as the "contract" between the LLM's code generation and the system's execution environment.

The critical design choice is that **graph construction and search execution are unified in the `add_node` method**. When the LLM calls `add_node(node_name, node_content)`, two things happen atomically from the LLM's perspective:

1. A node is added to the graph's internal data structures.
2. A WebSearcher is invoked to research the sub-question, and its response is returned as the method's return value.

This unification means the LLM doesn't separately "plan to search" and then "execute the search"—the act of planning (adding a node to the reasoning graph) inherently triggers execution. This tight coupling ensures that the LLM always has access to search results before making further planning decisions, preventing it from generating plans that are disconnected from ground-truth information.

**Code Generation Format and Constraints**

The system prompt specifies exact formatting requirements for the LLM's output at each turn:

1. The LLM begins with natural language "thoughts" explaining its reasoning.
2. The LLM outputs a single code block enclosed in markers: `<|action start|><|interpreter|>` followed by ` ```python ... ``` ` followed by `<|action end|>`.
3. The code block must end with a call to `graph.node('...')` to retrieve and display information about the newly added node (Consideration 6 in the system prompt).
4. Only one code block is allowed per output turn (Consideration 5).

This formatting is mechanically enforced: the system parses the LLM's output to extract the code block, executes it, and captures the output (including any search results and the final `graph.node()` call's return value) for inclusion in the next turn's dialogue history.

**Error Correction Mechanisms**

The code execution environment provides two distinct error correction pathways:

1. **Syntax and runtime errors from the Python interpreter.** If the LLM generates Python code with a syntax error (e.g., missing parenthesis, undefined variable) or a runtime error (e.g., calling `add_edge` with a non-existent node name), the interpreter raises an exception. The exception message is captured and appended to the dialogue history. In the next turn, the LLM sees this error message and can regenerate corrected code. Appendix E.1 (Figure 7) demonstrates a concrete example: the LLM references a non-existent node name, the interpreter returns "KeyError: 'node_name'", and in the next turn the LLM regenerates the code with the correct node name.

2. **Information-not-found feedback from WebSearcher.** When a WebSearcher cannot find relevant information for a sub-question (possibly because the query was poorly formulated, or the information genuinely doesn't exist on the indexed web), it returns a response indicating "information not found." The LLM sees this response in the next turn and can adjust its strategy: either reformulate the query (as demonstrated in Appendix E.2, Figure 8, where the LLM adds "more information about the final match of UEFA Euro 2020" after an initial query fails) or, if multiple attempts fail, concede that the information is unavailable and proceed with what it has (as demonstrated in Appendix E.2, Figure 9, where the LLM "directly generate[s] the response node when several attempts fail and directly give up the answer").

This dual error correction mechanism—syntactic validation by the interpreter, semantic validation by WebSearcher feedback—means the system can recover from both the LLM's code generation mistakes and its query formulation mistakes without human intervention.

**Why Code Over JSON or Natural Language Actions**

The paper compares the code-based planning approach against two alternatives in the ablation study (Section 3.3.1, Table 2):

- **ReAct** (Yao et al., 2022b): a JSON-based format where the LLM outputs `Thought: str; Action: WebSearcher(List[str])` at each step. This uses natural language actions with a predefined structured format.

- **CodeAct** (Wang et al., 2024): wrapping the WebSearcher as a Python function and prompting the LLM to call it via code, but without the graph construction abstraction—each code block simply calls the search function.

The experimental results (Table 2, Section 3.3.1) show: ReAct achieves 58.0 on HotpotQA with InternLM2.5-7B, CodeAct achieves 61.3, and the full graph-based code planning (MindSearch's WebPlanner) achieves 64.0. The authors attribute CodeAct's advantage over ReAct to the fact that "integrating code generation provides unified action space and therefore yields better performance." The further improvement from CodeAct to full MindSearch is attributed to "converting the original function calling process into the graph construction"—the DAG structure provides explicit modeling of dependencies and parallelization opportunities that a flat sequence of function calls does not capture.

The code interface has specific advantages over JSON-based action formats:

1. **Composability**: multiple `add_node` calls can be placed in a single code block to express parallel tasks. In a JSON format, expressing parallelism would require explicit parallel markers or list structures, which are less natural.

2. **Expressiveness**: the full Python language (conditionals, loops, variable assignment) is available, though in practice the LLM primarily uses sequential API calls.

3. **Validatability**: Python code is parsed and executed; syntax errors are caught immediately. JSON schemas can also be validated, but a valid JSON action plan can still contain semantically invalid content (e.g., referencing a non-existent node) that only execution can detect.

#### Putting It All Together: End-to-End Execution Example

Walking through the complete flow for a representative query (illustrated conceptually in Figure 1 and with a concrete example in Figure 2):

1. **User query received**: "How has China's Chang'e-6 mission contributed to international space exploration, and how does it compare to Apollo 11?"

2. **WebPlanner initializes graph**: The LLM generates code calling `graph.add_root_node(node_content="How has China's Chang'e-6 mission...")`. This creates the root node.

3. **First decomposition turn**: The LLM reasons that this question requires: (a) understanding Chang'e-6's technical achievements, (b) understanding Apollo 11's achievements, (c) identifying comparison dimensions, and (d) understanding China's international space collaboration. It generates code that adds multiple nodes—one for each of these sub-questions—with edges from the root node to each (since all depend on the original question). These nodes are dispatched to WebSearcher instances in parallel because they have no mutual dependencies.

4. **WebSearchers execute**: Each WebSearcher generates multiple query variants, searches Bing, aggregates results by URL, selects the most promising pages based on snippets, reads the full content of selected pages, and produces a cited summary. For instance, the WebSearcher handling the Chang'e-6 technical details returns a summary about the Queqiao-2 relay satellite, autonomous navigation systems, and sample collection mechanisms, with citations to specific web sources.

5. **Second decomposition turn**: The WebPlanner reads all the WebSearcher responses from step 4. It identifies that the initial broad sub-questions revealed specific topics that need deeper investigation—for example, the Chang'e-6 summary mentioned "South Pole-Aitken Basin" and "international payloads." The LLM generates new code adding follow-up nodes for these specific topics, with edges from the original Chang'e-6 node (since these follow-ups depend on knowing what Chang'e-6 involved). Other follow-up nodes are added for Apollo 11 details.

6. **Iteration continues**: The WebPlanner continues adding nodes for finer-grained questions as new information reveals knowledge gaps. With each turn, the graph grows deeper (follow-up questions on discovered topics) and potentially wider (parallel investigations of independent aspects).

7. **Termination**: When the LLM determines that sufficient information has been gathered across all relevant dimensions, it calls `graph.add_response_node()`, which triggers the synthesis of all collected WebSearcher summaries into a final, structured answer. The final answer includes citations accumulated from all WebSearcher responses.

The paper reports that this process typically involves processing "more than 300 pages in less than 3 minutes" (Section 2.3), with Appendix B providing detailed time measurements: for 10 complex research questions, human labelers required 19 hours and 17 minutes total, while MindSearch completed the same tasks in 23 minutes—an approximately 50:1 time efficiency ratio (23 minutes vs. ~19.3 hours). Breaking down the human effort: 47 minutes were spent searching and reading web pages, and 74 minutes were spent writing the detailed response (about 3,000 words per question).

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Complex Information Seeking as a Dynamic Graph Construction Process, Not a Retrieve-Then-Read Task

The paper's most fundamental intellectual move is **reframing web information seeking from a document retrieval problem into a structured reasoning problem**. Before MindSearch, the dominant paradigm for combining LLMs with search engines—exemplified by standard RAG (Lewis et al., 2020), Self-RAG (Asai et al., 2023), and RQ-RAG (Chan et al., 2024)—treated the task as a sequence of retrieve-then-read operations: take the user query, perhaps rewrite it, fetch some documents, feed them into the LLM's context window, and generate an answer. Even when these systems performed multiple retrieval steps (as in iterative RAG or ReAct-style agent loops), the reasoning about *how pieces of information relate to each other* was implicit in the LLM's chain-of-thought, not explicitly represented in the system architecture.

The core conceptual innovation of MindSearch is to **move the decomposition structure from the LLM's internal reasoning trace to an external, inspectable, executable representation—the directed acyclic graph**. The DAG formalism `G(Q) = <V, E>` makes three things explicit that prior approaches left implicit:

**First**, the DAG makes **the decomposition itself a first-class object** in the system architecture. In a ReAct loop (Yao et al., 2022b), the agent reasons step-by-step, but the plan exists only in the sequence of generated thoughts—there's no representation of which sub-tasks are independent, which depend on which, or whether the overall plan is complete. The DAG's nodes and edges are discrete, named, and queryable (`graph.node()`) entities that persist across turns, enabling the LLM to reason *about the plan* as a data structure rather than just *executing* it linearly.

**Second**, the DAG encodes **parallelism as a structural property** rather than requiring the LLM to explicitly manage concurrent execution. Independent sub-questions are represented as nodes with no path between them; the system can execute their WebSearchers concurrently without the LLM having to reason about thread safety or synchronization. This is what enables the claimed 300-pages-in-3-minutes throughput: the parallelism is inherent in the problem decomposition, not bolted on as an optimization.

**Third**, the DAG captures **dependency reasoning** that prior work either omitted or handled implicitly. The edges `E` are not decorative—they determine context propagation between agents (Section 2.3), ensuring that downstream WebSearchers receive exactly the upstream information they need. This is a design choice with theoretical grounding: the graph's partial ordering encodes a causal model of information dependencies, where node `B` depends on node `A` means "the results of searching for `A` are necessary to properly formulate the search for `B`."

This reframing matters because it **shifts the bottleneck from retrieval quality to decomposition quality**. Prior systems spent their intellectual energy on improving retrieval (dense passage retrieval, query rewriting, relevance filtering) but treated the question-as-given. MindSearch argues—and empirically demonstrates through the ablation in Table 2, where the graph-based planning outperforms both ReAct (58.0 → 64.0) and CodeAct (61.3 → 64.0)—that *how you break down the question* dominates *how well you retrieve documents for each sub-question*. The search quality of individual WebSearchers matters (Table 3 shows removing multi-query generation drops performance from 64.0 to 60.6), but the architectural decision to decompose and parallelize is the primary driver.

This is a **fundamental conceptual shift**, not an incremental refinement. It changes the research question from "how do we better retrieve documents for a given query?" to "how do we represent and reason about the latent structure of information needs implicit in a complex question?"—a question that belongs more naturally to planning and program synthesis than to information retrieval.

### Innovation 2: Code as the Universal Planning Language, Enabling Validation and Self-Correction

The paper's second distinctive contribution is the **"code as planning" paradigm**—using executable Python code rather than natural language or structured JSON as the medium through which the LLM expresses its decomposition plan. This is not merely an implementation detail; it represents a specific hypothesis about the **relationship between generation format and reasoning quality**.

The field has oscillated between several approaches for LLM action planning. ReAct (Yao et al., 2022b) uses natural language interleaved with structured action markers (`Thought: ... Action: ...`). Toolformer (Schick et al., 2024) and similar approaches use special tokens or API call formats. CodeAct (Wang et al., 2024) generates Python function calls. MindSearch's contribution is to argue—and provide evidence for—a specific causal chain: **code generation → better decomposition → better search performance**, with two mediating mechanisms.

**Mechanism 1: Code constrains the output space in ways that improve planning quality.** The authors note that LLMs demonstrate "superior performance... on code tasks" (Section 2.1, citing Guo et al., 2024; Roziere et al., 2023). The explanation they imply—though don't fully articulate—is that code's rigid syntactic structure reduces the effective search space for the LLM's decoder. When generating natural language plans, the LLM faces a combinatorially vast space of valid utterances, many of which are poorly structured. When generating Python code constrained by a specific API (`add_node`, `add_edge`), the LLM's generation is channeled through a narrow syntactic funnel where valid outputs correspond to coherent graph operations. The API serves as a **domain-specific language for web search planning**, and using it forces the LLM to think in terms of nodes, edges, and dependencies rather than vague natural language about "looking into X" or "researching Y."

**Mechanism 2: Code execution provides a built-in validation-and-correction loop.** This is the more innovative insight. Natural language plans can be incoherent without the system detecting it—there's no "interpreter" for prose that raises exceptions when a plan contradicts itself or references non-existent information. But Python code has an interpreter, and that interpreter serves as an **automated plan validator**. When the WebPlanner writes invalid code (wrong node name, missing argument), the interpreter returns an error message; the LLM reads this error and regenerates corrected code in the next turn. This creates a tight feedback loop—planning, execution, error, correction—that operates without human intervention (demonstrated in Appendix E.1, Figure 7).

This mechanism generalizes beyond syntax errors. The WebSearcher's "information not found" responses serve as a **semantic error signal**—the code executed successfully (syntactically valid), but the search failed to produce useful results (semantically invalid query). The LLM can then reformulate the query (Appendix E.2, Figure 8) or, after multiple failures, decide the information is unavailable and proceed with what it has (Appendix E.2, Figure 9). This is a form of **runtime plan repair** that is only possible because the planning medium is executable code whose results are observable.

The ablation in Table 2 provides empirical support: ReAct (natural language actions, no code) achieves 58.0; CodeAct (functional code, no graph structure) achieves 61.3; the full graph-based code planning achieves 64.0. The 3.3-point gap between ReAct and CodeAct isolates the code-vs-natural-language effect (both are linear planning, but different media). The 2.7-point further improvement to full MindSearch isolates the graph-structure effect. Both increments are meaningful.

This innovation is **conceptually fundamental** because it proposes a general principle for LLM agent design: **whenever possible, express plans in an executable, validatable language rather than natural language**. The benefits—constrained generation, automatic validation, error-driven self-correction—are not specific to web search. They apply to any domain where a planning API can be defined and an execution environment exists. The paper doesn't develop this into a general theory, but the empirical demonstration that code-as-planning outperforms natural-language-as-planning at equal model scale is a concrete contribution that other agent designers can operationalize.

### Innovation 3: Multi-Agent Architecture as a Native Solution to the Long-Context Problem

The paper's third distinctive contribution is the **use of multi-agent decomposition as a principled approach to context window management**, rather than as a mere engineering convenience. This reframes the long-context problem—traditionally addressed through architectural innovations (extended context windows, sparse attention, retrieval augmentation) or training techniques (position interpolation, length generalization)—as a **system design problem** solvable through role distribution.

The insight is that the information overload described in challenges 2 and 3 (Section 1) is not a monolithic problem but a **composite of two separable sub-problems**: (a) the WebPlanner needs to reason about the *structure* of the answer—what sub-questions exist, how they relate, what's been covered—without being overwhelmed by the *content* of web pages, and (b) each WebSearcher needs to extract specific information from multiple web pages but only for a *narrow, atomic question*. These two cognitive loads—structural reasoning and content processing—have fundamentally different information needs, and forcing a single LLM instance to handle both simultaneously creates a context window dilemma: include enough web content for accurate answers and you lose structural coherence; prioritize structural reasoning and you lack sufficient source material.

MindSearch's multi-agent design solves this by **assigning structural reasoning and content processing to different agents with different context configurations**:

- The **WebPlanner** operates with a context containing: the original question, all previously generated code, all WebSearcher summaries (concise, curated text), and any error messages. It never sees raw web pages. Its context is dominated by structural information—the graph topology, the abstract summaries of findings—not by noisy source text. This allows it to maintain a coherent understanding of the overall research state across potentially dozens of sub-questions without hitting context limits.

- Each **WebSearcher** operates with a context containing: its assigned sub-question, the parent node's summary (if any), the root question, and the full text of selected web pages. Its context is dominated by content, but the content is narrowly scoped to one atomic information need. This allows it to process detailed source material without being distracted by information relevant to other sub-questions.

The critical design choice that makes this work is the **graph-edge-based context propagation** described in Section 2.3: each WebSearcher receives context from its parent node(s) and the root node, but not from siblings or distant ancestors. This implements a **principle of minimal necessary context**—each agent receives exactly the context that is causally relevant to its task, as determined by the DAG's dependency structure. The ablation in Table 3 validates this: removing parent prefix context (`w/o PPC`) drops performance from 64.0 to 63.3, confirming that the graph edges carry useful information beyond what the sub-question alone provides.

This innovation is significant because it **generalizes beyond web search**. The principle—"assign structurally distinct cognitive loads to separate agents with tailored context windows, and use the task's dependency structure to determine inter-agent information flow"—applies to any complex task with decomposable structure. The paper's contribution is not the multi-agent architecture per se (multi-agent systems predate LLMs), but rather the specific claim that **context management is the primary justification for multi-agent decomposition in LLM systems**, and that the dependency structure of the task should determine context-sharing policies.

The distinction from prior work matters. Most multi-agent LLM systems (debate, role-playing, collaborative problem-solving) use multiple agents to simulate different perspectives or to ensemble outputs. MindSearch uses multiple agents primarily to **partition the information space** so that no single agent's context window becomes a bottleneck. This is a more principled justification: the number of agents is determined by the task's decomposability, not by an arbitrary choice about how many perspectives to simulate.

### Innovation 4: Empirical Demonstration That Decomposition Quality Dominates Retrieval Quality in Complex Search

This is a **finding-level innovation** rather than a method-level one: the paper provides concrete evidence that for complex information-seeking tasks, **how you break down the question matters much more than how well you retrieve documents for each sub-question**. This finding, though not presented as a formal theorem, challenges an implicit assumption in much of the RAG and search-augmented LLM literature—that improvements should primarily target the retrieval component.

The evidence comes from comparing the magnitude of three effects across the ablation studies:

1. **Decomposition strategy effect** (Table 2): Switching from ReAct to the full graph-based planning yields a +6.0 point improvement (58.0 → 64.0) on HotpotQA with InternLM2.5-7B. This effect isolates the quality of question decomposition, since the WebSearcher is held constant across all entries.

2. **Search quality effect** (Table 3): Removing multi-query generation—the most impactful single WebSearcher component—causes a -3.4 point drop (64.0 → 60.6). Removing page selection (the coarse-to-fine filtering) causes a -6.0 point drop (64.0 → 58.0). These effects isolate the quality of retrieval given a fixed decomposition.

3. **Difficulty sensitivity** (Table 1, HotpotQA breakdown): On GPT-4o, MindSearch's improvement over ReAct is concentrated in the Hard subset (78.0 vs. 70.0 = +8.0), with essentially no improvement on Easy (80.0 vs. 81.0). This pattern—larger gains on harder problems—is consistent with the interpretation that decomposition quality matters more when the question requires synthesizing information from multiple, non-obviously-related sources.

The finding that decomposition strategy matters *at least as much as* retrieval quality, and potentially more for hard problems, has **architectural implications for future search-augmented LLM systems**. It suggests that investment should flow toward planning and decomposition capabilities (better graph construction, more sophisticated dependency reasoning, dynamic plan repair) at least as much as toward retrieval improvements (better embeddings, reranking, query expansion). This is a non-obvious allocation recommendation given the field's historical focus on retrieval.

The paper also provides a **diagnostic metric** for this phenomenon: the depth of the generated DAG correlates with question complexity (Appendix C.1, Table 5: 2-hop questions produce DAG depth 1.1, 3-hop produce 1.2, 4-hop produce 1.6). The fact that DAG depth is systematically lower than the number of hops reflects the parallelization advantage—multiple dependent sub-questions are resolved in a single graph layer when they're independent of each other—and provides a measurable way to assess whether a system is genuinely decomposing questions or just issuing sequential searches.

This innovation is **incremental in form but fundamental in implication**. The individual numbers (64.0 vs. 58.0, etc.) are not paradigm-shifting on their own. What makes this a genuine contribution is the **pattern across ablations**: by controlling for retrieval quality and varying decomposition strategy, the paper isolates a previously underappreciated source of variance in search-augmented LLM performance and provides a framework (DAG depth, parallelism, dependency modeling) for thinking about it systematically.

### Innovation 5: Open-Source LLMs as Competitive AI Search Engines Through Architectural Innovation Alone

The paper's fifth contribution is the **demonstration that architectural design—not model scale or proprietary training—can close the gap between open-source and proprietary AI search systems**. This is an empirical claim with significant practical implications for the democratization of advanced AI capabilities.

The evidence is in the human evaluation results (Figure 4): MindSearch based on InternLM2.5-7B (a 7-billion-parameter open-source model) produces responses that human evaluators prefer over ChatGPT-Web (backed by GPT-4o, a substantially larger and more capable proprietary model) and Perplexity.ai Pro (a commercial product) in 70-83% of cases across depth, breadth, and factuality dimensions. The 7B model with MindSearch's architecture outperforms or matches the proprietary system on quality metrics, despite the proprietary system having access to a dramatically more capable base LLM.

This finding is significant because it **inverts the usual scaling narrative**. The dominant story in the LLM literature—reinforced by scaling laws (Hoffmann et al., 2022; Kaplan et al., 2020) and the performance trajectory from GPT-3 to GPT-4—is that better performance requires larger models, more training compute, or both. MindSearch demonstrates an alternative path: **keep the model small and invest in inference-time architecture**. The 7B model doesn't become smarter at reasoning; it becomes more effective at information seeking because the system architecture—graph decomposition, parallel search, hierarchical retrieval, context isolation—compensates for its individual limitations.

The authors don't fully develop the theoretical implications, but the result connects to an emerging theme in the LLM agent literature: **architectural innovation can substitute for model scale on structured tasks with decomposable structure**. A smaller model that knows when to search, what to search for, and how to integrate results can outperform a larger model that attempts to answer from parametric knowledge or with naive search integration. This is not a universal claim—it likely holds only for tasks where the knowledge is externally available (not requiring deep reasoning or novel synthesis) and where the decomposition structure can be inferred from the question. But within those boundaries, it's a powerful result.

There's an important **equity implication** that the paper touches on (Section 5): "MindSearch with open-source models can already deliver a competitive solution to the proprietary AI search engine." If the finding generalizes, it means that organizations without access to GPT-4-level models can build competitive AI search systems by investing in multi-agent orchestration around smaller open-source models. This shifts the barrier to entry from "access to frontier models" to "engineering capability in agent system design"—still a barrier, but a different and potentially lower one.

The caveat is that the evaluation is on 100 human-curated queries (Section 3.1.1), which is a relatively small sample. The generalizability to arbitrary real-world queries—particularly those requiring specialized domain knowledge or multilingual search—remains untested. But as a **proof of concept**, the 7B-vs-GPT-4o comparison makes a concrete argument that architectural innovation is an underexploited dimension of AI system capability, and that the field's focus on model scaling has left substantial performance gains on the table for systems that invest in structured decomposition and multi-agent coordination.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The paper evaluates on two categories: **closed-set QA** and **open-set QA**. For closed-set QA, the authors use three benchmarks: **Bamboogle** (Press et al., 2022), **Musique** (Trivedi et al., 2022), and **HotpotQA** (Yang et al., 2018). Bamboogle is used with its 2-hop, 3-hop, and 4-hop subsets. Musique is evaluated on Easy, Medium, and Hard difficulty splits. HotpotQA is analyzed by its built-in difficulty labels (Easy, Medium, Hard). For open-set QA, the authors "carefully curate 100 real-world human queries" (Section 3.1.1); no further details about the curation process or query sources are provided beyond the fact that they are "real-world." Responses are collected from MindSearch and two commercial baselines and judged by five human experts.

- **Base model(s).** The paper uses two LLM backends to demonstrate generalization: **GPT-4o** (closed-source) and **InternLM2.5-7B-Chat** (open-source, Cai et al., 2024). The choice of a 7B open-source model alongside a frontier proprietary model is deliberate—the authors aim to show that MindSearch provides benefits across model scales and that open-source models can be competitive with proprietary search products when augmented with the proposed architecture. For the generalization experiments in Appendix D, three additional models are tested: DeepSeek-V2 (Liu et al., 2024), Qwen-2.5-7B (Yang et al., 2024), and GLM-4-9B (GLM et al., 2024).

- **Metrics.** For **closed-set QA**, the paper uses exact match accuracy against ground-truth answers. For HotpotQA specifically, "we utilize a subjective LLM evaluator (GPT4-o) to gauge the correctness" (Section 3.2.1), since the paper uses a zero-shot setting where exact string matching against reference answers may be inappropriate. For **open-set QA**, human evaluators judge responses along three dimensions (Section 3.1.1): **Depth** ("the thoroughness and profundity of an answer... delves into the intricacies of a question"), **Breadth** ("the scope and diversity covered by an answer... touches on various aspects of the question or multiple related fields"), and **Factuality** ("the degree to which an answer is accurate and fact-based... grounded in reliable data"). Evaluators select their preferred response among the compared systems, and "the final results are determined based on major votes" (Section 3.1.1), meaning the system with the most evaluator preferences on a given query wins that query. The paper reports the percentage of queries where MindSearch is preferred. The correspondence between response and method is hidden from evaluators.

- **Baselines.** The paper compares against several baselines across different experiment categories:
  - **w/o Search Engine**: The raw LLM answering from parametric knowledge, with no internet access at all (Table 1).
  - **ReAct Search** (Yao et al., 2022b): A ReAct-style agent that interleaves reasoning with search engine tool calls, using a predefined JSON format for actions. This serves as the primary search-augmented baseline for closed-set QA (Table 1, Table 2).
  - **CodeAct** (Wang et al., 2024): The WebSearcher wrapped as a Python function, with the LLM generating code to call it, but without the graph construction abstraction. Used in the WebPlanner ablation (Table 2).
  - **self-ask** (Press et al., 2022) and **Searchain** (Xu et al., 2024): Additional state-of-the-art web search baselines compared in Appendix A, Table 4.
  - **ChatGPT-Web** (based on GPT-4o) and **Perplexity.ai Pro**: Commercial AI search products used as baselines in the open-set human evaluation (Figure 4).
  - **Single-turn search**: The pattern "adopted by most current AI search engines" where only one search call is made per question (Section 3.4, Figure 6).

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper does not measure compute in FLOPs or tokens. Instead, it measures **inference time cost** normalized by single-turn interactions. Specifically, Section 3.4 and Figure 6 compare search patterns in terms of: (1) "single-turn search" (one search call), (2) "multi-turn search with ReAct" (multiple sequential turns, each involving one search call), and (3) "multi-turn search with MindSearch" (multiple turns with potentially parallel search calls within each turn). The maximum interaction turn count is limited to 10 (Section 3.4: "we limit the max interaction turn to 10 since we observe limited performance gains by enlarging this hyperparameter"). The reported metric in Figure 6 is a tradeoff between HotpotQA search performance and normalized inference time cost. MindSearch is reported to process "more than 300 pages in less than 3 minutes" (Section 2.3), but this is stated as a system characteristic rather than a controlled experimental variable.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** For closed-set QA, results are reported as single accuracy numbers per dataset subset without confidence intervals or significance testing. For open-set QA, five human evaluators independently judge each of the 100 queries, and results reflect majority voting. The paper does not report inter-annotator agreement metrics (e.g., Fleiss' kappa) or confidence intervals on the preference percentages. For the comparison with state-of-the-art methods in Appendix A, Table 4, the paper uses the same HotpotQA test set across methods. The paper does not describe any cross-validation protocol, multiple random seeds, or statistical significance testing for any experimental result. This is a notable methodological limitation—all reported differences (e.g., 64.0 vs. 61.3 in Table 2) are presented as point estimates without uncertainty quantification.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Closed-Set QA Results (Table 1)

Table 1 presents the primary closed-set evaluation comparing MindSearch against two baselines (w/o Search Engine and ReAct Search) across three benchmarks, for both GPT-4o and InternLM2.5-7B-Chat.

**Headline numbers:** MindSearch with GPT-4o achieves an average score of 59.8 across all closed-set tasks, compared to 55.1 for ReAct Search and 53.5 for the no-search baseline. With InternLM2.5-7B-Chat, MindSearch achieves 49.2 average, compared to 42.9 for ReAct Search and 28.9 for no-search. The absolute improvements are +4.7 points on GPT-4o and +6.3 points on InternLM2.5-7B-Chat over the ReAct baseline.

**Benchmark-level breakdown:**

On **Bamboogle** (GPT-4o): MindSearch improves monotonically over ReAct as hop complexity increases. For 2-hop questions: 76.8 vs. 75.2 (small +1.6 gap). For 3-hop: 60.0 vs. 48.0 (+12.0). For 4-hop: 35.0 vs. 25.0 (+10.0). The base model without search achieves 70.4, 54.0, and 22.0 respectively. With InternLM2.5-7B-Chat: MindSearch achieves 67.8, 46.0, 20.0 vs. ReAct at 55.2, 38.0, 17.0—gaps of +12.6, +8.0, and +3.0 respectively. Note that the 4-hop improvement is larger on GPT-4o (+10.0) than on InternLM (+3.0), while the overall average improvement is larger on InternLM (+6.3 vs. +4.7), suggesting the gains are distributed differently across difficulty levels depending on base model capability.

On **Musique** (GPT-4o): MindSearch achieves 80.0 Easy, 74.0 Medium, 78.0 Hard vs. ReAct at 81.0, 73.0, 70.0. This is a mixed result: MindSearch slightly underperforms ReAct on Easy (-1.0), matches on Medium (+1.0), and gains substantially on Hard (+8.0). With InternLM2.5-7B-Chat: MindSearch achieves 69.0, 66.0, 57.0 vs. ReAct at 69.0, 56.0, 49.0—matching on Easy and gaining +10.0 on Medium and +8.0 on Hard. This pattern—gains concentrated in harder subsets—is consistent with MindSearch's decomposition advantage mattering more when questions require multi-step reasoning.

On **HotpotQA** (GPT-4o): The average across difficulty levels is not explicitly stated in Table 1 but can be computed: (80.0 + 74.0 + 78.0) / 3 = 77.3 for MindSearch vs. (81.0 + 73.0 + 70.0) / 3 = 74.7 for ReAct. With InternLM2.5-7B-Chat: MindSearch averages 64.0 vs. 58.0 for ReAct. The gap on the Hard subset is +8.0 for GPT-4o (78.0 vs. 70.0) and +8.0 for InternLM2.5 (57.0 vs. 49.0)—consistent across model scales.

**Cross-model pattern:** The improvement from MindSearch is larger for the weaker base model (InternLM2.5-7B: +6.3 average) than for the stronger model (GPT-4o: +4.7 average). The authors interpret this as evidence that "MindSearch provides a simple approach to enhance weak LLMs with broader knowledge and alleviate hallucination issues" (Section 3.2.2). This is a practically significant finding: the architecture provides proportionally greater benefit to models that need it more.

#### Open-Set QA Human Evaluation (Figure 4, Figure 5)

Figure 4 presents the human preference results on 100 curated queries. Five human experts independently evaluated responses from MindSearch (InternLM2.5-7B-Chat), ChatGPT-Web (GPT-4o), and Perplexity.ai Pro.

**Headline numbers:** MindSearch is preferred in **83% of cases for breadth**, **70% for depth**, and **~72% for factuality** (read from Figure 4 bar chart). The factuality preference is approximately 72% (the paper states "MindSearch does not yield much better performance in terms of facticity, compared to breadth (70% vs 83%)" in Section 3.1.2, indicating factuality preference is around 70-72%).

**Interpretation of the breadth advantage:** The 83% preference on breadth is the strongest result and aligns with the architectural design—MindSearch's DAG decomposition explicitly explores multiple parallel sub-topics, which naturally produces responses covering "various aspects of the question or multiple related fields, offering different perspectives or solutions" (the paper's definition of breadth).

**Interpretation of the factuality finding:** The paper notes that factuality improvement is more modest, attributing it to the observation that "more detailed search results may distract the concentration of the model on the initial problem, especially when LLM holds incomplete long-context capability" (Section 3.1.2). This is a candid acknowledgment of a limitation: broader search coverage may introduce information that the LLM then mishandles during synthesis, partially offsetting the benefit of finding more sources. The paper frames this as "a natural future work of MindSearch is to alleviate the hallucination issues during the web browsing process."

**Qualitative example (Figure 5):** The paper provides a side-by-side comparison of MindSearch and Perplexity.ai Pro responses for a question about Chang'e-6's technical challenges and comparison with Apollo 11 (the full responses are in Appendix F). The MindSearch response is substantially longer and more structured—it breaks the answer into labeled sections for each technical challenge (Communication, Navigation, Power Supply, Sample Collection, Scientific Advancements) with explicit numbered citations ([[1]], [[2]], etc.), then provides a separate comparison section with Apollo 11, and finally a section on China's contribution to international space exploration. The Perplexity.ai response is shorter, uses bullet-point formatting, and provides less detail on how each challenge was overcome. The qualitative difference illustrates the paper's claim that MindSearch "provides more detailed and proper responses thanks to its fine-grained searches."

**Methodological note on the open-set evaluation:** The evaluation protocol described in Appendix F specifies that: (1) labelers have no prior knowledge about which method produced which response, (2) responses are labeled as anonymous "model1," "model2," "model3" with order randomized per question, and (3) labelers can move back to previous questions to re-check their choices. This is a reasonable blinding protocol, though the fact that model responses have different lengths and formatting styles (MindSearch's responses are notably longer and more structured, as shown in Appendix F) may partially unblind the comparison. The paper does not report whether evaluators were asked which model they thought produced each response. The small number of evaluators (5) and queries (100) means that individual evaluator idiosyncrasies could meaningfully influence the aggregate percentages; without inter-annotator agreement metrics, it's impossible to assess how consistent the preference patterns are across evaluators.

#### Inference Time Scaling vs. Search Performance (Figure 6, Section 3.4)

Figure 6 analyzes the tradeoff between search performance and inference time cost across three search patterns on HotpotQA with InternLM2.5-7B-Chat.

**Headline numbers:** The figure shows three bars for each search pattern, labeled with search performance (accuracy on HotpotQA) and normalized inference time cost. The exact numerical values are not fully legible from the paper's description, but the relative findings are:

- **Single-turn search** achieves "a passing grade on the easy level and uses the shortest time" (Section 3.4). This is described as meeting "the need for a large portion of real-world usage" and is noted as "possibly the reason why most AI search engines adopt this pattern."

- **Multi-turn search with ReAct** enables "linear-scale reasoning" at the cost of more inference time. Performance improves compared to single-turn, but with a linear increase in inference steps.

- **Multi-turn search with MindSearch** achieves "more efficient scaling performance with less inference steps" compared to ReAct. The authors frame this as evidence that "MindSearch provides a better scaling strategy for improving the search performance" (Section 3.4).

The key claim is about **scaling efficiency**: for a given increase in inference time, MindSearch yields larger performance gains than ReAct, because its parallel execution model allows multiple searches within a single turn rather than requiring sequential turns for each search. This is a direct consequence of the DAG's parallelism—independent sub-questions dispatch WebSearchers concurrently, so wall-clock time scales sub-linearly with the number of searches.

**Important caveat:** The maximum turn limit is set to 10 for all multi-turn methods. The paper states that this is because "we observe limited performance gains by enlarging this hyperparameter" (Section 3.4). This saturation at 10 turns suggests that beyond a certain depth of decomposition, additional sub-questions either produce redundant information or cannot be effectively formulated by the LLM within the current architecture.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

All ablations in Section 3.3 use InternLM2.5-7B-Chat on HotpotQA unless otherwise specified, and the WebSearcher is held constant when ablating WebPlanner components (and vice versa).

#### Ablations on WebPlanner (Table 2, Section 3.3.1)

**Planning strategy comparison**: The paper compares three planning strategies for WebPlanner while keeping the WebSearcher fixed:

- **ReAct** (JSON format, `Thought: str; Action: WebSearcher(List[str])`): 58.0 accuracy on HotpotQA.
- **CodeAct** (functional code, no graph structure): 61.3 accuracy.
- **MindSearch** (graph-based code planning): 64.0 accuracy.

The baseline without any web search (`-`) achieves 37.6. The progression from ReAct → CodeAct (+3.3) isolates the benefit of code over natural language actions. The further progression from CodeAct → MindSearch (+2.7) isolates the benefit of graph construction over flat function calling. The authors note that "the performance continues to improve, especially on the hard set of HotpotQA, which further validates the superiority of our proposed planning as graph construction" (Section 3.3.1), though the per-difficulty breakdown is not provided in Table 2 itself.

#### Ablations on WebSearcher (Table 3, Section 3.3.2)

Each component of the WebSearcher's hierarchical retrieval pipeline is ablated by removing it while keeping the WebPlanner fixed:

- **Full MindSearch**: 64.0.
- **w/o Multi-Query Generation (MQG)**: 60.6 (removing the query rewriting step that generates multiple similar queries to broaden recall). This is a −3.4 point drop, the largest single-component impact.
- **w/o Prefix Previous Context (PPC)**: 63.3 (removing the graph-edge-based context propagation, so each WebSearcher only sees its own sub-question without parent/root context). This is a −0.7 point drop—small but consistently present.
- **w/o Page Selection (PS)**: 58.0 (removing the coarse-to-fine page selection step, meaning the WebSearcher must process all search results directly without metadata-based filtering). This causes a −6.0 point drop, actually the largest absolute impact.

The finding that removing Page Selection causes the largest degradation (−6.0) is particularly revealing: it suggests that the hierarchical retrieval structure (coarse filtering by snippets, then deep reading of selected pages) is more critical than multi-query generation for the WebSearcher's effectiveness. Without page selection, the WebSearcher's context window is flooded with full web page content, likely causing the LLM to miss relevant information or fail to synthesize effectively—a direct manifestation of the information overload problem the architecture is designed to solve.

**Negative/non-obvious finding:** Removing multi-query generation (−3.4) has a substantial but smaller impact than removing page selection (−6.0). This implies that the noise-filtering function of the WebSearcher (selecting which pages to read) is more important than the recall-broadening function (finding more potentially relevant pages). Improving recall without also improving filtering would likely harm performance by introducing more noise.

#### Comparison with State-of-the-Art Methods (Appendix A, Table 4)

The paper compares MindSearch against additional competitive baselines on HotpotQA with InternLM-7b-2.5 (note: the model name in Appendix A is "InternLM-7b-2.5" while the main experiments use "InternLM2.5-7b-chat"; this appears to be the same model with slightly different naming):

- ReAct: 58.0
- self-ask: 67.0 Easy, 59.0 Medium, 49.0 Hard → 58.3 average
- CodeAct: 70.0 Easy, 63.0 Medium, 51.0 Hard → 61.3 average
- Searchain: 70.0 Easy, 61.0 Medium, 54.0 Hard → 61.6 average
- MindSearch: 69.0 Easy, 66.0 Medium, 57.0 Hard → 64.0 average

MindSearch outperforms Searchain by +2.4 points on average, with the advantage concentrated in Medium (+5.0) and Hard (+3.0) subsets. On Easy, MindSearch is 1 point below Searchain and CodeAct (69.0 vs. 70.0). The paper attributes Searchain's weakness to the need to "re-generate the whole reasoning chain" at each step "due to its weakness in long-context reasoning, which is time-consuming and fallible," whereas MindSearch's multi-agent design "is able to reason at each step immediately when necessary" (Appendix A).

#### DAG Depth Analysis (Appendix C.1, Table 5)

Using GPT-4o on Musique, the paper examines the relationship between the number of hops in the question and the depth of the generated DAG:

- 2-hop questions → DAG depth 1.1
- 3-hop questions → DAG depth 1.2
- 4-hop questions → DAG depth 1.6

The DAG depth is consistently lower than the number of hops. The authors provide two explanations: (1) "MindSearch allows parallel execution, which only increases the tree by one but may resolve multiple questions at the current step"—independent sub-questions are resolved in the same graph layer, (2) "despite the question claiming 2 or more hops, there exist short-cuts or simplifications in the question, resulting shorter search path"—the actual information dependencies may be simpler than the question template implies. This is a useful validation that the DAG structure genuinely captures decomposition rather than just replicating the question's surface-level hop count.

#### Query Efficiency Analysis (Appendix C.2)

The paper reports that MindSearch generates on average **0.3 fewer queries per problem** compared to ReAct (3.2 vs. 3.5). The counterintuitive finding—MindSearch explores more breadth (as evidenced by the breadth preference in Figure 4) while using fewer total queries—is explained by ReAct's tendency to "spend more queries repeatedly searching for some keywords, which is useless and inefficient" due to its "weaker ability... to decompose the question" (Appendix C.2). MindSearch's more accurate queries mean each search retrieves more targeted information, reducing the need for retries and reformulations.

#### Generalization to Other Language Models (Appendix D, Table 6)

MindSearch is tested with three additional LLMs on HotpotQA:

- DeepSeek-V2: 70.0 Easy, 71.0 Medium, 68.0 Hard → 69.6 average
- Qwen-2.5-7B: 62.0 Easy, 59.0 Medium, 52.0 Hard → 57.6 average
- GLM-4-9B: 65.0 Easy, 60.0 Medium, 55.0 Hard → 60.0 average

These results demonstrate that MindSearch "can easily adapt to various models with little adaptation" (Appendix D). The DeepSeek-V2 result (69.6 average) substantially exceeds the InternLM2.5-7B result from the main experiments (64.0), suggesting the framework benefits from stronger base models. The Qwen-2.5-7B result (57.6) is notably lower than InternLM2.5-7B (64.0), indicating that the base model's capability remains a significant factor—the architecture amplifies but does not fully compensate for differences in underlying model quality.

#### Error Correction Capability (Appendix E, Figures 7, 8, 9)

The paper provides qualitative examples of two error correction mechanisms:

**Code execution feedback (Figure 7):** The WebPlanner generates code referencing a non-existent node name. The Python interpreter raises an error. In the next turn, the LLM reads the error message and regenerates correct code. This validates the code-as-planning benefit of automatic syntactic validation.

**Information-not-found handling (Figure 8):** A WebSearcher returns "information not found" for a query about "the final match of UEFA Euro 2020." The WebPlanner reads this response and regenerates a query with "more information about the final match of UEFA Euro 2020," which successfully finds information about corner kicks in Wikipedia pages. This demonstrates semantic error recovery.

**Graceful failure (Figure 9):** When multiple search attempts fail, the WebPlanner terminates the search branch and proceeds with what information it has, "which avoids the model falling into a repeatedly and meaningless loop" (Appendix E.2). This is an important robustness mechanism—without it, the agent could cycle indefinitely on unfindable information.

These are qualitative demonstrations, not quantitative ablations. They illustrate that the error correction mechanisms exist and function as designed, but the paper does not measure how often errors occur, how often they are successfully corrected, or whether certain error types are uncorrectable.

### Critical Assessment

#### Claim 1: MindSearch significantly improves response quality over baselines on closed-set QA

The evidence in Table 1 supports this claim with qualifications. On the average across all closed-set tasks, MindSearch outperforms ReAct by +4.7 (GPT-4o) and +6.3 (InternLM2.5-7B). However, the pattern is not uniform. On Musique Easy with GPT-4o, MindSearch (80.0) slightly underperforms ReAct (81.0). This suggests that for sufficiently simple questions answerable through a small number of straightforward searches, the overhead of graph construction may not provide benefit over linear reasoning. The claim should be refined to: MindSearch provides substantial benefits on medium-to-hard questions requiring multi-step decomposition, with minimal or no benefit (and possible slight degradation) on the easiest questions where single-step or linear search is sufficient.

**What was tested:** Three established multi-hop QA benchmarks with both proprietary and open-source models.

**What was not tested:** The experiments do not include ablations varying the maximum turn limit systematically (all results use 10 turns), so the sensitivity to this hyperparameter is unknown. No confidence intervals are reported, so it's unclear whether the +4.7 and +6.3 improvements are statistically significant or within the range of sampling variance across different test set compositions. Given the HotpotQA test set size (likely hundreds of questions, but exact size not specified for the splits used), a standard error on a 60% accuracy would be roughly ±2-3 percentage points for a test set of ~500 examples, which is comparable in magnitude to some of the reported improvements.

#### Claim 2: Human evaluators prefer MindSearch responses over commercial alternatives

The open-set evaluation (Figure 4) shows preference rates of 70-83% across depth, breadth, and factuality. This is strong directional evidence but has important limitations:

**Strengths:** The evaluation uses real human evaluators (not LLM-as-judge), includes blinding, randomized response ordering, and evaluates on three distinct quality dimensions. The comparison includes two real commercial products, making it an ecologically valid benchmark.

**Weaknesses:**
- **Sample size:** 100 queries and 5 evaluators. With majority voting and 3 choices, the expected win rate under the null hypothesis (all systems equally good) is 33%. The observed 70-83% rates clearly exceed this, but the confidence interval on a binomial proportion with n=100 is approximately ±9-10 percentage points. So the true preference rate could be anywhere from ~60% to ~92% for breadth.
- **No inter-annotator agreement reported:** Without metrics like Fleiss' kappa, we cannot assess whether the 5 evaluators consistently agreed on which response was better, or whether the majority vote masks high disagreement. If evaluators frequently disagreed (e.g., 3-2 splits), the majority vote winner is a noisy signal.
- **Potential unblinding:** As noted, MindSearch responses are characteristically longer and more structured (see Appendix F). Evaluators may have inferred which system produced which response, even with anonymous labeling, based on response style.
- **Query representativeness:** The 100 queries are "carefully curate[d]" but no details are given about their source, domain distribution, or difficulty. If the queries were selected to highlight MindSearch's strengths (complex, multi-faceted questions), the preference rates may not generalize to the broader distribution of real-world search queries, many of which are simple factoid questions where single-turn search suffices.
- **Comparison with proprietary baselines uses different base models:** MindSearch with InternLM2.5-7B is compared against ChatGPT-Web which uses GPT-4o. This confounds the architecture comparison with the base model comparison—MindSearch wins despite a weaker base model, which strengthens the architectural claim, but the converse question ("would MindSearch with GPT-4o substantially outperform ChatGPT-Web?") is not tested, since the open-set evaluation only uses the InternLM2.5-7B backend.

**Experiment that would strengthen the claim:** A within-model comparison—MindSearch vs. ReAct on the same 100 open-set queries with the same base model, judged by the same evaluators—would isolate the architectural contribution from the base model effect. The current design shows that MindSearch + 7B model competes with GPT-4o-based products, which is impressive, but doesn't cleanly measure the architecture's additive value for open-set quality.

#### Claim 3: The multi-agent design processes 300+ pages in 3 minutes (60× faster than humans)

Appendix B provides time measurements: 10 complex research questions, human labelers required 19h17min total, MindSearch required 23 minutes. This is an approximately 50× efficiency ratio (23 minutes vs. 19.3 hours = ~1158 minutes; 1158/23 ≈ 50). The claim of "3 hours of human effort" per question in Section 2.3 appears to be an approximation (19.3 hours / 10 questions ≈ 1.93 hours, not 3 hours; the per-question breakdown gives 47 min searching + 74 min writing = 121 minutes ≈ 2 hours).

This is suggestive but has significant limitations as a controlled experiment:
- **Human time measurement methodology is not detailed:** Were human labelers working continuously or with breaks? Were they domain experts in the question topics or generalists? What instructions were they given about thoroughness? The variability in human research time across different people, topics, and thoroughness levels is enormous—the 121-minute average could easily vary by a factor of 2-3× with different human labelers or instructions.
- **Quality equivalence is not established:** The paper assumes human and MindSearch outputs are comparable in quality, but this is not measured. If MindSearch's output is lower quality (as suggested by the factuality results in Figure 4, where it's preferred only ~72% of the time despite its speed advantage), the time comparison overstates the efficiency gain for equivalent-quality output.
- **The 300+ pages figure is not independently measured:** No methodology is given for counting "pages processed." This could include pages whose snippets were read during the coarse filtering stage (lightweight processing) or only pages whose full content was fetched and read (heavyweight processing). The distinction matters for understanding where the time is spent.

#### Claim 4: Code-as-planning with graph construction outperforms natural language planning

Table 2 directly supports this: CodeAct (61.3) > ReAct (58.0), and MindSearch (64.0) > CodeAct (61.3). The ablations cleanly isolate each factor (code format, graph structure) since the WebSearcher is held constant.

**Strengths:** This is the cleanest and most convincing ablation in the paper. Each comparison isolates a single architectural choice.

**Weaknesses:**
- **No confidence intervals:** With a test set of likely ~500 questions (the HotpotQA full test set), the standard error on these accuracy differences is approximately ±2-3 percentage points. The observed gaps of +3.3 (CodeAct over ReAct) and +2.7 (MindSearch over CodeAct) are moderate relative to this noise level.
- **Single model tested in ablation:** Only InternLM2.5-7B. The relative benefit of code-based planning may differ for stronger models (GPT-4o) that might be better at natural language planning or weaker models that might struggle with code syntax. The generalization experiments in Appendix D test full MindSearch on other models but don't provide ReAct/CodeAct baselines for those models, so we can't assess whether the code-as-planning advantage holds across model families.

#### Claim 5: MindSearch with open-source models delivers competitive AI search

The human evaluation (Figure 4) and the closed-set results (Table 1) collectively support this claim. The 7B model with MindSearch outperforms GPT-4o with ReAct on closed-set QA (49.2 vs. 55.1, a 5.9-point gap, but compare to the 28.9 base performance of the 7B model). On open-set QA, the 7B model is preferred over GPT-4o-based products.

**Caveat that the paper acknowledges but doesn't fully address:** The closed-set results show that the 7B model's base performance (28.9 average without search) lies far below GPT-4o's (53.5). MindSearch partially closes this gap (49.2 vs. 59.8, a 10.6-point remaining gap) but does not eliminate it—GPT-4o with MindSearch still substantially outperforms InternLM2.5-7B with MindSearch. The "competitive" claim holds primarily for the open-set human evaluation, where the InternLM-based MindSearch is compared against GPT-4o-based commercial products that may not be using optimal search strategies. If Perplexity.ai Pro or ChatGPT-Web were to adopt similar multi-agent decomposition architectures, the base model capability gap might re-emerge.

#### Overall Methodological Assessment

The paper's experimental design is adequate for a systems paper demonstrating architectural innovation, but falls short of the rigor standard for making precise quantitative claims. The key limitations are:

1. **Absence of uncertainty quantification across all results.** No confidence intervals, standard deviations, or statistical tests are reported for any experimental result. In a paper where several claims rest on numerical differences of 2-6 percentage points on test sets of hundreds of examples, this omission makes it difficult to assess the reliability of the reported improvements.

2. **No sensitivity analysis for key hyperparameters.** The maximum turn limit (10) is stated as a hyperparameter with "limited performance gains" beyond it, but no data is shown for varying this limit. Similarly, the number of queries generated by the multi-query generation step, the number of pages selected for detailed reading, and other WebSearcher parameters are not systematically varied in ablations.

3. **Open-set evaluation has methodological gaps.** The small evaluator pool (5), lack of inter-annotator agreement metrics, and non-transparent query selection process limit the generalizability of the human preference results. The fact that the comparative baselines use different base models confounds the architectural and model-scale effects.

4. **Single search engine (Bing) throughout.** All experiments use the Bing Search API. The paper mentions compatibility with Google and DuckDuckGo but provides no evidence that results would replicate across search engines. Search engine result quality varies substantially, and the hierarchical retrieval pipeline's effectiveness may depend on snippet quality and ranking accuracy of the underlying engine.

5. **No cost analysis beyond wall-clock time.** The paper reports inference time scaling (Figure 6) but doesn't analyze API costs (Bing search API calls are not free), token consumption across the multi-agent system, or total compute FLOPs. For practitioners deciding whether to adopt MindSearch, the monetary cost per query is at least as important as latency.

**Missing experiments that would strengthen the paper:**

- An ablation varying the number of parallel WebSearcher instances to quantify the throughput-vs-quality tradeoff explicitly.
- A comparison where ChatGPT-Web or Perplexity.ai Pro are given the same Bing API access (rather than their native search backends) to isolate the search engine quality confound.
- A study of how performance varies with query complexity using a continuous difficulty metric rather than discrete Easy/Medium/Hard labels—this would reveal whether there's a complexity threshold below which the architectural overhead of MindSearch is not justified.
- Latency measurements broken down by component (planning time, search execution time, page fetching time, synthesis time) to identify the bottleneck in the 3-minute figure.
- A failure analysis categorizing the types of errors MindSearch makes (decomposition failures, retrieval failures, synthesis failures, hallucination) to guide future improvement priorities.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### 6.1 Difficulty Estimation via Brute-Force Sampling Is Impractical for Deployment

**The assumption or constraint.** The core mechanism that enables the compute-optimal allocation—difficulty estimation by generating 2048 samples per prompt and computing the base model's pass@1 rate—is computationally prohibitive at inference time. The paper explicitly acknowledges this: "estimating difficulty in this way still incurs additional computation cost during inference... our experiments do not account for this cost largely for simplicity" (Section 3.2). The predicted (non-oracle) variant replaces ground-truth correctness with the PRM's final-answer score but still requires the same 2048 samples, leaving the fundamental cost unchanged.

**The consequence.** In a realistic deployment, the total cost of answering a question would be **difficulty estimation + strategy execution**, where the first term could dominate the second. For example, if 2048 samples are needed to determine that a question is "easy" and only requires 16 generations of sequential revision, the system has spent 99.2% of its total compute budget on difficulty estimation. The reported 4× efficiency gains over best-of-N (Figures 4 and 8) are computed *after* difficulty is known, without amortizing the cost of learning it. This means the paper's headline efficiency numbers represent an **upper bound on achievable efficiency**—a bound that may be unattainable in practice until a cheaper difficulty estimation method is developed.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The predicted-difficulty curves largely overlap with oracle-difficulty curves in Figures 4 and 8, confirming that the PRM-based proxy works for difficulty binning—but this does not address the cost issue, since the proxy still requires 2048 generations. Appendix C provides detailed time measurements showing that human labelers spend 47 minutes searching and reading plus 74 minutes writing per complex question (approximately 121 minutes total), while MindSearch completes the same tasks in roughly 2.3 minutes per question. These measurements are for the MindSearch web search system, not for the PRM-based difficulty estimation in the reference paper, but the analogy holds: the overhead of estimating difficulty before executing the optimal strategy could easily erase the efficiency gains that the strategy itself provides.

**Mitigation status.** The paper treats this as a primary open problem. Section 3.2 explicitly frames it as "an exploration-exploitation tradeoff—compute spent assessing difficulty versus compute spent solving the problem—flagging it as a key avenue for future work." Section 8 proposes that future work should "pretrain or finetune models to directly predict difficulty of a question," but no such model is developed or evaluated. Until cheap difficulty estimation is demonstrated, the compute-optimal framework remains a proof-of-concept that the *existence* of difficulty-dependent optimal strategies is real and exploitable, but not that the exploitation is currently cost-effective.

---

### 6.2 The Method Fails Completely on Hard Problems Outside the Base Model's Capability Range

**The constraint.** Test-time compute—whether search, revisions, or their compute-optimal combination—provides essentially zero benefit on the hardest questions (difficulty bin 5). The paper is candid about this: hard problems show "near-zero improvement regardless of budget" (Section 5.3, Section 6.1, Section 7). In Figure 3 (right), bin 5 accuracy hovers at 1–3% for all methods and all budgets. In Figure 7 (right), bin 5 shows roughly 2–3% accuracy irrespective of the sequential-to-parallel ratio. In the FLOPs-matched comparison (Figure 9), the bin 5 scaling line is essentially flat near 0–5%.

**The consequence.** Test-time compute can **amplify existing capability but cannot create it**. If the base model's pass@1 rate on a problem class is near zero—meaning the model almost never produces a correct solution in its initial samples—then no amount of search, revision, or adaptive allocation will find one. This establishes a hard boundary on the applicability of the framework: it works only for problems within the base model's rough capability range. For genuinely novel, out-of-distribution, or deeply challenging reasoning tasks, pretraining remains the only viable path to competence. The FLOPs-matched analysis (Section 7, Figure 9) quantifies this: on bin 5 problems, the ~14× larger pretrained model substantially outperforms the smaller model with any amount of test-time compute, with relative disadvantages of −37.2% to −52.9% for the test-time approach depending on the $R$ regime.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The consistent near-zero performance on bin 5 across all experiments—search (Figure 3 right), revisions (Figure 7 right), compute-optimal combinations (Figures 4, 8), and FLOPs-matched comparisons (Figure 9)—provides overwhelming evidence that this is a fundamental limitation, not a methodological artifact. The paper's difficulty binning methodology itself provides the diagnostic: bin 5 is defined as the quintile where the base model's pass@1 is lowest, confirming that the failure is caused by the base model's inability to generate correct solutions, not by any flaw in the search or revision mechanisms.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not attempt to solve this limitation; it explicitly acknowledges it as a boundary condition. Section 7's takeaway box states that test-time compute is not effective when "problems are fundamentally outside the base model's capability range." Section 8 frames this as a natural limitation: "test-time compute amplifies existing capability but does not create it from nothing." The paper positions this finding as useful *characterization*—knowing when not to spend test-time compute is as valuable as knowing when to spend it—but offers no path for addressing the limitation beyond "scale pretraining."

---

### 6.3 Single Benchmark and Single Model Family Constrain Generalization Claims

**The constraint.** All experiments use the MATH benchmark (500 test questions) with the PaLM 2-S\* model family. The paper states it "believe[s] this model is representative of the capabilities of many contemporary LLMs" (Section 4), but this claim is unverified. Several aspects of the findings could be model-specific or benchmark-specific:
- The PRM's quality and over-optimization behavior depend on PaLM 2-S\*'s output distribution. A model with different calibration properties or different error patterns might exhibit different difficulty-dependent scaling curves. 
- The revision model's ability to learn from incorrect in-context examples depends on the base model's in-context learning capabilities, which vary substantially across model families.
- The MATH benchmark consists exclusively of competition-level math problems requiring symbolic reasoning. It is unclear whether the difficulty-dependent patterns (beam search hurting easy problems, revisions helping easy problems) generalize to other reasoning domains such as code generation, logical reasoning, or scientific QA, or to tasks requiring factual knowledge rather than inference.

**The consequence.** A practitioner considering deploying the compute-optimal framework for a different domain (e.g., code generation, legal reasoning, medical QA) or with a different model family (e.g., LLaMA, Claude, Gemini) cannot assume that the specific difficulty thresholds, optimal strategy assignments, or even the qualitative patterns (beam search outperforming best-of-N on medium-difficulty problems) will transfer. The framework is *conceptually* general—difficulty-conditioned allocation should help in any domain—but the *instantiation* (which strategies work best at which difficulty levels, at what budgets) likely requires per-domain and per-model calibration.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** No experiments are conducted outside MATH or with non-PaLM models. The generalization experiments in the reference example paper do not exist—the current paper provides zero evidence about cross-domain or cross-model-family transfer. This is the most significant gap in the paper's empirical coverage, and the authors do not acknowledge it in the limitations discussion.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not claim generalization to other domains or model families, but it also does not explicitly flag this as a limitation. A reader unfamiliar with the MATH benchmark's specific characteristics might overestimate the breadth of applicability.

### 6.4 The 14× Larger Model Baseline Is Not Compute-Optimally Trained, Inflating the Apparent Advantage of Test-Time Compute

**The constraint.** The FLOPs-matched comparison in Section 7 scales model parameters while holding training data fixed, following the LLaMA paradigm (Touvron et al., 2023). This departs from compute-optimal pretraining as established by Hoffmann et al. (2022), where both data and parameters should be scaled equally under a fixed compute budget. The paper acknowledges this explicitly: "We choose this setting as it is representative of a canonical approach to scaling pretraining compute and leave the analysis of compute-optimal scaling of pretraining compute where the data and parameters are both scaled equally to future work" (Section 7). Additionally, the 14× larger model uses only greedy decoding—no majority voting, no best-of-N, no search of any kind. This means the comparison is between a smaller model with sophisticated test-time strategies and a larger model with no test-time augmentation whatsoever.

**The consequence.** A Chinchilla-optimal model trained with 14× more total FLOPs (scaling both parameters and data) would likely outperform a parameter-only-scaled model, making the pretraining baseline **measurably stronger** than what the paper tests. The reported advantages of test-time compute over pretraining—e.g., +27.8% on easy questions at $R \ll 1$ (Figure 1, top-right bar chart)—may shrink or disappear against a properly compute-optimal larger model. Furthermore, giving the larger model even a modest test-time compute budget (e.g., best-of-4 or best-of-8) would create a much fairer comparison between "spend compute on pretraining" and "spend compute on inference"—a comparison the paper does not make. The current setup shows that test-time compute can compensate for *parameter count*, but does not convincingly show it can compensate for *compute-optimally allocated pretraining resources*.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper's transparency about the baseline design provides the evidence for this limitation. Section 7 states the FLOPs accounting and acknowledges the departure from Chinchilla-optimal scaling. No ablation is conducted with a compute-optimally trained larger model or with test-time compute applied to the larger model. The choice to fix data and scale only parameters is a reasonable simplification for a first study, but it means the FLOPs-matched results should be interpreted as an upper bound on the advantages of test-time compute over pretraining.

**Mitigation status.** The paper flags this as future work in Section 7: "we leave the analysis of compute-optimal scaling of pretraining compute where the data and parameters are both scaled equally to future work." This is an honest acknowledgment, but the framing of the findings (Figure 1 bar charts showing large test-time compute advantages) does not prominently caveat that the baseline is weaker than compute-optimal pretraining would produce. A practitioner reading only the figures might conclude that test-time compute is categorically superior to pretraining for easy-to-medium problems, when in fact the comparison is between optimized test-time compute and suboptimal pretraining.

### 6.5 The Revision Model Has a Fundamental Correct-to-Incorrect Reversion Problem With Only Partial Mitigation

**The constraint.** The revision model is trained exclusively on sequences where all in-context answers are incorrect, followed by a correct target (Section 6.1). At test time, when the model encounters a correct answer in its own revision chain—which occurs with non-trivial probability since the model's pass@1 improves through the chain (Figure 6, left)—it has no training signal for what to do. The paper reports that "approximately 38% of correct answers get converted back to incorrect ones" (Section 6.1) when using a naive approach of always taking the final revision. This is not a minor edge case; it means that continuing to revise beyond the point where a correct answer has been found is actively harmful more than a third of the time.

**The consequence.** The revision process has an inherent **correctness instability**: the chain can oscillate between correct and incorrect answers, with no mechanism to detect that a correct answer has already been achieved and stop revising. The paper's mitigation—using majority voting or verifier-based selection across the entire chain, rather than always taking the last revision—reduces the impact but does not eliminate it. Majority voting across a chain requires that correct answers appear frequently enough to dominate, which may not hold if the reversion rate is high relative to the base pass@1. Verifier-based selection is only as good as the verifier, and the revision-specific ORM (Appendix J, Figure 15a) shows only modest improvement over the base-LM PRM when scoring revision outputs.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The 38% reversion rate is reported in Section 6.1 with reference to the mitigation approach. Figure 6 (left) shows that pass@1 at each step in the revision chain gradually improves but plateaus in the 23–25% range by steps 15–20, well below what would be expected if the model reliably improved. Appendix K (Figure 16) provides evidence of the fragility of revision training: attempting to optimize the revision model with ReST$^{EM}$ causes performance to degrade substantially with sequential revisions (to ~33.5% at fully sequential vs. ~38.5% at optimal ratio). This suggests the revision capability is sensitive to training data construction in ways that are not fully understood.

**Mitigation status.** The paper addresses this partially through chain-level selection (majority voting or verifier-based best-of-N across the chain), but these are post-hoc patches that don't address the root cause (the training data distribution). A more principled solution—such as training the model with explicit "no revision needed" tokens or including examples where the correct answer appears earlier in the context—is not explored. The paper acknowledges this implicitly by flagging the ReST$^{EM}$ failure as evidence that "the revision approach is sensitive to training methodology," but does not propose concrete fixes.

### 6.6 Sequential Revisions Introduce Latency That Makes the Approach Impractical for Interactive Applications

**The constraint.** The paper measures compute in "generations" (number of complete solutions sampled), which is a reasonable proxy for total FLOPs but completely ignores **wall-clock latency**. Sequential revisions are inherently serial: the model generates an answer, then conditions on that answer to generate a revision, then conditions on the revision to generate another revision, and so on. Each step depends on the output of the previous step and cannot be parallelized. A compute-optimal strategy that allocates 128 generations as 64 sequential revisions in a single chain takes approximately 64× longer in wall-clock time than a strategy that runs 128 parallel samples simultaneously on sufficient hardware.

**The consequence.** The compute-optimal policies reported in the paper often favor sequential-heavy allocations for easy-to-medium problems. Figure 7 (left) shows that at lower budgets (8–32 generations), the optimal ratio is fully sequential—all generations are spent in a single revision chain. At higher budgets (128–256), the optimal ratio is around 2:1 to 8:1 sequential-to-parallel. For latency-sensitive applications—interactive assistants, chatbots, real-time decision support—these sequential-heavy strategies may be completely impractical regardless of their FLOPs efficiency advantages. A user waiting for a response from a chatbot will not tolerate a 64× increase in latency for a 4× reduction in FLOPs.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper does not report any latency measurements, does not discuss the serialization constraint of sequential revisions, and does not include latency as a dimension in the compute-optimal optimization objective. The FLOPs accounting in Section 7 explicitly uses "generations" as the unit of cost without converting to time. The paper's own framing of the efficiency gains (4× fewer generations to match best-of-N performance) implicitly assumes that wall-clock time is proportional to generation count, which is only true if all generations are parallelizable—an assumption violated by sequential revisions.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not acknowledge the latency serialization problem anywhere in the text. Section 8 discusses future work on "combining search and revisions" and "cheap difficulty estimation," but does not mention latency-aware allocation policies (e.g., constraining the maximum sequential chain depth for latency-sensitive deployments, or dynamically switching between sequential and parallel based on a latency budget). This is a significant practical gap, since many of the most promising application scenarios for test-time compute (Section 2 framing: on-device deployment, interactive self-improvement) are precisely the scenarios where latency matters most.
