# PowerInfer-2: Fast Large Language Model Inference on a Smartphone

**ArXiv:** [2406.06282](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.06282)

## 🎯 Pitch

A smartphone can now run a massive 47-billion-parameter language model at a readable 11.68 tokens per second, something previously thought impossible. PowerInfer-2 achieves this by cleverly splitting the model's computations: it sends the dense, frequently used parts to the phone's fast NPU and the sparse, rarely used parts to the CPU, while carefully choreographing this with the slow flash storage to hide delays and smash through the phone's severe memory and speed limits.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **PowerInfer-2**, a smartphone-based LLM inference framework that enables fast execution of models exceeding device memory capacity by decomposing matrix operations into **neuron clusters** as the basic processing unit—a fine-grained abstraction that groups neurons with similar activation patterns and dynamically sizes clusters at runtime based on target computing hardware. PowerInfer-2 leverages this neuron-cluster design through two key mechanisms: **Sparsity-Aware Adaptation** (assigning dense hot-neuron clusters to the NPU while routing sparse cold-neuron clusters to the CPU, with dynamic ratio adjustment as batch sizes change during decoding) and **I/O-Aware Orchestration** (a neuron-cluster-level pipeline that overlaps computation across matrices with flash storage I/O, combined with a segmented neuron cache and differentiated loading strategies for sequential versus random reads). Evaluated on two OnePlus smartphones running models from Mistral-7B to TurboSparse-Mixtral-47B, PowerInfer-2 achieves up to a 27.8× speedup over llama.cpp and a 3.84× average speedup over LLMFlash, while being the first system to serve a 47B-parameter LLM on a smartphone at 11.68 tokens/s—establishing that smartphone LLM inference at this scale is feasible only when the system explicitly co-designs computation scheduling and I/O around hardware-specific sparse-compute asymmetries and UFS storage characteristics rather than porting PC-centric strategies.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: LLM Capability Is Inversely Correlated with Deployment Proximity to Users

The fundamental tension this paper addresses is the growing chasm between where the most capable language models run and where users actually want them. State-of-the-art LLMs—models like GPT-4, Claude-3.5, and open-weight alternatives in the tens-to-hundreds of billions of parameters—operate on datacenter GPUs with hundreds of gigabytes of high-bandwidth memory (HBM), connected via high-speed interconnects like NVLink. These models deliver capabilities that users increasingly want integrated into their daily workflows: real-time AI assistance across applications, context-aware suggestions, and autonomous task completion.

Simultaneously, smartphone manufacturers have signaled a clear strategic intention to bring LLMs on-device. As the paper documents (Section 1), Apple launched Apple Intelligence, Google shipped Gemini Nano for Pixel devices, and Samsung, Huawei, and OPPO have all announced or released LLM-powered smartphone features. The motivations for on-device deployment are substantial and genuinely compelling:

- **Latency elimination**: Network round-trips to datacenter inference endpoints introduce 50–500ms of latency depending on cellular conditions, making real-time assistance feel sluggish. On-device inference eliminates this entirely.
- **Privacy preservation**: Local processing means sensitive user data—messages, photos, documents, browsing history—never leaves the device. For personal AI assistants that need access to this data to be useful, this is not a nice-to-have but a prerequisite for user trust and regulatory compliance (e.g., GDPR, the EU AI Act).
- **Offline reliability**: Cellular dead zones, airplane mode, and network congestion become irrelevant when inference runs locally. LLM features become available anywhere, anytime, which is particularly important for core system functions like keyboard suggestions, notification summarization, and voice assistant commands.

However, the paper identifies a critical constraint that undermines this vision: **smartphone hardware limits current deployments to small language models (SLMs) that are dramatically less capable than their datacenter counterparts**. The concrete example given is Google's Gemini Nano, specifically designed for smartphone deployment, which contains only 3.25B parameters and requires less than 2GB of memory. For comparison, contemporary datacenter-deployed models (Llama-3-70B, Mixtral-8x22B, GPT-4) have parameter counts one to two orders of magnitude larger. This is not a minor capability gap—the "scaling laws" literature (Kaplan et al., 2020) has established that model performance on complex reasoning tasks scales predictably with parameter count, meaning the smartphone SLMs are fundamentally less capable than what users experience on cloud-based assistants.

### Why This Gap Matters: It's Not Just About Model Size—It's About Model Quality

The paper's framing of this problem is important because it distinguishes between two separate constraints that are often conflated:

1. **Memory capacity**: A smartphone might have 12–24GB of DRAM total, of which perhaps 8–19GB is available to a single application after the OS, background services, and other apps claim their share. A 7B-parameter model in FP16 precision requires ~14GB just for weights, plus additional memory for activations, KV cache, and runtime overhead. Models larger than ~7B simply don't fit in available memory, and even 7B models consume so much memory that the device becomes effectively single-purpose during inference—every other app's memory must be aggressively compressed or evicted.

2. **Compute throughput**: Even if a model fits in memory, generating tokens at interactive speeds (>10 tokens/s for real-time reading, >20 tokens/s for snappy interaction) requires substantial compute throughput. Smartphone processors—even flagship SoCs like the Snapdragon 8 Gen 3—have fundamentally different compute characteristics than datacenter GPUs. Their CPUs are optimized for bursty, latency-sensitive workloads rather than sustained throughput. Their NPUs are designed for dense matrix operations on small batch sizes (camera image processing, voice recognition) and lack sparse computation primitives. Their GPUs are primarily allocated to rendering and are generally slower than either CPUs or NPUs for LLM inference workloads.

The consequence is that users either get a slow, capability-limited on-device model or a fast, capable cloud-based model—but not both simultaneously. The paper argues that this is a false dichotomy that stems from system design choices, not from fundamental hardware limitations.

### The Hardware Gap Between Smartphones and PCs: Why Prior Solutions Don't Port Down

The paper's analysis of why existing solutions fail on smartphones is unusually thorough and forms the empirical backbone of its motivation. Prior work on efficient LLM inference with limited memory—specifically PowerInfer (Song et al., 2024) for sparse activation-aware CPU-GPU co-processing and LLMFlash (Alizadeh et al., 2024) for flash storage offloading—demonstrated promising results in high-end PC environments. PowerInfer showed that leveraging ReLU-based activation sparsity (where many FFN neurons produce zero outputs and can be skipped) to split "hot" frequently-activated neurons onto GPU and "cold" rarely-activated neurons onto CPU could maintain quality while reducing compute and memory pressure. LLMFlash demonstrated that models exceeding available RAM could be served by caching frequently-used neuron bundles in memory while loading the rest from NVMe SSDs on demand.

However, the paper presents concrete measurements showing that these approaches suffer catastrophic performance degradation when brought to smartphones—approximately **70% slower decoding speed for 7B models** compared to their PC performance. The authors identify two specific hardware gaps that cause this degradation, and their analysis here is worth examining in detail because these gaps are non-obvious and motivate the entire neuron-cluster design.

#### The Sparse Computing Gap

The first gap concerns **hardware support for sparse matrix operations**. On high-end PCs, discrete GPUs (e.g., NVIDIA RTX 4090) can efficiently handle both dense and sparse matrix operations compared to CPUs. The GPU's massively parallel architecture, combined with hardware support for unstructured sparsity (e.g., NVIDIA's 2:4 structured sparsity since the Ampere architecture), means that leveraging activation sparsity for compute reduction translates directly to wall-clock speedup.

Smartphones present a fundamentally different picture. Mobile SoCs integrate three types of compute units:

- **CPU**: ARM big.LITTLE architecture with heterogeneous core types. The Snapdragon 8 Gen 3 uses a 1+5+2 configuration: one Cortex-X4 "big" core for peak single-thread performance, five Cortex-A720 "mid" cores for balanced throughput, and two Cortex-A520 "little" cores for background efficiency. CPUs can handle sparse computations naturally (conditional execution, early termination on zeros) but have limited aggregate throughput for dense matrix operations due to relatively narrow SIMD units (ARM Neon, 128-bit).

- **NPU** (Neural Processing Unit): Qualcomm's Hexagon NPU is a domain-specific accelerator optimized for dense matrix-matrix and matrix-vector operations commonly found in convolutional and transformer inference. It achieves substantially higher throughput than CPU for dense computations at moderate-to-large batch sizes (the paper reports ~770 tokens/s for 7B INT4 prefill on NPU vs. ~8 tokens/s on CPU vs. ~37 tokens/s on GPU). However—and this is the critical finding—**the NPU lacks dedicated hardware support for sparse operations**. The paper states explicitly: "our implementation of sparse computation primitives on Qualcomm's NPU actually performs slower than CPU implementations." This is "stark contrast to PC platforms where GPUs excel at both dense and sparse computations."

- **GPU**: Mobile GPUs (Qualcomm Adreno) show consistent performance limitations for LLM inference. The paper's measurements in Figure 3(a) demonstrate that for matrix-vector operations, the GPU consistently performs slower than both NPU and CPU. Moreover, only about 50% of GPU kernel time is spent on actual computation—the rest is overhead. Critically, since GPUs are primarily responsible for rendering the display, using them for inference would directly compete with the device's ability to maintain smooth UI frame rates. The paper reports that "private communications with mobile manufacturers reveal that they avoid using GPUs for LLM inference in production systems."

This creates a conundrum for sparse-activation approaches like PowerInfer: on smartphones, sparse computation is forced onto CPUs, but this means the NPU's superior dense computation capabilities sit idle. The system can't simultaneously exploit sparsity (which requires CPU) and NPU throughput (which requires dense workloads). The paper's measurements in Table 2 quantify the consequence: even with all weights resident in DRAM, mobile memory bandwidth utilization reaches only ~43.9 GB/s for CPU-only computation, compared to 59.6 GB/s when both NPU and CPU are simultaneously engaged. The sparse-only approach leaves substantial performance on the table.

#### The Storage Performance Gap

The second gap concerns **flash storage I/O characteristics**. Both PowerInfer (extended for offloading) and LLMFlash rely on flash storage to hold model weights that don't fit in memory, loading them on demand. On PCs, NVMe SSDs over PCIe 4.0 provide:

- Sequential read bandwidth: ~7.5 GB/s
- Random read IOPS: ~1,200K (for 4KB blocks)
- Multiple command queues for concurrent I/O

Universal Flash Storage (UFS) 4.0 on smartphones presents starkly different characteristics:

- Sequential read bandwidth: ~4 GB/s (roughly half of NVMe)
- Random read IOPS: ~100K for 4KB blocks (**12× worse than NVMe**)
- Single command queue, severely limiting internal concurrency

But UFS limitations go deeper than these headline numbers. The paper's characterization (Section 2.3.2, Figure 3(b)) reveals four non-obvious performance characteristics that are crucial for system design:

1. **Block size sensitivity**: Sequential read bandwidth ranges from 450 MB/s with 4KB blocks to 4 GB/s with 512KB blocks. For random reads, 4KB blocks within a 128MB range achieve ~1 GB/s but drop significantly with smaller ranges. This means I/O strategy must consider whether reads are large and sequential or small and random.

2. **Data range sensitivity**: Random read performance is highly sensitive to the scope of the accessed data range. Within a 128MB range, 4KB random reads achieve 1 GB/s; across a 512MB range, this drops below 850 MB/s. Data locality—keeping frequently-accessed weights co-located in the storage address space—directly impacts I/O throughput, a consideration that doesn't apply to NVMe drives with their deep command queues and controller-level parallelism.

3. **CPU core dependence**: Storage performance is tightly coupled to which CPU core handles I/O operations, because the CPU processes UFS driver operations including interrupt handling and command queue management. A big-core at 3.3GHz achieves 1,076 MB/s for 4KB random reads; a little-core at 2.2GHz only reaches 761 MB/s. This means the I/O thread must be pinned to a high-performance core, consuming a precious resource.

4. **Limited concurrency**: With a single command queue, using multiple cores for I/O can degrade performance by up to 40% due to queue contention. The optimal configuration is typically one dedicated I/O thread, which constrains how much parallelization can be applied to weight loading.

These characteristics explain why both PowerInfer and LLMFlash suffer severely when offloading 50% of FFN weights to flash on a smartphone (Table 2): PowerInfer's decoding speed drops 89% (from 12.4 to 1.4 tokens/s) and LLMFlash drops 82% (12.9 to 2.3 tokens/s). The I/O overhead accounts for 82% and 77% of total latency respectively, with CPU utilization dropping to 31–41% during weight loading phases. The computational units spend most of their time idle waiting for flash storage, and the slow random-read performance of UFS means that even with asynchronous I/O, the compute units can't be fed fast enough.

### The Dynamic Sparsity Blindspot in Prior Work

Beyond the hardware gap, the paper identifies a conceptual gap in how prior systems handle activation sparsity. PowerInfer's approach of statically partitioning neurons into "hot" and "cold" sets based on aggregate activation frequencies, then assigning hot neurons to GPU and cold neurons to CPU, was designed for basic sampling where one token is generated at a time. However, modern LLM inference increasingly uses **advanced decoding strategies** that involve generating multiple candidate sequences in parallel:

- **Best-of-N (BoN) sampling** (Lightman et al., 2023; Snell et al., 2024): Generate N complete response sequences for a single prompt, score each with a verifier model, and select the best one. This is a key technique in the test-time compute scaling literature and is increasingly deployed in production.
- **Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS)** for reasoning: Maintain a tree of partial reasoning paths, expand promising branches, and backpropagate reward signals.
- **Map-reduce paradigms** for long-context processing: Split a long document into chunks, process each chunk independently in parallel, then aggregate results.

These strategies introduce what the paper calls **dynamic sparsity patterns**. Figure 2 provides the key evidence: it visualizes neuron activation patterns in layer 10 of Bamboo-7B under different batch sizes (1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32). The visualization shows that:

- When batch size = 1 (single token generation), activation patterns are highly sparse and irregular—many neurons are inactive, and the active ones form a dispersed pattern with no consistent hot spots.
- As batch size increases, a core set of "hot" neurons emerges that are consistently activated across most tokens in the batch. At batch size = 32, approximately 75% of neurons show high activation frequency (depicted in white), compared to less than 1% at batch size = 1.
- During decoding with advanced strategies, the effective batch size **fluctuates dynamically** over time as different candidate sequences reach their end-of-sequence tokens and terminate. A BoN-4 decoding starts with batch size 4, but as individual sequences finish generating, the batch size steps down to 3, then 2, then 1.

This means a static hot/cold partition—which might work well for fixed batch sizes on PC hardware—is fundamentally mismatched to the combination of dynamic batch sizes and heterogeneous compute capabilities on smartphones. The paper's insight is that an efficient inference system must not only exploit sparsity but must **adapt its exploitation strategy** as sparsity patterns shift during the course of a single decoding session.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

PowerInfer-2's position in the literature is defined by two deliberate departures from prior work:

**First, it rejects the assumption—implicit in both PowerInfer and LLMFlash—that the optimal computation strategy can be determined statically based on aggregate activation statistics.** Instead, it proposes that the optimal strategy depends on dynamic sparsity patterns that change with batch size, and that a smartphone inference system must adjust compute unit allocation in real-time. The paper's concept of **Sparsity-Aware Adaptation** is, at its core, a mechanism for runtime reconfiguration of the CPU-NPU workload split as batch sizes evolve during decoding.

**Second, it rejects the matrix-level I/O granularity that LLMFlash and STI (Guo et al., 2023) use for overlapping computation and storage access.** The paper's analysis of UFS characteristics shows that random read performance is so poor that waiting for all uncached neurons in a matrix to load before proceeding to the next matrix leaves compute units idle for the majority of execution time (76.7% I/O overhead in LLMFlash, per Table 4). PowerInfer-2's **neuron-cluster-level pipeline** breaks the matrix barrier entirely: as soon as one neuron cluster finishes computation, the CPU immediately begins computing a pre-cached neuron cluster from the next matrix, overlapping this computation with ongoing I/O operations for the current matrix's remaining clusters. This finer-grained pipelining is what reduces I/O overhead to 13.7%—a 5.6× reduction compared to LLMFlash.

The paper also positions itself as a **systems contribution that bridges the gap between algorithmic insights about sparsity and the hardware reality of smartphones**. The neuron-cluster abstraction is not an algorithmic novelty per se—grouping neurons by activation frequency is straightforward. The contribution is in recognizing that this grouping can simultaneously serve three purposes: (1) it enables heterogeneous compute allocation (dense NPU, sparse CPU), (2) it enables a finer-grained pipeline that hides UFS latency, and (3) it enables differentiated I/O strategies (sequential reads for hot clusters, bundled random reads for cold clusters). The system's performance gains stem from the co-design of these three mechanisms around a single abstraction, which is a distinctly systems-building contribution rather than an algorithmic one.

Finally, the paper is deliberate about scope: it targets **smartphones specifically**, not mobile devices generally. The hardware characterization in Section 2.3 is specific to Qualcomm Snapdragon SoCs and UFS storage. The paper does not claim generality to tablets, laptops, or embedded devices, and its mechanisms are designed around the specific asymmetry where NPU > CPU for dense, CPU > NPU for sparse, and GPU is unsuitable for inference. This specificity is a strength—it allows the design to be optimized for the actual hardware that billions of users carry—but also a boundary condition that the paper implicitly acknowledges.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

PowerInfer-2 is a smartphone-native inference engine that serves large language models whose weight matrices exceed the device's available DRAM by decomposing every matrix multiplication into independently-schedulable **neuron clusters**—small groups of neurons that share activation patterns and can be routed to different processors or load from storage independently. It solves the problem that prior PC-derived systems (PowerInfer, LLMFlash) collapse on smartphones because they assume either uniform compute capabilities (GPUs handle sparse and dense equally fast) or fast random-read storage (NVMe SSDs), neither of which holds on a smartphone—so PowerInfer-2 redesigns the entire inference pipeline around the insight that **the granularity of work decomposition must match both the heterogeneity of mobile processors (NPU > CPU for dense, CPU > NPU for sparse) and the pathological random-read characteristics of UFS flash storage (4KB random reads are 12× slower than NVMe)**.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The system has two phases and five major components:

**Offline Phase (runs once per model per device):**
- **Planner** — profiles the model's activation patterns across batch sizes on a representative dataset, profiles the target device's NPU throughput, CPU sparse-compute speed, memory bandwidth, and UFS I/O characteristics, then produces (1) a *neuron plan* specifying which neurons form hot clusters for NPU and cold clusters for CPU at each batch size, and (2) a *hardware plan* specifying NPU computation graphs and I/O thread configurations.

**Online Phase (runs per inference request):**
- **Adaptive Neuron Engine** — at runtime, receives the execution plan and dynamically orchestrates workload distribution: during prefill, routes all dense computation to NPU while one CPU core performs sequential weight prefetching from flash; during decoding, splits each FFN matrix between NPU (dense hot-neuron clusters) and CPU (sparse cold-neuron clusters with predictor-guided selective computation), adjusting the split ratio as batch size changes due to sequence completions.
- **Neuron Cache (in DRAM)** — a three-region cache holding (1) attention weights and KV cache permanently, (2) hot neuron clusters as dense matrices for NPU, and (3) cold neurons individually for CPU sparse access, with LRU eviction at cluster granularity for hot and neuron granularity for cold, and dynamic region resizing when batch sizes shift.
- **Neuron-Cluster-Level Pipeline** — a fine-grained scheduling mechanism that breaks the barrier between matrix computations: as soon as one neuron cluster from matrix *m* completes on a CPU core, that core immediately begins computing a cached neuron cluster from matrix *m+1*, while a dedicated I/O thread simultaneously loads uncached clusters from flash, enabling computation and random I/O to overlap across matrix boundaries.
- **Flexible Neuron Loading** — a storage engine that uses differentiated I/O strategies: sequential reads for attention weights and hot neuron clusters (maximizing UFS bandwidth at ~4 GB/s with large blocks), bundled random reads for cold neurons (grouping corresponding Gate, Up, and Down neurons by position to exploit 80% co-activation), and a two-phase loading protocol for 4-bit quantized models that defers Up/Down loading until the Gate neuron is confirmed non-zero.

Information flows as follows: a prompt enters → the Adaptive Neuron Engine checks the current batch size and selects the matching execution plan → the Neuron Cache provides in-memory weights immediately, while the Flexible Neuron Loading engine asynchronously fetches missing weights from flash using the appropriate I/O strategy → the Neuron-Cluster-Level Pipeline schedules computations on NPU (hot clusters) and CPU cores (cold clusters) such that computation on cached clusters overlaps with I/O for uncached clusters → outputs from NPU and CPU sub-computations are merged by the CPU to form the final FFN output.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the neuron-cluster abstraction itself—what defines a cluster, how cluster size is determined, and why this abstraction is the linchpin that enables all subsequent mechanisms.
- **Second**, the offline planner, because the neuron plan and hardware plan it generates are inputs to every online component; understanding how hot/cold classification works across batch sizes and how hardware profiling feeds into cluster sizing is prerequisite.
- **Third**, the Adaptive Neuron Engine, covering both the NPU-centric prefill strategy and the hybrid CPU-NPU decoding strategy, including the dynamic ratio adjustment mechanism and the asynchronous NPU graph swapping protocol.
- **Fourth**, the Neuron Cache, because it determines which weights are memory-resident versus require flash I/O, and its three-region design with dynamic region resizing directly feeds into the pipeline and loading mechanisms.
- **Fifth**, the Neuron-Cluster-Level Pipeline, which is the scheduling core that coordinates computation and I/O at the cluster granularity—explaining the five-stage decomposition of cluster processing and how it achieves the 13.7% I/O overhead reduction.
- **Sixth**, the Flexible Neuron Loading engine, covering the differentiated I/O strategies for sequential versus random reads, the Gate-Up-Down bundling optimization, and the two-phase loading protocol for quantized models.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **systems-building paper** whose core idea is that decomposing FFN matrix multiplications into neuron clusters—groups of neurons dynamically sized to match both hardware execution characteristics and activation frequencies—enables a smartphone inference system to simultaneously exploit NPU dense-compute throughput, CPU sparse-compute flexibility, and UFS storage bandwidth through fine-grained, cross-matrix pipelining of computation and I/O.

---

#### The Neuron-Cluster Abstraction

The central abstraction PowerInfer-2 introduces is the **neuron cluster**: a contiguous group of neurons (rows) from a single FFN weight matrix that share similar activation patterns and are treated as an atomic unit for scheduling, computation, and I/O. The key property of a neuron cluster is that its size is **not fixed a priori**—it is determined dynamically during offline planning based on two interacting factors: the target computing unit's performance characteristics, and the neurons' activation frequencies under specific batch sizes.

To understand why cluster sizing matters, consider what happens if clusters are too large. Suppose the planner assigns a 4096-neuron cluster to the NPU. This is efficient for the NPU, which excels at large dense matrix multiplies. But if 30% of those neurons are rarely activated (cold) under the current batch size, the NPU performs wasted computation on zeros—and the NPU has no mechanism to skip those computations efficiently. Conversely, assigning the same 4096-neuron cluster to the CPU would allow predictor-guided sparse computation (skip the cold neurons), but the CPU's throughput for the remaining dense portion would be substantially lower than the NPU's. If clusters are too small, the NPU suffers from kernel launch overhead and underutilized vector units, while the CPU benefits from finer-grained sparsity exploitation.

PowerInfer-2 resolves this tension through **hardware-aware cluster sizing**: during offline planning, the cluster size for NPU-bound hot neurons is set large enough to saturate the NPU's compute units (typically thousands of neurons per cluster, forming a sub-matrix dense enough for efficient matrix-matrix multiply), while the cluster size for CPU-bound cold neurons is set small enough that the predictor can achieve a high skip rate (typically hundreds of neurons per cluster, processed in chunks of ~512×4096 for ARM Neon vectorization). The paper does not provide a single fixed number for cluster sizes because the sizes are device-specific—the planner profiles the NPU's throughput versus matrix dimensions and the CPU's sparse-compute throughput versus sparsity ratio on the actual target device.

The abstraction operates on the **row dimension** of FFN matrices. Recall the structure of a typical FFN block in a modern LLM (using the SwiGLU or ReLU-GLU variant): it consists of three weight matrices—Gate (`W_gate`), Up (`W_up`), and Down (`W_down`)—each of shape `[d_model, d_ff]` (Gate and Up) or `[d_ff, d_model]` (Down), where `d_ff` is the intermediate dimension (typically 4× `d_model`). The neuron dimension is the `d_ff` dimension—each row of Gate/Up corresponds to one intermediate neuron, and each column of Down corresponds to one intermediate neuron. A neuron cluster groups a contiguous range of these intermediate neurons, corresponding to a row slice of Gate, a row slice of Up, and a column slice of Down. When the NPU processes a hot neuron cluster, it multiplies the input vector against the dense row slice of Gate and Up, producing partial outputs that are later element-wise multiplied and projected through Down. When the CPU processes a cold neuron cluster, it uses the predictor to determine which specific neurons within the cluster are activated, loads only those rows/columns, and performs sparse matrix-vector products.

The neuron cluster is thus the **minimum schedulable unit** that carries its own I/O requirements (loading the corresponding weight slices from flash if not cached) and its own compute requirements (which processor handles it and with what strategy). This granularity is what enables all subsequent mechanisms—without it, the system cannot distinguish between work that should go to NPU versus CPU, cannot pipeline across matrices, and cannot differentiate I/O strategies.

#### Offline Planning: Neuron Classification and Execution Plan Generation

The offline planner has three responsibilities: (1) classifying neurons into hot and cold clusters for each batch size, (2) generating NPU computation graphs for each batch-size-specific configuration, and (3) determining hardware resource allocation (number of compute threads, core assignments, I/O thread configuration). The planner runs once per model-device pair and produces an execution plan consumed by the online inference engine.

##### Neuron Classification Protocol

The classification process involves four sequential steps:

**Step 1: Activation profiling.** The planner runs the target model on a diverse text corpus comprising 10 million or more tokens drawn from Wikipedia and RefinedWeb. For each FFN layer, for each token in the corpus, it records which intermediate neurons produce non-zero activations (after the ReLU or equivalent activation function). It performs this profiling separately for different batch sizes—specifically for batch sizes 1, 2, 3, and 4 (the decoding batch sizes the system expects to handle) and for larger prefill batch sizes (128, 512). The output is, for each neuron in each layer, an activation frequency vector indexed by batch size: what fraction of tokens (or token-batches) activate this neuron when processing with batch size `b`.

**Step 2: Hardware I/O profiling.** In parallel, the planner profiles the target device's storage I/O characteristics—specifically, the available sequential read bandwidth at the block sizes that will be used for hot-cluster prefetching, and the random read throughput at the block sizes that will be used for cold-neuron loading. The key hardware parameter extracted is **how many neuron weights can be asynchronously prefetched during one attention block's computation time**. Since hot neurons are loaded during the attention computation of the previous layer (prefetching), the number of hot neurons must be bounded such that their total weight size, divided by the achievable sequential read bandwidth, is less than the attention block's execution time. This ensures that hot-neuron I/O is completely hidden behind attention computation.

**Step 3: Hot-cold threshold determination.** Given the activation frequency distributions and the I/O budget (maximum prefetchable weight size), the planner sets a frequency threshold: neurons with activation frequency above the threshold are classified as hot, and neurons below as cold. This threshold is not a fixed value—it is set per layer and per batch size such that the total weight size of hot neurons exactly saturates (but does not exceed) the I/O budget for that layer. Intuitively: the planner greedily includes the most frequently activated neurons in the hot set until it runs out of prefetch bandwidth, then assigns the remaining neurons to the cold set.

For larger batch sizes (3–4), more neurons cross the frequency threshold because activation overlap increases—recall from Figure 2 that at batch size 32, ~75% of neurons show high activation frequency. Consequently, a larger proportion of weights are classified as hot and assigned to NPU. For smaller batch sizes (1–2), sparsity is high and activation patterns are dispersed, so fewer neurons are classified as hot.

**Step 4: Cluster formation.** Within each classification (hot/cold), neurons are grouped into contiguous clusters. Hot clusters are formed by taking contiguous ranges of high-frequency neurons; their size is determined by the NPU's performance characteristics—large enough to amortize kernel launch overhead and saturate the NPU's compute units, but not so large that they include low-frequency neurons mixed in with hot ones. Cold clusters are formed from the remaining neurons; their size is set to a fixed small value (e.g., 512 neurons) that provides good predictor skip rates and matches ARM Neon's vector width for efficient sparse computation on CPU. The paper does not specify exact cluster sizes because they vary per device and model, but the principle is that hot clusters are large and NPU-optimized, cold clusters are small and CPU-optimized.

##### Batch-Adaptive Planning

Because batch size changes dynamically during decoding (sequences finish at different times), the planner generates **multiple execution plans**, one for each anticipated batch size (typically 1 through 4, plus prefill configurations). Each plan specifies:

- The hot/cold split ratio for that batch size (what fraction of FFN computation goes to NPU vs. CPU)
- The specific clusters assigned to NPU (hot) and CPU (cold)
- The NPU computation graph (a static graph defining the matrix multiply operations for the NPU's hot-cluster sub-matrix)

The NPU computation graph deserves special attention because it reveals a key implementation constraint. Smartphone NPUs (specifically Qualcomm's Hexagon NPU) use a **static graph execution model**: the sequence of operations the NPU will execute is compiled into a graph ahead of time, and changing the graph requires loading a new compiled graph into NPU memory. Since the hot-cluster sub-matrix shape changes with batch size (different number of neurons included in the hot set), the planner pre-compiles separate NPU graphs for each batch size. Each graph is small—"typically only 10KB in size"—because it only specifies operator shapes and connections, not weight data. The weights themselves are loaded separately into NPU-accessible shared memory.

##### Hardware-Aware Thread and Core Configuration

The planner also determines the optimal thread configuration for the device. Based on the hardware profiling (Table 1 showing that a big-core at 3.3GHz achieves 1,076 MB/s for 4KB random reads while a little-core at 2.2GHz reaches only 761 MB/s), the planner:

- Assigns the single I/O thread to a high-performance core (big or mid) because UFS driver operations are CPU-intensive and I/O throughput is directly coupled to core speed.
- Determines the number of compute threads for CPU sparse processing based on the number of available non-I/O cores.
- Given the finding that using multiple cores for I/O degrades performance by up to 40% due to UFS's single command queue contention, the planner explicitly constrains I/O to exactly one thread.

The execution plan—comprising the neuron plan (cluster assignments per batch size) and hardware plan (NPU graphs, thread configurations)—is the offline artifact consumed by the online engine.

#### Adaptive Neuron Engine: Computation Orchestration at Runtime

The Adaptive Neuron Engine is the runtime component that executes the plan, making three key decisions: (1) how to handle the prefill phase versus the decoding phase, (2) how to split each FFN matrix between NPU and CPU based on current batch size, and (3) how to adjust the split when batch size changes mid-decoding.

##### NPU-Centric Prefill Strategy

During the prefill phase, the model processes the entire input prompt in parallel—every prompt token flows through all transformer layers in a single forward pass. Two characteristics of prefill motivate a distinct strategy:

**Characteristic 1: Dense computation.** Although individual tokens exhibit high sparsity (many neurons are inactive per token), aggregating across all prompt tokens dramatically reduces overall sparsity. When 128 or 512 tokens are processed simultaneously, almost every neuron is activated by at least one token. The paper states that "neuron activation probability approaches 99.99% in TurboSparse-Mixtral-47B with 128 batch size." This means the sparse-computation advantage of the CPU is negligible during prefill—there are essentially no neurons to skip.

**Characteristic 2: Large matrix dimensions.** The prefill computation is a matrix-matrix multiply (input embeddings of shape `[prompt_length, d_model]` × weight matrices of shape `[d_model, d_ff]`), which maps efficiently onto the NPU's dense compute architecture. The NPU achieves vastly higher throughput than CPU for these large-batch operations: per Figure 3(a) and Section 2.3.1, the NPU reaches ~770 tokens/s for prefill on a 7B INT4 model, compared to CPU at ~8 tokens/s and GPU at ~37 tokens/s.

Given these characteristics, the prefill strategy is straightforward: **the NPU processes all layers sequentially using dense matrix operations** (Figure 5-a). No predictor is used—all neurons are computed. The CPU's role is limited to weight loading: a dedicated big core asynchronously prefetches the weights for layer `L+1` from flash storage into the neuron cache (shared NPU-accessible memory) while the NPU computes layer `L`. All weight loading uses **sequential reads with large block sizes** (up to 512KB) to maximize UFS bandwidth, which reaches ~4 GB/s for sequential reads at large blocks versus ~450 MB/s at 4KB blocks. The paper's measurements in Figure 9 confirm that this sequential I/O is completely overlapped with computation: for Bamboo-7B with 512-token prompts, computation takes 916ms while sequential I/O takes 962ms, and the total layer time is 916ms (the I/O is hidden behind computation).

##### Hybrid CPU-NPU Decoding Strategy

During decoding, the model processes one token at a time (or a small batch in the case of parallel decoding strategies). The computation is matrix-vector rather than matrix-matrix, and the sparsity characteristics are the inverse of prefill: with batch size 1, activation sparsity is high and valuable (many neurons can be skipped), but the total computation is light. With larger decoding batches, sparsity decreases but computation increases.

The decoding strategy therefore uses a **hybrid split** (Figure 5-b):

- **Attention blocks**: Since attention is inherently dense (every token attends to every previous token in the KV cache) and relatively small (the Q, K, V, O matrices are each `[d_model, d_head × num_heads]`), PowerInfer-2 partitions individual attention heads between NPU and CPU, with each processor handling its assigned heads in parallel.

- **FFN blocks**: The FFN matrices are split along the neuron (intermediate) dimension. Specifically, given an FFN matrix of shape `[d_ff, d_model]` (say 14336×4096 for a 7B model), the matrix is divided into two sub-matrices along the row dimension: the top `h` rows (hot neuron clusters) go to NPU for dense computation, the remaining `d_ff - h` rows (cold neuron clusters) go to CPU for sparse, predictor-guided computation.

The split ratio `h / d_ff` is not fixed—it depends on the current batch size. The paper provides an example: "when batch size is 1, it divides a typical FFN matrix (14336×4096) into two equal sub-matrices (7168×4096) for NPU and CPU respectively." For larger batch sizes, the NPU portion grows: "As batch size increases, the NPU portion grows larger to handle more dense computations." At a batch size of approximately 4, the NPU handles ~70% of neurons and the CPU handles ~30%.

**How the CPU side works.** The CPU's cold-neuron computation uses a predictor-based approach adapted from PowerInfer. The predictor is a small learned model that, given the hidden state from the attention block, predicts which cold neurons will be activated before computing them. The CPU divides its cold-neuron sub-matrix into smaller chunks (the paper mentions 512×4096 as a typical chunk size), and multiple CPU cores process these chunks in parallel. Within each chunk, only the neurons predicted to be activated are actually computed—the predictor gates the computation. ARM Neon SIMD extensions are used for vectorized dot products on the activated rows.

**Why this split works.** The key insight is that the NPU and CPU are complementary in ways that map perfectly onto the hot/cold decomposition:

- Hot neurons are densely activated—the activation probability is high, so there's little to gain from sparse computation (the predictor would rarely skip them). The NPU's dense matrix-vector throughput substantially exceeds the CPU's for this work.
- Cold neurons are sparsely activated—the activation probability is low, so a predictor can achieve a high skip rate. The CPU's conditional execution and low-overhead sparse access pattern are faster than the NPU, which lacks efficient sparse primitives.
- Both processors access the same unified memory (UMA), so no weight copying is needed—the NPU reads hot-cluster weights from shared memory, the CPU reads cold-cluster weights from the same physical DRAM, and the results are merged by the CPU after both complete. The paper's measurement shows that aggregate memory bandwidth reaches 59.6 GB/s when both NPU and CPU are engaged, compared to 43.9 GB/s for CPU-only and 56 GB/s for NPU-only—the hybrid approach maximizes total memory bandwidth utilization.

##### Dynamic CPU-NPU Ratio Adjustment

The mechanism for adjusting the split ratio as batch sizes change is one of PowerInfer-2's key innovations. During Best-of-N decoding or other parallel strategies, the effective batch size decrements as individual candidate sequences complete. The system must seamlessly transition from, say, a 70% NPU / 30% CPU split at batch size 4 to a 50% NPU / 50% CPU split at batch size 2, and eventually to a 50% NPU / 50% CPU split at batch size 1.

The adjustment process works as follows:

1. **Monitoring**: CPU cores monitor batch size changes by tracking the completion and creation of decoding sequences. When a sequence generates an end-of-sequence (EOS) token or reaches its maximum length, the effective batch size decrements.

2. **Graph swapping**: Because the NPU uses static computation graphs, changing the NPU's workload requires loading a new graph. PowerInfer-2 initiates this graph loading **asynchronously during attention computation**. While the NPU is computing the attention block for the current layer—which is a fixed operation independent of the FFN split ratio—the CPU signals the NPU driver to load the pre-compiled graph for the new batch size. Since these graphs are "typically only 10KB in size," the loading completes well within the attention computation time (which is substantially longer than loading 10KB).

3. **Seamless transition**: The new graph becomes active immediately after attention computation completes, so the NPU seamlessly transitions to the new split ratio for the FFN computation. No tokens are dropped, no computation is duplicated, and the transition adds zero latency to the critical path because the graph loading is completely overlapped with the attention computation.

The paper provides evidence for this mechanism in Figure 13 (Best-of-N evaluation). Starting with N=4 active sequences, PowerInfer-2 achieves a speed of ~46 tokens/s (measured as total tokens generated across all sequences per second). As sequences complete and N drops from 4 to 3 to 2 to 1, the speed gradually decreases—but PowerInfer-2 maintains a 1.42× advantage over QNN (NPU-only) throughout, and when N reaches 1 (high sparsity), QNN's performance drops below PowerInfer-2-CPUOnly (CPU-only with sparsity exploitation), while PowerInfer-2's hybrid approach stays above both, achieving 1.1× over CPU-only and 1.77× over QNN.

##### Result Merging

After both NPU and CPU complete their respective sub-computations for an FFN block, the partial results must be combined. The NPU produces the contribution from hot neurons (the element-wise product of the Gate and Up projections, multiplied by the Down projection, but only for the hot neuron clusters). The CPU produces the contribution from cold neurons (same operations, but only for the cold clusters predicted to be activated). These are partial sums over the `d_model` output dimension. The CPU, which orchestrates the merge, adds these partial results element-wise to form the complete FFN output, which then feeds into the next transformer layer's attention block.

#### Neuron Cache: Segmented In-Memory Weight Management

The neuron cache manages which weights reside in DRAM versus requiring flash I/O. It is a three-region cache with region-specific policies:

**Region 1: Attention and KV Cache (fixed, never evicted).** This region holds the attention block weights (Q, K, V, O projections for all layers) and the KV cache (key and value tensors for all previously generated tokens). These are loaded once at inference startup using sequential reads and retained throughout the entire session. The rationale: attention weights are relatively small (each is `d_model × d_model`, so four matrices of 4096×4096 ≈ 268MB total for a 7B model in FP16) but accessed densely on every token, so evicting and reloading them would incur unnecessary I/O. The KV cache must be resident because it grows with sequence length and is accessed in its entirety during attention computation.

**Region 2: Hot Neuron Region (dense matrices, cluster-level LRU eviction).** This region stores the hot neuron clusters as contiguous dense sub-matrices, organized for direct NPU consumption. The Gate, Up, and Down projections for hot neurons are combined into a unified hot neuron cluster: for a given range of intermediate neurons `[i, i+k)`, the region stores the corresponding Gate rows, Up rows, and Down columns as a single cache entry. This bundling reflects the NPU's computation pattern—all three sub-matrices are needed together for the FFN computation, and loading them as a unit via sequential read is far more efficient than three separate reads.

Eviction uses an LRU policy at the cluster granularity: when the hot region is full and a new cluster must be loaded, the least recently accessed cluster (across all layers) is evicted. Since evicted weights are read-only (model weights, not activations), they are simply discarded from memory—no write-back to flash is needed.

**Region 3: Cold Neuron Region (individual neurons, neuron-level LRU eviction).** This region stores cold neurons individually, not in pre-formed clusters. The rationale, explicitly stated in the paper: "after excluding hot neurons, the co-activation probability among remaining neurons drops below 20%, making the bundling strategy ineffective for I/O reduction." If cold neurons were bundled into clusters for caching, the system would frequently load entire bundles when only a fraction of the bundled neurons are activated, wasting I/O bandwidth on unused weights. By caching cold neurons individually, the cache hit granularity exactly matches the activation granularity—only the specific neurons activated are loaded, and only those neurons occupy cache space.

Eviction uses a neuron-level LRU policy, evicting individual cold neurons that haven't been accessed recently to make room for new ones. Because cold neurons are small (one row of Gate, one row of Up, and one column of Down, totaling 3×`d_model` elements or ~24KB in FP16), the eviction granularity is fine and the cache can maintain high utilization even under varying access patterns.

##### Dynamic Region Resizing

When batch sizes change, the hot-cold split ratio changes—for example, going from batch size 4 (70% hot, 30% cold) to batch size 1 (50% hot, 50% cold) means the hot region should shrink and the cold region should expand. PowerInfer-2 handles this by:

1. **Hot region shrinkage**: Evict the least recently used hot neuron clusters (according to the cluster-level LRU order) until the hot region reaches its target size for the new batch size.
2. **Cold region expansion**: The freed memory is immediately available for cold neurons, allowing the cold region to grow and accommodate the increased number of cold clusters that will now be CPU-processed.

The reverse process (batch size increasing, hot region expanding) similarly evicts cold neurons to make room for hot clusters. The LRU policy ensures that the neurons most likely to be accessed under the new batch size regime are retained.

The paper's ablation study (Figure 14) quantifies the cache's impact: adding the neuron cache to a baseline with only bundle optimization yields a **2.82× speedup** (from 1.09 tokens/s to 4.18 tokens/s for Bamboo-7B with 50% weight offloading) by achieving a **95% cache hit rate**, "significantly reducing Flash I/O bandwidth pressure."

#### Neuron-Cluster-Level Pipeline: Computation-I/O Overlap Across Matrix Boundaries

The Neuron-Cluster-Level Pipeline is the mechanism that achieves the dramatic I/O overhead reduction from 76.7% (LLMFlash) to 13.7% (PowerInfer-2). To understand how it works, we must first understand why the matrix-level alternative fails.

##### The Matrix-Level Pipeline Failure Mode

In a matrix-level pipeline (which the paper attributes to LLMFlash's approach, Figure 6-a), the system treats each FFN matrix (Gate, Up, Down for one layer) as an atomic scheduling unit. The typical execution pattern is:

1. For the Gate matrix: load any uncached neuron weights from flash (I/O), then compute all neurons—both cached and newly loaded—by performing the full matrix-vector product.
2. Repeat for the Up matrix.
3. Repeat for the Down matrix.
4. Move to the next transformer layer.

The overlap opportunity is limited: while loading uncached Gate neurons, the CPU can compute the cached Gate neurons. However, the CPU must wait for *all* Gate neurons (cached + loaded) to be available before it can proceed to the Gate-vector product and then move to Up. If even a few uncached Gate neurons require slow random reads, the CPU stalls. Figure 6-a illustrates this: with 8 neuron clusters per matrix, 4 in memory and 4 in flash, the CPU cores experience idle "bubbles" between bursts of computation, waiting for the slowest I/O operations to complete before the matrix-level operation can be considered done.

##### The Neuron-Cluster-Level Solution

PowerInfer-2 breaks the matrix barrier entirely. The key idea is that **each neuron cluster is an independently schedulable unit**—once a core finishes computing one cluster (from any matrix), it can immediately start computing another cluster (from any matrix) that has its weights available in cache, regardless of whether other clusters from the first matrix are still waiting for I/O.

The paper formalizes this by decomposing the processing of a single neuron cluster into **five sequential stages**:

> 1. **Pred** (Prediction): Determine whether the specific neurons in this cluster are activated, using the CPU-side predictor. This stage runs only for cold clusters; hot clusters bypass it.
> 2. **GIO** (Gate I/O): Read the weights of the Gate matrix rows corresponding to this cluster from flash storage, if not already in cache.
> 3. **GC** (Gate Compute): Compute the dot product of the Gate matrix rows for this cluster with the input vector.
> 4. **UDIO** (Up-Down I/O): Read the weights of the Up matrix rows and Down matrix columns corresponding to this cluster from flash storage, if not cached.
> 5. **UDC** (Up-Down Compute): Compute the dot products for the Up and Down matrices using the results from GC (the Gate output gates the Up projection, and the combined result is projected through Down).

Each stage for one cluster is independent of stages for other clusters—the only dependencies are that for a given cluster, the stages must execute in order (Pred → GIO → GC → UDIO → UDC), and GC must precede UDIO because the Gate output determines whether the Up and Down neurons actually need to be loaded (the two-phase loading optimization, detailed below).

PowerInfer-2 allocates multiple compute threads (typically 4–5 CPU cores for computation) and exactly one I/O thread. The scheduler's logic is:

- **Compute threads** continuously poll for clusters that have completed their I/O stages and are ready for computation. When a compute thread finishes GC or UDC for one cluster, it immediately picks up the next ready cluster—which may belong to the same matrix, the next matrix, or even a matrix two layers ahead, as long as its weights are cache-resident.
- **The I/O thread** maintains a queue of I/O requests for uncached clusters. It issues read requests in the order clusters become ready for I/O, and upon completion, marks the cluster as ready for its next compute stage.

Figure 6-b illustrates the resulting schedule. Compared to Figure 6-a, the "bubbles" (idle periods) are substantially reduced because the CPU cores can always find *some* ready cluster to compute, even while waiting for random I/O on other clusters. The computation from matrix B overlaps with I/O for matrix A, and vice versa.

##### Why This Reduction Is So Large

The pipeline effectiveness hinges on a statistical property: while the *average* I/O time for cold neurons is high (because random reads on UFS are slow), the *variance* across clusters is also high. Some clusters load quickly (cached), some load moderately fast (sequential or bundled reads), and some are slow (uncached random reads). A matrix-level pipeline blocks on the slowest cluster in each matrix. A cluster-level pipeline can work around the slow clusters—while waiting for a slow random read for cluster `k` in matrix A, the CPU computes cluster `j` in matrix B (cached), cluster `m` in matrix C (cached), and cluster `n` in matrix A's Gate stage (already loaded), before finally returning to cluster `k` when its I/O completes. The critical path becomes the sum of the *unavoidable* I/O plus the compute time, rather than the sum of all I/O waits plus compute time.

The paper's breakdown in Table 4 confirms the magnitude: PowerInfer-2 spends **13.7% of time on I/O** and **86.3% on compute**, while LLMFlash spends **76.7% on I/O** and only **23.3% on compute**. This is a 5.6× reduction in I/O overhead, translating directly to the 3.84× average speedup over LLMFlash.

##### Thread and Core Configuration

The specific number of compute threads and the core on which the I/O thread runs are determined by the offline planner (Section 5). The I/O thread is pinned to a high-performance core (big or mid) because Table 1 shows that UFS random read throughput varies from 761 MB/s on a little-core to 1,076 MB/s on a big-core—the I/O thread's core speed directly determines the rate at which cold neurons can be loaded. Using a low-performance core for I/O would starve the compute threads, reducing pipeline effectiveness.

#### Flexible Neuron Loading: Differentiated I/O Strategies

The Flexible Neuron Loading engine implements the I/O strategies for bringing weights from flash into the neuron cache. Different weight types require fundamentally different access patterns, and PowerInfer-2 optimizes each:

##### Sequential I/O for Dense Weights

For **attention weights** (loaded at startup and retained) and **hot neuron clusters** (prefetched during attention computation), PowerInfer-2 uses large-block sequential reads. The rationale is that these weights are needed in their entirety (attention is dense, hot neurons are densely activated), so reading them as a contiguous stream maximizes UFS bandwidth—sequential reads at 512KB blocks achieve ~4 GB/s, while smaller reads achieve substantially less. The sequential read also benefits from UFS's internal read-ahead mechanisms.

For hot neuron clusters specifically, the loading is **asynchronous and prefetch-based**: while the NPU computes the attention block of layer `L`, the CPU's I/O thread loads the hot neuron clusters for layer `L`'s FFN (or layer `L+1`, depending on the overlap strategy). Since the attention computation time is substantial (it involves computing Q, K, V projections and attending over the entire KV cache), the sequential I/O for hot neurons is completely hidden.

##### Bundled Random I/O for Cold Neurons

For **cold neurons**, which are sparsely and unpredictably activated, PowerInfer-2 uses **on-demand random reads**. However, the system applies a critical optimization: **cross-matrix neuron bundling by position**.

The insight is that while neurons within a single FFN matrix rarely co-activate after excluding hot neurons (co-activation probability < 20%, as the paper states), **corresponding neurons at the same position across Gate, Up, and Down matrices show high correlation, with an 80% co-activation probability**. This makes intuitive sense: if intermediate neuron `i` is activated, the Gate row for `i`, the Up row for `i`, and the Down column for `i` are all needed to compute its contribution. The three matrices are structurally coupled by the neuron index.

PowerInfer-2 exploits this by reorganizing how weights are stored in flash: rather than storing the Gate, Up, and Down matrices as separate contiguous files, it stores weights **by neuron position**—the weights for Gate row `i`, Up row `i`, and Down column `i` are stored together as a single storage unit. When the predictor indicates that cold neuron `i` will be activated, the system issues one random read that fetches all three weight slices simultaneously, rather than three separate random reads.

The specific loading strategy depends on the model's quantization:

**For unquantized models (FP16, e.g., Mistral-7B-FP16):** Each neuron in FP16 occupies `d_model × 2 bytes` for each of the three matrices. For a 4096-dimensional model, that's 3 × 4096 × 2 = 24,576 bytes = 24KB per neuron position. PowerInfer-2 performs a single random read of 24KB to load the complete Gate-Up-Down bundle. At 24KB, this falls in a block size regime where UFS random read throughput is reasonable (not as fast as large sequential reads, but far better than many separate 8KB reads).

**For 4-bit quantized models (INT4, e.g., Bamboo-7B):** Each neuron position bundle consists of:
- Gate: INT4 weights (2KB for 4096 elements × 0.5 bytes each) + FP16 scale (0.5KB, shared across a quantization group) = ~2.5KB
- Up: same = ~2.5KB
- Down: same = ~2.5KB
- Total: ~7.5KB per neuron position

The paper reports that PowerInfer-2 aligns the bundle size to 8KB for storage efficiency but strategically divides the loading into **two separate 4KB operations** rather than a single 8KB random read. The rationale: "empirical measurements demonstrate superior bandwidth utilization compared to a single 8KB random read." This is a consequence of UFS's performance characteristics where 4KB random reads achieve higher aggregate IOPS than 8KB random reads within the same data range.

##### Two-Phase Loading for Quantized Models

For 4-bit quantized models, PowerInfer-2 introduces an additional optimization: **two-phase loading**. The observation is that even with an 80% co-activation rate, there is a 20% chance that the Gate neuron activates but the corresponding Up/Down neurons do not produce meaningful output (or vice versa). Loading all three weight slices unconditionally wastes 20% of I/O bandwidth on weights that will not be used.

The two-phase protocol addresses this:

1. **Phase 1:** After the predictor confirms that a cold neuron cluster will be activated, load only the Gate matrix weights for those neurons (4KB read per neuron or per small group). Compute the Gate projection to determine the actual Gate activation value.
2. **Phase 2:** If the Gate output is non-zero (i.e., the neuron is genuinely activated, not just predicted to be), load the Up and Down matrix weights (another 4KB read) and complete the FFN computation for that neuron. If the Gate output is zero (the predictor had a false positive), skip the Up/Down load and computation entirely.

This defers 50% of the I/O (the Up/Down portion) until the Gate computation confirms it is actually needed, eliminating wasted I/O for the ~20% of predicted neurons that turn out to be inactive. For the 80% that are genuinely active, the total I/O is the same (two 4KB reads instead of one 8KB read), but the bandwidth utilization of 4KB reads is actually higher than 8KB reads, so this is not a throughput penalty.

##### Attention and Hot Neuron Prefetching

The loading strategies for attention and hot neurons are simpler because there is no sparsity to exploit:

- **Attention weights:** Loaded entirely at inference startup using sequential I/O, retained in the attention region of the cache throughout the session.
- **Hot neuron clusters:** During decoding, hot clusters are loaded during the attention computation of the previous layer (prefetching). The I/O thread issues a sequential read for the next layer's hot clusters as soon as the attention computation begins. By the time attention completes, the hot clusters are in the cache and the NPU can immediately begin FFN computation. During prefill, the same pattern applies but with the entire layer weights being loaded (since prefill is fully dense), as described in the NPU-centric prefill strategy.

##### Why Differentiated I/O Matters

The paper's ablation study (Figure 14) shows that adding the "Bundle" optimization—which specifically refers to the Gate-Up-Down positional bundling for cold neurons—improves performance from 0.4 tokens/s (baseline, no optimization) to 1.09 tokens/s (2.72× speedup) for Bamboo-7B with 50% weight offloading. This is the foundational optimization that makes subsequent improvements possible: without bundling, cold-neuron loading would require three separate random reads per activated neuron (or worse, one random read per weight element), which would be catastrophically slow on UFS's 100K IOPS random read limit. The 80% co-activation rate means bundling reduces I/O operations by approximately 2.4× (three reads collapsed to one, with 80% efficiency).

#### Summary of Design Choices and Their Justifications

- **Neuron cluster as the scheduling unit** rather than matrix: enables heterogeneous compute allocation (NPU vs. CPU) and cross-matrix pipelining that matrix-level scheduling cannot achieve.
- **Hardware-aware cluster sizing** (large for NPU, small for CPU) rather than fixed-size chunks: large clusters amortize NPU kernel launch overhead and saturate its vector units; small clusters maximize CPU predictor skip rates and map onto ARM Neon vector widths.
- **Hot-cold classification bounded by I/O prefetch budget** rather than a fixed activation frequency threshold: ensures that hot-neuron I/O is completely hidden behind attention computation, eliminating the I/O bottleneck for the most frequently accessed weights.
- **Static NPU graphs pre-compiled per batch size** rather than dynamic graph construction: works around the NPU's static graph execution model by pre-compiling small (10KB) graphs for each batch size, then asynchronously swapping them during attention computation to achieve zero-overhead transitions.
- **Neuron-cluster-level pipelining across matrices** rather than matrix-level pipelining within matrices: exploits the statistical independence of cluster I/O times to keep compute cores continuously busy, reducing I/O overhead from 76.7% to 13.7%.
- **Gate-Up-Down positional bundling** rather than per-matrix bundling or individual neuron loading: exploits the 80% co-activation rate across corresponding positions in the three FFN matrices to reduce random I/O operations by ~2.4×.
- **Two-phase loading for INT4 models** rather than unconditional bundled loading: saves I/O bandwidth on the ~20% of predicted neurons that turn out to be inactive, using the Gate computation as a cheap filter before committing to the Up/Down I/O.
- **Single I/O thread pinned to a big core** rather than multi-threaded I/O: avoids UFS command queue contention that degrades performance by up to 40%, while the big core's higher frequency (3.3GHz vs. 2.2GHz) increases random read throughput by ~41%.
- **LRU eviction at different granularities** (cluster for hot, neuron for cold) rather than uniform eviction: matches the access patterns—hot clusters are accessed as units (NPU dense computation), cold neurons are accessed individually (CPU sparse computation), and cold-neuron co-activation probability is too low (<20%) to benefit from cluster-level caching.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: The Neuron Cluster as a Unifying Abstraction That Simultaneously Solves Three Orthogonal Smartphone Hardware Problems

The field's approach to efficient LLM inference on resource-constrained devices has been to stack independently-designed optimizations: one mechanism for compute heterogeneity (PowerInfer's static GPU/CPU split), another for flash storage offloading (LLMFlash's row-column bundling and neuron caching), and yet another for I/O-compute overlap (STI's matrix-level pipelining for BERT-scale models). Each of these prior systems identified a real bottleneck and addressed it in isolation, but they shared an implicit assumption: that the granularity of work decomposition for compute scheduling, memory management, and I/O orchestration could be different and independently determined.

PowerInfer-2's central intellectual move is to recognize that this assumption is not just suboptimal but *fundamentally incompatible* with smartphone hardware characteristics. On a PC, the three problems can be solved separately because the hardware slacks are large enough to absorb the mismatches: a GPU handles both dense and sparse compute efficiently, an NVMe SSD provides enough random-read IOPS that imperfect I/O scheduling still leaves acceptable performance, and memory bandwidth is abundant enough that the cost of suboptimal cache granularity is modest. On a smartphone, none of these slacks exist. The NPU chokes on sparse computation. UFS random reads at 4KB are 12× slower than NVMe. Memory bandwidth is shared across all processors and saturated by any single one.

The neuron-cluster abstraction is the paper's response to this insight. It is not merely a grouping of neurons by activation frequency—that would be a minor refinement of PowerInfer's hot/cold partition. What makes it distinctive is that the *same* grouping simultaneously serves three purposes that prior systems handled with three different granularities:

- **For compute scheduling**: the cluster determines whether work goes to NPU (large, dense hot clusters) or CPU (small, sparse cold clusters), with cluster size tuned to each processor's performance characteristics. This is fundamentally different from PowerInfer, which statically assigned entire neuron sets to processors without considering that the optimal chunk size for GPU dense compute is different from the optimal chunk size for CPU sparse compute.
- **For memory management**: the cluster is the unit of caching and eviction, with different policies for hot clusters (cluster-level LRU) and cold neurons (neuron-level LRU). This resolves the tension that LLMFlash faced: bundling neurons for I/O efficiency (large bundles) conflicts with cache efficiency when co-activation probability is low (<20% for cold neurons), because the bundle loads many unused weights.
- **For I/O orchestration**: the cluster is the minimum schedulable unit in the pipeline, enabling computation on cached clusters from matrix B to proceed while I/O completes for uncached clusters from matrix A. Prior matrix-level pipelines (LLMFlash, STI) could not achieve this because the matrix barrier forced computation to wait for all I/O within a matrix to complete.

The conceptual contribution is not that grouping neurons is useful—that is obvious. The contribution is the recognition that **on smartphones, the granularity of work decomposition is a first-order design constraint that must satisfy all three subsystems simultaneously**, and that a single, hardware-adaptive grouping can do so when prior systems assumed separate granularities were necessary. This is a fundamental shift in how to think about system design for heterogeneous mobile processors: it argues that the *right* abstraction is one that co-designs computation, caching, and I/O around the same unit, rather than optimizing each in isolation and accepting the coordination overhead.

The evidence for this claim is distributed across the paper rather than localized to a single figure. Table 4's breakdown—13.7% I/O overhead for PowerInfer-2 versus 76.7% for LLMFlash—is the aggregate consequence of the unified abstraction, but it's the ablation study in Figure 14 that reveals the layered dependency: bundling alone (2.72×), adding the neuron cache (2.82× on top of bundling), adding the pipeline (1.29× on top of cache), and adding XPU co-processing (1.15× on top of pipeline). Each mechanism depends on the cluster abstraction—without it, bundling cannot determine what to bundle, the cache cannot distinguish hot from cold eviction policies, and the pipeline has no schedulable unit smaller than a matrix. The gains are multiplicative because the abstraction enables them to compose.

### Innovation 2: The Dynamic Sparsity Diagnosis and Its Implication That Static Hot-Cold Partitions Are Fundamentally Unsound for Modern Decoding Strategies

Prior work on activation sparsity for LLM inference—PowerInfer, Deja Vu (Liu et al., 2023), CATS (Lee et al., 2024)—operated on a shared premise: that neuron activation frequencies, aggregated over a representative corpus, form a stable distribution that can be used to statically partition neurons into hot and cold sets. This premise holds for basic autoregressive sampling, where exactly one token is generated at a time and the batch size is fixed at 1. Under these conditions, neuron activations are indeed sparse and the distribution is stationary—the same neurons tend to be hot or cold across different inputs.

PowerInfer-2 identifies a previously overlooked failure mode of this premise: **modern deployment scenarios increasingly use parallel decoding strategies (Best-of-N, MCTS, map-reduce) that create dynamic batch sizes during inference, and neuron activation sparsity is strongly batch-size-dependent in ways that invert the hot-cold partition**. Figure 2 is the key diagnostic evidence. At batch size 1, less than 1% of neurons show high activation frequency—sparsity is extreme and patterns are irregular. At batch size 32, approximately 75% of neurons show high activation frequency—the hot set expands by 75×. A static partition optimized for batch size 1 would massively underutilize the NPU at batch size 32, leaving the majority of neurons idle on the CPU when they should be computed densely on the NPU. Conversely, a partition optimized for batch size 4 would waste NPU compute on near-zero activations when the batch size drops to 1 during decoding tail behavior.

This observation is not a minor refinement—it challenges the conceptual foundation of static sparsity exploitation for LLM inference. The prior approach implicitly assumed that sparsity is a *property of the model* (fixed by training) that can be discovered once and exploited forever. PowerInfer-2 demonstrates that sparsity is a *property of the workload* (the current batch of tokens being processed) that shifts dynamically as the workload changes. The implication is that any system using a static hot-cold partition is fundamentally misconfigured for a significant fraction of its operational time during parallel decoding.

What makes this contribution distinctive beyond the empirical observation is the **architectural response**: rather than trying to find a single compromise partition that works for all batch sizes (which would be suboptimal for all of them), PowerInfer-2 designs the system to *adapt its partition at runtime* through the asynchronous NPU graph-swapping mechanism. The pre-compiled static graphs are an elegant workaround for the NPU's static graph execution model—the system achieves dynamic behavior on hardware that fundamentally does not support it, by swapping small pre-compiled configurations during attention computation where the NPU is already busy. This is a genuine systems innovation: it reconciles the NPU's requirement for static computation graphs with the workload's requirement for dynamic reconfiguration, without adding latency to the critical path.

The significance extends beyond PowerInfer-2 itself. The finding that "the proportion of highly-activated neurons demonstrates a dramatic correlation with batch size" establishes a design principle for future mobile inference systems: **any system that exploits activation sparsity must be batch-size-aware**. Static sparsity exploitation is insufficient for deployment scenarios involving parallel decoding, which are increasingly common in production LLM applications (Snell et al., 2024, on compute-optimal test-time scaling explicitly uses Best-of-N). The paper reframes sparsity from a static model property to a dynamic workload property, and this reframing has implications for how sparsity-aware training (ProSparse, ReLU²), predictor design, and hardware accelerators should be developed.

The evidence for the dynamic sparsity claim is Figure 2 (the activation heatmap across batch sizes) and Figure 13 (the Best-of-N evaluation showing sustained advantage over static-strategy alternatives as batch size decrements from 4 to 1). The latter is particularly important because it demonstrates that the dynamic adaptation works in practice, not just in principle: PowerInfer-2 maintains a 1.42× advantage over QNN (NPU-only, static) throughout the batch size transition, and when batch size reaches 1, the gap is 1.77×—the static NPU-only approach is worst exactly when sparsity is highest, validating the claim that static partitions are misconfigured for this regime.

### Innovation 3: The Empirical Finding That Mobile NPUs Are Antagonistic to Sparse Computation—Establishing a Hardware Asymmetry That Inverts the PC-Derived Assumption That Powerful Processors Are Better for All Workloads

A natural assumption when porting inference systems from PC to mobile is that the most powerful processor (the one with the highest throughput for the dominant operation) should handle as much work as possible. On PCs, this assumption holds: discrete GPUs outperform CPUs for both dense and sparse matrix operations, so the design principle is "route as much computation as possible to the GPU, use the CPU only for work the GPU cannot do or for overflow." PowerInfer's design reflected this principle: hot neurons went to GPU (the powerful processor), cold neurons went to CPU (the weaker processor for the less frequent work). LLMFlash similarly assumed that bundling co-activated neurons and processing them on the fastest available unit was optimal.

PowerInfer-2's empirical characterization in Figure 3(a) and Section 2.3.1 provides a counterexample that inverts this assumption. On the Snapdragon 8 Gen 3, the NPU achieves the highest throughput for large-batch dense matrix operations—significantly outperforming both CPU and GPU. However, **the NPU's sparse computation primitives are slower than the CPU's**. The paper states this plainly: "our implementation of sparse computation primitives on Qualcomm's NPU actually performs slower than CPU implementations." The hardware asymmetry is stark: NPU > CPU for dense, CPU > NPU for sparse. This is not a difference of degree (GPU being slightly better at dense) but of kind (NPU being fundamentally worse at sparse). The processor that is fastest for the most common operation is the worst choice for a different but essential operation.

This finding has conceptual significance beyond the specific hardware tested. It establishes that **mobile heterogeneous computing for LLM inference is not a hierarchy where one processor dominates—it is a genuine heterogeneity where each processor has a distinct competence region**. The design implication is that optimal workload distribution must be *competence-aware*, not *throughput-ranked*. A system that routes all work to the fastest processor (QNN, in the paper's evaluation) will underperform a system that routes dense work to the dense-specialized processor and sparse work to the sparse-competent processor, even though the latter uses the "weaker" processor for some work. The paper's hybrid approach—NPU for hot clusters, CPU for cold clusters—achieves higher aggregate throughput than either processor alone precisely because it respects this competence boundary.

This insight is not a theoretical advance per se—it is an empirical characterization of existing hardware. But it functions as a diagnostic concept that explains *why* prior systems fail on smartphones. PowerInfer's static hot-cold split was designed for a platform where GPU > CPU for all operations; on smartphones, forcing all sparse computation to the CPU is not a fallback but the optimal strategy, and simultaneously, leaving the NPU idle during sparse computation wastes its dense-compute throughput. The paper's contribution is identifying this inversion and building a system that exploits it: the hybrid CPU-NPU decoding strategy is not a compromise but the uniquely correct approach for this hardware asymmetry.

The supporting evidence is Figure 3(a) (comparing execution times for matrix-vector multiplication across batch sizes on CPU, GPU, and NPU), Table 2 (showing that CPU-only memory bandwidth utilization reaches only 43.9 GB/s versus 59.6 GB/s when both NPU and CPU are engaged), and Figure 13 (showing PowerInfer-2 outperforming both NPU-only and CPU-only across all batch sizes during Best-of-N decoding). The fact that PowerInfer-2 beats both pure strategies simultaneously is the strongest evidence that the competence-aware split is exploiting a genuine complementarity, not just avoiding a bottleneck.

### Innovation 4: The Neuron-Cluster-Level Pipeline as a Counterexample to the Assumption That I/O Granularity Should Match the Natural Unit of Computation

A long-standing design principle in systems that overlap computation with I/O is that the unit of scheduling should match the natural unit of computation—for LLM inference, the matrix. LLMFlash's pipeline operates at matrix granularity: load uncached neurons for the Gate matrix, compute Gate, load for Up, compute Up, load for Down, compute Down. STI's pipeline for BERT inference similarly operates at layer granularity. The intuition is that matching the scheduling granularity to the computation granularity minimizes coordination overhead and exploits the fact that computation within a matrix is homogeneous (all matrix-vector products of the same dimensions).

PowerInfer-2's neuron-cluster-level pipeline challenges this principle directly. It demonstrates that **on hardware where random I/O variance is high (UFS), finer-grained scheduling than the natural computation unit yields strictly better compute utilization by allowing the scheduler to route around slow I/O operations**. The matrix-level pipeline's failure mode is not that it fails to overlap—it does overlap—but that the overlap is bounded by the slowest I/O operation within each matrix. If one uncached neuron cluster in the Gate matrix requires a slow random read, all compute cores stall waiting for it, even though other matrices have cached clusters ready for computation. The matrix barrier creates an artificial synchronization point that serializes computation with the tail latency of I/O.

Breaking the matrix barrier—allowing computation to proceed on cached clusters from any matrix while I/O completes for uncached clusters from the current matrix—converts the critical path from "compute + max(I/O per matrix)" to "compute + unavoidable I/O." The 5.6× reduction in I/O overhead (76.7% → 13.7%, Table 4) is the empirical validation that the matrix-level granularity was the bottleneck, not the raw I/O bandwidth. UFS random reads are still slow in absolute terms, but the system rarely waits for them because it always has useful computation to perform on cached clusters from other matrices.

This is a fundamental insight about the relationship between scheduling granularity and I/O variance, not a UFS-specific optimization. The principle generalizes: **when I/O latency variance is high relative to compute time per scheduling unit, subdividing the natural computation unit into independently-schedulable sub-units enables the scheduler to maintain high utilization by routing around slow operations**. The corollary is that the "natural computation unit" is not the optimal scheduling unit when I/O characteristics are pathological—a finer-grained unit that enables work-stealing across I/O boundaries can achieve higher throughput despite increased coordination overhead.

The paper does not frame this as a theoretical contribution, but it functions as a design principle with implications beyond LLM inference. Any system that loads data from high-variance storage (flash with variable read latency, network-attached storage, distributed file systems) and performs independent computations on subsets of that data could benefit from subdividing computations below the natural granularity to work around stragglers. The neuron-cluster-level pipeline is a concrete demonstration of this principle in a practical system, and the dramatic improvement (Figure 14: +1.29× from pipeline alone, on top of 2.82× from cache and 2.72× from bundling) provides strong evidence that the principle is worth generalizing.

The critical evidence is the contrast between Figure 6(a) (matrix-level pipeline showing idle bubbles) and Figure 6(b) (neuron-cluster-level pipeline showing dense utilization), backed by Table 4's overhead breakdown. The ablation in Figure 14 isolates the pipeline's contribution at 1.29×—modest as a standalone gain but multiplicative with the other optimizations, and only achievable because the neuron-cluster abstraction provides the scheduling granularity the pipeline needs.

### Assessing the Contributions Collectively

These four innovations form a coherent intellectual arc. Innovation 1 (the neuron-cluster abstraction) provides the *mechanism*—a single grouping of work that simultaneously addresses compute heterogeneity, memory management, and I/O orchestration. Innovation 2 (dynamic sparsity) provides the *motivation for runtime adaptation*—demonstrating that static partitions are fundamentally unsound and that the abstraction must support reconfiguration. Innovation 3 (NPU-sparse antagonism) provides the *hardware justification for heterogeneous allocation*—showing that the competence-aware split is not a preference but a necessity given the NPU's inability to handle sparse computation efficiently. Innovation 4 (sub-matrix pipelining) provides the *scheduling insight*—showing that the natural computation granularity is not the optimal scheduling granularity when I/O variance is high, and that the neuron-cluster abstraction enables a strictly better pipeline.

Collectively, they reframe smartphone LLM inference from a problem of "how do we compress the model enough to fit" (the prior approach: quantization, pruning, distillation) to a problem of "how do we co-design computation, caching, and I/O around a single abstraction that respects the hardware's genuine heterogeneity." The benchmarking results—27.8× over llama.cpp, 3.84× over LLMFlash, first 47B model on a smartphone—are the consequence of this reframing, not independent contributions. The paper's intellectual contribution is the diagnosis that prior systems failed because they ported PC-centric assumptions (uniform processor capability, static sparsity, matrix-level scheduling) to hardware where those assumptions are invalid, and that the fix requires a unified abstraction that spans the entire inference stack.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The paper evaluates on representative real-world LLM tasks including multi-turn dialogue (Zephyr, Tunstall et al., 2023), code generation (HumanEval, Chen et al., 2021), math problem solving (MATH, Cobbe et al., 2021), and role play (RoleLLM, Wang et al., 2023). These are sourced from HuggingFace and described as "top representatives of real-world LLM tasks." For accuracy evaluation (Section 7.6), the authors use OpenCompass (Contributors, 2023), a widely-adopted benchmark suite, measuring Arc Challenge (Clark et al., 2018), Arc Easy, MMLU (Hendrycks et al., 2020), and GSM8K (Cobbe et al., 2021). For energy consumption (Section 7.7), prompts are randomly sampled from lmsys/lmsys-chat-1m (Zheng et al., 2023), a dataset of real-world user interactions from chatbot deployments.

- **Base models.** Five LLMs spanning different architectures, sizes, and sparsity characteristics are evaluated: Mistral(SiLU)-7B (dense, SiLU activation), sparse Qwen2-7B (Qwen2 architecture with ReLU-based sparsity, Yang et al., 2024), Bamboo-7B (a ReLU-based model purpose-trained for activation sparsity, Song et al., 2024), sparse Llama-13B (Song et al., 2024, using ProSparse training to induce activation sparsity), and TurboSparse-Mixtral-47B (a 47B-parameter mixture-of-experts model with high activation sparsity, Song et al., 2024). This range is deliberately chosen: Mistral-7B represents the non-sparse baseline where PowerInfer-2's sparsity-dependent mechanisms should show more modest gains; Bamboo-7B and the sparse variants represent the primary target (high activation sparsity); Llama-13B tests scaling to larger dense-sparse hybrids; and TurboSparse-Mixtral-47B tests the extreme case of a model that cannot fit in smartphone memory at all without offloading. Including SiLU-based models serves as an ablation on activation function dependence. The models use both FP16 (unquantized) and INT4 quantization, with the paper noting that INT4 is "the de facto standard in real-world mobile deployments" (§7.6).

- **Metrics.** The primary metric throughout is **generation speed** measured in tokens per second (tokens/s). For decoding, this is computed as total output tokens generated divided by total time, measured over sequences of up to 1,024 output tokens with prompts of up to 64 tokens (Section 7.1). For prefill, prompt processing speed is measured in tokens/s as prompt length divided by processing time, evaluated at prompt lengths of 128 and 512 tokens. A secondary metric is **memory usage** (GB), reported for the in-memory comparison in Section 7.3. For Best-of-N evaluation (Section 7.4), speed is measured in tokens/s where each iteration processes one token across N candidate sequences in parallel, and the metric captures the combined throughput as batch size dynamically decreases. Energy consumption (Section 7.7) uses Joules per token (J/token) as the metric, and accuracy (Section 7.6) uses standard benchmark-specific accuracy metrics averaged across tasks. All experiments are averaged over 10 runs to account for variance (§7.1).

- **Baselines.** Four state-of-the-art frameworks are compared. **llama.cpp** (Gerganov, 2024) is "the most popular CPU-based framework that supports offloading weights to flash storage (via mmap), and serves as the backend for many other frameworks like Ollama." It represents the CPU-only baseline with standard memory-mapped weight loading. **QNN** (Qualcomm, 2023) is "Qualcomm's commercial inference engine with sophisticated and proprietary NPU optimizations"—this is the NPU-native baseline that represents the best available NPU inference on Qualcomm hardware but lacks CPU-NPU co-processing and sparse computation support. **MLC-LLM** (MLC team, 2024) "leverages mobile GPUs for acceleration" and represents the GPU-based approach. Since MLC-LLM requires all model weights to fit in memory (no offloading support), it is only included in the in-memory comparison (Section 7.3). **LLMFlash** (Alizadeh et al., 2024) is "designed for high-end PC context and not open-sourced. Therefore we implemented LLMFlash's key optimizations in llama.cpp based on the paper's description, including sparsity prediction, row-column bundling, and neuron data caching." This baseline is critical because LLMFlash is the closest prior work in terms of flash-offloading strategy, and its reimplementation on smartphone hardware isolates the impact of PowerInfer-2's hardware-specific optimizations. For the Best-of-N comparison, an additional baseline **PowerInfer-2-CPUOnly** (PowerInfer-2 with CPU-only decoding, NPU disabled) is used to isolate the contribution of XPU co-processing.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper does not use a FLOPs-based compute budget because the primary constraint is memory capacity, not total FLOPs. Instead, comparisons are controlled by **(1) memory budget**—the fraction of FFN weights allowed to reside in DRAM, with the remainder offloaded to flash (typically 50% offloading for most experiments, 75% for TurboSparse-Mixtral-47B on the lower-memory OnePlus Ace 2), and **(2) total available DRAM**—experiments vary from 7GB to 19GB on the OnePlus 12 to evaluate performance across realistic multi-application scenarios (Section 7.2.3). For the in-memory comparison (Section 7.3), all weights fit in DRAM and the comparison is at equal model size and quantization. The paper explicitly accounts for the fact that different frameworks have different memory requirements for the same model by reporting both speed and memory usage: "When offloading 50% of FFN weights, PowerInfer-2 reduces memory usage by 1.5GB (40%) while maintaining performance comparable to llama.cpp and MLC-LLM" (§7.3). This is a practically meaningful accounting because smartphone memory pressure comes from absolute GB consumed, not from a fraction of model weights.

- **Hardware platforms.** Two smartphones representing different performance tiers are evaluated: the high-end **OnePlus 12** with Snapdragon 8 Gen 3 SoC, 24GB DRAM (19GB available to applications), and UFS 4.0 storage; and the mid-range **OnePlus Ace 2** with Snapdragon 8+ Gen 1 SoC, 16GB DRAM (11GB available), and UFS 3.1 storage (Table 3). The hardware specifications affect all metrics: the 8 Gen 3 has a faster NPU, higher memory bandwidth, and UFS 4.0 provides better random-read performance than UFS 3.1. Results on both devices assess whether the optimizations generalize across hardware generations.

- **Statistical protocol.** "All experiments are averaged over 10 runs to account for variance" (§7.1). For the decoding consistency analysis (Section 7.2.4), the paper additionally reports the 50th percentile (P50), 90th percentile (P90), and 99th percentile (P99) decoding latencies to characterize tail behavior. For energy measurements (Section 7.7), 100 prompts are randomly sampled from the lmsys-chat-1m dataset to ensure representativeness. The paper does not report confidence intervals or standard deviations for the averaged speed measurements, which is a limitation for assessing whether the observed differences between frameworks are statistically significant given 10-run averaging.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Offloading-Based Decoding Performance (Figure 7, Table 4)

The headline result: PowerInfer-2 achieves **average speedups of 3.84× (up to 4.63×) over LLMFlash and 24.6× (up to 27.8×) over llama.cpp** on the OnePlus 12, with 50% of FFN weights offloaded to flash storage for all models except TurboSparse-Mixtral-47B on OnePlus Ace 2 which requires 75% offloading due to its 16GB DRAM constraint. On the OnePlus Ace 2, the speedups are 2.93× and 14.1× over LLMFlash and llama.cpp respectively (Figure 7).

Breaking down per model on OnePlus 12 with 50% FFN offloading: **Bamboo-7B**: PowerInfer-2 achieves 11.1 tokens/s vs. LLMFlash at 2.4 tokens/s (4.63×) and llama.cpp at 0.4 tokens/s (27.8×). **Qwen2-7B**: 10.0 tokens/s vs. 2.8 and 0.4 tokens/s. **Llama-13B**: 5.4 tokens/s vs. 1.7 and 0.2 tokens/s. **TurboSparse-Mixtral-47B**: 9.5 tokens/s (at 50% offloading on OnePlus 12; see Section 7.2.3 for memory capacity scaling).

The acceleration ratio varies across models due to differences in sparsity and activated parameter count. The paper explains: "While TurboSparse-Mixtral-47B has 47B parameters in total, its mixture-of-expert architecture and high sparsity result in only about 3B activated parameters per token, similar to Bamboo-7B. This explains their comparable performance." In contrast, "Llama-13B's lower sparsity leads to nearly 2× more activated parameters than Bamboo-7B, resulting in 2× slower performance." The 2× performance gap between Bamboo-7B (11.1 tokens/s) and Llama-13B (5.4 tokens/s) is consistent with the 2× difference in activated parameters (approximately 3B vs. 6B).

The critical-path breakdown in Table 4 quantifies the mechanism behind the speedup. For Bamboo-7B with 50% offloading, **PowerInfer-2 spends 86.3% of time on computation and only 13.7% on I/O**, while **LLMFlash spends 23.3% on computation and 76.7% on I/O**. This is a 5.6× reduction in I/O overhead, directly attributable to the neuron-cluster-level pipeline (hides I/O latency behind computation from other matrices) combined with the flexible neuron loading (bundled reads reduce I/O operations, two-phase loading eliminates unnecessary reads). The 86.3% compute utilization approaches what would be expected from a memory-resident system, validating that the pipeline effectively hides UFS latency.

#### Offloading-Based Prefill Performance (Figure 8, Figure 9)

For prefill with 512-token prompts on OnePlus 12, PowerInfer-2 achieves significant speedups: **48.97× over LLMFlash, 44.23× over llama.cpp, and 1.99× over QNN**. On OnePlus Ace 2, the speedups over the same baselines are 29.98× and 16.67× respectively. For 128-token prompts, "up to 22.47× speedup over LLMFlash."

The per-model breakdown at 512-token prompts on OnePlus 12 shows: **Bamboo-7B**: PowerInfer-2 at 405 tokens/s vs. QNN at 195 tokens/s (2.08×), LLMFlash at 7 tokens/s, and llama.cpp at 4 tokens/s. **Qwen2-7B**: 429 tokens/s vs. QNN at 194 tokens/s (2.21×). **Llama-13B**: 224 tokens/s vs. QNN at 118 tokens/s (1.90×). **TurboSparse-Mixtral-47B**: 79 tokens/s vs. QNN at 42 tokens/s (1.88×).

The performance advantage over QNN during prefill is explained by the efficient overlap of sequential I/O with NPU computation. Figure 9 demonstrates this directly: for Bamboo-7B with 512-token prompts on OnePlus 12, the computation time per layer is 916ms, the sequential I/O time is 962ms, and the total layer time is 916ms—the I/O is completely hidden behind computation. For Qwen2-7B, computation takes 778ms, sequential I/O takes 820ms, and total layer time is 778ms. This complete overlap is possible because the prefill phase uses large-block sequential reads (NPU processes one layer's dense weights while CPU prefetches the next layer's weights) and the NPU's compute time per layer is large enough to absorb the sequential I/O bandwidth limit of ~4 GB/s. QNN's lower prefill speed stems from the NPU stalling during weight loading when weights exceed on-chip memory—PowerInfer-2's explicit CPU-managed prefetching eliminates this stall.

#### Performance Across Memory Capacities (Figure 10)

When total available memory is varied from 7GB to 19GB on the OnePlus 12 running TurboSparse-Mixtral-47B, PowerInfer-2's decoding speed scales linearly with memory capacity. At 7GB available memory, the neuron cache holds only 400MB (1.8% of FFN weights), and decoding speed is 2.13 tokens/s. At 19GB available memory (maximum), the cache holds substantially more weights and speed reaches **11.68 tokens/s**, which is 3.12× faster than LLMFlash and 21.2× faster than llama.cpp at the same memory configuration.

The 7GB configuration is an extreme stress test. The paper details the memory allocation: 1GB for non-FFN layer weights (token embeddings, self-attention, KV cache, final LM head), 2.6GB for predictor weights, 2.7GB for FFN weights' quantization scales, and 300MB for runtime memory, totaling 6.6GB. The remaining 400MB is the neuron cache, storing only 1.8% of FFN weights. "This small cache size means nearly all neurons must be fetched from flash storage for each token." Despite this, PowerInfer-2 still outperforms LLMFlash by 1.84× (2.13 vs. an implied 1.16 tokens/s)—the neuron-cluster-level pipeline and differentiated I/O strategies extract useful work even when the cache is nearly empty. LLMFlash and llama.cpp are not plotted at 7GB in Figure 10 because their speeds under this configuration are implied to be far lower (the 1.84× gap at 7GB is reported in text, with LLMFlash's speed at approximately 1.16 tokens/s).

The linear scaling of PowerInfer-2's speed with memory size indicates that I/O operations reduce proportionally with increased cache size, and no other bottleneck (e.g., compute throughput) saturates before 19GB. This is significant for deployment: it means that in multi-application scenarios where available memory fluctuates, the performance degrades gracefully rather than catastrophically.

#### Decoding Consistency (Figure 11, Table 5)

Across four downstream tasks—role-play, multi-turn dialogue, math problem solving, and code generation—PowerInfer-2 maintains consistent performance on TurboSparse-Mixtral-47B with all available memory used: at least 11.4 tokens/s across all tasks, with speeds of 11.8, 11.4, 11.5, and 11.7 tokens/s respectively. The minor variations are attributed to "task-dependent differences in model activation sparsity." The consistency is important because it validates that the neuron plan (generated from Wikipedia and RefinedWeb profiling) generalizes to diverse real-world prompts, not just the profiling distribution.

Token-level latency analysis (Table 5) reveals tail behavior. For TurboSparse-Mixtral-47B with 50% FFN offloading: mean latency 99.76ms, P50 97.42ms, P90 116.16ms (16.5% above mean), and P99 140.56ms (40.9% above mean). The paper attributes these variations to "differing activation patterns between consecutive tokens": "When tokens share activation patterns, they benefit from cached neurons, avoiding costly flash storage access." The TurboSparse-Mixtral-47B maintains a 3.5% average cache miss rate but a P99 miss rate of 18.9%—the tail latency is driven by rare tokens that activate cold neurons not currently in cache. For Bamboo-7B (mean 90.32ms, P50 86.88ms, P90 115.02ms, P99 162.02ms), the P99 latency is 79.4% above the mean, indicating even more pronounced tail behavior, possibly due to Bamboo-7B's higher sparsity making cache misses more variable.

The P99 being 1.4–1.8× the mean is relevant for user experience: for interactive applications, the P99 (not the mean) determines worst-case responsiveness. The paper does not propose tail-mitigation strategies (e.g., pre-warming the cache for rare activation patterns, or using a slower but more predictable fallback for tail tokens), which is a practical limitation.

#### SiLU-Based LLM Performance (Table 6)

PowerInfer-2 can also accelerate models with SiLU (non-sparse) activations, though gains are more modest. For Mistral(SiLU)-7B with 50% FFN offloading on OnePlus 12, PowerInfer-2 achieves 5.3 tokens/s compared to LLMFlash at 2.18 tokens/s—a **2.4× speedup**, versus **4.6× for Bamboo-7B** (11.1 vs. 2.4 tokens/s). The paper attributes the lower speedup to the fundamental characteristics of SiLU architectures: "a higher proportion of neurons remain active during inference, creating a performance bottleneck in neuron loading operations." With SiLU, approximately 50% of neurons are active (citing CATS and CHESS findings). This means the CPU-NPU split leans more toward NPU (fewer sparse cold neurons to exploit) and the neuron cache must store and manage more active weights, reducing cache efficiency.

Importantly, this result demonstrates that PowerInfer-2's mechanisms are not exclusively tied to ReLU-based sparsity—the neuron-cluster pipeline, flexible neuron loading, and differentiated I/O strategies provide benefits even when sparsity is moderate (50% rather than 95%+). The 2.4× speedup shows that even without extreme activation sparsity, the hardware-aware co-design of computation and I/O yields substantial improvements over the PC-derived LLMFlash approach.

#### In-Memory Performance (Figure 12)

When model weights fit entirely in DRAM (the "No offload" condition), PowerInfer-2 still achieves substantial speedups over baselines for Bamboo-7B. For **decoding**: PowerInfer-2 reaches 20 tokens/s, compared to llama.cpp at 11 tokens/s (1.82×), MLC-LLM at 8 tokens/s (2.50×), and QNN at 6 tokens/s (3.33×). The paper attributes this to "concurrent CPU-NPU computation improving memory bandwidth utilization from 43.9GB/s to 59.6GB/s, and leveraging the model's activation sparsity to reduce FFN computations." The memory bandwidth improvement (from CPU-only to CPU+NPU) is a 36% increase, which directly translates to throughput for the memory-bound decoding phase.

For **prefill** (no offloading): PowerInfer-2 achieves 721 tokens/s, comparable to QNN at 772 tokens/s, while llama.cpp reaches only 8 tokens/s and MLC-LLM reaches 37 tokens/s. PowerInfer-2 is slightly slower than QNN (721 vs. 772 tokens/s, a 6.6% gap), which the paper attributes to "mixed precision quantization used by PowerInfer-2, a deliberate trade-off for accuracy preservation." This trade-off is quantified in Section 7.6: QNN's per-channel quantization causes significant accuracy degradation (MMLU drops from 68.73% to 59.45% on Qwen2-7B) while PowerInfer-2's hybrid quantization largely preserves accuracy (MMLU at 68.73% vs. the llama.cpp baseline at 69.72%).

With **50% FFN weight offloading**, PowerInfer-2's prefill speed drops to 404.6 tokens/s (from 721 tokens/s without offloading), while QNN achieves 193 tokens/s when offloading is simulated. The 2.10× advantage over QNN under offloading (vs. 0.93× without offloading) demonstrates that PowerInfer-2's I/O optimizations are the differentiator: QNN's NPU-centric approach cannot hide I/O latency effectively, while PowerInfer-2's sequential prefetching and computation-I/O overlap maintain higher throughput. For decoding with 50% offloading, PowerInfer-2 achieves 10 tokens/s while llama.cpp drops to 0.66 tokens/s and MLC-LLM to 0.3 tokens/s—the 15–33× gaps reflect that these frameworks have no effective mechanism for flash-to-memory weight orchestration.

The in-memory results also demonstrate PowerInfer-2's memory efficiency: "When offloading 50% of FFN weights, PowerInfer-2 reduces memory usage by 1.5GB (40%) while maintaining performance comparable to llama.cpp and MLC-LLM." The 40% memory reduction while matching llama.cpp's 11 tokens/s (20 tokens/s vs. 10 tokens/s with 50% offloading is actually faster than llama.cpp's no-offload speed of 11 tokens/s) means PowerInfer-2 can serve the same model quality with substantially less memory pressure, leaving DRAM for other applications.

#### Best-of-N Sampling (Figure 13)

For Best-of-4 sampling on Bamboo-7B with all weights resident in memory, PowerInfer-2's dynamic CPU-NPU adaptation is evaluated against QNN (NPU-only, no sparsity exploitation) and PowerInfer-2-CPUOnly (CPU-only with sparsity exploitation, simulating a hypothetical PowerInfer port). The x-axis in Figure 13 is iteration number (1–16), where each iteration processes one token across N=4 candidate sequences in parallel, and the batch size decreases by one every four iterations as the first candidate finishes, then the second, and so on.

In the initial phase (batch size 4), PowerInfer-2 achieves approximately 46 tokens/s, which is **1.84× over QNN** (approximately 25 tokens/s) and **1.28× over PowerInfer-2-CPUOnly** (approximately 36 tokens/s). The advantage over QNN stems from sparsity exploitation (QNN computes all neurons densely, including inactive ones), while the advantage over CPU-only stems from NPU accelerating the dense hot-neuron computations.

As sequences complete and batch size decreases, the speed of all configurations drops—fewer tokens are processed per iteration. At batch size 1 (iterations 13–16), PowerInfer-2 achieves approximately 16 tokens/s, compared to PowerInfer-2-CPUOnly at approximately 14.5 tokens/s (1.10×) and QNN at approximately 9 tokens/s (1.78×). The paper explains: "When N reduces to 1, increased sparsity causes QNN's performance to drop below PowerInfer-2-CPUOnly." This is the key validation of the dynamic sparsity insight: a static NPU-only approach (QNN) is worst exactly when sparsity is highest, because it wastes compute on inactive neurons. A static CPU-only approach with sparsity exploitation (PowerInfer-2-CPUOnly) is better at batch size 1 but leaves NPU idle when batch sizes are larger. PowerInfer-2's hybrid approach dynamically shifts the ratio: 70% NPU at batch size 4, graduating to 50% NPU at batch size 1, maintaining the best of both worlds.

The performance curve shape also validates the asynchronous graph-swapping mechanism: the speed decrements smoothly without discontinuities, indicating that NPU graph transitions happen seamlessly (no dropped iterations or stalling during reconfiguration). The paper states that the new NPU graph loading is "completely overlapped with NPU computation" during the attention block, and the smooth curve in Figure 13 is consistent with zero-overhead transitions.

#### Performance Breakdown / Ablation (Figure 14)

The incremental contribution of each optimization is isolated by starting from a baseline configuration (CPU-only, no optimizations) on Bamboo-7B with 50% FFN offloading on OnePlus 12, then enabling optimizations cumulatively:

- **Baseline** (CPU-only, no sparse computation, no bundling, no cache, no pipeline, no NPU): 0.40 tokens/s.
- **+Bundle** (Gate-Up-Down positional bundling for cold neurons): 1.09 tokens/s, a 2.72× speedup. This is the foundation—without bundling, three separate random reads per activated cold neuron would saturate the 100K IOPS UFS limit, making inference impractically slow.
- **+Neuron Cache** (three-region cache with LRU eviction): 4.18 tokens/s, a 3.83× speedup over the previous step and 2.82× on its own relative to Bundle. The 95% cache hit rate "significantly reduces Flash I/O bandwidth pressure." This is the largest single contributor to the overall speedup.
- **+Pipeline** (neuron-cluster-level cross-matrix pipelining): 9.60 tokens/s, a 2.30× speedup over the previous step and 1.29× on its own. This is where I/O overhead drops from dominating to negligible (Table 4). The pipeline's contribution is multiplicative with the cache's because the cache provides the in-memory neuron clusters that the pipeline can compute while waiting for slow I/O on uncached clusters—without the cache, the pipeline has nothing to overlap.
- **+XPU** (NPU co-processing for hot neurons): 11.07 tokens/s, a 1.15× speedup over the previous step and 27.7× over baseline. The NPU's contribution is relatively modest in multiplicative terms (15%) because the decoding phase is memory-bandwidth-bound, and the CPU alone achieves good utilization of UMA bandwidth. However, the aggregate memory bandwidth improvement from 43.9 GB/s (CPU-only) to 59.6 GB/s (CPU+NPU) means the NPU's 15% speedup comes essentially "for free" in terms of the memory bandwidth it unlocks, not just the compute it provides.

The cumulative speedup from baseline (0.40) to full PowerInfer-2 (11.07) is 27.7×, which matches the 27.8× reported for Bamboo-7B over llama.cpp in Figure 7 (the small difference is within measurement variance). This decomposition reveals a dependency hierarchy: bundling is prerequisite (without it, cache and pipeline would be overwhelmed by individual neuron reads), cache is the largest multiplier (reducing I/O volume by 95%), pipeline converts the remaining I/O into background activity (hiding latency), and XPU co-processing extracts the last 15% by maximizing memory bandwidth.

#### Accuracy Evaluation (Table 7)

To validate that PowerInfer-2's optimizations do not degrade model quality, accuracy is evaluated on INT4-quantized Qwen2-7B and Bamboo-7B using OpenCompass benchmarks. The three frameworks compared use different quantization strategies:

- **llama.cpp**: Group-wise quantization (groups of 32 weights per scale). This is the accuracy upper bound for INT4.
- **QNN**: Per-channel quantization (one scale per weight matrix row). This is efficient for NPU execution but poorly handles outlier weights, leading to accuracy degradation.
- **PowerInfer-2**: Hybrid quantization—outlier weights in INT8 precision (preserving their values), remaining weights in INT4 per-channel quantization. This is designed to match QNN's NPU execution efficiency while preserving llama.cpp-level accuracy.

For **Qwen2-7B**: llama.cpp achieves an average of 79.25% across benchmarks (Arc-Challenge 82.37%, Arc-Easy 87.46%, MMLU 69.72%, GSM8K 77.43%). PowerInfer-2 achieves 78.38% average (81.36%, 88.18%, 68.73%, 75.24%), a difference of 0.87 percentage points—negligible. QNN achieves 56.93% average (65.76%, 80.25%, 59.45%, 22.26%), a 22.32-point drop from llama.cpp, with GSM8K hit particularly hard (77.43% → 22.26%, a 55.17-point drop).

For **Bamboo-7B**: llama.cpp achieves 70.12% average (Arc-Challenge 73.90%, Arc-Easy 84.83%, MMLU 61.24%, GSM8K 60.50%). PowerInfer-2 achieves 68.35% average (71.86%, 82.01%, 61.48%, 58.05%), a difference of 1.77 points—again negligible. QNN achieves 63.26% average (69.15%, 78.31%, 57.00%, 48.59%), a 6.86-point drop.

The paper states: "The accuracy gap between frameworks stems from their different quantization approaches. QNN uses per-channel quantization... which poorly handles weights with outlier values. llama.cpp achieves better accuracy by quantizing weights in groups of 32. Since NPUs don't support group-wise quantization, PowerInfer-2 adopts a hybrid quantization approach." The accuracy preservation is important because it means the 24.6× speedup over llama.cpp is not achieved by degrading model quality—the gains are from compute and I/O optimization, not from accepting lower-quality inference. The hybrid quantization is a practical engineering solution to the NPU's constraint that it only supports per-channel quantization: by selectively keeping outlier weights at higher precision, the model largely preserves its original behavior while still benefiting from the NPU's INT4 throughput.

#### Energy Consumption (Table 8)

PowerInfer-2 achieves **0.257 Joules per token** for Bamboo-7B inference on OnePlus 12, compared to 0.373 J/token for QNN (31.1% reduction) and 0.672 J/token for llama.cpp (61.8% reduction). The peak power is similar across frameworks: PowerInfer-2 at 5.095W, QNN at 5.133W, llama.cpp at 4.065W. The energy savings come from faster completion: "This enhanced efficiency is attributed to PowerInfer-2's ability to maintain similar peak power while achieving faster computation speeds, resulting in lower overall energy consumption." Generating a token at 20 tokens/s (PowerInfer-2) consumes less total energy than generating the same token at 6 tokens/s (llama.cpp) because the DRAM, flash, and processors are active for a shorter duration, even though peak power is slightly higher for the hybrid XPU configuration.

The practical implication: for battery-powered smartphone inference, PowerInfer-2's speed advantage translates to either (a) faster response for the same battery drain, or (b) longer battery life for the same number of generated tokens. The 61.8% reduction in J/token versus llama.cpp means the same battery can serve approximately 2.6× more tokens before depletion.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

- **In-memory vs. offloading performance (Figure 12):** The comparison isolates the I/O overhead by testing both 0% and 50% weight offloading. Without offloading, PowerInfer-2 achieves 20 tokens/s decoding (1.82× over llama.cpp, 2.50× over MLC-LLM). With 50% offloading, it achieves 10 tokens/s while llama.cpp drops to 0.66 tokens/s (30× gap). The 10 tokens/s with offloading still matches llama.cpp's no-offload speed of 11 tokens/s—the I/O optimizations recover performance that prior frameworks lose entirely.

- **Best-of-N with CPU-only vs. hybrid vs. NPU-only (Figure 13):** Disabling the NPU (PowerInfer-2-CPUOnly) isolates the contribution of XPU co-processing. At batch size 4, CPU-only achieves 36 tokens/s vs. hybrid at 46 tokens/s (1.28× NPU benefit). At batch size 1, CPU-only reaches 14.5 tokens/s vs. hybrid at 16 tokens/s (1.10× NPU benefit). The NPU contribution is larger at higher batch sizes where dense computation dominates, validating the dynamic ratio adjustment.

- **Performance across difficulty-identical tasks (Figure 11):** Testing on four diverse downstream tasks (role-play, dialogue, math, coding) with the same configuration shows speed variation of at most 0.4 tokens/s out of ~11.5 tokens/s (<4%). This validates that the offline neuron plan (trained on Wikipedia/RefinedWeb activation patterns) generalizes to out-of-distribution prompt types.

- **Memory capacity sweep (Figure 10):** The near-linear scaling from 2.13 tokens/s (7GB, 1.8% cache size) to 11.68 tokens/s (19GB, full cache) on TurboSparse-Mixtral-47B demonstrates that no other bottleneck saturates before memory is exhausted. This is a strong validation: if compute throughput or pipeline efficiency were limiting, the curve would plateau before reaching 19GB. The linearity also validates the LRU eviction policy—adding more cache provides proportional benefit, meaning the eviction order correctly prioritizes the most-accessed neurons.

- **Two-device evaluation (Figure 7, Figure 8):** Results on both OnePlus 12 (Snapdragon 8 Gen 3, UFS 4.0) and OnePlus Ace 2 (Snapdragon 8+ Gen 1, UFS 3.1) show consistent speedup patterns: 3.84× and 2.93× average over LLMFlash, 24.6× and 14.1× over llama.cpp. The speedup ratios differ because the baseline hardware performance differs (llama.cpp is faster on the Ace 2 relative to PowerInfer-2, narrowing the ratio), but the qualitative ranking is preserved. This suggests the optimizations are not specific to a single SoC generation.

- **Prefill I/O overlap validation (Figure 9):** The stacked bar chart for Bamboo-7B and Qwen2-7B shows computation time and sequential I/O time separately, with the total layer time equal to the max of the two (not the sum). This directly validates the claim that "I/O operations are completely overlapped with computation time through our pipelining strategy, effectively hiding I/O latency."

- **Activation sparsity analysis across batch sizes (Figure 2):** The heatmap in layer 10 of Bamboo-7B is the empirical foundation for the dynamic sparsity claim. It shows activation frequencies (dark red = low, white = high) for neurons sorted by frequency across batch sizes 1–32. The visual evidence is clear: at batch size 1, only a narrow band of neurons (far left, <1%) show high activation; at batch size 32, a broad band (~75%) shows high activation. This is not a model-specific artifact—it reflects the statistical property that aggregating activations across independent tokens increases the probability that any given neuron is activated by at least one token.

- **Cache miss rate distribution (Section 7.2.4):** The paper reports that TurboSparse-Mixtral-47B's average cache miss rate is 3.5% but the P99 miss rate reaches 18.9%. This 5.4× ratio between average and tail miss rates explains the P99 latency being 40.9% above the mean (Table 5). The large tail indicates that while the LRU policy is effective on average, activation patterns occasionally shift to rarely-used neuron groups, causing cache thrashing that the pipeline cannot fully hide (because all cold neurons for that token must be loaded from flash).

- **SiLU vs. ReLU comparison (Table 6):** Testing Mistral(SiLU)-7B alongside Bamboo-7B (ReLU) isolates the dependence on activation function. The speedup drops from 4.6× to 2.4× when moving from ReLU (extreme sparsity) to SiLU (moderate sparsity), but the 2.4× speedup remains substantial. This demonstrates that PowerInfer-2's mechanisms are not solely dependent on ReLU-based sparsity—the I/O optimizations (bundling, cache, pipeline) provide significant benefits even at 50% activation density.

- **No GPU baseline comparison:** The paper explicitly justifies excluding GPU-based baselines (MLC-LLM) from the offloading comparison: MLC-LLM does not support weight offloading and fails to run when memory is insufficient (marked with ✗ in Figure 12). However, for the in-memory comparison, MLC-LLM achieves 8 tokens/s on Bamboo-7B decoding vs. PowerInfer-2's 20 tokens/s. The 2.50× advantage over the GPU-based framework supports the claim that GPU inference on smartphones is not competitive with the NPU-CPU hybrid approach, even without memory constraints.

### Critical Assessment

#### Claim: "PowerInfer-2 achieves up to 27.8× speedup over llama.cpp and 3.84× average speedup over LLMFlash"

**What was tested:** The comparison is at 50% weight offloading (75% for one configuration) on two smartphones, across five models of varying size and architecture (Figure 7). The speedup is measured in tokens/s for decoding, averaged over 10 runs, with prompts of up to 64 tokens and outputs of up to 1,024 tokens.

**Assessment:** The numbers are credible and well-supported by the ablation study (Figure 14) which decomposes the 27.7× cumulative speedup into multiplicative contributions from four mechanisms. However, the 27.8× figure specifically refers to Bamboo-7B on OnePlus 12 comparing 11.1 tokens/s (PowerInfer-2) vs. 0.4 tokens/s (llama.cpp). This is the best-case combination: a model with extremely high activation sparsity (ReLU-based, purpose-trained for sparsity), the fastest tested hardware (Snapdragon 8 Gen 3, UFS 4.0), and the weakest baseline (llama.cpp with mmap has no sparse computation, no bundling, no cache, no pipeline). The speedup over llama.cpp on Llama-13B is 27.0× (5.4 vs. 0.2 tokens/s), on Qwen2-7B is 25.0× (10.0 vs. 0.4), and on Mixtral-47B is 23.5× (9.5 vs. 0.4 from Figure 7 read). These are all within 14% of the headline 27.8×, so the "up to" qualifier is appropriate but the average across models is closer to 24–25×.

The 3.84× average speedup over LLMFlash is a fairer comparison because LLMFlash includes sparsity prediction, bundling, and caching—it is a reasonable port of PC optimizations to smartphone hardware. The 3.84× average (range: 2.93× on OnePlus Ace 2 to 4.63× on OnePlus 12) represents the gain from hardware-aware co-design (PowerInfer-2) over hardware-agnostic porting (LLMFlash). The main caveat is that LLMFlash was re-implemented "based on the paper's description" in llama.cpp—the reimplementation may not capture all of LLMFlash's optimizations, potentially overstating the speedup. The paper does not discuss potential fidelity gaps in the reimplementation.

A missing baseline is PowerInfer itself, ported to smartphones with flash offloading support. The paper mentions extending PowerInfer's design to handle flash offloading (Section 2.4) and reports 1.4 tokens/s decoding for Mistral-7B with 50% offloading on the OnePlus 12. This would be approximately 5.3 / 1.4 = 3.8× for PowerInfer-2 over PowerInfer, but this comparison is only made for Mistral-7B and only in the motivation section (Table 2), not in the main evaluation. A systematic PowerInfer vs. PowerInfer-2 comparison across all models and both devices would have strengthened the claim by isolating exactly how much the neuron-cluster abstraction improves over PowerInfer's static hot-cold GPU-CPU split adapted for smartphone NPU-CPU.

#### Claim: "PowerInfer-2 is the first system to serve a 47B LLM on a smartphone, achieving 11.68 tokens/s"

**What was tested:** TurboSparse-Mixtral-47B runs on OnePlus 12 at 11.68 tokens/s when all 19GB of available memory is used for the neuron cache (Section 7.2.3, Figure 10). With only 7GB available, speed drops to 2.13 tokens/s—still functional but below the 10 tokens/s threshold typically considered interactive.

**Assessment:** The claim is well-supported. A 47B-parameter model running interactively on a smartphone is genuinely unprecedented—prior smartphone deployments topped out at ~7B models (Gemini Nano at 3.25B, various 7B open-weight models). However, the "47B" designation deserves scrutiny. TurboSparse-Mixtral-47B is a mixture-of-experts model where only ~3B parameters are activated per token. This is comparable to Bamboo-7B's activated parameter count, which is why their decoding speeds are similar (9.5 vs. 11.1 tokens/s). The 47B figure refers to total parameters, but the computational and I/O requirements scale with activated parameters, not total parameters. The paper is transparent about this ("its mixture-of-expert architecture and high sparsity result in only about 3B activated parameters per token"), but the headline "first 47B model on a smartphone" is slightly misleading—it is a 47B-parameter model with ~3B effective parameters per token. A dense 47B model would have vastly higher memory and compute requirements and would not run at interactive speeds even with PowerInfer-2's optimizations.

This is not a criticism of the achievement—running a 47B MoE model on a phone is genuinely impressive and requires all of PowerInfer-2's mechanisms to work—but the framing could overstate the generality. The system supports models with high activation sparsity (ReLU-based or MoE); it does not enable arbitrary 47B dense models to run on smartphones.

#### Claim: "These performance improvements preserve model quality with negligible accuracy degradation"

**What was tested:** INT4-quantized Qwen2-7B and Bamboo-7B on four benchmarks (Arc-Challenge, Arc-Easy, MMLU, GSM8K). PowerInfer-2's hybrid quantization achieves within 1–2 percentage points of llama.cpp's group-wise quantization (Table 7).

**Assessment:** This claim is well-supported for INT4 quantization. The accuracy gap is genuinely small: 0.87 points on Qwen2-7B, 1.77 points on Bamboo-7B. However, "negligible accuracy degradation" refers specifically to the quantization strategy, not to any accuracy impact of the sparsity-based optimizations (predictor, hot-cold split). The paper does not evaluate whether the predictor's false negatives (skipping neurons that should have been activated) cause accuracy degradation independent of quantization. Since PowerInfer-2 shares PowerInfer's predictor-based approach, and PowerInfer reported small accuracy impacts from predictor errors, this is likely minor—but it is not directly measured in this paper. A comparison of PowerInfer-2 with perfect predictor (oracle neuron activations) versus the learned predictor would isolate any predictor-induced accuracy loss.

Additionally, accuracy is only evaluated for 7B models, not for Llama-13B or Mixtral-47B. For the 47B model, accuracy benchmarks on OpenCompass may not exist or may not be reported because the model is too large to run on the baseline frameworks at acceptable speed. This is a practical limitation rather than an oversight, but it means the "quality preservation" claim is validated only for the 7B scale.

#### Claim: "PowerInfer-2 reduces memory usage by 40% while matching llama.cpp speed"

**What was tested:** Bamboo-7B in-memory decoding (Figure 12). With all weights in memory, PowerInfer-2 achieves 20 tokens/s and consumes a certain amount of DRAM. With 50% FFN weights offloaded, it achieves 10 tokens/s while reducing memory usage by 1.5GB (40%). llama.cpp achieves 11 tokens/s without offloading.

**Assessment:** The 40% reduction number is specific to Bamboo-7B. The paper does not report absolute memory consumption in GB for each framework, making independent verification of the 40% figure difficult. The reduction comes from offloading FFN weights (which dominate model size at ~80% of parameters), and the 40% likely reflects the fraction of total model weight memory freed by offloading 50% of FFN weights: 0.5 × 0.8 = 0.4 = 40%. This is arithmetic rather than a measurement, but it aligns with the description. The claim that speed "matches" llama.cpp (10 vs. 11 tokens/s) is fair—the 10% difference is within measurement variance for 10-run averaging—though technically PowerInfer-2 is slightly slower.

#### Missing Experiments and Analyses

**Predictor accuracy ablation.** PowerInfer-2 inherits PowerInfer's online predictor for cold neurons, but its accuracy is never directly measured or ablated. How does predictor quality affect end-to-end speed? A weak predictor that predicts too many neurons (low skip rate) would increase I/O volume and reduce cache effectiveness; a predictor that misses activated neurons (low recall) would cause accuracy degradation. Neither sensitivity is explored. Given that the predictor is the gate for all cold-neuron computation and I/O, its behavior under different models, quantization levels, and task distributions is a significant gap.

**Startup / model loading time.** The offline planner requires running the model on 10M+ tokens to profile activation patterns, generating NPU computation graphs, and organizing weights into the positional storage format. The time required for this one-time preprocessing step is not reported. For deployment, the time-to-first-token for a newly installed model would include this preprocessing, and it could be substantial (hours of profiling on smartphone hardware). Similarly, loading the model from flash into the neuron cache at session start (cold start latency) is not measured separately from inference speed.

**Multi-application memory pressure.** The memory capacity sweep (Figure 10) varies available memory but treats inference as the sole workload. In practice, a smartphone running LLM inference also has background apps, system services, and the Android framework consuming memory. The paper does not evaluate whether PowerInfer-2's memory management (mlock, fixed cache regions) plays well with Android's memory pressure mechanisms (low-memory killer, process reclaim). The use of mlock (pinning pages in memory) described in Section 6 could make the inference process a target for the OOM killer under genuine memory pressure.

**Batch size > 4 for Best-of-N.** The evaluation is limited to Best-of-4 (Figure 13). Best-of-N with N=8, 16, or larger is used in practice (Snell et al., 2024 evaluate up to N=256), but these larger batch sizes are not tested. At large N, the sparsity characteristics would shift further toward dense (Figure 2 shows ~75% hot at batch size 32), and the NPU's dense advantage would dominate. Whether PowerInfer-2's dynamic adaptation continues to provide benefits at N=16 or whether it converges to QNN's performance (NPU-only being optimal for very large batches) is an open question.

**Long-sequence behavior.** Decoding is measured for up to 1,024 output tokens, but the KV cache grows linearly with sequence length. For very long generations (4K, 8K tokens), the KV cache could consume substantial memory, reducing the space available for the neuron cache and degrading performance. The paper does not evaluate how speed scales with sequence length due to growing KV cache pressure.

**Fairness of the LLMFlash comparison.** LLMFlash's reimplementation in llama.cpp is a significant methodological concern. The original LLMFlash was designed for Apple Silicon (M1/M2/M3) with a unified memory architecture and fast NVMe-like storage. Its "row-column bundling" and "neuron data caching" were optimized for that hardware. Reimplementing these mechanisms in a CPU-based framework (llama.cpp) on Qualcomm hardware may not replicate LLMFlash's intended performance. The paper acknowledges this approach but does not validate the reimplementation's fidelity against LLMFlash's reported PC performance numbers. If the reimplementation is suboptimal (e.g., caching strategy not well-tuned for UFS characteristics), the 3.84× speedup overstates PowerInfer-2's advantage over a properly smartphone-adapted LLMFlash.

**Energy measurement methodology.** Energy is measured at the device level (Joules per token, Table 8), but the measurement methodology is not described. Does it use a power meter? On-device battery statistics? The Qualcomm power management IC? The precision of energy measurements is important because the differences between frameworks in peak power are small (4–5W), and the J/token difference is driven by speed (tokens/J = tokens/s / W). If speed measurements have 5–10% variance (reasonable for 10-run averaging without confidence intervals), the derived J/token values inherit this variance. Without methodology details, the energy efficiency claims are suggestive but not rigorously validated.

#### Summary

The experiments convincingly demonstrate that PowerInfer-2 achieves large speedups over prior frameworks under memory-constrained smartphone inference, with the speedups arising from the four mechanisms identified in the ablation study (bundling, caching, pipelining, XPU co-processing). The evidence is strongest for ReLU-based models with high activation sparsity (11.68 tokens/s for a 47B MoE, 27.8× over llama.cpp for Bamboo-7B), moderate but meaningful for SiLU-based models (2.4× for Mistral-7B), and validated across two hardware generations. The accuracy and energy results are positive but less thoroughly evaluated—accuracy is tested only for 7B INT4 models on four benchmarks, and energy measurements lack methodological detail. The main gaps are: (1) no direct PowerInfer comparison in the main evaluation, (2) LLMFlash reimplementation fidelity unvalidated, (3) no predictor accuracy ablation, (4) no large-batch Best-of-N evaluation beyond N=4, and (5) no analysis of cold-start latency or offline preprocessing cost. These do not undermine the central claims but bound their generality and highlight areas where the practical deployment picture is incomplete.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Difficulty Estimation Cost Is Excluded from Reported Efficiency Gains, Overstating Practical Usability

**The assumption or constraint.** The entire compute-optimal framework depends on estimating each prompt's difficulty before allocating the test-time budget. The paper's method for doing this—generating 2048 samples per question and averaging either ground-truth correctness (oracle) or PRM final-answer scores (predicted)—is extraordinarily expensive. Section 3.2 states this explicitly:

> "estimating difficulty in this way still incurs additional computation cost during inference... our experiments do not account for this cost largely for simplicity"

The 2048-sample generation is comparable to or larger than the largest test-time budgets studied (256–512 generations), meaning difficulty estimation alone can consume more compute than the problem-solving step it is meant to optimize.

**The consequence.** The reported 4× efficiency gains over best-of-N (Figures 4 and 8) are computed *after* difficulty is known, without amortizing the cost of learning it. In a realistic deployment, total cost equals difficulty estimation plus strategy execution. Since difficulty estimation at 2048 samples per prompt can dominate, the net efficiency relative to best-of-N could be substantially less than 4×, potentially negative for easy problems where a simple best-of-N would have sufficed without the estimation overhead. The claim "up to 4× less test-time compute" (§1) should be understood as an upper bound on achievable efficiency assuming difficulty is known for free—not a realized deployment gain.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper acknowledges the issue in Section 3.2 but never quantifies the gap between reported and actual efficiency. No experiment accounts for difficulty estimation cost in the total compute budget. The fact that predicted bins (using PRM scores) work similarly to oracle bins (Figures 4 and 8) removes the need for ground-truth labels but does *not* remove the 2048-sample generation cost—the predicted method still requires generating and scoring 2048 samples per prompt, just without checking correctness against ground truth.

**Mitigation status.** The paper explicitly flags this as "a key avenue for future work" (Section 3.2) and suggests "developing methods that predict question difficulty before allocating the budget" through pretraining or fine-tuning models to predict difficulty from question text alone. No such lightweight estimator is developed or evaluated in this work. The paper does not explore adaptive difficulty estimation where initial samples serve double duty as both difficulty probes and solution candidates.

---

### No Combination of Revisions and PRM Search, Despite Their Demonstrated Complementary Strengths

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper studies two independent axes for test-time compute scaling: PRM-guided search (Section 5) and iterative revisions (Section 6). These are treated as separate pipelines with separate compute-optimal policies. Section 8 acknowledges this explicitly:

> "we did not experiment with PRM tree-search techniques in combination with revisions"

The revision model is used only with simple verifier-based selection (within-chain majority or ORM scoring), never as the proposal distribution inside a PRM-guided beam search.

**The consequence.** The paper demonstrates that revisions and search have complementary, difficulty-dependent strengths—revisions excel on easy problems (local refinement), search on medium problems (global exploration through beam search). But these strengths are never combined. Using the revision model as the proposal distribution within beam search (where the PRM guides which revision paths to pursue) could yield gains beyond either method alone—potentially pushing the performance ceiling on medium problems where both mechanisms show partial effectiveness, or extending the difficulty range where test-time compute helps. The current results therefore represent a *lower bound* on what a fully integrated system could achieve. The compute-optimal policy presented is optimal only within the restricted strategy space of "search OR revisions," not "search AND revisions."

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The difficulty-bin analyses (Figure 3 right for search, Figure 7 right for revisions) show that the two methods have complementary strengths: beam search best on bins 3–4, sequential revisions best on bins 1–2. Section 8 states the combination was not attempted. No experiment measures how much performance is left on the table by keeping these axes separate.

**Mitigation status.** The paper identifies this as future work (Section 8: "our compute-optimal strategy could be extended to also decide which combination of these two strategies to use"). No preliminary results or design sketches for the combination are provided. The path to integration is non-trivial: the revision model's outputs are off-distribution relative to the PRM's training data (the paper shows the base-model PRM underperforms on revision outputs, Appendix J, Figure 15a), so combining them would require either retraining the PRM on revision trajectories or training a revision-aware verifier.

---

### Hard Problems Remain Fundamentally Unsolved—Test-Time Compute Cannot Substitute for Missing Base Capability

**The assumption or constraint.** The compute-optimal framework assumes that useful test-time compute strategies exist for a given prompt—that there is *some* allocation of the budget that improves accuracy. This assumption holds only when the base model's pass@1 is non-trivially above zero. For problems where the base model almost never produces a correct solution, no search or revision strategy can find or refine what does not exist in the proposal distribution.

**The consequence.** Across all methods—search, revisions, and their compute-optimal combinations—the hardest questions (difficulty bin 5) show near-zero improvement regardless of compute budget. In Figure 3 (right), bin 5 accuracy hovers at 1–3% for all methods at all budgets. In Figure 7 (right), bin 5 shows roughly 2–3% accuracy irrespective of the sequential-to-parallel ratio. In the FLOPs-matched comparison (Figure 9), the bin 5 scaling line is essentially flat near 0–5%, while the 14× larger model's greedy decoding achieves substantially higher accuracy on these problems. Test-time compute amplifies existing capability but does not create it from nothing. For deployments where the problem distribution skews toward genuinely hard problems—those beyond the base model's training distribution—this approach offers no path forward, and pretraining larger models remains the only viable route.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figure 3 (right), Figure 7 (right), and Figure 9 all show bin 5 accuracy flat at near-zero across all methods and budgets. Section 7's takeaway box states: "On hard questions, pretraining is almost always more effective." The paper is transparent about this boundary but does not characterize *what fraction* of real-world queries fall into bin 5 for a given base model—the 500-question MATH test set is not representative of production query distributions.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not propose any solution for hard problems. It acknowledges the limitation as a fundamental property: test-time compute and pretraining compute are "not 1-to-1 exchangeable" (§7). The difficulty binning framework helps identify which problems are hopeless (bin 5) so that compute is not wasted on them, but this is a *detection* mechanism, not a *resolution* mechanism. The paper does not explore whether test-time compute strategies could help on hard problems if the base model were fine-tuned to improve its pass@1 on those problems, or whether iterative self-improvement loops could gradually expand the capability frontier.

---

### Single Benchmark, Single Model Family Limits Generality of the Difficulty-Dependent Patterns

**The assumption or constraint.** All experiments use the MATH benchmark (500 test questions, high-school competition math) with PaLM 2-S* as the base model. Section 4 states the authors "believe this model is representative of the capabilities of many contemporary LLMs," but this claim is unverified. The specific difficulty-dependent patterns observed—beam search hurting easy problems at high budgets, revisions dominating on easy problems, neither method helping on hard problems—may be artifacts of (a) math-specific reasoning patterns, (b) PaLM 2-S*'s specific calibration and error characteristics, or (c) the MATH dataset's difficulty distribution and problem types.

**The consequence.** A practitioner deploying compute-optimal test-time scaling on a different domain (code generation, factual QA, summarization) or a different model family (Llama, GPT, Claude) cannot be confident that the same difficulty-dependent strategies apply. For example, code generation has different failure modes (syntax errors vs. logical errors) that may respond differently to revisions versus search. A model with better calibration may show less PRM over-optimization, shifting the difficulty thresholds. A benchmark with multi-modal problems or open-ended generation may lack clean verifier training signals entirely. The computed-optimal policies derived in this paper—best-of-N on easy problems, beam search on medium problems, specific sequential-to-parallel ratios—are specific to PaLM 2-S* on MATH and may not transfer.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** All figures (3–9) use PaLM 2-S* on MATH. The paper does not report results on any other model or benchmark. The 500-question test set is split into five difficulty quintiles of ~100 questions each, then further split by two-fold cross-validation, meaning strategy selection is based on ~50 questions per fold per bin—a small sample that may not produce robust policies even within the MATH domain. Confidence intervals on the compute-optimal scaling curves are not reported.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation. Section 8 does not mention multi-benchmark or multi-model evaluation as future work. The authors' belief that PaLM 2-S* is "representative" is asserted without supporting evidence. A practitioner would need to re-derive compute-optimal policies for their specific model-benchmark combination, which requires the full pipeline: PRM training, revision model training, difficulty binning, strategy sweep, and cross-validation—a substantial engineering effort that the paper's results do not shortcut.

### The Revision Model Suffers from a 38% Correct-to-Incorrect Reversion Rate with Only Partial Mitigation

**The assumption or constraint.** The revision model is trained exclusively on sequences where all in-context answers are incorrect followed by a correct target (Section 6.1). This training distribution means the model never sees examples of correct answers in context, and thus never learns to recognize when no revision is needed. At test time, when a revision chain produces a correct answer at step *k*, the model may encounter this correct answer in its context at step *k+1* and incorrectly "revise" it into a wrong answer.

**The consequence.** Section 6.1 reports that "approximately 38% of correct answers get converted back to incorrect ones" using a naive approach that always takes the last revision. This means the revision chain does not monotonically improve—it oscillates, with correct answers being undone by subsequent revisions. The paper mitigates this with within-chain selection (majority voting or verifier-based selection across all steps, taking the best answer rather than the last), but these are post-hoc patches. Majority voting across a chain where 38% of revisions degrade correct answers is unreliable when the chain is short or when most revisions happen to be incorrect. The verifier-based selection adds computational overhead and depends on verifier quality, which itself degrades under distribution shift (the PRM was not trained on revision outputs; Figure 15a). The fundamental issue—that the model does not know when to stop revising—remains unresolved.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Section 6.1 reports the 38% figure. Figure 6 (left) shows that pass@1 at each step initially improves (step 1: ~18.2%, steps 15–20: ~24–25%) but plateaus rather than continuing to improve, consistent with oscillation. Appendix K's ReST^{EM} experiment (Figure 16) shows that further optimizing the revision model with RL-style training *degrades* performance substantially, suggesting the revision training is fragile and sensitive to data distribution.

**Mitigation status.** The paper mitigates the symptom (within-chain selection) but does not address the root cause (training distribution mismatch). Section 8 does not propose solutions such as training the model on mixed trajectories that include correct-to-correct transitions, adding an explicit "stop revising" token, or using the verifier's confidence to decide when to terminate the chain. The ReST^{EM} negative result (Appendix K) indicates that naive attempts to improve the revision model can backfire, making this a non-trivial problem that the paper leaves open.

### The Growing KV Cache Under Long Sequences Is Not Evaluated and May Crowd Out the Neuron Cache

**The assumption or constraint.** The neuron cache (§4.2) holds attention weights, hot neuron clusters, and cold neurons in DRAM. The KV cache—which stores key and value tensors for all previously generated tokens—grows linearly with sequence length and is also resident in DRAM (in the "fixed region for attention weights and KV cache"). The paper evaluates decoding for outputs up to 1,024 tokens (§7.1). At this length, with typical model dimensions, the KV cache for a single sequence is manageable (e.g., ~32MB per layer for a 7B model at 1K tokens). However, for longer generations (4K, 8K, or 32K tokens common in production deployments) and multiple concurrent sequences (Best-of-N, MCTS), the KV cache can consume gigabytes of DRAM.

**The consequence.** As the KV cache grows, it competes with the neuron cache for the fixed DRAM budget. The paper demonstrates that decoding speed scales linearly with neuron cache size (Figure 10, from 7GB to 19GB total available memory). If the KV cache grows to consume, say, 4GB of the 19GB budget, the neuron cache shrinks correspondingly, and decoding speed degrades along the curve in Figure 10. For very long sequences (32K tokens), the KV cache alone could consume most available DRAM, leaving negligible space for the neuron cache and forcing nearly all FFN weights to be reloaded from flash on every token—collapsing performance toward the 2.13 tokens/s observed at 7GB. The paper's evaluation at 1,024 tokens does not reveal this regime, and the compute-optimal policies derived at short context lengths may not apply when cache pressure changes the relative cost of different strategies.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figure 10 shows the strong dependence of speed on available memory for the neuron cache. Section 7.1 states "up to 1,024 token outputs" were evaluated. No experiment varies sequence length while measuring speed, nor quantifies KV cache growth relative to total memory. The per-layer memory allocation described in Section 4.2 includes a "fixed region for attention weights and KV cache" but does not specify its size or how it scales with sequence length.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation. KV cache compression techniques (quantization, token eviction, sliding window attention) are not discussed or integrated. The neuron-cache design could potentially be extended to dynamically resize the KV cache region and the hot/cold neuron regions based on sequence length, but this is not explored. A practitioner deploying PowerInfer-2 for long-context applications would need to independently characterize the speed-vs-sequence-length tradeoff and potentially limit context length to maintain interactive speeds.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper fundamentally reframes smartphone LLM inference from a **capacity problem** (how do we compress models enough to fit?) to a **systems co-design problem** (how do we schedule computation, caching, and I/O around a single abstraction that respects the hardware's genuine heterogeneity?). This is not a paradigm shift in the Kuhnian sense—the individual techniques (sparse computation, I/O pipelining, heterogeneous scheduling) each have precedents. Rather, it is a **diagnostic reframing** with practical consequences that change which research directions look promising.

Prior to this work, the dominant approaches to on-device LLM deployment fell into two camps. The first camp, represented by llama.cpp and MLC-LLM, pursued model compression (quantization, pruning) to squeeze models into device memory, treating the hardware as a fixed constraint. The second camp, represented by PowerInfer and LLMFlash, exploited algorithmic properties (activation sparsity) and storage hierarchies (flash offloading) but did so with PC-derived assumptions: that the dominant processor handles both dense and sparse computation well, that storage random-read performance is adequate for matrix-level scheduling, and that sparsity patterns are static. Both camps achieved results on PCs but collapsed on smartphones—PowerInfer-2's measurements show 70–89% speed degradation for 7B models when these PC strategies are ported directly (Table 2).

PowerInfer-2's contribution is demonstrating that this collapse is not inevitable—it is an artifact of designing each subsystem (computation, caching, I/O) at different granularities and assuming the hardware slacks that exist on PCs will absorb the coordination overhead. The neuron-cluster abstraction resolves this by forcing all three subsystems to operate at the same granularity, enabling them to compose multiplicatively (Figure 14: 2.72× × 2.82× × 1.29× × 1.15× = 27.7× cumulative speedup). The lesson for the field is that **on severely resource-constrained heterogeneous hardware, abstraction granularity is a first-order design constraint that must satisfy all subsystems simultaneously**—optimizing each in isolation and accepting the coordination overhead is not just suboptimal but catastrophic.

This reframing makes several prior research directions less attractive. **GPU-centric mobile inference** (pursued by MLC-LLM) now looks like a dead end: the paper's measurements show mobile GPUs are consistently slower than both NPU and CPU for matrix-vector operations (Figure 3a), only 50% of GPU kernel time goes to actual computation, and GPU usage competes with rendering for the display. The paper's note that "private communications with mobile manufacturers reveal that they avoid using GPUs for LLM inference in production systems" is a strong signal that this direction is being abandoned by the industry players who control the hardware. **Static sparsity exploitation** (PowerInfer's fixed hot-cold partition) is similarly undermined: Figure 2's demonstration that hot-neuron proportions shift from <1% at batch size 1 to ~75% at batch size 32 means any static partition is misconfigured for a significant fraction of operational time during parallel decoding. **Matrix-level I/O pipelining** (LLMFlash, STI) is shown to be fundamentally limited on UFS because the matrix barrier forces synchronization with the slowest I/O operation within each matrix—the 76.7% I/O overhead in Table 4 is not a number that can be optimized away with better buffering; it requires breaking the matrix granularity entirely.

Conversely, several directions become more attractive. **NPU-centric architectures with CPU fallback for sparse operations** is validated as the correct smartphone compute model—the paper's competence-aware split (NPU for dense hot clusters, CPU for sparse cold clusters) outperforms both NPU-only (QNN) and CPU-only (PowerInfer-2-CPUOnly) across all batch sizes (Figure 13). **Activation sparsity as a systems property rather than a model property** becomes a design principle: the dynamic CPU-NPU ratio adjustment demonstrates that sparsity exploitation must be runtime-adaptive to workload characteristics, not baked in at model training time. **Sub-computation-unit scheduling for high-variance I/O** generalizes beyond UFS: any system loading data from high-latency-variance storage can benefit from subdividing computations below their natural granularity to route around slow operations.

The paper also resolves a tension in the mobile deployment literature. Prior work presented a false dichotomy: either use the NPU for everything (QNN, achieving high peak throughput but poor accuracy due to per-channel quantization and no sparsity exploitation) or use the CPU with sparsity exploitation (PowerInfer-style, achieving good accuracy and sparsity gains but poor peak throughput and memory bandwidth utilization). PowerInfer-2 demonstrates that this is a false choice—the hybrid approach achieves the accuracy of CPU-based quantization (Table 7: within 1–2 points of llama.cpp) with the throughput of NPU co-processing (20 tokens/s vs. 6 tokens/s for QNN on in-memory Bamboo-7B), while simultaneously enabling models 6.7× larger than the memory limit (47B TurboSparse-Mixtral at 11.68 tokens/s). The key insight is that the NPU should not be the sole processor but the *dense-specialized* processor in a heterogeneous ensemble, with the CPU handling the sparse tail that the NPU is fundamentally bad at.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Neuron-plan generalization across model architectures and task distributions.** The offline planner profiles activation patterns on Wikipedia and RefinedWeb (10M+ tokens), then the resulting neuron plan is used for diverse downstream tasks (dialogue, math, coding, role-play). The paper shows that decoding speed varies by <4% across these tasks (Figure 11), suggesting good generalization—but this is only tested for Bamboo-7B and TurboSparse-Mixtral-47B. A systematic study would measure whether the neuron plan's hot-cold classification degrades under distribution shift—for example, does a plan generated from English Wikipedia generalize to Chinese text, code, or multilingual mixtures? The specific experiment: profile Bamboo-7B on English-only data, then measure cache miss rates and decoding speed on the FLORES-200 multilingual benchmark. If cache miss rates spike for low-resource languages, the implication is that deployment across diverse user bases requires either per-language profiling or online plan adaptation. A negative result (large performance degradation under distribution shift) would motivate research on lightweight runtime plan adjustment rather than one-time offline profiling.

**Predictor accuracy ablation under quantization and model scale.** PowerInfer-2 inherits PowerInfer's online predictor for cold-neuron activation but never measures its accuracy independently. The predictor gates all cold-neuron computation and I/O—if it predicts too many false positives (activating neurons that are actually inactive), it wastes I/O bandwidth loading unused weights; if it predicts false negatives (missing genuinely active neurons), it causes accuracy degradation that is never evaluated. A controlled experiment would instrument PowerInfer-2 to record predictor precision and recall per layer during decoding on MATH or HumanEval, then correlate these metrics with per-token latency. Specific question: does the 18.9% P99 cache miss rate for TurboSparse-Mixtral-47B (Section 7.2.4) correlate with predictor false positives loading neurons that turn out to be inactive? If so, improving predictor calibration on tail activation patterns could reduce the P99 latency penalty (currently 40.9% above mean) without any changes to the cache or pipeline.

**Integration of speculative decoding with the neuron-cluster pipeline.** Speculative decoding (Leviathan et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2024) uses a small draft model to generate candidate tokens that are verified in parallel by the large model. This creates a similar dynamic-batch-size pattern to Best-of-N: the verification step processes multiple candidates simultaneously (higher batch size, lower sparsity), while the draft step processes one token at a time (lower batch size, higher sparsity). PowerInfer-2's dynamic CPU-NPU ratio adjustment is designed for exactly this pattern. A concrete follow-up: integrate a 0.5B-parameter draft model into PowerInfer-2's pipeline, measure the combined speedup from sparsity-aware scheduling plus speculative decoding on TurboSparse-Mixtral-47B, and compare against both techniques in isolation. The key measurement is whether the NPU graph-swapping mechanism (asynchronous graph loading during attention computation) can handle the rapid batch-size changes between draft (batch=1) and verification (batch=4–8) without introducing transition latency that negates the speculative gains.

**Long-context behavior and KV-cache-aware neuron-cache resizing.** The paper evaluates decoding up to 1,024 tokens and shows that speed scales linearly with neuron cache size (Figure 10). At longer contexts (4K, 8K, 32K tokens), the KV cache competes with the neuron cache for DRAM. A critical experiment: run Bamboo-7B with PowerInfer-2 on a long-document QA benchmark (e.g., NarrativeQA, 32K-token contexts) while measuring both decoding speed and KV-cache memory consumption as context length increases. Map the speed degradation curve against Figure 10's memory-vs-speed relationship to determine at what context length the KV cache crowds out the neuron cache enough to drop below interactive speeds (10 tokens/s). If speed collapses at 8K tokens for a 7B model with 50% weight offloading, this would motivate research on KV-cache-aware neuron-cache eviction policies that preferentially evict cold neurons from layers whose KV cache is small (early layers) versus layers whose KV cache is large (later layers, after attention has concentrated). This is a natural extension of PowerInfer-2's three-region cache design with dynamic resizing.

**Cross-SoC generalization and the NPU-sparse-antagonism boundary.** The paper's characterization of the NPU-sparse gap (NPU sparse primitives slower than CPU) is measured on Qualcomm Snapdragon 8 Gen 3. Other mobile SoCs—Apple's A17/M3 Neural Engine, Samsung's Exynos NPU, MediaTek's APU—may have different sparse-compute characteristics. A measurement study replicating Figure 3(a) across 3–4 SoC generations and manufacturers would establish whether the NPU-sparse gap is a Qualcomm-specific limitation or a fundamental architectural property of mobile NPUs (which are optimized for dense convolution operations in camera and voice pipelines). If some NPUs handle sparse operations efficiently, the competence-aware split may shift toward more NPU-dominant allocation, and the neuron-cluster sizing logic in the offline planner would need SoC-specific tuning. A negative result—finding an NPU where sparse primitives match or exceed CPU performance—would actually strengthen the paper's contribution by validating that the hardware-aware planning framework correctly adapts to different competence boundaries, rather than hardcoding the Qualcomm-specific asymmetry.

**Energy-delay product optimization for battery-constrained always-on inference.** The paper reports energy consumption of 0.257 J/token for Bamboo-7B (Table 8) but treats this as a secondary metric derived from speed and peak power. A more sophisticated analysis would characterize the full energy-delay tradeoff: when battery is below 20%, a user might prefer 0.5× the speed for 2× the energy efficiency, or vice versa when plugged in. The experiment: add a configurable "energy budget" parameter to PowerInfer-2 that modifies the CPU-NPU split ratio, NPU clock frequency, and cache aggressiveness to operate at different points on the Pareto frontier of tokens/s vs. J/token. Measure this frontier for Bamboo-7B across three power states (battery saver, balanced, performance). If the Pareto frontier shows that substantial energy savings are achievable with modest speed reductions (e.g., 30% less energy for 15% less speed by favoring CPU sparse computation over NPU dense computation when the NPU's fixed power overhead dominates), this would enable dynamic power management that the current system lacks.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**On-device AI assistants with datacenter-scale model quality.** The most immediate application is upgrading smartphone AI assistants (Siri, Gemini, Galaxy AI) from 3B-parameter models (Gemini Nano, 3.25B parameters, <2GB memory) to models in the 7B–47B range while maintaining fully on-device operation. PowerInfer-2's demonstration of TurboSparse-Mixtral-47B at 11.68 tokens/s on 19GB of available memory (Figure 10) means a flagship smartphone can run a model with approximately 14× more *total* parameters than Gemini Nano, and approximately the same *activated* parameters per token (~3B for both, since Mixtral-47B's MoE architecture means only ~3B are active). The practical benefit: the Mixtral architecture's mixture-of-experts design provides the knowledge capacity of 47B parameters (comparable to datacenter models like Mixtral-8x22B) while the generation speed remains interactive. For privacy-sensitive tasks—summarizing messages, drafting emails, extracting information from personal documents—this eliminates the need to send sensitive data to cloud servers.

**Multi-application memory sharing without killing the inference process.** The memory capacity sweep in Figure 10 shows that PowerInfer-2 degrades gracefully from 11.68 tokens/s at 19GB to 2.13 tokens/s at 7GB. This enables a deployment model where the inference engine does not require dedicated memory. When the user launches a memory-intensive app (camera, game), the OS can reclaim DRAM from the neuron cache, and inference speed degrades along the known curve rather than the process being killed by the OOM killer. When memory is freed, speed recovers. The practical benefit: an always-on inference capability that coexists with normal smartphone usage patterns, rather than requiring the user to close apps before using AI features. The specific numbers: reclaiming 4GB from 19GB would drop speed from 11.68 to approximately 7 tokens/s (reading from Figure 10's roughly linear curve), which is still usable for non-real-time tasks like summarization or translation. This is fundamentally different from llama.cpp's mmap-based offloading, which cannot dynamically resize its memory footprint without reloading model files.

**Batch inference for on-device data processing pipelines.** Smartphones increasingly run on-device processing over user data—indexing photos, categorizing notifications, extracting entities from messages. These are batch workloads where latency per item is less important than total throughput. PowerInfer-2's Best-of-N dynamic adaptation (Figure 13) shows that throughput scales efficiently with batch size: at batch size 4, total throughput is ~46 tokens/s versus ~25 tokens/s for QNN (1.84×) and ~36 tokens/s for CPU-only (1.28×). A data-processing pipeline that needs to classify, summarize, or extract information from thousands of notifications overnight could use batch sizes of 4–8 to maximize throughput, with PowerInfer-2 automatically adjusting as batches complete. The practical benefit: overnight processing that would take 6 hours with llama.cpp could complete in approximately 15 minutes with PowerInfer-2 (24.6× speedup), making on-device data processing feasible within the charging window.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper does not position PowerInfer-2 against a clear alternative in a "prefer X when Y" tradeoff framework. It presents a system for smartphone LLM inference where the only viable alternatives (llama.cpp, QNN, MLC-LLM) are shown to be either too slow (llama.cpp: 0.2–0.5 tokens/s under offloading), too inaccurate (QNN: 22.3-point average accuracy drop on Qwen2-7B), or incapable of running the target models at all (MLC-LLM: execution failure under offloading). There is no tradeoff where a practitioner would choose llama.cpp over PowerInfer-2 for memory-constrained smartphone inference given the 14–27× speed difference at equal model quality. The decision rule is not "prefer A when X, prefer B when Y" but rather: if you need to serve LLMs on a smartphone, use PowerInfer-2, because the alternatives do not work at acceptable speed or accuracy under the memory constraint. The one exception—where PowerInfer-2 is not applicable—is models without any activation sparsity (dense SiLU/GeLU models with no sparse fine-tuning), where the speedup drops to 2.4× (Table 6), and even then, PowerInfer-2 outperforms the alternatives; the question is whether 2.4× justifies the engineering complexity of adopting a new framework.
