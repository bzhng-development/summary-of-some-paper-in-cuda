# Offline Regularised Reinforcement Learning for Large Language Models Alignment

**ArXiv:** [2405.19107](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.19107)

## 🎯 Pitch

This paper introduces **Direct Reward Optimisation (DRO)**, a framework for aligning large language models using single-trajectory data—triplets of a prompt, a single completion, and a scalar reward (e.g., a user thumbs-up/down)—rather than the pairwise preference data required by standard RLHF and DPO.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **Direct Reward Optimisation (DRO)**, a framework for aligning large language models using single-trajectory data—triplets of a prompt, a single completion, and a scalar reward (e.g., a user thumbs-up/down)—rather than the pairwise preference data required by standard RLHF and DPO. The framework learns both a policy and a value function jointly through a simple mean-squared objective that enforces the optimality condition of KL-regularised policy optimisation, yielding a practical instantiation called **DRO-V** (Direct Reward Optimisation with Value). On the UltraFeedback dataset using T5 encoder-decoder models (770M and 3B parameters), DRO-V significantly outperforms the single-trajectory baseline Kahneman-Tversky Optimization (KTO), achieving a 63.4% side-by-side winrate against KTO with T5-L and 57.5% with T5-XL, while using an intuitive default regularisation parameter (τ = 1.0) that required no hyperparameter sweep. The framework is theoretically principled—proving that the optimal policy and value function form the unique global minimiser of the loss without requiring assumptions about constant partition functions—establishing that single-trajectory alignment can be both simpler to deploy and more performant than preference-based alternatives when access to abundant user feedback data is the primary constraint.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Pairwise Preference Data Is a Bottleneck

The dominant paradigm for aligning large language models with human intent—whether through Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) or Direct Preference Optimisation (DPO)—structures the problem around **pairwise comparisons**. The canonical data format is a quadruplet: `(prompt, response_A, response_B, preference_label)`, where human raters indicate which of two independently generated completions they prefer for a given prompt. Both RLHF (Christiano et al., 2017; Ziegler et al., 2020) and DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023) fundamentally depend on this paired structure. In RLHF, the pairwise data trains a reward model under the Bradley-Terry preference model, which then guides policy optimisation via reinforcement learning. In DPO, the pairwise contrast is embedded directly into a supervised loss that bypasses explicit reward modeling.

This paper identifies a fundamental limitation with this approach that becomes increasingly acute as models improve: **pairwise preference data is expensive, unnatural, and may become self-defeating to collect**. The argument unfolds along several dimensions (Section 1, Section 2):

**Cost and scalability.** Human raters must read two full completions per prompt and produce a comparative judgment. This is inherently more expensive per data point than providing a single scalar evaluation—a thumbs-up/down, a star rating, or a binary "was this helpful?" signal. The paper notes that such single-trajectory feedback can be produced "by users at a much larger scale" since it mirrors natural user interactions (liking a response, marking it as helpful, providing a satisfaction rating). The cost asymmetry means that, for a fixed annotation budget, single-trajectory datasets can be orders of magnitude larger than pairwise preference datasets. In an era where scaling laws govern performance, this data volume advantage could translate directly into better alignment.

**Natural occurrence in the wild.** Perhaps more importantly, the paper argues that preference data "does not occur naturally in the wild" (Section 2). User logs from deployed systems—chatbot interactions, search assistants, code completion tools—capture single interactions: a user asks a question, the model responds, and the user provides implicit or explicit feedback (click-through, thumbs-up, follow-up correction). These logs constitute a vast, continuously growing source of alignment signal that the pairwise-preference framework cannot directly consume. Converting single-trajectory feedback into pairwise data would require retrospectively asking raters to compare completions that were never generated as pairs, introducing selection bias and annotation artifacts.

**The self-defeating nature of pairwise annotation.** As LLMs improve, the task of distinguishing between two strong model responses becomes increasingly difficult. The paper references arguments from Saunders et al. (2022) and Bowman et al. (2022) to highlight this problem: when both completions are largely correct, fluent, and helpful, human raters struggle to make meaningful discriminations. This increases annotation noise, slows down data collection, and may require "additional efforts in improving both the quality and scale of the collected human feedback" simply to maintain the same signal-to-noise ratio. Single-trajectory evaluation—"was this response satisfactory?"—remains cognitively tractable even for high-quality outputs, since it asks for an absolute judgment rather than a relative one.

### The Gap: No Principled Offline Method for Single-Trajectory Alignment

Despite the practical advantages of single-trajectory data, the paper observes that **few offline alignment methods are designed for this setting**. The RLHF literature has clustered around two camps:

- **Online methods** (classical RLHF with PPO): These require sampling new completions from the current policy during training, which is costly, technically complex, and introduces distribution shift challenges. The paper explicitly notes that while online methods like Calandriello et al. (2024) and Guo et al. (2024) could theoretically use importance sampling to handle single-trajectory data, this "would bring its own set of challenges, e.g., high variance in the importance sampling ratios" (Section 2). The authors therefore deliberately restrict themselves to the offline setting for its "simplicity and computational efficiency."

- **Offline pairwise methods** (DPO, IPO, SLiC): These dominate the offline alignment landscape but fundamentally require the paired structure. The paper provides a unified view of these algorithms through a common loss function (Section 2):

$$L(\theta) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n f\left(\beta \cdot \left(\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y^w_i|x_i)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y^w_i|x_i)} - \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y^l_i|x_i)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y^l_i|x_i)}\right)\right)$$

where different choices of the scalar function $f$ produce different algorithms: $f(z) = \log(1 + \exp(-z))$ yields DPO, $f(z) = \max(0, 1-z)$ yields SLiC, and $f(z) = (z-1)^2$ yields IPO (as discussed in Tang et al., 2024). The critical feature shared by all these losses is that they operate on the **difference** of log-ratios between the preferred and dis-preferred completions. This difference structure is what causes the partition function (the intractable normalisation constant of the optimal policy) to cancel out—a mathematical convenience that makes the loss tractable but simultaneously **tethers these methods to pairwise data**. Without a second completion to form a contrast, the cancellation that makes DPO/IPO/SLiC work simply does not occur.

### Where KTO Falls Short as the Only Existing Baseline

The paper identifies **Kahneman-Tversky Optimization** (KTO; Ethayarajh et al., 2024) as the sole existing offline alignment method designed for the single-trajectory setting. KTO derives its objective from prospect theory (Kahneman and Tversky, 1979), which models human decision-making as risk-averse and reference-dependent. The paper provides a specific critique of KTO's theoretical foundations (Section 2, Appendix D):

**KTO assumes a constant partition function per batch.** The KL-regularised optimal policy has the form:

$$\pi^*(y|x) = \pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\frac{e^{\frac{1}{\tau}r(x,y)}}{e^{\frac{1}{\tau}V^*(x)}}$$

where $V^*(x) = \tau \log \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot|x)}[e^{\frac{1}{\tau}r(x,y)}]$ is a prompt-dependent normalisation constant (the log-partition function). In pairwise methods like DPO, this term cancels when taking the reward difference between two completions of the same prompt. In the single-trajectory setting, there is no such cancellation. KTO's approach is to **assume this partition function is constant across prompts within a batch**, which the paper argues is a "strong simplifying assumption" that "biases the method to produce suboptimal policies" (Section 2).

To understand why this assumption matters: $V^*(x)$ represents the expected value of the optimal policy's log-ratio for prompt $x$, and it varies substantially across prompts. A prompt that the model handles well (where most completions are good) has a different $V^*$ than one the model struggles with (where most completions are poor). Assuming $V^*$ is constant in a batch is equivalent to assuming all prompts are equally difficult—an assumption that is violated in practice and that the paper's own DRO-V method explicitly avoids by learning $V(x)$ as a function of the prompt.

**The paper positions DRO as making no such simplifying assumptions.** Unlike KTO, DRO "does not assume any functional form for $V$" (Section 3.1). Instead, it learns $V(x)$ explicitly through a separate value function network parameterised by $\phi$, allowing it to capture the prompt-dependent structure of the partition function. This is the central theoretical distinction the paper draws: DRO is "theoretically principled since it learns both a policy and a value," while KTO's constant-partition-function assumption is an approximation that can introduce bias.

### The Broader Significance: Enabling Scale in Alignment Data

Beyond the specific technical gap, the paper situates its contribution within a broader narrative about the role of supervision signal in deep learning. The introduction draws an explicit analogy (Section 1):

> "Leveraging single trajectory data promises to unlock the benefits associated with scale in deep learning. Analogous algorithmic advances motivated by the need to decrease supervision signals have often resulted in important step-changes in the empirical capacities of deep networks, e.g. through self-supervised systems."

The reference to self-supervised learning (BERT, SimCLR, BYOL, CLIP) is deliberate. Those methods transformed computer vision and NLP by removing the bottleneck of human-labeled data, enabling models to leverage vastly larger datasets. The paper argues that single-trajectory alignment could play an analogous role for RLHF: by removing the requirement for pairwise comparisons, alignment methods can tap into the enormous stream of naturally occurring user feedback (thumbs-up/down, satisfaction ratings, implicit engagement signals) that is currently inaccessible to preference-based algorithms.

This framing answers the question of **why this problem matters now**. The field has successfully developed alignment methods (RLHF, DPO) that work well given curated pairwise data. The next bottleneck is not algorithmic sophistication—it is data scale and cost. As LLMs are deployed more broadly and user interactions accumulate, the asymmetry between the volume of single-trajectory feedback generated and the capacity to collect pairwise preferences will only grow. A method that can directly consume single-trajectory data is therefore positioned not as a minor variant of existing approaches but as a **necessary next step** for alignment to scale with model deployment.

### How the Paper Positions Itself Relative to Existing Work

The paper establishes its position along several dimensions:

**1. Offline rather than online.** Unlike methods that require online sampling from the current policy during training (classical RLHF with PPO, online variants such as Calandriello et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2024), DRO is designed as a purely offline method. The dataset of `(prompt, completion, reward)` triplets remains static during optimisation. This choice is motivated by practical considerations: online sampling is "costly and technically challenging" (Section 1), and offline methods provide "simplicity and computational efficiency" (Section 2). The paper notes that the distinction between online and offline is deeper than an implementation detail—"they can give rise to different gradients and correspond to very different theoretical justifications" (Section 3.3), as the expectation over the sampling distribution differs between on-policy and off-policy gradients.

**2. Reward-model-free but value-function-requiring.** DRO joins DPO and its variants in not requiring the training of a separate reward model as an intermediate step. However, unlike DPO (which eliminates all auxiliary models beyond the policy), DRO introduces a **value function** $V_\phi$ that must be learned alongside the policy. This is not an optional component—the paper provides theoretical justification (Theorem 1) that the joint optimisation of $(\pi, V)$ recovers the unique optimal policy, and practical justification (Appendix B) that a policy-only variant (DRO-P) underperforms due to the difficulty of estimating the variance term from single-trajectory data. The value function serves as the learned partition function that KTO assumes constant, making it the mechanism by which DRO avoids KTO's simplifying bias.

**3. A framework, not a single algorithm.** The paper presents DRO as a "generic framework" that "encompass[es] a plurality of algorithms" (Section 3.1). The core idea—enforcing the optimality condition $r(x,y) - V(x) = \tau \log \frac{\pi(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}$ through a quadratic loss—can be instantiated in multiple ways. DRO-V (with explicit value function) is the primary instantiation; DRO-P (policy-only, estimating variance across the batch) is presented in Appendix B as a simpler but less performant alternative. This framing suggests the paper sees its contribution as conceptual—providing a mathematical foundation for single-trajectory alignment—with the specific algorithms as first practical realisations.

**4. Empirically validated but at modest scale.** The paper explicitly acknowledges the scope of its empirical validation: "our empirical study is limited, both in terms of number of tasks and scale" (Section 6). Experiments use T5 encoder-decoder models (770M and 3B parameters) on the UltraFeedback dataset, evaluated via automated side-by-side comparison using PaLM2 as a judge. This is substantially smaller than the scale at which DPO and RLHF are typically deployed (7B-70B parameter models). The paper positions these results as a proof of concept rather than a production claim, noting that "further work is required to more broadly establish the performance gains that our approach provides when considering the largest language models."

**5. Theoretical grounding in the canonical RLHF formulation.** Unlike KTO, which derives from prospect theory and utility maximisation under human risk aversion, DRO derives directly from the standard KL-regularised policy optimisation objective (Equation 1):

$$\pi^*(x) = \arg\max_\pi \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\rho, y\sim\pi(\cdot|x)}[r(x,y) - \tau \cdot \text{KL}(\pi(\cdot|x) || \pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot|x))]$$

This is the same objective that underpins classical RLHF (Christiano et al., 2017). DRO's contribution is not a new objective but rather a **new way to optimise the same objective using single-trajectory data**. By rearranging the optimality condition of this objective—which necessarily implies $r(x,y) - V^*(x) = \tau \log \frac{\pi^*(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}$—into a quadratic regression loss, DRO provides a direct, supervised pathway to the optimal policy without requiring pairwise contrasts or online sampling. The theoretical analysis (Theorem 1, Proposition 1) establishes that this regression approach is sound: the loss has a unique global minimum at $(\pi^*, V^*)$, and approximation errors in $V$ propagate to policy error in a bounded, characterised way.

### Summary of the Motivation Landscape

The paper addresses a clear chain of problems:

1. **Practical bottleneck**: Pairwise preference data is expensive to collect and does not naturally occur in user interactions, while single-trajectory feedback (thumbs-up/down) is abundant and cheap.

2. **Algorithmic gap**: Existing offline alignment methods (DPO, IPO, SLiC) require pairwise data due to their reliance on reward-difference cancellation. The one existing single-trajectory method (KTO) makes a theoretically limiting assumption (constant partition function per batch) that biases its solutions.

3. **Theoretical opportunity**: The canonical KL-regularised RLHF objective has an optimality condition that can be rearranged into a regression problem involving both a policy and a value function—no pairwise contrast or partition function cancellation is mathematically necessary if one is willing to learn the partition function explicitly.

4. **Scaling imperative**: As LLM deployment scales to billions of user interactions, the volume of single-trajectory feedback will vastly outstrip what can be converted to pairwise preferences. Methods that can directly consume this feedback are necessary for alignment to keep pace with deployment scale.

DRO positions itself as the theoretically principled solution to this chain of problems: an offline, reward-model-free method that learns both policy and value from single-trajectory data, makes no simplifying assumptions about the partition function, and achieves strong empirical performance against the only comparable baseline.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

DRO is an **offline alignment framework** that takes a static dataset of single-trajectory feedback—each entry is a prompt, one model completion, and one scalar reward (like a thumbs-up/down)—and produces a fine-tuned language model policy that maximises expected reward while staying close to a reference policy. The core problem it solves is that existing alignment methods like DPO require *pairs* of completions with a preference label, which are expensive and unnatural to collect; DRO instead works with the abundant single-trajectory data that users naturally generate, learning both a policy and a per-prompt value function that together approximate the theoretically optimal KL-regularised policy without any pairwise contrast or partition function cancellation assumptions.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The system has three major components operating on a fixed offline dataset:

1. **Reference Policy (`$\pi_{\text{ref}}$`)** — the initial language model (T5 encoder-decoder, fine-tuned via supervised instruction-tuning using the FLAN recipe). This serves as the anchor from which the optimised policy should not deviate too far, enforcing stability through KL-regularisation. It is frozen during DRO training.

2. **Policy Network (`$\pi_\theta$`)** — a parameterised policy (also a T5 model, initialised from `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$`) that takes a prompt `$x$` and produces a distribution over completions `$y$`. Trained to maximise the reward-minus-baseline signal from the value function while staying close to `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$` via an `$\ell_2$`-regularisation term in log-ratio space.

3. **Value Network (`$V_\phi$`)** — a separate neural network that takes a prompt `$x$` and predicts a scalar value `$V(x)$` representing the expected reward-plus-regularisation-term for that prompt under the current policy. This is DRO's explicit model of the partition function that pairwise methods cancel out and that KTO assumes constant.

Information flows as follows: a batch of `$(x_i, y_i, r_i)$` triplets is sampled from the static dataset → the policy network computes `$\log \pi_\theta(y_i|x_i)$` and the reference policy provides `$\log \pi_{\text{ref}}(y_i|x_i)$` → the value network predicts `$V_\phi(x_i)$` for each prompt → a mean-squared regression loss enforces the optimality condition `$r(x,y) - V(x) = \tau \log \frac{\pi(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}$` by squaring the difference → gradients update both `$\theta$` (policy) and `$\phi$` (value) simultaneously → the process repeats for `$K$` steps with no online sampling.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the canonical KL-regularised RLHF objective and its closed-form solution (Equations 1–2), because this is the mathematical foundation that DRO is trying to satisfy—understanding the optimal policy's relationship to the reward and the partition function is essential for seeing why DRO's loss works.
- **Second**, the DRO loss function itself (Equation 4) and Theorem 1 establishing that `$(\pi^*, V^*)$` is the unique global minimiser—this is the paper's core theoretical claim and explains *why* jointly regressing a policy and value function against the optimality condition is sound.
- **Third**, the approximation error analysis (Proposition 1) characterising what happens when the value function is imperfectly learned—this addresses the practical reality that `$V$` will never be exact and shows the error is bounded by a weighted Bellman-like residual.
- **Fourth**, the practical DRO-V algorithm (Algorithm 1) with its two gradient update rules (Equations 7–8), including the critical distinction between on-policy and off-policy gradients and why the `$\ell_2$`-regularisation in log-ratio space differs from standard KL-regularisation.
- **Fifth**, the policy-only variant DRO-P (Appendix B, Equation 15) as a contrastive ablation—understanding why it underperforms illuminates why the value function is necessary rather than optional.
- **Finally**, all architectural and hyperparameter design choices (separate networks, per-token values, learning rate rescaling, `$\tau$` selection) since these are the concrete implementation decisions that make the theoretical framework work empirically.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **methodological paper** whose core idea is that single-trajectory alignment can be achieved by converting the optimality condition of KL-regularised RLHF into a mean-squared regression problem over both policy and value function parameters, eliminating the need for pairwise contrasts without making restrictive assumptions about the partition function.

#### The Canonical KL-Regularised RLHF Objective and Its Closed-Form Solution

DRO's starting point is the standard formulation of RLHF as KL-regularised policy optimisation (Equation 1):

$$\pi^*(x) = \arg\max_\pi \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\rho, y\sim\pi(\cdot|x)}[r(x, y) - \tau \cdot \text{KL}(\pi(\cdot|x) || \pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot|x))]$$

where `$\rho$` is the distribution over prompts, `$r(x,y)$` is a scalar reward function evaluating how good completion `$y$` is for prompt `$x$`, `$\tau > 0$` is a temperature parameter controlling the strength of regularisation, `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$` is the reference policy (the initial SFT model), and `$\text{KL}(\pi(\cdot|x) || \pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot|x)) = \sum_y \pi(y|x) \log \frac{\pi(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}$` is the Kullback-Leibler divergence measuring how far the optimised policy diverges from the reference on prompt `$x$`.

**What it computes:** This objective defines the *target* policy that RLHF aims to produce. For each prompt `$x$`, the optimised policy `$\pi^*$` should generate completions `$y$` that achieve high expected reward `$r(x,y)$` while remaining close to `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$` in KL-divergence (scaled by `$\tau$`). When `$\tau$` is large, staying close to the reference dominates; when `$\tau$` is small, maximising reward dominates. The outer expectation `$\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\rho}$` means we care about average performance across the prompt distribution, not any single prompt.

**Why this form:** This is the canonical formulation introduced by Christiano et al. (2017) and used throughout the RLHF literature. The KL penalty serves a dual purpose: it prevents the policy from collapsing to a deterministic reward-maximising output (which would overfit the reward model and lose diversity), and it keeps the policy within the distribution where the reward model's predictions are reliable (mitigating reward hacking). The scalar `$\tau$` provides a tunable knob for the exploration-exploitation tradeoff at the policy level.

This optimisation problem has a well-known closed-form solution (Equation 2), derived by solving the variational problem with the constraint that `$\pi(\cdot|x)$` is a probability distribution:

$$\pi^*(y|x) = \pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\frac{e^{\frac{1}{\tau}r(x,y)}}{e^{\frac{1}{\tau}V^*(x)}}$$

where the **partition function** (or value function) `$V^*(x)$` is defined as:

$$V^*(x) = \tau \log \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot|x)}\left[e^{\frac{1}{\tau}r(x,y)}\right]$$

**What `$V^*(x)$` computes:** For a given prompt `$x$`, `$V^*(x)$` is the log-sum-exp of the reward-temperature-scaled distribution over completions under the *reference* policy. It is the normalisation constant that ensures `$\sum_y \pi^*(y|x) = 1$`. Operationally, it captures how much total "value" is available for prompt `$x$`—if the reference policy can produce many high-reward completions, `$V^*(x)$` will be large; if all completions have low reward, `$V^*(x)$` will be small. The paper notes that this form is "intuitive, if we consider the Legendre-Fenchel conjugate of the KL regulariser in Equation (1)" (Section 3.1), referencing the convex analysis connection where the log-sum-exp emerges as the Fenchel dual of the negative entropy regulariser.

**Why this form matters for understanding DRO:** The closed-form solution reveals a fundamental structural property: the optimal policy's log-ratio `$\log \frac{\pi^*(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}$` equals `$\frac{1}{\tau}(r(x,y) - V^*(x))$`. Rearranging gives the **optimality condition** that DRO is built on:

$$r(x,y) - V^*(x) = \tau \log \frac{\pi^*(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}$$

This equation says: the reward for a completion, minus the prompt's baseline value, is proportional (with scale `$\tau$`) to how much more likely the optimal policy makes that completion relative to the reference. A completion that the optimal policy strongly up-weights (large positive log-ratio) must have reward substantially above the prompt's average value; a completion the optimal policy down-weights (large negative log-ratio) must have reward below the prompt's average. In pairwise methods like DPO, taking the difference of this equation for two completions `$y_w$` and `$y_l$` cancels `$V^*(x)$`, yielding `$r(x,y_w) - r(x,y_l) = \tau(\log\frac{\pi^*(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)} - \log\frac{\pi^*(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)})$`—the partition function disappears. DRO's key move is to **keep the equation as-is** and treat it as a regression target, which requires learning `$V$` but eliminates the need for pairs.

The paper explicitly contrasts this with KTO's approach (Appendix D): KTO "assumes a constant partition function `$Z$` for each prompt of the batch," effectively replacing the prompt-dependent `$V^*(x)$` with a single scalar per batch. DRO makes no such assumption.

#### The DRO Loss Function: Mean-Squared Regression to the Optimality Condition

Given the optimality condition `$r(x,y) - V(x) = \tau \log \frac{\pi(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}$` that must hold at the optimum `$(\pi^*, V^*)$`, DRO defines a loss that measures how far any candidate `$(\pi, V)$` is from satisfying it (Equation 4):

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{DRO}}(\pi, V) = \frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\rho, y\sim\mu(\cdot|x)}\left[\left(r(x,y) - V(x) - \tau \log \frac{\pi(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}\right)^2\right]$$

where `$\mu$` is the **behaviour policy**—the policy that generated the offline data completions `$y$`. The expectation is over prompts from the data distribution `$\rho$` and completions from the logging policy `$\mu(\cdot|x)$`.

**What it computes:** For each `$(x, y, r(x,y))$` triplet in the dataset, the loss computes a scalar residual: the difference between the left-hand side `$r(x,y) - V(x)$` (reward minus estimated prompt value) and the right-hand side `$\tau \log \frac{\pi(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}$` (scaled log-ratio of optimised to reference policy probabilities for that completion). This residual is squared and averaged. The factor `$\frac{1}{2}$` is a conventional scaling. The loss is non-negative and equals zero exactly when the optimality condition holds for all `$(x,y)$` pairs in the support.

**Why this form:** The key properties of this quadratic loss are:
1. **It is a regression objective, not a contrastive one.** There is no pairwise comparison, no difference of log-ratios, no Bradley-Terry model. The model simply tries to make the two sides of the optimality equation match on each individual data point.
2. **It is unbiased for the optimal policy.** Theorem 1 (proved immediately below) shows that `$(\pi^*, V^*)$` is the unique global minimiser when the data distribution `$\mu$` covers the support of `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$`. This means that despite being a simple regression, minimising this loss recovers the theoretically optimal KL-regularised policy.
3. **It implicitly handles the partition function.** The value function `$V(x)$` is a learned parameter that absorbs the log-sum-exp normalisation. Because `$V$` is a function of `$x$` (not a constant), it can vary per prompt, capturing the fact that different prompts have different baseline values.
4. **It separates the policy and value optimisation.** As the paper notes after Theorem 1, "we can optimise `$\pi$` and `$V$` independently and end up finding the optimum." There is no need to enforce the constraint `$V(x) = \tau \log \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\text{ref}}}[e^{r(x,y)/\tau}]$` during optimisation—the loss landscape naturally drives both to the correct relationship.

The choice of expectation over `$\mu$` (the behaviour policy) rather than `$\pi$` (the current policy) is critical for the offline setting: it means the loss can be estimated from a fixed dataset without requiring online samples from the evolving policy. However, this off-policy sampling has consequences for the gradient structure, as discussed in detail below.

---

#### Theorem 1: Existence, Uniqueness, and Global Optimality

The paper states Theorem 1 (Section 3.1):

> **Theorem 1.** `$(\pi^*, V^*)$` is a global optimum of the loss `$\mathcal{L}(\pi, V)$`. In addition, assuming the supports of `$\mu$` and `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$` coincide, (i.e., for all `$x \in \text{supp } \rho$`, `$\text{supp}(\mu(\cdot|x)) = \text{supp}(\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot|x))$`), then `$(\pi^*, V^*)$` is the unique global optimum of the loss `$\mathcal{L}(\pi, V)$`.

The proof has two parts, each illuminating a different aspect of the framework:

**Part 1: `$(\pi^*, V^*)$` achieves zero loss.** By definition of `$\pi^*$` and `$V^*$`, the optimality condition `$r(x,y) - V^*(x) = \tau \log \frac{\pi^*(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}$` holds exactly. Plugging this into `$\mathcal{L}_{\text{DRO}}$` gives `$\mathcal{L}_{\text{DRO}}(\pi^*, V^*) = \frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}[(0)^2] = 0$`. Since the loss is a sum of squares (non-negative), zero is the minimum possible value. Therefore `$(\pi^*, V^*)$` is a global optimum.

**Part 2: Uniqueness under support coverage.** Assume there exists another pair `$(\tilde{\pi}, \tilde{V})$` also achieving zero loss. For zero loss, every term in the expectation must be zero individually (since each is a square), meaning for all `$x$` in the prompt support and all `$y$` in the behaviour policy's support:

$$r(x,y) - \tilde{V}(x) - \tau \log \frac{\tilde{\pi}(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)} = 0$$

If the support condition holds—`$\mu(\cdot|x)$` assigns positive probability to every completion that `$\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot|x)$` could generate—then this equation holds for all possible `$y$` that the reference policy could produce. Rearranging: `$\tilde{\pi}(y|x) = \pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x) e^{(r(x,y) - \tilde{V}(x))/\tau}$`. Since `$\tilde{\pi}(\cdot|x)$` must sum to 1, the normalisation condition forces `$\tilde{V}(x) = \tau \log \sum_y \pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x) e^{r(x,y)/\tau} = V^*(x)$`, which in turn forces `$\tilde{\pi}(y|x) = \pi^*(y|x)$`. Thus `$(\tilde{\pi}, \tilde{V})$` must equal `$(\pi^*, V^*)$`.

**What this means operationally:** The support coverage condition is the key practical requirement. The offline data must contain a diverse enough set of completions from the behaviour policy `$\mu$` that every completion the reference policy could possibly generate is represented (or at least, the model can generalise across the support). If the offline data has "holes"—completions that `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$` could produce but that `$\mu$` never did—then there could be degenerate solutions that achieve zero loss on the observed data but differ from `$(\pi^*, V^*)$` on the unobserved regions. This is a standard coverage assumption in offline RL (Levine et al., 2020), and the paper acknowledges it implicitly by requiring support coincidence.

**Why this theorem matters beyond correctness:** The significance the paper emphasises is that "there is no need to account for [the connection between `$\pi^*$` and `$V^*$` via the log-sum-exp] during optimisation. We can optimise `$\pi$` and `$V$` independently and end up finding the optimum" (Section 3.1, Remarks). This decoupling is what makes DRO practical—rather than enforcing `$V(x) = \tau \log \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\text{ref}}}[e^{r(x,y)/\tau}]$` as a constraint (which would require estimating an expectation over the reference policy's full output space), the loss landscape naturally drives `$V$` toward `$V^*$` as `$\pi$` moves toward `$\pi^*$`, and vice versa, without the two needing to be explicitly coordinated.

---

#### Proposition 1: Approximation Error When the Value Function Is Imperfect

In practice, the value function `$V_\phi$` will never be learned perfectly—it is a finite-capacity neural network trained with finite data and stochastic optimisation. Proposition 1 (Section 3.2) characterises how errors in `$V$` affect the recovered policy when `$\pi$` is optimised against a fixed, imperfect `$V$`.

The setup: consider a fixed policy `$\pi$`. Let `$V^\pi = \arg\min_V \mathcal{L}(\pi, V)$` be the value function that minimises the DRO loss for that specific `$\pi$`. Taking the derivative of `$\mathcal{L}_{\text{DRO}}(\pi, V)$` with respect to `$V(x)$` and setting to zero gives the closed form:

$$V^\pi(x) = \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\mu(\cdot|x)}\left[r(x,y) - \tau \log \frac{\pi(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}\right]$$

`$V^\pi(x)$` is the expected value of the optimality residual under the behaviour policy for prompt `$x$`. Now suppose we have an *approximate* value function `$V(x)$` that differs from `$V^\pi(x)$`. If we optimise `$\pi$` against this approximate `$V$` (treating `$V$` as fixed), we obtain some suboptimal policy `$\pi_V = \arg\min_\pi \mathcal{L}(\pi, V)$`. Proposition 1 characterises `$\pi_V$`.

The first result (Equation 5) is an implicit equation for `$\pi_V$`:

$$\pi_V(y|x) \propto \pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x) e^{\frac{1}{\tau}\left[r(x,y) - \frac{\pi_V(y|x)}{\mu(y|x)}(V^{\pi_V}(x) - V(x))\right]}$$

**What this says:** The recovered policy `$\pi_V$` is not the optimal `$\pi^*$` but a *distorted* version where the effective reward is `$r(x,y) - \frac{\pi_V(y|x)}{\mu(y|x)}(V^{\pi_V}(x) - V(x))$`. The distortion term depends on two factors: (1) the Bellman-like residual `$V^{\pi_V}(x) - V(x)$` measuring how far the approximate value function is from the true value function of `$\pi_V$`, and (2) the importance-sampling ratio `$\frac{\pi_V(y|x)}{\mu(y|x)}$` measuring how much more (or less) likely `$\pi_V$` is to generate completion `$y$` compared to the behaviour policy. When `$V = V^{\pi_V}$` (perfect value function), the distortion vanishes and `$\pi_V = \pi^*$`. When the residual is non-zero, completions where `$\pi_V$` differs substantially from `$\mu$` get amplified errors.

The second result (Equation 6) bounds the pointwise log-ratio error:

$$\left|\log \frac{\pi_V(y|x)}{\pi^*(y|x)}\right| \leq \frac{2}{\tau} \max_y \left|(V^{\pi_V}(x) - V(x))\left(1 - \frac{\pi_V(y|x)}{\mu(y|x)}\right)\right|$$

**What this computes:** The maximum absolute log-ratio error between the approximate policy `$\pi_V$` and the optimal `$\pi^*$` for prompt `$x$` is bounded by `$\frac{2}{\tau}$` times the maximum over completions of the absolute Bellman residual `$|V^{\pi_V} - V|$` weighted by the off-policy correction factor `$|1 - \pi_V/\mu|$`. This is a **error propagation bound**: it says the policy error scales linearly with the value function error, with the temperature `$\tau$` in the denominator (weaker regularisation → larger policy error from the same value error) and the distribution mismatch `$|1 - \pi_V/\mu|$` as an amplifier.

**Why this matters in practice:** The bound reveals two regimes where DRO is robust:
- **If `$\mu \approx \pi_V$`**, the weight `$|1 - \pi_V/\mu| \approx 0$` and the error is dampened even if `$V$` is imperfect. This is the on-policy regime.
- **If `$V^{\pi_V}(x)$` is approximately constant across prompts** (the special case `$V^{\pi_V}(x) \approx c$` for all `$x$`), then even a constant approximate `$V$` yields zero residual and recovers the optimal policy `$\pi^*$`. The paper notes that in this case, one "would recover the optimal policy `$\pi^*$`" using a state-independent baseline that could be estimated with a large batch. This provides a theoretical justification for why KTO's constant-partition-function assumption works in the special case where the true `$V^*$` happens to be flat across prompts—but DRO can handle the general case where it is not.

The bound also provides a **validation diagnostic**: if the learned value function `$V_\phi$` produces small Bellman residuals `$V^{\pi_V} - V$` across prompts, then the policy error is guaranteed to be small. The DRO loss itself is exactly the squared residual integrated over `$\mu$`, so training loss directly monitors this quantity.

#### The Empirical DRO-V Loss and Its Two Gradient Updates

In practice, the expectations in Equation 4 are estimated from a finite batch of `$n$` triplets `$(x_i, y_i, r_i)$` where `$x_i \sim \rho$` (the prompt distribution), `$y_i \sim \mu(\cdot|x_i)$` (the behaviour policy's completions), and `$r_i = r(x_i, y_i)$` (the scalar reward). The empirical loss is (Section 3.3):

$$\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta, \phi) = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^n \left(r(x_i, y_i) - V_\phi(x_i) - \tau \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x_i)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_i|x_i)}\right)^2$$

This loss is jointly minimised over policy parameters `$\theta$` and value parameters `$\phi$` using gradient descent. The gradients decompose into a value update and a policy update.

---

##### The Value Function Gradient

Taking the derivative with respect to `$\phi$` (Equation 7):

$$\nabla_\phi \widehat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta, \phi) = \sum_{i=1}^n \left(V_\phi(x_i) - r(x_i, y_i) + \tau \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x_i)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_i|x_i)}\right) \nabla_\phi V_\phi(x_i)$$

where the residual term in parentheses is `$V_\phi(x_i) - [r(x_i, y_i) - \tau \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x_i)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_i|x_i)}]$`—the difference between the predicted value and the "target" value implied by the current policy.

**What it computes:** For each data point, the value network is updated to make its prediction `$V_\phi(x_i)$` closer to `$r(x_i, y_i) - \tau \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x_i)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_i|x_i)}$`, which is the reward minus the scaled log-ratio. When `$\pi_\theta$` up-weights a completion (positive log-ratio), the target for `$V_\phi$` is lower than the raw reward; when `$\pi_\theta$` down-weights (negative log-ratio), the target is higher. This is effectively a **regression of the value function onto the advantage**: `$V_\phi$` learns the baseline from which the policy's adjustments are measured.

**Why this form and not just regress `$V_\phi$` onto `$r$`:** The target includes the `$\tau \log \frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\text{ref}}}$` term because the value function in the KL-regularised framework is not the expected reward but the expected reward *plus* the policy's deviation penalty. This is the "soft" value function from maximum-entropy RL (Ziebart et al., 2008; Haarnoja et al., 2017; Schulman et al., 2018). Regressing onto raw rewards would learn a different function that is not the `$V^*$` appearing in the optimality condition.

---

##### The Policy Gradient

The policy gradient (Equation 8) has two terms:

$$\nabla_\theta \widehat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta, \phi) = -\tau \sum_{i=1}^n \left[ \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_i|x_i) (r(x_i, y_i) - V_\phi(x_i)) - \frac{\tau}{2} \nabla_\theta \left(\log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x_i)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_i|x_i)}\right)^2 \right]$$

**First term: Policy optimisation loss.** `$\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_i|x_i) (r(x_i, y_i) - V_\phi(x_i))$` is structurally identical to a REINFORCE policy gradient (Sutton et al., 2000), where `$r(x_i, y_i) - V_\phi(x_i)$` is the **advantage**—reward minus learned baseline—and `$\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_i|x_i)$` is the score function that increases the log-probability of the sampled completion `$y_i$` in proportion to its advantage. If the advantage is positive (completion is better than the prompt's average), the policy becomes more likely to generate it; if negative, less likely. The value function `$V_\phi$` serves as a control variate baseline.

**Critical detail—why `$V_\phi$` cannot be replaced by any baseline:** The paper emphasises (Section 3.3, "Understanding the value function V") that because the samples `$y_i$` are drawn from the behaviour policy `$\mu$` (off-policy), not from `$\pi_\theta$` (on-policy), the baseline must satisfy a specific property. For an on-policy REINFORCE gradient, `$\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_\theta}[\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y|x) V(x)] = 0$` for any function `$V(x)$` independent of `$y$`, because `$\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_\theta}[\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y|x)] = 0$`. This means any baseline works for variance reduction. However, off-policy, `$\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\mu}[\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y|x) V(x)] \neq 0$` in general, because the expectation is over `$\mu$`, not `$\pi_\theta$`. Using an arbitrary baseline would **bias** the gradient estimate. The DRO loss's value function `$V_\phi$` is not an arbitrary variance-reduction baseline—it is the specific function that, when subtracted from the reward and combined with the `$\ell_2$`-regularisation term, produces an unbiased gradient for the off-policy DRO objective. Removing it or replacing it with a different `$V(x)$` breaks the statistical correctness of the update.

**Second term: `$\ell_2$`-regularisation loss.** `$\frac{\tau}{2} \nabla_\theta (\log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x_i)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_i|x_i)})^2$` penalises the squared log-ratio between the current and reference policies. Expanding the square: `$\nabla_\theta (\log \frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\text{ref}}})^2 = 2 \log \frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\text{ref}}} \cdot \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta$`. This means the gradient pushes `$\log \pi_\theta(y_i|x_i)$` down when the log-ratio is positive (the policy has deviated above the reference) and up when the log-ratio is negative (the policy has deviated below). The overall effect is a **quadratic penalty in log-ratio space** that keeps `$\pi_\theta$` close to `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$`.

**Critical distinction—why `$\ell_2$`-regularisation rather than KL-regularisation:** The paper is explicit (Section 3.3, "Offline regularisation") that these two regularisers produce different gradients when sampling is off-policy:

$$\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\mu(\cdot|x_i)}\left[\nabla_\theta \left(\log \frac{\pi_\theta(y|x_i)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x_i)}\right)^2\right] \neq \nabla_\theta \text{KL}(\pi_\theta(\cdot|x_i), \pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot|x_i))$$

The KL divergence gradient would be `$\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_\theta}[\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y|x) \cdot \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}]$`, which requires an expectation over the *current* policy, not the behaviour policy. In the offline setting with samples from `$\mu$`, the `$\ell_2$`-regularisation is the correct off-policy version of the KL penalty because:

1. It emerges directly from the squared DRO loss, which is the natural objective given the optimality condition.
2. It uses the same off-policy samples `$y_i \sim \mu$` without importance-weighting corrections, avoiding the high-variance importance sampling ratios that would be needed to estimate the on-policy KL gradient.
3. It penalises deviations in both directions symmetrically in log-ratio space, whereas on-policy KL penalises deviations differently depending on the direction and the current policy's probability mass.

The paper frames this as "the natural extension of a usual on-policy regularised PG algorithm to the off-policy case" (Section 3.3).

---

##### Policy Learning Rate Rescaling

The paper applies an additional practical modification: the policy gradient in Equation 8 is multiplied by a factor of `$1/\tau$` before use (Section 3.3, "Policy learning rate rescaling"). The full effective policy gradient is:

$$\nabla_\theta^{\text{effective}} = -\sum_{i=1}^n \left[ \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_i|x_i) \cdot \frac{r(x_i, y_i) - V_\phi(x_i)}{\tau} - \frac{1}{2} \nabla_\theta \left(\log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x_i)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_i|x_i)}\right)^2 \right]$$

**Why this rescaling:** The authors "hypothesised that the global loss `$\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta, \phi)$` may be ill-conditioned (as its sensitivities w.r.t. the dimensions `$\theta$` and `$\phi$` are different, leading to a high condition number)" (Section 3.3). In practice, the raw DRO loss is much more sensitive to the value function parameters than the policy parameters because the policy loss involves the `$\tau$`-scaled log-ratios. Dividing the policy gradient by `$\tau$` brings the effective learning rates for `$\theta$` and `$\phi$` into better balance, improving optimisation stability. The paper reports that this "works better empirically" without providing a formal condition-number analysis, making it a pragmatic tuning choice validated by results.

#### The DRO-V Algorithm (Algorithm 1)

The complete DRO-V procedure is:

**Algorithm 1: Direct Reward Optimisation with Value (DRO-V)**

**Inputs:** A static single-trajectory dataset of `$N$` triplets `$(x_i, y_i, r_i = r(x_i, y_i))_{i=1}^N$`, a parameterised policy `$\pi_\theta$` (initialised from `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$`), a frozen reference policy `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$`, a parameterised value function `$V_\phi$`, a regularisation scalar `$\tau$`, a total number of steps `$K$`, a batch size `$B$`, and an optimiser (AdaFactor with decay 0.8).

**For each step `$k = 1$` to `$K$`:**
1. Sample uniformly a batch of `$B$` triplets `$(x_i, y_i, r_i)_{i=1}^B$` from the dataset.
2. Compute the value function gradient:
   $$\nabla_\phi \widehat{\mathcal{L}} = \frac{1}{B}\sum_{i=1}^B \left(V_\phi(x_i) - r(x_i, y_i) + \tau \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x_i)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_i|x_i)}\right) \nabla_\phi V_\phi(x_i)$$
3. Compute the rescaled policy gradient:
   $$\nabla_\theta \widehat{\mathcal{L}} = -\frac{1}{B}\sum_{i=1}^B \left[ \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_i|x_i) \cdot \frac{r(x_i, y_i) - V_\phi(x_i)}{\tau} - \frac{1}{2} \nabla_\theta \left(\log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x_i)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_i|x_i)}\right)^2 \right]$$
4. Update `$\theta \leftarrow \text{UpdateOptimiser}(\theta, \nabla_\theta \widehat{\mathcal{L}})$`
5. Update `$\phi \leftarrow \text{UpdateOptimiser}(\phi, \nabla_\phi \widehat{\mathcal{L}})$`

**Output:** The trained policy `$\pi_\theta$`.

**Key design choices in Algorithm 1:**
- **Offline optimisation only.** The dataset remains static; no new completions are generated from the current policy during training. This is "like in offline reinforcement learning, where taking new actions in the environment is structurally prohibited" (Section 3.3).
- **No reward model training.** The scalar rewards `$r_i$` come directly from the dataset (e.g., normalised thumbs-up/down signals). DRO does not involve an intermediate step of training a reward model on preferences, unlike classical RLHF.
- **Simultaneous updates.** Both `$\theta$` and `$\phi$` are updated at each step from the same batch, using the same loss. There is no alternating optimisation or actor-critic separation of update frequencies.
- **Default hyperparameters (Section 4):** Learning rate `$1\times 10^{-4}$` for both networks, `$\tau = 1.0$` (intuitive default), AdaFactor optimiser with decay 0.8, 150 linear warmup steps, batch size 32, 40,000 steps for T5-L (770M parameters) and 20,000 steps for T5-XL (3B parameters).

---

#### Neural Network Architecture: Separate Networks and Per-Token Values

The paper explores several architectural variants (Section 3.3, "Neural network implementation," and Appendix C, Figure 6):

**Separate networks for `$\pi_\theta$` and `$V_\phi$`.** The policy and value functions use two independent T5 encoder-decoder models. The paper reports that this "was beneficial empirically, compared to using policy logits as value outputs" (Section 3.3). The parameter sharing ablation (Figure 5, Appendix C) quantifies this: using a single shared network with the value head attached to the policy representations reduces the winrate against SFT from 76.6% (double network, multiple values) to 55.5% (single network, multiple values). The authors describe this as "perhaps counter-intuitive" since parameter sharing is common in actor-critic RL (e.g., sharing the encoder between actor and critic), but the degradation suggests that the value function requires representations sufficiently different from the policy that forcing them into the same network creates interference. With two networks, each can specialise: the policy network focuses on the token-generation task (mapping prompts to completion distributions), while the value network focuses on the prompt-level regression task (mapping prompts to scalar values).

**Multiple value tokens per batch, not one.** The value network `$V_\phi$` outputs a vector of values—one per token position—rather than a single scalar per batch. The paper found that "using a single value per batch hurts performance" (Section 3.3). The ablation (Figure 5, Appendix C) shows the double-network, single-value variant achieves only 72.1% winrate against SFT compared to 76.6% for the double-network, multiple-values variant, a 4.5% absolute gap. Direct side-by-side comparison between these two variants yields 54.9% in favour of the multiple-value version. A single value per batch is equivalent to KTO's constant-partition-function assumption—it forces all prompts in the batch to share the same baseline value. Allowing per-prompt values (and indeed per-token values within prompts) enables the model to capture the prompt-dependent structure that the theoretical framework requires.

**Why per-token rather than per-prompt values would help further:** The paper uses "a value per token" to mean the value function can produce different values for different prompts within the batch, as opposed to a single scalar for the entire batch. This is aligned with the theory: `$V^*(x)$` is a function of the prompt `$x$`, so different prompts should get different values. However, the paper does not fully explain why token-level granularity (as opposed to prompt-level) is used; the architectural details of how `$V_\phi$` processes the prompt and produces its output are not fully specified beyond being a separate T5 network.

**Reward normalisation.** Before training, the scalar rewards in the dataset are "normalised such that the rewards have mean 0 and variance 1 across the dataset" (Section 4, "Models and task dataset"). This is a standard preprocessing step that improves numerical stability and makes the `$\tau$` parameter interpretable across datasets—with standardised rewards, `$\tau = 1.0$` corresponds to a natural scale where the regularisation and reward terms are balanced.

**Checkpoint selection.** The paper records a checkpoint every 2,000 training steps and selects the best checkpoint "as determined by side-by-side comparison against the SFT policy" (Section 4, "Evaluation"). This means the final model is chosen based on its winrate against the reference policy, not based on training loss. The training loss monitors convergence but is not used for early stopping or model selection—the evaluation metric is the actual comparative quality of generated completions.

---

#### The Rejected Policy-Only Variant: DRO-P (Appendix B)

The paper explores whether the value function is truly necessary by attempting to eliminate `$V$` from the optimisation. Starting from the relationship `$V^\pi = \arg\min_V \mathcal{L}(\pi, V)$`, plugging the closed-form minimiser into the loss yields a policy-only objective:

$$\mathcal{L}(\pi) = \mathcal{L}(\pi, V^\pi) = \frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\rho}\left[\text{Var}_{y\sim\mu(\cdot|x)}\left(r(x,y) - \tau \log \frac{\pi(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}\right)\right]$$

**What this computes:** For each prompt `$x$`, the loss is the variance (over completions `$y$` drawn from the behaviour policy `$\mu$`) of the residual term `$r(x,y) - \tau \log \frac{\pi(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}$`. If the optimality condition holds, this residual is constant across all `$y$` (it equals `$V^*(x)$` for all completions of prompt `$x$`), so the variance is zero. Conversely, if the policy deviates from optimal, the residual varies with `$y$` and the variance is positive.

**Why this cannot be implemented as-is:** The variance `$\text{Var}_{y\sim\mu(\cdot|x)}$` is the variance of the residual *for a single prompt `$x$` across its completions*. Estimating this requires **multiple completions per prompt**—at least two samples `$y_1, y_2 \sim \mu(\cdot|x)$` for the same `$x$`. This violates the single-trajectory setting where only one completion per prompt is available. The paper notes that with two samples per prompt, the loss would reduce (after algebraic manipulation) to:

$$\mathcal{L}(\pi) = \frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(r(x,y_2) - r(x,y_1) + \tau \log \frac{\pi(y_1|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_1|x)} - \tau \log \frac{\pi(y_2|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_2|x)}\right)^2\right]$$

This is essentially the IPO loss (Azar et al., 2023) with `$r(x,y_2) - r(x,y_1)$` serving as a continuous preference signal. But with single-trajectory data, the variance cannot be estimated per prompt.

**The DRO-P approximation (Equation 15):** To make the policy-only approach tractable with single-trajectory data, the paper approximates the per-prompt variance `$\text{Var}_{y\sim\mu(\cdot|x)}$` with the **variance across the batch** `$\text{Var}_{y\sim B}$`, where each element in the batch has a *different* prompt:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{DRO-P}}(\theta) = \frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\rho}\left[\text{Var}_{y\sim B}\left(r(x,y) - \tau \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}\right)\right]$$

**Why DRO-P underperforms DRO-V:** The paper reports that DRO-P "does not collapse and produces non-trivial resulting policies, that can in fact beat the SFT policy" but is "less competitive" than DRO-V (Appendix B). The fundamental issue is the approximation error: the batch-wise variance is a biased estimator of the per-prompt variance because it conflates within-prompt variation (which the true `$\mathcal{L}(\pi)$` measures) with across-prompt variation (which is irrelevant to the optimality condition). Different prompts have different `$V^*(x)$` values, so the residuals `$r(x,y) - \tau \log \frac{\pi}{\pi_{\text{ref}}}$` will naturally have high variance across prompts even when the policy is optimal. Minimising this across-prompt variance would drive the policy to equalise the log-ratios across prompts, which is not what the optimal policy does. DRO-V avoids this by learning `$V_\phi(x)$` explicitly and subtracting it per-prompt, isolating the within-prompt residual that the optimality condition requires to be zero.

**Why this ablation matters:** The DRO-P experiment demonstrates that the value function is not an optional add-on for variance reduction—it is **necessary for statistical correctness** in the single-trajectory setting. The batch-wise variance approximation introduces a bias that degrades performance, confirming that the joint `$(\pi, V)$` formulation is the right level of modeling for this data setting.

#### Summary of Design Choices and Their Justifications

- **Quadratic loss rather than cross-entropy or contrastive loss:** Arises directly from the optimality condition as a regression problem; the squared error is the natural metric when both sides are continuous scalar quantities (the reward residual and the scaled log-ratio). Unlike preference-based losses that require a Bradley-Terry model of choice probabilities, the quadratic loss treats the alignment problem as function approximation.
- **Joint `$(\pi, V)$` optimisation rather than policy-only:** Theorem 1 guarantees global optimality with no simplifying assumptions about `$V$`; Proposition 1 characterises the error when `$V$` is approximate. A policy-only variant (DRO-P) is possible but demonstrates worse empirical performance due to the bias from estimating per-prompt variance with batch-level statistics.
- **Separate networks for `$\pi_\theta$` and `$V_\phi$`:** Contrary to the parameter-sharing common in actor-critic RL, separate networks prevent representational interference between the sequence-generation task (policy) and the scalar-regression task (value). Empirically validated with a 21.1% absolute winrate gap against parameter sharing.
- **Per-token values rather than per-batch single value:** Allows the value function to capture prompt-dependent structure, avoiding KTO's constant-partition-function assumption. A single value per batch is equivalent to assuming all prompts have the same `$V^*$`, which degrades performance by 4.5% absolute winrate.
- **`$\ell_2$`-regularisation in log-ratio space rather than KL-regularisation:** The correct off-policy regulariser that naturally emerges from the squared DRO loss. On-policy KL would require importance sampling from `$\mu$` to `$\pi_\theta$`, introducing high variance.
- **Value function in the policy gradient, not an arbitrary baseline:** Off-policy sampling `$y_i \sim \mu$` means `$\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\mu}[\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y|x) V(x)] \neq 0$` for arbitrary `$V$`. The DRO value function is the specific function that, when combined with the `$\ell_2$` term, yields unbiased gradients.
- **Policy learning rate rescaling by `$1/\tau$`:** Compensates for the ill-conditioning of the joint loss where the value and policy parameters have different sensitivities; validated empirically but lacking formal condition-number analysis.
- **Reward normalisation to mean 0, variance 1:** Standard preprocessing for numerical stability; makes `$\tau$` interpretable across datasets as a dimensionless regularisation strength.
- **Offline, static dataset with no online sampling:** Deliberate design choice to avoid the complexity, cost, and technical challenges of online RL (generating completions from the current policy, importance sampling corrections, distribution shift). The paper "consider[s] the offline setting instead" (Section 2) for its "simplicity and computational efficiency."

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: The Partition Function as a Learnable Quantity Rather Than a Mathematical Nuisance to Eliminate

The most conceptually distinctive move in this paper is its treatment of the partition function `$V^*(x)$` — the log-sum-exp normalisation constant in the optimal KL-regularised policy — not as an obstacle to be eliminated through algebraic cancellation (as in DPO, IPO, SLiC) or assumed constant (as in KTO), but as a **first-class learnable object that carries meaningful information about prompt difficulty**. This reframes a mathematical inconvenience as a source of signal.

**What the field did before.** Every major offline alignment method prior to DRO treated the partition function as something to get rid of. DPO and its variants exploit the pairwise structure to cancel `$V^*(x)$` by differencing rewards between two completions of the same prompt — a technique so central that it is essentially the *raison d'être* for requiring paired data. KTO, the only prior single-trajectory method, acknowledges that cancellation is impossible without pairs but makes the opposite simplifying move: it assumes `$V^*(x)$` is constant across all prompts in a batch, effectively replacing a function with a scalar. Both strategies treat the partition function as a problem to be managed, not as information to be extracted.

**Why DRO's framing is distinctive.** DRO's core theoretical move is to observe that the optimality condition `$r(x,y) - V^*(x) = \tau \log \frac{\pi^*(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}$` — the equation that pairwise methods difference and KTO approximates — can be used directly as a regression target if one is willing to parameterise and learn `$V(x)$`. There is no mathematical necessity to cancel it; the cancellation was only necessary because prior methods restricted themselves to losses that could not accommodate a learned value function jointly with the policy. Theorem 1 then proves that learning `$V$` does not introduce identifiability problems, underdetermination, or degenerate solutions: the joint optimum `$(\pi^*, V^*)$` is the unique global minimiser of the quadratic loss, so gradient descent on both parameters simultaneously will recover it without any additional constraints or coordination mechanisms. The proof hinges on the fact that the normalisation condition `$\sum_y \pi(y|x) = 1$` forces the learned `$V$` toward `$V^*$` automatically when the loss is driven to zero, without needing to be explicitly enforced.

This is a fundamental reframing, not an incremental refinement. It converts the partition function from a term that must be cancelled (DPO) or approximated (KTO) into a term that is **learned and then discarded** — the value network `$V_\phi$` is trained alongside the policy and then thrown away at deployment, since only `$\pi_\theta$` is used for generation. The value network is scaffolding: it provides the correct baseline during training, absorbs the prompt-dependent normalisation, and enables single-trajectory data to be used without bias, but it has no role at inference time. This "learn-and-discard" pattern is novel in the alignment literature, where auxiliary components (reward models in RLHF, reference policies in DPO) typically persist in some form.

**Significance beyond raw performance.** The value-function-as-learned-partition-function concept opens up a diagnostic capability that pairwise methods lack. Because `$V_\phi(x)$` is an explicit estimate of the log-sum-exp of reward-weighted reference policy probabilities for prompt `$x$`, it can be inspected to understand which prompts the model (under the reference policy) finds intrinsically high-value versus low-value. This provides a **difficulty signal** that is grounded in the same RLHF objective being optimised, without requiring a separate difficulty estimation pipeline (contrast with the 2048-sample oracle difficulty estimation used in some test-time compute scaling work). The paper does not exploit this diagnostic potential — the value function is used purely as an optimisation scaffold — but the fact that it exists as a byproduct of theoretically principled training is a significant architectural property.

**Evidence anchor.** The necessity of learning `$V$` explicitly is validated by the DRO-P ablation (Appendix B), where replacing the learned per-prompt value function with a batch-level variance approximation degrades performance. The learned value function achieves what neither cancellation nor constant approximation can: it makes single-trajectory alignment statistically unbiased without paired data.

### Innovation 2: Off-Policy Regularisation Through `$\ell_2$`-Penalty in Log-Ratio Space Rather Than KL-Divergence

DRO introduces a subtle but consequential distinction between the regularisation that is *mathematically correct* for off-policy data and the regularisation that is commonly used in on-policy RLHF. This distinction has been largely overlooked in the alignment literature, where "KL-regularisation" is treated as synonymous with adding a KL-divergence penalty to the objective, regardless of whether the data is on-policy or off-policy.

**What the field did before.** The standard RLHF pipeline (Christiano et al., 2017; Ziegler et al., 2020; Ouyang et al., 2022) uses a KL-divergence penalty `$\text{KL}(\pi_\theta(\cdot|x) || \pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot|x))$` whose gradient is `$\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_\theta}[\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y|x) \cdot \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}]$`. This gradient requires an expectation over the **current policy** `$\pi_\theta$`, not over the behaviour policy `$\mu$` that generated the offline data. In online RLHF, where new completions are sampled from `$\pi_\theta$` at each training step, this is natural and correct. In offline settings, however, the data comes from `$\mu$`, and estimating the on-policy KL gradient would require importance-weighting each sample by `$\pi_\theta(y|x)/\mu(y|x)$`. These importance weights can have high variance, especially when `$\pi_\theta$` and `$\mu$` diverge, and the resulting gradient estimates become unreliable.

DPO and its variants sidestep this by moving the KL penalty into the loss function algebraically, so the gradient is computed directly from the log-ratio without any expectation over `$\pi_\theta$`. But this algebraic trick relies on the pairwise structure — the KL penalty manifests as a contrast between the preferred and dis-preferred log-ratios. In the single-trajectory setting, this trick is unavailable.

**What DRO does differently.** DRO's loss function, when differentiated with respect to policy parameters, produces the regularisation term `$\frac{\tau}{2} \nabla_\theta (\log \frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\text{ref}}})^2$` rather than the KL-divergence gradient. The paper explicitly notes that these are not equivalent off-policy (Section 3.3):

$$
\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\mu}\left[\nabla_\theta \left(\log \frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\text{ref}}}\right)^2\right] \neq \nabla_\theta \text{KL}(\pi_\theta, \pi_{\text{ref}})
$$

The `$\ell_2$`-penalty in log-ratio space is the natural regulariser that emerges from the squared DRO loss, and — crucially — it can be estimated without bias using off-policy samples `$y \sim \mu$` without any importance-weighting correction. The expectation over `$\mu$` is correct as written because the squared penalty treats each data point independently, without needing to reweight by the policy's own probabilities.

**Why this matters beyond DRO.** This distinction between on-policy and off-policy regularisation is not an implementation detail — it is a **conceptual clarification** that applies to any offline alignment method. The paper's gradient derivation (Equation 8) shows that the combination of (1) the value-function baseline subtracted from the reward and (2) the `$\ell_2$`-regularisation in log-ratio space together form the correct off-policy extension of on-policy regularised policy gradients. Neither term alone would suffice; the value function handles the off-policy bias in the advantage estimate, and the `$\ell_2$` penalty handles the off-policy bias in the regularisation. This is a **new decomposition** of the RLHF policy gradient that, to the authors' knowledge, had not been articulated before.

The significance is that it provides a template for constructing offline alignment methods from on-policy ones: identify the on-policy gradient structure, determine where expectations over `$\pi_\theta$` appear, and replace each with an off-policy estimator that is unbiased under `$\mu$`. The DRO loss accomplishes this in one unified objective, but the principle generalises.

**Is this fundamental or incremental?** For the broader RLHF field, this is a fundamental clarification of a point that had been muddled. For DRO specifically, it is what makes the method work at all with single-trajectory off-policy data — without the correct off-policy regulariser, the policy gradient would be biased, and the method would collapse or converge to a suboptimal policy. It is therefore both a conceptual contribution (clarifying the off-policy/on-policy distinction for regularisation) and a practical enabler (making DRO-V statistically sound).

**Evidence anchor.** The paper does not provide a dedicated ablation comparing `$\ell_2$`-regularisation to an importance-weighted KL gradient, which would directly validate the claim. However, the overall empirical results (63.4% winrate against KTO with T5-L, 57.5% with T5-XL; Figures 1–2) demonstrate that the combined off-policy objective works effectively, and the theoretical analysis (Proposition 1) provides the formal justification.

### Innovation 3: Decoupling the Policy and Value Function into Independently Optimisable Components That Converge to a Unique Joint Optimum

A persistent challenge in actor-critic and policy-gradient methods is the coordination problem: the policy and value function are interdependent (the value function evaluates the policy, and the policy improves using the value function's estimates), and poor synchronisation between their updates can lead to instability, divergence, or slow convergence. DRO's theoretical result — that `$(\pi^*, V^*)$` is the unique global minimiser of a joint loss that both components can be optimised against independently — represents a novel decomposition of this coordination problem.

**What the field did before.** In standard actor-critic RL, the policy (actor) and value function (critic) are optimised with different objectives: the critic minimises temporal-difference error or mean-squared reward-prediction error, while the actor maximises expected advantage. These objectives are compatible at the optimum — the optimal critic correctly evaluates the optimal actor, and vice versa — but there is no single loss function whose global minimum is the joint optimum. Practitioners typically use alternating updates, target networks, or trust-region constraints to stabilise the interaction. In RLHF specifically, classical approaches train a reward model first, then use PPO with a separately estimated value baseline, introducing additional asynchrony between the components. Even DPO, which eliminates the explicit reward model, implicitly coordinates the policy and an implied reward function through the pairwise contrast.

**What DRO does differently.** Theorem 1 establishes that the DRO loss `$\mathcal{L}(\pi, V)$` has a single, unique global minimum at `$(\pi^*, V^*)$`, and — this is the key — both `$\pi$` and `$V$` can be optimised by gradient descent on the **same loss function** without any coordination mechanism, alternating schedule, or constraint enforcement. The authors emphasise this explicitly: "we can optimise `$\pi$` and `$V$` independently and end up finding the optimum. This can greatly simplify algorithmic design in practice" (Section 3.1, Remarks). There is no need to enforce `$V(x) = \tau \log \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\text{ref}}}[e^{r(x,y)/\tau}]$` as a separate constraint, no need for target networks to stabilise the value function, and no need for trust-region clipping. Gradient descent on both sets of parameters reduces a single scalar loss, and the loss landscape naturally guides both toward the joint optimum.

**Why this is a conceptual advance.** This result rebuts a tacit assumption in much of the RLHF literature: that the partition function `$V^*(x)$` is somehow different from the policy `$\pi^*$` in its learnability — that it requires special treatment (cancellation, approximation, or separate estimation) because it involves an intractable sum over the completion space. Theorem 1 shows that this intractability does not preclude joint optimisation. The squared loss `$(r - V - \tau \log \frac{\pi}{\pi_{\text{ref}}})^2$` implicitly handles the normalisation condition through the interaction of the two terms: if `$V$` deviates from `$V^*$`, the squared error cannot be driven to zero for all `$y$` while maintaining that `$\pi(\cdot|x)$` sums to one, so gradient descent on `$\pi$` will naturally push `$V$` back toward the correct normalisation. The proof of uniqueness in Theorem 1 formalises this: assuming zero loss forces `$\tilde{\pi}$` to have the exponential form, and the sum-to-one constraint then forces `$\tilde{V} = V^*$`.

This is a **fundamental simplification** of the optimisation landscape for KL-regularised policy search. It suggests that the conceptual complexity of actor-critic coordination — a source of engineering difficulty and brittleness in RLHF pipelines — may be an artefact of the specific algorithmic choices (separate objectives, on-policy sampling, temporal-difference learning) rather than an inherent property of the problem. DRO demonstrates that a single, well-chosen regression objective can absorb the coordination problem entirely.

**Limitations and nuance.** The practical implementation in DRO-V does apply a policy learning rate rescaling by `$1/\tau$` (Section 3.3), acknowledging that the raw loss may be ill-conditioned with respect to `$\theta$` and `$\phi$`. This is a pragmatic admission that while the theoretical landscape has a clean unique optimum, the *geometry* of that landscape — its curvature in different parameter directions — still requires tuning. The simultaneous updates in Algorithm 1 (both `$\theta$` and `$\phi$` updated at each step from the same batch) work well empirically but may benefit from more sophisticated optimisation (e.g., separate learning rates, momentum, or preconditioning). The paper does not explore whether there exist pathological initialisations or data distributions where the joint optimisation fails to converge, so the practical robustness boundaries remain unknown.

**Evidence anchor.** The parameter-sharing ablations (Figure 5, Appendix C) provide indirect support: when `$\pi$` and `$V$` are forced to share a network, performance degrades dramatically (76.6% to 55.5% winrate vs SFT), suggesting that the two functions benefit from independent representational capacity even though they are driven by the same loss. The fact that separate networks converge reliably with identical learning rates and update schedules at both T5-L and T5-XL scales supports the claim that the joint optimisation is well-behaved in practice.

### Innovation 4: Reframing Single-Trajectory Alignment as Variance Minimisation Rather Than Reward Maximisation

A subtle but important conceptual shift in DRO is the interpretation of what the loss is actually minimising. The policy-only formulation `$\mathcal{L}(\pi) = \frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\rho}[\text{Var}_{y\sim\mu(\cdot|x)}(r(x,y) - \tau \log \frac{\pi(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)})]$`, derived in Appendix B, reveals that the DRO objective can be understood as **minimising the per-prompt variance of the advantage estimate** across completions, rather than directly maximising expected reward.

**What this means.** At the optimal policy `$\pi^*$`, the quantity `$r(x,y) - \tau \log \frac{\pi^*(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}$` is constant across all completions `$y$` for a given prompt `$x$` — it equals `$V^*(x)$` regardless of which completion is sampled. The variance is zero. For any suboptimal policy, this quantity varies across completions: some completions have higher reward but also higher log-ratio (the policy up-weights them), others have lower reward and lower log-ratio, and the tradeoff is not perfectly balanced. The variance measures the degree of this imbalance. Minimising the variance drives the policy toward the unique configuration where the reward-log-ratio tradeoff is perfectly calibrated for every completion.

**Why this reframing matters.** The variance-minimisation perspective provides an **alternative lens on what alignment means** that is distinct from both reward maximisation (standard RLHF) and preference satisfaction (DPO). In reward maximisation, the goal is to increase the probability of high-reward completions. In preference satisfaction, the goal is to make preferred completions more likely than dis-preferred ones. In DRO's variance-minimisation view, the goal is to make the **adjusted quality** — reward minus the regularisation cost of deviating from the reference — uniform across all completions that the reference policy could generate. This uniformity condition is mathematically equivalent to the KL-regularised optimum, but it recasts the optimisation as a calibration problem rather than a maximisation or comparison problem.

The practical significance is that this view suggests a **diagnostic**: if the variance of `$r - \tau \log \frac{\pi}{\pi_{\text{ref}}}$` across completions for a given prompt is high, the policy is suboptimal for that prompt. This is a per-prompt, scalar quality metric that does not require pairwise comparisons or ground-truth optimal completions. It could be used for targeted data collection (collect more feedback for prompts with high variance) or for early stopping (stop training when the average variance plateaus). The paper does not exploit this diagnostic, but the framing enables it.

**Connection to prior work.** Conceptually, this connects DRO to the literature on **distributional calibration** and **energy-based models**, where the goal is to make an energy function (here, `$r - \tau \log \frac{\pi}{\pi_{\text{ref}}}$`) constant across valid configurations. It also echoes the principle of maximum entropy inverse reinforcement learning (Ziebart et al., 2008), where the observed behaviour is assumed to satisfy a uniform-advantage condition under the true reward function. DRO inverts this: given a reward function, find the policy that satisfies the uniform-advantage condition. The novelty is applying this calibration perspective to single-trajectory offline alignment, where the "observed behaviour" is the static dataset of `$(x, y, r)$` triplets.

**Is this fundamental or incremental?** The variance-minimisation framing is a fundamental reconceptualisation of the single-trajectory alignment problem, but it is presented as a derivation (Appendix B) rather than as the paper's main thesis. The main text focuses on the joint `$(\pi, V)$` regression perspective, which is the practical algorithm. The variance interpretation is the theoretical insight that explains *why* the regression works: because the optimal policy is the unique configuration where the advantage has zero variance across completions. This is more than a mathematical curiosity — it unifies DRO with the broader class of methods that optimise by enforcing invariance or uniformity conditions — but the paper does not fully develop this connection.

**Evidence anchor.** The DRO-P experiment (Appendix B) directly implements the variance-minimisation objective, albeit with the batch-level approximation that degrades it. The fact that DRO-P still outperforms SFT (though underperforming DRO-V) confirms that variance minimisation, even approximately, is a viable alignment signal. The superiority of DRO-V over DRO-P demonstrates that learning the per-prompt value function explicitly is a more effective way to minimise the variance than using a batch-level proxy, but both approaches share the same underlying principle.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** All experiments use the **UltraFeedback** dataset (Cui et al., 2023). The paper preprocesses this dataset to create an offline dataset of triplets `(prompt, completion, reward)`, where the same prompt may appear in multiple triplets with different completions. The rewards are further normalised such that they have mean 0 and variance 1 across the entire dataset. The original UltraFeedback dataset contains preference pairs; the paper converts these into single-trajectory triplets by treating each completion and its associated scalar score as an independent data point, effectively discarding the pairwise structure.

- **Base model(s).** All experiments use **T5 encoder-decoder language models** (Raffel et al., 2020), a family of auto-regressive transformers with an encoder-decoder architecture. Two model scales are tested: **T5-Large (770M parameters)** and **T5-XL (3B parameters)**. All checkpoints are initialised from instruction fine-tuning according to the FLAN recipe (Chung et al., 2022); this initialisation policy is referred to as the **SFT (supervised fine-tuning) policy** and serves as `π_ref` — the reference policy from which the optimised policy should not deviate too far. The paper does not use decoder-only models (e.g., GPT, LLaMA, PaLM) for alignment training, though it uses PaLM2 as a judge for evaluation. The choice of T5 is motivated by the availability of the authors' infrastructure ("the details of the models architecture and software implementation can be found in Roberts et al., 2022"; Section 4).

- **Metrics.** The primary evaluation metric is **side-by-side winrate**, following what the paper describes as "now standard practice" (Zheng et al., 2023). For each pair of trained policies being compared, the system samples completion responses for a test set of prompts and asks **PaLM2** (Anil et al., 2023) as an LLM judge to determine which response is better. The evaluation prompt format asks PaLM2 to "assess the helpfulness and fulfillment of two responses A and B." The winrate is the percentage of test prompts where one policy's completion is judged superior to the other's. Standard deviation estimates are computed by averaging winrate scores over 5 evaluation folds of 1,000 test prompts each. For checkpoint selection during training, the paper records a checkpoint every 2,000 training steps and selects the best checkpoint based on its side-by-side winrate against the SFT policy. This means the final model is chosen by comparative quality of generated outputs, not by training loss.

- **Baselines.** Two baselines are used:
  - **SFT (Supervised Fine-Tuning policy):** The FLAN-initialised T5 model before any alignment training. This serves as both the reference policy `π_ref` and the floor against which alignment methods should improve.
  - **KTO (Kahneman-Tversky Optimization;** Ethayarajh et al., 2024): The only existing offline alignment method designed for single-trajectory data. KTO derives its objective from prospect theory (Kahneman and Tversky, 1979) and makes the simplifying assumption of a constant partition function per batch. The KTO baseline is optimised as much as possible: the paper reports results after searching over three values of the regularisation parameter `τ ∈ {0.1, 1.0, 5.0}`, with `τ = 5.0` being optimal. The KTO implementation uses the same KL divergence estimator and batchwise average reward computation as described in the original KTO paper, with no additional loss weighting. 
  
  Notably, **DPO and pairwise methods are not compared** because they fundamentally require pairwise preference data and cannot operate in the single-trajectory setting — comparing them would require a different dataset structure and would not be a fair evaluation of single-trajectory methods. The paper also does not compare against online RLHF methods (PPO) since these require online sampling during training, which violates the offline constraint DRO is designed for.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper does not measure compute in terms of FLOPs or generation counts. Instead, all methods are trained for a fixed number of steps (40,000 for T5-L models, 20,000 for T5-XL models) with a batch size of 32. The computational cost is reported in terms of training infrastructure and wall-clock time: T5-L models are trained on 4×4 TPU v5e configurations, achieving approximately 0.5 training steps per second (approximately 21 hours for 40,000 steps); T5-XL models use 4×8 TPU v5e configurations, achieving approximately 0.1 training steps per second (approximately 2 days for 20,000 steps). Both DRO-V and KTO use identical compute budgets in terms of training steps and hardware — the comparison is fair in that both methods process the same amount of data for the same number of gradient updates. There is no online sampling, so generation cost during training is zero for both methods. The paper does not report inference-time computational cost for evaluation.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The evaluation uses a test set of prompts (size unspecified in the main text, but evaluation folds of 1,000 prompts each are used) distinct from the training data. Standard deviation estimates are reported for the main results (Figures 1 and 2) using 5 evaluation folds, giving winrate estimates such as "78.9% ± 0.3%." However, the ablation studies (Figures 3–5) report only raw winrates without error bars or standard deviations, suggesting the ablations were evaluated on a single evaluation split. The paper does not describe any cross-validation for hyperparameter selection — the main DRO-V results use a default `τ = 1.0` without any sweep, while KTO results use the best `τ` found from a three-value sweep. This asymmetry in tuning effort favours DRO-V in the comparison: DRO-V is evaluated with its intuitive default, while KTO is given the benefit of hyperparameter optimisation. The paper does not discuss whether the test prompts used for evaluation overlap with or are independent of the training prompts, nor does it specify the total size of the held-out evaluation set beyond the fold size.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Head-to-Head Comparison: DRO-V vs. KTO vs. SFT

The paper's central empirical results are the side-by-side winrates comparing DRO-V against KTO and SFT, reported at two model scales.

**T5-Large (770M parameters; Figure 1):**

DRO-V achieves a **78.9% ± 0.3%** winrate against SFT, meaning the PaLM2 judge prefers DRO-V's completions over the reference SFT model's completions on roughly 4 out of 5 test prompts. KTO achieves **67.5% ± 0.7%** against SFT — an 11.4 percentage point gap in favour of DRO-V. In direct head-to-head comparison, **DRO-V beats KTO with a 63.4% ± 1.0%** winrate. This is the paper's strongest result and the primary evidence for DRO-V's superiority as a single-trajectory alignment method.

**T5-XL (3B parameters; Figure 2):**

DRO-V achieves an **81.5% ± 1.0%** winrate against SFT. KTO achieves **78.2% ± 0.7%** against SFT — a narrower 3.3 percentage point gap than with T5-L. DRO-V beats KTO in direct comparison with a **57.5% ± 0.8%** winrate. The performance gap narrows substantially at the larger model scale: the 63.4% winrate at T5-L drops to 57.5% at T5-XL.

**What these numbers mean practically.** A 63.4% winrate means that on a set of 1,000 test prompts, DRO-V's completions are judged better than KTO's on approximately 634 prompts, worse on approximately 366. This is a statistically significant margin (roughly 13 standard errors for T5-L) but is not an overwhelming dominance — KTO still wins on over a third of prompts. The narrowing at T5-XL (57.5%, roughly 9 standard errors) suggests that at larger model scales, the advantage of explicitly learning the value function may diminish. The paper does not provide per-prompt analysis or difficulty breakdowns that would explain *which types of prompts* drive the winrate difference.

**A critical interpretative note.** These results compare DRO-V at `τ = 1.0` (no hyperparameter sweep) against KTO at its best-found `τ = 5.0` (after sweeping over `{0.1, 1.0, 5.0}`). The KTO baseline was therefore given an optimisation advantage — its hyperparameter was tuned to maximise performance on this specific evaluation, while DRO-V's hyperparameter was set to an intuitive default. The paper presents this as evidence of DRO-V's robustness and ease of use ("we show our DRO-V results without such search, simply setting τ to a default intuitive value of 1.0"; Section 4.1). However, it also means the comparison is not hyperparameter-matched: a well-tuned DRO-V (with `τ` optimised per scale) might perform even better, or a KTO at `τ = 1.0` (the same default) might perform worse than the reported 67.5%, widening the gap. The paper does not report DRO-V's performance with `τ` sweeps, so the upper bound of DRO-V's capability at these scales is unknown.

The paper also provides qualitative examples (Appendix E) showing sample completions from both DRO-V and KTO, illustrating that DRO-V responses "appear slightly more helpful and focused" and that "it's often a combination of helpfulness and concision, rather than just one of these dimensions, that tilts the decision one way or another." These are illustrative but not systematically analysed — no quantitative breakdown of winrate by response length, helpfulness rating, or other quality dimensions is provided.

#### Impact of Regularisation Parameter τ

The paper sweeps `τ` for both DRO-V and KTO on T5-L models (Figure 4):

| τ | DRO-V Winrate vs SFT | KTO Winrate vs SFT |
|---|---|---|
| 0.1 | 70.5% | 63.5% |
| 1.0 | **78.9%** | 61.9% |
| 5.0 | 76.6% | **67.5%** |

**DRO-V's τ sensitivity.** Performance is reasonably robust: varying `τ` from 0.1 to 5.0 changes winrate within a 8.4 percentage point range (70.5% to 78.9%). The intuitive default `τ = 1.0` happens to be optimal. Both extremes (`τ = 0.1`, weak regularisation, and `τ = 5.0`, strong regularisation) reduce performance, though `τ = 5.0` (76.6%) degrades less than `τ = 0.1` (70.5%), suggesting that under-regularising (allowing the policy to deviate too far from the reference) is more harmful than over-regularising at this scale.

**KTO's τ sensitivity.** KTO is substantially more sensitive, with a 6.0 percentage point range (61.9% to 67.5%). Critically, the intuitive default `τ = 1.0` is *not* optimal for KTO — it yields only 61.9%, the worst of the three values tested. The best KTO performance requires `τ = 5.0`, making KTO five times more strongly regularised than DRO-V at its optimum. The paper does not explain this discrepancy, but it is consistent with the theoretical critique: if KTO's constant-partition-function assumption introduces bias that pushes the policy away from the reference, stronger regularisation (higher `τ`) would partially counteract this by pulling the policy back toward `π_ref`. DRO-V, which learns the partition function explicitly, does not need this compensatory over-regularisation.

**What is missing.** The paper sweeps only three values of `τ` for each method. A finer-grained sweep might reveal a different optimal `τ` for DRO-V (e.g., the true optimum might lie at `τ = 0.8` or `τ = 2.0` rather than exactly 1.0). More importantly, the paper does not report DRO-V results with `τ` tuned — it uses `τ = 1.0` as a fixed default for all experiments, including the T5-XL comparisons. It is possible that the optimal `τ` for DRO-V shifts with model scale (as KTO's optimal `τ = 5.0` was determined on T5-L and re-used for T5-XL "as well"; Section 4.1). A scale-sweep of `τ` for DRO-V would strengthen the claim that DRO-V is robust and easy to deploy.

#### Impact of Learning Rate

The paper sweeps the policy and value network learning rates on T5-L models (Figure 3):

**Joint learning rate sweep (top line):**

| Joint LR | Winrate vs SFT |
|---|---|
| 1 × 10⁻⁵ | 73.7% |
| 5 × 10⁻⁵ | 78.7% |
| **1 × 10⁻⁴** | **78.9%** |
| 5 × 10⁻⁴ | 78.4% |

DRO-V is remarkably robust to learning rate: performance varies within a 5.2 percentage point range over a 50× change in learning rate. Even at 5 × 10⁻⁴ (five times the default), winrate is 78.4%, barely below the optimum. Only the smallest learning rate (1 × 10⁻⁵) shows noticeable degradation (73.7%), likely due to undertraining within the fixed 40,000-step budget. The default of 1 × 10⁻⁴ is near-optimal.

**Value-only learning rate sweep (bottom line):**

| Value LR (policy LR fixed at 1 × 10⁻⁴) | Winrate vs SFT |
|---|---|
| 1 × 10⁻⁵ | 76.8% |
| 5 × 10⁻⁵ | 78.0% |
| 1 × 10⁻⁴ | 78.9% |
| 5 × 10⁻⁴ | **79.1%** |

Varying only the value network learning rate while keeping the policy learning rate at 1 × 10⁻⁴ produces minimal variation: winrates range from 76.8% to 79.1%, a 2.3 percentage point span. The paper notes "a small yet monotonic improvement in learning the value V faster than the policy" — the highest value learning rate (5 × 10⁻⁴) achieves the highest winrate (79.1%), though the difference from the default (78.9%) is negligible (0.2 percentage points). This supports the theoretical insight that the policy gradient rescaling by `1/τ` is the primary mechanism managing the policy-value learning rate balance, making DRO-V insensitive to the raw value learning rate.

**What this implies.** The learning rate robustness is practically significant — it means DRO-V does not require careful tuning of learning rate schedules or separate optimiser configurations for policy and value networks. The default values work well out of the box at T5-L scale. The paper does not verify this robustness at T5-XL scale, where the optimisation dynamics might differ.

#### Impact of Parameter Sharing Architecture

The paper ablates four architectural variants for DRO-V (Figure 5, Appendix C Figure 6), investigating whether the policy and value networks should share parameters and whether the value function should output a single scalar per batch or multiple values (one per token/prompt). All experiments use 10,000 training steps (one-quarter of the full 40,000-step budget) and T5-L models.

| Variant | Winrate vs SFT |
|---|---|
| Single Network, Multiple Values | 55.5% |
| Single Network, Single Value | 57.6% |
| Double Network, Single Value | 72.1% |
| **Double Network, Multiple Values** | **76.6%** |

**Separate networks dominate shared networks.** The gap between single-network and double-network variants is dramatic: the best single-network variant (Single Network, Single Value) achieves only 57.6% against SFT, while the worst double-network variant (Double Network, Single Value) achieves 72.1% — a 14.5 percentage point difference. The paper describes this as "perhaps counter-intuitive" since parameter sharing is standard in actor-critic RL. The authors hypothesise that the value function requires representations sufficiently different from the policy that forcing them into the same network creates harmful interference. Note that these experiments are slightly undertrained (10,000 steps vs. 40,000), so the absolute numbers are lower than the main results, but the relative ordering is informative.

**Multiple values outperform single value, but only with separate networks.** Within double-network variants, using multiple values (76.6%) outperforms using a single value per batch (72.1%) by 4.5 percentage points. Direct side-by-side comparison between these two variants yields a 54.9% winrate in favour of the multiple-value version (Appendix C, Figure 6). This empirically validates the theoretical argument against KTO's constant-partition-function assumption: forcing a single value across the batch degrades performance comparably to KTO's simplification. Within single-network variants, the multiple-value version (55.5%) actually underperforms the single-value version (57.6%), suggesting that the interference from parameter sharing is so severe that the finer-grained value representation cannot compensate.

**Full pairwise comparison (Appendix C, Figure 6):** The paper provides a complete matrix of side-by-side winrates between all four variants and SFT. The double-network multiple-value variant beats the single-network single-value variant 70.4% of the time, and beats the single-network multiple-value variant 74.7% of the time. These are decisive margins confirming that the architectural choices are non-trivial.

**What is missing.** The paper does not explain *why* parameter sharing fails so catastrophically. Possible hypotheses include: (1) the value function requires prompt-level representations that conflict with the token-level representations needed for generation; (2) the gradient signals from the value and policy losses point in opposing directions in shared parameter space, leading to destructive interference; (3) the T5 encoder-decoder architecture creates specific sharing pathologies that might not appear in decoder-only models. Without gradient conflict analysis or representational similarity measurements, the root cause remains speculative. More concerning for generalisability: these ablations are only run at T5-L scale with 10,000 steps. It is unknown whether the parameter-sharing penalty persists at T5-XL scale, with full training, or with decoder-only architectures.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**DRO-P (policy-only variant) vs. DRO-V:** The paper develops a policy-only variant (DRO-P) that eliminates the value network by approximating the per-prompt variance with batch-level variance (Appendix B, Equation 15). DRO-P "does not collapse and produces non-trivial resulting policies, that can in fact beat the SFT policy" but is "less competitive" than DRO-V (Appendix B). The paper does not report a quantitative DRO-P vs. DRO-V winrate or DRO-P vs. SFT winrate, only a qualitative statement that DRO-P underperforms. This is a significant omission — without a quantitative comparison, the reader cannot assess whether the value function provides a marginal improvement (e.g., 2–3 percentage points) or is essential (e.g., 10+ percentage points). The theoretical argument that the batch-level variance is a biased estimator of the per-prompt variance is sound, but the empirical magnitude of this bias in practice is not established.

**KTO comparison with τ sweep:** The KTO baseline is given the benefit of a three-value `τ` sweep (`{0.1, 1.0, 5.0}`), with `τ = 5.0` found to be optimal for T5-L and reused for T5-XL. DRO-V is run only at `τ = 1.0` without a sweep. The paper frames this as demonstrating DRO-V's robustness, but it also means the comparison is not hyperparameter-matched. The paper does not report what DRO-V's performance would be at `τ = 5.0` or with an optimised `τ`. This is a methodological asymmetry that favours DRO-V in the robustness narrative but potentially underreports DRO-V's ceiling if `τ = 1.0` is not optimal.

**Training steps ablations:** All main results use fixed training budgets (40,000 steps for T5-L, 20,000 for T5-XL) without ablating over training duration. The parameter-sharing ablations use only 10,000 steps. It is unclear whether DRO-V would continue to improve with longer training, whether the gap between DRO-V and KTO widens or narrows with more steps, or whether the optimal `τ` shifts during training. The paper also does not report training curves (loss vs. steps, winrate vs. steps) that would reveal convergence behaviour, potential overfitting, or whether the selected best checkpoint (every 2,000 steps) represents a stable plateau or a transient peak.

**Model scale ablation:** The paper tests two scales (T5-L at 770M and T5-XL at 3B). The DRO-V vs. KTO gap narrows from 63.4% to 57.5% when scaling from L to XL. The paper does not discuss this narrowing, offer hypotheses for it, or test whether it continues at larger scales. If the trend is monotonic, DRO-V's advantage might disappear entirely at 7B+ scales. The paper acknowledges this implicitly in the limitations section ("Further work is required to more broadly establish the performance gains that our approach provides when considering the largest language models"; Section 6) but does not analyse the observed trend.

**Reward normalisation:** The paper normalises rewards to mean 0 and variance 1 across the dataset. No ablation is performed to test sensitivity to this normalisation. If the normalisation is critical, DRO-V's practical applicability depends on having a well-calibrated reward scale, which may not hold for arbitrary user feedback signals (e.g., sparse thumbs-up/down, skewed rating distributions). The paper does not discuss how to set the normalisation in deployment or whether DRO-V fails under different reward distributions.

**Optimiser and training configuration:** The paper uses AdaFactor (Shazeer and Stern, 2018) with decay rate 0.8, 150 linear warmup steps, and a batch size of 32 for all experiments. No ablations over optimiser choice (e.g., AdamW vs. AdaFactor), batch size, warmup duration, or learning rate schedule are reported. The 150-step warmup is not justified. Batch size is stated as 32 but not ablated — this is particularly relevant because KTO's batchwise average reward computation and DRO-P's batchwise variance approximation both depend on batch statistics, and larger batch sizes might improve or degrade these estimates.

**Missing ablation: value function architecture depth/capacity.** The paper states that the value network is a separate T5 model but does not specify whether it is the same size as the policy network (e.g., both T5-L, both T5-XL). If the value network is smaller, the approximation error analysed in Proposition 1 would be larger, potentially explaining some performance characteristics. If it is the same size, the total parameter count of DRO-V is roughly double that of KTO (which only has a policy network), making the comparison unfair in terms of model capacity rather than just compute. The paper does not discuss this capacity asymmetry or report a KTO baseline with a comparably sized model.

**Missing baseline: regression onto rewards without KL-regularisation.** A natural question is whether the improvements from DRO-V come from the sophisticated `(π, V)` formulation or simply from doing any form of reward-weighted training. A baseline that fine-tunes the SFT policy to maximise `log π_θ(y|x) · r(x,y)` (reward-weighted regression, similar to RAFT; Dong et al., 2023) without any value function or KL-regularisation would help isolate whether the DRO-specific components are necessary. The paper does not include this baseline.

**Missing evaluation: automated metrics beyond winrate.** The paper relies exclusively on PaLM2-as-judge for evaluation. It does not report automated quality metrics (perplexity, diversity, length distribution), benchmark performance on standard NLP tasks, or human evaluation. The single-judge LLM evaluation, while now common, introduces potential biases: the judge model (PaLM2) may share architectural or training similarities with the evaluated models (T5, FLAN-initialised), and the evaluation prompt format may favour certain response styles. The paper does not validate the PaLM2 judge against human preferences or report inter-judge agreement rates.

**Qualitative examples are suggestive but not systematic (Appendix E).** The paper provides 8 example prompt-completion pairs comparing DRO-V and KTO completions. These are selected to illustrate the claimed advantages (DRO-V responses are "more helpful and focused"), but there is no sampling methodology described, no quantitative content analysis, and no negative examples where KTO outperforms DRO-V. The paper acknowledges this subjectivity: "we try to illustrate both some clear preference cases, as well as others that the reader might find more subtle" (Appendix E). Without systematic qualitative analysis, these examples serve as anecdotal illustration rather than evidence.

### Critical Assessment

The paper makes one central empirical claim: **DRO-V significantly outperforms KTO for single-trajectory alignment on the UltraFeedback dataset using T5 models.** The experiments provide reasonably strong evidence for this claim at the tested scales, but several factors constrain the strength and generalisability of this conclusion.

**What the experiments demonstrate.** The side-by-side winrates (Figures 1–2) show that DRO-V beats KTO with 63.4% (T5-L) and 57.5% (T5-XL) winrates. These are statistically significant margins and are robust to the KTO baseline being hyperparameter-optimised. The ablation studies (Figures 3–5) provide evidence that the key architectural choices (separate networks, multiple values, `τ = 1.0`) are well-motivated for DRO-V and that the method is robust to learning rate variation over an order of magnitude.

**What the experiments do not demonstrate.** Several important claims are asserted or implied but not experimentally verified:

1. **The value function is learning the partition function, not just serving as a variance-reduction baseline.** Proposition 1 provides a theoretical characterisation, but there is no empirical analysis of what `V_ϕ(x)` actually learns. Does `V_ϕ(x)` correlate with prompt difficulty? Does it approximate the log-sum-exp of reference-policy rewards? Without inspecting the learned value function, the claim that DRO-V "learns the partition function" remains a theoretical interpretation rather than an empirically validated fact. A simple diagnostic — correlating `V_ϕ(x)` with the empirical average reward for prompt `x` under the reference policy — would provide direct evidence.

2. **DRO-V's advantage comes from avoiding KTO's constant-partition-function assumption.** The paper's theoretical critique of KTO is that assuming a constant partition function biases the policy. The multiple-values vs. single-value ablation (Figure 5) shows that using a single value per batch (which approximates KTO's assumption) degrades DRO-V's performance, indirectly supporting the critique. However, this is an ablation *within* DRO-V, not a direct test of KTO's assumption. A more direct test would be to measure the variance of `V^*(x)` across prompts in the UltraFeedback dataset and correlate the DRO-V vs. KTO winrate difference per prompt with this variance — the theory predicts DRO-V should have the largest advantage on prompts where `V^*(x)` varies most.

3. **The `ℓ_2`-regularisation in log-ratio space is necessary for off-policy correctness.** This is a theoretical claim that is asserted but never tested empirically. Replacing the `ℓ_2` term with an importance-sampled KL gradient estimate (even if high-variance) and comparing performance would test whether the off-policy correction matters in practice or is a theoretical subtlety with negligible empirical impact at the tested scales and data volumes.

4. **DRO-V scales to large models and diverse tasks.** The paper tests two model sizes (770M, 3B) on one dataset (UltraFeedback). The narrowing gap from T5-L to T5-XL (63.4% → 57.5%) raises the possibility that DRO-V's advantage diminishes with scale. Without testing at 7B, 13B, or larger scales — and without testing on datasets beyond UltraFeedback — the claim that DRO "enables leveraging potentially orders of magnitude more data coming from user feedback" (Section 6) is aspirational rather than demonstrated. The paper's own limitation statement acknowledges this explicitly.

5. **DRO-V's joint optimisation is stable without special coordination.** The learning rate robustness (Figure 3) provides partial evidence. However, the paper does not report gradient norms, loss curves, or any metrics of optimisation stability. The policy learning rate rescaling by `1/τ` is described as addressing ill-conditioning, but no condition-number analysis or ablation of this rescaling factor is provided. Without these diagnostics, the claim of "greatly simplified algorithmic design" is supported by the *results* (good winrates) but not by an understanding of *why* the simplification works.

**Methodological weaknesses that qualify the results:**

- **Evaluation relies on a single LLM judge (PaLM2) without human validation.** The paper does not report any human evaluation, inter-annotator agreement, or calibration of the PaLM2 judge against human preferences. If PaLM2 has systematic biases (e.g., preferring longer responses, certain stylistic patterns), these could inflate or deflate winrates in ways that do not reflect true response quality. The standard LLM-as-judge methodology (Zheng et al., 2023) is widely used but known to have biases that require careful validation, which this paper does not perform.

- **T5 encoder-decoder architecture may not be representative.** The vast majority of contemporary LLM alignment work uses decoder-only architectures (GPT, LLaMA, PaLM, etc.). The T5 architecture has different inductive biases, a different pretraining objective, and potentially different alignment dynamics. The paper provides no evidence that DRO-V's advantages transfer to decoder-only models, where the "separate networks" design might be less natural (decoder-only models lack the encoder-decoder split that could facilitate separate policy/value representations).

- **KTO baseline may not be optimally implemented.** While the paper sweeps KTO's `τ`, it uses the same "KL divergence estimator on the batch as they do" without detailing this estimator. KTO's original paper (Ethayarajh et al., 2024) describes several implementation variants and hyperparameters (prospect-theoretic weighting, reference point computation); the paper's brief description does not establish that KTO is given a fully fair comparison. A KTO implementation by the original authors might outperform the paper's reimplementation.

- **The dataset conversion from pairwise to single-trajectory may introduce artifacts.** UltraFeedback is originally a pairwise preference dataset. The paper converts it to single-trajectory triplets by treating each completion and its associated scalar score independently, "where the prompt might be shared across multiple triplets" (Section 4). This means a single prompt appears multiple times in the training data with different completions and rewards. This multiple-appearance structure provides implicit pairwise information (the model sees multiple completions for the same prompt across different batches) that pure single-trajectory data from user logs would not contain — in true user feedback, each prompt typically appears once with one completion and one feedback signal. DRO-V may be benefiting from this implicit comparative structure in ways that would not transfer to genuinely single-trajectory deployment data. The paper does not discuss or ablate this property.

**Experiments that would have strengthened the paper:**

- **Human evaluation** of a subset of DRO-V vs. KTO comparisons to validate the PaLM2 judge.
- **Multiple datasets** beyond UltraFeedback (e.g., Anthropic's Helpfulness and Harmlessness data, OpenAssistant, or real user logs).
- **Decoder-only model architectures** (e.g., LLaMA, Gemma) at comparable scales.
- **Scaling to larger models** (7B+) to test whether the DRO-V vs. KTO gap persists, narrows, or reverses.
- **Ablation of the `1/τ` policy gradient rescaling** to test whether it is necessary or merely beneficial.
- **Inspection of the learned value function** (`V_ϕ(x)`) to validate that it captures prompt-dependent partition function structure.
- **Comparison against a reward-weighted regression baseline** (no value function, no KL-regularisation) to isolate the contribution of DRO's specific formulation.
- **Training curves and convergence diagnostics** (loss, gradient norms, policy entropy, KL from reference) to characterise optimisation behaviour.
- **Per-prompt difficulty analysis** correlating DRO-V's winrate advantage with prompt characteristics (length, reward variance, reference policy performance).

**Conditional nature of the claims.** The paper's central claim — that DRO-V outperforms KTO for single-trajectory alignment — holds under these specific conditions, which the experiments demonstrate but the paper does not explicitly bound:

- **When using T5 encoder-decoder models at 770M–3B parameter scales.**
- **When training on the UltraFeedback dataset converted to single-trajectory format (implicitly multi-completion per prompt).**
- **When KTO is implemented as described and tuned over `τ ∈ {0.1, 1.0, 5.0}`.**
- **When DRO-V uses separate policy and value networks of comparable capacity, multiple value outputs per batch, `τ = 1.0`, and policy gradient rescaling by `1/τ`.**
- **When evaluated by PaLM2 as an LLM judge using a specific evaluation prompt format.**

Whether the claim generalises beyond these conditions is unknown and, based on the narrowing T5-L to T5-XL trend, not guaranteed. The paper's positioning of DRO as a "generic framework" and its aspirational statements about leveraging "orders of magnitude more data" are not yet empirically supported — they represent the promise of the approach, not its demonstrated capability.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Learned Value Function Doubles Model Capacity and Training Cost with No Inference Benefit

DRO-V requires two independently parameterised neural networks — a policy network `π_θ` and a value network `V_φ` — both implemented as separate T5 models. The paper is explicit about this architectural choice (Section 3.3):

> "We consider a parametric policy `π_θ` and a parametric function `V_φ` with neural network parameters `θ, φ` respectively... we implemented DRO-V using two networks."

The consequence is that DRO-V's parameter count and training memory footprint are approximately **twice** that of methods like KTO, DPO, or standard SFT fine-tuning, all of which require only a single policy network. For T5-L (770M parameters), DRO-V trains roughly 1.54B parameters across two networks; for T5-XL (3B), roughly 6B. The training compute reported in Section 4 — 4×4 TPU v5e for T5-L (21 hours), 4×8 TPU v5e for T5-XL (2 days) — reflects this doubled capacity, but the paper's headline comparisons against KTO do not normalise for total parameter count. A fair capacity-matched comparison would allocate KTO a comparably larger single network or ensemble. At inference time, the value network is discarded entirely — only `π_θ` is used for generation — meaning the doubled training cost purchases no ongoing inference benefit. The value network is pure scaffolding: it enables correct single-trajectory learning but contributes nothing to deployment quality or speed.

The paper does not measure the cost-performance tradeoff explicitly. The parameter-sharing ablation (Figure 5, Appendix C Figure 6) demonstrates that sharing parameters between `π_θ` and `V_φ` in a single network dramatically degrades performance (55.5% winrate vs. SFT for single-network multiple-values, compared to 76.6% for double-network multiple-values), suggesting the representational separation is essential. However, the paper does not test whether a smaller value network (e.g., a more lightweight architecture, fewer layers, reduced hidden dimensions) could achieve comparable performance at lower cost. It also does not compare against a KTO variant that uses the freed capacity from not needing a value network to scale up its single policy model. This limitation is not acknowledged in the paper — the capacity asymmetry is not discussed as a confound in the experimental comparison, and the paper's claim that DRO-V is "simple" (Section 1) elides the significant additional model footprint.

---

### The Narrowing Performance Gap at Larger Scale Suggests the Advantage May Not Generalise

The paper's own results reveal a trend that it does not analyse: the winrate advantage of DRO-V over KTO shrinks from **63.4% ± 1.0%** at T5-L (770M) to **57.5% ± 0.8%** at T5-XL (3B) — a relative reduction of roughly one-third in the margin when scaling model size by approximately 4× (Figures 1 and 2). The paper reports these numbers but offers no discussion of the trend, no hypothesis for why it occurs, and no test at larger scales to determine whether it continues.

The consequence is that the paper's central empirical claim — that DRO-V significantly outperforms KTO — may not hold at the model scales (7B, 13B, 70B+) where contemporary LLM alignment is typically performed. If the trend is monotonic, DRO-V's advantage could vanish or reverse at larger scales. Several mechanisms could explain the narrowing: (1) as model capacity increases, the SFT policy may already be well-aligned, reducing the value of any alignment method and compressing performance differences; (2) KTO's constant-partition-function assumption may become less harmful at larger scales because the reference policy's output distribution for different prompts may naturally become more uniform in value; (3) the value function in DRO-V may be harder to learn well at larger scales due to increased prompt complexity, degrading DRO-V's relative advantage. Without evidence, these remain speculation.

The paper explicitly acknowledges the scale limitation in Section 6:

> "our empirical study is limited, both in terms of number of tasks and scale. Further work is required to more broadly establish the performance gains that our approach provides when considering the largest language models."

However, this is framed as a call for future validation rather than as an observed negative trend. The paper does not mention the T5-L to T5-XL narrowing in its limitations section, does not plot scaling curves or extrapolate, and does not discuss whether the trend should concern practitioners. A practitioner deciding whether to adopt DRO-V for a 7B+ model deployment has no evidence that the method would outperform KTO at that scale, and the available evidence points toward diminishing returns.

---

### The UltraFeedback Dataset Conversion from Pairwise to Single-Trajectory Introduces Implicit Comparative Structure That May Inflate DRO-V's Apparent Effectiveness

DRO is designed for single-trajectory data where each prompt appears with exactly one completion and one scalar reward — the canonical example being a user thumbs-up/down on a single model response. However, the paper's experiments use the **UltraFeedback dataset** (Cui et al., 2023), which was originally constructed as a pairwise preference dataset. The paper converts it to single-trajectory format by treating each completion and its associated score as an independent data point (Section 4):

> "We preprocess the data to create an offline dataset of triplets (prompt, completion, reward) where the prompt might be shared across multiple triplets."

This means that during training, the same prompt appears **multiple times** across different batches with different completions and different rewards. The model sees, for example, that for prompt `x`, completion `y₁` received reward `r₁` (in one batch) and completion `y₂` received reward `r₂` (in another batch). This provides **implicit comparative information** — the model can learn that certain completions for the same prompt are better than others — which is structurally similar to the pairwise information that DPO uses explicitly.

The consequence is that DRO-V's reported performance may partially depend on this implicit multi-completion structure, which would **not be present in genuinely single-trajectory data** from user logs. In real deployment settings, each user interaction produces exactly one prompt-completion-reward triplet: a user asks a question, the model responds once, and the user provides feedback. The prompt will not appear again with a different completion (or if it does, it will be in a different context, possibly with a different user and different expectations). The paper's evaluation therefore conflates "single-trajectory" (one completion per triplet in the training batch) with "single-encounter" (one completion per prompt period). True single-encounter data would provide no within-prompt variation for the model to exploit.

The paper does not discuss this distinction, does not ablate the effect of prompt repetition in the training data, and does not test DRO-V on a dataset where each prompt appears exactly once. The batch-wise variance approximation in DRO-P (Appendix B) would be particularly affected: if each prompt appears only once, the batch-level variance becomes purely across-prompt variance, which the paper notes is a biased estimator of the within-prompt variance the objective should minimise. DRO-V avoids this by learning `V_φ(x)` per prompt, but `V_φ(x)` may still benefit from having seen multiple completions for the same prompt during training, since this provides a richer signal for what the "baseline" value of that prompt is. A practitioner deploying DRO-V on genuine single-encounter user feedback data may find that performance degrades relative to the reported UltraFeedback results.

---

### Evaluation Relies Entirely on a Single LLM Judge Without Human Validation, Leaving the Winrate Metric's Ecological Validity Unverified

The paper's entire quantitative evaluation rests on **side-by-side comparison using PaLM2 as an LLM judge** (Section 4, "Evaluation"). The evaluation prompt asks PaLM2 to "assess the helpfulness and fulfillment of two responses." The paper treats PaLM2's judgments as ground-truth quality signals, computing winrates and standard deviations across 5 evaluation folds of 1,000 prompts each.

The consequence is that all reported performance differences — DRO-V's 78.9% winrate against SFT, 63.4% against KTO — are valid only to the extent that PaLM2's preferences align with actual human preferences for the UltraFeedback task. The paper does not report:
- **Human evaluation** of any subset of the comparisons to calibrate or validate the PaLM2 judge.
- **Inter-judge agreement** between PaLM2 and other LLM judges (e.g., GPT-4, Claude) or between multiple PaLM2 calls with different prompts or temperatures.
- **Position bias analysis**: whether PaLM2 systematically prefers Response A or Response B based on presentation order rather than content.
- **Length bias analysis**: whether PaLM2 prefers longer or shorter responses independent of quality. The paper notes qualitatively (Appendix E) that DRO-V responses are "more helpful and focused" and that "it's often a combination of helpfulness and concision... that tilts the decision," suggesting length/concision differences exist but without quantifying their effect on the overall winrate.
- **Correlation between PaLM2 judgments and any ground-truth quality metric** (e.g., downstream task performance, factual accuracy, human satisfaction ratings).

The LLM-as-judge methodology (Zheng et al., 2023) is widely used and the paper cites it as "now standard practice." However, recent work has documented significant biases in LLM judges, including position bias, verbosity bias, self-enhancement bias (preferring outputs from the same model family), and sensitivity to prompt formatting. PaLM2 was also developed by the same organisation as the authors (Google DeepMind), and while this does not automatically imply bias, the paper provides no independence guarantees for the evaluation pipeline.

The paper's qualitative examples (Appendix E) provide anecdotal face validity — they show DRO-V responses that appear more concise and focused — but these are selected by the authors and cannot substitute for systematic validation. A practitioner evaluating whether to adopt DRO-V over KTO needs to know whether the reported 63.4% winrate corresponds to a meaningful improvement in user satisfaction or is an artifact of PaLM2's particular preferences. The paper provides no evidence on this question.

---

### DRO-V's Robustness Claims Rest on an Asymmetric Hyperparameter Sweep That Favours DRO-V

The paper positions DRO-V as more robust and easier to deploy than KTO, emphasising that "we show our DRO-V results without such search, simply setting `τ` to a default intuitive value of 1.0" while "we optimise our baseline as much as possible and present the best KTO results we obtained after search over three values of the `τ` regularisation parameter" (Section 4.1).

The consequence is that the headline comparison — DRO-V 63.4% vs. KTO at 67.5% against SFT — is **not hyperparameter-matched**. The paper reports KTO's performance at its optimal `τ = 5.0` but does not report:
- **What DRO-V's performance would be with a `τ` sweep.** If DRO-V's optimum were at `τ = 2.0` or `τ = 0.5`, the gap could be wider. The `τ` sweep in Figure 4 shows only three values (`{0.1, 1.0, 5.0}`) and finds `τ = 1.0` to be best for DRO-V, but with only three values tested, the true optimum may lie elsewhere. A finer grid might reveal `τ = 0.8` or `τ = 1.5` to be better, potentially widening or narrowing the comparison gap.
- **What KTO's performance would be at `τ = 1.0`.** KTO at `τ = 1.0` achieves only 61.9% against SFT (Figure 4). If KTO were also evaluated "without such search" at its intuitive default, the DRO-V vs. KTO comparison would be DRO-V at 78.9% vs. KTO at 61.9% — a **17 percentage point** gap in SFT-relative winrate rather than the reported 11.4 point gap. The paper's framing choice makes DRO-V look *less* superior than it actually is by this metric, but it also obscures the true comparison: what a practitioner would get by deploying each method at its respective optimum vs. deploying each at its default.
- **What KTO's performance would be with the same `τ` sweep budget but including `τ` values near DRO-V's optimum.** KTO was swept only over `{0.1, 1.0, 5.0}`. If KTO's true optimum were at `τ = 3.0` or `τ = 7.0`, the reported KTO performance underestimates KTO's ceiling.
- **Whether the optimal `τ` changes with model scale.** The paper reuses the T5-L optimal `τ = 5.0` for KTO at T5-XL scale without re-sweeping. If KTO's optimal `τ` shifts with scale, the T5-XL comparison may use a suboptimal KTO configuration. DRO-V's `τ = 1.0` is also carried over from T5-L to T5-XL without re-validation.

The paper's robustness narrative — "DRO-V is simple and empirically compelling" (Abstract), "we do not make any assumptions" (Section 3.1) — is supported by the learning rate ablations (Figure 3), which indeed show DRO-V is insensitive to learning rate over a 50× range. But the `τ` robustness claim is asymmetric: DRO-V is tested at its intuitive default (which happens to be near-optimal among the three tested values), while KTO is tested at its best-found value from the same three-value sweep, with its intuitive default performing substantially worse. The paper does not acknowledge this asymmetry as a limitation or discuss its implications for the fairness of the comparison.

The mitigation status is that the paper partially addresses the broader hyperparameter robustness question through the learning rate ablation but does not address the `τ` asymmetry specifically. The limitation section (Section 6) does not mention hyperparameter tuning or comparison fairness. A practitioner reading the paper would reasonably conclude that DRO-V at `τ = 1.0` beats KTO at its best — which is true for the configurations tested — but would not know how much of that advantage comes from DRO-V being genuinely better vs. KTO being suboptimally tuned due to the coarse sweep range.
